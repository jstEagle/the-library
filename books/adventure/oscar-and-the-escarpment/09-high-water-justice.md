## 9. High Water Justice

The water came for the camp the way a creditor comes for a debtor—patiently at first, then all at once.

Noor felt it through the soles of her boots before she saw it: a shudder in the ground, deep and seismic, as though the escarpment itself had decided to shrug off everything built against it. The river had risen a full fathom in the last hour. Halloway's base camp—a sprawl of canvas and crates and cable perched on the shelf of rock above the tunnel mouth—was coming apart board by board, crate by crate, and the men who had built it were running in circles, shouting orders that the rain drowned before they could be heard.

And Noor Crane stood in the middle of it with a dead man's radio battery strapped to her back and a confession crackling in her ears.

"Say it again," she said. "Slower."

Ilsa's voice came through the handset, thin and steady, the voice of a young woman who had spent three days in a flooded culvert deciding what she would do if she lived. Behind her, muffled by rain and stone, Edmund Halloway was shouting—Ilsa had told Noor what he was shouting, and it was not a denial. It had stopped being a denial somewhere around the moment the water reached the crates of survey records and he had tried, with his own two hands, to shove them into the current.

*"The supports were cut,"* Ilsa repeated. *"Cut with a charge placed under the third trestle on the north approach. Halloway ordered it. The line was closed for insurance and the right-of-way was bought back at a fraction of its value. His father's company held the paper. Oscar Vane's father was the inspector on record. Halloway paid him to sign off on rotten timber and then buried him with the blame when the inquiry came."*

Noor's thumb rested on the transmit key. The whole valley was listening. She had opened every channel on the band—every station, every relay, every river town with a receiver and a kettle on the stove—and she could hear them now, faint under Ilsa's voice: the click of sets being turned up, the rustle of people leaning in. Somewhere downriver, in a town where Noor had once signed her name to a report that helped bury this very line, someone was hearing her father's compass come true.

"Go," she said.

Ilsa read it again. All of it. The dates, the amounts, the names of the men who had taken the payoff—Noor's father's name among them, spoken flat and without mercy, because mercy was not what this moment was for. Noor listened to her own inheritance being read aloud to a valley full of witnesses and found that she could breathe. That was the strange thing. She had carried the debt papers for three years like a stone sewn into her coat, and now that the stone was being dragged into the light she felt lighter, not heavier. Complicity, she understood at last, was not the weight. Concealment was.

*"That's the confession,"* Ilsa finished. *"He said it himself. All of it. He's saying it again right now."*

And he was. Noor could hear him through the open channel, raw with fury and flood, screaming at Ilsa, at the river, at the sky—*you have nothing, you have nothing*—and every denial was a second confession, every word carried on Noor's signal to a hundred ears in a hundred kitchens.

The water hit the camp's lower tier then. A wall of it, brown and muscular, taking the supply tent whole and folding it into the dark like a hand closing. Men scattered uphill. Noor climbed onto the radio crate, the highest ground left, and looked down at the tunnel mouth where the river was pouring into the old rail bed, filling it, reclaiming it, the closed line drowning in the lie that had closed it.

* * *

Oscar came out of the flooded forest like something the river had decided to give back.

Noor saw him first as movement among the drowned trees—dark, low, impossibly calm—and then he was beside the boat, hauling himself over the gunwale, and his face was a thing she had not seen before. It was not rage. It was not even grief, or not only. It was the face of a man who had stood in the place his father died and found the name waiting for him there, intact, and had picked it up.

"Logbook?" she asked.

He held it up. Wrapped in oilcloth, tied with wire, soaked at the corners but whole. The original inspection log. The signature that wasn't his father's, the initials that were. Oscar set it inside the boat's dry box with a care that made her throat tighten, and then he looked up at the camp, at the chaos, at the figure standing on the highest rock with his arms full of paper.

Edmund Halloway had stopped running.

Noor saw it happen. The water was at his knees, then his thighs, and instead of climbing he turned—turned his back on the boat, on the survivors, on the whole listening valley—and began gathering the floating contracts out of the current, one by one, pressing them to his chest like a man gathering his own children from a fire. They were only paper. Everyone on the band knew what was on the paper now. And he gathered them anyway.

"Halloway!" Noor shouted into her handset and into the rain. "The boat's here! Come down to the boat!"

He looked at her. Even at that distance, through the sheets of rain, she saw his eyes find her and understand her—understood that she was the engineer, the daughter, the debt-carrier, the voice on the band—and he smiled. It was the worst thing Noor had ever seen, that smile: not madness, but arithmetic. A man calculating that a drowned man's contracts could not be read aloud.

"The river keeps what the river keeps," he called back. He said it like a blessing. He said it like the closing line of his own story, and Noor understood with a cold, clarifying certainty that he had written this ending for himself years ago, in an office somewhere dry, when he first decided that a line and a man could be closed with the same signature.

The water took him a minute later.

It did not rage or roar. It simply rose, the way it had risen all day, the way it had risen for the whole history of the valley, and Halloway stood in it clutching his contracts until it took him off his feet, and then he was in it, and then he was of it, a dark shape turning in the brown flood with white paper streaming from his fists like the feathers of a drowned bird. Noor watched until the river folded him under. She did not look away. Looking away, she thought, was how all of this had happened in the first place.

On the open band, in a hundred kitchens, a hundred valleys' worth of people heard Ilsa say quietly: *"He refused rescue."* And that was the verdict. Not a courtroom's—there would be an inquiry, papers, months of it—but the first verdict, the human one, rendered by witness. The valley had heard him confess with his own voice and refuse mercy with his own body, and no lawyer alive could unsay either.

* * *

They pulled the others aboard in the worst hour of the flood.

There were four: the two foremen who had stayed loyal out of ignorance rather than malice, a cook who wept without sound, and Ilsa, who came down the rock face on a rope with Halloway's own field ledger strapped to her back because she had decided, three days ago in a culvert, that someone had to carry the evidence out even if the river disagreed. Oscar took the bow. Noor took the throttle of the patched boat—their boat, the one that had torn open on submerged debris and been sewn back together with canvas, wire, and Oscar's father's old caulking recipe—and drove it into the current the way her father had taught her to read a grade: not fighting the water, but negotiating with it.

The river wanted them downriver. For once, Noor agreed with the river.

They ran the flood through the drowned forest, Oscar calling the channels from the bow in a voice that never lifted, never dropped—*left of the deadfall, hard right at the culvert mouth, mind the wire*—and Noor steered, and the boat that should have come apart held together out of what Noor could only conclude was spite. Behind them the escarpment vanished into rain. Above them, on the open band, the valley was still talking. Station after station was coming up on the channel now, voices she didn't know, asking questions, confirming dates, one old telegraph operator down at Junction reading back the payoff amounts in a voice that shook. The confession was loose in the world. It could not be gathered up and drowned. It could not be bought back at a fraction of its value.

Ilsa sat beside Noor at the stern, gray-lipped, hands finally still for the first time in three days.

"They'll want statements," the young surveyor said. "Inquiry boards. Lawyers."

"I know," Noor said.

"You'll be a witness. So will I. So will he." She nodded at Oscar's back. "It'll take a year. Maybe two."

Noor looked down at the satchel at her feet. Her father's papers were in it—the debt, the ruin, the whole architecture of obligation that had been built on a cut trestle and a forged signature, the papers she had carried from town to town like a woman carrying her own sentence. She had meant to burn them on the dock, back when it was only about her. It was not only about her anymore. But some things, she thought, you still did for yourself.

"Let them take their year," she said. "I've got somewhere to be in the meantime."

The river opened ahead of them, wide and brown and, for the first time since she'd known it, honest—running exactly where it said it would run, carrying exactly what it said it would carry. Noor brought the throttle up. The patched boat lifted its bow, and the valley, still listening, heard the engine fade downriver toward the towns, toward the inquiry, toward the morning when the papers would burn and a dead inspector's name would come home.

Oscar turned once at the bow and looked back at the escarpment, at the tunnel mouth disappearing in the rain, and Noor saw him say something—two words, no more, lost to the storm but not to her.

His father's name.

He said it like a man taking it back.
