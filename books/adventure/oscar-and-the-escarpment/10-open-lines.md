## 10. Open Lines

The river dropped the way a fever breaks — not all at once, but in slow degrees, each morning revealing another sandbar, another drowned fence post standing up out of the water like a man rising from his knees. Oscar Vane noticed it first in the sound. For weeks the escarpment had roared, a constant white noise that swallowed thought and speech alike, and now, suddenly, there were gaps in it. Quiet places. The river clearing its throat.

He stood on the dock at Corren's Landing with his hands in his pockets and watched the water run brown and heavy but no longer angry, carrying leaves and branches south toward the delta instead of carrying houses. The pilings wore a fresh band of pale wood where the flood had scoured them. Everything along the bank looked scrubbed, new-minted, as if the monsoon had been less a disaster than an enormous act of erasure.

Three weeks since High Water Justice, as the papers had already begun calling it, though the papers got most of the details wrong. They said Halloway had been swept away by accident. They did not say he had refused the rope, that he had gone into the current clutching his contracts to his chest like a man embracing the only child he'd ever loved. They said the survivors of the Halloway expedition had been rescued through "coordinated efforts." They did not say that Noor Crane had sat in a flooding camp with her hands steady on a dead radio's battery and broadcast a confession to every station on the band, so that half the valley heard Edmund Halloway's voice — thin, furious, cornered — admit what he'd done before the water took his transmitter too.

They said a great deal about the logbook, though. That part they got right.

Oscar turned his face toward the escarpment, visible today for the first time in a month, its cliffs washed clean and sharp against a rinsed blue sky. Somewhere up there was the tunnel mouth. Somewhere up there, thirty years ago, his father had walked into a cut rail line with a lantern and an inspection book and had never walked out again — not really, not with his name intact. The valley had called him the man who broke the line. Saboteur. Wrecker. A name Oscar had spent his whole life trying to shed, running boats under another man's surname, answering to *Vane* the way you answer to a coat that doesn't fit.

The inquiry had reopened eleven days ago. Yesterday, the finding came down: the collapse of 1931 was engineered. Cut supports, dressed to look like rot. The inspection log — his father's own hand, careful and exact, flagging the sabotage two days before the "accident" — entered into record. His father's name cleared in a single paragraph of bureaucratic prose that could not possibly hold the weight of three decades.

Oscar had read the paragraph four times. Then he had walked down to the water's edge, alone, and said his father's name out loud for the first time since he was a boy.

*Aldous.*

It fit. God help him, after all these years, it fit.

* * *

Noor Crane arrived at the dock at midmorning, when the mist had burned off and the light lay flat and silver on the river. She came down the plank road from the rail office carrying a leather satchel and her father's compass, and she moved differently than she had the day she'd first hired him — looser through the shoulders, like someone who had set down a load she'd forgotten she was carrying.

She stopped beside him at the dock's edge and looked out at the water without speaking for a while. That was one of the things he'd come to respect about her: she didn't fill silence the way most people did, didn't treat quiet as a hole to be patched.

"The inquiry posted the findings this morning," she said finally.

"I heard."

"Your father's log is going in the permanent record. They're talking about a memorial plaque at the tunnel mouth." She glanced sideways at him. "You don't have to go to any of it, you know. The hearings, the ceremonies. You've given them everything they need."

"I know." He watched a heron stalk the shallows below the dock, patient as stone. "I'm going anyway."

She nodded, unsurprised. "Good."

A barge groaned past downstream, low in the water, loaded with creosoted ties and new rail stock — the first shipments of the restoration survey. The line was coming back. Not the old line, exactly; the engineers wanted real supports this time, honest ones, inspected by people whose names meant something. But the grade was still there, cut into the escarpment face by men who'd believed the route was worth building, and the route had never been the lie. Only the ending of it had been.

"You've heard from the survey office," Oscar said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen the way she carried the satchel, official-weight, not debt-weight.

"Chief surveyor wants me on the resurvey team." Noor's mouth twitched — not quite a smile, but the shape of one waiting for permission. "My father's maps are apparently the best terrain record anyone has of the upper escarpment. Turns out the disgraced daughter of a disgraced engineer kept better notes than the department did." She shrugged. "They want me to sign on. Full contract. Restoration survey, start to finish."

"And?"

"And I told them I'd take the job on one condition." She turned to face him fully then, and the flat river light caught the gray in her eyes. "That I get to choose my pilot."

Oscar looked at her. The river ran beneath them both, brown and steady, carrying the season away.

"You know I'll do it," he said. "You knew I'd do it before you walked down here."

"I know." Now the smile came, small and crooked and entirely hers. "But I wanted to hear you say it. I've spent my whole life having things taken from me because other people made decisions in rooms I wasn't in. I'm done with that. From here on, everything I agree to, I agree to out loud, to someone's face."

"Then let me say it properly." He straightened, and found — to his mild surprise — that the words came easier than any he'd spoken in years. "Noor Crane. I'll run your boat. Up the escarpment, down it, through whatever weather the valley decides to throw at us. Every day of the survey, start to finish." He paused. "Under my father's name. Aldous. I filed the paperwork yesterday."

Something moved across her face — not pity, nothing so cheap as that. Recognition, maybe. One person watching another come home to themselves.

"Aldous," she repeated, testing it. Then she extended her hand, formal as a contract signing, and he took it, and her grip was as steady as it had been on the radio during the flood. "Pilot Aldous. Welcome aboard."

* * *

They burned the papers that afternoon, on the dock, because Noor said she wanted witnesses and Oscar was the only one she trusted to understand.

Not all of them — the legitimate contracts stayed, the ones for the survey work, signed and countersigned and folded into the satchel. But from the bottom of her bag she drew a bundle tied with string, soft-edged from years of handling, and she held it a moment before untying the knot.

"My father's debts," she said. "Every note, every lien, every promise he signed against the railroad's future. Against *my* future, once he was gone." She weighed the bundle in her palm. "Halloway bought some of this paper years ago. Did you know that? He held markers over half the families on the upper line. That's how he kept the sabotage story buried — everyone who might have talked owed him money."

Oscar hadn't known. It explained so much, and so economically, the way the worst truths always did.

"The inquiry voided them yesterday," Noor went on. "Fraud-tainted instruments. Legally, they're worth nothing now. I could keep them as evidence. I could frame them." Her jaw tightened. "Or I could do this."

She struck a match against the dock rail. The wind off the river tried to take the flame twice before it caught, and then the bundle lit all at once, dry paper going up in a bloom of orange that reflected doubled in the brown water below. She held it until the heat reached her fingers, then dropped the burning mass into an oil drum kept on the dock for just such purposes, and they stood together and watched her father's debts curl and blacken and lift as ash on the updraft.

"Restless hope," she said quietly, when the last of it had gone gray. "That's what I was carrying, all this time. I told myself it was indifference. It wasn't. It was just hope with nowhere to point."

Oscar reached into his pocket and brought out the compass — her father's compass, which she'd pressed into his hands at the tunnel mouth three weeks ago, in the worst hour of the flood, telling him to navigate them out when she couldn't hold anything steady anymore. He'd meant to return it days ago. He'd kept finding reasons to wait.

He handed it over now. Noor opened the case. The needle swung, wobbled, and settled — north, true north, unwavering, as if it had been waiting all these years for the country around it to stop lying.

"There," she said softly. "Somewhere true."

The ash in the drum stirred and rose, a small gray ghost climbing the afternoon air, and the river carried the rest of the season south, and above the escarpment the first survey flags were already going in — bright cloth on the cliff face, marking a road that would, at last, be built on honest ground.

Oscar Aldous stood on the dock with the compass open between them, pointing home.
