## 1. The Debt of Clear Skies

The rain in Saltmere Port didn't fall so much as settle, the way an old debt settles on a man — patiently, from every direction at once. Dominic Underhill stood under the awning of the Harborside Credit Office and watched it come down in gray sheets over the yard where his three convoy trucks sat like patient animals, tarps flapping, while his crew worked beneath them.

He'd been standing there for eleven minutes because Mr. Fenwick had asked him to wait, and Mr. Fenwick always said *please* when he ruined a man's life.

Through the fogged glass he could see the office interior: warm lamplight, a kettle steaming on a side table, Fenwick's junior associate — a young man named Pell whose smile arrived a half-second before the rest of his face — arranging papers with the tenderness of someone handling a sleeping infant. That was the thing about Calloway's people that Dominic had never gotten used to. They were never cruel. Cruelty would have been easier. Cruelty you could hate. What they offered instead was courtesy so complete it felt like being buried politely.

"Mr. Underhill." Fenwick opened the door himself, holding it wide, rain beading on his umbrella. "Come in out of that dreadful weather. Tea?"

"I'm wet enough to drown," Dominic said. "But sure."

Inside smelled of bergamot and carpet cleaner. He took the chair across from Fenwick's desk — the good chair, the one without a wobble — and accepted a cup he didn't want because refusing seemed like the kind of small rebellion that would cost him later, in some ledger he couldn't see.

Fenwick settled behind the desk, folded his hands, and regarded Dominic with the expression of a physician about to deliver news that was serious but manageable. "Now. I'll be direct, because I know you're a busy man."

*Direct.* In eleven years of dealing with Calloway's collectors, Dominic had never once heard anything direct come out of that office. Directness was for people who could afford it.

"The principal stands at forty-one thousand, two hundred marks," Fenwick said. "Interest accruing monthly. Your last payment covered the interest only." A pause, gentle as snowfall. "Mr. Calloway is not unsympathetic to your situation. The storm season has been hard on everyone in your line of work."

"My line of work is why you have a line of work," Dominic said.

Fenwick smiled as if Dominic had made a charming joke at a dinner party. "Quite. So. We're prepared to offer another extension. Sixty days. In exchange, we'd ask for a modest adjustment to the terms — the collateral schedule expands to include the trucks themselves."

Dominic set down the tea before his hand could do something embarrassing. "The trucks are the business."

"Yes," Fenwick agreed warmly. "Which is precisely why we've been so patient."

There it was. The whole architecture of the thing, laid out in one pleasant sentence. They had been patient the way a tide is patient. And now the tide wanted the boats.

Outside, through the streaming window, he could see Tomás on his back beneath the second truck, boots sticking out into the rain, arguing with Priya about torque values in the particular language of long partnership — half shouting, half laughing. Idris, their rookie scout, stood lookout under a tarp with a thermos, pretending not to watch the credit office the way you pretend not to watch a doctor's door. His crew. Three people who trusted him to keep the wheels turning, and every month he brought them a little closer to this warm lamplit room with its polite men and its expanding collateral schedules.

"Sixty days," Dominic said.

"With the expanded schedule, yes. Or—" Fenwick lifted one finger, a magician about to produce the dove, "—there is a third option. Mr. Calloway occasionally extends work contracts to clients in... transitional circumstances. Hauling contracts. Escort work. Well-compensated. It would clear a meaningful portion of the principal."

"What kind of hauling?"

"That depends on the contractor. We simply facilitate introductions." Fenwick slid a card across the desk, cream-colored, heavy stock. No name printed on it. Just an address in the upper town and a time: *this evening, seven o'clock.*

Dominic looked at the card and felt the specific nausea of a man being handed a leash and told it's a gift. "And if I don't come?"

"Then sixty days and the expanded schedule remain available. We are flexible, Mr. Underhill. Within reason." Fenwick walked him to the door, opened his umbrella over him personally, and shook his hand with genuine warmth. "Do stay dry. This weather — it gets into everything, doesn't it? Walls. Ledgers. People."

* * *

By late afternoon the rain had thinned to a fine persistent drizzle, and the yard had turned to soup. Dominic found Priya in the cab of the lead truck, cross-legged on the bench seat with her navigation charts spread around her like a fortune-teller's table, marking routes in grease pencil.

"We're not taking the coast road," she said, before he'd even climbed up. "Two washouts past Tarn Bridge. And don't say the river road, because the river road is a rumor with potholes."

"So we go through the foothills."

"So we go through the foothills, slowly, praying to whatever god handles drainage." She glanced up at him, and her eyes did the thing they always did — read him like a chart, all the annotations showing. "You went to see Fenwick."

"Fenwick saw me. There's a difference."

"And?"

"They want the trucks as collateral. All of them." He lowered himself onto the passenger seat, which groaned. Everything in his life groaned lately, including him. "Or there's a job. Some contract work, upper town, tonight."

Priya put down her pencil. When Priya put down her pencil, it meant something; she guarded that pencil like other people guarded weapons. "Dominic. Contract work from Calloway isn't work. It's a hook with better bait."

"I know what it is."

"Do you?" She turned to face him fully. "Because I've watched you take extensions for two years, and every time you come back from that office looking like a man who's been complimented by a pickpocket. You keep thinking if you're smart enough, decent enough, one more run will square it. But that's not how they built it. It doesn't square. It rounds down. Always in their favor, just barely, so you can't prove you're being robbed."

"You done?"

"No." She held his gaze. "Tomás heard about the collateral. He's been quiet all afternoon, which for Tomás means he's terrified. Idris asked me if we'd lose the trucks. He's nineteen, Dominic. He signed on because you fixed his mother's generator for free and told him he had good eyes. If you take some Calloway contract to save this outfit, make sure it's a contract worth what it costs."

She picked her pencil back up. Conversation over. That was Priya — she never argued past the point of usefulness. She just planted the flag and let you walk around it.

Dominic climbed down into the drizzle and found Tomás under the third truck, finally emerged, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up its original color sometime last year.

"She talked to you," Tomás said.

"She talks to everybody. It's her superpower."

Tomás grunted and leaned against the fender, looking at the truck the way a man looks at a sick horse. "I ran the numbers on the payout, if the contract's real. Enough to clear the principal. Maybe enough left over to rebuild the winch on number two." He paused. "I also ran the numbers on losing these trucks. Both sets came out ugly, Dom. One's just uglier longer."

That was the trouble with Tomás: he was honest in a way that felt like betrayal but wasn't. He just refused to lie about arithmetic.

"Upper town, seven o'clock," Dominic said. "I'll hear them out. Hearing them out isn't saying yes."

"No," Tomás agreed. "It's just the first step on the road to yes. I've walked it. Everybody's walked it." He pushed off the fender and headed for the tarp, then stopped. "Wear the good coat. Those people judge."

* * *

The address in the upper town turned out to be a private club called the Meridian, all dark wood and low light, where the rain outside seemed to exist only for the pleasure of making the interior feel warmer. Dominic wore the good coat. He felt like a crow in a cathedral.

A woman was waiting for him in a corner booth, and he knew her before he reached the table, because some faces you never forget, even after nine years, even when the last time you saw one it was shouting at you across a flooded depot about route choices and dead men.

Maren Voss had gone gray at the temples since then. Otherwise she was exactly as he remembered: composed, precise, sitting with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door, wearing field clothes under a city coat like a woman who never quite unpacked from somewhere else.

"Dominic." She didn't stand. She knew he wouldn't want her to.

"Maren Voss." He sat across from her. "I heard you'd gone respectable."

"I went corporate. Respectable was never on the table." She slid a folder across the table. "You got Fenwick's invitation. Good. Saves me the trouble of finding you through less pleasant channels."

"So you're the work."

"I'm the client. Calloway finances. I plan. You drive." She tapped the folder once, didn't open it. "A crossing. Convoy escort, Kestrel Range, before first snowfall. Salvage recovery at high altitude. Four weeks, maybe five. The payout clears your entire principal, with margin."

The words landed one at a time, each heavier than the last. *Kestrel Range.*

Dominic looked at her for a long moment. Around them the club murmured — glasses, laughter, the soft economics of comfortable people — and none of it touched the cold spot opening behind his sternum.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard the details."

"I don't need details. I swore off that route nine years ago, and I swore it over four coffins and a cairn that the river ate anyway. There is no version of this conversation where the answer changes."

"Elias mapped half that range," Maren said quietly. "Your mentor. Half the safe crossings anyone uses up there exist because Elias Vann drew them. And you won't drive his own route."

"Don't you say his name like you knew him."

"I did know him. That's rather my point." Something moved behind her composure then — brief, quickly filed away, but real. "Dominic. The station we're recovering is Station Nine. You know what happened to Station Nine."

Everyone knew what happened to Station Nine. It drowned. The whole ridge facility, swallowed in the flood year, along with its instruments, its records, and eleven people who weren't lucky enough to be driving cargo instead of science.

"And the data cores inside it," Maren went on, "are sealed. Weather records. Forecast models. Nothing you'd care about." Her eyes held his, steady, and Dominic had spent enough years reading storms to know when a front was lying about itself. "Four weeks' work. Your debt gone. Your trucks yours. Your crew paid through next winter."

He should have stood up. Every part of him that remembered black water running through the depot, that remembered Elias's voice on the radio getting thinner and thinner until it was just static shaped like a man — every part of him said *stand up, walk out, eat the extension, lose the trucks, lose anything except that.*

Instead he heard himself say, "What's the margin?"

Maren almost smiled. "Enough to rebuild your winch."

Dominic stared at the closed folder between them, cream-colored as Fenwick's card, and understood that he had already lost the argument he was still having with himself. Pride said walk. Desperation had already signed.

"One condition," he said. "We route it ourselves. My navigator draws the line. Nobody touches her charts."

"Agreed."

"And if I say the weather turns, we turn. No client overrides. Not you, not Calloway, not God."

Something flickered across Maren's face at that — too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Agreed," she said again.

She slid the folder toward him at last, and he opened it, and the first page was a map of the Kestrel Range with a route inked in red, and the route passed within six kilometers of the depot where Elias had died, and Dominic felt the mountain reach out of the paper and close its hand around him, patient as a tide, patient as a debt.

"Convoy assembles in four days," Maren said. "Welcome back to the Range, Dominic."

Outside, the rain kept falling, getting into everything.
