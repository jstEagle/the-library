## 2. What the River Kept

Maren Voss ordered tea the way she did everything else — precisely, and as if the universe had already agreed to it. Two pots, one black, one with cardamom, set down on the scarred table of the Blue Heron's back room while rain drummed the tin awning outside like a creditor's knuckles.

Dominic didn't touch his. He'd learned years ago that nothing Maren bought him came free.

"Salvage," she said, laying a folder on the table between them. "That's all this is. Station Nine went under in the flood six years ago. The instruments inside were sealed units — pressure housings, data cores, the works. They're worth more than everything in your convoy combined, and they're sitting at the bottom of a reservoir doing nobody any good."

"Reservoirs have roads around them," Dominic said. "You don't need me for that."

"The station sits above the waterline nine months of the year. It's the other three that are the problem." She opened the folder. Topographic maps, contour lines stacked like fingerprints, a red route inked through the Kestrel Range. "The melt season ends in five weeks. After that, the approach floods again and the passes close. I need someone who can get a team up there and back before the snow."

"And you thought of me." He kept his voice flat. "Out of everyone with trucks and a death wish, you thought of Dominic Underhill."

"I thought of the best route man on this coast."

"You thought of the cheapest one desperate enough to say yes."

Maren didn't flinch. That was the thing about her — she never flinched, not when they'd been rivals running competing convoys, not now, sitting across from a man she knew was drowning. "I thought of someone who's been to Station Nine," she said quietly. "Someone who knows the ground."

The rain filled the silence.

"You know I haven't been back," he said. "You know why."

"I know what happened." For just a moment, something moved behind her eyes — something that wasn't business. Then it was gone, shuttered away like cargo before an inspection. "Elias Reyes. Four crew. The Kestrel flood took them, and it took the station, and it took you. I'm asking you to go take something back."

Dominic looked at the map. The red line crossed the river valley where he'd last seen Elias — standing on the station roof, waving the younger trucks forward, certain there was time. There had been no time. The water had come down the valley like the hand of God closing, and Dominic had watched from high ground with eleven survivors and no mentor, counting heads and coming up four short, then five.

He pushed the folder away. "No."

"Dominic—"

"No. Find another fool."

* * *

Priya found him an hour later on the dock, watching Tomás weld a patch onto the second truck's fuel line. Sparks spat and died against the wet air. His mechanic worked with the grim economy of a man who knew exactly how many kilometers of life were left in each part of each vehicle.

"He offered how much?" Priya asked, falling into step beside him. She didn't look at him. She'd learned long ago that looking at Dominic during hard conversations made him stubborn.

"Enough to clear Calloway twice over."

"And you said?"

"I said no."

She nodded slowly. "Good. Now let's see if it holds." She stopped at the rail, watching the gray water slap the pilings below. "Dom, listen to me. A woman like Maren Voss doesn't spend that kind of money on instrument cores. Salvage companies exist. Insurance exists. She came to you because of what happened to you, and that means the job is about what happened to you, and I don't like jobs that wear our dead as a disguise."

"She says the cores are sealed scientific instruments."

"I say she's lying about something. Maybe not the cores. Maybe the reason. But she's lying about something, and people like us can't afford to be lied to by people like her." Priya finally turned to face him. She was small, sharp-eyed, and had navigated him out of more trouble than either of them could count. "We refuse. We find another way with Calloway. We always find another way."

"Do we?" He nodded toward Tomás. "Look at him. Look at the trucks. We're running on borrowed everything — borrowed parts, borrowed time, borrowed money at interest that would make a loan shark blush. Another way means another extension, and extensions run out, Priya. You know they do."

Tomás killed the torch and lifted his visor, soot-striped and squinting. "Are we talking about the Voss contract? Because I vote yes. Enthusiastically. With confetti."

"You haven't heard what it is," Priya said.

"I heard the number." Tomás set down the torch and wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than rag. "That number pays off Calloway. That number buys new axles, new tires, actual food. That number means I stop sleeping in the truck I'm afraid will be repossessed." He smiled, but it was tired at the edges. "What's the job?"

"Station Nine," Dominic said.

Tomás's smile died. The welding shop noise seemed to die with it. Everyone on the coast knew what Station Nine meant. Everyone knew whose name was welded to it in memory.

"Oh," Tomás said softly. "Oh, Dom. That's — that's a lot of money to pay a man to walk into his own worst day."

"That's exactly what it is," Priya said. "A payment. And payments buy things, Dom. They buy obedience. Think about why she needs *you*, specifically, and tell me the answer doesn't scare you."

Dominic looked out at the harbor. Fishing boats sat low in the rain, and beyond them the Kestrel Range stood up behind the coastal hills, white-capped already, patient as a debt collector. He had sworn off that route. He had said words over Elias's grave — real words, spoken aloud, the kind you don't get to unsay.

And yet.

"Give me until morning," he told Maren's messenger that evening, and hated himself a little for not saying no again.

* * *

Maren came to the yard herself at dawn, when the fog was still peeling off the water and the crew was loading in the half-light. She stood at the edge of the convoy like a woman appraising horses, and Dominic walked over to her with the folder under his arm and something bitter lodged in his throat.

"One crossing," he said. "Up to the station, recover the cores, straight back down the valley route. No detours. No surprises. And when we're done, my debt to Calloway is settled — every cent, in writing, before we roll."

"In writing," she agreed. "Before you roll."

"And Maren—" He held her gaze. "If I find out this job is anything other than what you've told me, I'll leave those cores at the bottom of the reservoir and drive home. Contract or no contract."

Something flickered across her face then — too fast to name. Guilt, maybe. Or fear. Or the particular expression of a person carrying a secret heavy enough to bend her spine.

"Fair terms," she said.

He turned to go, and her voice caught him.

"Dominic. Why did you change your mind?"

He didn't turn around. "Because Elias spent his whole life saying the mountain gives back what you're willing to carry up to it. And because I'm tired of being owned by men who never climbed anything in their lives." He shouldered the folder. "Roll out in twenty minutes. Bring your fixer and stay out of my crew's way."

Behind him, he heard Priya mutter to Tomás, low and unhappy: *"I still say we refuse."*

And Tomás, tightening the last strap on the lead truck, answered with the simple arithmetic of a man who'd counted every bolt he couldn't afford: *"And I say we're already rolling."*

Above the yard, past the fog, the Kestrels waited — and somewhere up there beneath six years of cold water, Station Nine kept whatever the river had kept for it.
