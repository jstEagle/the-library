## 3. Convoy of Ghosts

Priya had a superstition about departure days: never leave before the tea is finished. It wasn't mysticism so much as arithmetic — every expedition she'd been on that skipped the morning cup had found some way to punish them for it, and every one that waited had at least failed politely.

So when Tomás came clanking out of the truck bay at dawn, wrench in hand and grease to his elbows, and announced, "We're ready, we're ready, the mountain won't wait," Priya did not look up from her thermos.

"The mountain," she said, "has been there for four million years. It can wait nine more minutes."

Tomás grinned and sat down on an ammo crate across from her, which was how he asked permission to share her silence. He was a big man built like something assembled from spare parts of larger men, and he had the mechanic's particular faith that everything in the world could be fixed if you hit it correctly. "You slept?"

"Badly."

"You dreamed about the river again?"

"I dreamed about the *paperwork*," Priya said, which was true and also not the whole truth. She had dreamed about the river wearing the paperwork's face. In the dream, Maren Voss's contract had been printed on water, and every time Priya tried to read the liability clauses, the letters swam downstream.

Dominic emerged from the office trailer then, folding a paper map along creases that were already soft as cloth. He looked like he'd slept even less than she had, which was his custom before a crossing — as though sleep were a debt too, and he refused to owe anyone anything else. His eyes went to her thermos, and he opened his mouth.

"Don't," Priya said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You said it with your eyebrows. Nine minutes."

He sighed and sat down beside Tomás, and the three of them watched the light come up over the Kestrel Range, where the clouds were already stacking themselves like plates in a kitchen nobody was washing. The peaks wore their first white of the season, thin and mean, the kind of snow that was less weather than warning.

"Beautiful," Tomás said.

"Liar," said Priya.

"Both things can be true."

That was Tomás all over. He'd signed on for the payout — everyone knew it, Tomás loudest of all, because Tomás believed shame was a rust you could grind off if you just kept talking. He had a daughter in the valley towns whose school fees were measured, these days, against the price of axle seals. Priya understood this. She understood it the way you understand a current under calm water: by keeping well clear of looking directly at it.

Idris arrived last, jogging from the gate with his pack half-buckled and his radio antenna poking out of it like a heron's leg. He was twenty-three, all elbows and enthusiasm, and he still called Dominic "sir" in a way that made everyone wince slightly, including Idris.

"Scout Idris reporting," he said. "Weather service says the front holds off until Thursday."

"Weather service," Dominic said, "has never met this range."

"That's why they're the weather service and not the range, sir."

There was a pause while everyone decided whether that had been funny. Tomás laughed first, generously, the way he laughed at everything Idris said, because someone had to teach the boy that the world could be kind. Priya allowed herself half a smile into her tea.

And then there was Halloran.

Maren's fixer came around the side of the second truck without anyone hearing him arrive, which was his entire personality expressed as locomotion. He was a lean, gray man with a face like a closed ledger, and he nodded once at the group as if confirming an inventory. His gear was packed already. Of course it was. Halloran had probably been packed since Tuesday, possibly since birth.

"He doesn't drink tea?" Idris whispered.

"Halloran drinks tea," Priya said. "Halloran simply does not require witnesses."

She poured him a cup anyway and set it on the crate beside him, and after a moment — long enough that she almost checked whether he was breathing — he picked it up and drank it in three unhurried swallows. It felt, absurdly, like a treaty being ratified.

* * *

They rolled out at 7:40, two trucks and a scout bike, and the road gave out forty minutes later exactly as promised, becoming the kind of track that was less a path than a rumor other drivers had started. The foothills took them in stages — scrub first, then pine, then pine gone dark and close, holding the cold like a grudge.

Priya rode shotgun in the lead truck with the navigation board glowing on her knees. Her job was simple to describe and impossible to do: know where they were, always, even when the mountain declined to say. She'd learned it from Elias Varga, back when Elias was alive and the Kestrels were just lines on a chart he annotated in pencil, muttering, *"The map is a promise the terrain has never signed."*

She missed him in the specific way you miss a tool that fit your hand. Not constantly. But at moments like this one, when the cloud deck lowered itself over the ridgeline like a lid finding its pot, her thumb went to the corner of the board where his old route notes used to live, and found only her own files, and the absence clicked shut like a buckle.

Behind them, Tomás's truck was singing. That was the only word for it — Tomás drove with the radio up and himself up higher, hollering some ballad about a woman who left him for a fisherman, and Idris, riding with him, was audibly learning the chorus. The sound carried up the switchbacks, bouncing off rock, and Priya caught herself humming it too, and hated it, and kept doing it.

Dominic drove in silence, eyes moving between the track and the sky with the same flat attention, giving neither priority. Once, without turning his head, he said, "Clouds are wrong."

"They're clouds," Priya said. "Wrongness is available upon request."

"They're building from the south. Monsoon pattern."

"It's October. The monsoon ended in September."

"Elias would have agreed with you." A beat. "Elias is also dead."

The words landed the way his words always landed now — carefully aimed, apologetic in trajectory, brutal on impact. Priya looked at the southern sky. The clouds were building from the south. Stacked high, organized, patient. Not storm clouds yet. Storm clouds *practicing*.

"Log it," Dominic said. "Every hour. If the pattern holds past noon, we reassess the schedule."

"Yes, boss."

"And Priya—" Now he glanced at her, and for a second the expedition leader fell away and it was just the tired man underneath, the one who owed money to people who smiled while collecting. "Thank you for coming. I know what you think of this route."

"What I think of this route," Priya said, "is that it's a bad idea executed competently, which puts it ahead of most expeditions I've worked."

He almost smiled. The truck climbed.

* * *

It was Idris who found the first marker.

They'd stopped at midday in a bowl of gray stone where a stream braided itself thin and cold, and while Tomás walked the axles and Halloran stood at the perimeter radiating vigilance, Idris scrambled up the talus slope the way young men scramble up things — pointlessly, joyfully — and then stopped scrambling.

"Hey," he called down. His voice had changed key. "Hey, uh. Sir? Priya?"

They climbed up to him. Wedged in a cleft of rock, lichened nearly to invisibility, was a steel stake with a faded pennant still knotted to it. Red. Torn to ribbons by years of wind, but red.

Route marker. Expedition standard. And stamped on the plate at its base, worn but legible: **V-9 SURVEY**.

V for Varga. Nine for the ninth field day of the last crossing either of them had made up this mountain — the crossing that had come down as a flood instead.

"That's not possible," Priya heard herself say, in the tone of a woman reading a number twice because the first reading was insulting.

"This is the east approach," Dominic said quietly. He crouched, touched the stake, rubbed the corrosion between finger and thumb. "Elias marked the west approach. We came up the valley. This is ten kilometers east of any line he ever walked."

"Could another crew have—"

"No other crew uses his code." He said it flatly, factually, the way you report a mechanical fault. But Priya had known Dominic eleven years, and she saw what the flatness was costing him. "I burned his route books myself. After. Everything. I didn't want anyone following him up here."

"Then whose stake is it?"

Nobody answered. Above them the clouds had thickened without seeming to move, the way ice forms on a pond — no motion, just a different world than the moment before.

Tomás, down at the trucks, called up cheerfully that lunch was ready, and the ordinary world resumed like a song picking up its chorus. They ate bread and tinned fish and drank tea from the big pot, and Idris told a story about a goat that had gotten into his grandmother's kitchen, and for twenty minutes the marker on the slope was just a thing they'd seen.

Then Halloran, walking the perimeter again, stopped.

"There's another one," he said. His voice was quiet and even, a voice designed to deliver information without delivering alarm, and somehow that made it worse.

The second marker was fifty meters north, driven into the bank of the dry streambed. Same stamp. Same red pennant, this one more intact, the cloth stiff with frost. And it was *newer*. The steel still shone where the driving face had bitten the rock. The lichen hadn't begun.

Priya crouched beside it and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Frost-stiffened cloth meant it had been here through at least a few cold nights. Weeks, maybe. Months.

Someone had been climbing Elias Varga's erased route — in order, in sequence, marking it as they went — sometime within the last year.

"Reconnaissance," Halloran offered, unhelpfully calm.

"For what?" Tomás asked. "Who surveys a dead man's route?"

"Maren," Idris said, and then went pink, as if he'd sworn in church. "I mean — she sent us up here. Maybe she scouted it first."

"Maren hasn't been on this mountain since the flood," Priya said. "She was in the valley. She watched it happen from the operations tent." She remembered that; everyone who'd been in the valley remembered it. Maren Voss standing in the rain outside the comms tent, soaked through, saying nothing to anyone for six hours.

Dominic hadn't spoken. He was looking up-slope, following the line the markers implied, toward the high country where Station Nine lay drowned beneath its lake of rock and meltwater. His face had gone very still, the stillness of a man listening for something under other sounds.

"Third one will be above the tree line," he said finally. "If we find a third one."

"And if we do?"

"Then somebody wanted us to follow this route." He turned back toward the trucks, and his voice was level and tired and absolutely certain. "And I want to know who, before we're high enough that it matters."

* * *

They made camp at dusk in a hollow below the snowline, engines blanketed, stove ticking. The southern clouds had continued their slow, organized work all afternoon, and now the stars were going out overhead one constellation at a time, smothered from the horizon inward.

Priya took the first watch, wrapped in two blankets, logging the barometer with numb fingers. Falling. Falling faster than the forecast said it should. In her notebook she wrote the numbers, and then, because the notebook was hers and no one else would read it, she wrote beneath them:

*The mountain has been waiting for us.*

She crossed it out immediately. Superstition was for people with fewer instruments.

But on her next round of the perimeter, her headlamp swept the talus above camp, and she stopped, and stood very still in the cold.

A third marker. Above the tree line, exactly where Dominic had said. Its pennant snapped softly in the rising wind, red as an open wound, pointing up the mountain — toward the station, toward the drowned vault, toward whatever it was that Maren Voss actually wanted out of the ice.

And below it, faint on the frost-hardened ground where no snow had yet fallen: boot prints. More than one set. Heading down.

Toward camp.

Priya stood in the wind a long moment, doing the arithmetic again, getting the same answer both times.

Then she went to wake Dominic.
