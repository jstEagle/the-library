## 4. Station Nine

The valley opened like a held breath.

We came around the shoulder of the ridge in the last grey light of afternoon, and there it was — the basin where the river had once been a river and was now a wide, sullen lake, brown-green and still as poured glass. Station Nine sat at its center like a drowned man's hand reaching up: the upper floor of the main block above water, roof caved at one corner, antenna mast bent into a question mark against the sky.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. The engines idled. Somewhere up-valley, water dripped off rock in a rhythm too slow to be rain.

"That's it," Maren said, from the passenger seat beside me. Not a question. She'd known exactly what we'd find, because she'd seen it before — or seen pictures of it, which amounted to the same thing from her.

"That's it," I agreed, and put the truck in gear, because if I sat still any longer I was going to think about what this place meant, and I had promised myself I'd do my thinking underwater, where nobody could see my face.

* * *

We made camp on the shelf of gravel above the shoreline, close enough that the lake lapped at our dreams all night. Tomás cooked something he insisted was stew and Priya insisted was "a war crime with potatoes," and Idris ate three bowls of it with the untroubled appetite of the very young, which is either a blessing or a warning sign, depending on how long you've known him.

The plan was simple, the way plans are before they meet reality. The vault — the sealed records room Elias had helped design, back when Station Nine was going to be the pride of the Kestrel weather network — lay below the old waterline. Flooded for six years. The cores were rated for submersion; that was the whole point of them, that data could outlive the building around it. We'd dive, cut the seal, pull the cores, come up. In and out before the weather turned.

"In and out," Priya repeated that night, watching me check my rig by lantern light. She had a way of repeating things that made them sound like questions even when they weren't. "Like a dentist appointment."

"Exactly like a dentist appointment."

"Dominic." She lowered her voice so Tomás wouldn't hear from the other truck. "You don't have to be the one who goes down."

"Yes, I do."

"You knew this station. You knew these people—"

"Elias designed that vault door," I said. "I'm the only one who knows how it opens without a cutting torch, and a torch down there risks cracking the seal on everything we came for. It's not sentiment, Priya. It's plumbing."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I could see her deciding whether to let me have the lie. She was kind enough to let me keep it, and honest enough to let me know she knew.

"Plumbing," she said. "Fine. But you take Halloran as your surface man, and you come up the second your gauge says so, or I will come down after you and drag you up by the collar, and I want you to know I've been practicing my grip on Tomás."

"Tomás is twice your size."

"Tomás is soft," she said fondly. "Like bread."

From across the camp, without looking up from his stew: "I heard that."

* * *

Morning came cold and colorless, the lake steaming faintly where the air touched it, like the whole basin was breathing. I suited up on the shore while Halloran checked my lines with the silent thoroughness of a man who did everything as though someone were grading him. He hadn't said forty words since the foothills. I'd started to think of him less as a person than as a piece of equipment that occasionally walked.

"Air's good," he said finally. "Line's good. Watch your depth on the stairwell — the flood maps show debris."

"Flood maps," I said. "Whose?"

He paused, just half a beat too long. "Maren's."

Maren stood apart, arms crossed, watching the water with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to. Whatever she was feeling about this place, she'd packed it away somewhere deep, the way she packed everything. I'd known her fifteen years and I could count on one hand the times I'd seen her actually surprised.

I went in.

* * *

The cold hit me like a slap from a dead friend. Six years of snowmelt sitting in a mountain basin does not warm up for anybody. I gave myself thirty seconds to hate it, then kicked down along the antenna mast, following the guide line Halloran paid out behind me.

Station Nine rose up out of the green gloom, and I will tell you honestly: I wasn't ready for it.

I'd expected ruin. What I got was preservation. The water had kept everything — the corridor windows still in their frames, a chair drifting gently near the ceiling it used to sit under, papers fanned across a desk like a hand of cards somebody had walked away from mid-game. Silt hung in the water like weather. A school of pale fish scattered from a doorway as I passed, offended at the intrusion.

The mess hall. I knew that doorway. I'd eaten breakfast in that room with Elias, the morning before the last run down-mountain, arguing about whether his coffee was technically soup. He'd said a man who took his coffee black enough didn't need soup. I'd told him that was the stupidest thing a brilliant man ever said to me. He'd laughed so hard he knocked his cup over.

His cup was still on the table. Or what was left of it.

I hung there in the cold water and let the grief come, because I'd learned the hard way that it comes anyway, and it's cheaper to pay it on your own schedule. Then I found the stairwell and went down.

The vault door was where Elias had left it, six years ago, in a building he'd sworn would stand a hundred years. He'd been almost right. The mechanism was a joke between us — a manual override hidden behind a panel that looked like a breaker box, because Elias trusted locks the way he trusted weather forecasts, which is to say not at all. My hands shook inside the gloves. Cold, I told myself. Just cold.

The panel came away. The wheel turned. The door swung open into darkness, and my lamp found the racks.

Twelve data cores, seated in their cradles like eggs in a nest. Sealed, intact, waiting. Six years at the bottom of a drowned valley, and they'd waited, because that's what Elias built them to do. *Data outlives us all,* he used to say. *That's either comforting or terrifying, and I've never decided which.*

I loaded the carry bag until my shoulders ached, and I took one last look around the vault — at the chalkboard still bolted to the wall, ghost-letters of some old equation bleeding through the silt film — and I went up toward the light.

* * *

They laid the cores out on a tarp in the weak afternoon sun, twelve dark cylinders in a row, and Tomás rigged the portable reader while Priya ran diagnostics and Idris hovered with the barely contained excitement of a boy at a magic show. Halloran stood watch at the perimeter, or pretended to. Maren watched me.

"Whatever's on them," she said quietly, "remember why we're here. Salvage. That's the job."

"Right," I said. "Salvage."

The first core held instrument records — barometric series, river gauges, snowpack telemetry. Dry stuff. Beautifully clean dry stuff; Elias's instruments had never missed a day. Tomás whistled at the resolution. Then Priya cross-loaded the second core, and the room went quiet in a way rooms shouldn't go quiet.

It was the forecast model.

Not a forecast — *the* forecast. Run twenty-six days before the flood. Complete. Validated. With confidence intervals so tight they might as well have been carved in stone. The model showed the meltwater surge, showed the saturation curve of the upper slopes, showed — in red, flagged, annotated in Elias's own shorthand — the exact week the Kestrel river towns would drown.

Twenty-six days. Twenty-six days in which every soul in those valleys could have walked out with their photograph albums and their grandmothers and their goats.

And then the annotations changed hands. Different initials. Official language, bureaucratic and bloodless, layered over Elias's urgent scrawl like ash over embers. *Advisory withheld pending economic impact review.* *Public warning deferred — downstream operations critical to regional output.* And beneath one entry, dated eleven days before the water came, a line that stopped my breath:

*Underhill expedition advised to proceed. Schedule maintained per agreement.*

Per agreement.

I read it four times. The words didn't change. That was the horror of it — I wanted them to change, wanted the silt and the water damage to have corrupted something, anything, but the core was pristine, because Elias had built it to be pristine, and the truth had survived six years underwater better than I had survived six years above it.

They'd sent us down. They'd read the model, they'd buried the warning, and then they'd sent Elias and his crew down through those valleys on schedule — because a convoy on the move was useful cover, because our permits were convenient, because dead storm chasers make a flood look like weather instead of what it was.

A decision.

My debt. Every coin I owed Calloway, every extension, every smile from every collector in every rain-soaked port town — it all traced back to the compensation settlements, the convoy insurance, the loans I'd taken to bury four people and keep the fifth truck running. Calloway money had caught me as I fell. And Calloway money, it turned out, had pushed me first.

"Dominic." Priya's voice, far away. "Dominic, sit down."

"I'm sitting."

"You're standing."

I looked down. I was standing. The ground seemed to have opinions about its own stability that I couldn't verify. Tomás took my elbow and steered me onto a crate, and I sat there holding the reader's printout like it might bite, while the vertigo slowly spun itself out.

"They knew," Idris said. He was young enough to say it plainly, no armor on it at all. "They knew, and they just — they let it happen? For *mines*?"

"For margins," Maren said. Her voice was flat, controlled, and I noticed — filed away without understanding yet — that she wasn't asking to see the printout. She wasn't coming closer. "Mining interests. Calloway-backed. Which is why those cores go in a locked case, and nobody radios ahead about what we found. Nobody. Is that understood?"

Halloran, at the perimeter, shifted his weight. Just slightly. Just enough.

I should have noticed. I want to tell you I noticed. But I was still reading that line — *schedule maintained per agreement* — and somewhere in the roaring in my ears, I heard Elias laughing about coffee, and I thought: *old friend, they used you as a schedule, and they used me as a receipt, and I've spent six years thanking them for the loan.*

Priya knelt in front of me and took the printout out of my hands, gently, the way you take something from someone who might crush it.

"We're leaving tomorrow," she said. "First light. Down the low pass, fast, before the weather."

Above the lake, over the western peaks, the clouds had begun to build — high, bright, innocent-looking towers that any child would call pretty and any storm chaser would call early.

"Agreed," said Maren.

And Halloran, at the edge of the firelight, reached up and checked his radio aerial, the way a careful man does, the way he'd done every night since the foothills.

Only this time, I saw his thumb rest on the transmit key a moment longer than checking an aerial requires.
