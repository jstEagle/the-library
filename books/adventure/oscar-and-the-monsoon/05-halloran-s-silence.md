## 5. Halloran's Silence

Idris had learned three things about Halloran in four days on the mountain, and he was proud of all of them.

One: the man drank his coffee black but always held the cup in both hands, like it might bolt. Two: he could sleep sitting up, eyes half-open, which Idris found less impressive and more disturbing the longer he thought about it. Three: he never talked. Not small talk, not big talk. When Tomás had asked him where he was from, Halloran had said "south," and Tomás had laughed for a full minute and then quietly stopped liking him.

So when Idris came around the tail of the second truck at dawn and saw Halloran standing apart from camp with the radio handset pressed to his ear, talking — actually *talking*, low and clipped, words coming out of that mouth like coins from a slot machine — every one of his three facts rearranged itself into something colder.

He stopped behind the truck's rear wheel. The engine block ticked as it cooled. Above them the Kestrel foothills went up and up into cloud, gray on gray, and somewhere up there Station Nine sat under its lake holding its dead.

Halloran said, "—no. No, they don't suspect. They're loading now." A pause. "I'll handle it."

Idris stood very still. He was twenty-three years old and the newest member of this crew by a margin so wide it wasn't really a crew yet, more of a loose association of people who happened to be walking in the same direction. He'd been hired because he could read terrain, because he was cheap, because Priya had vouched for him after one season of scouting together. He was not, by any measure, the person you wanted catching a fixer mid-transmission.

But nobody else was awake. And the handset was already going back onto its cradle, and Halloran was already turning, and there was nowhere for a twenty-three-year-old to hide behind a truck that was mostly windows.

"Morning," Idris said, too loudly.

Halloran looked at him. It was the same look he gave everything — patient, faintly amused, like a man watching weather. "Morning."

"Cold one."

"They're all cold ones up here."

"Yeah." Idris scratched the back of his neck. His heart was doing something embarrassing. "You, uh. You get through to anyone? Priya said the relay tower at Kestrel Gap was down since the storm."

Something moved behind Halloran's eyes. It was very small, and if Idris hadn't been staring directly into them it would have been nothing at all. "Static," Halloran said. "Mostly static."

"Right. Static." Idris nodded several times, an amount of nodding that would have been suspicious on its own even without everything else. "Well. Breakfast is on. Tomás made the eggs again, so, you know. Condolences."

Halloran almost smiled. He walked past Idris toward the fire, and Idris watched him go, and thought: *mostly static*. Nobody says *mostly* static. You say static, or you say I got through. *Mostly* is what you say when you're counting the seconds between what you did and what someone heard.

He didn't eat breakfast. He faked it, moving eggs around his tin plate while Tomás complained about the rear axle bearing on truck two and Priya studied her maps with the fierce concentration she used when she was worried and didn't want anyone to know. Maren Voss ate standing up, looking at the clouds. Dominic wasn't eating either; he was looking at the pass.

The low route ran along the river gorge — the old haul road, wide enough for the trucks, sheltered, the sensible way down to Station Nine's valley and back out before winter. Dominic had been quiet about it all morning in the way of a man standing next to a grave. Idris knew the story the way everyone in the trade knew it: the monsoon year, the flood, Elias Vance and four others gone in one night of brown water. This road had eaten them. Now they were driving back into its mouth for data cores, and everyone was pretending that was normal.

They broke camp at first light plus forty minutes, which was Priya's rule, and rolled down toward the gorge in convoy, Idris riding point in the scout rig with the window cracked so he could smell the air. That was his actual job, the part no instrument did well: smell. Rain coming. Snow coming. Rock coming. The mountains told you things if you kept your nose out the window like a dog.

By midmorning he smelled rock.

Not the clean mineral smell of ordinary scree. Something older, disturbed — dust and turned earth and the particular sourness of soil that has been recently asked to be somewhere else. He keyed the radio. "Dom. Pulling up. There's something wrong with the slope above the narrows."

Dominic's voice came back flat and immediate. "Define wrong."

"Fresh. Big. Come look."

They walked the last two hundred meters on foot, all five of them, because Dominic Underhill was not a man who sent other people around corners he wouldn't go around himself. The narrows were exactly that — the haul road pinched between the river below and a wall of broken granite above, maybe sixty meters of exposed slope. Except now most of those sixty meters were lying across the road.

Rock, dirt, shattered pine, a slab the size of a chapel. The river foamed white against the new dam of debris. Where the road continued on the far side, it disappeared under rubble for as far as they could see.

Tomás whistled, long and low. "That's a lot of mountain in the road."

"That's not runoff," Priya said. She was already climbing the near edge of the slide, boots finding holds, reading it the way Idris read air. "Look at the scar line. Look at the crown." She pointed up at the raw crescent where the slope had failed. "There's no water channel feeding it. No spring seep. The failure plane is clean."

"So it just fell," Maren said. "Mountains do that."

"Mountains do that after rain, or freeze-thaw, or an earthquake." Priya came back down, brushing grit off her hands. "It hasn't rained in six days. It hasn't frozen. And I felt no quake. This slope was cut."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The river made its noise against the rocks.

"Cut how?" Idris asked, because someone had to.

"Charges," Halloran said.

Everyone turned. Halloran stood at the edge of the slide with his hands in his pockets, calm as furniture. "Blasted at the crown, low yield, timed to look like natural failure. I've seen it done before. Mining companies clear roads this way when they want a detour." He shrugged. "I'm telling you what it looks like. You were all thinking it."

"We were all thinking it," Tomás agreed, "but we were thinking it *quietly*, like civilized people."

Dominic hadn't spoken. He was staring up at the scar, and Idris watched his face do something complicated — grief and arithmetic at the same time. Then he said, "The high route."

Priya's head came around. "Dom, no. The high route is Elias's shortcut. It's unmapped since the flood, it climbs above four thousand meters, and the snowline dropped five hundred meters overnight. I can see it from here." She gestured east, where the peaks wore their new white like a threat delivered early. "We'd be crossing the col in weather that hasn't decided yet whether it's autumn or January."

"The alternative is turning around," Dominic said. "Going home. Telling Calloway the road was closed." He let that sit. Everyone heard what lived inside it: telling Calloway anything at all, right now, with whatever Halloran had transmitted at dawn still traveling down the mountain at the speed of light.

Maren spoke carefully. "If someone closed this pass, they wanted us on the high route. Or they wanted us stopped. We should consider both."

"If someone closed this pass," Dominic said, "they wanted us slow. Stopped men are caught men. The high route is fast if you know it, and Elias mapped it, and I drove it once before the flood took the lower marks." He looked at each of them in turn, ending on Halloran, holding there a beat longer than politeness required. "We take the high route. We move today. And from here on, the radio stays cold except for scheduled check-ins, and every transmission goes through me. Company policy. Effective immediately."

"Whose company?" Tomás asked.

"Mine," Dominic said. "I'm the only one whose name is on the contract."

Idris caught Priya's eye across the rubble. She gave him a small nod — *good catch* — and he realized with a start that she'd seen his face at breakfast, read the whole thing off him the way he read the sky. He'd have to tell her properly later. He'd also have to tell her that he'd nodded like an idiot instead of saying anything useful, and hope she valued honesty over competence.

* * *

They spent two hours winching and cutting a track up out of the gorge, and it was Tomás who finally said it, hanging off the back of truck two with a chain wrapped around his fists.

"You all know it was him."

"Him who?" Maren said, though her voice said she knew.

"Our silent friend. Our man of few words and many frequencies." Tomás hauled the chain taut. "I liked him better when he didn't talk. Turns out he talks plenty, just not to us."

"He could've been checking in with Voss's office," Priya said. "Fixers report. It's normal."

"It's normal to report *to you*," Tomás said. "He waited until everyone was asleep and walked away from camp to do it. I only know because Idris told me, and Idris only knows because—" he pointed a wrench at Idris, "—you never sleep, which is a separate problem."

"I sleep," Idris said.

"You hum when you're awake. You were humming at three in the morning."

"That was a different night."

Dominic straightened up from the winch controls. His hands were steady; his voice wasn't quite. "Here's what I know. Someone at Calloway's end knew what was in those cores before we pulled them — Maren, you want to tell us how?" Maren opened her mouth and he shook his head. "Later. Fine. But someone knew, and someone told our fixer, and our fixer reported that we had them. And then a slope fell on the only easy way home." He looked up at the white line creeping down the eastern peaks. "I don't need a confession. I need everyone watching the road and each other. Both. At the same time. That's the job now."

"And Halloran?" Priya asked quietly.

"Halloran drives with me," Dominic said. "From now on, Halloran rides in my cab."

There was a silence while everyone absorbed what that meant — that Dominic intended to spend the next three days locked in a metal box with the man he suspected, rather than let him drift. It was either the bravest or the stupidest thing Idris had ever heard, and he was starting to understand those were often the same thing on this crew.

They gained the shoulder of the high route by late afternoon. Below them the gorge slid away, the rockslide a gray wound across the green, and ahead the trail climbed into country none of them but Dominic had ever seen — switchbacks stitched up a face of dark stone, cairns appearing at intervals like old friends waving from a distance. Elias's marks. Some were fresh, re-stacked, the stones fitted with a care that made Idris's throat hurt. Somebody had come up here recently and rebuilt the dead man's path.

"Somebody's been maintaining these," he said.

Dominic stopped the scout rig and got out and stood over the nearest cairn for a long time. He didn't touch it. "Elias used to say the mountain keeps what you give it," he said finally. "I always thought that meant the bad stuff."

Above them, the clouds had begun to organize — that purposeful stacking that Idris had learned to fear before he'd learned its name. He put his nose out the window. Cold iron. Snow, and soon, and a lot of it.

Behind them, far down the valley, so small it might have been a trick of the light, something glinted on the old haul road. Glass. Sunlight off windshields, moving.

Idris didn't say anything yet. He just started counting the hours until dark, and wondering how fast a convoy could climb when it finally understood it was being followed.
