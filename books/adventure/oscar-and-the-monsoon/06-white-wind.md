## 6. White Wind

The wind arrived before the snow did, the way bad news always does.

Priya felt it first as a pressure in her ears, a low hum in the truck's frame that she'd learned to read the way sailors read swells. She was navigating from the passenger seat of the lead rig, watching the altimeter creep past four thousand meters while the road — if you could call a shelf of scraped rock and old avalanche debris a road — narrowed to a ribbon with nothing on the left but air.

"Clouds are wrong," she said.

Dominic glanced up. "They're clouds. They're always wrong."

"These are *early*." She tapped the window. Above them the Kestrel peaks had vanished into a gray ceiling that hadn't been there an hour ago, and the ceiling was coming down like a lid on a pot. "Barometer's dropped eleven millibars since noon. That's not weather, Dom. That's a door slamming."

In the back seat, Idris pulled his jacket tighter and said nothing, which by now Priya understood to mean he was thinking about Halloran. Everyone was thinking about Halloran. The fixer rode in the second truck with Maren, silent as ballast, and every time Priya checked her mirrors she caught herself counting trucks the way you count fingers after an argument.

The radio crackled. Tomás, from the rear rig: "Axle's singing again. Left rear. Sounds like a cat in a blender."

"Can it make it to the col?" Dominic asked.

"Define 'col.'"

"A sheltered saddle. Three kilometers."

"Three kilometers of shelf road with a dying axle and a storm behind us." A pause, filled with the sound of Tomás doing math he didn't like. "It can make it if the axle agrees. Axles are notorious liars."

They made two kilometers.

* * *

The blizzard did not begin so much as arrive — one moment gray light, the next a white wall that erased the world beyond the windshield. Dominic eased the lead truck against the cut bank and threw the hazards on, and thirty seconds later Tomás's rig loomed out of the white and stopped close enough that Priya could see the fear on his face through two layers of falling snow.

The axle did not agree.

Tomás stood in the howl with his hood cinched to a slit, shouting things Priya couldn't hear over the wind, gesturing at the left rear wheel where something had sheared and hung at an angle no axle should hang. The bearing housing, probably. Maybe the hub. In a garage it was a three-hour job. Here it was a question of whether any of them would be alive when the answer mattered.

"We can't stay on the shelf," Priya said inside the cab, both hands flat on the dash as if she could hold the mountain still. "If this snow stacks all night, we're buried. If the wind shifts, we're off the edge. We have to fix it now."

Dominic looked at her, and she saw him do the thing he did — the arithmetic of grief, weighing lives against hours. "Then we fix it now."

What followed was the worst hour of Priya's life, and she had once spent a night in a flooded culvert listening to water rise.

They backed the second truck upwind to make a wall, rigged tarps between it and the dead wheel, and Tomás crawled under with heat packs taped to his chest and a headlamp that turned the tarp-shadows into a flickering cave. Idris held the light steady because Tomás's hands shook too badly to hold anything. Priya fed tools and called out wind gusts like a spotter calling artillery. And Dominic lay down in the snow beside the wheel housing, shoulder to shoulder with his mechanic, passing parts through a gap no wider than a coffin lid.

The cold found everything. It crept through boot soles, into knuckles, behind eyes. Twice the wind peeled a corner of the tarp and filled the work space with spinning white, and twice they wrestled it closed while Tomás swore with a creativity that would have been funny anywhere else on earth.

"Talk to me," Tomás said at one point, voice slurred, and Priya realized with a lurch that slurred was new. "Keep me here."

"You're here," she said. "You're fixing an axle in a blizzard because you're the only person alive stubborn enough."

"Flattery," he mumbled. "Works. Keep going."

"Your hands are going purple, you ridiculous man."

"Purple's my color."

By the time the last bolt seated, Tomás couldn't feel his fingers and said so cheerfully, twice, which frightened Priya more than screaming would have. They hauled him into the lead cab, stripped his wet gloves, and packed his hands against skin under armpits while he laughed at them — a thin, breathless laugh, the laugh of a man whose body was spending its last warmth on jokes.

"Don't you die on me," Priya told him. "I refuse to navigate home without someone to complain about my route choices."

"Noted," he whispered. "Complaints... pending."

* * *

Maren came to them in the worst of it.

Priya saw her first as a shape fighting through the white between the trucks, bent double, one hand on the lead rig's mirror for balance. When she hauled open the cab door the wind came in with her, a fistful of ice, and for a moment nobody spoke. Halloran stayed in the second truck. Of course he did.

"How is he?" Maren asked.

"He'll live if his hands do," Priya said. "Why are you out here? This is suicide weather."

Maren crouched by Tomás, touched his forehead with the back of her glove, and then sat down hard on the floor mat, and Priya watched something happen in the woman's face that she had never seen there: the collapse of a performance. Maren Voss, who negotiated like a scalpel, who never raised her voice because she never needed to, looked suddenly like a tired woman in her fifties sitting on a truck floor in a blizzard.

"I need to tell you all something," she said, "before anyone else dies for my sake."

Idris leaned in from the sleeper berth, snow melting in his hair. Dominic stood in the doorway, letting the cold in, not caring.

"The contract was real," Maren said. "The salvage was real. Station Nine, the cores, all of it — I needed those instruments brought down. But I wasn't hired to recover them." Her breath fogged and hung between them. "I was hired to make sure nothing came back down the mountain. The recovery was my cover. My instruction was to see the data destroyed above the snowline and report the convoy lost. Weather. Rockslide. It writes itself up here; Calloway has done it before."

The wind screamed against the glass, filling the silence, and Priya heard her own heartbeat in her ears.

"You've known since the port," Dominic said quietly. Not a question.

"I've known since before the port." Maren met his eyes and didn't flinch away, though Priya could see it cost her. "I signed the suppression order eight years ago. My company held the mining leases. The forecast crossed my desk six weeks before the flood — six weeks, Dominic — and I buried it because pulling the crews would have bankrupted us, and I told myself the models were uncertain, and men I will never stop seeing drowned in towns I have driven through a hundred times since." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "Calloway knew what I was. They hired me precisely because I'd bury it again — or so they thought. I took the contract because it was the only way back up this mountain. It was supposed to be my penance. Instead I brought four more people into it."

Tomás, half-frozen on the bunk, started laughing. Thin, wheezing, unstoppable.

"What," Maren said.

"You," he gasped. "You climbed through a *blizzard* to confess. To people holding your contract in their heads. That's either the bravest or stupidest—" He coughed. "Both. Definitely both."

"It's neither," Priya heard herself say, and was surprised by the steadiness of her own voice. "It's arithmetic. She just changed the numbers."

Everyone looked at her.

"Think about it," Priya said. "She could have let the mountain do it. One more day of weather and the convoy's a news brief — 'four lost attempting the Kestrel crossing,' very sad, very common. Instead she walked through a whiteout to hand us the truth while we were all too cold and too scared to lie to each other. That's not a confession. That's a defection. She's telling us Calloway expects wreckage, and wreckage is the one thing we can still choose not to be."

Maren stared at her. "You're navigating for my soul now?"

"Somebody has to. Dom's busy."

Dominic almost smiled. It was a terrible, exhausted almost-smile, gone in a second, but Priya catalogued it the way she catalogued breaks in cloud — evidence that somewhere above the ceiling there was still blue.

"Halloran knows you're here," Idris said from the berth. Flat. Certain.

"Halloran has known everything since the station," Maren said. "He'll have reported the delay. Which means Calloway's second team isn't waiting for spring. They're already climbing."

Priya looked out at the white fury and felt something settle in her chest — not fear, exactly, and not calm either, but the strange weightless quiet that comes when every safe option burns away and what's left is simply the truth and the road. They had no radio worth trusting, a half-dead mechanic, a defected employer, and data cores that eight years of powerful men wanted dead.

And tomorrow, if the wind gave them even one clean hour, they would have a mountain to cross.

"Get some sleep," Dominic said to the cab at large, in the voice he used when retreat had stopped being a word. "All of you. First break in this sky, we move."

Priya kept the watch. Somewhere past midnight the wind shifted, and through a tear in the clouds she saw stars — cold, indifferent, magnificent — and below them, far down the valley they'd climbed from, a chain of lights moving slowly upward.

Headlights. Too many, and gaining.
