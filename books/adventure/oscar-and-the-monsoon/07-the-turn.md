## 7. The Turn

The storm gave them six hours of mercy, and Dominic spent four of them pretending to sleep.

He lay in the cab of the lead truck with his boots on and his jacket zipped, listening to the wind work at the canvas of the camp shelter like a patient thief. Priya had rigged the convoy in a horseshoe against a ridge of blue ice, engines nose-in, and Tomás had wrapped the data cores in a thermal blanket and stowed them under his own bunk, which was the kind of gesture that meant more than any speech. Idris was on first watch. Halloran had volunteered for second.

That should have been the first warning. Halloran never volunteered for anything.

Dominic drifted anyway — you learned to sleep in fragments on a mountain, the way a sailor sleeps in weather — and when he surfaced it was past midnight and the camp was wrong. Too quiet. The generator was still running, but there was no scrape of Idris's boots on the ice, no low murmur of the kid talking to himself the way he did on watch because silence made him jumpy.

He unzipped the cab and stepped down into cold that hit like a slap.

Idris was standing at the edge of the firelight with his hands raised, palms out, in the universal language of *don't do anything stupid*. Ten paces from him, at the mouth of the supply truck, stood Halloran with a pry bar in one hand and Tomás's thermal bundle under his arm, and between them lay the reason nobody had spoken: the crevasse.

It hadn't been there yesterday. The glacier shrugged and shifted in its sleep, and now a seam of black opened in the ice not twenty meters from the trucks, breathing up cold air like something alive. The bridge plates they'd laid over it for hauling gear sat skewed at the edge, half-fallen in.

"Halloran," Dominic said, keeping his voice level the way you'd keep your hand flat near a dog you didn't know. "Put it down."

Halloran didn't turn around. In the firelight his face looked carved out of something older than the man wearing it. "You don't understand what's coming up that road, Underhill."

"Then tell me."

"They're not collectors." Halloran's voice was quiet, almost gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Calloway doesn't send collectors above the snowline. They sent me to make sure the mountain kept what it took. And if the mountain won't keep it—" he lifted the bundle slightly, "—I will."

"You were going to drop them in the ice." Idris's voice cracked on it. He was nineteen and trying very hard to be a man about it. "You were going to walk past me while I slept."

"I was going to walk past you while you froze," Halloran said. "There's a difference. It's kinder."

Dominic moved without deciding to move — three steps, closing the angle, watching Halloran's weight shift onto his back foot. "Elias Varga taught me everything I know about reading weather," he said. "Four other people died with him because men like Calloway read the same forecast we just pulled out of Station Nine and decided a river valley full of people was cheaper than a mine delay. You want to hand those cores back to them? Then you'll have to go through the part where I stop being reasonable."

Something flickered across Halloran's face — not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing his own orders reflected back at him from the wrong side.

He swung the pry bar anyway.

It was ugly and short. Dominic caught the bar on his forearm and felt the bone sing, got a grip on Halloran's collar, and then the ice did what ice does, which is betray everyone equally. Halloran's boot found the skewed bridge plate. It went out from under him with a sound like a struck bell, and for one suspended second he was airborne — bundle flying free, arms wheeling, eyes finding Dominic's with an expression that wasn't anger at all but apology, or maybe just arithmetic — and then the crevasse took him.

The dark swallowed the sound before it finished.

* * *

They roped up and went down after him, all four of them, because that's what crews did, even for men who'd tried to kill them. That was the rule and Dominic held to it with both hands while the wind came back and the temperature fell through the floor.

They found him forty feet down, wedged in a shelf of ice, alive but wrong — leg folded at an angle legs don't fold, breath fogging shallow and fast. He was conscious. He looked up at the circle of headlamps leaning over the lip of his tomb and said, clearly, "Don't waste the rope."

"Shut up," said Tomás, who never swore at anyone, and started rigging the haul system.

Getting him up took two hours and cost them the radio — the long-range set, lashed to the haul line when the anchor shifted, gone into the black with a splash of static that sounded almost like laughter. When they finally laid Halloran on a stretcher bed in the supply truck, splinted and gray and shivering, he caught Dominic's sleeve with fingers too weak to mean it.

"The second team," he rasped. "Left base camp two days ago. Six men, snowcats, armed. They knew your route before you did, Underhill. I'm the slow version. They're the fast one."

"How far behind?"

"At this pace?" Halloran closed his eyes. "By morning, close enough to see our exhaust."

Priya appeared at Dominic's elbow, her face lit blue by the map screen, and she didn't bother with the question because they'd already run the numbers together in their heads. Retreat downvalley meant meeting the team head-on on a road built for snowcats. Hold here meant a siege they couldn't win and couldn't fuel. Which left exactly one direction, and it pointed up.

"The high col," she said. "Elias's route."

"It's not a route," Tomás said. "It's a rumor Elias drew on a napkin after his third beer."

"It's a rumor with a sixty percent grade and a cornice the size of a cathedral," Priya agreed. "And it's the only way over the range before the passes close."

Dominic looked at the cores, rewrapped now in two blankets and strapped inside the cab of the lead truck where someone would have to go through him to reach them. He looked at Halloran, unconscious or pretending to be, breathing in the back of a truck he'd tried to empty. He looked at Idris, who had stopped shaking somewhere in the last hour and whose face had settled into something new — not fear, not bravado, just a young man discovering the exact weight of the thing he was carrying.

"We can't carry the cores and Halloran and enough fuel over that grade," Priya said quietly. Not cruelly. Just honestly, because she was Priya and honesty was her religion. "We have to choose."

"No," Dominic said. "We split the load. Cores stay with me. Fuel and the light trailer go with you. We rope the trucks in pairs on the steep sections and winch where we have to. And Halloran rides with us, because we are not the people who leave people in crevasses, and I refuse — *refuse* — to let Calloway decide what kind of man I die as."

Nobody argued. That was the strange thing, he thought later. Nobody argued.

* * *

They broke camp at 4 a.m., in the hour the old-timers called the wolf's hour, when the cold is honest and nothing pretends. Tomás drove point with Idris beside him, navigating by memory and prayer. Priya ran sweep. Dominic took the middle with the cores under his seat and Halloran's shallow breathing in the mirror, and behind them, far below on the glacier road, headlights moved through the dark like a slow procession of lanterns — six of them, patient as tide.

"There," Idris said over the radio, though every radio in the convoy could see it plain enough.

"Yeah," Dominic said. "I see them."

He thought about Elias then, the way he always did at altitude — not the drowning, which he kept locked in its box, but the living man: Elias standing on a ridge with his coat flapping, laughing at the sky, saying *the mountain doesn't hate you, Underhill, it just doesn't know you're there.* Well. The mountain knew them now. And so did the men coming up behind with Calloway's money in their pockets and Calloway's silence in their orders.

Retreat was gone. Bargaining was gone. Every version of this story except the one where they crossed had been taken off the table, and Dominic found — to his genuine surprise — that what he felt wasn't fear.

It was relief. Terrible, clarifying relief. A man with no exit finally stops looking over his shoulder and starts looking at the road.

"Priya," he keyed. "When we hit the col, I want options. Real ones."

There was a pause, and then her voice came back, dry as the wind off the ice field. "Dominic Underhill asking for options instead of giving orders. Mark the date. Tomás, write it on the axle."

"Copy," said Tomás. "Inscribing it now. It'll outlast us all."

Ahead, the col showed itself for the first time — a notch of bruised sky between two walls of black rock, white wind streaming off its crest like smoke from a burning house. Behind them, the headlights climbed.

Dominic put the truck in gear and drove at the gap, and the whole convoy followed him into the wind.
