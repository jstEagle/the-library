## 8. Elias's Route

Maren Voss had spent thirty years learning to read rooms — boardrooms, hearing rooms, the quiet anterooms where men decided other men's futures. She had never learned to read weather, and now it was the only thing left worth reading.

The sky above the Kestrel Range had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Below it, the convoy crawled along a shelf of scree that Elias Kade had once called a shortcut and everyone else had called suicide.

She rode in the second truck, beside Idris, who drove the way he did everything: carefully, as if the world might apologize to him later for its roughness. Through the windshield she watched Dominic's truck lead them up a slope no map admitted existed, following markers that shouldn't have survived.

There they were again. Little cairns of stacked stone, each one topped with a scrap of faded orange fabric. Elias's route. Elias's flags. Elias, dead eleven years, still navigating them home like a lighthouse that refused to stop burning out of spite.

"You knew him well," Idris said. Not a question. The boy had a way of asking questions without asking them that she suspected he'd learned from her.

"I hired him," Maren said. "Twice."

"And?"

"And both times he was right, and both times I wished he hadn't been."

Idris glanced at her, then back at the road. "That doesn't sound like wishing."

No, she thought. It didn't.

* * *

They stopped at noon in the lee of a granite shoulder, engines ticking, wind worrying at the tarps. Tomás was under the lead truck making noises of grievance and affection in equal measure — the man spoke to axles more tenderly than he'd ever spoken to a woman, Priya said. Priya herself stood at the edge of the shelf with her binoculars, watching the pass behind them, where nothing moved yet but which all of them, without saying so, believed was not empty.

Maren climbed to Dominic's truck. He sat in the driver's seat with the door open, studying Elias's old field notebook — recovered from Station Nine, water-stained, its margins dense with his handwriting. Watching Dominic hold it was like watching a man hold a live wire and pretend it was a pencil.

"That spur," Dominic said, tapping the page. "He marked it *viable in dry season only*. It's October."

"He also wrote *Dominic will argue*, so plan accordingly," Maren said. "I'm told you argue."

"I'm told correctly." He almost smiled. It was the closest either of them had come to warmth in eleven years, and it lasted about as long as a match in this wind.

"Maren." He closed the notebook. His voice dropped into something flat and careful. "Halloran reported to you. Before. So tell me what you were actually hired to do, because I've stopped believing in salvage."

The moment arrived the way such moments always do — not announced, not dramatic, just suddenly there, standing in the doorway of your life with wet boots.

She looked past him at the range. At the river valley far below, invisible from here but present in every one of them, the way a scar is present under a sleeve.

"I wasn't hired to recover the data," she said. "I was hired to make sure it never came down the mountain. That's why Calloway paid me. That's why Halloran is mine. And that's why I'm going to tell you something I should have told you eleven years ago, and you're going to want to throw me off this shelf, and I'd ask you to wait until after the crossing, because I'm apparently useful."

Dominic said nothing. Priya had drifted closer without seeming to move; Tomás emerged from under the truck wiping his hands; even Idris appeared, silent as weather. They formed a half-circle around her the way people form around a fire, or an accident.

"The forecasts were real," Maren said. "Station Nine modeled the monsoon six weeks early. Six weeks. There was time to evacuate the river towns. There was time to reinforce the levees. There was time." Her voice did something she hated — it thinned. Thirty years of boardroom composure, and it *thinned*. "The mining consortiums upstream — the ones whose permits my company processed, whose bonds we underwrote — an evacuation order would have shut the valley for a season. Maybe two. The cost projections came to my desk, and I signed the suppression order, and I told myself it was a judgment call. A hard one. The kind executives are paid to make."

"Four people died," Priya said quietly. "Plus Elias. Five."

"Two hundred and six," Maren said. "In the towns. I've read the names. All of them. For eleven years, I've read the names." She let that sit. Nobody softened. She hadn't earned softening. "Calloway financed the suppression. My signature made it legal enough to survive an audit. When Station Nine's cores surfaced on a salvage manifest last spring, he didn't call a lawyer. He called me. Because I'm the person who buries things. It's what I'm *for*."

Tomás spat, deliberately, into the scree. It was, Maren thought, the most honest review she'd received in a decade.

"So the blizzard," Dominic said slowly. "The rockslide. Halloran's radio calls. You weren't guiding us."

"No. I was shepherding you somewhere the mountain could finish the job politely. An accident at altitude. Very sad. Very unfalsifiable." She met his eyes. "And then I got up here, and I saw Elias's cairns, and I watched you hold his notebook like it was a hand you were afraid to let go of, and I found that I could not do it. So yes, Dominic. I betrayed you twice. Once eleven years ago, when I helped kill your mentor and your crew and two hundred strangers. And once this month, when I agreed to try to kill you. You may weigh them however you like."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere above, ice cracked off a face and fell a long way.

"Why confess now?" Priya asked. Not cruelly. Curiously, the way she seemed to do everything — even suspicion conducted with good manners.

"Because tomorrow one of us might die, and I refuse to die a liar," Maren said. "Also because I'm sixty-one years old and I've discovered that penance isn't a feeling. It's a route. You walk it or you don't. This is me walking it."

"And what do you want?" Dominic asked. "Absolution?"

"No," she said. "I know better than to shop for things that aren't for sale."

* * *

They took Elias's spur in the afternoon, because there was no longer any reason to be careful with their lives and every reason to be quick.

It was, Maren had to admit, exactly the kind of route Elias would love — a goat track with ambitions, switchbacking up a rib of black stone, narrow enough that Tomás drove with his mirrors folded and his eyes shut for portions of it ("I don't shut my eyes," he insisted afterward. "I *narrow* them"). The cairns came faster here, Elias's orange scraps bright against the gray, and Maren understood suddenly that he had built them not for navigation but for company. Every marker was a small argument against being forgotten.

She rode with Idris again. The boy drove. She talked, which surprised her.

"He used to keep sweets in the glovebox," she said. "For children in the villages we passed through. He'd toss them out the window like a man feeding gulls. Drove the accountants mad — unreceipted expenditures, line item: *confectionery, miscellaneous*."

Idris smiled at the road. "What was he like? Really."

"Stubborn. Cheerful in a way that felt like a rebuke. He once told a minister of transport to his face that his infrastructure plan was 'a eulogy with a budget.'" She huffed something that in a better life would have been a laugh. "I admired him enormously. That's the part I can't forgive myself for. Not that I ignored him. That I *admired* him, and signed anyway, and slept fine. I slept *fine*, Idris. Do you understand what that means? It means the capacity was always there. The flood didn't make me capable of it. It revealed it."

Idris was quiet for a long stretch of switchbacks. Then: "Priya says guilt is just grief pointed inward. She says you get to pick which direction it points."

"Your navigator is wiser than the entire regulatory apparatus of two nations."

"She knows. We remind her daily."

At the top of the spur the route joined a high bench below the col, and there — absurd, impossible, perfect — stood one final cairn, taller than the rest, topped not with orange cloth but with a strip of reflective tape that caught the low sun and threw it back at them like a signal.

Dominic got out and stood before it. He didn't touch it. He stood the way people stand at graves they've argued with for years.

"He mapped this whole route," Maren said, coming up beside him. "Before the flood. He filed it with the regional survey office. It was rejected — insufficient commercial value." She paused. "I signed that rejection too. It's possible everything I've done in my life is a signature."

Dominic finally turned to look at her, and his face held no absolution, and she was grateful for it. Absolution would have been a theft — one more thing taken from the dead.

"Elias used to say the mountain keeps honest books," he said. "Everything entered, everything balanced, eventually." He nodded back down the valley, toward the pass behind them, where — faint, patient, unmistakable — headlights had appeared. Three sets. Moving. "Looks like the audit's here."

Below them, the snowline had begun to fall.
