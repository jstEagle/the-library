## 9. The Col Before Snowfall

The storm found us at the col the way an old creditor finds a man who's changed his name — patiently, and from every direction at once.

We'd been climbing since before dawn, six bodies and four vehicles' worth of salvage lashed onto two trucks that Tomás had coaxed across terrain no engineer would forgive. The wind came off the Kestrel icefield in long white breaths, erasing our tracks behind us as if the mountain were tidying up after guests it didn't intend to let leave. Priya drove lead. I rode shotgun with the data cores in a padded case between my boots, and every time the truck lurched I put my hand on that case like a man checking his own pulse.

"Col's ahead," Priya said. "Half a klick."

"And behind us?"

She checked the mirror. The mirror showed nothing but white, which was either very good news or very bad news, depending on your theology.

Behind us — maybe forty minutes back, maybe less — was Calloway's second team. Six men in two machines built for exactly this kind of weather, machines that hadn't spent three weeks being drowned, rockslid, frozen, and repaired with wire and stubbornness. Halloran had radioed them before Idris stopped him, which meant they knew where we were going, what we carried, and roughly how fast we could move. Which meant they'd be waiting.

"They'll be at the col," I said.

"You don't know that," Tomás said from the back seat, where he sat cradling a thermos like a firstborn child.

"I know Calloway," I said. "Calloway doesn't chase. Calloway waits where you have to arrive."

There was a silence in the cab, the particular silence of people doing arithmetic they don't like. Then Tomás said, "Well. On the bright side, if they kill us, we die owing them money, and there's a certain poetry in that."

"That's not the bright side," Priya said.

"It's *a* bright side."

* * *

The col itself was a saddle of gray scree between two shoulders of black rock, maybe two hundred meters wide, with the glacier falling away to the north and a slope of wind-loaded snow rising to the south — a big, beautiful, treacherous slab of it, the kind of slope every mountaineer looks at and thinks *avalanche* and then politely doesn't say out loud. We came over the last rise and there they were: two vehicles parked broadside across the narrowest point, engines idling, headlights cutting cones through the snowfall. Six figures in white parkas standing between them, rifles slung, casual as men waiting for a ferry.

Maren's truck pulled up beside ours. She stepped out into the wind without hurrying, which I had learned was her way of lying about how afraid she was.

"Dominic Underhill," called the man in front, and even through the wind I recognized the voice — Verrone, who'd sat across from me in a warm office two years ago while his colleagues explained, with great kindness, the terms of my debt. He'd smiled the whole time. He was smiling now, or trying to; the storm kept blowing his face sideways. "You've made this very dramatic. Nobody wanted drama."

"Nobody ever does," I said. "That's how you can tell it's coming."

"We just want the cores. Hand them over, walk down the south side, and everyone goes home. Calloway will even call it even on your account." He spread his hands. "A clean slate, Dominic. Think of it."

And here is the thing I want to set down honestly, because everything else in this story has been honest whether I liked it or not: for one full second, I thought about it. A clean slate. No more collectors on my dock, no more interest compounding like frost, no more smiling men. Elias dead four years and the world offering to pretend the whole ledger balanced.

Then I looked at the case between my boots. At Priya's hands steady on the wheel. At Maren standing in the wind with her chin up and her guilt showing through her parka like a brand through cloth. And I understood that a clean slate wasn't forgiveness. It was just another way of being erased.

"The flood killed eleven people," I said. "They knew it was coming for six weeks. Your bosses read the forecast and buried it because the water might inconvenience a mine."

Verrone's smile flickered. "Old news."

"Not to the river towns."

"Dominic." He sighed, patient, reasonable, the voice of every man who'd ever explained terms to me. "You're standing in a storm holding a box. That's all any of this is. Hand over the box."

I turned my head slightly, the way you do when you're addressing someone beside you, and said, quietly, "Priya?"

"I see it," she said.

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about Priya Chandrasekaran, my navigator, who has saved my life more times than I've saved hers and keeps a running tally she pretends doesn't exist: she had been looking at that southern slope since the moment we crested the rise. Not at the guns. At the snow. Because Priya reads terrain the way Tomás reads engines and I read contracts — looking for the trap, or the out, or both.

Wind-loaded slab, thirty degrees, two hundred meters above the col, crowned with a cornice the storm had been building all day. Below it, Calloway's two vehicles, parked broadside, engines warming the snow beneath them. Everything about the setup was wrong in the way that makes a chaser's spine hum.

"You're sure?" I asked her, under the wind.

"I've run the numbers twice," she said. "It comes down clean. It stops at the scree line. It buries their trucks to the roofline and it doesn't touch a single one of those idiots, because the slide fans east and they're standing west." A pause. "Probably."

"*Probably?*"

"Dominic, I'm navigating a blizzard with a hand level and a grudge. Everything today is probably." But her hands were already moving — pulling the charge kit from under her seat, the seismic charges Tomás had insisted on bringing for road-clearing and which Tomás now handed forward with the expression of a man passing his child to a stranger. "Two charges, cornice line, sixty meters apart. I place them, we back off, I fire on your word."

"On my word," I said. My mouth was dry. Fury had been living in my chest for days now, ever since Station Nine, ever since the cores told me what I'd already known in my bones — and fury is a useless thing in a storm. But watch what happens to fury when you give it a slope angle and a fuse length. Watch it become arithmetic. Watch it become *aim*.

Maren had come up alongside us. She looked at the slope, then at the men below, and I saw her do her own arithmetic — the kind with years in it instead of seconds.

"Do it," she said.

I raised my hand. Verrone saw the gesture and misread it completely, bless him forever. "Smart man," he called. "Knew you'd come around—"

"Priya," I said. "Now."

* * *

Later, Tomás would describe the sound as God clearing His throat. Idris, who never exaggerated anything, described it accurately: a flat double crack, like the mountain snapping its fingers, and then a sound underneath the sound, vast and low, the whole slope exhaling.

The cornice went first, a curling lip of white detaching into the air, and then the slab followed it, and then the mountainside itself was moving — a white tide two hundred meters wide, silent at this distance, almost gentle-looking, the way a flood is gentle-looking from high ground. The Calloway men had perhaps four seconds to understand what was happening. Four seconds to stop being an ambush and start being six men sprinting west, away from their vehicles, away from the fan, arms windmilling, dignity abandoned entirely.

The slide took the trucks the way a wave takes sandcastles. One moment they were there, headlights blazing, defiant; the next they were white humps, then white ripples, then simply part of the slope, buried under three meters of settled snow with their radios still faintly transmitting panic to nobody. The slide ran out at the scree line exactly as Priya promised, sighing to a halt fifty meters short of the nearest man, who stood knee-deep in spill-over powder, hatless, rifleless, staring at the place where his ride home used to be.

Nobody died. I checked. I made Priya check twice through the glasses, and I'd be lying if I said some small, ugly part of me hadn't wondered, briefly, whether I'd mind. I did mind. That mattered too. Elias would have minded. You don't beat men like Calloway by becoming the thing they send.

Verrone stood in the settling snow and looked up at our trucks, and I looked down at him, and for once neither of us smiled.

"Tell them," I called through the wind. "Tell Calloway the mountain voted."

* * *

We crossed the col while the pursuers were still digging for their radios, and by the time the last truck cleared the saddle, the storm had closed the pass behind us — properly closed it, the way the Kestrel closes things, with drifts ten meters deep and cornices collapsing under their own weight. Whatever came next, it wouldn't come from behind. The route that had killed Elias, that had nearly killed Tomás, that had taken everything from all of us, sealed itself shut like a book whose last page had finally been read.

Tomás laughed about it, actually — a wild, giddy, oxygen-starved laugh, hanging half out of the cab to watch the pass disappear. "It's keeping them!" he shouted. "The mountain's keeping them! Best security system I ever worked with, and I only had to feed it two charges!"

Priya drove with both hands and a small, fierce smile. Maren slept in the back for the first time in nine chapters of not sleeping, which is its own confession. Idris watched our six with the patience of a lighthouse.

And I sat with the case between my boots and felt the strangest thing, which was lightness. The debt was still there — every figure, every smiling collector, every term I'd signed in a rain-soaked port town because pride and desperation make poor accountants. None of that had gone anywhere. But somewhere on that slope, watching Calloway's machinery vanish under white, I had stopped being a man paying what he owed and started being a man carrying what he owed *forward*. There's a difference. The difference weighs less.

Downvalley, past the tree line, past the last of the storm, the river towns were waiting — the towns that had buried their dead without ever being told why the water came. The cores were still cold in their case. The truth was still in them, patient as snowpack.

All we had to do now was carry it down.
