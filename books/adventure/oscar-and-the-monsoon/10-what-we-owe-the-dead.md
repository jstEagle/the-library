## 10. What We Owe the Dead

The river towns smelled like woodsmoke and wet wool, and for the first time in six weeks nobody in the convoy was arguing about tire pressure.

Priya noticed the silence first. Six weeks of Tomás's running commentary on the state of his axles — a commentary that had escalated, somewhere above the snowline, into full theological grievance — and now nothing but the tick of cooling engines and the river going about its business below the levee road. She'd forgotten what quiet sounded like when it wasn't the kind that came before something falling on you.

"Your truck's leaking," she told Tomás anyway, because silence made her nervous.

"That's not a leak," Tomás said, without opening his eyes. He was sprawled across the hood of Number Three like a man sunbathing in November. "That's the truck crying with relief. Let it."

Idris was down at the water's edge skipping stones, badly, which was somehow worse than if he'd been good at it. Halloran's seat in the second truck was empty and would stay empty; they'd lowered what they could recover of him into the mountain two days ago, and none of them had said much, because there was nothing to say about a man who had chosen the wrong side of a crevasse. Maren Voss stood apart from all of it at the tailgate, arms folded, watching the town the way you watch a jury.

And Dominic — Dominic was sitting on the levee wall with the data cores in a canvas duffel between his boots, looking like a man who had carried something heavy up a mountain and then discovered the real weight only came off when you set it down in front of other people.

"You're stalling," Priya said, sitting beside him.

"I'm thinking."

"You've been thinking since the col. You thought the whole way down Elias's route. You thought through the ford at Miller's Crossing while Idris nearly drowned us in four feet of water." She nudged the duffel with her elbow. "That's not thinking. That's hiding in plain sight."

Dominic huffed something that wanted to be a laugh. Downriver, the town of Ashford spread itself along the banks — rebuilt, mostly, in the seven years since the flood, but rebuilt the way people rebuild after grief: carefully, with the new houses set back from the water like they were giving it room to apologize.

"There's a lawyer in Corvane," he said. "There's a press office in Corvane. There are three judges who owe Calloway favors and one who doesn't, and I couldn't tell you which is which." He turned a core over in his hands, its casing scarred from the vault, from the cold, from Halloran's boot. "Seven years I assumed somebody upriver just didn't know. Bad instruments, bad luck, an act of God with bad timing. And it was a spreadsheet. It was a *decision*, Priya. Somebody sat in a warm office and decided these towns were worth less than a mine lease." His jaw worked. "I keep thinking there's a right door to knock on. A proper one. With paperwork."

"And?"

"And there isn't." He looked out at the water. "Elias used to say the river keeps its own ledger. I thought he meant it as poetry. He meant it as instructions."

* * *

They found the assembly hall by following the sound of an argument, which Priya maintained was the most reliable navigation method left in the world.

It was a low stone building near the church, the kind of place that had clearly been a granary once and a hundred other things since. Inside: forty-odd chairs arranged in a lopsided semicircle, a stove ticking away, and a woman in her sixties standing at the front with a sheaf of papers, mid-sentence about levee funding, addressing a room that was listening with the particular patience of people who had heard every possible version of bad news and ranked it accordingly.

She stopped when the five of them filed in, mud to the knees, frost still in their collars, one of them carrying a duffel bag like it contained ordnance.

"We're closed till Thursday," someone called from the back.

"We're not here about the levee fund," Dominic said. "We're here about the flood."

The word went through the room the way cold goes through a wall. Priya watched forty faces change at once — not shock, exactly. Recognition. The way a body remembers an old injury before the mind catches up.

The woman at the front set down her papers. "You'd better sit down," she said. "I'm Ada Ferro. I chair this assembly. And you'd better start from the beginning, because we've had seven years of men starting from the middle."

So Dominic started from the beginning. The contract. The crossing. Station Nine, drowned and sealed, and the dive into the vault where the instruments had kept recording through the whole disaster — pressure gauges, forecast models, warnings issued and warnings withdrawn, dates stamped in government ink. He laid the cores out on the assembly's table one at a time, and when they brought in a reader — old Tomás knew a schoolteacher with a salvaged terminal, and the schoolteacher knew everyone within fifty miles — the room read seven years of its own history in documents that had been buried under sixty feet of water to keep it from them.

Nobody shouted. That was the thing Priya hadn't expected. She'd braced for shouting, for weeping, for the kind of fury that breaks furniture. Instead the room got very quiet and very still, the way a river gets before it decides where it's going.

A man near the back — Priya learned later his name was Okonjo, and he'd lost a boatyard and a brother — read the suppression order twice, slowly, lips moving. Then he looked up at Dominic.

"The mining company. Calloway money."

"Calloway money," Dominic confirmed. "Which also holds my debts. All of them. They know what we brought down the mountain. There will be lawyers, probably this week, offering to buy this table's contents. There will be nicer lawyers after that. I want to be clear that I'm not here to sell you anything or lead you anywhere. This isn't mine to give to a court or a paper. It came out of your river. It belongs to your dead." He stepped back from the table. "What you do with it is yours."

Ada Ferro looked around the room. "Well," she said dryly, "that's a novelty. An honest man with evidence." A ripple of tired laughter moved through the chairs. "We'll deliberate. We're good at deliberating — it's the only thing anyone's ever let us do."

* * *

They camped that night on the levee above the town, and Tomás cooked, which meant the entire valley smelled faintly of burnt onions and triumph.

Maren didn't eat with them. She stood at the edge of the firelight until Priya finally walked over and shoved a tin bowl into her hands, because whatever else Maren Voss was — signatory of suppression orders, penitent, ghost of the Kestrel Range — she was also the reason any of them had come down off the mountain alive, and Priya refused to let her starve out of self-punishment.

"They'll go to the papers first," Maren said, staring into the bowl. "Ferro's type always does. Then the courts. Then it'll take nine years and end in a settlement nobody likes."

"Probably," Priya agreed.

"I signed that order to save my company. I told myself it was arithmetic." Maren's voice was flat as slate. "I came up that mountain to bury it properly and instead I—" She stopped.

"You led us to Elias's route," Priya said. "In a blizzard. Off a map you'd kept secret for seven years. That wasn't arithmetic either."

Maren said nothing. But she ate the onions, every last burnt one, and stayed inside the firelight, which Priya counted as progress of a kind.

Later, Priya found Dominic at the levee rail, watching the water slide past in the dark.

"They'll decide tomorrow," he said.

"Mm."

"He'd have liked this. Elias. Not the flood — never the flood — but this. Forty people in a granary arguing about justice." Dominic smiled, small and crooked. "He always said data was just memory with better handwriting. We gave them their memory back."

"And what about you?" Priya asked. "Calloway still owns your trucks. Your debt didn't drown in the crevasse with everything else."

"No," Dominic agreed. "It didn't." He was quiet a moment. "But here's the thing I figured out up there. Debt's a number. Being owned is a story you agree to. I spent seven years agreeing to their story — that I was what I owed. Standing in that hall today, watching Okonjo read his brother's name out of a government file..." He shook his head. "They can send collectors to the ends of the earth. They can't make me care about the number anymore. I've got a different ledger now."

"The river's ledger," Priya said.

"The river's ledger." He glanced at her sideways. "That was almost poetic. Are you feeling all right?"

"Don't get used to it."

* * *

The assembly convened at dawn, and by noon Ada Ferro stood on the granary steps with the whole town pressed into the square below, and read the decision aloud: the records would go to every newspaper that would print them, every court that would hear them, and — copied by hand, by schoolchildren, in fair cursive — to every family that had lost someone, so that no household in the valley would learn the truth secondhand. The originals would stay in the granary, under the assembly's lock, "where the river's people keep what the river gave back."

Then she looked directly at Dominic, standing at the edge of the crowd with his crew around him, and added, "And the assembly notes, for the record, that some debts are paid in full by delivery."

Priya saw something go out of Dominic's shoulders then — not the debt, which would follow him down every road for years yet, but the older weight, the one he'd carried up the Kestrel Range and back. He touched two fingers to his brow, half salute, half apology, and turned away before anyone could thank him further, because that was Dominic all over: allergic to gratitude, fluent in weather.

They loaded the trucks in the gold light of late afternoon. Tomás patted Number Three's flank and pronounced her, for the first time in recorded history, "basically sound." Idris climbed into the scout seat already arguing about which pass they'd take south, though there was only one pass, and everyone let him have it.

Maren stood by her own vehicle, unhitching it from their convoy, going wherever penance went next. At the last moment she crossed to Dominic and held out her hand. He took it.

"Voss," he said.

"Underhill." A pause. "If you ever swear off another route, do me the courtesy of swearing it somewhere I'm not hiring."

"No promises."

Priya took the navigator's seat and spread the map across her knees — the whole country south of the range, roads and rivers and small towns with granaries and arguments in them. As the convoy rolled out onto the levee road, she caught movement in the side mirror: a rider coming up the valley fast, kicking dust, a courier's satchel bouncing at his hip, heading straight for the granary steps where Ada Ferro still stood.

From Corvane, most likely. From the papers, or the courts, or possibly from Calloway itself, carrying whatever came next — an offer, a threat, a summons.

Dominic saw it too. He didn't slow down.

"Well," he said, eyes on the road south, voice light as a man setting down something heavy for good. "Let them chase the story. We've got a head start, three sound axles, and nowhere in particular to be."

Priya smiled, folded the map to the southern passes, and gave him the heading.
