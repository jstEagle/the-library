## 1. The Weight of Nothing

The salt flats taught you two things, if you let them: how small you were, and how loud your own thoughts could get when there was nothing else to hear.

Ada Nystrom had learned both lessons years ago and had never quite managed to unlearn either one. She walked the white crust at a steady ranger's pace—three and a half miles an hour, no more, no less—the pace that got you across forty miles of nothing without killing you. The sun stood high and merciless. The horizon curved at its edges like the inside of a bowl, and somewhere out in all that glare was a man who had made the mistake of running from her.

Not that he knew he'd made a mistake yet. That was the thing about thieves. They always thought the hard part was the stealing.

She paused, crouched, and touched two fingers to the crust. The salt here had been broken recently—a fine powder still settling into the crack where his boot heel had punched through. Fresh. An hour old, maybe less. She allowed herself a small, dry smile.

"You're walking too fast, friend," she said to no one. The flats didn't answer. They never did.

Walking too fast on salt was the classic error. You panicked, you saw the horizon not moving, and you started burning yourself like a lamp turned up too high. By evening he'd be stumbling. By tomorrow morning he'd be sitting in whatever shade he could invent—which would be none—and by tomorrow afternoon she'd have him.

It wasn't personal. She kept telling herself that. It was a contract, same as any other. A man had taken something that didn't belong to him, and the Meridian Consortium had posted a bounty for his return, alive and reasonably intact, and Ada had signed her name at the bottom of the posting because signing her name at the bottom of things was what she did now. It was practically a hobby.

The compass hung at her belt, and she did not look at it.

That was the discipline of it. She could go whole days without looking. But her hand drifted toward it anyway, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth, and when she finally gave in and unclipped it, the needle swung in a slow, drunken circle and settled pointing southeast, which was wrong, because southeast was behind her, and then it sighed and pointed nowhere in particular at all.

Broken. Had been broken for eleven months. She carried it anyway, because some things you carry not because they work but because of what they'd mean to throw away.

Her brother had given her that compass. Rurik, with his big laugh and his terrible singing and his conviction that the world was fundamentally navigable if you just paid attention. *North is a promise, Ada,* he used to say. *Everything else is negotiation.* And then he'd gone out on a run upriver—one contract, one manifest, one signature at the bottom of which hers sat neat as a hymn—and the river had come back without him, and the compass had come back bent, needle loose in its housing, pointing wherever grief told it to point.

She clipped it back on her belt and walked on.

* * *

By late afternoon the light went long and gold, and the salt began to show its moods. Out here the crust wasn't flat forever; it buckled into low ridges, and wind had carved channels like the dried beds of vanished streams. Good country for hiding, if you were foolish enough to think hiding helped.

Ada climbed the tallest ridge she could find and glassed the west.

There. A smudge against the glare, moving. Not an animal—animals moved with purpose or not at all. This smudge moved the way exhausted men move: forward, stagger, forward, as though arguing with each step.

She marked his bearing against a distant butte, checked the angle of the sun, and did the arithmetic. He'd make maybe four more miles before dark. Then he'd stop, because everyone stopped, because stopping felt like safety even when it was the opposite.

Ada had tracked forty-one people across this territory. She'd brought back thirty-eight. Of the three she hadn't brought back, one had been dead before she found him, one had been dead because of choices she'd rather not revisit, and one—

Well. One was a story for a different kind of woman.

She descended and made camp early, while there was still light to gather greasewood. A small fire, screened by a ridge, mostly for the morale of hot tea. She ate hard bread and dried fruit and watched the first stars come out over the flats, and she let herself think the thought she'd been not-thinking all day:

*Why does Meridian want this one so badly?*

Bounties came in flavors. Most were routine—a clerk who'd embezzled, a drifter with a warrant, somebody's runaway nephew. Those paid modestly and nobody cared much how it ended. But this posting had been different. *Ilya Voss*, the paper said. *Return alive. Discretion required.* And the pay was triple scale, and the Consortium's agent had looked at her with that particular softness companies use when they're deciding whether you're smart enough to ask questions or obedient enough not to.

Ada had asked exactly one question—"What did he take?"—and the agent had smiled and said, "Company property," in a voice like a door closing.

Company property. As if a man were worth triple bounty over a ledger book or a strongbox, and worth killing discretion over, and worth sending a desert ranger with her reputation after, when half a dozen cheaper trackers would've jumped at half the fee.

She'd taken the job anyway. That was the part she chewed on now, watching the fire. She'd taken it because the debt came due in six weeks, and the debt did not care about her curiosity, and the debt certainly did not care about her conscience, which had been getting louder lately, like a radio drifting toward a station she didn't want to hear.

The debt was unnamed. That was the trick of it. Nobody had ever said to her, *You owe us for your brother's death.* There was no paper, no figure, no interest rate. It lived in the space between what the company had done—paid out a settlement, small and swift and insultingly efficient—and what Ada had signed afterward: an exclusivity contract, five years, ranger services rendered exclusively to Meridian interests. They hadn't asked. They'd simply offered, at the funeral reception, with casseroles going cold on the table, and she had signed because refusing would have meant admitting what the offer actually was.

A leash looks like a lifeline if you're drowning.

"Tomorrow," she told the fire, and the fire crackled agreeably, being the only conversationalist available.

* * *

She found him at midmorning, and it went wrong immediately, which was to say it went interestingly.

He was sitting on a salt ridge with his back to her, which no fleeing man does unless he's given up, and when she crested the rise with her rope coiled and her voice ready, he didn't startle. He turned around slowly, like a man greeting a bus he'd been expecting.

"You took your time," he said.

Ilya Voss was thinner than his poster and younger than his reputation, with dark hair going gray at the temples far too early and eyes that had the unsettling quality of someone who has read your file. His lips were cracked. His boots were ruined—he'd been walking on salt in city shoes, which explained the staggering—and he was holding a canteen that was very obviously empty, because he tipped it upside down in greeting and nothing came out.

"You knew I was coming," Ada said.

"I counted your fires." He nodded eastward. "Two nights. You bank them small. Ranger habit." He said it without accusation, almost with approval, and something about that pricked her. "Also, you walk in a straight line. Nobody walks in a straight line across the Kettle Flats unless they know exactly where they are. I figured if I stopped moving, you'd come to me, and we'd both save our boots."

"That," Ada said, "is the single most annoying surrender I've ever received."

"It's not a surrender." He held up the empty canteen again, as evidence. "It's a negotiation. I'm told you're reasonable. I'm hoping the rumors are right, because I've been drinking my own urine since yesterday and I'd really like to move past that chapter of my life."

Despite herself—despite eleven months of practice at not being amused by anything—Ada felt the corner of her mouth move. She killed it fast. "Rope first," she said. "Negotiation second."

"Fair."

He offered his wrists without being asked, which also annoyed her. She bound them in front—generous, and she noted that he noticed the generosity, and filed it away—and gave him water from her own supply, watching him drink with the careful greed of a man who knows better than to gulp. Then she sat down across from him on the salt, rifle across her knees, and studied him, and he studied her right back with those file-reading eyes.

"So," she said. "What did you take?"

"Company property."

"I read the posting. What *was* it?"

"A ledger," he said.

Something cold walked down Ada's spine, wearing small boots. "A ledger."

"Meridian keeps books, Ranger Nystrom. All companies do. Most of their books are boring." He smiled, and it was a tired smile, and underneath the tiredness was something hard and bright, like a coin at the bottom of a well. "This one isn't boring."

"The agent said property. Valuables. I assumed—"

"You assumed money. Everyone assumes money. Money would be easier." He leaned back against the salt ridge, wrists roped, utterly at ease, and that ease was the most alarming thing about him. "Ask me why they're paying triple scale for a bookkeeper, Ranger. Go on. You've been asking yourself since the landing office. I can see it. You have a face like a ledger yourself—everything written down, nothing posted yet."

"I have a face like a professional," Ada said, "which is more than I can say for yours."

"Gentle humor under pressure. The rumors were right about that too." He closed his eyes briefly, savoring the water, the shade of her presence, the simple miracle of not being alone on the salt anymore. When he opened them, he was serious. "Here's what I'll trade you. Water for words, day by day. You keep me alive and walking, and every evening I tell you one true thing about what's in that ledger. By the time we reach the Consortium's people, you'll know whether you want to hand me over."

"And if I say no? If I just deliver you silent?"

"Then you'll spend the rest of your career wondering," he said mildly, "and I don't think you're built for wondering. I think you're built for knowing. It's a curse. I have it myself."

The wind moved across the flats, carrying that sound the salt makes when it shifts—a whisper, a long exhale, like the land murmuring in its sleep. Ada looked at the man's roped hands, at the empty canteen, at the horizon where the Consortium's river stations lay waiting like hooks in water.

Eleven months she'd worked for Meridian. Eleven months of contracts and bounties and signatures, and never once had anyone offered to tell her the truth as payment.

"One true thing a night," she said. "Starting tonight. And if I catch you lying, the deal's off and you walk the rest of the way in irons."

"Agreed." He stuck out his bound wrists toward her, absurdly formal, a handshake with no hands available. "Pleasure doing business, Ranger Nystrom."

"We haven't done business," she said, standing, shouldering her pack. "We've made an arrangement. Business implies I trust you."

"Give it a week," said Ilya Voss, and got to his feet, and turned northeast, toward the river, toward the green country and the Consortium's waiting arms, and Ada fell into step beside him with her rifle slung and her broken compass hanging silent at her hip, pointing nothing, promising nothing, north being a promise she was no longer entirely sure anyone intended to keep.

Behind them the salt flats stretched away white and endless, holding their footprints for exactly as long as the wind allowed, which was to say: not long at all.
