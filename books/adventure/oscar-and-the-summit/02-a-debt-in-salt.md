## 2. A Debt in Salt

The night before the Meridian man came, Ada dreamed of her brother's hands.

Not his face—she had lost the face years ago, the way you lose a word you've known all your life, one morning it's simply gone and the hole where it was is shaped exactly like the word. But the hands she still had. Big, cracked at the knuckles, always faintly stained with grease no amount of lye would lift. In the dream he was turning them over and over in front of her, palms up, palms down, like a street magician showing there was nothing hidden, and he was saying, *Ada. Ada. Look what you gave me,* and she couldn't look, and she woke with salt on her lips that wasn't from the air.

She lay on her cot in the ranger station at Cinder Gap and listened to the wind work the tin roof loose, one sheet at a time, patient as a dentist. The station had been hers for six years. It had a stove that smoked if you looked at it wrong, a radio that only liked certain frequencies, and a window that framed the salt flats going violet at dawn like something holy that had no business being holy. She had chosen it herself. That was the thing people didn't understand about punishment. You got to pick your own.

On the shelf above the stove, between the water-testing kit and a tin of tea she was saving for no occasion in particular, sat her compass. It had been her brother's before it was hers. The needle swung when it should have pointed, dragged, wandered, settled on a lie. A compass with a cracked magnet, the repair man in Kell's Landing had told her, shaking his head like a doctor delivering news. You can't fix a heart like that. You can only learn its moods. So Ada had learned them. North was a little left of where the needle said. True north lived somewhere in the drag, and if you knew your instrument was broken—if you *admitted* it, every day, with humility—you could still find your way.

It was the only kind of forgiveness she trusted. The kind you had to keep re-earning.

* * *

Three years earlier, and a hundred miles south where the salt gave way to scrub and the scrub gave way to the green throat of the Karst Reach, Ada had signed a piece of paper.

She thought about that paper the way some people thought about a scar: touching it to check it still hurt. It did. It always did.

The paper had been a contract. A hauling contract, Meridian Consortium letterhead, blue ink, her signature in her own careful hand—the careful hand of a twenty-four-year-old who believed that paperwork was a kind of armor. Her brother Oscar had run cargo on the river then, the *Larkspur*, a flat-bottomed barge he loved with the unembarrassed love of a man who had never learned to be cool about anything. He'd wanted the Meridian run. Steady pay, he said. Steady pay meant he could fix the *Larkspur's* engine, and a fixed engine meant he could take the high-valley route, and the high-valley route meant—

"You're doing the thing," Ada had said, in the kitchen of their mother's old house, while he paced with a mug of tea he kept forgetting to drink.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you build a cathedral out of one contract."

"One contract is how cathedrals start." He'd grinned at her, that grin, and she had felt the future lean toward her like a friend. "Besides. You signed the Meridian paperwork. You're the one who says they're straight."

And she had. That was the poison in it, the part she turned over at three in the morning the way you turn a stone to see what's underneath. She was the ranger. She was the one whose job—the actual, salaried, badge-carrying job—was to know whether the companies were straight. She'd checked Meridian's manifests for two years. She'd found nothing. What she hadn't known—what she couldn't have known, the inquiry later said, the inquiry with Meridian men on its oversight board—was that the manifests she was checking were the clean copies. That the *Larkspur's* last run upriver carried cargo that was never written down, on a route that was never supposed to be reported, through a stretch of the Karst Reach where a survey crew had found something in the rock that Meridian wanted and the survey crew's own contract said they couldn't have.

The *Larkspur* went down in the Narrows on a clear night in the dry season, when the river was low and gentle as a sleeping animal. Boiler failure, the report said. Eleven souls. The report was four pages long and Ada had read it so many times she could recite it, and every time she reached the words *no evidence of foul play* her mouth filled with salt.

Because there *had* been a thing. A small thing. A week before the run, a Meridian liaison had come to her station, friendly, unhurried, a man who smelled of cedar and good coffee, and he'd asked her—off the record, just between professionals—whether the Narrows channel markers had been verified that season. And she'd said yes. Because she'd verified them herself in the spring. Because she was competent, and competent people say yes.

The markers had been moved. She'd never been able to prove it. The river had spent a season rearranging itself, the way rivers do, and by the time anyone thought to look, the truth was silt.

She had said yes. Her brother had gone upriver. The river had come back without him.

* * *

The Meridian man arrived at Cinder Gap on the second Tuesday of the dry season, in a hover-truck too clean for the road it came in on, wearing a suit the color of river water before it goes bad.

Ada watched him come from the window, and her first thought—her honest, uncharitable, wide-awake thought—was *cedar and good coffee.* Her second thought was that she was getting old enough to hate a smell.

He introduced himself as Mr. Renholt, of Meridian's Regional Assets Division. He admired her station. He admired her stove. He admired, with a sincerity so polished it had to be machine-made, the view of the flats.

"Miss Nystrom," he said, accepting the tea she gave him because her mother had raised her to and would have risen from the grave to say something about it otherwise, "you come recommended."

"By whom?"

"By your file." He smiled. He had a way of making compliments sound like inventory. "Six years, no incidents, no complaints. You know this country the way the river knows its bed. And—" here he paused, delicately, the way a man steps over a body, "—you have a personal familiarity with the Reach."

The stove chose that moment to smoke. Ada got up and thumped it, and used the time to arrange her face into something that wasn't anything.

"There was an inquiry," she said, sitting back down. "It cleared me."

"The inquiry cleared everyone," Renholt said comfortably. "That's what inquiries are for." He set a folder on the table between them, squared it to the edge with two fingers, and did not open it. "There's a man. Ilya Voss. Formerly of our finance department. He's taken something that doesn't belong to him and gone to ground somewhere in the flats, headed for the Reach. We'd like him found."

"Found how?"

"Alive. Preferably. He's not dangerous—he's an auditor, they're mostly spine. But he's clever, and clever men in empty country die of their own cleverness, and my employers would take that poorly." He slid the folder across. "There's a fee. A substantial one. And there's—" the pause again, the stepping-over again, "—an understanding. That a woman with your history might welcome the chance to demonstrate, formally, on the record, where her loyalties sit."

Ada looked at the folder. She thought about the inquiry, four pages, *no evidence of foul play.* She thought about the word *cleared* and how it had never once, in three years, felt like the word it was supposed to be. She thought about the debt—the real one, the one with no number on it, the one she owed to eleven names she said to herself some nights like a rosary with the beads worn smooth.

And she felt the trap close, and here was the terrible thing, the thing she would examine for a long time afterward with the same grim thoroughness she gave everything: she felt *relief.* Because a debt you can work off is a debt you can survive, and Meridian was handing her a shovel and pointing at the dirt.

"What's he taken?" she asked.

"Company property."

"What kind of company property?"

Renholt's smile did not move, but something behind it did, the way a fish moves under ice. "The kind that's worth more returned than discussed. The fee is in the folder. The route he was last seen taking is in the folder. Your radio silence obligations are in the folder." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and paused at the door with the air of a man adding a postscript. "For what it's worth, Miss Nystrom, my employers speak very highly of your brother's service. The *Larkspur* was a reliable vessel."

Ada looked at him for a long moment.

"Get out of my station," she said, pleasantly, in the voice she used for lost hikers, and he went, and the hover-truck's wash scattered salt across her clean floor, and she stood in the doorway watching the flats until the light went the color of an old bruise.

* * *

She didn't open the folder that night. She opened the tin of tea instead, the one saved for no occasion, because it occurred to her that this might be the occasion, or that if she waited for a better one she'd be waiting until she was dust.

She drank it on the step, and the flats went dark, and she talked to her brother the way she did sometimes, out loud, quietly, because the wind was loud enough to keep it private and because the dead are the only audience who never interrupt.

"He wants me to prove my loyalties," she told the dark. "To *them.* As if I—" She stopped. Restarted. "As if I don't know where they went wrong. As if I haven't been walking the line of it for three years."

The wind said nothing. The wind was very good at that.

The truth, which she examined now with the tea going cold in her hands, was that she didn't know what she'd do if she found this Voss. She told herself she'd deliver him, because a contract was a contract and her word was the one thing she had left that was worth anything, and she believed it the way you believe a load-bearing wall: completely, and without looking too closely at the cracks. She told herself the money didn't matter, and that was even true, in the sense that she'd have done it for free, which was its own kind of damning.

What she wanted—what she wanted with a hunger that embarrassed her—was to be on the river again. To go up the Reach, past the Narrows, to the place where the markers had lied, and stand there, and *know.* Not prove. Knowing was more than the inquiry had ever given her and she'd stopped expecting proof, the way you stop expecting rain in the dry season. She just wanted to stand on the bank and say the eleven names out loud where the water could hear them.

If a thief happened to be the key that opened that road—well. The desert had always taught her to be grateful for whatever water came, and never to ask too hard where it had been.

She went inside. She opened the folder.

The fee was obscene. She read it twice and felt the number like a hand on the back of her neck, guiding her gently forward, and hated that it worked, and noted, in the small cold ledger she kept behind her eyes, that Meridian knew exactly what it was buying. Not her competence. Her guilt. They'd priced it. They'd priced it precisely, the way you'd price a manifest, and the precision was the most honest thing they'd shown her all day.

The route was sketched on a survey map, and she had to give them this: it was a good map. Last confirmed sighting, a well station two days out past the White Sinks. Voss alone, on foot, with a pack. A man walking into the flats in the dry season with a pack was either a fool or a man who knew something about water that she didn't, and Ada had spent six years learning that the second kind was the dangerous kind.

She checked her kit by lamplight, the old ritual, hands moving on their own: water, salt tabs, the long rifle she'd never once had to point at a person and prayed she never would, the repair kit, the flare kit, the paper maps because electronics lied in the heat. Last, the compass. She held it in her palm and watched the needle drag toward its lying north.

"You and me," she told it. "Still finding our way. Still a little broken about it."

The needle trembled, and settled, and pointed almost true.

* * *

Two days later she stood at the edge of the White Sinks with the sun hammering the salt into a white glare that made the world look like it had been overexposed, and she found the first sign of Ilya Voss, and the sign was wrong.

Footprints, first of all—light, economical, a walker's prints, not a wanderer's. But around them, at the well station itself, something stranger: the water cache had been used, carefully, and the level marked. Marked. A thief, running from the most patient and vindictive company in the territory, had stopped at a desert well and *left a record of how much he'd drunk,* chalked on the casing stone in a small neat hand: *4 liters. Will repay. —I.V.*

Ada stood looking at it for a long time, her canteen heavy on her hip, the sun pressing down like a thumb.

Nobody did that. Fools didn't do that, and liars didn't do that, and men running from Meridian's Regional Assets Division had no earthly reason to leave their name behind in chalk. It was either a trap, or a message, or a man so strange that the desert itself hadn't figured out what to make of him yet.

She looked south, toward the Reach, where the river ran through its green throat past the Narrows and up toward the high valley that ate names. The tracks led that way, unhurried, almost polite.

"Four liters," she said to nobody, and shook her head, and felt—despite everything, despite the folder and the fee and the hand on the back of her neck—the first small unapproved flicker of curiosity. "You're either the honest man I've ever hunted or the best liar this salt's ever grown."

She marked the well on her broken-hearted compass's bearing, shouldered her pack, and walked north after him, into the glare, toward the well where the thief was waiting—though she didn't know yet that he was waiting, and wouldn't, until the moment she came around the rocks with her rifle slung and found him sitting calmly beside his pack, hands visible, offering her the last of his water like a man greeting a guest.
