## 3. The Thief at the Well

The well sat in the crook of two hills like an old man cupping his hands around the last of his water, and Ilya Voss was sitting on its rim as if he'd been waiting for her all day.

Ada stopped at the crest of the ridge and looked down at him for a long moment, because this was not how it was supposed to go. She had spent nine days crossing the salt flats after him. Nine days of reading his sign — the careful boot prints that avoided soft ground, the fire rings built small and dead out, the way he'd once backtracked a full mile to cover his own trail like a man embarrassed to be followed. She had imagined this moment a dozen ways: him bolting from some roadside inn, him caught mid-sleep with his hands under his head, him cornered in a dry wash with nowhere left to be clever.

She had not imagined him sitting on a well in the late afternoon light, eating dried apricots out of a paper bag, looking for all the world like a man enjoying his vacation.

"You're the ranger," he called up to her. Not a question. "Ada Nystrom. You have a dog, don't you? Or you used to. There's dog hair in your bedroll."

"I don't have a dog."

"That's sad," he said, and offered her an apricot.

* * *

She came down the ridge the way she did everything — carefully, without wasting motion, her hand never far from the short baton she carried instead of a gun because guns were for people who expected trouble and Ada preferred to simply *be* the trouble. The well was a good one, stone-lipped, older than any of the maps she carried, with a rope worn smooth by generations of hands. A windmill creaked beside it, turning lazily, pumping nothing anymore but keeping up appearances.

Ilya Voss was thinner than his bounty sheet suggested, which she noted with professional irritation, because it meant the photograph was old and someone at Meridian had been sloppy. Dark hair going gray early at the temples. Hands that looked like they belonged to a man who wrote things rather than stole them, though she supposed those could be the same thing. He wore a good coat gone shabby and boots resoled twice, and there was ink on the side of his right hand — a smudge of it along the edge of his palm, the mark of a man who wrote a great deal and wrote it fast.

"Sit," he said, patting the well's rim beside him. "It's a long way from anywhere and you've been walking since dawn. I watched you come over the flats. You walk like the ground owes you money."

"The ground does owe me money." She stayed standing. "You're Ilya Voss."

"Guilty. Well — allegedly guilty. We can discuss the difference." He ate another apricot. "You're the one they sent. Meridian's little errand ranger. The one with the reputation for always bringing them back alive."

"Alive is cheaper than dead. Funerals require paperwork."

"That's the most honest thing anyone from Meridian has ever said." He smiled, and it was a good smile, crooked and tired, and Ada distrusted it immediately and thoroughly. "So. Here we are. You've crossed ninety miles of salt to collect me. I'd hate to waste your trip. Ask me anything."

"I'm not here to chat."

"Everyone's here to chat. They just don't know it yet." He set the apricot bag down between them like a peace offering or a trap. "You want to know what I took. That's why you haven't pulled that baton yet. A thief who steals coin gets dragged in without a word. But your contract says 'recover stolen property,' doesn't it? Singular property. Valuable. And nobody at Meridian would tell you what it was, because they didn't want you asking questions about it."

Ada said nothing, which was answer enough, and something flickered across his face — not triumph, exactly. More like relief. Like a man who had been carrying a heavy thing alone for a long time and had finally found someone strong enough to complain to.

"It's a ledger," he said.

* * *

"A ledger," Ada repeated.

"A ledger." He spread his hands. "Not money. Not jewels. Not the payroll sack they told you I lifted from the Kell's Landing office. A book. Bound in green leather, about so—" he measured a span with his ink-stained fingers, "—with a brass clasp and a coffee stain shaped like a fish on page forty if you care about such things. That's what I have in my pack. That's what they want back badly enough to pay you whatever they're paying you."

"And what's in this ledger?"

"Numbers." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't match his voice. His eyes were doing arithmetic of their own, and she got the distinct feeling she was part of the sum. "Entries. Dates. Names on one side, payments on the other. It's a very boring book, Ranger Nystrom. It would put you to sleep in an afternoon. Unless you happened to know what the numbers meant."

"And you do."

"I wrote half of them." He said it plainly, no flourish, and then reached into the well's shadow and drew up a bucket of water, cool and dark, and held it out to her. "Drink first. Then decide whether to arrest me. I find decisions go better wet."

She should have taken the bucket after cuffing him. She knew the order of operations. But the salt flats had taken everything moist out of her body nine days ago and her throat felt like a boot sole, and the bucket was right there, and he held it the way you hold a thing out to a horse — steady, patient, no sudden moves.

She drank. It was the best water of her life and she hated him a little for it.

"Here's my problem," she said, wiping her mouth. "You talk too much. Thieves who talk too much are either innocent or very good. I haven't decided which you are."

"Innocent and very good aren't opposites."

"They usually are."

"Well." He shrugged. "Then I'm the exception, and you can write me up as such."

* * *

She made him sit on the ground while she stood over him, because standing over people was most of what authority amounted to and she'd learned to economize. She went through his pack while he narrated, which he did, relentlessly, like a museum guide.

"Blanket. Good blanket, actually, you can tell by the weave. Rope — borrowed, I'll return it. Dried apricots, obviously, the cornerstone of any diet. A tin cup. A book of poetry, which you may mock freely, everyone does." He paused. "And the ledger."

It was where he said it would be, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, beneath the poetry. Green leather. Brass clasp. She weighed it in her hand and it was heavier than a ledger had any right to be, or maybe that was imagination, or maybe it was the way certain objects carry more than paper.

"If I take this and bring you in," she said slowly, "Meridian gets both back. And I get paid. And everyone lives happily ever after except possibly you."

"Possibly," he agreed. "There's a phrase in their internal correspondence I overheard before I left. 'Processing.' As in, 'Voss will be processed upon recovery.' I've worked for bureaucracies my whole life, Ranger. I know what processing means when it's applied to a person instead of a form."

"So run."

"I did run. For nine days. You noticed?"

"I noticed."

"You're better at this than the last three they sent." He sounded genuinely admiring, which was somehow worse than flattery. "One of them shot at me near the Sable Wash. Missed. Woke up every lizard in the county."

"You didn't report that."

"To whom? The company whose men were shooting at me?" He laughed, short and dry. "Ranger. Look at me. Do I look like a man who robbed a payroll office? Where's the money? Thieves spend money. I've been eating apricots and sleeping under a blanket I already owned. If I'd taken their cash I'd be on a boat to somewhere with better weather and worse morals."

Ada looked at him. She looked at him longer than was strictly professional. The light was going gold and long across the hills, and the windmill creaked its empty creak, and the man sitting in the dust at her feet looked less like a thief than like a clerk who had wandered out of an office and forgotten to go home.

"What's in the ledger," she said again, not a question this time. A door held open a crack.

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, the performance had drained out of his voice, and what was underneath was flat and careful, like a man walking on ice he knew was thin.

"Accidents," he said. "That's what the entries are called. 'Accident, Karst Reach, four souls, settled.' 'Accident, survey crew seven, settled.' Settled means paid. Paid to whom? To families, sometimes — hush money dressed as condolence. Paid to magistrates, other times, to close the files. Paid to crews, once, to keep quiet about what they saw." He looked up at her. "Do you know how a mining consortium claims land it has no right to? It doesn't fight for it. It waits for the people on it to die. Conveniently. Repeatedly. On schedule. And then it files the paperwork."

The windmill turned. Somewhere down the valley a bird complained about the evening.

"My brother ran freight," Ada heard herself say, and stopped, because she hadn't meant to say it, hadn't said it aloud in three years, and certainly hadn't planned to say it to a bounty sitting in the dirt.

Ilya Voss went very still.

"Freight," he repeated softly. "Upstream? Through the Reach?"

"Last run was through the Reach."

He closed his eyes briefly, and when he opened them, all the humor had gone out of his face entirely, and what remained was worse — recognition. Pity, even. And Ada felt the first spark of doubt catch in her chest like a coal dropped in dry grass, small and bright and dangerous, because she had signed a contract with these people, she had taken their advance against her debt, and this stranger in the dust was looking at her the way you look at someone standing on a floor you know is rotten.

"What date?" he asked quietly. "Your brother's run. What date?"

"Why?"

"Because I need to know," he said, "whether it's in the book."

* * *

She didn't tell him the date. She wanted to — her mouth actually opened around it — and she shut it, and stood there with her jaw tight and the ledger heavy in her hand and the sun going down behind the western hill like a coin dropping into a pocket.

"No," she said. "No. You don't get to do that. You don't get to sit there and make me curious. You're a thief. I'm a ranger. Tomorrow morning I walk you back downriver and I collect my fee, and whatever's in this book stays between you and Meridian's lawyers."

"Of course," he said. And then, gently, in the tone of a man laying down a card he'd been holding all game: "Except you'll read it tonight. After you think I'm asleep. You'll untie the oilcloth and you'll open to the accident pages and you'll look for a date, and you'll tell yourself it's procedure, verifying evidence, and it won't be procedure at all. It'll be hope. Hope that it isn't there. And Ranger—" he rose slowly, dusting off his knees, unhurried, giving her every chance to stop him and knowing she wouldn't, "—I hope so too. I liked your brother's name before I ever met you. Crew manifests cross my desk for years. Somebody on that river kept meticulous books, and I signed off on half of them, and I have been running from that signature for eleven months."

The windmill creaked. The first star came out over the eastern ridge, early and eager.

"You're lying," Ada said, but her voice didn't believe itself.

"I'm a great many things," Ilya Voss said. "A liar, occasionally, and a thief, technically, and a coward on at least two documented occasions. But I am not lying about the ledger, and I am not lying about being sorry, and I am not — despite abundant evidence — going to run. Because I've been running for eleven months and it turns out you can't outrun a signature." He nodded at the well. "There's room for two at that fire ring behind the windmill. I have apricots and you have jurisdiction. We can share."

She should have cuffed him then. Every procedure she'd ever been taught said cuff him now, march him at dawn, ask questions never. Instead she stood in the blue dusk holding a green leather book that weighed far too much, watching a thief build a small careful fire, and thought about her brother's name, and whether it was written down somewhere in a stranger's neat hand, filed under *accident*, settled, paid, closed.

The fire crackled. He put the kettle on.

"One night," she said finally. "You talk. I listen. In the morning I decide."

"Fair terms," said Ilya Voss. "I'd expect nothing less from the best tracker they have."

"I'm not theirs," Ada said, before she could stop herself. "I work for them. That's different."

"Is it?" he asked, and handed her a cup of tea, and out past the hills the desert went on being enormous and indifferent, and somewhere in her pack, wrapped in oilcloth, a ledger waited with the patience of all bad news.
