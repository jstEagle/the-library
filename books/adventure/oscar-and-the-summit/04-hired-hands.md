## 4. Hired Hands

The dust came first.

Ada had been sitting with her back against the well's sun-baked stones for the better part of an hour, sharing a canteen with the man she'd spent eleven days hunting across the salt flats, when she saw it—a brown haze rising off the eastern track, too low and too fast to be weather.

Ilya Voss followed her gaze. "Friends of yours?"

"Not mine." She stood, brushing grit from her knees, and felt the familiar cold settle into her stomach, the one that arrived whenever a job stopped being simple. "Company vehicles don't come out here unless somebody called them."

"Would you look at that." Ilya said it mildly, the way a man might remark on an interesting cloud formation, but his hands had gone very still around the tin cup of well water Sefa's cousin had drawn for them. "Somebody called them."

"I didn't call them."

"I know." He set the cup down on the well's lip with exaggerated care, as though it were evidence in a trial. "That's what worries me. If you'd called them, at least they'd be *your* monsters."

Ada didn't answer that, because there wasn't a good answer. The contract was clear enough—she'd read it four times on the ride out from Meridian Station, the way she always read contracts, hunting for the trapdoor. *Deliver subject alive and ambulatory to Consortium representatives or designated processing facility. Bonus contingent upon condition of subject.* She'd assumed *condition* meant he shouldn't arrive half-dead of thirst. It had not occurred to her, until this exact moment, that the clause might be there because someone else intended to make him half-dead first.

Three vehicles crested the ridge, running without lights despite the failing afternoon, kicking up a rooster tail of salt dust. Grey hulls, no markings except the small silver chevron of Meridian Consolidated stenciled near the hood. Ada had seen that chevron her whole life—on supply crates, on magistrate's doors, on the flag over the ranger station where she'd taken her oath at nineteen. It had always meant *order*. Things getting done. The desert made manageable.

Right now it meant something else, and her body knew it before her mind caught up. Her hand drifted to the pistol on her hip without her permission.

"Easy," Ilya said quietly. He hadn't moved from the well. "You're the law out here. Act like it."

"I'm a bounty contractor," she said. "It's not the same thing."

"It is today."

* * *

There were six of them. Four got out of the lead vehicle in matching grey field jackets, moving with the loose-shouldered confidence of men who had never once been told no by the landscape. The other two stayed with the vehicles, and Ada noted—one part professional habit, three parts dread—that both kept their hands resting near their hip holsters in a way that was casual the way a loaded trap is casual.

The one in front was broad through the chest, with a sunburned face and the kind of smile that gets laminated onto recruitment posters. He looked at Ada first, which she appreciated, and then dismissed her, which she did not.

"Ranger Nystrom." He pronounced it carefully, like a word from a language he found faintly amusing. "Corven. Asset Recovery Division." He stuck out a hand.

She didn't take it. "Meridian doesn't run Asset Recovery in the Karst Reach. That's outside your charter. This is ranger territory."

"Charter's been updated." Corven let the hand fall, unbothered. "You've got our man?"

"He's not a man, he's a contract, and the contract says delivery to the survey station at Kell's Landing, into the custody of the independent survey team. Not to you."

"The survey team's been stood down." He said it easily, the way you'd mention a road closure. "Administrative matter. We're the designated custodians now. You'll get paid either way. More, actually—the expedited rate." He produced a slate from his jacket, thumbed it awake, and turned it toward her. The number on it was nearly double what she'd contracted for.

Behind her, she heard Ilya exhale through his nose. Not quite a laugh.

"You find something funny, Voss?"

"Just admiring the efficiency," Ilya said. "Eleven days of desert, one leaky boat, and now they double the price the moment I'm actually in reach. You'd think they'd have budgeted better."

One of the grey jackets behind Corven shifted his weight, and Ada watched his hand move—not to a holster, but to a short black baton clipped at the small of his back. Her stomach dropped a full register.

"Contract terms are between me and my employer," she said, stepping sideways, deliberately placing herself in the line between Ilya and the vehicles. "And my employer's signature says Kell's Landing. So if the survey team's been stood down, we wait at the station until whoever replaced them shows up with paperwork I can verify. That's how chain of custody works. Even you people used to care about that."

Something flickered across Corven's face—impatience, mostly, with a thin seam of contempt underneath—and Ada understood, with the clarity of cold water, that the paperwork argument was going to lose. It wasn't that her logic was wrong. It was that logic had never been invited on this trip.

"Here's how chain of custody works today," Corven said, still pleasant, still smiling, and that was the worst part, the smile never moving while everything under it moved. "We take him. You take your money. Everybody drives home before dark. Or—" he spread his hands, magnanimous, "—you can file a complaint. With the magistrate at Salt Crossing. Who I believe is on our retainer now. Your choice, Ranger."

*The law she serves.* Her father's voice, from years ago, teaching her to read a contract by lamplight: *A signature is only as good as the person who honors it, Ada-girl. Read who signs, not what's signed.* She'd thought that was about merchants.

"Voss comes with me," she said, "to Kell's Landing. Alive. Unmarked. That was the deal, and I don't renegotiate in the dirt with men who won't give me their charter number."

Corven sighed, genuinely regretful, an actor hitting his mark. "Take him."

* * *

Later—much later, on the boat, in the dark—Ada would replay the next ninety seconds frame by frame and hate herself in every single one.

She went for her pistol. She was fast; eleven years of desert work had made her fast, faster than most, and she'd have sworn on her brother's grave that she could draw on any two men in the Reach and win.

But there were six, and the two at the vehicles had never been watching Corven at all. They'd been watching her. The moment her thumb touched leather, the nearest one fired—not at her, that was the insult of it—at the ground beside her boot, a stun round that cracked the salt crust and spat white dust up her leg, numbing it to the knee. She staggered. And in the half-second that bought, two of the grey jackets simply walked past her, the way you walk past furniture, and took Ilya Voss by the arms.

He didn't fight. That was the thing she noticed even through the buzzing in her leg—he didn't fight, he *talked*, hands open, voice level: "Gentlemen, I'm coming willingly, there's no need—" and then the baton came around in a short, practiced arc and caught him across the kidney, and the talking stopped, and the sound he made was not a sound a man should make.

"No," Ada said. It came out wrong, thin, swallowed by the wind off the flats. She tried again, louder, putting her badge into it, twenty years of institutional weight: "*Meridian contractors, stand down! You are striking a detainee in lawful custody!*"

The second blow landed across the backs of Ilya's thighs and folded him. They let him drop to his knees in the salt, and one of them put a boot between his shoulder blades, casual as pinning a moth, and leaned.

"Lawful custody," Corven repeated, tasting it. He'd come closer, hands in his pockets, watching the work with the mild interest of a man inspecting a fence line. "You know what I admire about you rangers, Nystrom? You really believe it. The words. Custody. Jurisdiction. Charter." He shook his head fondly. "Out here, the only law is what the company pays for. You're learning that right now. Look at you—badge, pistol, and standing on one leg watching us do the job you were hired for. Except cheaper, and we don't have to feed you."

Ilya's face was pressed into the salt crust. Ada could see his eyes, though, turned toward her—not pleading, which would have been easier to bear. *Assessing.* Watching what she did next. Cataloguing her, the way she'd catalogued him for eleven days across the flats, and finding out what she was made of.

Her leg was coming back. Pins and needles, then fire, then something like strength. Her hand was still on her pistol grip. Six of them. Two more in the vehicles. A radio that would summon ten more inside an hour.

And somewhere under all of that arithmetic, quieter than the wind, her father's voice again: *Read who signs.*

She drew.

Not to fire. She drew and racked the slide in one motion and put the muzzle six inches from Corven's right eye, close enough that he could see the rifling, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect.

"Step away from my prisoner."

The flats went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Nystrom," Corven said, and for the first time the smile cost him something. "Think this through. You shoot a Meridian officer, there's no desert far enough."

"Good thing I'm not shooting. Yet." She thumbed the hammer. "But here's what happens if that boot stays on him. I put a round through your knee—low, so you live, because unlike some people I deliver what I'm paid to deliver. Then I carry Voss to Kell's Landing myself, and I file a report with photographs, and names, and the serial numbers off those vehicles, and I hand copies to every magistrate and every newspaper between here and the coast. I'm not clever like you, Corven. I'm just stubborn, and I write things down. Ask anyone."

A long pause. Somewhere behind her, Ilya made a small wet sound that might have been a laugh.

Corven looked at her for five full seconds. Then he lifted two fingers, and the boot came off Ilya's back, and the grey jackets stepped away with the bored precision of men following an order they'd expected all along.

"Expedited rate's off the table," Corven said, straightening his jacket. "Standard fee. Delivery to Kell's Landing within the week, subject intact." He paused at the edge of her reach, close enough that she could smell the recycled air of the vehicle cab on him. "You're making a mistake, Ranger. But it's an honest one. Those are rare." He tipped two fingers off his brow, mock salute, and walked back to the lead truck.

The engines wound up. Dust climbed. And then the flats were empty again except for the wheel ruts filling slowly with windblown salt, and the two of them—Ilya on his knees in the crust, breathing in careful, rationed sips; Ada standing over him with a pistol she couldn't remember lowering.

* * *

She helped him to the shade of the well's parapet and made him drink, and he flinched when she peeled his shirt up to look at the damage. Two welts, angry red, already purpling. Nothing broken, as far as she could tell. Kidneys were tricky, though. She'd known a prospector once who'd taken one boot to the back and bled out his water for a week.

"They'll come back," Ilya said. His voice was rough but level. "Not today. Maybe not this week. But Corven reports to someone, and that someone will decide I'm worth more dead in a ditch than alive at a survey station. You understand that now, yes? What you just bought yourself?"

"I understood it before you said it."

"Did you?" He looked at her then, really looked, and there was no mockery in it, which somehow made it worse. "Because from where I was kneeling—which, for the record, was face-down in a salt flat—it looked like a woman discovering, mid-sentence, that the law she serves has teeth. And that the teeth were never meant for people like me. They were meant for people like you, if you ever stop pointing the right direction."

Ada sat back on her heels. The sun was going down over the flats, turning the salt pink and gold, beautiful in the way that only merciless things are beautiful. Her leg still hummed where the stun round had kissed it. In her breast pocket, the contract rustled against her heartbeat, and for the first time since she'd signed it, she made herself think about the signature line. Her name. And above it, printed in small grey type she'd skimmed past: *Meridian Consolidated, Asset Recovery Division, authorized agent.*

The same division whose boot prints were all over the man in front of her.

"There's a riverboat runs from Sefa's landing," she heard herself say. "Two days downstream. The captain owes me a favor, and she doesn't ask questions at checkpoints." She stood, shouldered her pack, and did not offer him a hand up, because some pride needed preserving, his and hers both. "Kell's Landing. Like the contract says. Get up, Voss. We're burning daylight."

Ilya rose slowly, testing his legs, wincing. At the top of his stretch he paused and studied her with something new in his face—not trust, nothing so generous, but maybe its first cousin.

"For what it's worth," he said, "that was the stupidest, bravest thing anyone's done for me in a long time."

"It wasn't for you." She turned east, toward the river, toward whatever was waiting at the end of it. "It was for the contract."

"Mm," said Ilya Voss, thief, liar, and possibly the only honest man in the Karst Reach. "Keep telling yourself that, Ranger. We've got a long boat ride ahead."

Far behind them, down the eastern track, a second column of dust had begun to rise.
