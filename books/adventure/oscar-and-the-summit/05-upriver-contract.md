## 5. Upriver Contract

The dust column behind them did not hurry, which was somehow worse than if it had. Dust that hurries is a man on a horse with a job to do. Dust that strolls is an organization that knows the desert has no exits.

Ada watched it for a long moment from the shade of the well's crumbling parapet, doing arithmetic she didn't like. Two riders, maybe three. The agents she'd left groaning by the water trough would be found, or not found, and either way someone in a clean shirt at Meridian's regional office would open a file and write her name in it. *Nystrom, A. Contract irregularity.*

Her name had been in enough files lately to fill a drawer.

"We should move," Ilya said. He'd found a bucket somewhere and was drinking like a man who'd recently been half-drowned in dust, which he had. Water ran down his chin and dripped off his collar and he didn't waste a drop of it or a second of shame about it. "Unless you're planning to explain to them why their men are sleeping face-down in the sand."

"I'm not planning anything," Ada said. "That's the problem. I've been improvising since yesterday and it's exhausting me."

"First time?"

She gave him a look that had ended conversations with better men than him, and he had the grace to pretend not to notice.

* * *

They walked east through the afternoon, keeping to the dry wash where the ground held no hoofprints worth reading. Ada set the pace—fast enough to make distance, slow enough not to kill a man whose ribs had met a boot that morning. Ilya complained exactly once, quietly, when he thought she wasn't listening, and then stopped complaining entirely, which told her something about him. Men who complained loudly wanted sympathy. Men who swallowed it wanted to be somewhere else fast, and were calculating how.

"You're limping on your right side," she said, without turning around.

"I'm walking with character on my right side."

"Your ribs are cracked. Two of them, maybe three. If we have to run, you'll fall behind, and I don't leave people behind. It's in my contract." She paused. "It used to be in my contract."

"And now?"

"Now it's just a habit I haven't broken."

He was quiet for a few steps. "You could break it," he said. "With me. Nobody would blame you. I'm the one who stole—" He caught himself, glancing at her, recalibrating. "I'm the one they want."

"The one who stole what?" Ada said. "You still haven't finished that sentence. You said you took a ledger. Ledgers are paper. Paper is heavy to carry and worthless to sell. So either you're lying to me, or you're carrying something worth more than its weight in gold, and I've carried gold, and gold doesn't make consortiums send three men across forty miles of salt to retrieve it."

Ilya walked a while without answering. The wash narrowed around them, walls of baked clay the color of old bone, and his voice came back off them slightly flattened, as if the landscape itself were editing him.

"It makes sense to tell you," he said finally. "You're going to hear it eventually. Probably from them, worse."

"That's reassuring."

"I was an auditor." He said it flatly, the way a man says *I was married* or *I was young*. "Meridian Consortium, Karst Reach division. Four years. My job was to look at numbers and make sure they added up."

"And do they?"

"No." He laughed, but there was nothing in it. "That's rather the point of me. They add up beautifully. That's what frightened me. Deaths add up beautifully, if you keep the categories small. 'Equipment failure.' 'Weather event.' 'Worker error.' Line item, line item, line item. Forty-one over six years in the upper valley alone, and every single one signed off, verified, closed." His hand went to his chest, to the oilskin packet under his jacket, an unconscious gesture, the way a man touches a wound. "I signed some of those verifications myself."

Ada kept walking. Her boots crunched on salt-crusted gravel. Somewhere above them a hawk turned in the white heat, patient as arithmetic.

"So you stole the proof of your own crimes," she said.

"I stole the proof of the company's crimes. Mine are just the part I can't put down." He looked at her sideways. "You asked once what the debt was. Yours. You didn't answer, so I'll trade you: I'll tell you mine if you tell me yours. Fair weight."

"Nothing about you is fair weight," Ada said, but her voice came out less sharp than she intended, and he heard it, damn him, and smiled a small tired smile and let it go.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the wash as the light went copper and then violet. Ada built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand and heated water for tea that tasted mostly of tin. Ilya sat across from her, working a strip of dried meat with his back teeth, favoring his ribs.

"Here's my situation," Ada said, because she'd decided somewhere in the last mile that saying it out loud might make it true, or at least survivable. "I was contracted to deliver you alive to Meridian's regional office at Kell's Landing. Alive being the operative word, and even that they treated as negotiable this morning. I am no longer delivering you anywhere. What I am doing—" she turned her cup slowly, watching the tea leaves spin, "—is escorting you upriver to the survey station at Cold Hollow. There's an independent team there. Hydrologists, cartographers, a magistrate's observer. Crown-chartered, not company. They can verify a ledger."

"They can," Ilya said carefully. "If the station's still there."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I filed the requisitions for that station's supplies for two years. Last quarter, the supply shipments stopped appearing in the books. Not delayed. Stopped. And when shipments to a remote station stop appearing in the books, Ranger, it means one of two things. Either the station's gone, or somebody wants it gone and is paying to make the paperwork agree."

Ada felt the cold arrive in her stomach ahead of any reason for it. "Then we go anyway."

"That's not a plan, that's a stubbornness."

"I'm aware. I've built a career on the difference." She set down the cup. "Here's what I know. If I take you to Kell's Landing, you disappear into a processing van and the ledger disappears into a furnace, and whatever happened in that valley stays buried, and I get paid, and I go home, and I lie awake for the rest of my life listening to forty-one line items. If I take you to Cold Hollow and the station's standing, we get verification, we get witnesses, we get the truth into hands that aren't Meridian's. If the station's gone—" she shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than she showed, "—then we've learned something too, haven't we. We've learned how far upriver this goes."

Ilya studied her across the fire. The flames put moving light on his face and she noticed, not for the first time, that he had the kind of features that changed depending on the angle—honest-looking from one side, scheming from the other, and she genuinely couldn't tell which was the true profile.

"Why?" he said. "Genuinely. Why? You're paid by them. Your badge says them. This morning you watched them try to beat me to death in front of you and you—" he shook his head. "People don't turn. Not like that. Not for a stranger."

"I'm not turning," Ada said. "I'm renegotiating. There's a difference, and the difference is that I intend to bill someone for this."

The laugh came out of him before he could stop it, a real one, startled and rusty, and he winced and pressed his hand to his ribs and kept laughing anyway, quietly, helplessly, until tears stood in his eyes, and Ada found herself fighting the corner of her own mouth and losing.

"Oh, you're going to be trouble," she said.

"Me? I'm an auditor."

"Worse. Auditors think they're funny."

* * *

They talked terms by firelight, because Ada Nystrom did nothing without terms; it was the one religion she still practiced.

"One," she said, holding up a finger. "The ledger stays on your body or mine at all times. We don't separate from it, we don't sleep more than arm's length from it, and if it comes to running, you throw it to me and run lighter. Agreed?"

"Agreed. Two: nobody else sees it until Cold Hollow. Not boat crews, not ferrymen, not friendly strangers. Especially friendly strangers."

"You've met friendly strangers on this river?"

"I've met everything on this river," Ilya said darkly. "Three: if we reach Cold Hollow and the team verifies the ledger, it goes to them. All of it. I don't keep copies, I don't negotiate with it, I don't use it as leverage. It stops being mine the moment it becomes evidence."

Ada raised an eyebrow. "That's a strange thing for a thief to say."

"I told you. I'm not a thief anymore. I'm a witness with excellent filing skills." He hesitated, and then, quieter: "Four. When it's done—if it's done—you let me walk. No bounty, no warrant, no following me to the edge of the map to finish the job proper. Whatever I was, I pay for it by doing this. That's the whole price. That's all I have."

The fire popped. Down the wash, night wind moved through the saltbush with a sound like slow applause.

"That fourth term isn't mine to grant," Ada said. "There are warrants with names on them. There's a company with lawyers."

"There's a company with lawyers," Ilya agreed, "and a ranger who watched those lawyers' employees try to drown me in a horse trough this morning, and chose otherwise. You've already granted it, Ada. You granted it when you stood between me and the boot. I'm just asking you to say it out loud so I can sleep tonight."

She looked at him a long time. She thought about her brother, and did not say so, and the not-saying filled her chest like water fills a hull, silently, below the line.

"If the ledger checks out," she said at last, "if it's real, if it says what you say it says—then yes. You walk. I'll even point you toward the softest border crossing I know. But if you've lied to me, Voss, if any part of this is theater—"

"Then you're contractually entitled to shoot me," he said. "Put it in writing. I'll sign. I'm very good at signing things. It's practically my tragedy."

* * *

She woke once in the black hours, the way rangers do, some internal bell tolling, and found Ilya sitting up against the wash wall, awake, the oilskin packet in his lap, looking east toward the river in the dark as though he could see it.

"Can't sleep?" she murmured.

"Sleeping's fine. Waking up is the hard part." He didn't look over. "Do you hear that?"

She listened. Wind. Saltbush. The far-off complaint of a night bird. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried down the dry air from the west: thunder. Long, rolling, wrong-seasoned thunder, out of a sky with no clouds.

"No storm out here," she said slowly.

"No," Ilya agreed. "But there's rain up in the Reach this time of year. Monsoon season starts early in the high valleys. Which means the river's going to rise, and rise fast, and every ferryman between here and Cold Hollow is about to get very rich or very drowned." He finally looked at her, and in the starlight his expression was unreadable, both profiles at once. "We need to be on that river tomorrow, Ada. Before it rises. Before the checkpoints close. Before whoever owns that second dust column decides we're worth more than the paperwork of catching us properly."

Ada lay back and watched the stars wheel and listened to the impossible thunder rolling down out of mountains she couldn't see, and thought: *the river, the leaking boats, the strangers, the rising water.* Every instinct she owned told her she was walking out of the law and into the flood.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "Up river. Cold Hollow."

"Upriver," Ilya agreed softly, and settled back against the clay wall, the ledger held to his chest like a sleeping child, while the thunder came on, patient and enormous, out of the dark heart of the Karst Reach.
