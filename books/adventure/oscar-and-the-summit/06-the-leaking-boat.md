## 6. The Leaking Boat

The river town of Pell's Landing smelled like tar, wet rope, and the particular despair of a place that had given up on being anywhere important. Ada stood at the top of the mud-slick stairs with her pack on one shoulder and Ilya Voss standing a careful three feet to her left — close enough that anyone watching would assume they were traveling together, far enough that she could reach her knife without stretching.

"You're staring," Ilya said.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring *assessingly*. There's a difference, and the difference is that you're doing it at me."

She was, in fairness, doing it at him. Three days out from the well where she'd cornered him, and she still hadn't decided what he was. A thief who talked too much. An auditor who'd gone soft, if his story was true. A man who'd watched Consortium agents put a boot in his ribs and hadn't blamed *her* for standing there, which was either forgiveness or something worse.

"Boats," she said, by way of explanation, and nodded down at the landing.

There were four. A government ferry flying the Meridian colors, which was out for reasons that began with the word *checkpoint* and ended with the word *processing*. A grain barge whose captain wanted more coin than Ada had seen in a year. Two fishing skiffs whose owners laughed before she finished asking.

And then there was the boat at the far end of the dock, tied up with the casual incompetence of someone who didn't care whether it stayed or went. It was maybe forty feet of riverboat that had been old when Ada's mother was young, deck planks silvered and split, a wheelhouse like a tin hat, and a name painted on the stern in letters that had faded to a rumor: *OSCAR'S PRIDE*.

"Oscar," Ilya read aloud. "Who's Oscar?"

"No idea." Ada started down the stairs. "Probably long dead. Like our chances of dry socks."

* * *

The woman at the rail was mending a net with the economy of someone who had done ten thousand knots and hated every one of them equally. She was fifty-ish, built like a dock piling, with gray shot through her black hair and a scar running from her left ear down into her collar, neat as a signature. She looked up once, took them both in — Ada's ranger coat, Ilya's city boots, the way they stood relative to each other — and went back to her net.

"We need passage upriver," Ada said. "As far as you go."

"I don't go far."

"The Karst Reach."

The knotting stopped. The woman set down the net, folded her hands, and gave them the full weight of her attention, which was considerable. "The Reach is consortium water now. Checkpoints every thirty miles. Patrols. You want passage into the Reach, you're not tourists."

"No," Ada agreed.

"You're running from them or toward them."

Ada felt Ilya shift beside her and put a hand back, lightly, against his chest. *Stay out of this.* "Does it matter?"

"It matters for what you pay." The woman rose. Up close she was taller than she'd seemed, and her eyes were the flat brown of river water in flood. "Running *from*, I charge double, because they follow. Running *toward*—" she shrugged, "—I charge triple, because nobody comes back to complain about the price."

"That's extortion."

"That's commerce." She stuck out a hand. "Sefa."

"Ada Nystrom." They shook. Sefa's grip was a formal apology delivered in advance.

"And him?"

"My brother," Ada said.

Sefa looked at Ilya. Ilya, who could not lie convincingly to save his life and had proven it repeatedly over three days of desert, said, "We have very different mothers."

A silence. Then Sefa snorted — an actual, unguarded snort, quickly strangled — and turned away. "Triple rate. Half now. You sleep on deck, you eat what I eat, and if my boat starts sinking, you bail first and ask questions after."

"Why would it start sinking?" Ilya asked.

Sefa paused at the wheelhouse door. For the first time, something moved behind the flat brown eyes — not quite humor, not quite apology. "Because she always does."

* * *

*Ilya*

The boat leaked.

This was not a metaphor, which Ilya found almost refreshing after a week of things that were. The *Oscar's Pride* leaked from the bow, from the stern, from a seam midships that Sefa had named — named! — "the Weep," and from a spot beneath the engine housing that produced water at a rate Sefa described as "seasonal."

"It's seasonal?" Ilya crouched by the offending plank, watching a bead of river swell and fall, swell and fall, patient as arithmetic.

"Rains upstream, she weeps more. Dry season, she sulks." Sefa didn't look up from her engine. She'd been under it since dawn, arms buried to the elbow in grease, talking to Ilya over her shoulder as though he were furniture that happened to be useful. "You're the accountant."

"Auditor."

"Same animal, different teeth." She passed him a bucket without looking. "Numbers man. So run me numbers. This boat takes on—" she rattled off a figure, "—per hour at cruising speed. Bilge pump moves half that when it feels like moving anything. How long before we swim?"

Ilya did the math. It wasn't hard math. It was, in fact, the kind of math that made you want to sit down, and he was already sitting. "Two days. Maybe less if the pump has opinions."

"Good." She emerged from under the engine holding a gasket like a trophy fish. "That means I only have to worry about it tomorrow."

"That's not— that's not how worry works."

"Spoken like a man who's never owned a boat." She stood, cracked her back in three distinct movements, and finally looked at him straight on. Up close, in daylight, the scar was older than he'd guessed — twenty years, maybe more, the skin around it weathered past any chance of fading. "Your sister says you're going to the survey station in the high valley."

"She's not my—" He stopped. "Yes."

"Independent team? Hydrologists, two of them, a cartographer, a kid who ran the radio?" Something in her voice had changed. Not warmer. Sharper, the way ice is sharper than water.

"You know them."

"I know *of* them." Sefa wiped her hands on a rag that was itself beyond saving. "Everyone on this river knows of them. Last honest instruments between here and the glacier. Which is why your sister wants to reach them, I expect, and why the consortium wants them quiet." She tossed him the rag. "Make yourself useful. And when the Weep gets ambitious tonight, you're on first watch."

He bailed. That was the shape of his days now: a man who had once signed off on the deaths of forty-three people across eleven falsified manifests, hauling river water out of a rotting boat by the bucketful, and finding — this was the part that unsettled him most — a strange, scrubbing cleanliness in it. Water came in. He threw it out. Cause, effect. No signatures required.

Ada appeared at dusk and watched him work for a while without comment, leaning on the rail with her arms crossed, her broken compass open in her palm like a dead beetle she couldn't stop examining.

"You're smiling," she said finally. "At the leak."

"I'm smiling at the simplicity." He heaved another bucket over the side. "Water comes in. Water goes out. Nobody asks me to certify that the water was never in the boat to begin with."

She considered that. "That's bleak, Voss."

"That's *restful*."

* * *

*Ada*

The trouble with rivers, Ada decided on the second night, was that they gave you nothing to do. In the desert, travel was a full-time occupation — reading sand, rationing sweat, watching the horizon for the shimmer that meant trouble or rain. On the *Oscar's Pride*, the river did all the work while she sat in the bow like cargo and listened to the boat argue with itself.

It argued constantly. Timbers groaned, the engine coughed, the Weep seethed, and somewhere below decks something knocked in a rhythm that Sefa claimed was "the rudder telling jokes" and refused to elaborate on.

"You get used to it," Sefa said, materializing beside her with two cups of something that smelled like bark boiled in regret. "The noise. First night everyone thinks she's dying. By the third night it's a lullaby."

"What is she dying *of*, though? Honestly."

"Honestly?" Sefa handed her a cup. "Everything. She's held together with paint and stubbornness. But she's faster than she looks, she draws less water than anything else on this river, and there's no record of her in any registry I've cared about since—" She stopped. Sipped. "Since a while."

"Since the scar?"

The look Sefa gave her could have chilled beer. But then, unexpectedly, she answered. "Twenty-two years ago there was a valley upriver called Sen-Maru. Mining town. Good town. My sister ran the assay office there." She said it flatly, the way you'd read coordinates. "One winter the company needed the claims cleared for a new buyer. There was a fire. Official finding: faulty lamp. Eleven dead." Her thumb moved once along the scar, unconscious, a habit worn smooth as river stone. "My sister was the twelfth. She didn't burn. She drowned, two months later, swimming out to a boat she thought was leaving with evidence aboard. The river kept her."

Ada held her cup and said nothing, because there was nothing that wasn't either too small or too large.

"So when a ranger and her 'brother' ask me to run consortium water toward a station full of people who check other people's numbers," Sefa finished, "I say yes. Not for you. For the ledger I'm assuming he's carrying, because no one runs *toward* the Reach for the scenery." She drained her cup. "When this is done, whatever's in that book burns. That's my price. Not coin. Ash."

"And if what's in it needs to survive? If people need to see it?"

Sefa stood, unhurried, and looked down the black ribbon of the river toward the mountains she could not yet see. "Then you'd better make sure seeing it is worth what it costs. Because it always costs." She paused at the wheelhouse door. "First watch is yours. Wake me at moonset. And Nystrom — if your brother snores, I'm selling him at Kell's Landing."

"He doesn't snore."

"He snores," said Ilya's voice, muffled, from the pile of netting that was his bed. "Like a landslide with grievances."

* * *

*Ilya*

On the third day the mountains arrived.

They came all at once, the way mountains do — one bend of the river and suddenly the world tilted upward, blue-gray shoulders rising through the haze, white crowns hidden in cloud. The Karst Reach opened ahead of them, a canyon of limestone so pale it looked bleached, the river threading through it green and fast.

Ilya stood at the rail and felt something in his chest do a thing he refused to name. He'd spent six years behind desks looking at these mountains on maps, on surveys, on manifests where villages appeared as line items and rivers as transport costs. Seeing them actual size was like hearing a piece of music you'd only ever read as notation.

"There." Ada pointed. "Smoke. That'll be the first checkpoint — Kell's Landing, maybe eight miles."

"Eight miles," Ilya repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "Which means papers, which means questions, which means a man with a clipboard comparing faces to a list, and my face is on several lists, Ada. Several. I've *seen* the lists. I used to *maintain* one of them—"

"Breathe."

"—and the photo they use is terrible, but it's terrible in a recognizable way—"

"*Breathe.*" She caught his sleeve, and he stopped, and realized he was gripping the rail hard enough to whiten his knuckles. Below them the river ran on, indifferent, carrying them anyway.

"Here's what happens," Ada said, low and even, the voice she must have used on panicked travelers and wounded animals alike. "Sefa handles the approach. I handle the talking. You handle the bilge."

"The bilge."

"The bilge is dark, it smells terrible, and no inspector on this river has ever voluntarily entered a bilge. Congratulations, Voss. You've been promoted to smelliest man aboard."

He looked at her. She looked back, and there — under the ranger-flat calm — was the ghost of a smile, the first he'd ever caught her wearing, gone almost before it landed.

"That's it?" he said. "That's the plan? I hide in the wet hole?"

"That's the plan. It's a good plan. Plans don't need to be clever, they need to *work*." She released his sleeve and turned toward the wheelhouse. "Besides, you should be flattered. It's the same plan I'd use for contraband."

Ahead, the smoke thickened. Somewhere beyond the bend, a whistle sounded — two short blasts, the universal grammar of *heave to and show papers* — and Sefa's voice drifted back from the wheelhouse, laconic as ever:

"Voss. Bilge. Now."

He went. At the hatch he paused, one leg down into the dark and the diesel-and-river stink of the hold, and heard Ada behind him say, quietly, to Sefa:

"How much time do we buy if this goes wrong?"

"None," said Sefa. "So let's make sure it doesn't."

The hatch closed over Ilya's head, and the *Oscar's Pride*, leaking faithfully, carried them all toward the guns at Kell's Landing.
