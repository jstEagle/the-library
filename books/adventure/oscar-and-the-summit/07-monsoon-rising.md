## 7. Monsoon Rising

The storm announced itself the way a debt collector does — politely, once, and then all at once.

Ada felt it first in her teeth. She was on deck, wedged between a crate of tarps and the rail, watching the sky over the western ridge go the color of a bruise that had been pressed too long. The river had gone glassy and strange, the way it did when the air above it stopped moving entirely. Even the birds had shut up. Somewhere behind them, faint as a held breath, thunder cleared its throat.

"Barometer's falling like my opinion of this boat," Sefa said from the wheelhouse doorway. She had a tin cup of something steaming and a look on her face that Ada was learning to read: not fear exactly, but the professional resignation of a woman who had done this before and intended to do it again, complainingly.

"How bad?" Ada asked.

"Bad enough." Sefa sipped. "The *Oscar's Pride* has two speeds in weather — sinking slowly and sinking quickly. We're going to find out which one tonight."

From below came a muffled thump, then another, then Ilya Voss's voice drifting up through the deck boards, indignant: "There is *water* down here."

"Yes," Sefa called back, without raising her voice much. "That's the bilge. It collects things. Water. Regret. Occasionally shoes."

"It's collecting it very *quickly*—"

"That'll be the leak."

* * *

It started raining an hour after sunset, and by midnight it wasn't rain anymore so much as the sky giving up on the distinction between itself and the river.

Ada had been in storms before. Desert storms were theater — wind, sand, a lot of shouting, and then it was over and you dug yourself out and complained about your sinuses for a week. This was different. This was a storm with *patience*. It settled over the Karst Reach like something intending to stay, hammering the deck, erasing the banks, turning the world down to the size of the boat and then smaller, until the boat was just the wheelhouse and the lantern swinging in it and the two figures bailing in the dark.

Sefa navigated blind. There was no other way to put it. The channel markers were invisible, the banks were invisible, the sky and the river had merged into one howling gray nothing, and Sefa stood at the wheel with her collar up and her hat tied down and read the water by feel — the lean of the current against the hull, the sound the rain made where the shallows boiled white, some private arithmetic she ran behind her eyes without ever appearing to think about it.

"Hard to starboard," she said, to no one, and turned the wheel, and the boat heeled, and somewhere below Ada heard the crash of everything not lashed down rearranging itself.

Ada was in the hold.

She'd gone down an hour ago to check on their stowaway-turned-passenger and found him fighting the river alone with a bucket that held perhaps four liters and an expression of furious optimism. She hadn't asked permission. She'd just taken off her coat, hung it on the nail, picked up the second bucket, and started bailing.

Ilya had looked at her for a long moment across the sloshing dark.

"You don't have to," he said.

"No," she agreed, and kept bailing.

They worked out a rhythm without discussing it. He filled; she threw. She filled; he threw. The water went over the side and came back through the leak and went over the side again, a war against an enemy with infinite reinforcements, and there was something almost meditative about it, if you didn't think about what it meant. Ada thought about what it meant constantly. She simply declined to say so.

"The pump," Ilya shouted over the drumming overhead, "is decorative."

"I noticed."

"I checked it this morning. It made a noise like a dying accordion and then stopped making noises altogether."

"Sefa knows."

"She says it's 'character.'"

"She would." Ada threw another bucketful through the scupper. "This whole boat is held together by character. Character and rust."

He laughed — short, surprised out of him, half-swallowed by the storm — and Ada pretended not to notice that the sound did something inconvenient to her chest. She was paid to deliver this man. That was the entire shape of their relationship: bounty and bounty-ee, escort and escorted, a contract with a signature on it and a number attached. Anything else was weather. And there was plenty of weather.

* * *

Around two in the morning the boat hit something.

Not hard — a glancing kiss off a submerged log or a sandbar's shoulder — but enough to make the hull groan in a new register, a lower note, and enough to bring Sefa's voice down through the boards, calm as a woman reading a grocery list:

"We just got a second leak. Congratulations. You've been promoted."

Ada got the lamp and they found it together: a seam near the keel weeping a thin silver thread of river, patient as anything. Ilya knelt over it with the lamp while Ada rummaged through Sefa's stores — a miracle of chaos, rope and tar and tinned fish and a jar of pickled eggs whose vintage she chose not to investigate — until she came up with a brick of pitch, a roll of canvas, and a mallet.

"Hold the canvas flat," she said. "I'll set the pitch."

"You know how to do this?"

"I grew up on a dry riverbed full of broken boats people kept meaning to fix." She warmed the pitch over the little spirit stove, watched it soften, go glossy. "My brother could patch anything. Said a leak was just a boat asking for attention."

The words were out before she could weigh them. Ilya glanced up, and she saw the question form behind his eyes — the careful, auditor's question, the one about the brother and the company and the contract — and she saw him decide, visibly, not to ask it. Not tonight. Not down here in the dark with the river trying to get in.

"Press," she said instead, and he pressed, and she poured the pitch along the seam, and the mallet drove the canvas home, and the thread of silver slowed, thinned, stopped.

For a moment they both just knelt there, listening to it hold.

"Well," Ilya said. "Your brother sounds like he'd have hated this boat."

"He'd have loved this boat. That's worse." Ada sat back on her heels. "People who love broken things end up responsible for them."

"Is that how you ended up responsible for me?"

The lantern swung. Rain hammered. Somewhere above, Sefa swore at the river in three languages, two of which Ada suspected she'd invented.

"I haven't decided yet," Ada said, "what you are."

"An investment," Ilya offered. "A liability. A man who is currently very good at holding canvas."

"A thief."

"I stole a ledger, Nystrom. Nobody's ever been injured by a ledger. They're the least dangerous thing in the world right up until someone reads them." He said it lightly, but his hands weren't light; they were flat and steady on the patched seam, holding it the way you hold something closed that wants to open. "Read it sometime. That's all I keep asking. Just — read it."

"Not tonight."

"No," he agreed. "Tonight we bail."

* * *

They bailed until dawn, or until the thing that replaced dawn — a gray suggestion of light, the storm still leaning its whole weight on the river. They learned each other's rhythms the way you learn anything under duress: fast, completely, and slightly against your will. She knew now that he counted under his breath when he was tired, in a language she didn't recognize, and that he sped up when she sped up without being asked, and that he had a scar on his left forearm he touched absently every time the hull groaned, like checking on an old wound of the boat's rather than his own.

He knew that she hummed — barely, tunelessly, a habit she'd deny under oath — and that she always threw the last bucket in a bucket-line even when it was his turn, because she didn't like leaving work unfinished, and that when she was frightened she got quieter, not louder, which he privately filed away as the most frightening thing about her.

At some point Sefa came down the ladder, soaked to the skin, took one look at the patched seam, the two exhausted people kneeling in six inches of water, and the general state of the bilge, and said:

"Huh. Still floating."

"Barely," Ada said.

"Barely counts. Barely is how this boat has traveled its whole life." Sefa crouched, pressed a thumb against the patch, nodded once — the highest praise her face was built to produce — and handed down a thermos. "Drink. Both of you. If you die of exhaustion in my hold I'll have to write it up and I hate paperwork."

The tea inside was terrible — oversteeped, oversweetened, faintly of diesel — and it was the best thing Ada had ever tasted. She passed the cup to Ilya first and only realized afterward that she'd done it, that some instinct in her had reached for him before herself, and she covered it by being brisk about the second cup.

"Storm breaks by noon," Sefa said, standing. "We'll be at Kell's Landing by mid-afternoon. Which means—" she looked at Ilya, "—you should probably come up out of the bilge and stop smelling like one."

"What's at Kell's Landing?" Ilya asked.

Ada and Sefa exchanged a glance over his head. It was a brief exchange but a complete one, an entire negotiation conducted in the space of a heartbeat, and what it concluded was: *he doesn't know, and we're not telling him yet.*

"Fuel," Sefa said. "And paperwork."

"I hate paperwork," Ilya said.

"So does everyone worth knowing." Sefa climbed back up into the rain.

* * *

The storm broke mid-morning, all at once, like a door slamming open onto sunlight. The river lay steaming and swollen, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past them at a walking pace. The banks dripped. Birds resumed their arguments as though nothing had happened.

Ada stood at the rail drying her hair with a rag that smelled of pitch and pickled brine, and watched Ilya sleep.

He'd collapsed on the cargo tarp aft, mid-sentence, in the middle of telling her something about his mother's garden — the first personal fact he'd offered unprompted — and simply gone out like a lamp. His face in sleep was younger. Unguarded. She found she couldn't look away, and made herself look away, and failed, and gave up, and let herself have the moment the way you let yourself have one more log on the fire: knowingly, and paying for it later.

*You haven't decided what he is,* she reminded herself. *Remember that.*

But her hands remembered the rhythm of the buckets. Her hands remembered pressing canvas beside his hands. Her hands, apparently, had made some decision already and simply hadn't told her.

Ahead, around the long bend, the smoke of Kell's Landing rose thin and gray against the washed sky — woodsmoke, coal smoke, and beneath it something else, something industrial and wrong for a landing that size. Sefa appeared at her elbow with the tin cup and her laconic eyes fixed forward.

"Checkpoint's going to be thick today," she said. "Post-storm, they'll be bored, wet, and looking for somebody to bother. Papers out. Story straight." A pause. "And whatever happens up there—" she tipped her chin toward the sleeping thief, "—nobody looks at the bilge."

Ada nodded. Then, because the morning had softened something in her and honesty was cheaper before noon:

"Sefa. Last night. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank the boat." Sefa drained her cup, turned to go, and paused. "Though between us — the *Pride's* never floated that well with anybody bailing. Whatever you two did down there, the boat noticed." She walked away before Ada could decide whether that was a compliment, a warning, or a joke, and knowing Sefa, it was all three, delivered in the order listed.

Behind them the river ran high and fast, carrying them toward the guns at Kell's Landing, and ahead the smoke thickened, and the whistle sounded — two short blasts, *heave to and show papers* — and Ada went to wake the man in the bilge, because whatever happened next, they were going to need every pair of hands aboard.
