## 8. Checkpoint at Kell's Landing

The rain had been falling for two days in a way that Ada could only describe as personal.

Not the polite desert rain she knew, which arrived, apologized, and left before you'd finished being grateful for it. This was river rain — a gray, patient downpour that seemed to have signed some kind of contract with the sky and intended to honor it through the season. It drummed on the boat's cabin roof, ran in cold threads down the back of her collar, and turned the Karst Reach into a broad brown muscle of water flexing between walls of limestone.

"Cheerful, isn't it," Ilya said from the doorway of the cabin. He was holding two tin mugs of Sefa's tea, which tasted like boiled pine needles and optimism, and he offered one to Ada with the expression of a man who knew exactly how bad it was and respected her enough not to pretend otherwise.

"It's raining."

"It's *raining*," he agreed solemnly. "I said cheerful."

Ada took the mug anyway. Her hands were cold enough that the tin burned pleasantly. "Where are we?"

Sefa answered without turning from the wheel, her voice pitched low under the drum of rain, as if the river might be listening. "Kell's Landing. Two bends." She nodded ahead, where the channel narrowed between cliffs furred with wet green, and Ada saw what Sefa had already seen: a chain stretched across the water, sagging under its own collected rainwater, and beyond it a wooden platform built out from the bank like a dock's aggressive cousin. Lanterns burned under an awning despite the hour. Figures moved behind them.

Ada's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, like a stone going down a well.

She knew what a Consortium checkpoint looked like. She'd staffed three of them herself, back when her badge meant something and the worst thing she did all day was confiscate unlicensed fishing nets from men who needed those nets. This one had the same architecture — the chain, the platform, the lanterns arranged so that anyone approaching was lit up like a specimen while the inspectors stayed in shadow — but there were differences too. The Consortium flag hung limp and soaked. And there were more men than a routine checkpoint needed. Six, maybe seven. One of them held something long and barreled under an oilcloth.

"They're not checking cargo," Ada said quietly.

"No," Sefa said.

"They're looking for someone."

"Yes."

Ilya set his mug down on the chart table with a small ceramic click that sounded, in the quiet cabin, like a gunshot announcing itself politely in advance. "Well," he said. "This is fine."

"You stole from them," Ada said. "You knew they'd be watching the river."

"I knew they'd be watching the river *eventually*. I was hoping eventually would be after we arrived somewhere with witnesses and a magistrate who wasn't on their payroll." He rubbed his face. "In my defense, I have been wrong about several things lately, and I'm trying to learn from each one individually rather than all at once."

Sefa cut the engine. The silence that followed was enormous and wet. The boat began to drift toward the chain with the unhurried confidence of a thing that had nowhere better to be.

"Papers," Sefa said. It wasn't a question; it was an inventory. "Who has papers?"

"I have a ranger's warrant," Ada said. "Which is either very useful or very incriminating, depending on whether they've flagged me yet."

"Have they reason to?"

Ada thought about the message she'd sent from the last fuel stop — the one requesting verification of her contract, addressed not to Meridian's regional office but to the independent survey station upriver. The one that would have been sitting in a Consortium relay office for two days now, being read by people whose job titles included words like *integrity* and *compliance* and whose actual jobs involved neither.

"Probably," she said.

"Wonderful," said Ilya.

"And you?" Sefa asked him.

"I have a very convincing mustache in my bag and no papers whatsoever."

"Do you actually have a mustache?"

"I have a mustache and a hat and a set of documents identifying me as a grain inspector named Oskar Pell, who is real, exists, works out of Millbrook, and has the misfortune of resembling me if you squint and have recently been hit on the head."

Ada stared at him. "How do you have that?"

"Because I made it," Ilya said, "when I was still employed by the people now searching this river for me. Auditors get travel documents. It's part of the indignity package." He paused. "Also I want it noted that my fake name is Oskar, which I chose because it felt sturdy, and I will not be taking questions about it."

* * *

There was no time for a plan, so they had a conversation instead, which is what plans look like when they're born in a hurry.

Sefa would handle the money. She had done this before — her face went flat and bored in a way that suggested the memory of doing it was not a happy one — and she knew the landing master by reputation, a man called Dreele who had once been honest and had found the wages of honesty insufficient. Ada would stand beside him as the hired escort, warrant visible, mouth full of official-sounding nothing. And Ilya —

"Bilge," Sefa said.

"The bilge," Ilya repeated. He looked at the hatch in the deck with the expression of a man being introduced to a new business partner he already disliked. "The bilge where the water lives."

"The bilge where the water mostly lives," Sefa corrected. "There's a dry corner under the forward frame. Mostly."

"Mostly dry. Under a frame. In a hole." Ilya nodded slowly. "You know, when I imagined my life as a fugitive, there was considerably more riding at dawn and considerably less lying in boat soup."

"Get in the soup," Sefa said, and handed him a bucket. "If we take water, you bail. Quietly."

He got in the soup.

Ada helped him lower the hatch, and just before it closed he caught her wrist — not gripping, just touching, the way you touch a doorframe in a house you're not sure you'll see again. His eyes in the lamplight were doing something complicated.

"If this goes badly," he said, "the ledger goes with you. Not me. You understand? I'm replaceable. It isn't."

"That's a horrible thing to say about yourself."

"It's an accurate thing to say about myself. There's a difference. It's taken me years to learn it and I'd hate for it to go to waste."

Ada didn't answer, because the answer was *no*, because she had watched men say things like that before and then die anyway, and because her brother had been the last person to tell her something similar and she had failed him in a way she couldn't yet put into words. So instead she pressed the hatch down until it seated, and stood on it briefly, as if her weight alone could keep the world outside from getting in.

It couldn't. Nothing could. The boat drifted on.

* * *

Kell's Landing resolved out of the rain gradually, the way bad news does.

First the chain, dripping. Then the platform, where a man in a soaked Consortium slicker stood with a clipboard in a waterproof sleeve, radiating the specific authority of someone whose entire power derives from a piece of laminated plastic. Behind him, four others. The one with the oilcloth-wrapped barrel shifted his weight, and Ada catalogued him automatically — young, nervous, finger probably inside the trigger guard, the most dangerous kind of armed man there is.

Sefa brought the boat alongside with a bump soft as a handshake and looped a line over a cleat without being asked.

"Landing master Dreele?" she said, before anyone else could speak. Her voice had changed — flatter, slower, the voice of a woman for whom this was Tuesday. "Cargo's timber tools and salt. Three souls aboard. Papers are current."

Dreele consulted his clipboard with the ceremony of a priest consulting scripture. "Vessel registration."

Sefa produced it. Ada watched the exchange with the attention she usually reserved for tracking animals across broken ground — the angle of Dreele's shoulders, the flick of his eyes toward the cabin, the way the young man with the barrel took half a step forward and was stopped by nothing more than Dreele's boredom.

"Manifest says two souls," Dreele said.

"Manifest's old. Picked up a passenger at Bend Nine. Paid cash." Sefa produced coins from somewhere in her coat with the smoothness of long practice. "River folk don't file amendments, Dreele. You know that. Paperwork's for people with roofs."

Something passed between them — not friendship, but the tired recognition of two people who had both learned which rules were load-bearing and which were decorative. Dreele's hand closed around the coins and did not open again.

Then his eyes found Ada.

"And you are?"

Here it was. Ada stepped forward and let the rain run down her face, and produced her warrant, and heard herself speak in a voice she barely recognized — steady, clipped, faintly irritated, the voice of a woman with institutional weight behind her.

"Nystrom. Contracted escort. My client prefers discretion, which is why my passenger is belowdecks and why your man doesn't need to come look for him." She let that sit for exactly one beat too long, then added, with the weariness of the perpetually underpaid, "Unless Kell's Landing has started inspecting private security arrangements, in which case I'll need that in writing, because my client's lawyers eat small stations like this for breakfast and I'd hate for you to be the amuse-bouche."

It was a gamble dressed as a threat wearing a uniform. Ada had spent eleven years learning how officials talked — how they used boredom as armor, how they made other people feel like the ones wasting time. Now she spent it all in ninety seconds, every trick she'd ever resented, deployed against the institution that had taught her.

Dreele looked at the warrant. Looked at her face. Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe. Or the animal sense, common to men who take bribes, of another predator in the room.

"Contracted escort," he repeated.

"For a survey concern. Independent." She met his eyes and did not blink, because blinking was for people with something to hide, and she had decided, in the last thirty seconds, to be a person with nothing to hide, even though she was standing on a hatch beneath which a fugitive floated in bilgewater holding a bucket.

Behind Dreele, the young man with the barrel said, "Sir, the bulletin said—"

"The bulletin," Dreele said, without turning, "says a great many things. The bulletin is not paid to think." But his eyes came back to Ada, and stayed, and the rain filled the silence with static.

Ada understood, in that moment, with terrible clarity, what she was doing. Not the performance — the performance was easy, the performance was just her old self turned inside out like a glove. What she understood was the shape underneath it. She was lying to a lawman while harboring a fugitive, on a bribed boat, carrying stolen property, and every word out of her mouth was a brick in the wall of a new life she hadn't chosen so much as backed into, step by step, each one reasonable, each one defensible, until here she stood on the far side of a line she never remembered crossing.

*I'm the criminal now,* she thought, and was surprised to find that the thought didn't panic her. It just sat there, cold and clear as the rain. Somewhere behind it, quieter, another thought stirred: *and the men on this platform work for the people who killed my brother's crew, and nobody on this river calls them criminals at all.*

The law, it turned out, was a matter of paperwork. She'd known that for years. She'd simply never been on the wrong side of the filing system before.

Dreele handed back the warrant. "Passage granted. Keep your speed down past the narrows — water's high." A pause. "Ranger Nystrom." He said her name carefully, tasting it, and Ada felt the hook go in — he knew the name, or would know it soon, or had already sent it upriver in his head. "Safe travels."

"Always," said Ada, who had never once traveled safely in her life.

* * *

They didn't cast off. Not immediately. That was the trap she only saw afterward — the protocol of it, the way Dreele insisted on walking the length of the deck himself, boots ringing on wet planks, glancing into the cabin, pausing at the forward hatch.

The bilge hatch.

Ada's heart went from resting pace to gallop in the space of a single footstep. She positioned herself casually against the rail, between Dreele and the hatch, and launched into conversation — anything, everything, the narrows, the water level, the scandalous price of lamp oil at Bend Twelve — and Dreele answered in monosyllables while his eyes kept sliding sideways, and Sefa appeared at the cabin door with a kettle and a voice like gravel and offered him tea, and the whole thing became a strange slow dance of three people pretending, and one man under the deck holding his breath in six inches of river water.

Dreele stopped at the hatch. Looked down at it.

"Forward storage?" he asked.

"Chain locker," Sefa said. "Fouls constantly. You don't want to see it."

"I believe you," Dreele said, and Ada could not tell — could genuinely not tell — whether he believed her, or whether he had decided that whatever was under that hatch was worth more to him undisturbed than reported, or whether he was simply a tired man in the rain who wanted this boat gone before the bulletin updated. Corruption, Ada reflected, was a fog: you could never see exactly where it ended, only that it was everywhere and touched everything.

He walked back down the gangplank. The chain dropped. The engine woke with a cough and a grumble, and Kell's Landing slid backward into the rain, lanterns shrinking to smudges, the young man with the barrel watching them go with the fixed stare of someone memorizing a hull number.

Ada stayed on deck until the landing was gone. Then she went below, and opened the hatch, and hauled Ilya up by both arms like a drowned cat.

He emerged streaming, shivering, clutching his bucket, with bilge water in his hair and an expression of profound philosophical grievance.

"There was a fish," he announced.

"A what?"

"A fish. In the bilge. With me. For forty minutes. We came to an understanding." He sneezed violently. "Its name is Gerald. Gerald stays."

"Gerald does not stay."

"Gerald and I have been through something together, Ada."

She wrapped a blanket around his shoulders before she could stop herself — her hands deciding before her pride could intervene — and he went quiet under it, looking at her with the rain-light behind him, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

"You lied well," he said finally. Softly. "Back there. The lawyer line. The *amuse-bouche*."

"I learned from professionals."

"Was it strange?" he asked. "Being on that side of it?"

Ada looked out the cabin window at the brown river, the rain, the country ahead full of drowned villages and erased names and a valley that ate them. She thought about her badge in her pocket, which weighed exactly what it had always weighed and somehow much more.

"No," she said, and was surprised to find it was true. "That's the strange part."

From the wheel, Sefa spoke without turning, her voice cutting clean through the drum of rain.

"Two days to the survey station," she said. "Weather's turning worse. And Nystrom—" a pause, the wheel creaking under her hands, "—that man knew your name before you gave it. Whatever you sent upriver, somebody read it. Somebody's answering."

Ada's hand went to her pocket, to the warrant that had gotten them through, and found it suddenly felt less like a shield and more like a leash.

Ahead, through the gray curtain, the first lightning of the evening flickered over the high country — silent, distant, like a signal no one had asked for.
