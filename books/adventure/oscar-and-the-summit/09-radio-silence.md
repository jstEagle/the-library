## 9. Radio Silence

The river changed its mind three times before the survey station appeared.

First it ran brown and fat with monsoon melt, pushing them along at a pace that made Sefa hum—three notes, over and over, like a woman thinking in a language with no words. Then it widened into a slack brown lake where the current forgot itself, and the boat drifted while Ilya took the bail bucket and worked it in slow, hypnotic strokes, water whispering out over the gunwale, rain whispering in. Then the river narrowed again, gathered itself, and turned north into a gorge so steep the morning sun didn't reach the water until nearly ten.

Ada stood at the bow with her collar up and her eyes moving. That was most of her job, really, when you stripped it down: eyes moving, always, in the pattern her first instructor at the Ranger Academy had drilled into them until it became breathing. Near, middle, far. Near, middle, far. She'd done it so long she sometimes caught herself doing it at dinner, reading the room the way she read a ridgeline.

Near: the bilge, still seeping, but slower since Toma's pitch—no, not Toma, that came later, that was Sefa's own tar and canvas, worked in during a miserable hour at a shingle beach while Ilya held the lantern and complained about the smell in the polite, precise way he complained about everything, as though filing a grievance with the universe's customer service department.

Middle: the gorge walls, gray limestone riddled with caves, some of them stacked with old nesting platforms, the kind river people built a hundred years ago and abandoned when the companies came through buying up the water rights.

Far: a thread of smoke.

She watched it a long time before she said anything. Smoke could mean a hundred harmless things. A cook fire. A signal. A survey team drying its socks, because in Ada's experience survey teams spent approximately forty percent of their professional lives drying their socks.

But the smoke was wrong. It rose straight and thin and gray, and it came from the wrong place—not the station's kitchen chimney, which according to the chart Sefa kept rolled in an oilcloth should have sat on the bluff above the water, but from down among the boathouses, low, where nobody cooked.

"Sefa," she said.

"I see it."

"Chart says the station has a generator."

"Chart also says this river has fish." Sefa adjusted the tiller a half-inch. "I have not seen a fish since Kell's Landing. Draw your own conclusions."

Ilya stopped bailing. "You think something's wrong at the station."

Ada didn't answer immediately, which was its own answer, and she saw him understand that. He set the bucket down with the carefulness of a man who had learned, somewhere in his past, that sudden movements got noticed. She was starting to build a picture of where he'd learned it, and the picture troubled her.

"Options," she said. "We can run past and keep going. We can land and look. We can land, look, and be ready to leave fast."

"Option three," Sefa said immediately. "Option one wastes the only chance we'll get to resupply before the Reach. Option two is how you end up in a shallow grave with your boots on the wrong feet."

"You've given this a lot of thought."

"I've given everything a lot of thought. It's why I'm old."

"You're not old," Ilya said, and Sefa gave him a look that contained, in compressed form, an entire philosophy about the kindness of the young and its uses.

They took the boat in slow, along the eastern shore, where a shingle spit ran out toward the station dock. The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist that made everything look like a half-finished sketch of itself. Ada counted structures: the main house on its bluff, two boathouses below, a drying rack, a mast for the radio antenna—

The mast was down.

Not broken. Down. Lowered on its hinge, the way you'd leave it if you were doing maintenance, except the guy-wires had been cut and coiled with a neatness that made her stomach tighten. Nobody doing maintenance coils the wires they've cut. Nobody doing maintenance lowers the antenna and then lets the cook fire drift gray smoke from the wrong chimney.

"Somebody wanted this station to look alive from the river," she said quietly.

"Somebody succeeded," Sefa said, "for about four days. Smoke's the wrong color. Wood smoke's blue-gray when it's fresh; that's damp coal. Station's got a generator and a wood stove. That's coal from the company barges." She spat over the side, a small economical gesture of contempt. "Company men don't know how a survey station smells. They know how it looks."

Ada looked at her. "You've done this before."

"I've been alive before," Sefa said. "Several times. Tie off at the third piling. If anything happens, I cast off and you swim for the boat. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Ada said.

"I was talking to him," Sefa said, nodding at Ilya. "You strike me as the type who'd swim for the station."

Ada was, in fact, the type. She didn't say so.

* * *

The dock gave under their boots with a soft, rotten complaint. Ada went first, pistol out and low, Ilya three steps behind her holding the boat hook like it was a very inadequate sword, Sefa staying with the boat because Sefa always stayed with the boat; it was the one promise she seemed to consider binding.

The main house door stood open six inches. In the mist, the gap was black.

Ada pushed it with two fingers.

Inside, the station had been turned inside out by people who were looking for something specific. Not ransacked—ransacking was chaos, and this wasn't chaos. Drawers opened and their contents fanned across the floor in neat fans. Books pulled from shelves and riffled, spines cracked, set back in the wrong order. The survey charts on the drafting table unrolled, weighted at the corners with coffee mugs, every contour line of the upper valley traced and retraced in pencil by somebody who'd been very interested in the glacier approach.

Somebody had been looking for a ledger.

"They didn't find it," Ilya said behind her. His voice had gone flat, the voice of a man reading his own obituary in advance. "It's here. Or it was. This is what a search looks like when it fails and the searcher is angry but disciplined."

"How do you know what a failed search looks like?"

"Professional interest," he said, and she let it go, because there was no time and because she was starting to keep a private list of his professional interests and the list was getting strange.

The radio was against the east wall, under the window. It had been a good set—government surplus, the kind with brass fittings that outlived their owners. Someone had taken a fire iron to it. Not smashed in rage; dismantled in method. The valves were cracked one by one and laid in a row. The transformer had been cut out entirely and was missing. The antenna lead was severed at the wall plate.

"They took the transformer," Ada said. "You don't take a transformer unless you're worried someone repairs the set."

"Thorough," Ilya said.

"Expensive," Ada said. "That's the tell. Rage smashes. Worry dismantles. Whoever did this is worried about a specific message getting out." She crouched, touched the brass. Cold, but not long-cold. "Day. Maybe two."

The kitchen chimney smoke, she realized, was coming from the boathouse.

* * *

The survivor was in the second boathouse, under a canvas tarp behind a stack of empty fuel drums, and Ada nearly shot him, because what her eyes found first was a hand—just a hand, reaching out from under the canvas, fingers black with grease—and her body made the decision before her mind caught up. She redirected the muzzle into the ceiling at the last possible moment, and the shot cracked the boathouse air and dropped a fine rain of dust and old spider silk, and then they were all three of them frozen, listening to the river, listening to the gorge, listening to nothing.

Nobody came. That was almost worse.

The man under the tarp was maybe fifty, bearded, in the gray wool of the Survey Service, and he had a fever on him like a coat. His lips were cracked, his eyes were bright and wrong, and when Ada pulled the canvas back he flinched from her the way you flinch from a door slamming.

"Easy," she said. "Ranger. Ada Nystrom. You're safe."

"No such thing," he said, with the mild precision of the delirious, "no such thing as safe, only unrecorded, only unwritten, only—"

"What's your name?"

He looked at her then, and for a moment the fever receded like a tide, and underneath it was something so frightened and so lucid that Ada felt it in her teeth.

"Osk," he said. "Surveyor, second class. Osk Lindqvist." Then the tide came back in. "They came at night. No lights. No lights at all, that's how you know, that's how you always know—company boats run dark, they run dark up here because the dark is theirs, they bought the dark—" He grabbed her wrist with shocking strength. "Did you come by the river? Did anyone see you come?"

"Nobody," Ada lied gently.

"Good. Good." He sagged. "They took the others. Took them downriver, said it was for questioning, said it was procedure. Nine of us. Nine." He held up both hands, then seemed confused by the number of his own fingers. "I got into the water. The water doesn't write names. The water doesn't—"

"Osk." Ada kept her voice low and even, the voice she used on panicked hikers and spooked livestock, which were more similar than the Academy admitted. "Who took them? Names. Company name."

He laughed. It was a terrible sound, small and dry, like paper tearing. "Names. That's the joke. That's the whole joke. They're buying the names. You can't be disappeared if you were never written down. The magistrate at Kell's Landing—bought. The district registrar—bought. The survey manifests—burned and reissued. There's a valley up there—" his hand rose, trembling, pointing north, upriver, up-gorge, toward the white thread of the glacier visible between the walls like a scar of light— "there's a valley up there that eats names. You go in and the maps forget you. The registries forget you. Your own mother gets a letter in a stranger's handwriting saying her son died in a boating accident and here is a modest settlement, please don't inquire."

Ilya had gone very still beside her. Ada could feel the stillness coming off him like cold.

"Osk," she said. "The ledger. A survey ledger. Was it here?"

Something moved behind his eyes. The fever receded again, and this time what was underneath was cunning, and the cunning was exhausted and desperate and human.

"Everyone wants the ledger," he whispered. "The company wants it. The man who came through on the barge wanted it, the one with the gray coat, he showed me a photograph, asked had I seen the man in the photograph, and the man in the photograph—" Osk's eyes slid sideways, to Ilya, and back. "The man in the photograph is standing in this boathouse."

Ada didn't move. She was aware of her own heartbeat, slow and loud, and of Ilya beside her, not breathing.

"What did you tell him?" she asked.

"I told him I'd never seen him." Osk's cracked lips shaped something that was almost a smile. "I'm a surveyor. I know how to read a man. The company man had a list of questions. The man in the photograph had a face. You can tell a lot from a face, Ranger. His face said he was carrying something he was afraid of. And anything the company is afraid of—" the smile again, the paper-tear laugh— "anything the company is afraid of is worth more than my life, so I kept it, I kept it safe, I kept—"

"Where," Ada said. Not unkindly. But firmly.

"Under the floor of the main house. Under the drafting table. There's a loose board, third from the wall, there's a tin, in the tin—" His breath caught, hitched, and the fever surged up and took him under again, and he began to whisper, over and over, a phrase like a rope he was holding onto: "the valley that eats names, the valley that eats names, the valley that eats names."

* * *

They got him into the boat. It wasn't a discussion. Sefa took one look at him as they carried him down the dock and said nothing at all, which from Sefa was a paragraph, and cleared a space among the supply crates with two economical sweeps of her arm.

The tin was where Osk said it would be, under the third board, wrapped in oilcloth with a surveyor's neatness: not the ledger itself—Ilya checked it with rising, trembling urgency—but a copy, hand-inked, forty pages of manifests and payment authorizations in a clerk's careful hand, and on the first page, a note in the same hand:

*If I am taken, this goes downriver. Tell the press at Saltmouth. Tell the magistrate at Ferrow. Tell anyone who still reads.*

Ilya sat on the dock in the rain with the pages in his hands and didn't say anything for a long time. Ada stood over him and let the silence do its work.

"You knew him," she said finally. "The man in the photograph. That's you."

"Yes."

"You were here. Before. You came downriver with the ledger and stopped here."

"I hid it," Ilya said. "I was going to come back for it with witnesses. With people the company couldn't buy. And then I ran, and I got tracked across the salt flats by a ranger with a broken compass, and the ledger stayed in a tin under a floor." He laughed, and it was a hollow sound, and it broke halfway through. "Nine people, Ada. He said nine. They took nine people because of what I carried through their valley."

"You didn't take them."

"I signed the manifests that taught them how." He looked up at her, and the rain ran down his face, and she couldn't tell which of it was rain. "That's the thing about being the auditor, Ada. You don't pull any triggers. You just write down the numbers afterward, neatly, in the correct columns. And the numbers are always correct. That's what makes you complicit. Not the errors. The accuracy."

Ada looked north, up the gorge, to where the glacier hung between the walls like a held breath. Somewhere up there was a valley that ate names. Somewhere up there were nine people who had become numbers in a column, and one of the columns, on a run three years gone, had a name she'd signed her own name beside, and she had never let herself read it all the way down.

She'd come upriver to deliver a thief. That was the contract. That was the debt. That was the whole shape of her life: deliver, collect, keep moving.

But the radio mast lay folded on its hinge, and a surveyor was shivering under a tarp in the bottom of a leaking boat, whispering about the dark being bought, and somewhere upstream the company was paying out settlements in a stranger's handwriting.

"Sefa," Ada said.

"Still here."

"How long to the Reach?"

"Two days, if the river's kind." Sefa looked at Osk, at the tin, at Ada, with her old woman's flat assessment. "The river is never kind."

"Then we go up," Ada said, and heard herself say it, and found that the words didn't feel like a decision so much as a recognition, the way you recognize your own handwriting. "We go up, and we find out what else they've erased."

Behind her, in the bottom of the boat, Osk Lindqvist opened his fever-bright eyes and whispered to no one, to the rain, to the river:

"They know the tin is gone. They'll come counting."

And the first drumroll of the next storm rolled down the gorge from the north, from the valley, from the direction they were going, like something clearing its throat.
