## 10. What the Ledger Says

The rain came back in the night, and by morning it had settled into a rhythm that felt less like weather and more like an argument the river was having with itself—patient, repetitive, going nowhere fast.

They had tied up in a side channel where the willows grew thick enough to make a green room of the boat, and Sefa had declared, in her way—which was to say she'd said nothing at all, merely pointed at the bank and then at the sky—that they weren't moving until it let up. Ada spent the morning bailing on principle even though the bilge was nearly dry, because her hands wanted work and her head did not want to be alone with itself.

Osk Lindqvist slept in the bow under every dry blanket they owned, still burning through his fever in slow degrees, muttering fragments. *Counting. They'll come counting.* His hands moved in his sleep like a man shuffling papers only he could see.

Ilya sat amidships with the ledger on his knees.

He had been sitting like that for two days. Not opening it. Just holding it, the way you might hold a sleeping animal that had already bitten you once. The book itself was nothing to look at—a fat rectangle bound in gray oilcloth, corners worn round, heavier than its size suggested, the way bad news always is. He'd carried it out of the Consortium's records annex sewn into the lining of his coat, which explained the coat's terrible fit, which Ada had privately mocked him for at the well, back when mocking him had been simple.

"You're going to wear a hole in it," she said now, wringing out the bail rag over the side. "Staring at it."

"I'm not staring."

"You've read the same page four times. Your eyes do a thing when you reread. A little stutter."

"My eyes don't stutter."

"They do. Like a motor catching." She wrung the rag again, though nothing came out. "What page is it?"

He didn't answer. Rain ticked on the canopy Sefa had rigged from an awning and half a tarp and what appeared to be a church banner, faded blue, embroidered with a saint whose face had rotted away, which Ada felt was thematically appropriate for their entire expedition.

"It's not a page," Ilya said finally. "It's the index."

"The index."

"There's a numbering system. Cross-references. It's—" He stopped, and Ada watched him decide something, watched the decision move across his face like weather moving across water. "It's organized very well. That's the worst part. Whoever kept this took pride in it."

Sefa appeared at the stern hatch with three cups of something that claimed to be tea and had probably never met a tea leaf in its life—some river herb, bark maybe, tasting faintly of iron and resignation. She handed them around without ceremony and settled herself on the gunwale, which was how she listened: present, silent, arms crossed, like a piece of the boat that had learned to frown.

Ada drank. The tea tasted like drinking a rusted nail politely.

"Open it," she said.

"I don't think—"

"Ilya." She set down the cup. "Two days ago I pulled you out of a flooded survey station while men were shooting at us. Yesterday I lied to a Consortium patrol boat with your head in my lap pretending you were my drunk husband. I have earned the index."

That almost got a smile out of him—the first one since the station, thin and crooked, gone as soon as it arrived. "Drunk husband," he said. "You improvised that."

"You were very convincing. You drooled on me."

"That was seasickness."

"That was commitment to the role."

But the joke ran out of road, the way jokes did between them lately, and the silence came back and sat down between them like a third passenger. In the bow, Osk murmured something about manifests. The rain went on arguing with itself.

Ilya untied the oilcloth.

* * *

The ledger was not, Ada discovered, a ledger in any sense she understood. She'd expected columns—figures, dates, sums. Money in, money out. What she got instead was a history written in the language of paperwork, and it took her several pages to understand that this was precisely the point. Nothing in it was illegal. That was the horror of it. Every entry was clean. Every entry was *documented.*

There were sections. Acquisition. Remediation. Community Relations—a title so bland it should have been funny, and would have been, if the entries under it hadn't been what they were.

"Read it aloud," Sefa said. First words she'd spoken in hours. Her voice was flat as the river in dead calm. "Some things sound different out loud."

Ilya looked at her, then at Ada, and began.

"'Acquisition, Parcel Fourteen, Karst Reach upper valley. Method: negotiated.'" He turned the page. "'Negotiation unsuccessful. Remediation authorized.' And then there's a code. R-3."

"What's R-3?"

He was quiet a moment. "Remediation Class Three. I signed off on these classifications for six years. Class One is a buyout offer sweetened until it can't be refused. Class Two is a buyout offer made unpleasant enough that refusal stops seeming wise—permits denied, credit called in, a magistrate suddenly interested in old tax disputes." His thumb rested on the page without moving. "Class Three is an accident."

The rain filled the pause.

"An accident," Ada repeated.

"A rockslide on a mine access road. A ferry that swamps in good weather. A fire in a dry season, origin undetermined. The claim holder dies, the heirs inherit land they can't work and grief they can't carry, and Meridian's regional office makes a generous offer to grieving families, because we are compassionate, because we are a family company." His voice stayed level the whole way through, and Ada understood that the levelness cost him more than breaking would have. "Every Class Three has a cost estimate attached. Do you know what a human life was budgeted at?"

She didn't answer.

"Four hundred marks," Ilya said. "Plus disbursement fees. There's a column for disbursement fees. Bribes have a *line item*, Ada. They're itemized. The magistrate at Kell's Landing gets twelve marks per incident. The coroner at Vess gets eight. Someone named M. Hale signs off on everything above fifty, and there's a note in the margin in different ink—'M.H. prefers incidents before quarter-end.'"

Ada found she was gripping the gunwale. Not hard. Just enough to know the wood was there.

"Turn the page," Sefa said.

* * *

He turned pages. The rain kept time. And somewhere in the middle of the book—in the section labeled *Historical Precedents*, because the Consortium had apparently been doing this long enough to develop precedent—Ada stopped hearing the individual entries and started hearing the pattern underneath them, the way you stop hearing individual drops and start hearing the flood.

Then one entry reached up out of the page and took her by the throat.

*Run 88-C, freight contract, seasonal. Crew of five. Incident type: winch failure, loaded skiff, high water. Classification: industrial misadventure, no fault assigned. Settlement paid to next of kin: none located.*

Five names followed. She knew them before she finished reading the first one.

Elias Nystrom. Boatmaster.

Her brother's name, sitting in a ledger, in a neat hand, between a cost figure and a margin note.

The world did not go white or red or any of the colors people promised you. It went very quiet and very precise, the way it does in the last second before a fall, when you notice absurd details—the grain of the wood, a drop of water hanging off the canopy, Ilya's mouth still moving as he read the next entry aloud—and then it went loud, all at once, everywhere, like the glacier calving she'd heard about from rivermen and never believed.

Because she had booked that run.

She had stood at the freight office in Salt Landing with the contract in her hand and she had said *the rates are good, Eli, take it,* and he had laughed and said *you always find me the good ones,* and the winch hadn't failed, the winch hadn't failed, the winch had been *budgeted,* there was a number for her brother in this book and the number was four hundred marks plus disbursement fees and someone had preferred it happen before quarter-end—

"—Ada."

She was standing. She didn't remember standing. The ledger was on the deck boards where it had fallen, open, splayed like a broken bird, and Ilya was halfway up out of his seat with his hands out—not reaching for her, reaching *near* her, the way you reach near a wild thing.

"Ada. Breathe."

"Don't tell me to breathe."

"Fine. Then don't. But sit down first."

She looked at her own hands. They were shaking, and some distant competent part of her noted it with clinical interest, the way she'd note a change in current. *Hands shaking. Cause identified. Prognosis poor.*

"Elias Nystrom," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like it belonged to someone reading a list. "Boatmaster. Run 88-C. Winch failure." She laughed—one syllable, awful. "Winch failure. I signed the contract. I *found him the run.* I'm the reason—"

"No." Ilya's voice cracked like ice going out. "No. Listen to me. Listen." He picked up the ledger slowly, keeping his movements small, and held it toward her but didn't touch her. "This is what they do. This is the whole machine. They find the person who loves you and they make *her* sign the paper. That's not a flaw in the system, Ada. That's the design. They used you the way they used me—I signed the classifications. I wrote the word 'remediation' in reports about funerals. We are both pen strokes in this book. We are not the hand."

"The hand is Hale."

"The hand is Hale. And above Hale there's a board, and above the board there's nothing at all, just the number going up." He set the ledger down between them, closed, his palm flat on the cover. "You asked me once why I stole it. I told you I stole it. That was true the way a map is true—it leaves things out. I didn't steal it. I *took* it, the night I finished auditing run 88-C."

The rain stopped. Not really—but Ada stopped hearing it.

"I traced the disbursements," Ilya said quietly. "Your brother's settlement. 'Next of kin: none located.' That was a lie, wasn't it. There was next of kin."

"There was me."

"They never intended to pay you. 'None located' means the file was flagged closed before anyone looked. I know because I flagged files like that. Six years." His hand pressed flatter on the cover, knuckles whitening. "I flagged yours."

From the bow, a dry voice, hoarse with fever but horribly clear:

"He's telling it right. I processed the closure. Osk Lindqvist, records division." A cough, long and wet. "Eighteen years. I counted the runs. I counted everything. That's all I ever did—count. And then one day I counted myself into the book and couldn't get out."

Nobody spoke. Sefa rose, went forward, and put a cup of the terrible tea in Osk's hands, and he drank it like a man taking medicine, which, in a sense, he was.

Ada sat down.

She sat down because her legs made the decision and the rest of her ratified it afterward, and for a long time she just breathed, and looked at the ledger, and let the grief do what grief does when it finally detonates after years of being stored improperly—not explode outward, but collapse inward, compacting into something dense and hard and permanent at her center. A new organ. She could feel it settle into place beside her heart. It would be there for the rest of her life. Fine. She had room.

When she could speak steadily, she said: "How many?"

Ilya opened the book again, to the index. "Class Threes in the Karst Reach alone, twenty-year period: sixty-one entries. Confirmed fatalities: eighty-eight. Some incidents took crews." He swallowed. "There's a summary page. Annual totals. It reads like rainfall figures."

"Sixty-one accidents," Sefa said from the stern. Her voice had changed—gone low and even, a blade drawn rather than raised. "In twenty years."

"In one valley system. The company operates in nine."

Sefa nodded slowly, looking at the water, and Ada saw her lips move once, silently, shaping a name. She didn't ask. Some names you recognized on sight, even unspoken.

"So we take it to the survey team," Ada said. "The independent assessors. That was the plan. That's still the plan. We go upriver, we put this in front of people who can verify it, and the whole rotten structure comes down in daylight."

"Yes," Ilya said.

"But."

He looked at her.

"But the survey station was ransacked," she said, and heard her own voice come out level and cold and clear, the ranger's voice, the voice that read currents and weather and country, except now it was reading something else. "The radio was smashed with a pry bar. The assessors are missing or worse. Osk says they'll come counting. Which means somebody upstream already knows this book exists, and they didn't smash a radio over a *disagreement.*" She turned to look upriver, where the gorge narrowed and the rain came down out of the north like a curtain being drawn across the rest of the world. "So the question isn't whether we deliver the ledger. The question is whether there's anyone left alive up there to receive it."

Osk laughed from the bow—a small, cracked, terrible laugh, the laugh of a man whose fever had burned away everything except the punchline.

"Oh, there's someone," he said. "There's always someone. The valley keeps its own books, ranger. Ask the drowned villages. Ask the ferryman who owes a debt." His eyes rolled toward Ilya, bright and knowing and half-mad. "Ask *his father.*"

Ilya went very still.

"You knew my father?" he said.

Osk smiled, closed his eyes, and slipped back under the fever, humming something that might have been a lullaby and might have been a count—*one, two, three, four*—all the way down.

Sefa was already at the tiller. She hadn't asked anyone. She'd simply untied the line and swung the bow into the current, north, upriver, into the rain, and when Ada caught her eye the captain said only:

"Faster we go, fewer we bury."

And the boat leaned into the current, carrying four hundred marks of blood money's worth of paper toward the valley that ate names, while behind them the storm they'd outrun gathered itself again over the flats, and ahead of them the storm they hadn't met yet waited in the narrows like a debt coming due.
