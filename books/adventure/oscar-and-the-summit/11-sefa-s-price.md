## 11. Sefa's Price

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and in its wake the river lay flat and gray as poured pewter, breathing fog. Ada sat on the boat's stern bench with the ledger closed on her knees and her hands still shaking.

She had not slept. Nobody had. The lamp in the cabin had burned until the oil ran out, and by its light Ilya had turned page after page while she read over his shoulder, and somewhere around the fourth hour she had stopped reading names and started hearing them — *Vasquez, Okafor, Lindqvist, Nystrom* — a litany of men and women who had gone into the Karst Reach under contract and come out as line items. *Settlement paid. Incident closed. No further inquiry recommended.*

Her brother's crew had been the eleventh entry. She'd counted.

Now the fog was lifting off the water in slow rags, and Sefa stood at the wheel with one hand resting on it like a woman resting her hand on the shoulder of an old horse, watching the current do most of the work. She hadn't said a word since midnight. Neither had Ilya. He was asleep below decks, finally, curled in the bilge corner like a man who had learned to sleep anywhere bad things might happen, which, Ada supposed, he had.

Ada climbed to the wheelhouse. Sefa didn't look at her, but she shifted half a step to make room, which from Sefa was roughly equivalent to rolling out a carpet and pouring tea.

"You read it," Sefa said. Not a question.

"Enough of it."

"Mm." The river bent ahead of them around a bluff hung with wet ferns. Somewhere up in the green a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling. "And?"

"And I don't know what to do with it," Ada admitted. It cost her something to say it out loud, and saying it out loud, oddly, gave some of the weight back. "I've spent eleven days chasing this man because the Consortium told me he stole from them. Turns out what he stole was the proof they're murderers. And I'm still being paid by them."

"That is a problem," Sefa agreed placidly.

"It's more than a problem."

"It is also a problem." Sefa adjusted the wheel two degrees for no reason Ada could see. "You want my advice, ranger?"

"I want your opinion. Advice implies you'll still respect me if I ignore it."

Something moved at the corner of Sefa's mouth. On another face it would have been called a smile. "My opinion is that you already know what you're going to do, and you're waiting for somebody to tell you it's allowed."

Ada looked out at the water. A log drifted past, traveling faster than they were, patient as anything. "It's not that simple."

"It never is. That's how you know it matters." Sefa nodded forward, upriver. "There's a bend coming where we either turn back or we don't. You should decide before we get there, not after. Decisions made after bends are usually regrets wearing work clothes."

* * *

They took breakfast — if a tin of smoked fish, hard bread, and tea brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in could be called breakfast — in the cabin, all three of them, because Sefa came below without being asked and sat down across from Ilya, and the way she sat told Ada that whatever was about to happen had been decided at the wheel sometime before dawn.

Ilya looked terrible. He looked, in fact, exactly like a man who had spent the night showing a stranger the record of his own worst sin, which was precisely what he was. His eyes were red-rimmed. His hands were steady only when he made himself hold the mug with both of them.

"I owe you both an apology," he began.

"No," Sefa said.

He blinked. "No?"

"You owe us the truth, and you paid it last night. Apologies are for people who intend to keep doing better. You haven't earned yours yet. Save it." She tore a piece of bread with her teeth and chewed, unhurried, letting him sit in it. Then: "Page forty-one. The ferry at Sorrel Bend. Tell me about it."

Ada watched Ilya's face go through several stages of weather. "That was — there were three incidents on the lower reach that year. The ferry was listed as a collision with debris—"

"The ferry at Sorrel Bend," Sefa repeated, in the tone of a woman who had all day and infinite patience and would use both to bury him alive, "carried six people. Five of them got off the water. My sister did not."

The cabin went very quiet. Above them, rain started again on the deck, soft as fingers drumming.

"She was a surveyor," Sefa went on. "Independent. She'd been hired by farmers along the bend to measure water rights, because the Consortium kept buying land cheap from people whose wells had mysteriously gone dry, and the farmers wanted to know why. She found out why. She wrote it down. Two weeks later her ferry hit 'debris.'" Sefa set down her mug with great care, the way you put down something when throwing it would feel too good. "I was nineteen. I didn't know what debris meant. I thought the river had done it. Rivers do things. That's what rivers are for."

"Sefa," Ilya said hoarsely, "the entry on page forty-one — I signed the closure on that one. It was my second year. I flagged it. I flagged it twice, and my director showed me a letter from the magistrate confirming accidental cause, and I—" His voice cracked and he pressed on through the crack like a man wading a cold river because stopping would be worse. "I let it go. I let forty of them go before I couldn't anymore. Your sister is on my signature."

"I know," Sefa said. "I read the page last night while you two were sleeping. Well — while you two were pretending to sleep."

Ada startled. "You went through the ledger?"

"I went through the ledger." Sefa looked at her, unbothered. "You left it on the chart table under a jacket. In my boat. I'm a careful woman, ranger; I check everything that comes aboard. Cargo, passengers, conscience." She turned back to Ilya. "Here is what I want to say to you, auditor. I have spent twenty years learning every mile of this river so that no one could ever again make people disappear on it without me knowing the water. I have smuggled, lied, bribed, and once — this stays between us three — set fire to a Consortium warehouse in Kell's Landing, though officially that was an accident involving debris." A pause, perfectly deadpan. "Rivers do things."

Despite everything, despite the grief sitting in the cabin like a third passenger, Ada felt her mouth twitch.

"So understand me," Sefa continued. "When I say what I am about to say, it is not forgiveness. Forgiveness is a door I may never open, and it is mine to open, not yours to knock on. What I am offering is something else. It is a job."

"A job," Ilya repeated.

"A job." She leaned forward. "You are going to take that book upriver to the mining camp, and you are going to get it out again with copies made, or testimony sworn, or whatever it is you two have been whispering about instead of sleeping. I will take you there. Past the checkpoints, past the narrows, all the way to the ice if it comes to that. I have reasons of my own to see that camp's lights from the water, and now I have one more." Her jaw tightened. "But hear my price, and hear it whole, because I will not say it twice."

Ada felt the cabin get smaller.

"When it is done — when the copies exist, when the magistrates have it, when the world knows — the original comes back to this boat, and I burn it. Page by page, in front of witnesses, on this deck." Sefa's voice did not rise at all, which somehow made it worse and better at once. "Every name in that book has a family. Most of them think their people died of rivers, rockfall, bad luck, bad roads. They have spent years carrying a weight that isn't theirs, blaming water and mountains and God. That book can give them the truth. But it cannot give them their people back, and if it survives — if it sits in an archive, gets quoted in newspapers, becomes a *story* — then their mothers and sons become entries forever. Footnotes. Evidence." She shook her head slowly. "I watched my sister become a line item once. I will not watch it happen twice. The truth goes out into the world, and then the paper dies, and the names go home to the people who loved them. That is my price."

Nobody spoke. The rain ticked on the deck. Downriver, behind them, everything they'd survived was getting smaller.

"The families deserve to see it," Ilya said quietly. "Some of them will want it preserved. Testimony has to—"

"Some of them will," Sefa agreed. "Which is why you'll make your copies first. Photographs, transcriptions, sworn statements, whatever your press friends need. Copy everything. I'm not a fool, and I'm not asking you to be one. But the original — the thing Hale wants burned, the thing the Consortium will hunt forever — that ends in fire on this deck, and I strike the match." She spread her hands. "Take it or leave it. There is a bend coming, and I told the ranger decisions belong before bends."

Ada looked at Ilya. He was staring at the ledger between them on the table, and she watched him wrestle with it — she could see the wrestling, the auditor's instinct screaming that evidence must be preserved, chained, guarded — and then she saw something else win. Something older.

"They signed off on deaths," he said at last, mostly to himself. "For twenty years I thought the crime was the killing. But it's worse than that. The crime is that nobody ever got to grieve honestly. Everyone downstream of those pages has been mourning a lie." He looked up at Sefa. "If I agree, I want one condition. When you burn it, we read the names aloud first. All of them. Every person in that book gets said out loud, on this river, before the paper goes. If the Consortium erased them, then we un-erase them, even if it's only for five minutes and an audience of three."

Sefa held out her hand. "Done."

They shook on it. Then, after a moment, she extended the same hand to Ada, eyebrow raised.

"Don't look at me like that," Ada said, taking it. "I haven't decided anything yet."

"Yes, you have," Sefa and Ilya said, almost in unison, and then glanced at each other in mild surprise, and Ada decided that being ganged up on by a smuggler and a defected auditor was, all things considered, not the worst company she'd ever kept.

* * *

They passed the bend at midday.

Ada stood at the bow as the current took them around it — the left fork running shallow and bright toward home, the right fork deepening into the green shadowed gorge of the upper reach, where the maps in her saddlebag went vague and apologetic, contour lines thinning out like the cartographer had lost his nerve. The air coming down the gorge smelled different. Colder. Older. Like stone that had been waiting a long time for someone to ask it questions.

She had made her decision somewhere around the second cup of terrible tea, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before admitting it. The debt she owed wasn't to Meridian. It had never been to Meridian. It was to a man named Tomas Nystrom and five crewmates whose names she'd read by lamplight and would carry now until she could say them aloud somewhere they'd count.

"You're doing the thing," Ilya said beside her. He'd come up quietly, hair still damp, looking marginally less like a drowned accountant.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at a horizon like it owes you money."

"It's a family expression."

"Ah." He leaned on the rail next to her. For a while neither of them said anything, and the boat talked instead — the creak of the hull, the mutter of water against strakes, Sefa humming something tuneless and contented at the wheel behind them.

"Can I ask you something honest?" Ilya said eventually.

"You can ask. Honesty depends on the question."

"When we get to the camp. When it's done, and the copies are out, and Sefa burns the book." He was looking at the water, not at her. "What happens to us? To you and me. Because I've been trying to figure out what I am to you, and the options seem to range between 'prisoner' and 'something I'm afraid to name,' and the uncertainty is starting to affect my operational judgment."

Ada considered this. The fog had burned off entirely now, and up ahead the gorge walls rose sheer and dripping, and somewhere far upstream, days away, a glacier sat holding a pass shut like a fist.

"Honestly?" she said. "Right now you're a man I arrested, who arrested me right back. We're both in custody. Of each other." She let that sit, and watched him try not to smile, and failed to stop herself smiling back. "Ask me again when we're through the ice."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I've got that doesn't jinx it." She pushed off the rail and headed aft, then paused. "Also, your operational judgment was affected long before I entered the picture. You faked your own death with paperwork. Who does that?"

"It was very thorough paperwork."

"It had a typo in the date."

"That typo," Ilya said with dignity, following her, "was intentional disinformation."

Behind the wheel, Sefa hummed louder, which Ada suspected was her version of laughing, and the boat ran on into the narrowing green, where the river got deeper and quieter and the first drowned rooftops of a village no map remembered began to show themselves beneath the clear cold water.

"There," Sefa said softly, nodding at the shapes below. "You wanted to know what kind of people we're fighting. Look down. They bought this valley off the maps, and the maps sold. First lesson of the Reach, ranger: nothing here drowns. It just gets renamed."

Ada looked down through the water at the small stone chimneys standing in the green dark like sentries, and understood that the next chapter of this had already begun, whether she'd signed on or not.
