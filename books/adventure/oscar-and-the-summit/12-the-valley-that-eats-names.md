## 12. The Valley That Eats Names

The river changed its mind three times in the morning.

Ada had learned to read water the way other people read faces—quickly, and with a healthy suspicion of what it wanted you to believe—but the Karst Reach was a different animal entirely. Up here past Kell's Landing, the current braided and unbraided itself around limestone spires that rose out of the flood like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die a very long time ago and hadn't finished the job.

"Left," Sefa said from the wheel.

The channel went left. Then it went right, immediately, as if embarrassed about the left.

"You could warn me," Ada said, hauling on the tiller line.

"I did." Sefa's face didn't move. It never moved. Ada had begun to suspect it was less a face than a rumor of one. "I said left."

"That was four seconds before the right!"

"Yes." Sefa adjusted her cap with two fingers, the closest thing to an apology she'd produced in six days of river. "Water lies. I don't."

Ilya laughed from the bow, where he'd spent most of the morning with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth across his knees like a sleeping cat he wasn't sure he trusted. He'd been laughing more lately. Not much—a breath of it, a crack in the ice—but enough that Ada had started cataloguing the sounds without meaning to. She told herself it was tactical. Knowing when your prisoner relaxed was useful information.

She had stopped believing herself somewhere around day four.

* * *

They passed the first drowned village just after noon.

Sefa cut the engine without being asked, and the boat drifted, quiet except for the tick of cooling metal and the slap of brown water against the hull. Below them, visible in the strange clarity of the flood season, lay rooftops. Whole streets of them. A church tower leaned at an angle that suggested it had been pushed rather than fallen, and a stone well mouth broke the surface like a single open eye.

"Serel," Sefa said.

"There's a town under there?"

"There was." Sefa lit one of her thin cigarettes, cupping the match against a wind that wasn't blowing, purely out of habit. "Dam went in eleven years back. Consortium subsidiary. Flooded the valley for a reservoir they never used. The water rights were worth more than the water."

Ada looked down through the green light. A window frame drifted past beneath the keel, curtains still hanging, bleached to ghosts. "Where did everyone go?"

"Some downstream. Some up." Sefa exhaled smoke through her nose. "Some nowhere. You can't be nowhere if nobody writes down that you were somewhere. That's the trick of it."

Ilya had gone very still at the bow. When Ada glanced at him, he was looking at the drowned church tower with an expression she recognized now—the auditor's expression, the one that meant a number had come up wrong and he was doing the arithmetic of how wrong.

"The resettlement compensation," he said quietly. "Line item 44. I signed off on it. It looked generous on paper."

"It was generous on paper," Sefa agreed. "Paper floats. People sink."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. The river carried them past the well mouth, and Ada found herself watching it until the bend took it away, the way you watch a grave until the road takes it away, because looking away first felt like a small betrayal.

* * *

They made camp that night on a shelf of limestone above the waterline, under an overhang that Sefa called, with no explanation, "the post office." There was no post office. There was a rusted tin box bolted to the rock, and inside it, when Ilya opened it, a bundle of letters tied with wire and a child's drawing of a boat done in charcoal.

"People leave word for each other," Sefa explained, accepting the tin back and replacing it exactly as she'd found it. "River folk. When the checkpoints got bad, this became cheaper than the mail. And safer."

"Safer than the mail," Ada repeated.

"The mail gets read." Sefa shrugged. "By people paid to read it. Magistrates, surveyors, health inspectors. Half the officials on this stretch draw Consortium stipends so small they don't even show up in the ledger Ilya's carrying. They're not corruption, those ones. They're maintenance."

Ilya sat down hard on a rock. "Maintenance," he said. "That's the word for it. That's—" He put his hands over his face. "I built the spreadsheets. I know every category. Line 12, civic stipends. Line 13, seasonal retainers. I thought line 13 was for dock labor."

"It is dock labor," Sefa said. "And magistrates. And the man who runs the ferry schedule. And the woman who decides which villages get medicine shipments. Everyone who touches paper touches the company, and everyone who touches the company forgets which way is up." She nodded at the darkening river. "You want to know why the maps are wrong? Maps cost money. Somebody decided where the money went."

Ada had brought out her broken compass without thinking about it—the habit of twenty years, thumb finding the cracked crystal in her pocket the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. The needle swung lazily, agreeing with nothing, pointing at whatever it pleased. Her brother had given it to her. Her brother, whose name was currently sitting in a ledger forty feet away wrapped in oilcloth, filed under *incident, unresolved*.

She'd have to open that book eventually. She knew that. Every mile upriver made the knowing heavier.

"Your needle's drunk," Ilya observed, settling beside her with two cups of Sefa's terrible tea.

"She's honest," Ada said. "That's worse. A drunk compass at least has an excuse."

He smiled at that, and handed her the tea, and for a moment the two of them just sat, watching the last light go copper on the karst spires, drinking something that tasted like boiled bark and regret.

"My father ferried this stretch," Ilya said suddenly. "Before. He had names for all these rocks. Said the river ate names too—that every flood took somebody's farm, somebody's grave, and the water kept them. He thought that was holy." He turned his cup in his hands. "I think the company just borrowed the trick."

* * *

Two days later they stopped at a river town that wasn't on any map Ada owned.

It should have been. It had a market street, a customs house, a chapel, maybe four hundred souls, and a name—Vessik—which Sefa pronounced with the flat contempt of someone biting tinfoil. The customs house was the reason for their stop: Sefa needed a stamp on her manifest, and stamps required charm, and Sefa's charm supply was roughly equivalent to her facial expressions.

"You'll do the talking," she said to Ada, tying off at the rotten pier.

"Why me?"

"Because you're a ranger and rangers make clerks nervous in a useful way. Because the thief looks like a thief. And because I'm going to stay here and guard my boat, my cargo, and my cigarettes, in ascending order of importance."

The customs house smelled of mildew and old ink. The clerk behind the desk was a young man with an official's collar worn soft at the edges and eyes that flicked to the door every time it opened, as though expecting someone better than him to walk through it. Ada set her ranger's warrant on the desk—her real one, God help her—and watched him read it.

"Ranger Nystrom," he said. "Upriver business?"

"Survey escort. Independent team working the high valley." She let the pause stretch. "You'll have seen their traffic."

Something happened to the young man's face. It didn't collapse; it simply closed, like a shop pulling its shutters. "There's no survey traffic," he said. "There's no independent survey. There's no—" He stopped himself, and Ada watched him physically swallow the rest of the sentence, and understood that she was not looking at a corrupt man. She was looking at a frightened one.

"No what?" she asked gently.

He glanced at the door again. Then, very quietly, without moving his lips much, he said: "There's no valley. Ask anyone. Ask the magistrate. Ask the priest. There's no valley upriver, and if you're smart you'll stamp your own papers and go home."

"And if I'm not smart?"

He stamped her manifest. His hand shook only a little. "Then I never saw you," he said, "and neither did this office, because this office doesn't see anything above the narrows. Nothing above the narrows exists." He slid the papers back. "Welcome to Vessik. Enjoy your stay. Please don't enjoy it long."

Outside, the market street bustled in the sun—fish, fruit, rope, a man selling hats shaped like deflated ambitions—and Ada walked through it feeling as though she were wading chest-deep through everyone else's held breath. A woman selling oranges wouldn't meet her eye. Two men by the chapel stopped talking when she passed and resumed only in a register too low to catch. On the customs house wall, someone had painted the town's name and founding date, and beneath it, older paint, half-scraped but legible, another name entirely: *Old Vessik, est. —* and then a date that predated the one above it by two hundred years.

They hadn't erased the town. They'd erased the town's history and repainted it thinner.

Ilya found her staring at it. "The registry revision," he said. His voice had gone hoarse. "2019. I processed it. Boundary adjustments, I thought. Cartographic cleanup." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Do you know how many boundary adjustments I approved? Do you know how many places stopped existing while I was having lunch?"

Ada touched his arm. It was the first time she'd initiated contact, and they both pretended it was nothing, which was its own kind of lie, and both of them allowed it.

"We'll need supplies," she said, because someone had to say something ordinary or the whole street would hear the ground shifting under it. "And we need to be gone by dark."

* * *

Sefa had a different opinion about dark.

"We go now," she said, coiling rope with unnecessary violence. "The narrows are best run at first light, but we are not sleeping at this pier. The harbor master drinks with the patrol officers. The patrol officers ask questions about boats heading upvalley. I have answered those questions before." She tied the coil off and slung it. "I didn't like my answers."

"What happened?" Ilya asked.

Sefa looked at him for a long moment, and Ada saw her decide something—not whether to answer, but how much of the truth the answer would contain. "Last time," she said, "a boat asked the same questions I'm asking now. Carried a doctor. Woman named Ryn. She'd heard the high valley had sickness nobody was treating." Sefa's hands went still on the rail. "Her boat came back down. She didn't. The report said drowning."

"The report was written by—"

"The same clerk who writes all of them." Sefa stepped onto the bridge and rang the engine bell once, softly, like a mourner. "So. Narrows at dusk instead of dawn, running blind in the worst light there is, because the alternative is being a report." She looked back at them both. "Get your gear stowed. And whatever either of you is carrying in your chests besides cargo—I'd batten that down too. The narrows don't care what you're grieving. They drown it along with everything else."

Ada took the tiller. Ilya took the bail bucket, unbidden, and settled himself amidships with the grim competence of a man who had bailed a great deal of water recently and expected to bail a great deal more. The ledger rode against his ribs. Ada's broken compass rode against hers. Between them, the river ran fast and brown toward the gap in the karst where the walls drew close and the water, according to Sefa, "gets ideas."

The light was already failing. Ahead, the narrows opened like a throat.

From somewhere upstream—too far to see, close enough to hear—came a sound Ada had never hoped to hear on open water: engines. More than one. Heavy, low-revving, the sound of boats built to push weight around rather than get anywhere fast.

Gunboats.

Sefa heard it too. Her hand moved to the throttle.

"Hold course," she said quietly, "and pray the water lies better than I do."

The walls of the narrows began to slide past, close enough on either side to touch, and the engine noise grew teeth.
