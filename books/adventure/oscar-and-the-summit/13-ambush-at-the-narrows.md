## 13. Ambush at the Narrows

The first gunboat came around the bend like a rumor made flesh.

It was a flat-bottomed patrol craft, the kind the Consortium used for hauling equipment and intimidating fishermen, with a mounted spotlight that swept the water ahead of it in slow, arrogant arcs. Its hull was painted the gray of old dishwater, and stenciled along its flank in letters that had once been white was the word MERIDIAN-7.

"Two more behind it," Ilya said from the bow, where he'd been keeping watch since the engine noise started. His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man reading numbers off a page he wished weren't real. "One holding back at the entrance. They're not chasing us. They're waiting."

"They're herding," Sefa corrected. Her hands stayed easy on the wheel, but Ada had learned to read the captain's stillness the way other people read shouting. Sefa's calm had layers, like ice on a pond, and you could hear how thick it was by what moved underneath. "See how the lead boat's sitting? Dead center of the channel, lights sweeping wide. That's not a search pattern. That's a door closing."

Ada crouched low behind the gunwale and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. The narrows ran maybe two kilometers between walls of limestone that rose sheer on either side, close enough here that the river squeezed itself into a single furious channel. There was no beach to land on. No tributary to duck into. The map she'd memorized showed one thing past the midpoint: a place where the river dropped through a jumble of drowned boulders that the survey charts marked, with bureaucratic delicacy, as *impassable at all stages*.

Which meant the Consortium boats would expect them to stop. Anyone sensible would stop.

"Sensible people stop," Sefa said, as if Ada had spoken aloud. Maybe her face had done the talking. "That's the whole trick of checkpoints. They don't need to catch you. They just need to be standing where stopping is the only reasonable thing left."

"And we're not going to be reasonable," Ada said.

Sefa glanced back at her, and for the first time since Ada had known her—since the rotting dock at Pell's Crossing, since the price haggled down to three words and a handshake—the old woman smiled. It was not entirely a comforting smile. It had rust in it.

"I'm going to show you why they call this stretch the Throat," Sefa said. "Get below. Both of you. Take anything that floats and hold onto it."

"The bilge—" Ilya started.

"Is full of my spare parts and my regrets, and you'll be joining them if you don't move." She spun the wheel hard over, and the *Kestrel's Daughter*—a name Ada finally noticed painted on the stern, half flaked away, as if the boat had been trying to forget it too—heeled toward the north wall.

* * *

Below decks was less a cabin than an argument between a storage closet and a flood. Crates of tarred rope. A crate of oranges gone soft as fists. Coils, hooks, a tin stove, and everywhere the smell of diesel and river mold that Ada had stopped noticing days ago and now found almost homey, which was its own small alarm.

Ilya wedged himself against the bulkhead and pulled a coil of line into his lap like a man hugging a pillow through a nightmare. Ada took the opposite bench. Through the hull she could feel the river change under them—the deep muscular pull of open current giving way to something faster, shallower, angrier.

Above, muffled by planking, came a voice amplified through a megaphone. It had the practiced boredom of every official voice Ada had ever heard, the tone that said *this is procedure, none of this is personal, your life is a formality.*

"*Vessel in the narrows. Cut your engine and prepare for inspection. This is a Meridian security operation. Compliance will be noted favorably.*"

"Favorably," Ilya repeated softly. "You hear that? Somewhere there's a man who wrote that sentence and got paid for it."

"Somewhere there's a man who wrote worse," Ada said. She was thinking of contracts. She was always thinking of contracts lately, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. "Compliance will be noted favorably. My brother's outfit had a clause like that. Section eleven. *Cooperation ensures continued engagement.*"

"What did cooperation get them?"

Ada looked at him. In the dim light coming through the hatch, his face was all angles and apology, and she realized he already knew the answer—that he'd read it in a ledger, probably, in his own neat auditor's hand, before any of this started.

"You know what it got them."

"I know what the paper says happened to them." His jaw tightened. "There's a difference. There has to be a difference, or—" He stopped. Above them, the megaphone voice repeated itself, patient as erosion.

The engine note changed. Not Sefa's engine—theirs. The gunboats were moving now, spreading out across the channel mouth behind them, and ahead, around the next bend, Ada heard the third boat answer with a long blast of its horn. *We have you. We have always had you.*

Then Sefa did something that made no sense at all, which meant it made perfect sense.

She opened the throttle all the way.

* * *

The *Kestrel's Daughter* surged forward like an old dog remembering it had once been a wolf, and the cabin became a shaking, roaring box. Ada heard Sefa's boots cross the deck above, heard the hatch bang, and then the captain's voice came down through the boards, conversational as ever, as though she were discussing weather over tea:

"When I say dive, you'll feel the boat stand on its nose. Don't fight it. Fight the water after."

"Dive?" Ilya shouted up through the floor. "Dive where?"

"Where the river goes!"

Ada scrambled up the ladder first. What she saw when her head cleared the hatch she knew she would carry for the rest of her life, however long the rest of her life turned out to be.

The narrows ended ahead in a wall of white.

Not mist—water. The river, pinched between cliffs no wider apart than a city street, poured itself over a staircase of drowned rock and fell ten meters into a churning basin, then gathered itself and fell again, twice more, a triple stair of rapids descending into a gorge that curved out of sight. Spray hung in the air like weather. The sound was enormous—not loud so much as *total*, a sound that occupied the whole world and left no room for engines or megaphones or anything human.

And behind them, filling the channel wall to wall, came the gunboats.

The lead one had seen what Sefa had seen. It had also, evidently, decided differently about what to do with the information. It was slowing, its spotlight swinging wildly, its horn sounding short confused blasts. The men aboard it had procedures for vessels that stopped. Ada would have bet her badge—and these days her badge wasn't worth much—that they had nothing at all for a vessel that chose the waterfall.

"She can't be serious," Ilya said, arriving beside her, soaked instantly by spray.

"She's been serious this whole time. We're only now catching up." Ada grabbed the wheel housing to steady herself and found Sefa's shoulder beside hers, solid as a piling. Water streamed off the old woman's oilskin hood. Her eyes were fixed forward with an expression Ada could only call devotion.

"Twenty years I've run this river," Sefa said, mostly to herself, mostly to the boat. "Twenty years, and every pilot says the Throat eats boats. You know what I say?" She didn't wait. "The Throat doesn't eat boats. The Throat eats *hesitation*. Boats go through fine. It's the pilots who stop halfway that get chewed."

The first drop came up fast.

"Hold something!" Sefa roared, and the world went vertical.

* * *

Later, Ada would try to describe those ninety seconds and fail, because language was built for flat ground. The bow lifted until the stars swung overhead. Then the hull slammed down into the trough and the river hit them broadside like a freight argument, and the *Kestrel's Daughter* spun a quarter turn while still doing the speed of a falling object, and Sefa—Sefa *laughed*, a dry bark of pure joy, wrestling the wheel through the chaos with hands that seemed to know the water's mind before the water did.

"Left channel!" she screamed, to no one, to the river, to God. "I know, I KNOW—"

They threaded the second drop sideways. Ada saw a boulder the size of a chapel slide past her hip close enough to read the moss on it. Ilya was beside her with both arms locked around a stanchion, and his face—she'd remember this too—was not terror. It was the face of a man watching a proof complete itself, every terrible term clicking into place.

The third drop was the worst, because it was the last. The boat went over it nearly end-for-end, hung at the top of the fall for a heartbeat that lasted a season, and plunged into the black pool below with a impact that folded Ada over the rail and drove the breath out of her in a single punched-out grunt.

Cold water. Total darkness. Silence except for the roar receding behind them and—

—a new sound. A wrong sound. A gurgle from beneath the deck that no healthy boat ever made.

"We're holed," Sefa said. She said it the way she said everything, level and unhurried, but her hand was already on the throttle, killing the engine, and her other hand was already pulling the plug wrench from its bracket. "Starboard side, below the waterline. Something kissed us on the way down. Probably the same boulder that tried to shake my hand."

"How bad?" Ada forced herself upright. Her ribs sang. Everything sang.

"Bad enough that we're having this conversation. Good enough that we're having it in the present tense." Sefa pointed with her chin. "Bilge. Now. Both of you. Buckets are under the netting. Bail like the river's charging rent, because it is."

* * *

They bailed.

It was not heroic work. It was work. Ada knelt in six inches of freezing water that became eight inches, that became ten, passing a dented galvanized bucket hand to hand with a man she'd once chased across a salt flat with handcuffs in her pocket, and the rhythm of it became a kind of prayer. Scoop. Pass. Dump. Scoop. The lantern swung overhead and threw their shadows huge against the curved ceiling, two giants laboring inside the belly of a dying fish.

In the dark, in the cold, with the river muttering outside the hull, Ilya started talking. Not because he wanted to. Because people talk when their hands are busy and their hearts are scared, and because some things can only be said in places where nobody can look at anybody.

"My father fished water like this," he said. "Up in the high country, before the companies came. He used to say a river tells you the truth about a person faster than a magistrate ever could. Put two strangers in a leaking boat, he said, and you'll know within the hour whether you can trust them with your life. You'll know whether they bail or whether they blame."

"Which am I doing?"

"Bailing." He passed the bucket back. His hands were shaking—from cold, she told herself, from cold. "So am I. So is she. That's the whole test, isn't it. Nobody's blaming yet."

"Give it time," Ada said, and heard him laugh—a real laugh, startled out of him, brief and warm as a struck match.

Above them, Sefa's voice came down through the deck, and it had changed. The dryness was gone. What remained was quiet and very tired and very clear.

"They're not following."

Ada climbed up. The gorge had widened into a long dark lake walled in by cliff, the rapids a pale scar of noise far upstream. At the top of it, tiny with distance, the gunboats' spotlights swept back and forth across the churning water—searching the pools at the base of the falls, the eddies, the rocks. Looking for wreckage. Looking for bodies.

Nobody, Ada understood, was looking downstream. Nobody sane would be.

"They think we died in the Throat," she said.

"The Throat thinks so too, most days." Sefa was at the stern, one boot pressed on a plank that flexed wrong under her weight, studying it with the expression of a doctor delivering news. "But the Throat and I have an arrangement. Here's tonight's bill." She looked up, and in the lantern light her face was gray under its brown, and not only from age. "Hull's cracked along three planks. I can keep her floating till dawn if the water stays kind. After that, we find ground or we swim. And Ada Nystrom—" the old woman's eyes found hers, sharp as a filleting knife, "—there's a light on the south bank. Two miles down. You see it?"

Ada looked. Far down the black corridor of the gorge, almost lost among the stars' reflections, a single yellow point glimmered. A lamp in a window. Smoke, maybe, thinning upward from a fire.

"Somebody lives down there," Sefa said. "Somebody who owes me, or owes somebody, or owes somebody's father—which on this river amounts to the same thing. We make that bank, we might live. We miss it—" she shrugged, and the shrug cost her a wince, "—the gorge does the Consortium's work for free."

Ilya appeared at Ada's shoulder, dripping, bucket still in hand, and followed her gaze to the distant light.

"That's two miles," he said. "In a sinking boat. In the dark."

"Yes," said Sefa.

"And if we don't make it?"

"Then the ledger sinks with us," Ada said quietly, "and the valley keeps eating names."

Nobody answered that. The engine coughed, caught, coughed again—Sefa nursing it like a sick animal—and the *Kestrel's Daughter* leaned into the current, listing badly to starboard, limping down the dark throat of the gorge toward a stranger's lamp.

Ada put her shoulder against the gunwale and watched the light grow, one bucket of hope at a time.
