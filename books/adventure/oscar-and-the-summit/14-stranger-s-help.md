## 14. Stranger's Help

The river spat them out the way a dog spits out a stone it has decided it doesn't like.

Ada came up coughing, one hand still locked around the strap of her pack—the ledger's oilskin bundle inside it, pressed against her spine like a second heartbeat. The current shoved her sideways into something soft and yielding that turned out to be Ilya, who grabbed her by the collar with both hands and hauled her toward the shallows with the desperate strength of a man who had recently learned exactly how much he did not want to drown.

"Boat," she rasped.

"Gone." He said it flatly, the way you say a thing you've already grieved and are only now reporting for the record. "Sefa?"

"Sefa—" Ada twisted in the water. The narrows were behind them now, the gunboats' engines a distant angry wasp-sound swallowed by the gorge walls. Downstream, snagged against a gravel bar, lay half of Sefa's riverboat, the stern section, listing like a drunk leaning on a fence post. And standing on the bar itself, hip-deep in churning brown water, was Sefa, hauling their supplies ashore one crate at a time as if the river were a warehouse clerk who'd gotten an order wrong and she intended to correct it personally.

"She's fine," Ada said, and then, because the river had nearly killed all three of them and honesty seemed suddenly cheap at any price: "She's always fine."

They dragged themselves onto the bank—a narrow shelf of grey shingle wedged between the water and a wall of karst so sheer the mist couldn't even be bothered to climb it. Ada knelt and shook. She hated that her body did that, shook like a frightened animal after every close call, but she'd learned long ago not to fight it. Fighting it just made it last longer. She let it run its course while Ilya wrung out his shirt and stared at the wreckage with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"The hull," he said finally. "There's no hull anymore. There's a suggestion of hull. There's a rumor of hull."

"There's a hole in the hull," Ada corrected. "A big one, below the waterline, where the rocks went through it. Which is technically what hulls are for having."

"That's not—that's not what hulls are *for*—"

"It's what they're *good at*, apparently."

From the gravel bar, without turning around, Sefa said: "Both of you shut up and help me with the crates before the river takes my stove."

They helped her with the stove. It took all three of them, because Sefa's stove was cast iron and clearly weighed more than Ilya did, and by the time they'd wrestled it above the waterline Ada's arms were trembling for a second reason entirely. They built a driftwood fire while the light went coppery and then gone, and ate cold rice out of a dented pot, and nobody said anything about the fact that they were alive, or how unlikely that was, or what came next. Some conversations need darkness first.

It was full dark when Ada heard the bell.

Not a church bell—nothing so orderly. A single deep bronze note, struck once, rolling down the gorge from upstream, bouncing off the canyon walls until it seemed to come from everywhere at once. Ilya's head came up like a deer's. Sefa didn't move at all, which Ada had learned meant she was paying very close attention.

"What is that?" Ada whispered.

"Ferry bell," Sefa said. "Somebody lives up there." She nodded at a thread of lamplight, far above them on the cliff face, that Ada would have sworn hadn't been there ten minutes ago. "Old Toma's stretch of river. If he's ringing his bell at night, either he's seen us, or he's seen who's chasing us, and he wants both of us to know he's seen."

"And that's good or bad?"

"Yes," said Sefa.

* * *

The ferryman came down at dawn, and he came down the way water comes down—not hurrying, not stopping, simply arriving eventually at the lowest point available.

He was old in the way that river stones are old: rounded, patient, impossible to guess at. A white beard cropped short, hands like root systems, and a rope slung over one shoulder with the casualness of a man for whom rope was less a tool than a limb. He stood at the top of the shingle and looked at the three of them for a long moment—at the wrecked stern section, at the scorch mark where a bullet had kissed the rail, at Ada's ranger badge still glinting on her collar—and Ada watched him take all of it in and file it away somewhere behind eyes the color of river fog.

"You ran the Narrows at monsoon," he said. His voice was low and gravelly, the voice of a man who had spent fifty years being heard over water. "In a boat older than sin."

"It wasn't my first choice," Ada said.

"No," he agreed. "First choices are for people the river likes." He looked past her then, and something happened to his face. Something small. He looked at Ilya the way you look at a door you thought had been bricked over years ago and find standing open.

"Voss," he said quietly. "You're Voss's boy."

Ilya went very still. "You knew my father?"

"Knew him." The old man came down the shingle, boots crunching, and stopped an arm's length away. Up close Ada could see the network of scars on his forearms, old rope-burns layered like tree rings. "Knew him the way you know a man you've pulled out of a river. Thirty-one years ago, spring flood, he went through the upper gorge clinging to a plank and a prayer, and I went in after him because the bell told me to and I've never once regretted listening to it." He held out his hand—not to shake, palm up, open, an offering rather than a greeting. "Toma. And a life saved is a life owed. That's not sentiment. That's bookkeeping. The oldest kind there is."

Ilya took his hand. His voice, when it came, was rougher than Ada had ever heard it. "He never mentioned—he never talked much about the river."

"He wouldn't." Toma smiled, and it transformed his whole craggy face into something almost boyish. "Men like your father don't talk about the debts other people pay for them. It's the talking-about that costs. But he wrote me. Every year, a letter, on the flood date. *Still floating, Toma. Hope you are too.*" The smile faded by degrees. "Letters stopped four years back. I wrote the address. Got nothing back. I told myself the postal service in the cities is what it is."

"It is what it is," Ilya said softly. "He died four years ago. Fever."

Toma nodded slowly, once, the way a man nods when a load he's been carrying gets set down—even a heavy load, even a sad one, is lighter than an unanswered question. Then he looked at the wreck, at the three of them, at the gorge walls, and clapped his hands together with a crack like a starting pistol.

"Well. You can't stay here. The Consortium's boats will sweep this bank by tomorrow noon—they always do after a chase, looking for bodies and evidence, and you two are going to be inconveniently neither. So." He jerked his thumb upstream. "My cove's two hours up, hidden behind the split rock. There's a dry hut, a smoke rack, and enough canvas and pine pitch to make a liar out of any hole in any hull. We'll want the good half of your boat, though. Can you swim it up?"

"We can swim anything up," Sefa said, "if the current agrees."

"The current," said Toma, "owes me favors too."

* * *

Getting the stern section upstream was the hardest work Ada had done since the salt flats, and the salt flats had tried to kill her with heat instead of moving water, which she now considered a courtesy. They lashed the hull fragment between two of Toma's log floats, rigged a bridle, and walked it along the bank on ropes while the old man read the current aloud like scripture—*slack water along the shadow side, pull on my count, rest when I say rest*—and by some miracle of stubbornness and rope-craft they brought it into the cove as the afternoon rain began, fat warm drops hissing on the river's skin.

The cove itself was a secret the geography kept. The river bent around a spur of limestone split clean down the middle—Toma called it the Split Rock and spoke of it with the familiarity of an old rival—and behind it lay a pool of green stillness where the current never reached, ringed by birch and hung with mist. The hut was low and dark and smelled of woodsmoke and pine pitch and fish, and it was, in that moment, the finest building Ada had ever stood inside.

She hadn't realized how exhausted she was until she sat down on a bench and discovered that standing back up was no longer among her body's supported features.

"You," Toma said, pointing a thick finger at her, "sleep. You—" the finger swung to Ilya, "—eat. You—" Sefa, "—come look at my pitch stores and tell me if they've spoiled, because you have the eyes of a woman who has patched many boats badly and doesn't intend to again."

"They're not *badly* patched," Sefa muttered, already following him to the shed. "They're *honestly* patched."

Ada slept eleven hours. She knew because she woke once, briefly, to the sound of rain and low voices and the smell of someone frying fish, saw Ilya sitting by the stove with a mug in both hands laughing at something Toma had said—a real laugh, unguarded, a sound she hadn't known he could make—and slept again without a single dream. When she woke properly it was morning, gray-green light through the shutter cracks, and her body felt like it belonged to her again instead of to the river.

She found the two men outside in the drizzle, standing over the salvaged hull, arguing about wood the way other men argued about politics.

"—and I'm telling you, that strake is sound, it's the *seam* that's opened—"

"The seam opened *because* the strake is tired. Wood gets tired, boy. So do seams. So do ferrymen. Everything on this river is tired except the river."

Ada leaned against the doorpost and watched them, and felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was tight. Ilya had sawdust in his hair. Toma was gesturing with a mallet. Neither of them noticed her, and for a moment she simply stood there and let herself be a woman watching two people she cared about argue about caulking in the rain, and let that be the whole of the world.

Then Ilya glanced over, caught her, and grinned—grinned, wet and sawdusted and alive—and the world got considerably larger again.

"Good news," he called. "We're not dead, and the boat might not be either."

"Toma says?"

"Toma says three days, if we work."

"Toma says five," Toma said, "because the boy counts like an auditor. Everything takes less time when you're counting it than when you're doing it." He squinted at Ada. "You. Ranger. Can you use a drawknife?"

"I can use most things with edges."

"Then welcome to the shipyard."

* * *

The days found a rhythm, and the rhythm was a mercy.

Mornings they worked the hull: Toma directing, Ilya sawing and fitting, Ada shaping planks with the drawknife until her palms blistered and then callused, Sefa doing the delicate interior work she refused to let anyone else touch, sealing seams with pitch and pounded oakum and swearing at the wood in three languages. Afternoons the rain came, as it always came, and they retreated to the hut, and Toma cooked things out of the river and out of his smokehouse and told stories that were forty percent true if you were generous.

It was on the third evening, mending nets on the porch while the mist came up off the pool, that Ada asked the question she'd been carrying since the shingle.

"The life-debt," she said. Toma sat beside her, mending with fingers that moved without his attention. "Does it really work like that? One rescue, thirty-one years of obligation?"

Toma snorted. "No. Of course not. Nobody *has* to do anything." He tied off a knot. "But here's what I know, ranger. Your friend's father was carried through my stretch of river by my hands, and everything that man did afterward—every letter, every kindness he sent downstream, and he sent plenty, ask anyone on this water—came out of that day. A debt like that isn't a chain. It's a seed. You plant it and you find out what grows." He looked at her sidelong, fog-colored eyes gone sharp. "And sometimes it isn't the man who owes who ends up paying. Sometimes the debt skips, like a stone. Lands on whoever's standing nearest when it matters."

Ada looked across the yard, where Ilya was splitting kindling in the drizzle, methodical and careful, a man who had signed off on deaths and then spent four years turning himself into a weapon aimed at the people who'd asked him to.

"I'm standing pretty near," she said.

"Mm," said Toma, and went back to his net.

That night she checked the ledger bundle—dry, safe, sleeping in its oilskin under her bedroll—and lay awake listening to the rain and thinking about seeds and stones and the strange arithmetic of grace. She had come upriver to deliver a thief. She had lost her boat, her badge's meaning, her certainty, and very nearly her life, and somehow she had gained a shipyard, a laugh she hadn't known existed, and an old man who fed strangers because a drowning man's son was standing nearby.

The debt she owed, the real one, the one with her brother's name on it—it wasn't with the Consortium anymore. She'd known that since the station, since the ledger, since the narrows. But lying in the dark in a stranger's hut, she understood it more completely than she ever had: debts don't get paid to companies. Companies don't have hands. Debts get paid to the dead, and to the living who loved them, and the payment is always the same thing: the truth, carried wherever it has to go, by whoever's standing nearest.

She was standing nearest.

In the morning Toma came in early, before the others woke, and stood over her bunk with a lamp, and his face told her before he spoke that the easy days were over.

"Patrol boat came down in the night," he said quietly. "Slow, lights off, sweeping the banks with a searchlight. They'll find the wreck site today. When they do, they'll work upstream." He set the lamp down. "And there's something else you should see. Come to the porch. Both of you. There's a thing I have to tell you about this valley, and about why an old man lives alone behind a split rock—and there's a thing about the glacier you'll want to hear before you decide anything at all."

Behind him, through the open door, the mist was lifting off the pool, and far upstream, faint as memory, the mountains showed white.
