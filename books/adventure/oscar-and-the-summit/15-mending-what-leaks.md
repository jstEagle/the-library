## 15. Mending What Leaks

The boat had a name once. You could still see the ghost of the letters under the fresh scar of hull-paint Toma had slapped over them years ago — *something* and then an O, worn to a shadow. Ilya ran his thumb across it while Ada hauled the last of their soaked gear onto the shingle.

"What do you think she was called?" he asked.

"Doesn't matter," Ada said. "She's called Leaky now."

"That's cruel."

"That's accurate." She dropped the tarp-wrapped bundle at his feet. "You want sentiment or you want dry socks?"

He considered this with the gravity of a man weighing a contract clause. "Dry socks," he admitted.

Toma's cove was the kind of place maps forgot on purpose. A crescent of gray shingle cupped between two shoulders of black rock, a stand of wind-bent alders behind it, and above all of it the old ferryman's hut with its roof of river stone and its chimney that smoked sideways no matter which way the wind blew. The boat lay half-beached at the tide line, holed below the waterline where the narrows had chewed her, listing like a drunk leaning on a lamppost that wasn't there.

"She'll float again," Toma had said that first evening, spitting into the river with the confidence of a man who'd made and lost four boats in his life. "Whether she'll float *well* is between her and God."

* * *

The work filled the days before either of them could fill the silence.

Ada woke first — she always woke first, some ranger instinct wired into her spine that treated sleep as a checkpoint rather than a destination — and found Toma already up, already boiling something that claimed to be coffee and had clearly never met a coffee bean in its life. He handed her a tin mug without being asked.

"You grind it twice," he said, by way of explanation for the taste.

"It tastes like it was ground with gravel."

"Gravel gives it character."

By the third morning she'd stopped grimacing. By the fifth she caught herself asking for a second cup, and Toma smiled into his beard like a fisherman who'd finally landed something.

The repairs were slow, stubborn work. They rolled the boat on greased logs until her wounded flank faced the sky, and there she was: a gash the length of Ada's forearm, planks sprung, caulking gone to pulp. Toma directed from a stump like a general who'd been demoted to quartermaster and decided to enjoy it. Ilya sawed and planed replacement stock from the alders. Ada did the caulking, because her hands were steadier, because she'd patched worse things than boats, though she didn't say what those things were.

The resin had to be mixed hot and worked fast. The first day they ruined two batches — one because Ilya stirred too slowly, one because Ada stirred too fast, which led to a brief academic debate about whose fault it was that ended when Toma said, "Both. Next batch," in a tone that closed the subject forever.

It was during the fourth batch, working side by side with their sleeves rolled and the smell of hot pitch sharp in the cold air, that Ada noticed Ilya humming.

Not loudly. Barely a thread of sound under his breath, some tuneless thing that rose and fell while his hands moved. She glanced over. He didn't notice her noticing, and she found she liked that — liked watching him without him arranging his face for her benefit. Up close, in daylight, without a gun anywhere in the conversation, he looked younger than the wanted poster had made him. The poster had given him hard eyes. In person his eyes were only tired, and occasionally, when the resin came out right, quietly pleased, like a boy who'd gotten away with something small and harmless.

"You're humming," she said.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's terrible."

"It's not a song. It's arithmetic." He spread the next strip of caulking. "I used to hum through audits. Helped me count."

"Did it help?"

"No. That's why they transferred me to fieldwork." He paused, and something crossed his face — the ledger-shaped shadow that lived behind everything he did now — and then passed. "Worse idea, in hindsight."

Ada let it go. There would be time for ledgers later. There always would be; that was the trouble with ledgers. But the resin was setting, and the sun had come through the clouds for the first time in three days, and Ilya Voss was humming bad arithmetic beside her, and she let herself have the morning.

* * *

In the afternoons, when the light went long and gold off the water, Ada walked down to the point where the cove opened into the main channel, and she read the river.

Ilya followed her the second day, hands in his pockets, wearing the expression of a man who has been invited to a lecture he suspects will be long.

"Why?" he asked.

"Why what?"

"Why do you look at it? It's water. It goes downhill. I've read the reports — rivers are extremely predictable. Gradient, volume, discharge rates—"

"Sit down," Ada said, and pointed at a flat rock, and he sat, because even auditors know when the numbers stop mattering.

She crouched at the waterline and pointed. "See the line where the current splits? Off that boulder? Most people see white water and panic. White water is just the river telling you where it doesn't want to go. The smooth tongue in the middle — that's the road. The V pointing downstream. You follow the Vs."

"And the dark patches?"

"Calm water can be deep or shallow. The shallow kind will flip you just as dead as the rapids. You watch for texture. Glassy means deep. Wrinkled means rocks underneath, close enough to touch the surface." She flicked a pebble out into the channel and watched it vanish. "The river writes everything down. You just have to read it."

Ilya was quiet a moment. Then, in the careful voice he used when he was about to be professionally pedantic: "That's an audit."

"What?"

"What you're doing. Reading a system's surface behavior to infer its hidden structure. Currents are just — transactions. Water moving from one account to another. The eddies are where money pools. The whitewater is where someone's been cooking the books." He looked almost embarrassed. "Sorry. Everything sounds like accounting if you do it long enough."

Ada stared at him. Then she laughed — a short, surprised sound, rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in a season. "That is the single worst description of a river I've ever heard."

"But it's correct."

"It's *horrible*. 'Eddies are where money pools.'" She laughed again, harder, and had to put a hand against the rock. He watched her laugh with an expression she couldn't quite read — something open and startled, like a man watching weather he hadn't forecast.

"I'm going to teach you to read current properly," she said, recovering, "and you're going to keep your banking metaphors to yourself."

"Deal," he said. "But when we run the next set of rapids and I call out a hostile takeover mid-channel, you'll thank me."

* * *

So it became a ritual. Every afternoon, the point, the rock, the river. She taught him to spot the seam lines, the eddy fences, the telltale boil of a submerged boulder. He learned faster than she expected — faster, she suspected, than he wanted her to know he could learn anything, since his whole identity these days seemed built on being the useless city man among practical people.

And in exchange — though neither of them framed it as a trade, because framing it would have meant admitting it was happening — he taught her to rest.

It started small. She'd finish a repair task and immediately find another: re-stowing gear that was already stowed, sharpening tools that were already sharp, walking the perimeter of the cove for threats that Toma had assured her, repeatedly, did not exist. On the fourth day Ilya watched her re-coil a rope for the third time and simply took it out of her hands.

"That rope has done nothing wrong," he said.

"It was coiled wrong."

"It was coiled *differently*. Sit down, Nystrom."

"I don't—"

"You've been moving since dawn. You move like someone who thinks if she stops, something will catch her." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "Nothing's catching you here. Sit down. Look at the water. That's an order from your student."

"I outrank you."

"You're off duty. I checked the contract. Very thorough, my reading of contracts."

She sat. It felt like stepping off a ledge. For the first ten minutes her leg bounced and her eyes kept sweeping the tree line and she catalogued, involuntarily, seventeen tactical features of the shoreline. Then Ilya started talking — not about anything, about nothing, about whether Toma's coffee qualified as a war crime, about the correct way to eat a dried fish (there wasn't one, he argued, it was punishment food), about a childhood dog named Biscuit who had been, by every account, a criminal — and somewhere in the middle of the Biscuit saga Ada realized her shoulders had come down from around her ears for the first time in weeks.

She didn't tell him that. But she came back to the rock the next afternoon, and the one after that.

* * *

On the sixth day, Sefa's fever broke.

They'd been taking turns sitting with her in the hut — the gunshot wound in her side had gone bad before Toma's poultices and willow bark turned it, and for two days she'd drifted in and out, muttering river depths in her sleep, calling once, heartbreakingly, for a sister's name that Ada now knew belonged to the valley. When she finally surfaced for good, gray-faced and furious about it, her first full sentence was: "If either of you has been touching my boat, I'll drown you myself."

"We fixed your boat," Ada said.

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. "Then I'll drown you slower," she said, and went back to sleep, and Toma chuckled from the corner like this was the finest medicine he'd ever administered.

After that, Sefa insisted on being carried down to the shingle each day to supervise the repairs from a chair, wrapped in blankets, offering commentary. Her commentary was mostly insults, but they were *informed* insults, and the boat improved visibly under the threat of them.

"You missed a seam," she said on the second day of supervision, pointing with her chin.

"There's no seam there."

"There's a seam where I say there's a seam. Run your hand along it. Feel that? That's a seam." Ada ran her hand along it. It was a seam. She caulked it, and Sefa nodded once, which from Sefa was a parade with brass bands.

* * *

On the seventh evening, the rain held off, which was rare enough that the whole cove seemed to notice. The clouds broke in the west and poured late gold light down the channel, and the river turned the color of hammered bronze, and Toma announced he was walking up to check his fish traps and would be back after dark and they should *not* wait dinner, which everyone correctly understood to mean *please wait dinner.*

Ada was at the point, on the rock, reading the evening current — the river calmed at dusk, went glassy and secretive, told fewer lies — when Ilya came and sat beside her. Closer than usual. Not quite touching. The space between their shoulders was charged, the way air is before lightning.

"The boat floats," he said.

"Toma tested her this morning. She floats."

"She leaks a little."

"Everything leaks a little." Ada turned a pebble over in her fingers. "Question is whether she leaks slower than you bail. Same as people."

Ilya looked at her. She felt it, the weight of it, and kept her eyes on the water because looking back felt like standing up on a boat in rough seas.

"When we leave here," he said, "it gets hard again. You know that. The pass, the camp, Hale. Whatever this is—" he gestured vaguely at the space between them, at the seven evenings stacked behind it like coins, "—it doesn't get to be safe. Nothing ahead of us is safe."

"I know how to read water, Voss. I know what a rapid looks like before we're in it."

"Do you?" His voice was quiet. "Because I keep trying to chart this and I can't. There's no data. I've never—" He stopped. Started again, more honestly: "I'm not good at things I can't verify."

Ada finally turned and looked at him. The light was doing something generous to his face, and his expression was the one from the first morning — open, startled, unarranged — and she thought: *oh.* Not with alarm. With a strange, settling calm, like watching a seam line appear in chaos and knowing, suddenly, exactly where the safe channel runs.

"Then don't verify," she said. "Just read the current."

He breathed out something that was almost a laugh. Their hands rested on the rock between them, near enough that the smallest movement would close the distance, and neither of them made it, and both of them knew the other knew, and it was — somehow — enough. More than enough. A full hold, waiting.

From the hut, Sefa's voice carried down the shingle, ragged but imperious: "Are you two reading the river or writing poetry? Dinner's getting cold and I nearly died, so show some respect."

Ada stood, and offered Ilya her hand, and pulled him up, and neither of them let go quite as fast as they needed to.

* * *

But the mountains had been watching too, and they were not sentimental.

Ada woke in the gray hour before dawn to a sound she'd been listening for all week without admitting it: wind. Not the cove's usual soft breathing through the alders, but a low, freighted roar from upriver, from the high country, from the glacier itself. She stepped out of the hut into air that had changed overnight — colder, heavier, tasting of snow that hadn't fallen yet.

Toma was already at the water's edge, staring up the channel toward the white peaks, and his face in the pre-dawn gloom was the face of a man reading a river that had begun to lie.

"Storm's coming down out of the pass," he said, without turning. "A big one. It'll pin us here — three days, maybe five. And after it—" he spat, an old habit that today looked less like habit and more like prayer, "—after it, the calving window opens. And closes. We'll have one chance, girl. Maybe not even that."

Behind Ada, inside the hut, Ilya slept on, one arm flung over his eyes, trusting the morning in a way she never had.

She looked at the black water, already beginning to wrinkle with the first far-off breath of the storm, and started counting supplies.
