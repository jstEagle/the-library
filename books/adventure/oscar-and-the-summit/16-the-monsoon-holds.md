## 16. The Monsoon Holds

The rain came on the fourth morning and did not leave.

It arrived not as a storm, at first, but as a mood — a gray ceiling lowering over Toma's cove until the cliffs on either side of them dissolved into wet wool. Then the wind found its voice, and the rain found its teeth, and by noon the whole world had been reduced to the sound of water hitting water.

Ada stood under the lean-to they'd built against the boat shed and watched the river climb. It had swallowed the gravel spit where she'd taught Ilya to read current. It had swallowed the willows. It was working on a boulder the size of a chapel, and it showed no sign of being impressed by the boulder.

"Well," said Ilya beside her, "on the bright side, we're not bored."

"We are absolutely bored."

"I'm saying boredom is the *bright* side. Boredom means nothing's broken. Boredom means Sefa's stitches held and the hull patch held and nobody's bleeding." He tilted his head back and let rain hit him square in the face, which seemed to delight him in a way Ada found faintly deranged. "I spent six years in an office where boredom was considered a personal failing. I intend to enjoy every minute of it."

"You enjoyed it for about two days."

"And then the fish ran out," he admitted. "Yes."

That was the truth of it, laid out plain between them like a gutted catch. The storm had pinned them — Sefa's wound still angry, the boat sound but not yet *sound enough*, the pass days away even in good weather — and pinned things consume. They'd come into the cove with a hold full of Toma's smoked fish, a sack of barley, a jar of something pickled that Toma called *preserves* and everyone else called *a dare*. Four days of rain later, the fish were down to their last dozen, the barley was measured out in cups with a miser's hand, and the jar of preserves sat untouched because none of them were brave enough to open it and none of them were desperate enough yet.

Ada did the arithmetic again, though she'd done it that morning and it hadn't improved overnight. It never did. Arithmetic was loyal that way; it gave you the same answer every time you asked.

"Two more days of this," she said, "and we're eating the rope."

"The rope's tarred. Might be the best thing in the hold."

"Sefa says the same about her coffee."

Sefa herself was inside the boat shed, propped against the hull on a nest of nets, supervising the weather with the expression of a woman who had personally offended it. Her shoulder wound had gone from angry to sullen — hot at the edges, she admitted when pressed, and only when pressed hard. She'd stopped arguing about resting, which worried Ada more than any fever would have. Sefa arguing was Sefa being Sefa. Sefa going quiet was Sefa spending strength she didn't have.

Ada ducked inside, shook the water off like a dog, and got a look at the shoulder while Sefa pretended to be interested in the ceiling. The stitches held. The skin around them did not want to talk about it.

"It needs to drain properly," Ada said. "It needs heat and dry air and about a week of you lying there insulting us."

"A week." Sefa laughed, which cost her something; Ada saw her hide it. "A week from now the calving window opens and closes. You'll be watching it close from here, drinking my coffee."

"Your coffee is a war crime."

"My coffee is *rationed*," Sefa said, with dignity, "which is different."

* * *

The argument came that evening, as arguments do — not announced, not planned, arriving the way weather arrives, off the water and sudden.

They were in the shed around the little stove Toma had rigged, three people and a pot of barley soup thin enough to read through. Rain hammered the roof in long rolling volleys. Somewhere up-cove, Toma's ferry sat under its tarp like a patient animal, and Toma himself had gone up-valley that afternoon to check his traps, because a man who has lost one livelihood does not like to sit idle near the bones of it.

It started small. It usually did.

"We should decide," Ilya said, stirring his soup without eating it. "About the window."

"There's nothing to decide," Ada said. "The window opens. We go."

"If the window opens. If the boat holds. If Sefa can stand a watch." He kept his voice easy, which was how she knew he'd been rehearsing. "I've been doing the arithmetic too, Ada. Two days of this rain and we don't have the food to make the pass even if everything else goes right. We turn back now, while the river's high enough to carry us fast, we're in Kell's Landing in four days with our lives and most of our copies—"

"Our copies are worthless in Kell's Landing." She set her spoon down carefully, the way you do when the alternative is throwing it. "Kell's Landing is a Consortium town. You taught me that yourself, at the checkpoint. The magistrate there takes Hale's money to look at the sky and call it clear. We walk those copies into Kell's Landing and they're kindling by Friday."

"They're kindling if we starve in a cove holding them!"

The words came out louder than he meant them to, and then the rain filled the silence after, hammering, indifferent.

Ada made herself breathe. Made herself see it from his side of the stove, because she could, and that was the trouble with loving someone — you kept having to look through their eyes whether you wanted to or not. From his side: a man who had already died once for this ledger, who had signed his name to deaths and then un-signed them at terrible cost, who looked at a dwindling food sack and saw the whole enterprise tipping over not from guns or glaciers but from *barley*. From her side: a brother at the bottom of a company ledger, a line item, an *accident*, and a debt she had carried so long it had grown into her spine.

"He's dead either way," she said quietly.

Ilya went still. "What?"

"My brother. Turning back doesn't bring him back. Pushing on doesn't either. That's not why—" She stopped. Started again, slower. "I used to think the debt was what drove me. Pay it down, discharge it, be free. But it's not a debt anymore, Ilya. It's a *name*. Eleven names on a manifest that a company bought and burned, and nobody living knows them except me, and if I turn back now they stay dead in a drawer forever. Quiet. Convenient. Exactly the way Hale drew it up."

"That's duty talking."

"Of course it's duty talking. What else would talk?"

"*You*," he said. "You could talk. Have you noticed you haven't once said what *you* want? Not the debt, not the names, not the badge you're going to burn. You." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, rain-light moving on his face. "Because I've been sitting here four days watching you, and I think you don't know. I think somebody trained the wanting out of you so early you can't even find the scar."

The stove ticked. Outside, the river ate another yard of bank.

"That's not fair," Ada said.

"No," Ilya agreed. "It's worse than fair. Fair would be easier."

And there it was — the thing underneath the argument, surfaced at last, blinking in the lamplight. Not the pass. Not the ledger. The fact that she wanted to stay. That some traitorous part of her had looked at this leaking shed, this thin soup, this man who stirred pots badly and read currents worse and looked at her like she was a person instead of an instrument — and had thought, *here, I could be happy here,* and had immediately sentenced that thought to hard labor carrying gear up a glacier.

"I want to stay," she said. It came out rough. "There. Said it. I want to stay in this stupid shed until the rain stops and then maybe longer, and I hate that I want it, because wanting things is how companies get their hooks in you. Want your brother safe — sign here. Want your debt cleared — deliver the thief. Every bad thing I ever did started with a want I let someone price for me. So yes, Ilyusha, I turned the wanting off, and it worked, right up until a man who steals ledgers started making me soup."

The nickname slipped out before she could stop it. She watched it land. Watched him go soft-eyed and careful, the way he did when she handed him something breakable.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. So we both want to stay. And we both know staying kills the names." He rubbed his face. "This is a horrible bargain."

"The worst."

"Pushing on might kill us too."

"Yes."

"And Sefa can barely stand."

At the sound of her name, Sefa opened one eye from her nest of nets. "Sefa," she said, "can hear everything, and would like it noted that she has run the Narrows with a bullet in her and a hole in her boat, and that the two of you argue like an old married couple, and that if you're going to decide to die on a glacier you should at least finish the soup first, because waste is a sin."

She closed the eye again. The point, as ever, stood.

Ada looked at the pot. At Ilya. At the rain coming down like the sky meant it personally.

"Half rations," she said finally. "We stretch it five days. If the rain breaks, we go. If it hasn't broken by the fifth day—" she hated the sentence, pushed it out anyway, "—we take it to a vote. All three of us. Even you," she told Sefa, "especially you, since it's your boat."

"Democracy," Sefa said, to the ceiling. "On my boat. The world's gone mad."

But her mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling, and Ada counted it a victory.

* * *

The fifth day, the rain did not break, and the vote did not need taking, because Toma came back from his traps.

He came down through the trees at dusk, soaked through, moving faster than a man his age had any business moving, and he wasn't carrying fish. He was carrying news, and you could see it on him the way you see weather far off over salt flats — a darkness with weight behind it.

Ada met him at the shed door. Behind her, Ilya sat up. Sefa's eye opened.

"Toma," Ada said. "What is it?"

The old ferryman stood dripping in the doorway, rain running off his beard, and looked at each of them in turn — the ranger, the thief, the wounded captain — as if checking they were all still there, all still real.

"Patrol boat came up-valley today," he said. "Consortium colors. Asking questions at the drowned villages." He paused, and the pause was worse than anything he could have said. "Asking about a woman ranger, a man with auditor's hands, and a riverboat that should be at the bottom of the Narrows."

The stove ticked. The rain hammered. And somewhere upstream, past the drowned villages and the erased maps, the glacier waited with its two-day window, opening for no one.

"How long," Ada said, "do we have?"

Toma looked out at the black water, and then back at her, and his old eyes were full of something that had happened to him once before, twenty years ago, in a valley that no longer appeared on any map.

"Less than that," he said.
