## 17. Toma's Story

Toma did not answer right away. He rose instead, slow in the way old men are slow when the slowness is a choice rather than an ailment, and went to the shelf above his stove. Ada had noticed the shelf before—she noticed most things, it was the one talent she'd never been able to put down—but she had filed it away as clutter. A tin of hooks. A chipped cup holding pencils. A jar of river glass, green and brown and one improbable piece of blue.

Now she saw that the shelf was not clutter. It was a shrine, and like most shrines it was dressed as something ordinary so that strangers would leave it alone.

Toma took down the blue glass and turned it in his hands. Outside, the rain kept up its argument with the roof, and the river kept its low constant voice beneath everything, the way a heartbeat runs under a conversation you're not sure you want to have.

"Sit," he said. "Both of you. This is a sitting story."

Ilya sat. Ada sat. Sefa, who had been mending a net with the grim concentration of a woman who would rather do anything than talk, tied off her knot and sat too, which told Ada more than anything Toma could have said. Sefa knew the story. Sefa had been keeping her back to it for three days.

* * *

"The valley has a name," Toma began, "but I'm not going to give it to you yet. Not because it's a secret. Because names are how they work, and I want you to understand the working before you hear the name."

He set the blue glass on the table between the lamp and the teapot, where it threw a small crooked piece of light.

"Twenty years ago there were eleven families up there. Karst people, mostly—limestone country, caves and cold springs, the kind of farming where you fight a rock for every cabbage. Poor. But it was *their* poor, if you follow me. They'd been there longer than the surveys. Longer than the maps. The maps came late and got things wrong, and the valley people found it funny. They used to say the cartographers drew the river with a straightedge because they couldn't imagine water having opinions."

Ilya made a sound that was almost a laugh. Toma looked at him with something soft and complicated in his face.

"You'd have liked them," he said. "Your father would have. That's partly why I'm telling this."

Ada watched Ilya go still. She had learned in the last weeks that Ilya went still the way other men flinched—quickly, and then covering it with motion. But there was nowhere to cover to, in Toma's cove, in the rain, in a story that had been waiting for him since before he was the man he now was.

"Your father ferried me out of that country once," Toma said. "Before your time. Before mine went bad. He carried a man in his boat who'd been shot in the leg, and he didn't ask who shot him, and he didn't charge me. He said—" Toma's voice did something, a small hitch, like a wheel finding a rut in an old road. "He said, *the river doesn't keep books. That's what the river's for.*"

The rain hammered. Sefa's hands, resting on the table, had curled into loose fists.

"So," Toma said. "The company came. Not this company. The name on the letterhead was different—there's always a different name; they breed them like mules, all the muscle and none of the memory. They came up the same way you came, by water, with papers, with a survey. They said *minerals*. They said *jobs*. They said the word *partnership* so many times it stopped meaning anything, the way a word does when you say it too much. And the valley people said, we'll think about it, which is what free people say when they mean no but are too polite to wound you."

He poured tea. Nobody drank it. The tea steamed and cooled and became a small domestic casualty of the story, which Ada thought later was probably its function.

"The survey was real," Toma went on. "That's the part people don't understand, when I tell it. The minerals were real. The ore was real. Everything was real except the honesty. You can steal a thing outright and the wound is clean, it heals, you know what happened to you. But they didn't steal. They *bought*. They bought the magistrate in the district town—a decent man, I knew him, he'd once fined me for an unlit lantern at night like the law was a real thing that lived in him. They bought the freight office. They bought the priest, or the priest's silence, which in that country is the more expensive of the two. And then the accidents started."

"Accidents," Ada said. The word came out of her flat and cold, and she heard in it the ledger's language, the columns she'd seen by lamplight with Ilya's hands shaking over them. *Fatality, industrial. Settlement paid. Claim closed.*

"Accidents," Toma agreed, and his eyes were old and level on her. "A cable that frays just where the inspector stopped looking. A tunnel that floods on a dry week. A man who drinks—everyone knew he drank—and falls into the sluice. Eleven families, and in two years, nine funerals. Do you know what a valley does with nine funerals in two years? It runs out of black cloth. It runs out of the *habit* of grief, which is worse, because grief is a thing you can carry only if everyone carries it together, in rhythm, like a boat over a bar. And the company sent flowers. Real ones. Hothouse flowers, up the river, in the dry season, which cost more than the settlements did. I have thought about those flowers for twenty years. There is a kind of money that exists only to tell you it can reach anywhere."

He turned the blue glass a quarter turn, so its light fell across the table and touched Ilya's folded hands.

"The valley people went to the magistrate. The magistrate had been bought. They went to the papers. The paper's owner had been bought. They went to the company office, where a very kind man with a very good chair explained that these things were tragedies, that safety was a journey, that the company was *committed*—and Ada, I want you to hold that word, *committed*, because I have since learned that it is the favorite word of men describing the direction in which they intend to kill you."

Sefa spoke for the first time, without looking up from her fists. "He's not exaggerating. He never exaggerates. It's his worst quality."

"I have exactly one quality," Toma said, "and I am committed to it."

The small joke landed in the room like a bird landing on a wire—there, and then gone, and the wire still trembling. Ada understood suddenly that the joke was not a deflection. It was a survival. A man who had carried a story this heavy for twenty years had learned to make it lighter at intervals, or it would have crushed the carrying out of him.

* * *

"The end came in the spring," Toma said. "The calving season, as it happens. The same window. The same ice."

He was quiet a moment. The stove ticked. Down in the cove, the repaired boat knocked gently against its mooring, patient as a dog.

"There was a man in the valley—a surveyor, company man, but a good one, the kind they always have one of, to make the rest of the paperwork look clean. He'd found something in his own figures. Not minerals. *Costs.* The cost of the accidents, set against the cost of the ore, and a third column that wasn't supposed to exist, and in the third column the accidents weren't accidents at all, they were line items. Budgeted. He'd signed some of them himself, early on, before he understood what he was signing. So he couldn't go to the company. He couldn't go to the magistrate. He did what a man in that position does." Toma looked at Ilya. "He put the papers in a boat."

The rain filled the silence. Ada's heart was doing something slow and enormous, and she kept her face the way she kept it on stakeouts, because the alternative was to let it be seen.

"The company found out he was carrying them out," Toma said. "I never learned how. A bought clerk, a read letter, a bribed ear—it doesn't matter. What matters is that on the last night of the calving window, when the pass was open and the ice was running, eleven people got into three boats to go downriver with him. Not just the surveyor. The families. Because the families had decided—this I know, this was told to me by the one man who lived—if the valley was going to be taken, they would not be in it when it was taken. They would go downriver and they would stand in front of whatever court was left that wasn't bought, all of them together, with the papers, and they would make the company say the names out loud in a room where saying them cost something."

"And?" Ilya said. His voice came out sanded down to the grain.

"And the company did not wait for the court." Toma's hands were flat on the table now, and steady, and Ada understood that the steadiness had been purchased at a price she did not want to know. "The narrows. You ran the narrows, yes? You know how the water speaks there. There is a shelf of rock on the north side where a man can stand with a rifle and be invisible from the water and inescapable from above. They were waiting. All three boats. The surveyor's boat made it through—holed, half-sunk, four people in it, two of them children. The other two boats did not make it through. Seven people. The company reported it as a calving wave. A natural event. Ice from the glacier, come downriver at the exact hour, in the exact reach, to drown exactly the people who were on their way to testify. The magistrate ruled it an act of God." Toma smiled, and it was the worst thing Ada had seen in a long time, that smile. "God has an excellent lawyer."

Nobody moved. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as if it too were listening.

"The surveyor lived," Toma said. "He lived, and he went to the papers, and the papers were bought, and he went to the capital, and the capital was far, and the company had a different name by the time he got there. He lived another six years and died of a fever in a boarding house in Kell's Landing, and the last thing he said to me—I ferried him, the last year, he'd come down to look at the river, our river, he'd sit in my boat and look at it—was, *Toma, they don't need to win. They only need to outlast the remembering.*"

He picked up the blue glass. Held it to the lamp.

"This was his wife's," he said. "She was in the second boat. He gave it to me at the landing, the last time, and he said, *keep it where the light can find it.*" He set it down again, precisely, in the middle of the table. "So. That's the story. That's the valley that eats names. It doesn't eat them all at once. It eats the maps first, then the magistrates, then the men, and the names go last because the names are what would bring everything back if anyone said them."

* * *

For a while, the only sound was the rain and the river, and the small tick of the stove, and Ada sat with her hands around cold tea she had no memory of picking up.

She was thinking about a contract she had signed. One signature, one line, one clause about *delivery of assets, condition: alive*, and how the paper had felt in her hand—not heavy, not light, just paper, the way a knife is just metal. She was thinking about her brother, and a run she had booked, and a manifest she had never read because reading it was the dispatcher's job, and how the dispatcher had been a decent tired man who also hadn't read it, because reading it was someone else's job, all the way down, a chain of not-reading with a company's name at the top of it and a grave at the bottom.

"Twenty years," Sefa said. Her voice was rough. She was looking at the blue glass. "Same pattern."

"Same pattern," Toma said.

"Same company."

"Under different names. But I've read their contracts, the ones that surface. There's a phrase they use. *Variance management.* It's in the old papers and it's in the new ones. A man keeps a phrase like a knife-thrower keeps a knife. They've been throwing the same knife for twenty years."

Ilya had not spoken since the story began. Ada looked at him now and saw a man doing arithmetic he had hoped never to finish. He had been the auditor. He had sat in the chair with the good view and signed the columns, and he had told her in the bilge, in the dark, in the smell of bilgewater and honesty, that he had signed off on the accidents before he understood them—and Ada had believed him, and still believed him, but belief had a new weight now. Because Toma's surveyor had done the same thing. Had signed, and understood, and put the papers in a boat. History did not repeat, her father used to say; it *rhymed*. And Ilya was sitting in the rhyme, holding the third column in his head, and the company that had drowned seven people to stop one boat was upstream, and it knew what he carried.

"They'll have a file on the valley," Ilya said at last. His voice was steady, which cost him; Ada could see the cost, the way you can see a man carrying something heavy by the set of his shoulders. "The old operation. If it's the same company under new names, the old papers will be in the new archive. The surveyor's third column. The settlement records. Maybe the names of the men on the rock shelf at the narrows." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and clear. "It's not just my ledger. It's theirs. It's *evidence with a twenty-year chain of custody*, and it's sitting in a filing cabinet in the camp we're sailing into."

"Or it's ash," Sefa said. "Companies burn their history when it starts to smell."

"Then it's ash and the ledger is all that's left, which makes the ledger worth more, not less." Ilya's hands had flattened on the table, mirroring Toma's. "You understand what I'm saying. This isn't a theft anymore. It never was. It's a *continuity*. Twenty years, two valleys, one knife. If we get this downriver, we're not exposing an operation. We're exposing a *practice*. That's the difference between a scandal and a reckoning."

Ada heard herself say, quietly, "And the difference between a thief and a witness."

Toma turned to her. The old man's eyes were not soft anymore. They were the eyes of a ferryman reading water—measuring her, her boat, her load, her draft, deciding whether she'd hold together in the narrows.

"That's the question, isn't it," he said. "What you are when you come out the other end of this. The surveyor never got to find out. The river decided for him." He rose, joints cracking like small green wood, and went to the window, where the rain ran down the glass in long silver ropes and the black cove breathed against the black shore. "So I'll tell you what I know, and then you can decide what you are while you still have the choosing of it."

He pulled back the edge of an oilcloth tacked over the window's corner, and pointed upstream, into the dark where the river came down out of the high country.

"The glacier opens in four days," he said. "Maybe five, if the rain stays cold. And the company knows the window too. They've had twenty years to learn it. Which means, girl, that somewhere up that river tonight, there are men being paid to watch the ice—and they are not watching it to admire it."

The oilcloth fell back over the glass. The lamp flame leaned, as if the whole room had inhaled.

"Four days," Ada said.

"Four," Toma said. "And the last man who tried to carry the truth through that pass in that window is buried where his wife's glass came from." He looked at the blue shard on the table, then at each of them in turn—Ada, Ilya, Sefa—and the rain came down and the river went on saying the only thing it ever says.

"So," he said. "Decide what you're carrying. Because the ice doesn't care. But I do."

* * *

Later, when the lamp was turned low and Sefa had gone out to check the moorings and come back soaked and saying nothing, Ada sat awake on her bunk and listened to the cove.

Ilya was awake too. She could tell by his breathing, the way she could now, which was a fact she filed away with the others she wasn't ready to look at directly. After a while his voice came through the dark, quiet, almost lost under the rain.

"The surveyor," he said. "The one who lived. What was his name?"

Toma's voice, from across the room, was a long time coming. When it came, it came the way the river comes down from the ice—cold, clear, and carrying everything with it.

"Oscar," he said. "His name was Oscar."

And in the dark, Ada Nystrom lay still, and thought about names, and about the valley that ate them, and about the one name that had survived twenty years in the mouth of an old man in a cove the maps had forgotten—and she understood, with a certainty that felt like cold water, that they were going to be the ones who carried it back.
