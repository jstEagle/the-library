## 18. Patrol

The cove woke them with gulls.

Not the polite kind, either — the shrieking, argumentative sort that seemed personally offended by the existence of sleeping humans. Ada lay in the hull of the half-mended boat, staring up at a sky gone the color of wet slate, and listened to Ilya mutter something in a language she didn't know that was almost certainly not a prayer.

"They've been at it since before dawn," he said, sitting up. His hair had achieved a state that could only be described as architectural. "I think one of them is nesting in the mast step."

"That's Toma's problem."

"Toma's problem is our problem until the caulking holds." He said it lightly, but there was no lightness underneath it, and they both knew it. Six days in the cove. Six days of tar and oakum and Toma's endless, patient instruction, and the *Kestrel's* belly was still weeping in two places like a child who'd learned that crying got results.

Ada swung her legs over the gunwale and dropped onto the shingle. The stones were cold through her boots. Somewhere beyond the headland, the river ran high and brown with monsoon melt, and beyond *that* — two days' hard poling, if Toma's charts were honest — the mining camp sat in its stolen valley, fat and quiet and full of paper that could hang men.

She'd stopped counting the days since Kell's Landing. That way lay madness.

Toma was already down at his ferry, which was less a boat than a floating argument between eras — flat-bottomed, broad-beamed, painted a green so old it had faded into an apology. He stood on her deck with a mug in each hand and the expression of a man who had made peace with every morning ever offered to him.

"You look," he said, handing Ada a mug, "like a woman who dreamed about ledgers again."

"I don't dream about ledgers."

"You talk in your sleep, then." He passed the second mug to Ilya, who had come clattering down the shingle with all the grace of a man still learning where his feet ended. "You said 'page forty-one' four times. Very stern about it."

"I did not," Ilya said.

"Page forty-one," Toma agreed serenely, and drank his tea.

Ada felt her ears go hot and blamed the wind off the water. Page forty-one was the manifest for the *Cormorant*, the run her brother had booked through her office eleven years ago. She had read it so many times now that the numbers lived behind her eyelids. Eleven names. Eleven men who had signed on for hazard pay and gotten a rockslide instead, arranged and paid for by a company whose signature she recognized because — this was the part that woke her at night — she had once signed the same line herself, in different ink, for smaller sums.

"Tea's good," she said, to change the subject, and it was, because everything Toma made was good; it was one of the many infuriating things about him.

They worked through the morning. Ilya below, driving oakum into the forward seam while Ada held the light and called out thin spots, and Toma up top re-lashing the cargo net and telling a story about a mule named Prosperity who had once eaten an entire customs declaration, which Toma maintained was the single most sensible act of governance the province had ever seen.

"Prosperity understood," he said, cinching a knot, "that paperwork is how the river gets strangled. Not with guns first. With paper first. Guns are just what happens when the paper runs out of patience."

"You're a philosopher," Ada said.

"I'm a ferryman. Same thing, cheaper."

It was nearly noon when the dog started.

Toma kept a dog — a brindled, grizzled thing named Biscuit who had greeted them on arrival with the enthusiasm of a customs official and had since decided, in his own time, that Ilya was acceptable and Ada was tolerable and Sefa was the only creature in the cove worth sitting beside. Sefa spent most days in Toma's hut above the tideline, her ribs taped where the narrows had thrown her against the wheel, reading his small hoard of books and pretending not to be bored out of her skull.

Now Biscuit stood at the headland path, ears up, and made a sound Ada had never heard from him: low, continuous, wrong.

Toma set down his tea. All the philosophy went out of his face at once, and what remained was older and harder and much more interesting.

"Boat?" Ada asked.

"Engine." He cocked his head. "Two, maybe. Coming down from the north bend, slow. Nobody comes down from the north bend slow unless they want to be heard coming." He looked at Ada, and then at Ilya, who had come up out of the hatch with tar to his elbows and a question already forming. "Get your people under my floor. Now. And I mean *under*, boy, not behind — I've hidden smugglers back there and I'll tell you, they found every last one."

"How long do we have?"

"Long enough if you stop asking questions."

* * *

The space beneath Toma's floor was not a hiding place so much as a punishment. It was a crawl of perhaps eighteen inches between the joists, floored with fish scales thirty years deep, smelling of tar and brine and something older that Ada declined to identify. She lay on her back with her cheek against a beam and Ilya pressed along her side, close enough that she could feel his heartbeat doing something faster than hers, which she found unreasonably comforting.

Above them, boards shifted. Toma's boots crossed the floor twice, unhurried. Then the door, and his voice, pitched to carry: "Sefa! Girl, bring the tea things down, we've company."

A pause long enough to say everything. Then Sefa's voice, rough with feigned sleep: "Company? At this hour?"

"Patrol," Toma said pleasantly. "Come to admire my ferry, no doubt."

Through the gap between two boards Ada could see a slice of the room: the table, the window, the gray light. She heard the engines die outside. Heard voices on the shingle — three, maybe four, boots on stone, the particular crispness of men who had never once carried anything heavier than their own opinions.

The door opened.

"Afternoon." A voice like a filing cabinet. "Meridian Consortium river patrol. Routine sweep."

"Of course, of course." Toma's chair creaked; he'd settled into it, she guessed, the way a mountain settles. "Routine. We get so few of those. Sit, sit. Sefa, tea."

"We won't take tea."

"Then you'll take tea," Toma said, "because refusing an old man's hospitality is bad luck on this water, and you gentlemen strike me as men who can't afford worse luck than you have."

Silence. Then the filing-cabinet voice again, tighter: "We're looking for three fugitives. A woman ranger, discharged. A man named Voss. And a boat captain. They were sighted running the narrows six days ago in a damaged vessel."

"Six days ago," Toma repeated, wonderingly. "Was that the day of the storm? Terrible weather. Half my nets went downstream. If a boat came through that, it wasn't damaged, it was blessed."

"It was reported damaged."

"Reported." Toma let the word sit in the room like a dead fish. "By whom?"

"The Consortium."

"Mm. And does the Consortium report to itself, or is there someone above even them? Because I've been on this river fifty years, young man, and I have learned that whenever a thing is *reported*, the truth has usually been filed somewhere else entirely."

Ada closed her eyes. *Don't push them, you magnificent old fool.* But she could hear the smile in his voice, and she understood suddenly that the pushing was the point — that Toma had done this arithmetic already, standing in his doorway with his tea, and had decided exactly how much of their attention he wanted on himself and none of it anywhere near his floorboards.

A boot scraped. Through the gap she saw a uniformed shoulder enter her slice of the world, then a hand — gloved, gesturing. "We're going to search the premises."

"Search away." Toma sounded delighted. "Mind the third floorboard, it bites. There's pickled fish under the bed my late wife would rise from her grave to defend. And if you find anything else, I'll thank you to arrest it properly, with paperwork, in triplicate — because I know how you people love your triplicate."

More silence. Longer. Ada realized she was holding Ilya's wrist without having decided to, and that he was holding hers back.

The boots moved. Out the door, across the shingle. Down toward the water.

Toward the boats.

* * *

What followed was twenty minutes that Ada would measure afterward in heartbeats.

They searched the *Kestrel* first. She heard them come aboard — the hull rocked, tarps dragged, Ilya's breath went shallow against her ear. One of them whistled at the repair work. "Somebody's been patching this tub."

"Fisher wrecked on the narrows," another voice said. "Half the river wrecks on those narrows."

"This isn't a fisher's patch job. Look at the seams. That's professional."

"Everything's professional lately. Hale's got the whole reach paranoid."

The name landed in Ada's chest like a dropped anchor. *Hale.* Even here, even among the patrolmen, the director's name was a weather system.

Then the boots came to Toma's ferry, and Ada heard the change in them — the briskness draining away, replaced by something slower, more deliberate. The ferry was the obvious place. The ferry was where anyone would hide anyone. She heard a hatch thrown open, storage bins overturned, Biscuit barking himself hoarse until someone threatened him and Toma's voice cut across the shingle like a whip:

"You leave that dog alone or you and I are going to have a conversation about manners, and I warn you, I'm older than your whole chain of command put together."

"Sir, step back."

"I am stepping back. Look at me. Stepping. Back."

Something crashed — deliberately, she thought, the sound of a bin lid being flung. Then the filing-cabinet voice, closer now, right above the floorboards, right above her face: "There's a crawlspace under here."

Ada's heart stopped being a heart and became a fist.

"There is," Toma agreed, from the doorway. "Fifty years of river rats have slept under this floor, son. You'll find nothing down there but the smell, and the smell will follow you home, and your wife will ask questions."

"I don't have a wife."

"No," Toma said, and there was something in his voice then that Ada never forgot as long as she lived — gentle, almost sorry. "No, I imagine you don't. Men who burn other men's boats rarely do."

A long pause. Long enough for the patrolman to decide whether he'd been insulted. Then: "Check it anyway."

Boots on the deck above her. A board pried. Gray light widened over her face, and Ada looked straight up into the eyes of a young man with a Consortium armband and a crowbar and the particular emptiness of someone following orders into a hole he didn't want to be in.

He looked down at her.

She looked back.

One second. Two. She watched him understand — watched it move across his face like weather: the woman in the crawlspace, the fugitive, the bounty, the report he would have to write, the career it might make him, the smell of fish scales and the old man in the doorway and whatever lived behind that old man's eyes. She watched him weigh all of it.

And then the young man lowered the crowbar, straightened up, and said, "Nothing down there but bilge."

"You sure?" the filing cabinet asked.

"Crawlspace runs to the keel. It's flooded aft. You want to go swimming in bilgewater, be my guest." He stepped back from the hatch. "Ferry's clean."

Ada lay in the dark and breathed and thought: *I will remember your face. Whatever happens, whoever wins, I will remember that you looked down and chose.*

* * *

They didn't leave.

That was the thing she hadn't planned for. The patrol finished its search, stood around on the shingle drinking Toma's tea after all because there was nothing else to drink and the morning was cold, and then the filing-cabinet voice said, casually, terribly: "We'll hold position here overnight. Sweep resumes downriver at dawn. Your berth's convenient."

Ada, folded back into the crawlspace with Ilya's knee in her ribs, heard Toma's pause. It lasted half a breath. Anyone else would have missed it.

"Course it is," Toma said. "Mi casa es su casa, as my grandmother used to say, though she stole the saying from a passenger and never gave it back."

They laughed. Boots tramped up to the hut. And in the dark under the floor, Ilya turned his head — carefully, carefully — until his mouth was near her ear.

"He can't feed four of them tonight," Ilya whispered. "He barely feeds us."

"He'll manage," Ada whispered back. "He'll make it a lesson, too. He'll probably serve the fish with a parable."

And he did. Through the boards that evening they heard it: Toma moving around his kitchen, telling the patrolmen about the flood of '09, about the year the river took the east bank and everyone learned which neighbors counted, and the patrolmen laughing at the right places, and Sefa's voice pitched low and hard through the wall of the inner room where she'd been ordered to stay out of sight, saying words Ada was grateful the walls mostly swallowed.

At midnight, Ada crawled out.

It was Toma who found her, an hour later, sitting on the cold shingle looking at his ferry. He lowered himself down beside her with the groan of a man who had earned every joint complaint he owned, and handed her half of something wrapped in cloth. Bread. Still warm. Of course it was.

"You should be under the floor," he said.

"The floor smells like a crime scene."

"So does the river, these days. We've just gotten used to it." He ate his half of the bread, watching the water. The patrol's lantern burned in his hut window. Two silhouettes moved behind it. "You're blaming yourself. I can see it from here. You've got a face built for blaming yourself — very efficient, no wasted motion."

"They came because of us."

"They came because of the ledger," Toma said. "We are all just the paper it's printed on." He nudged her shoulder with his own, gently, like mooring a small boat. "Besides. I've been waiting fifty years for them to notice me. A man hates to be overlooked."

"What did you do, Toma? Before this?"

He was quiet for a while. The river muttered against the stones.

"I ferried people," he said finally. "That's all. That's always been all. But you ferry long enough, on this water, and you learn that some nights the people crossing aren't running *from* something. They're carrying something. And you learn to look at the water and not the cargo." He brushed crumbs from his knees. "Your brother's crew. The *Cormorant*. I knew two of those men. Ferried them myself, spring of that year. Big fellow, Oskar — sang badly, tipped well."

Ada's throat closed. She hadn't said her brother's name aloud in the cove. Hadn't needed to; the ledger had said it for her, page forty-one, in a clerk's indifferent hand.

"Oskar," she managed.

"Good name," Toma said. "Worth carrying."

* * *

It happened at dawn, and it happened fast.

Ada woke to orange light that wasn't sunrise — flickering against the hut wall, against the underside of the clouds — and was moving before her mind caught up, barefoot onto the shingle, Ilya behind her, Sefa lurching out of the inner room with her taped ribs and her face white as bone.

Toma's ferry was burning.

Not smoldering — burning, wholly, cheerfully, flames climbing the mast and rolling off the tarred planks in black ropes, and four patrolmen stood on the shingle watching it with the studied blankness of men who had been ordered to watch and not intervene. The filing-cabinet officer stood apart, arms folded.

"Accident," he said, when he saw Ada. "Lamp oil stores. Tragic. Old boats, old mistakes."

Toma stood ten feet from the fire, and he was not looking at the fire. He was looking at the officer, and his face was perfectly calm, and that calm was the most frightening thing Ada had ever seen on a human being.

"Lamp oil," Toma repeated.

"Tragic," the officer said again, and Ada heard it now for what it was — not an accusation, not quite. A message. *We know you hid them. We can't prove it. So we'll take the thing you love and call it weather.* Her stomach turned over. This was how the Consortium worked; she'd known it in her bones since Kell's Landing, but knowing it in a file and watching it eat an old man's livelihood in the gray morning light were different animals entirely.

"Ferry's how you live," Ilya said quietly, beside her. Not a question.

"Ferry's how I've always lived," Toma said. "Which is not the same thing." He turned from the fire at last, and his eyes found Ada's, and there was no blame in them, which was somehow worse than blame, which was infinitely heavier. "Heat rises, girl. Fire climbs. But rivers run downhill, and everything on them ends up at the sea eventually. Including truth." He nodded toward the *Kestrel*, patched and ugly and afloat. "Your boat's ready. Caulking held through the night, didn't it? I checked at first light."

"You checked—" Ilya started.

"While your patrol friends slept. Yes. I also packed you food, because apparently I'm the only adult on this river." He said it dryly, but his voice cracked on *apparently*, just slightly, just once, and Ada saw Sefa see it too.

The patrol loaded into their launch and idled out into the current, unhurried, making a point of it. As they passed the burning ferry, the filing-cabinet officer touched two fingers to his cap. To Toma. Almost respectful. Ada decided she would spend the rest of her life deciding what that gesture meant.

The fire burned until midmorning. Toma watched all of it, tea in hand, and refused help, and refused sympathy, and finally sat down on the shingle next to Biscuit and scratched the dog's ears and said, to no one in particular:

"Well. Nothing left to be discreet about now."

Sefa sat down on his other side. She didn't say anything. She just stayed, which from Sefa was a speech.

It was Ilya who broke the silence, voice rough: "Toma. The pass. You said the glacier opens a window. When?"

Toma looked up at the high country, where the clouds had torn open at last to show white peaks going gold in the sun, and somewhere up there, invisible, a river of ice was grinding toward its once-a-year surrender.

"Day after tomorrow," he said. "Maybe three, if the monsoon argues. After that, the pass shuts until next season, and Hale's valley keeps its secrets another year." He drained his tea and stood, joints crackling like the dying embers behind him. "So. Day after tomorrow, you sail. And you'd best believe, children — they'll be watching the water now. Every mile of it."

Ada looked at the blackened skeleton of the ferry, at the shingle where it had rested for fifty years, at the old man standing straighter than he had any right to.

No going back. For any of them.

"Then we'd better be worth the watching," she said.
