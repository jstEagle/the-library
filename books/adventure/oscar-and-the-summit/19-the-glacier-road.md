## 19. The Glacier Road

Toma woke them before dawn with the smell of woodsmoke and fried fish, which was, Ada decided somewhere between sleep and waking, a far better alarm than any whistle she'd ever been issued.

"Up," he said, not unkindly, setting a dented pan on the flat stone he used as a stove. "The river's talking this morning. I want you to hear it before you argue with me."

Ada sat up in the lean-to he'd built against the hull of his half-dismantled ferry, her back stiff from three nights on planks that had opinions of their own. Across the cove, Ilya was already awake — or pretending to be, sitting on the overturned skiff with his arms around his knees, watching the water the way a man watches a door he's afraid will open.

The river *was* talking. She'd noticed it yesterday and filed it away without understanding: a low, rhythmic groaning under the usual rush of the current, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. This morning it was louder. Every few minutes a deep concussion rolled down the valley and thumped through the soles of her boots.

"That's the glacier," Toma said, catching her look. He handed her a tin plate with two fish and a heel of bread gone hard as shingle. "Karst Glacier. Twenty kilometers up-valley, but sound carries on water. It moves all year, slow as a miser counting coins. But come late monsoon, the meltwater gets underneath it and—" he made a gesture like a man shrugging off a coat "—it lets go."

"Lets go how?" Ilya asked.

"You'll hear." Toma lowered himself onto his stool by the fire with the care of a man whose knees had been negotiating with him for decades. "Now eat. Because what I have to tell you next, you'll want to be sitting down for anyway."

* * *

He drew the map in the ash at the edge of the fire ring, using a stick worn smooth as a spoon. Ada had seen rangers sketch terrain a hundred ways — charcoal on paper, knife-scratches on bark, once an entire route laid out in ration tins on a mess table — but there was something about the way Toma did it, unhurried, certain, that made the ash feel more reliable than any survey map she'd carried.

"The mining camp sits here." A short line. "At the mouth of the high valley, where the river comes off the ice. One road in, one road out, and both of them watched — you know this already, since your friend Sefa ran the narrows rather than knock politely."

"She doesn't knock," Ilya said. "It's a whole philosophy."

"The camp watches the road because the road is the only way anything heavy moves. Ore goes down by barge during low water. Men go up and down on foot. But the road ends here—" the stick stopped "—at the ice wall. Above the camp, the valley keeps climbing to the pass, and the pass is the only other way out. Over the top, down the far side, away from everything Hale owns."

"So we go over the pass," Ada said. "Simple."

"No," Toma said, with the mild finality of a man correcting a child who has just proposed swimming across the sea. "You cannot go over the pass, because the pass does not exist for most of the year. The glacier tongue fills the notch like a cork in a bottle. You could no more climb over it than climb over the inside of a bell. Except—"

He tapped the ash twice.

"—except when it calves."

The word landed strangely in the quiet cove. Ada had heard it before, in training, in old reports — the great ice sheets of the coast shedding bergs into the sea — but she'd never connected it to a mountain pass.

"When the meltwater gets under the tongue," Toma went on, "the whole face lets go at once. Ice falls into the lake behind the camp, the level drops, and for a day — maybe two, if the season is generous — there is a gap in the wall. A corridor. The old people called it the glacier road. My father took cargo through it. Sheep, salt, brides." A dry rustle that might have been his laugh. "Once, a fugitive magistrate. That was a good year."

"And when the window closes?"

"The tongue advances again. Not fast — a body length a day, then slower. But anyone caught in the corridor when it starts closing is caught in a lane walled with ice that weighs more than a mountain and is actively trying to lie down." He looked up at her, firelight pooling in the seams of his face. "So. You do not dawdle on the glacier road."

"How long until it calves?" Ilya asked. His voice had gone careful, the voice of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

Toma considered the river, the smell of the air, some private ledger of his own. "Four days. Five if the storms hold off. Six if we're lucky, and we have not been lucky lately, so plan for four."

Ada felt it settle over the little camp like a change in pressure: four days. Four days to finish patching a boat that had taken a bullet below the waterline, to get up-valley past a mining camp crawling with Consortium men, to position themselves at an ice wall that would open exactly once and only briefly, and then to cross a glacier pass in monsoon weather carrying a ledger that half the valley wanted burned.

She ate her fish. It seemed important to keep her hands busy.

* * *

Sefa surfaced from the boathouse mid-morning, moving stiffly, her bandaged shoulder held the way a woman holds a grudge — close, protective, and with intent to use it later. The wound was bad; everyone knew it was bad; Sefa had made it clear that acknowledging this aloud constituted a personal insult. She'd spent two days below decks "supervising" the repairs, which meant lying on a cot shouting instructions at Ilya while he caulked seams, and had emerged now only because Toma had promised news.

He gave it to her plainly, repeating the shape of the morning's talk: the camp, the wall, the calving, the window. Four days.

Sefa listened with her jaw working, staring up-valley at the white shoulder of the glacier visible between the ridgelines, thin as a scar.

"I've heard the boom every night this week," she said finally. "Thought it was rockfall." She turned to Ada. "Your ranger's maps. Do they show the pass? Elevation, exposure, anything?"

"They show a blank space and a note that says 'unreliable,'" Ada said. "Officially, the high valley doesn't exist. Unofficially—" she thought of the drowned villages, the bought magistrates, the names scraped off manifests "—officially is the problem."

Sefa snorted. Then she winced, because snorting pulled the wound, and glared at her own shoulder as though it had betrayed her too.

"Four days," she said. "The hull needs three, if Ilya stops caulking like he's apologizing to the wood. That leaves us one day to get past the camp. In daylight. On a river they're watching."

"We don't go past the camp," Ada said slowly. The idea had been assembling itself all morning, piece by piece, the way Toma built things — patiently, from whatever was at hand. "We go *to* it."

Three faces turned toward her.

"Hale knows someone's coming. He's known since the narrows. He'll be watching the river, the road, the treeline — every approach except the one nobody uses, because nobody can use it except during a calving." She tapped the ash map, right on the line of the ice wall. "But the corridor opens *behind* his camp. If we time it right, we come down the glacier road after the window opens, and the camp is between us and the pass — no. Ahead of us and below us. We drop in on him from the direction nothing has ever come from."

Silence. Down the cove, the river boomed again, patient and enormous, like a drumbeat keeping time for something very large that hadn't yet decided to stand up.

"That's either brilliant or insane," Sefa said at last.

"In my experience those arrive together," said Toma.

Ilya hadn't spoken. Ada glanced over and found him looking at the ash map with an expression she'd learned to read on the river — the auditor's face, the one that weighed risk in columns. But there was something else under it now, something rawer.

"There'll be people in that camp who aren't there by choice," he said quietly. "Contract laborers. Families. When the ledger goes public, Hale burns everything and runs, and the workers are left holding the smoke. If we come in from above—" he stopped, started again. "If we do this, we do it knowing what we're walking into. All of it."

"All of it," Ada agreed.

It was strange, she thought, how naturally the words came now. Two months ago she'd have said *that's above my contract*. Two months ago she'd been a different animal wearing the same coat.

* * *

The afternoon went to work, and the work was good for all of them, because work is a place to put your hands when your head is too loud.

Sefa directed hull repairs from a chair Toma dragged to the waterline, issuing verdicts on caulking with the gravity of a judge. Ilya had gotten genuinely competent with pitch and oakum — Ada had watched the transformation over the past week, from a man who handled tools like borrowed pens to one who knew the boat's moods — and today he worked steadily, humming something tuneless, pausing whenever Sefa barked.

Ada split wood. It was the job Toma assigned her and she suspected he'd chosen it deliberately: something simple, repetitive, impossible to do while brooding. She split wood and listened to the glacier boom and let the rhythm do its quiet surgery.

Toma himself disappeared up the goat track above the cove for two hours and came back with a coil of old rope, a crate of pitons, and a look of grim satisfaction.

"My father's gear," he said, dropping the crate by the fire. "For the crossing. Rope's sound — hemp keeps better than men do. Spikes are rusty but rust is mostly a moral problem." He counted out lengths with thick fingers. "You'll rope together on the ice. All four of you. And you'll trust the rope."

"Even the person at the other end of it," Ada said. It wasn't quite a question.

"*Especially*," Toma said, "the person at the other end of it. That is the entire technology of the rope. Everything else is decoration."

That evening, cooking rice over the fire, Ilya drifted over and stood beside her at the woodpile, taking the axe from her hands without asking. He split the next log in two clean strokes. Show-off, she didn't say.

"Four days," he said.

"Four days."

"When it opens — when we're actually standing on it — what does it feel like? The calving. Did Toma say?"

"He said you'll hear it." She fed another log to the pile. "He also said his father took brides across once, so apparently it's survivable enough for wedding parties."

"A wedding party on a glacier." Ilya set the axe down and leaned on it. "Somebody should write that down. Before the company finishes erasing everything else, somebody should write down that people got married on the glacier road."

Ada looked at him. Firelight, tired eyes, pitch stains to the elbow, and underneath all of it that stubborn decency he kept trying to hide behind irony and failed. Her chest did the thing it had been doing lately, the small unauthorized expansion, and she looked back at the woodpile before it could become a conversation they weren't ready for.

"Somebody already is," she said. "It's in the ledger. Page forty-one. Grain shipments, bribes, and one wedding." She'd read it three times now; she knew the ledger the way she knew her own beat reports. "Hale's clerks recorded everything, even the parts that made them look human. They never imagined anyone would read it as anything but evidence."

Ilya was quiet a moment. "My handwriting's on page forty-one," he said. "The countersignature. I approved the grain. I told myself it was the least-bad signature I could give." He picked the axe back up, swung harder than the log required. "Four days is enough time to be terrified properly. Good. I'd hate to walk onto that ice under-terrified."

"You won't be alone on the rope," Ada said.

He glanced at her sideways. "No," he agreed. "I suppose that's the point of it."

* * *

She woke in the black hours before dawn to the loudest boom yet — close enough now that grit sifted from the lean-to's ridgepole, close enough that the boats at their moorings knocked together like nervous teeth — and lay listening as the echo rolled away up-valley and died against the heights.

Then, fainter, from the direction of the fire: Toma's voice, low, talking to the river in the old way, the way people talked to rivers before anyone decided rivers were property.

Ada got up quietly and walked to the waterline. The sky was clearing for the first time in days, stars sharp over the ridge, and up-valley the glacier's shoulder had come into view properly for the first time — vast, blue-white, faintly luminous, like a sleeping animal whose breathing moved the ground.

"It's early," Toma said without turning. He'd heard her; of course he had. "The meltwater's ahead of schedule. Warm rains last month." He nodded up at the shining mass. "Could be three days now. Could be two."

"Two," Ada repeated.

"Mm." He poked the coals. "The window opens when it opens. The glacier doesn't negotiate, and it doesn't send notice." He looked at her then, and in the starlight his old face was hard to read, but his voice was gentle. "Whatever you mean to do at that camp, girl — decide it tonight. There won't be time to decide it on the ice."

Behind her, she heard Ilya stir in his sleep, and Sefa turn over with a hiss of pain she'd deny in the morning, and the river running steady toward the sea with everything they carried hidden in its hull.

Two days.

Ada stood a long time at the waterline, watching the glacier breathe, and began to decide.
