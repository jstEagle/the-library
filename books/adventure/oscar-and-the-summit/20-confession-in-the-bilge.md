## 20. Confession in the Bilge

The rain had settled into a rhythm by the fourth day in Toma's cove—not the screaming sideways assault of the first monsoon, but a steady, patient drumming on the tarp stretched over the boat's open hull, like someone upstairs tapping out a message nobody wanted to read.

Ada lay flat on her back in the bilge with a caulking iron in one hand and oakum in the other, sealing seams she could barely see. The work was miserable and exact. You packed the fiber into the gap, drove it home, slathered it with pitch, and then you did the next one, and the next one, and if your mind wandered for even a moment you'd drive the iron into your own thumb, which she had now done twice.

"Third time's the tradition," Ilya said from the other side of the keel. He was doing the same work, mirrored, and they spoke through the ribs of the boat like two people talking through a fence.

"I'm aware."

"You're bleeding again."

"I'm aware of that too."

He appeared above her, upside down, holding out a rag. She took it without looking up, which required a kind of trust she didn't examine too closely. His hands were stained black to the wrist. There was pitch in his hair. Three days ago he hadn't known which end of a caulking mallet to hold, and now he worked seams nearly as fast as she did, because that was the thing about Ilya Voss—whatever he turned his attention to, he learned with an appetite that bordered on greed. Currents. Knots. The names of clouds. Her.

That last one worried her more than the others.

* * *

Sefa spent the days ashore at Toma's fire, sleeping mostly, her shoulder wound wrapped in poultices that smelled like pine tar and something sharper underneath. She said little. When she did speak it was usually to correct their technique or to announce, apropos of nothing, that the fish in this cove were cowards and deserved what they got.

Toma himself came down to the boat each evening, lowered himself onto the gunwale with the groan of a man whose joints kept their own ledger, and inspected their work in silence. On the second night he'd run his thumb along a seam Ada had finished and said, "Hm," which she had come to understand was the highest praise available in the cove.

The repairs were going well. That was the problem. Every seam they sealed was a day closer to the glacier road, and every day the ledger sat wrapped in oilcloth under the stern boards, growing heavier in a way that had nothing to do with weight.

Ada knew what was in it. She'd read enough—the claim numbers, the dates, the neat columns where human beings had been converted into line items marked *site cleared*. She knew her brother's crew was in there somewhere, reduced to a notation. What she didn't know—what she'd been circling for days like a bird over open water—was how Ilya had gotten hold of it. A thief who stole paper instead of money. A thief who read ledgers the way she read weather. A thief who, when Consortium agents had put a boot to his ribs at the well, had curled around the satchel instead of his own head.

Tonight, while Sefa slept by the fire and the rain kept its patient time, she decided she would ask him straight out.

She didn't get the chance. He asked himself first.

* * *

They were working the last three seams near the transom, the worst of them, in a space so cramped they had to take turns breathing. Ilya was wedged against the hull with the lantern between his feet, and he'd gone quiet in a way that wasn't his usual quiet. His usual quiet was comfortable, almost smug—the quiet of a man enjoying his own thoughts. This quiet had edges.

"You're driving that seam crooked," Ada said.

"I know." He didn't fix it. He set the iron down carefully, the way you set down something you might need to pick up quickly, and looked at her across the lantern light. "Ada. Before we go up there. Before the pass, and the camp, and whatever Hale has waiting—I have to tell you something, and you're going to want to hit me with that mallet, and I'd ask you to aim for the shoulder."

Her stomach dropped in a way that had nothing to do with the boat rocking. "Ilya."

"I didn't steal the ledger."

"You said—"

"I said I took it. Which is true. But stealing implies—" He stopped. Started again. Rain drummed on the tarp overhead, filling the pause, patient as ever. "I was the Consortium's auditor. Meridian Consolidated, Southern Reaches division. For six years. It was my signature on the accident assessments. All of them."

The lantern flame stood perfectly still between them, as if it were listening too.

"The *what*?"

"Auditor. I verified claims. When a site needed clearing—a homestead, a fishing crew, a survey team that saw something they shouldn't—there was paperwork. There's always paperwork, that's the whole genius of it. An assessment of liability. A finding of misadventure. A payout schedule for next of kin, calculated to the day." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "My job was to make sure the numbers balanced. And they always balanced, Ada. That was the horror of it. Murder is expensive and messy, but murder done properly balances beautifully."

Ada found she was gripping the caulking iron. She set it down. Slowly. "Your brother's crew," she said, before she could stop herself, and watched him flinch. "You told me at the well you took the ledger because of what happened to my brother's run. You made it sound like—like you'd discovered it. Like you were—" 

"I was the one who signed off on it." He said it plainly, no flinch this time, taking the blow full in the face like a man who'd been practicing. "Run 44, out of Kell's Landing. Nine souls. Capsized in weather the manifests said was impossible for the season. I wrote the assessment myself. *Unforeseeable meteorological event.* I remember being pleased with the phrasing. Unforeseeable. As though the storm hadn't been scheduled."

The bilge seemed to tilt, though the boat was beached and still. Ada thought of her brother's name in her mouth, the only prayer she had. Thought of handing this man water on the riverboat. Teaching him to read current—or rather, letting him think she was teaching him. Sleeping ten feet away from him for eleven nights.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because we're about to walk into a mining camp full of men who know my face, and you deserve to decide whether you're walking in with me or turning me over." He almost smiled. "Also because you've been asking with your eyes for four days and it was starting to feel rude."

"I have not."

"You absolutely have. You do this thing where you look at me and then look at the ledger and then look at me again, like a magistrate deciding sentence. Very flattering, actually. I've never been sentenced by anyone I liked before."

"Don't." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "Don't do that. Don't make it charming. Not this."

He nodded, and the charm went out of him like a lamp turned down, and what was left underneath was worse—younger, and tired, and afraid. "No. You're right. I'm sorry. It's a habit. Six years of making terrible things presentable." He pulled the oilcloth bundle from under the stern boards and set it between them, but didn't unwrap it. "Do you want to know why I defected? Because it wasn't conscience, not at first. I'll spare you the redemption story. It was a clerical error."

"A clerical error."

"There was a village upriver—Kovac's Landing, you won't find it on any map anymore—and the payout schedule went out with the wrong surname attached. A widow received compensation for a death that hadn't happened yet. Two weeks early. She wrote to the regional office to report the mistake, bless her, thought it was fraud against *her*, wanted to return the money." His jaw tightened. "And I realized, sitting there with her letter in my hand, that somewhere in the system there was a document saying her husband was already dead. Approved. Scheduled. And I had either signed it or would sign it, and I couldn't tell which, because I signed forty of them a month and I'd stopped reading the names. I'd stopped reading the *names*, Ada. They were just load-bearing figures to me."

"So you ran. With the ledger."

"So I ran. With the ledger. And I told myself it was penance, and maybe some of it was, but mostly I was terrified, because once you've seen the machine from inside, you know exactly how it handles people who see it from inside." He looked at her then, directly, and didn't look away. "I lied to you at the well. I let you believe I was a thief because a thief gets caught and hanged, and an auditor who defects gets disappeared, and I thought—if you knew what I was, you'd leave me for the agents at that well and sleep fine afterward. I was trying to manage you. It's what I did for a living."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere up the slope, a log collapsed in Toma's fire, sending up a brief orange bloom through the trees.

Ada discovered she was crying, which infuriated her, because these weren't even her tears yet—they were pre-grief, standing in line ahead of the real thing. "My brother's name," she said. "Was it just a figure to you? When you wrote it?"

"Yes." No flinch. No softening. He gave her the ugly truth whole, like a man laying a weapon on the ground between them. "Yes. And then later, after Kovac's Landing, I went back and read every file I'd ever closed, all six years, and I found his name and I read it a hundred times trying to make it hurt the way it should, and it never quite did, because you can't grieve on demand. That's the punishment they don't list in the payout schedules. You spend the rest of your life reading names."

Ada wiped her face with the back of her wrist, leaving a streak of pitch across her cheekbone. She thought about hitting him. She honestly considered it. The mallet was right there.

Instead she said: "The agents at the well. They weren't sent for a thief."

"No. They were sent for me specifically. Hale keeps a short list."

"And the bounty job. The one Meridian posted. 'Deliver alive.'" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "They hired me to retrieve their own auditor. I tracked a man across the salt flats for a company that killed my brother, and I felt *proud* of it. I was good at it. I was so damnably good at it."

"You were," he agreed quietly. "You are. It's how they get people like us. They don't hire the corrupt, Ada. Corrupt people ask questions. They hire the grieving and the indebted and the very, very competent, and they point us at things." He paused. "I know because I used to write the contracts."

She stared at him. The lantern hissed. And beneath the fury—which was real, and enormous, and would need days or years to burn down—she felt something else settle into place, cold and clear as the cove water. Because if the Consortium wrote contracts designed to catch the grieving and the indebted, then the contract she'd signed after her brother died—the one that had bound her to them, the debt she'd never been able to name properly—

It hadn't been an accident either. It had been a design. Someone had audited *her* grief and found it useful.

"I need to be alone," she said.

"Of course." He gathered his tools, moving carefully, a man in the presence of something unstable. At the gunwale he stopped. "For what it's worth—the ledger has everything. Signatures, dates, my handwriting on half of it. If we get it downriver, it doesn't just hang them. It hangs me with them. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm telling you I've already convicted myself. You'd just be agreeing with the verdict."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you feel *sure*. There's a difference." He climbed out into the rain, and she heard his footsteps fade up toward the fire, and then it was just her and the lantern and the boat they'd mended together, lying in the bilge like a woman at the bottom of a well.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in the dark with the ledger in her lap and unwrapped it, though she knew its contents by heart now, and she ran her thumb along the edge of the pages the way Toma ran his thumb along her seams. Looking for the crooked place. Finding it everywhere.

Here was the thing she kept arriving at, from every direction, like a current bending back on itself: she had spent two years carrying a debt she couldn't name, and tonight the man in the bilge had named it for her. It wasn't owed to Meridian. It had never been owed to Meridian. The contract was bait; the grief was the hook; and the debt—the true one, the only one—was written in nine names on a manifest she'd never been allowed to read.

Somewhere up the slope, Ilya sat by Toma's fire with his confession still wet on him, waiting to learn whether he'd sleep alone tonight or in custody, or not at all.

Ada wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth, tied it, and held it a moment longer than she needed to.

Then she reached for her boots. There was someone by that fire she needed to talk to—and it wasn't Ilya.
