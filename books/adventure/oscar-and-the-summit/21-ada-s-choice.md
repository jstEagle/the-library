## 21. Ada's Choice

Ada didn't sleep.

She lay in the stern of the half-mended boat with her bedroll pulled to her chin and listened to the cove breathe — water lapping at the hull, Toma's stove ticking as it cooled, somewhere in the dark the glacier muttering to itself like an old man arguing with a dream. Across the boat, Ilya slept the way guilty men sleep: on his side, curled small, one hand open near his face as if he'd fallen asleep mid-apology.

*He was the auditor.* The words kept turning over in her like river stones. *He signed off on the accidents.*

She had spent three days hating a thief. Now she had to reorganize her whole heart around a defector, and the furniture wouldn't fit.

But that wasn't what kept her awake, and she knew it. She rolled onto her back and looked up through the gap where the canopy had torn away, at the monsoon clouds moving over the peaks like slow ships.

What kept her awake was a form. A single sheet of paper, cream-colored, with a Meridian Consortium watermark she could still draw from memory — she'd filled out so many of them. A cargo manifest. Run number 4471. And in the field marked *BOOKING AGENT*, in her own careful ranger's handwriting, learned from her father who had learned it from his:

*A. Nystrom.*

Her brother Oscar had died on a run she booked.

---

It hadn't felt like a decision at the time. That was the thing about complicity — it never arrived wearing its own face. It arrived as paperwork. As a favor. As a Tuesday.

Three years ago, spring, the depot at Salt Hollow smelling of diesel and frying onions. Oscar had come to her desk grinning, dust on his boots, talking too fast the way he did when he was excited and trying to hide it.

"They want a crew for the high run," he'd said. "Karst Reach, all the way to the survey stations. Double rate."

"Nobody runs the high reach in spring," Ada had said, not looking up from her ledger. "Ice comes down off the shoulders wrong this time of year."

"Meridian's offering hazard pay."

"Meridian's offering hazard pay because people die, Os. That's what hazard pay *means*. It's the price they've calculated for your life, rounded down."

He'd laughed and stolen a date biscuit off her desk, which he always did, which she always pretended not to notice. "Come on, Freckles. You know the dispatcher. Put in a good word. I need the money — Mama's roof, remember? And after this run I'm done. I'll take the coastal work. Fish boats. Boring as church."

She had known the dispatcher. Knew him well enough to share coffee with, well enough that when she said *my brother's on this one, keep it clean*, he'd nodded and made a note. And the run had gone out on schedule, with a full crew, into a season she'd flagged as dangerous in writing two months earlier — a memo that had been acknowledged by a regional office and filed under a reference number she'd never been able to find again.

The official report said icefall. Natural causes. A tragedy of timing.

The report had been countersigned by a Meridian safety auditor.

Ada had read that name once, three years ago, in a stack of closing documents, and thought nothing of it. *I. Voss.* Just a name among names, a signature among signatures, the machinery doing what machinery does.

Now that name was sleeping six feet away from her in a leaking boat, and the machinery had a face, and the face looked young and tired and ashamed, and none of it changed the arithmetic at all.

Because here was the arithmetic, and she could no longer avoid it: if the Consortium bought deaths — if the ledger Ilya carried listed run 4471 alongside eleven other "accidents," each with a payout, each with a signature — then her brother hadn't died of icefall. He'd died of a line item. And she was the one who'd written him into the column marked *available*.

Hazard pay, rounded down.

---

She got up before dawn because lying still had become unbearable. Toma was already at his fire, boiling something that smelled like pine and regret.

"You didn't sleep," he said. Not a question. He poured a second cup without being asked and held it out.

"Neither did you, by the look of you."

"I'm old. Sleep and I are acquaintances now, not friends." He watched her sit on the stone beside him, cup cradled in both hands against the pre-dawn chill. For a while neither spoke. Then Toma said, gently, in the way of a man who has spent twenty years alone and has forgotten how to be tactful: "The boy's confession went badly?"

"It went honestly," Ada said. "That's worse."

"Hm." Toma poked the fire. "You know, when I found out what the company had done in the valley — back then, twenty years ago, different name, same hands — I didn't sleep for a season. I kept thinking: I ferried them. I ferried their men upriver, their crates, their instruments. I took their coin. I told myself I was just a ferryman, that the river carries what the river carries." He shook his head. "But the river doesn't choose, Ada. We do. Every crossing is a choice."

"I booked my brother's run."

Toma went still. The fire popped.

"He came to me excited," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "Double rate. Hazard pay. I told him it was dangerous and then I picked up my pen and I wrote his name on the manifest anyway, because he needed the money and because I wanted to help him and because — " She stopped. Started again. "Because it never occurred to me that the danger wasn't weather. You check the ice, you check the gear, you check the crew. Nobody teaches you to check whether the company wants the crew to come home."

"And now you know."

"Now I know." She stared into the fire. "Toma, Hale offered me a deal. Back at the camp, before — he offered to clear my debt, wipe my record, everything, if I delivered Ilya and burned the ledger. And I said no. I thought I said no for good reasons. But sitting here in the dark, I have to ask myself an ugly question." She turned to look at the old man. "Was I saying no to *him*? Or was I just saying no because burning the ledger would burn the proof of what I did? Because if my name's in that book — and it might be, there were agent fields on those contracts — then every page of it is a mirror, and I've been carrying a mirror up a mountain telling myself it's a weapon."

Toma considered this for a long moment, the way he seemed to consider everything, at geological speed.

"When my sister drowned," he said finally, "I hated the river for ten years. Ten years I wouldn't fish, wouldn't swim, wouldn't even wash in it properly — I'd heat water on the stove like a fool. Then one day my neighbor, a woman older than the hills, said to me: *the river took her, but the river didn't owe you anything. The question is who you owe.*" He shrugged. "I didn't understand it for another ten years. But I think maybe you understand it tonight."

"The dead," Ada said quietly.

"The dead," Toma agreed. "Not the company. Not the law. Not your guilt, either — guilt is just debt wearing a costume. The dead themselves. What do they want? Not your suffering. Suffering is cheap; anyone can produce it. They want their names said. They want it to mean something. They want the next crew to come home."

Ada looked across the cove, toward the boat, toward the shape of Ilya still asleep under the torn canopy.

"He signed the reports," she said. "He signed the one for my brother's run. I read his name three years ago and felt nothing, and he sat in an office and felt nothing, and between the two of us we produced exactly enough nothing for eleven crews to die in."

"Yes," Toma said. "And?"

"And what?"

"And so what do you do?" Toma spread his hands, firelight catching the creases of his palms. "You can hate him. You can hate yourself. Both are easy; I recommend neither, they're very filling and have no nutrition. Or — " he leaned forward — "you can do the only thing that has ever made any difference in this valley, or any valley. You carry the truth downriver. All of it. Even the parts that indict you. *Especially* those parts. A truth with pieces missing isn't truth, it's just a better lie."

---

Ilya woke to the smell of pine tea and the sound of Ada Nystrom sharpening her knife, which he was learning was simply how she processed emotion, like other people went for walks.

She was sitting on the gunwale, whetstone whispering along the blade, and she didn't stop when she saw him stir. She waited until he'd sat up, hair flattened on one side, eyes wary.

"We need to talk," she said.

"That's what people say right before the knife comes into the conversation."

"Smartass." But there was no edge in it. She set the knife down, which somehow alarmed him more. "Last night you told me what you were. Auditor. The man who signed off on the accidents. My brother's accident included."

Ilya's breath went out of him. He'd been waiting for this since the words left his mouth in the bilge — waiting for it the way you wait for a wave you can't outrun. "Ada —"

"Let me finish." She held up a hand. "I've had one night and a very old man to think, which turns out to be a dangerous combination. Here's what I've got." She counted on her fingers, brisk as a supply inventory. "One: you signed the reports. That's true, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't, and you're not going to pretend it's forgivable just because you feel bad now. Two: you walked away. Most people don't. Most people take the salary and the house and the pleasant numbness until they die of it. You stole a ledger instead, which is the single most insane act of conscience I've ever heard of, and I include my own life in that assessment. Three — "

She stopped. Her jaw worked. When she continued, her voice had dropped, gone rough.

"Three. I booked my brother's last run. Run 4471. Karst Reach, spring season. I knew it was dangerous and I wrote his name on the manifest because he needed the money for our mother's roof." She looked at him straight on, and her eyes were dry in the way that eyes get when they're past the country where drying happens. "So when I say I know what those signatures cost, I'm not speaking theoretically. Your pen and mine were on the same page, Ilya. Maybe literally. There may be an agent field with my name in it. Which means I don't get to be the avenging ranger in this story. I'm a co-signer."

Ilya opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "I didn't know," he said finally, uselessly. "About your brother. About any of them, really — I knew the numbers, I knew the pattern, but knowing numbers isn't — " He dragged a hand through his hair. "The day I understood, actually understood, I was standing in the records room and I realized I could recite the payout schedule from memory. Like a prayer. Like something I'd memorized for church. Eleven incidents, eleven settlements, all within four percent of projected savings. Four percent, Ada. They killed eleven crews to save four percent, and I had *memorized it*, and my first feeling wasn't horror. It was professional admiration. That's when I knew what I'd become." His voice cracked on the last word. "I'm sorry. I know it's worthless. Sorry is the cheapest currency there is and I'm spending it anyway because it's all I have."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere up-valley, the glacier boomed — a deep, tectonic groan, the sound of a thing deciding.

"Here's what I've decided," she said. "Not how I feel. Feelings can wait; feelings are like weather, they'll change on their own schedule regardless of our plans. I've decided what I *owe*, and to whom." She held up the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on the thwart between them. "This isn't yours anymore. It stopped being yours the moment you took it. And it was never mine, and it was never Hale's, and it was sure as hell never the Consortium's. It belongs to them." She tapped the cover. "To Oscar's crew. To Sefa's sister. To the eleven. To the valley Toma lost. The dead are the creditors now, and the debt gets paid in one currency only: the truth, delivered whole, delivered everywhere, with our names attached to our parts in it. Yours as signer. Mine as booking agent. All of it."

Ilya stared at her. "They'll come for us. If we put our own names in the testimony —"

"They're already coming for us. Have you *met* us?" And then, despite everything, despite the night she'd had, a ghost of a smile crossed her face — quick and crooked and real. "Besides. Think about what Hale fears most. Not the ledger — ledgers can be disputed, dismissed, called forgeries. What he can't survive is the story being true *and* the tellers being honest about their own guilt. A martyr with clean hands is a symbol. Two confessed accomplices handing over the evidence against themselves?" She shook her head. "That's not a symbol. That's a verdict. No magistrate in the coast districts could bury that and keep his seat."

"You'd lose everything," Ilya said quietly. "Your badge. Your record. Everything you built."

Ada looked out at the cove — at Toma's burned ferry hull beached like a whale skeleton, at the grey water, at the glacier shouldering up into the clouds. She thought about her badge, the weight of it, the way it had gotten lighter every year as the law it represented bent itself around company money. She thought about a cream-colored form and a field marked *booking agent*.

"I already lost everything that mattered," she said. "Three years ago, on paper I signed myself. Everything since has just been interest." She stood, and slung the oilcloth bundle over her shoulder, and looked down at him with something new in her face — not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground where forgiveness might someday be planted, if the season allowed. "Get up, Voss. The window opens in two days, and we've got copies to make and a pass to cross and a director to ruin. The dead aren't getting any more patient, and neither am I."

Ilya scrambled up after her, and as he reached the shore he caught her arm. "Ada. For what it's worth — Oscar. Was he anything like you?"

She paused. Something moved behind her eyes, old and warm and hurt.

"Worse," she said. "He laughed at everything. Especially danger. Especially me." She looked up the valley, toward the white line of the pass. "Which is probably why I'm going to see this through to the top of the world. Somebody has to laugh at it for him."

Above them, the glacier groaned again — longer this time, deeper, a sound like a door beginning, very slowly, to open.
