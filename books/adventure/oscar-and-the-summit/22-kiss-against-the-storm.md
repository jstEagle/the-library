## 22. Kiss Against the Storm

The storm came back that evening, and with it, the smell of Toma's cooking.

Ada smelled it before she heard it—onions and river fish and something resinous he scraped from the pines above his cove—and her stomach made a decision her pride hadn't authorized. She'd spent the day recaulking seams on the *Marta's* hull in a drizzle that couldn't decide whether to be rain or fog, and her hands were gray with pitch, her shoulders ached in a way that felt structural, and she wanted, more than she had wanted anything in weeks, to sit down and not think.

She found Ilya already at Toma's fire, under the lean-to of driftwood and old ferry canvas, holding a bowl like it was something precious. He looked up when she came in, and there was that thing in his face again—the one she kept pretending not to catalog. The softening. The way his whole expression rearranged itself around her arrival, like a room being tidied for a guest.

"You look," he said carefully, "like a woman who has been fighting a boat."

"The boat won."

"Did you get the stern seam?"

"Mostly." She held out her hands, black to the wrist. "I got most of it. If we hit anything harder than bathwater tomorrow night, we'll find out how much of it was mostly."

Toma grunted from his stool by the fire without looking up. He was rendering fat in a dented pan, and he had the particular stillness of a man who had learned long ago that the best contribution to other people's moods was silence and food. "Sit," he said. "Eat. Argue after."

"We weren't arguing."

"You will," Toma said placidly. "You argue like weather. It builds all day and breaks after dark."

Ilya laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, and Ada felt it land somewhere below her ribs and stay there. She sat down across the fire from him because sitting next to him had started to feel like a decision she wasn't ready to make, and not sitting next to him had started to feel like one too.

They ate. The rain thickened against the canvas overhead, drumming, then easing, then drumming again, and Toma told a long, rambling story about a moose he'd once ferried across the Reach—"not by choice, mind, it swam out to meet us, and what was I going to do, charge it fare?"—and Ada laughed until her face hurt, and realized somewhere in the middle of it that this was the first time in longer than she could name that she had laughed without checking over her shoulder first.

That was the trouble with coves. They made you forget you were hunted.

* * *

Later, when Toma had gone to check his fish traps in the last gray light—"the eels run stupid in a storm, they come right to you, no sense wasted"—Ada and Ilya ended up alone under the lean-to with the fire burned down to coals and the rain coming steady now, a real monsoon rain, the kind that erased the far shore entirely.

She was working on the compass.

It had become a habit, these past days in the cove. She'd take it out of its oilcloth wrap and turn it over in her hands, needleless, dead, its glass clouded where the crystal had cracked years ago, and she'd hold it up as if some part of it might remember what it was for. Her father's compass. Her brother's before it was hers. Broken since the salt flats, or maybe broken long before that and she'd only just noticed.

"It doesn't work," Ilya said. Not a question. He'd watched her do this every night.

"Nothing works out here. That's why I keep checking."

"May I?"

She almost said no. It was hers, the way the debt was hers, the way the guilt was hers—carried so long it had grown into the bone. But she set it in his palm anyway, and watched him turn it over with those auditor's hands, precise and gentle, thumbs finding the seam of the case like he was reading it.

"The pivot's gone," he murmured. "And the needle's demagnetized—or it lost its magnet, which is worse, because you can't remagnetize a needle without another magnet, and there's no magnet within two hundred miles of this cove except maybe Toma's personality." He looked up. "But the housing's sound. Someone who knew what they were doing could rebuild it. Given time. Given parts."

"There's no time. There's a glacier calving in—" She stopped. Counted. "Two days."

"I know." He set the compass down between them on the plank bench, carefully, like it might wake. "Two days."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere out on the black water, thunder rolled along the mountains and took its time dying.

"Ilya." She said his name and then didn't know what came after it, which was unlike her; she was a woman who always knew what came after, who navigated by bearing and consequence. "Tomorrow night, when we go through the pass—"

"When we go through the pass," he agreed, watching her.

"If it goes badly. If Hale's people are waiting on the far side, if the ice takes the boat, if any of the forty ways this can end finds us—" She made herself say it plainly, because she'd buried enough people who'd died on euphemisms. "I need you to know that I don't regret it. Any of it. The checkpoint, the narrows, Toma's ferry burning—I'd do all of it again. I chose this. I want you to know that I *chose* it, so that whatever happens, you're not carrying the weight of my choice along with your own."

He was quiet for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face.

"That's a strange kind of gift," he said finally. "Absolution in advance."

"It's not absolution. It's inventory. I'm an accountant of a different kind than you were. I count what I owe, and I've decided who I owe it to, and none of it is the Consortium, and none of it is you."

"And yourself? What do you owe yourself?"

The question went through her like a splinter finding its depth. She looked out at the rain instead of at him. "I'll settle that account later. If there is a later."

"There's a later," he said. "I've decided. I did the math."

"You did the math."

"I'm very good at math. It's how I ended up here—good at math, bad at conscience, until the conscience arrived late and loud." He turned toward her fully now, and the coal-light caught the tired lines around his eyes, the bruise fading yellow along his jaw from the narrows. "Ada. Can I tell you something that isn't about the ledger, or the pass, or Hale, or any of the machinery we've been living inside for a month?"

The word *machinery*. She almost smiled. He talked about the world like that sometimes, like everything human was gears and loads and tolerances, and she'd come to love it in a way she hadn't examined closely, because examining it would mean admitting what it was.

"Tell me," she said.

"I was an auditor for eleven years. Do you know what an auditor actually does? Everyone thinks we find wrongdoing. We don't. We find *explanations*. Somebody hands you a ledger full of beautiful explanations—accidents, attrition, acts of God—and your job is to decide whether the explanations add up. And for eleven years mine added up, because I wanted them to, because the alternative was admitting that the numbers were people." His voice stayed level, but his hands had closed around each other, knuckles white. "Then I met you, and you were the opposite of an explanation. You were a fact. Stubborn, competent, impossible, standing in the desert with a broken compass telling me the truth didn't care whether I believed it. And I have been trying to audit my own heart ever since, and the numbers don't reconcile, and I am done pretending they do."

The rain roared softly on the canvas. Ada discovered her heart was doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional.

"Ilya—"

"I know what tomorrow is," he said. "I know the window closes early, I know Hale's men are behind us and the ice is ahead of us, and I know that whatever this is, we may be buying it exactly one night of interest before the whole note comes due. I'm not asking you for a future. I'm asking you to stop auditing me from across the fire. Sit over here. Or tell me no, and I'll sit over here and be perfectly professional about it for the rest of my short and eventful life."

Ada looked at him—at this man she'd cornered at a well with a drawn weapon, whom she'd delivered like cargo, whom she'd bailed water beside through a drowning night, whose hands had taught her current and whose patience had taught her rest—and felt the last of her bearings fail in the best possible way.

Her whole life she'd navigated by instruments: duty, contract, debt. All of them pointing at other people's norths. And here was a moment with no instrument at all, just weather and firelight and a man waiting, and she understood suddenly that this was what the broken compass had been training her for—to move true when nothing pointed.

She crossed the space between them. Sat beside him. Their shoulders touched, and he exhaled like he'd been holding that breath since the salt flats.

"For the record," she said, "I'm terrible at resting. You've seen me try. I fidget."

"Noted. I'll manage expectations."

"And I snore, according to Sefa, 'like a landslide with opinions.'"

"Sefa says that about everyone. She told me the river snores."

"The river does snore," Ada admitted. "I've heard it."

He turned his hand palm-up on the bench between them, an offering, unhurried, and she put hers in it. His fingers were warm and ink-stained even now, some ledger ghost that never washed off, and she laced hers through and held on.

"This may cost everything," she said quietly. "You understand that. Tomorrow we walk into the teeth of it, and either we come out the other side with the truth in our hands, or we don't come out, and either way this—us—it doesn't get a long, comfortable life. It gets a storm and a fire and one night in a cove."

"Everything worth having gets exactly that," Ilya said. "A season. A window. A calving gap that opens twice and closes forever. The trick isn't keeping it. The trick is being brave enough to sail through while it's open."

She turned to look at him and he turned to look at her and the argument, such as it was, ended the way Toma had predicted: after dark, breaking all at once.

She kissed him, or he kissed her—the accounting would never be settled, and neither of them ever tried—and the rain came down on the canvas like applause from a very large, very wet audience, and the fire settled into embers, and somewhere out in the dark the glacier groaned against the mountain like a door being tested in its frame.

When they finally drew apart, foreheads touching, her hand still locked in his, Ada laughed—low, helpless, real.

"What?" he asked.

"Toma owes me money."

"He absolutely does not."

"He bet Sefa three coppers we'd 'sort ourselves out' before the pass. I bet him five we wouldn't. I just lost five coppers and I have never been happier about a financial loss in my life."

Ilya grinned, and it transformed his whole face, the auditor vanishing and someone younger, lighter, standing in his place. "I'll help you pay. I'm good with numbers."

"Don't you dare. This loss is mine."

* * *

They didn't sleep much, and it wasn't only for the reasons Toma would smugly assume. They lay side by side on the narrow bunk in the *Marta's* cabin—he insisted on propriety, she rolled her eyes and pulled him down anyway—with the rain hammering the deck above and their voices low, trading the things people trade when dawn might be the last one: childhoods, regrets, the names of songs, the shape of grief.

He told her about his mother's kitchen, the smell of cardamom, how he'd learned arithmetic counting jars of preserved plums. She told him about Oscar.

She hadn't said his name aloud in years. It came out rough, like a stone turning over in a riverbed.

"My brother. He was the navigator, really, not me. I just memorized what he showed me. He used to say a compass doesn't point north, it points *home*, and everything else is just geography." Her thumb traced the crack in the compass glass where it lay on the blanket between them. "When the company letter came—'regret to inform,' all that beautiful language—they sent his effects in a box. The compass was in it. Cracked. Nobody could tell me how. Nobody would tell me anything. I signed the settlement because I owed money on his funeral and I was twenty-three and stupid, and I have been paying that signature ever since."

Ilya's hand found hers in the dark.

"Tomorrow," he said, "when we carry the ledger through, we carry his name too. And the crew's. All of them. Whatever else happens, that happens. That's certain."

"Certain," she repeated. It was a good word. She held onto it.

Above them, the storm worked itself toward its peak, and beneath the storm, deeper, patient as arithmetic, the glacier waited with its two-day window, opening soon, closing sooner.

Near midnight, boots on the dock. Toma's tread, heavy and hurried, and then his fist against the cabin door—not knocking, striking.

"Get up," the old man called through the wood, and his voice had none of the moose-story warmth left in it. "Both of you. Lights on the water. Three boats, running dark, coming up the cove—and the lead one's flying colors I haven't seen since the massacre year."

Ada was moving before he finished, cold air flooding in, the warm night torn open like fabric. Beside her, Ilya was already reaching for the ledger's oilcloth bundle, his face pale and set.

The window, it seemed, intended to close early.
