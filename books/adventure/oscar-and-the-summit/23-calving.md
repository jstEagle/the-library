## 23. Calving

The mountain woke them an hour before dawn.

Not with sound — not yet — but with a tremor that came up through the ground and into the floorboards of Toma's cove shack, rattling the tin cup on its nail and setting the hanging lamp swinging in slow, patient arcs. Ada was awake before her eyes opened, hand already finding the knife under her bedroll, and for one disoriented heartbeat she thought: *patrol*. Then she remembered where they were, what day it was, and what the trembling meant.

"It's starting," she said to the dark.

Across the room, Ilya's cot creaked. "I heard it."

"You were snoring a second ago."

"I was *resting strategically*," he said, with the wounded dignity of a man who had been told, repeatedly and at length, that he slept like a sack of wet rope. "There's a difference."

Ada sat up and pulled her boots on by feel. Outside, through the gap in the shutters, the sky over the glacier was doing something it hadn't done any of the previous nights: glowing. Not sunrise-glow, which came gold and sideways from behind the eastern ridge, but a pale, sourceless luminescence rising off the ice itself, as if the glacier had swallowed a moon and couldn't keep it down.

Toma was already on his porch when they came out, wrapped in a coat older than either of them, holding three cups of something hot and terrible that he called coffee and everyone else called *that stuff Toma makes*. He handed the cups around without looking at anyone, his eyes fixed up-valley on the white wall of the pass.

"Listen," he said.

They listened. Beneath the wind there was a new sound now, deep and grinding, like a millstone the size of a cathedral turning somewhere inside the mountain. And threaded through it, faint but unmistakable, came cracks — sharp reports like rifle fire, echoing off the valley walls, each one followed by a silence that felt held.

"The ice is letting go of itself," Toma said. "It does this every year. The river pushes, the sun pulls, and the face of the glacier comes apart." He sipped his coffee and grimaced at it, as he did every morning, as if hoping this time it would be different. "Two days, maybe less. The channel opens, the water runs clean between the bergs, and you can take a boat through to the high valley road. Then it closes again until next season."

Ada looked at Ilya. He was watching the glacier with an expression she'd learned to read over the past weeks — the auditor's look, the one that measured and weighed and found most things wanting. But underneath it, tonight, there was something else. Something almost like reverence.

"You're sure about this," she said. It wasn't really a question, but she wanted to hear him say it.

"I'm sure the ledger doesn't get to the families unless we go through that pass," he said. "I'm sure Hale's men are two days behind us and closing. And I'm sure Sefa's boat is patched well enough to float and not one plank better than that." He turned to her, and in the strange glacial light his face looked younger and older at once. "So yes. I'm sure. In the way that a man is sure when all the other doors have already closed."

"That's the only kind of sure that ever got anybody anywhere," Toma said, and drained his cup.

* * *

Sefa was down at the waterline before first light, running her hands along the hull of the boat they'd spent nine days mending, checking every seam the way a mother checks a child's fever. The boat — she refused to call it anything so sentimental as a name, though Ilya had taken to calling her *the Argument*, because she was long, narrow, and full of holes that had been filled with conviction rather than skill — sat low in the black water, riding heavy with supplies.

"She'll hold," Sefa said, when Ada reached her. "Probably."

"Probably is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"Every word does work on this river." Sefa straightened, pressed a palm flat against the gunwale, and Ada saw her wince — small, quickly hidden, but there. The wound from the dock at the mining camp, three weeks old now, kept insisting it wasn't healed no matter how many times Sefa told it to be. Ada had watched her bind it in private twice. Neither of them mentioned it. It was a treaty they'd signed without paper.

"How's the shoulder?" Ada asked anyway, because someone had to say it out loud eventually.

"Attached," Sefa said. "Which is more than my ferry can say." She nodded upriver, toward the blackened pilings where Toma's boat had burned, and her jaw tightened. "We go today. While the window's open and while I'm still angry enough to steer straight."

From the shack, Toma came down the path carrying the last of their stores in a net bag, plus a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that he handed to Ilya without ceremony.

"What is it?"

"My father's chart of the upper reach," Toma said. "Drawn before the company came, before they erased the villages from the maps. Every eddy, every shoal, every place the ice likes to gather after a calving." He tapped the oilcloth once. "He made it for a man who saved his life. That man was Voss. Your father. So it comes back to a Voss now. Debts travel strangely in this valley."

Ilya held the bundle like it might bite him, or bless him; Ada couldn't tell which he feared more.

"Toma," he started, "what happened twenty years ago—"

"Is a story for people who survive today," Toma said, not unkindly. "Go earn the telling."

* * *

They launched as the sun cleared the ridge, and the river took them.

For the first hour it was almost gentle — the meltwater running high and fast but honest, the channel wide enough that Sefa could hold a lazy line down the middle while Ada stood at the bow with the boathook, fending off driftwood. The Argument complained constantly, a chorus of creaks and groans from her patched seams, but she complained the way she always did, and complaint, Ada had learned, was different from failure. You could trust a boat that grumbled. It was the quiet ones that drowned you.

Ilya sat amidships with the chart open across his knees, calling out markers. "Shoal on the left, past the split rock. Toma wrote *here the water lies* — whatever that means."

"It means the current looks calm and will eat your oar," Sefa said from the tiller. "Your father's generation wrote charts like riddles because they didn't want just anyone coming up here. Only people who'd been taught."

"Were you taught?"

"I was married to someone who was." A pause, the length of a breath, no longer. "Before the company decided she knew too much about the high valley."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't anything to answer it *with*. Ada picked up the boathook again and watched the water.

It was Ilya who heard it first — a change in the grinding roar ahead, the millstone sound swelling into something with edges on it. He stood up so fast the boat rocked. "Ada. Listen—"

She was already listening. The cracks had stopped being rifle shots. They had become artillery.

Around the next bend, the glacier filled the world.

Ada had seen it from Toma's porch, a white wall miles upstream, impressive the way mountains are impressive — distant, decorative, safely abstract. This was not that. Up close, the calving face rose four hundred feet out of the river, blue-veined and fissured, streaked with a century of stone and grit, and it was *moving* — not sliding, nothing so orderly, but shedding. Chunks the size of houses leaned away from the face with the slow, terrible grace of something deciding to fall. The air above it shimmered with ice-dust that caught the morning sun and turned the whole valley into the inside of a lamp.

"Oh," Ilya said. Just that. Just *oh*, small and reverent, the way men talk in churches.

Then the glacier spoke.

The first collapse came from the eastern face — a tower of ice, five stories tall, tilting, separating, falling. The sound arrived a half-second after the sight, a crack like the sky splitting, and then the impact, and then the wave.

"Sefa!" Ada screamed.

"I see it!" Sefa had already thrown the tiller hard over, swinging the bow toward the surge. "Everyone down and hold on! When it hits, breathe *before* it, not during!"

The wave came at them as a brown-green wall, crowned with foam and spinning shards, and Ada had time for exactly one coherent thought — *the window, this is what the window means, the door opens by breaking* — and then the river hit the boat like the hand of something enormous and indifferent.

The world became noise and cold. Ada's grip on the gunwale was the only fact she trusted. Water broke over the bow, over her head, tore the boathook out of her hands, and for three seconds — she would count them later, lying awake, three seconds exactly — the Argument was underwater and Ada was holding onto a drowning thing in a drowning world.

Then the boat surged up, streaming, rolling, righting herself with a groan of patched timber, and they were through the first wave and into the chaos beyond.

The channel was alive with ice. Bergs the size of cottages drifted and spun in the disturbed water, some of them freshly calved and still crackling as they released the pressure of centuries. Smaller floes clattered against the hull like applause from a hostile audience. Somewhere behind them, another section of the face let go, and the sound rolled down the valley and slapped off the walls and came back doubled.

"Starboard!" Ilya shouted, pointing. "Starboard, big one, it's turning!"

Ada saw it — a house-sized berg pivoting slowly in an eddy, its submerged mass catching current they couldn't see, swinging its blunt end across their line like a gate closing. Sefa threw the tiller. The Argument heeled, shipped a gallon or two of freezing water, and slid past the berg close enough that Ada could have touched it, could have read the layers in it like tree rings, ten thousand years of winters compressed to blue glass.

"Left side of the channel!" Ilya called, chart flapping. "Toma marked it — *after the calving, keep to the left hand of the current, the right gathers ice* —"

"I know what my father-in-law's chart says," Sefa snapped, but she was already steering left, and Ada caught the ghost of something that was almost a smile under all the salt and fury. For a moment — one bright, absurd moment in the middle of the roaring white chaos — they were working like a crew. Like people who had done this a hundred times. The thief calling the water, the ranger swinging on the boathook she'd retrieved from where it had wedged under a thwart, the widow at the tiller reading the river like a letter from home.

A floe the size of a dining table came at the bow. Ada braced, hooked it, and shoved — arms screaming, boots slipping — and it spun away, and she laughed. Actually laughed, breathless and shocked at herself, in the middle of it.

"What's funny?" Ilya yelled.

"Nothing! Everything!" She hauled the hook in. "My brother used to say the desert was the only honest landscape! No cover, no tricks, what you see is what kills you!" Another wave of spray hit her full in the face. "He never met a glacier!"

"He'd have hated it!"

"He'd have *loved* it!" And she found, to her astonishment, that saying it out loud — even shouted over thunder, even soaked and terrified — didn't hurt the way she'd expected. It hurt a different way. A way with air in it.

* * *

The worst of it lasted an hour. Then two hours of careful threading. Then, gradually, unbelievably, the channel widened and quieted, the bergs spreading out across a broad lake of meltwater, drifting in slow rotation like a fleet at anchor. The glacier fell astern, still booming at intervals, still shedding its towers, but from a distance now — grandeur instead of threat.

Sefa finally eased the tiller and let the boat find her own way, and the three of them stood in the wreckage of the deck — bailed water, scattered stores, one boot belonging to nobody — and looked back at what they'd come through.

"Well," Ilya said. His voice was hoarse from shouting. "The Argument held."

"Don't praise her yet," Sefa said. "She knows what you're doing. She'll spring a leak out of spite."

Ada checked the bilge. Two inches of water, steady, manageable. "She's leaking."

"She's *breathing*," Sefa corrected. "Bail it and don't insult her."

Ilya sat down suddenly, right there on a coil of rope, and put his face in his hands, and his shoulders shook. Ada dropped down beside him, alarm spiking before she understood — he was laughing. The same unhinged, relieved laughter that had climbed out of her an hour ago, the kind that lives next door to crying and borrows its sugar.

"We're through," he said, into his hands. "We actually — the window — we're *through*."

"For now." Sefa nodded upvalley, where the lake narrowed again into the high gorge, and beyond the gorge, invisible but present to all of them, lay the mining camp, Director Hale, and everything they'd come to break open. "The window closes when the melt slows, tomorrow night maybe. After that, the channel refreezes from the banks inward, and there's no way through and no way back until next season." She looked at Ada, and then at Ilya, and her voice went flat and clear as the water. "Whatever happens up there, we're in it. All the way in. I want you both to understand that before we see the camp smoke."

Ada understood. She thought about the ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath the false floorboards, the names inside it — her brother's crew among them, the run she'd booked, the contract she'd signed. She thought about Hale's offer, still waiting for her upriver like a trap baited with absolution. She thought about the debt, and who it was actually owed to, and felt the answer settle in her chest with the weight and rightness of ballast.

"I understand," she said.

Ilya looked at her a moment longer than the words required — he'd been looking at her like that since the narrows, since the night in the bilge when he'd confessed what he'd been, what he'd signed, and she hadn't walked away — and then he nodded and went forward to bail.

Ahead, the gorge walls closed in, and the water quickened, and from somewhere far up the high valley came a sound that none of them could mistake: the thin, industrial cough of machinery, and beneath it, almost lost in the wind, a bell ringing in a village that no longer appeared on any map.

Sefa's hand tightened on the tiller.

"There it is," she said quietly. "The valley that eats names."

And the Argument carried them up into its throat.
