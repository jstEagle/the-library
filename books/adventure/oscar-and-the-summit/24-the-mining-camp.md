## 24. The Mining Camp

The bell stopped ringing before they reached it.

Ada noticed that first—the way the sound simply ceased, mid-peal, as if a hand had been laid over the mouth of the valley itself. Sefa heard it too. Her jaw tightened, and she brought the Argument around the last bend with the careful, unhurried grace of a woman who had decided long ago that panic was a luxury she could no longer afford.

"There," Ilya said, pointing.

The camp sat on a shelf of gravel where two streams met, and at first glance it was almost beautiful: rows of white prefabricated buildings with green roofs, floodlights on steel poles, a crane swinging its arm over an open pit like a patient heron. Smoke rose from a generator shed in a straight, disciplined column. Everything about it said *order*. Everything about it said *legitimate*.

That was what made Ada's stomach turn. She had spent eleven years reading landscapes for lies, and she knew that honest places were never this tidy. Honest places had rust in them. Honest places had mud tracked where mud shouldn't be and arguments leaking out of windows. This place had been arranged, the way a stage is arranged before a play.

"Contractors," she said quietly. "We're contractors now."

Sefa cut the engine and let the boat drift into a stand of drowned willows downstream of the camp, where the hull disappeared against the grey water. She'd patched the Argument twice since Toma's cove—once with canvas and pitch, once with a strip of tin and more faith than any shipwright would endorse—and every mile upstream had been a negotiation between the river and the rivets. The boat had won so far. Ada didn't trust it to keep winning.

"Remember your names," Sefa said. "I'm Mara Quill, haulage broker out of Kell's Landing. You're my crew. Ice-road assessment, seasonal contract, Meridian subsidiary." She handed them each a laminated card that looked, at arm's length and in bad light, exactly like the real thing. Toma's nephew had made them; the boy had a gift for forgery and, according to Toma, no other marketable skills whatsoever.

"And if someone looks closely?" Ilya asked.

Sefa smiled without warmth. "Then you're looking at the wrong card, friend. Nobody up here looks closely. That's the whole point of this valley. Looking closely is how people disappear."

* * *

They walked in from the river along a corduroy road of rough-hewn logs, hauling their gear in canvas sacks like the hired muscle they were pretending to be. Ada kept her eyes down and her ears open. It was a discipline her old training officer had drilled into her back when she still believed the badge meant something: *the land tells you everything, ranger, if you shut your own mouth long enough to listen.*

The land was telling her plenty.

It told her there were forty-one souls in the camp, because she counted the boots hanging outside the barracks door—forty-one pairs, all sizes, all muddied the same uniform brown. It told her the camp had been here longer than its permits claimed, because the willows along the stream had grown through and around the fence posts, and willows did that in three years, not three months. It told her somebody was afraid, because the guard tower by the pit had sandbags stacked facing *inward*.

And it told her something else, something that made her hands curl into fists inside her gloves: there was a schoolhouse.

A small one, prefab like the rest, with a hand-painted sign—MERIDIAN CONSORTIUM COMMUNITY OUTREACH FACILITY—and a swing set beside it with one chain snapped, the seat hanging crooked, turning slowly in the wind with nobody on it.

Children. There had been children here. Or there were children here who were being taught, very carefully, not to ask questions.

"Cold," Ilya murmured beside her, meaning the look on her face, not the weather.

"I'm fine," Ada said, which was a lie, and he let it lie, which was its own kind of kindness.

* * *

The camp office smelled of diesel, wet wool, and instant coffee—a smell so aggressively ordinary that Ada almost laughed. Behind a desk piled with manifests stood a clerk named Ostrow, a soft-bodied man with ink-stained fingers and the harried expression of someone drowning in his own paperwork.

"Haulage?" he said, squinting at Sefa's forged credentials. "Nobody told me about haulage."

"Seasonal ice-road assessment," Sefa said smoothly. "Your glacier route closes in nine days. Somebody has to tell you it's closing."

Ostrow sighed the sigh of a man who had learned that arguing with paperwork was worse than accepting it. He stamped their cards, waved them toward the mess tent, and returned to his manifests with the gratitude of a man glad to have one fewer thing to think about.

It was the manifests that got Ada.

She saw them because Ostrow was careless—or perhaps because some small, buried part of him *wanted* them seen, wanted witnesses, wanted anyone at all to know what passed across his desk each day. The top sheet lay open under his elbow, and Ada read upside down the way she'd learned to read tracks: quickly, sideways, without seeming to look.

*Cargo manifest 114-C. Origin: High Valley Works. Destination: Meridian Consolidated, Coastal Office. Contents: aggregate stone, 200 tons.*

Beneath it, another. *Manifest 115-C. Contents: aggregate stone, 180 tons.*

And beneath that, in a different hand, a shipping receipt from downriver: *Received, Meridian Consolidated: timber, 340 tons.*

Two hundred tons of stone out. Three hundred and forty tons of timber received. The numbers didn't touch. They weren't supposed to. The manifests weren't records; they were erasures. Every ton of ore going downriver was being written downriver as something else—stone instead of silver, gravel instead of gold—so that when the auditors came asking, the books balanced beautifully and told a story in which nothing valuable had ever come out of this valley at all.

Which meant no royalties. Which meant no taxes. Which meant the valley wasn't being mined so much as *unmade*—its wealth extracted and its existence simultaneously denied, so thoroughly that in ten years there would be no paper trail proving the high valley had ever produced anything worth stealing.

"They're not hiding the mine," Ilya breathed beside her, so quietly only she could hear. His face had gone the color of the fog off the glacier. "I signed off on accounting like this. I thought—I told myself it was rounding errors. Sloppy bookkeeping. But this—" He swallowed. "This isn't hiding profit. This is hiding the *place*."

"Keep your voice down," Sefa said, not looking at either of them, smiling at the coffee urn as though it had paid her a compliment.

* * *

They ate lunch in the mess tent among forty-one men who did not speak to strangers. The stew was good—mutton and turnip, real salt, the first proper meal Ada had had in weeks—and she ate it mechanically while her eyes worked the room.

On the far wall, pinned up with the same cheerful care as a safety poster, hung a framed document. She drifted over to it under the pretense of refilling her tin cup.

It was a magistrate's certificate. *By order of the Honorable D. Ferren, Circuit Magistrate, District Nine: all land claims within the High Valley watershed are hereby confirmed as held in good standing by Meridian Consolidated, Ltd.* Signed, stamped, sealed. Perfectly legal. Perfectly official. Hanging on a wall in a mess tent four hundred miles past the edge of the magistrate's actual jurisdiction, confirming claims to land whose rightful owners had, per the ledger in Ilya's waterproof satchel, mostly died in boating accidents, rockslides, and fires that burned suspiciously fast and left suspiciously few bodies.

There was a notation at the bottom in smaller print. *Certification fee waived, per standing arrangement.*

*Standing arrangement.* Four little words doing the work of a hundred bribes. Ada stared at them until the letters blurred, and what she felt was not the hot rage she might have expected. It was colder than that. It was the fury of a woman who had spent her life believing that the law was a thing you served, discovering that the law was a thing that got *bought*—not broken, never broken, just purchased, politely, in advance, with fees waived.

Her brother had died under that seal. Somewhere in a coastal archive there was a file with his name in it, stamped *accident*, certified by a man who had waived his fee.

"You're staring at the magistrate's certificate," said a voice at her elbow.

Ada turned. An old man stood there, one of the forty-one, holding his tin cup in both hands like something warm he was afraid to spill. He had a miner's forearms and a schoolteacher's eyes, and he looked at her the way men look at a stranger when they've decided, after long internal debate, to take a risk.

"It hangs there," he said, "so everybody remembers the law is on the company's side." He took a slow sip. "Old Ferren comes up twice a year. Stays in the green building. Drinks the director's brandy. Signs whatever's put in front of him." He shrugged, a shrug that contained whole decades. "My grandfather mined this valley before there was a Meridian. His claim's in that certificate somewhere. Confirmed as held in good standing. By people who never met him."

"What happened to him?" Ada asked, though she already knew. She was learning the shape of this valley's silences.

"He went into the narrows one spring," the old man said. "Boat found empty. Very sad. Very common, up here." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were not sad at all. They were waiting. "You're not really a haulage crew."

Ada said nothing. Beside her, Ilya had gone very still.

"No," the old man continued, agreeably. "Haulage crews don't count boots. Haulage crews don't read upside down. And haulage crews don't look at that certificate like they want to burn it and frame the ashes." He drained his cup. "I'm not going to say anything. Forty of us eat this stew every day and none of us say anything, because saying things up here gets boats emptied. But I'll tell you one thing for free, since you're going to do something foolish regardless—I can see foolishness coming off you like heat off a stove."

He leaned in slightly.

"The director keeps the true ledgers in the green building. Not the office—the *green building*. Second floor, north room. He reads them at night. And the magistrate arrives tomorrow on the morning barge." He straightened, patted Ada once on the shoulder with a hand like a shovel handle, and walked away whistling, a small tuneless sound that somehow managed to convey both resignation and hope.

* * *

Dusk came down the valley like a lid closing. From the willow stand where the Argument lay hidden under netting, Ada watched the camp lights bloom one by one, and beside her Ilya spread their stolen knowledge out on the boat's damp floorboards like a hand of losing cards.

"The green building," he said. "Second floor, north room. If the original ledgers are there—the ones before my falsified copies—we don't need to photograph anything. We can take the truth itself."

"We photograph anyway," Ada said. "Copies survive. Originals get lost. Ask me how I know." She touched the compass in her pocket without quite meaning to, the broken one, the needle that spun. "Magistrate comes tomorrow morning. Which means tonight the director will be reading his ledgers, and tomorrow he'll be signing new ones with a bought man's pen. Whatever we do, we do tonight."

Sefa crouched at the bow, watching the floodlights hum. For a long moment she said nothing, and the river said nothing, and even the machinery up the slope seemed to hold its breath.

"My sister's name isn't on any manifest," she said finally. "But it's in those ledgers. I know it is. Line item. Cost of doing business." She rolled her shoulders once, like a woman settling a weight she intended to carry the rest of the way. "So we go tonight. And we go quiet."

Ada looked up at the green building, its windows glowing against the darkening slope, and felt the cold fury in her chest settle at last into something steadier and far more dangerous: purpose.

Tomorrow a magistrate would arrive to make the theft legal. Tonight, she intended to make it documented.

"One problem," Ilya said, and pointed.

Below the green building, passing slowly through the pool of a floodlight, a figure in a long coat paused, lit a cigarette, and turned his face briefly toward the river.

Ada knew that face. Everyone in the consortium's service had known it, back when she'd been proud to serve.

Director Hale had come up early to meet his magistrate.

And he was looking at the water.
