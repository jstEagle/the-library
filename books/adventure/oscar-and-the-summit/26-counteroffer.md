## 26. Counteroffer

The mess tent smelled of diesel and boiled cabbage, and Ada had never hated a smell more in her life.

Director Hale sat across the folding table from her with the ease of a man who owned the table, the tent, the mountain it stood on, and probably the weather. He'd poured her coffee himself from a dented thermos, and he'd done it correctly—no sugar, because he'd watched her drink coffee for three days now and noticed. That was the thing about Hale that made her skin crawl worse than any gunboat: he paid attention.

"You understand," he said, "that I'm not asking you to do anything you haven't already done."

Ada wrapped both hands around the tin cup. Outside, the monsoon hammered the canvas like a fist on a door, and somewhere beyond that, up-glacier, Ilya was sitting in a supply hut believing she was negotiating his release terms. Which, technically, she was. Just not the ones he thought.

"Enlighten me," she said.

"You tracked him across four hundred miles of salt and karst. You delivered him to Consortium custody once already." Hale smiled, small and reasonable. "All I'm asking is that you finish the job. The ledger burns. Voss goes into processing—humanely, I give you my word—and your file closes clean. No debt. No record. You walk out of this valley with a badge and a bank account and nobody, ever again, asks you what happened to the *Meridian Star*."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. Her brother's ship. Her brother's crew. Eleven names she'd spent six years not saying out loud.

Hale saw it land. Of course he did. He was watching her hands, not her eyes—a ranger's trick, and he knew it, which meant he'd known rangers before, which meant he'd bought them before.

"The debt," Ada said carefully, "was cleared when I signed the delivery contract."

"The debt was *deferred*," Hale corrected gently. "You know how Meridian accounting works. Everything is deferred until it's an accident." He let that sit between them, obscene and calm. "I'd rather you weren't one. You're good at your work, Nystrom. It would be a waste."

She drank the coffee. It was terrible. She drank it anyway, because her hands needed something to do besides shake.

"I'll need time," she said. "He trusts me. If I move wrong, he runs, and you lose the ledger's location—he doesn't keep it on him, you know that or you'd have taken it off him at the dock."

Something flickered behind Hale's eyes—the first honest thing she'd seen there. Greed does that; it makes even careful men briefly careless.

"How much time?"

"A day. Maybe two. The pass is closing regardless. Let me bring him down to the lower camp on the pretext of evacuating ahead of the meltwater surge. You take him at the bridge. Clean, quiet, no sirens."

"And the ledger?"

"He'll trade it," Ada said. "For me. He's that kind of stupid." She made her voice flat with contempt, and hated herself with a thoroughness that surprised her. "You just have to make him believe I'm worth more than paper."

Hale studied her for a long moment. Then he laughed—not cruelly, which was somehow worse—and reached into his jacket.

He slid a folded document across the table. Her name at the top. Her record, expunged. Her commission, restored. At the bottom, a signature line waiting for her like an open grave.

"No rush," he said. "Sign it when it's done. I find people sign faster after."

* * *

She found Ilya in the supply hut, cross-legged on a crate of blasting caps—she'd have to speak to someone about the storage arrangements in this place if she survived to be pedantic about anything ever again—counting tinned peaches by lamplight.

"Eleven cans," he said without looking up. "We could eat like kings for two days. Or like moderately anxious princes for five."

"We're leaving tomorrow. Before dawn."

His head came up. "Leaving where? Downriver? Ada, the window—"

"Not downriver." She kept her voice level, kept her eyes on his face, and lied to it. "Down-camp. The lower station. There's a meltwater surge coming—Sefa heard it from the dockworkers. The whole upper valley floods by midweek. We can't make the pass through that; we wait it out below and come back up when the water drops."

It was a good lie. She'd built it the way Toma taught her to read current—find where the surface looks smooth and put your weight there. Sefa *had* been talking to dockworkers. The flood was real. Only the direction was false.

Ilya frowned. "And the ledger?"

"Goes with us. In the boat kit, under the spare line, same as always." She watched relief loosen his shoulders and felt each inch of it like a knife going in sideways. "Ilya. Listen to me. Whatever happens tomorrow—whatever you see—"

"Don't do that." He smiled, tired and fond. "Don't do the dramatic farewell speech. We've survived gunboats, whiteouts, and your cooking. We're going to be fine."

*Your cooking.* Six weeks ago he'd said that as an insult. Somewhere around week three it had become a joke, and somewhere around week five a compliment, and now it sat in her chest next to the eleven names and the unsigned contract burning a hole in her coat like a coal.

"Yeah," she said. "Fine."

He tossed her a can of peaches. She caught it. Neither of them said anything else, and the lamp hissed, and outside the rain went on hammering the world like it was trying to get in, or trying to get something out.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in the boathouse with the ledger open on her knees, reading it again by the light of a storm lantern, because she needed to remember exactly what she was buying time to protect.

Page forty-one. The *Meridian Star*, listed as a hull loss, "weather event." Payout to next of kin: one line. Next of kin: herself. She remembered signing that acknowledgment in a Meridian office that smelled like the mess tent did tonight—diesel and cabbage and money. She'd signed because the clerk told her the alternative was a lawsuit for breach of charter, and grief is a bad negotiator.

Page fifty-three. Kell's Landing, the magistrate who'd waved their boat through. Monthly retainer, fourteen years running.

Page sixty. A column of names under a heading marked only *RESOLVED*. She'd stopped counting at thirty. Sefa's sister was number nine.

The ledger weighed less than a kilo. It had taken her six years, one stolen compass, one leaking boat, and every scrap of law she'd ever believed in to finally hold it, and tomorrow morning she was going to hand its keeper to the men who wrote it.

No. Not hand him over. *Buy time.* There was a difference. She turned the phrase over and over like a stone, looking for the crack in it, the flaw that proved it wasn't what it looked like.

She found the crack eventually, around the third hour: the plan required Ilya to trust her right up until the moment it broke him. And if Hale's men were half a second slow, or half a degree angry—if they decided processing was easier with a bullet—Ilya would die believing she'd sold him. Believing it with the same certainty she'd once believed her brother died of weather.

Ada closed the ledger and pressed her forehead against it, and for a while the boathouse held nothing but rain and a woman breathing like she was hauling on a rope.

* * *

Dawn came gray and mean. The rain had thinned to a drizzle that couldn't decide whether to commit, and the glacier up-valley groaned like an old man changing position—calving season, still days out, but restless.

They loaded the boat in silence broken only by Ilya's inventory muttering. Sefa appeared at the dock with her arm bound tight against her ribs and her expression suggesting she'd read every word of last night's conversation written on Ada's face.

"Well?" Sefa said quietly, as Ilya carried the kit bag past.

"It's handled."

"Is it." Sefa looked at her the way she looked at weather she intended to sail into anyway. "You know what I asked you for. After."

"After," Ada said. "I remember."

Sefa held her gaze a moment longer, then spat neatly off the dock into the gray water and went to cast off, which in Sefa's language was roughly equivalent to a blessing.

They ran downriver with the current doing half the work, the mountains closing in green and dripping on either side. Ilya sat in the bow reading the water and calling hazards back to her—"sweepers on the left, deadfall, we'll want the far channel"—and every competent, trusting word of it was another stitch in the thing Ada was going to have to unpick with her own hands.

Near midday they pulled into an eddy below a shale bank to rest and eat, and Ilya passed her the peach can they'd opened that morning, saving himself the juice, the way he always did, the way he'd been doing since the salt flats when sharing was the only currency either of them had.

"Two days down-camp," he said, licking syrup off his thumb, "and then back up for the calving. You realize this means Toma's cove is directly upstream of a flooding valley and he's going to be insufferable about it. 'The river provides, but it also takes, and also I told you so.'"

Ada laughed. It came out real, which was the cruelest part. "He'll charge us interest on the mending."

"He charges interest on *gratitude*." Ilya shook his head, grinning, and then the grin faded into something quieter. He looked at her across the little boat, rain-beaded and serious. "Ada. What you're doing—for the record, whatever happens—I know what it costs you. Going back down. Giving up the pass. I want you to know I see it."

She looked at the man who had walked out of the Consortium with the truth in his hands and given up everything to carry it, and who trusted her more than he trusted dry ground, and she said:

"You should. It costs plenty."

And Ilya Voss nodded, satisfied, and turned back to watch the river, and Ada Nystrom sat in the stern with a forged peace in her pocket and a real one dissolving in her chest, and steered them both toward the bridge where the ambush waited.

Behind them, far up-valley, the glacier spoke once—a long, deep report, like a door being tested in its frame.

Sooner than expected, Ada thought. Sooner than anyone expected.

She just hoped the door held long enough for her to change what was waiting on the other side of it.
