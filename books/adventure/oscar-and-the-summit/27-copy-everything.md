## 27. Copy Everything

The lamp in the dockmaster's office had a bad habit of buzzing when it thought no one was listening, and Ada Nystrom had spent the better part of an hour deciding whether to kill it or let it live.

She killed it. Darkness was cheaper than electricity, and tonight they were paying for everything in other currencies.

Below her, through the warped floorboards, the river moved against the pilings with its endless patient sound—*shush, shush, shush*—like the whole Karst Reach breathing in its sleep. Somewhere out on the water, Sefa was keeping the boat warm and the engine cold and her opinions to herself, which for Sefa constituted a kind of prayer.

Ada turned back to the table, where Ilya Voss sat hunched over the Consortium's ledger like a man defusing something.

"Page forty-one," he said quietly. "You should see page forty-one."

"I've seen enough pages."

"Not this one." He turned it toward her without touching it further, as if it might bite. "This is where they stopped pretending. Look at the column headers."

She looked. The elegant bookkeeping of the earlier entries—the neat little sums, the euphemisms (*site remediation*, *personnel attrition*, *contractual wind-down*)—gave way here to bare numbers and initials. No more dressing. Just names crossed against payments, dates matched to disasters she'd read about in dispatch reports for years and never once questioned.

"That's my brother's run," she said. Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. She'd expected it to crack.

"I know," Ilya said. "I signed the authorization. Page thirty-eight is mine."

"You don't have to keep saying that."

"Yes, I do." He said it simply, the way you'd say *the sky is up*. "Every time you look at me like you're not sure whether to trust me, I need to remember that you're right not to. Not fully. Not yet." He tapped the ledger. "This is how we get there. Copies first. Forgiveness later, if we're lucky."

Ada pulled out a chair. It groaned like an old man asked to dance. "Then let's make copies before I change my mind about the forgiveness part."

* * *

The copying operation had been Toma's idea originally—or rather, Toma's cousin's cousin, a dockworker named Brannoc Halek who ran the night shift at the Meridian freight yard downriver and hated the Consortium with the quiet, banked heat of a man whose brother had drowned in a "survey accident" nobody investigated. Toma had given them his name along with the last of his tea, saying only: *"Brannoc keeps his mouth shut and his hands busy. Busy hands are useful hands. Tell him the old ferryman says the ice is thin."*

They'd reached the freight yard at dusk, slipping in among the evening shift like two more pieces of cargo, and now, past midnight, the yard slept around them in the particular way industrial places sleep—not peacefully, but with a grudging silence, machines cooling, metal ticking, one dog somewhere barking at nothing with great professional commitment.

Brannoc himself was enormous. That was the first thing. He filled the doorway of the copy room the way weather fills a valley, and he looked at Ada and Ilya with small, shrewd eyes set in a face like a cliff face.

"The ferryman's regards," Ada said.

Something moved behind the small eyes. "Toma." He said the name the way you'd say the name of a saint or a debt. "He still alive?"

"Barely. His ferry's ash."

Brannoc's jaw worked. "Meridian burned it?"

"They didn't light the match themselves. They never do."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "They hire people like me." He stepped aside to let them into the copy room. "Which is why I'm doing this. Come in. Touch nothing. And if anyone asks, you're both new hires and you're both terrible at your jobs, which explains why you're here at midnight."

"I'm excellent at my job," Ilya said.

"You're an auditor," Brannoc said. "That's worse than terrible. That's invisible. Sit down."

* * *

The copying machine was an ancient drum-fed beast that Brannoc had rescued from a scrap pile three years ago, officially because the yard needed it, unofficially, Ada suspected, because a man who planned to betray his employers liked having a machine that made no entry in any Consortium inventory.

It worked like this: Ilya opened the ledger flat under the glass, Brannoc fed in paper, the drum turned, and the machine produced copies with a grinding, asthmatic wheeze that sounded, to Ada's straining ears, loud enough to wake the entire valley.

"It's too loud," she said for the fourth time.

"It's always too loud," Brannoc said for the fourth time. "That's why we do it during shift change, when the loaders run their engines. You worry like a grandmother."

"My grandmother worried accurately," Ada said. "She died of it."

Ilya, bent over the ledger, huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Her name was Marta," he said. "Your grandmother. You mentioned her once, on the boat. She kept a salt jar for every debt in the village."

"She did. And she said a debt hidden is a debt doubled." Ada watched the drum turn. "I used to think that was just something old women said to justify being nosy."

"And now?"

"Now I think she'd have taken one look at Meridian's books and started a second shelf."

The machine wheezed. A page emerged, warm and slightly damp, the ink ghosted but legible. Ilya laid it on the growing stack with the tenderness of a man handling a sleeping child.

They worked in a rhythm that surprised Ada—she'd expected clumsiness from him, some auditor's fumbling, but his hands were quick and sure, squaring each page, checking each image, catching the two smudged sheets and running them again without being told. Somewhere between page sixty and page ninety she realized they'd developed a wordless economy: he lifted the glass, she turned the page, Brannoc fed the drum, and none of them spoke except when necessary, and the necessary things were small.

*"Faster. Footsteps. Hold."*

Three men passed in the corridor outside, arguing about a crane coupling. The dog barked. The footsteps faded. They resumed.

*"That one twice—the drum skipped."*

*"I saw it."*

*"There. Done."*

At page one hundred and twelve, Ilya paused over an entry so long that Ada leaned in to see why. It was a single line item, dated eleven years ago, for a payment so large it didn't fit the column, annotated in a hand she recognized from the earlier pages—Hale's hand, Director Hale's, precise and unhurried:

*Consolidation of outstanding liabilities. High Valley. Final.*

"Final," Ada repeated.

"That's what he called it," Ilya said. "When I asked him about the high valley, years ago—he told me it was already handled. 'Final,' he said. Like closing an account." His voice went thin. "Eleven years ago. There were still families up there. I found their names in the resettlement files. Forty-one households. The ledger doesn't say where they went."

"The ledger never says where they go," Ada said. "That's the trick of it. You pay for the leaving, not the arriving."

Brannoc, feeding the drum, said nothing, but his huge hands slowed for exactly one page, and then sped up again, and Ada understood that the cliff-faced man was holding something very heavy very still.

* * *

They finished at half past three in the morning. Two hundred and six pages, copied twice—one stack for the coastal press, one for the magistrate in Lowmarsh who, according to Toma, owed the old ferryman a favor dating back to a flood and a rowboat and a story neither man ever told sober.

Two hundred and six pages, times two. Hope, photocopied.

Now came the harder part: making it disappear.

"Four hiding places," Brannoc said, spreading a grease-stained diagram of the freight yard on the table. "Not one. One hiding place is a grave; four is a rumor. If they find one stack, they'll tear this place apart looking for the rest, and they won't find them, and by then the doubt will be worth more than the paper." He tapped the diagram. "Stack one goes into the lubricant drums—sealed, waxed, in with the barrel shipments for the coastal run. Stack two—" he glanced at Ilya, almost apologetically— "goes with the church group. Sister Anselm takes relief supplies upriver every month. Nobody searches a nun. Nobody has ever searched a nun. It's the one law Meridian respects."

"And stacks three and four?"

"Three stays with me, sewn into the tarpaulins on my own bunk. Four—" Brannoc hesitated, and for the first time the big man looked uncertain. "Four I wanted to ask you about. There's a woman. Runs the chandlery by the fish market. Petra. She—" He stopped, started again. "Her daughter was one of the forty-one households. Up in the high valley. Eleven years ago."

The room went very quiet. Even the river below seemed to lean in.

"She doesn't know what happened to them," Brannoc said. "No one does. But she's kept a room ready for eleven years. Bed made. Window open in summer." He looked at the ledger stack on the table as if it weighed as much as it did. "I think she should be the one to hold a piece of this. Not because she's safe—she isn't—but because she's owed. You understand?"

Ada understood. It was the same arithmetic her grandmother had kept in salt jars. Some debts can't be paid forward; they can only be carried by the people they belong to.

"Give it to Petra," she said.

* * *

Distributing the copies took until dawn, and it was the strangest work Ada had ever done—sneaking hope through a freight yard in wax paper bundles, handing truth to strangers in doorways and alley mouths, watching each of them take it with the same expression: fear first, always fear, the animal flinch—and then something else rising underneath the fear like water finding its level.

The lubricant drums took stack one, sealed and sunk among fifty identical drums, anonymous as a grain of sand in a beach. Sister Anselm—a small, iron-spined woman with flour on her habit, who listened to Brannoc's whispered explanation with her head tilted and then said only, "Under the bandages, with the medical supplies. God hides things there all the time"—took stack two without blinking. Stack three disappeared into Brannoc's tarpaulins with a needle and thread and a competence that suggested he'd done this sort of sewing before, and recently.

Petra's chandlery was last.

She was awake—they all seemed to be awake, the whole river working its night shift of grief—and she was a spare, gray woman with rope-burned hands and eyes that had been crying recently or were about to be, Ada couldn't tell which, maybe both, maybe always.

Brannoc made the introductions haltingly. Ada held out the bundle.

"This is from the high valley," she said. "Part of it. The record of what was done. Your household is in it. Your people are named."

Petra took the bundle the way you take a newborn—both hands, instinctively, pulling it close before her mind caught up with her arms. She didn't open it. She stood there holding it, and her chin trembled once, and then she mastered herself with a visible effort, the way you'd master a boat in rough water.

"Eleven years," she said. Her voice was steady. Her eyes weren't. "Eleven years I've set a place at the table. My sister told me to stop. Said I was keeping a wound open." She pressed the bundle against her chest. "I wasn't keeping a wound open. I was keeping a door open. This—" she lifted it slightly— "this is someone finally walking through it."

She looked at Ada, and then at Ilya, standing half in shadow by the door, and something in her gaze sharpened.

"You're the one from inside," she said to Ilya. "The one who carried this out."

"I'm the one who helped write it," Ilya said. "Carrying it out is the least of what I owe."

Petra studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the nod of a woman adding a name to a list she kept somewhere behind her eyes.

"Then live long enough to pay it," she said. "That's all I ask. Live long enough."

* * *

They walked back along the waterfront as the sky went the color of dishwater, the monsoon clouds stacking up over the ridge in slow gray towers. Ilya was quiet for most of the walk, and Ada let him be quiet, because she knew this kind of silence—it was the silence after surgery, when the wound is open and the work is done and all that's left is to wait and see if the body accepts what was done to it.

Halfway along the seawall, he finally spoke.

"You know what's strange? For eleven years I thought the ledger was the worst thing I'd ever seen. All those numbers. All those deaths in tidy columns." He kicked a loose stone into the water. "But tonight, handing it out piece by piece—it felt different. Smaller. Like once it was divided among enough people, it stopped being a monster and started being evidence."

"That's the whole trick," Ada said. "A secret is a weapon. A secret shared is a case file. Meridian understands that better than anyone—that's why they buy magistrates and burn ferries. Not because they're afraid of the truth." She looked out at the river, where Sefa's boat sat low and patient at its mooring, a single lantern burning in the stern. "Because they're afraid of multiplication."

Ilya smiled—a real one, tired and crooked. "Marta's salt jars."

"Marta's salt jars," Ada agreed. "Debt hidden is debt doubled. Debt shared is debt collectible."

They reached the boat. Sefa was sitting up in the stern, wrapped in two blankets despite the warm night, and she looked at them with the expression of a woman who had been counting the hours since midnight and had decided, provisionally, not to be angry about it.

"Well?" she said.

"Copies made," Ada said. "Copies hidden. Four places. They'd have to burn the whole river down to find them all."

Sefa considered this. "They might try," she said. "But it's a big river." She jerked her chin at the eastern sky, where the cloud towers had begun to organize themselves into something with intent. "Weather's turning. Storm by tomorrow night, if the barometer's honest. Which means your glacier window opens early—which means Hale knows it too, and he'll move his men up-valley before the pass closes behind him."

Ada felt the plan shift under her feet, the way a deck shifts. "So we go now. Today. Ahead of the storm, ahead of Hale."

"So we go now," Sefa agreed. "And Ada—" she paused, and for once there was something almost gentle in the laconic old face, quickly buried, like a coin dropped in deep water— "my shoulder's holding. Don't ask. But when the shooting starts up there, and it will start, you put me where the shooting is thickest. That's not bravery. That's bookkeeping."

Ada looked at her—at the bandage under the blanket, at the flare gun cleaned and loaded beside her knee, at the whole stubborn architecture of the woman—and didn't argue, because some debts can't be paid forward either. They can only be carried by the people they belong to.

"All right," she said. "Bookkeeping."

She cast off the line. The engine coughed, caught, settled into its work-rumble. The river took them, and the current leaned them gently toward the narrows, toward the storm, toward the white thunder of the calving glacier waiting somewhere above the mist.

Behind them, in the waking town, four bundles of truth lay hidden in drums and tarpaulins and a nun's bandages and a mother's open door, and Ada Nystrom found—to her considerable surprise—that her hands had almost stopped trembling.

Almost.

Ahead, far up the gray water, the first wind of the storm came down the valley carrying the smell of ice, and beneath it, fainter, another smell entirely: woodsmoke, and engine oil, and men.

Hale was already moving.
