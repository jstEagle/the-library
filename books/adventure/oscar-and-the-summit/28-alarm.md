## 28. Alarm

The copies were done by the third hour past midnight, and Ada had never been so grateful for a damp dock and a bad lamp in her life.

They worked in the back room of Orla's chandlery, where the dockworkers who owed Toma their lives—or at least their livelihoods—had set up an old letterpress and a ream of paper that smelled of mildew and honest labor. Ilya's hands moved with the mechanical precision of a man who had spent years signing other people's fates, page after page of the Consortium's ledger feeding through the press while Ada stood watch at the door and tried not to think about how many names she was copying. Not numbers. Names. That was the trick of it, the thing Hale's accountants had never understood: a ledger full of figures was just arithmetic, but a ledger full of names was a graveyard with a table of contents.

"Forty-one," Ilya said quietly, pressing another sheet flat. "Forty-one 'accidents' in eleven years. And those are only the ones he bothered to itemize."

Ada didn't answer. She'd stopped being able to speak somewhere around page thirty, when she'd found her brother's run listed between a bridge inspection and a fuel purchase, filed under *miscellaneous losses*.

Orla herself—a broad woman with forearms like mooring rope and a laugh that could be heard over engines—came in from the front with three cups of something hot and terrible. "Drink," she ordered. "You look like drowned cats, and drowned cats make mistakes."

"We're not done," Ilya said.

"You're done for tonight." She set the cups down with the finality of a gavel. "The rest goes to the *Kestrel*'s hold before dawn, like we agreed. Petro and his boys will spread them up the coast faster than gossip. But nobody moves anything until the tide turns, and until then, you two are going to sit there and drink my coffee like civilized people."

Ada took the cup. It tasted like boiled rope and possibly actual rope. She drank it anyway, because Orla had the kind of face you didn't argue with, and because her hands needed something to do besides shake.

Ilya sat down across from her, and for a moment they just looked at each other across the steam. He looked older than he had at the well, back when he'd been nothing but a thief with a clever mouth. He looked like a man carrying forty-one graves.

"It'll work," he said. Not confidently. Hopefully, which was different, and braver.

"I know," Ada lied gently.

* * *

It did not work.

Later, Ada would replay the night a hundred times, hunting for the moment it turned, and every time she'd land on the same one: the dog. There had been a dog on the dock, some mangy yellow thing that belonged to nobody and everybody, and it had trotted alongside them as they carried the first bundle of copies toward the *Kestrel*, and Ada had scratched its ears without thinking, because a dog is a dog, and kindness is cheap when you're terrified.

And the dog had been friendly to everyone. Including the night watchman making his rounds along the seawall.

He was a small man, the watchman, unremarkable in every way except the lantern he carried and the way the light swung toward them as the dog bounded happily between both parties, tail high, delighted to be the center of attention. Ada saw the man's face do the thing faces do—blank, then working, then catching—and she saw him look at Ilya, really look, and she watched recognition arrive like a ship coming into harbor.

"*Voss?*" the man said. Just the name. Nothing else. He said it the way you'd say the name of a ghost.

Everything happened very fast after that.

"Walk," Ada breathed, not turning her head, keeping her pace exactly as unhurried as it had been. "Don't run. Running confirms."

But the watchman was already reaching for the alarm post at the end of the pier—the brass handle, the crank, the bell that would wake half the harbor—and Ada made the calculation she'd spent her whole career training herself to make, the one about distance and time and what a man can reach versus what a woman can cover, and the calculation came back ugly.

She ran.

She covered the thirty yards in what felt like one long stride and caught the watchman's wrist just as his fingers closed on the crank. He was stronger than he looked, or more frightened, which amounts to the same thing in a struggle, and they went down together onto the wet planks with the lantern rolling free and painting wild shadows across the pilings.

"Don't—" he gasped, and then Ilya was there, hauling the man's arm behind him, and Ada had the strange, sick clarity of seeing herself from above: a ranger, a lawwoman, pinning a dock watchman to the ground of a free port at midnight, doing the exact work she'd sworn her life against.

"The bell," she said. "Don't touch the bell."

"I have a family," the watchman whispered. His eyes were wide and wet and human. "They said if Voss came through here—there's a bounty, they said—please, I have a family."

Ada's grip loosened. It was the wrong instinct. It was the right instinct. She still doesn't know which.

That was when the second watchman, the one neither of them had seen, cranked the bell.

* * *

The sound went through the harbor like a knife through water.

One clang, then another, then a rhythm, urgent and stupid and enormous, and lights began blooming along the quay like a rash—lanterns in windows, lamps on masts, the green-and-white glow of the Consortium office at the head of the pier snapping from sleep to full brightness in under a minute, because of course they were ready, of course they'd drilled for this, a company that murders by paperwork drills its alarms the way churches drill choirs.

"Run now," Ada said, and this time there was no ambiguity in it.

They ran. The bundles of copies went into a hatch on the *Kestrel*—Petro's hands appearing out of the dark like a magic trick, taking the weight without a word—and then the quay erupted behind them with boots and shouting, and Ada grabbed Ilya's sleeve and pulled him away from the main street, toward the water, toward the one place she knew they might still have a chance.

Sefa.

They found her at the boat, of course. Where else. She was sitting on the gunwale with a mug in her hand, watching the chaos spread along the waterfront with the expression of a woman watching weather she'd predicted days ago.

"There she is," Sefa said. "Trouble, right on schedule."

"They know Ilya's here. They'll seal the harbor." Ada was already throwing off the mooring lines. "We need to be out of the basin before they close the chain boom."

"The boom takes four minutes to raise. You can't get a boat through the gap in four minutes." Sefa sipped her drink. "Not this boat. Not loaded like she is."

"Then we go out the east channel—"

"The east channel's got a Consortium cutter anchored midstream, love. Been there since Tuesday." Sefa set down the mug, and Ada saw her do the same calculation Ada had done on the pier, the distance-and-time arithmetic, except Sefa's version had a different answer, because Sefa had a different variable available to her, and the variable was herself.

"No," Ada said, because she saw it coming the way you see a wave coming, from far off, tall and inevitable.

"Mm," said Sefa.

"Sefa. No."

"Somebody's got to give them something loud to look at." She was already moving, kicking off the brake on the winch, checking the fuel line with quick, practiced thumbs. "This old girl draws attention. Always has. Ugly as sin and twice as noticeable." She patted the cabin roof almost tenderly. "I'll take her out the main channel, running lights blazing, making all the noise she's ever made. Every eye, every lantern, every gun in this harbor follows the biggest, loudest thing on the water. That's not tactics, that's physics."

"And then what?" Ilya's voice cracked on it. "Then what happens to you?"

"Then I'm a fast boat in the dark with a captain who knows these channels better than the men chasing her." Sefa grinned, and it was her real grin, the crooked one, the one that had gotten them through checkpoints and rapids and the narrows. "I've been shot at before, auditor. Mostly missed."

"Mostly," Ada repeated.

"Statistically favorable." Sefa threw them each a canvas bag—supplies, wrapped tight, prepared hours ago, because of course she'd prepared; she'd known since the chandlery, maybe since the narrows, maybe since Kell's Landing, that it would come to this. "East slip, below the fish market. Toma's cousin keeps a skiff there. Be on the water before dawn and gone before anyone thinks to count skiffs." She paused, and for just a moment the grin faded and something else showed underneath, something old and tired and fierce. "Get the ledger downriver. All of it. Every name. Promise me."

"We promise," Ada said.

"Then we're square." Sefa climbed aboard, and the engine coughed, caught, roared. "Oh—and if I don't see you again, tell my sister's grave the company finally paid its bill."

She never gave them a chance to argue. The *Wren*—ugly, loyal, impossible *Wren*—backed out of the slip with a wash of black water and swung for the main channel, running lights lit up like a festival, horn blasting three short blasts, long, three short, the old distress-and-defiance signal that every harbor rat in a hundred miles would recognize and every Consortium officer would have to answer.

Every light on the waterfront turned toward her. Every boot. Every gun.

"Come on," Ada said hoarsely, and they ran for the east slip.

* * *

They heard it before they saw it.

The cutter's searchlight snapped on—a white column sweeping the black water—and found the *Wren* instantly, because Sefa wanted to be found, and behind the light came the flat crack-crack-crack of rifle fire skipping across the harbor.

Ada froze at the top of the fish-market stairs, one bag strap in her fist, and watched the little boat run. And she was magnificent—that was the unbearable part, the part Ada would carry forever. The *Wren* zigzagged through the moored fleet like a fox through a henyard, trailing noise and wake, daring the whole harbor to catch her, and Sefa stood at the wheel with her coat flying open and did not duck, did not flinch, steered into the searchlight on purpose so that every muzzle flash would find her and no one else.

"She's going to make the breakwater," Ilya whispered. "Look—she's going to—"

The searchlight stuttered. Swung. Held.

Ada saw the *Wren* shudder—not dramatically, not like in stories, just a small lurch, a hitch in her stride, like a runner catching a toe—and then she kept going, kept going, kept going, out past the last mooring buoy, and the firing stopped, and the cutter turned to pursue, and the boom chain at the harbor mouth stayed down because the cutter was through it and everyone was looking out to sea instead of in.

"She made it," Ilya breathed. "She made the open water—"

But Ada had watched boats her whole life, on rivers and lakes and the hard gray coast, and she knew what a wounded boat looked like from a distance, the way it sits wrong in the water, the way its wake goes ragged. The *Wren*'s wake was ragged. And she wasn't running clean anymore. She was limping toward the dark beyond the breakwater, smaller and smaller, and the cutter was gaining, and there was nothing—nothing—Ada could do but stand on the stairs with a bag of stolen truth on her shoulder and watch.

"Come on," Ilya said softly. "Ada. Come on. She told us where to be."

They went down to the east slip. Toma's cousin's skiff was exactly where promised, low in the water, smelling of fish and tar, and Ilya got the small engine going on the third pull while Ada cast off with hands that wouldn't quite obey her.

They slipped out along the shadowed eastern shore, past the sleeping fish market, past the seawall, out into the channel where the water lay black and shivering under a sky just beginning to consider dawn. Behind them the harbor seethed with light and shouting. Ahead the sea opened, gray and endless and indifferent.

Ada kept looking back.

At first there was too much glare to see anything. Then the cutter's searchlight swept far to the north, following some report, some rumor, and the space where the *Wren* had been was just darkness and the faintest smudge of smoke against the brightening east.

Smoke.

"Ada." Ilya's voice was very quiet. "There's a flare. Look. North, past the point."

A single red star rose over the water, hung there burning, and fell. Sefa's flare gun. The signal they'd agreed on weeks ago, in another life, on a river running the wrong direction: *one red star means I'm down but alive.*

Except Sefa had also told them, once, lazily, in the cove while mending nets: *two red stars means don't come.*

The sky over the northern point stayed empty for ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty.

Then a second star went up—shaky, lower, dying halfway through its arc—and Ada turned the skiff's tiller hard before Ilya finished saying her name.

"That's north," he said. "That's away from everything. If we go north we lose the tide, we lose the window, we lose—"

"She held the dock for us." Ada's voice came out flat and cold and completely steady, which surprised her, because inside she was anything but. "She stood in a searchlight so we could walk out of a fish market. We're going north."

Ilya looked at her for a long moment. Then he moved forward in the skiff and put his hands on the throttle beside hers, and together they turned the bow toward the second star's falling place, into the gray water and the coming day, while behind them the harbor bells rang on and on for a boat that was no longer answering.
