## 29. Sefa's Stand

The skiff ran north for eleven minutes before Ada turned it around.

She counted them. She couldn't help counting them—eleven minutes of gray water slapping the hull, of Ilya's jaw set hard enough to crack walnuts, of the harbor bells fading behind them like a conversation she'd walked out of mid-sentence. Eleven minutes, and then she eased off the throttle until the engine dropped to a mutter, and Ilya looked up from the bow where he'd been pretending to study the shoreline.

"No," he said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You turned the boat around. That's a whole paragraph."

Ada kept her eyes on the water ahead, on the smudge of the fish market roofs coming back out of the dawn haze. "She's alive."

"You don't know that."

"Harbor bells," Ada said. "They rang for a boat that wasn't answering. You don't ring bells for a body floating in the channel, Ilya. You ring them when someone's made it to a berth, or a beach, or a friend's slip, and you're too angry at them to do anything but make noise about it." She fed the throttle back in, gentle, the way you'd approach a horse that had already spooked once tonight. "Sefa's too mean to die in a place with good acoustics. She'd hate the echo."

Ilya stared at her. Then he laughed—a short, cracked sound, half relief and half despair—and dragged both hands down his face. "You're insane."

"Probably." She scanned the northern shore as they came about. "But we're going back along the far side of the breakwater, quiet, and we're going to find where she beached that boat, and then we are going to leave this harbor properly, with all three of us, before Hale's people finish their headcount. Agreed?"

He didn't answer right away. He looked back south, toward the smoke still standing over the fish market district, toward the searchlights sweeping the water in slow, patient arcs, like hands feeling under furniture for coins. Then he moved aft and took the tiller from her without asking, which was its own kind of agreement.

"Far side of the breakwater," he said. "And if there are gunboats—"

"There won't be. They think we ran north. Every searchlight is pointed where they think we went." Ada allowed herself one grim smile. "Congratulations. Tonight you learned how a ranger thinks. It's mostly just assuming everyone else is lazy."

* * *

Later—much later, when the three of them were huddled in the dripping dark under a collapsed net-drying rack on the mudflats below Kell's Landing, and Sefa had stopped bleeding enough to start complaining—Ada would ask her what it had actually been like, standing on that dock.

Sefa's answer was typical. She shrugged with her whole body, winced, and said, "Loud."

But Ada had seen some of it. Seen it from the water, over her shoulder, in the seconds between the alarm sirens and the moment the skiff swallowed them into the canal shadows. And what she saw, she would carry a long time.

* * *

It had gone wrong fast, the way things do.

One minute they were moving through the fish market in the blue-black hour before dawn, crates of ice stacked around them, Ilya's ledger copies sewn into the lining of Ada's coat, everything working, everything quiet. The next minute a dockhand—the young one, the one who'd smiled at Sefa two nights ago when she bought diesel and haggled him down to an insulting price—had looked at Ilya's face and gone white as the ice in the crates.

Ada never learned his name. She thought about that later too. He hadn't even shouted. He'd just frozen, and then turned, and then run, and the running was the shout. Somewhere behind the market a whistle blew, then another, and then the floodlights came on all at once along the consortium pier, four white suns blooming over black water.

"Split," Sefa had said, calm as a woman reading a tide table. "Boat's mine. You two take the canal path, north end, there's a skiff tied at the third piling—gray tarp, don't touch the tarp, it's hiding rust, not cargo." She'd pressed the flare gun into Ada's hands without looking down to do it, the way you hand someone salt across a dinner table. "Take this. Don't waste it."

"What are you—" Ilya started.

"I'm going to be extremely visible," Sefa said, and stepped out of the shadow of the ice crates into the open dock, and lit a cigarette.

That was the part Ada kept returning to afterward. Not the gunfire, though there was gunfire. Not the shouting, though there was plenty. The cigarette. Sefa standing alone in the middle of a floodlit pier with her hands in her pockets and a cigarette burning, waiting for the guards to come to her, because every second they spent coming to her was a second they weren't spending on the canal path.

They came, of course. Four of them first, then more, boots hammering planks. And Sefa—five foot nothing, sixty-some years old, a woman built like a mooring post and about as talkative—looked at them and said something Ada couldn't hear from the water but could read plainly from the shape of her mouth, and it was not polite.

Then she ran. Not away. Along. Down the length of the consortium pier itself, drawing them after her like a lure drawn through water, away from the canal, away from the skiff, away from everything that mattered, her boots loud on the planks on purpose. A guard lunged and caught her jacket; she shrugged out of it without breaking stride, which meant she'd left it unzipped for exactly that reason, which meant she had planned this in the time it took to light a cigarette.

"She planned it," Ada said now, on the mudflats, wrapping the wound above Sefa's hip with strips of sailcloth boiled in seawater because it was all they had. "You planned it. The jacket, the flare—"

"The coat was unzipped because it was hot," Sefa said. "Everything else was planned."

Something hit her on the pier—a rifle butt, or a boot, or the deck itself when they threw her down—and Ada heard the crack of it across forty yards of black water and felt it in her own ribs. And then Sefa rolled onto her back in the searchlight, surrounded by men with guns, and raised one arm, and in her fist was the second flare gun, the one Ada hadn't known about, the one she'd kept for herself.

She fired it straight up.

The flare went off directly above the consortium pier, a burning red star hanging over their own floodlit operation, and every guard within a mile looked up at it, and every guard within a mile was therefore silhouetted, outlined, catalogued—from the canal path, from the fishing boats riding at anchor, from the dark windows of a hundred homes whose owners the consortium had believed were asleep, or bought, or afraid. For six full seconds the flare burned, and in those six seconds Sefa lay on the planks laughing, Ada could see her shoulders shaking, laughing at them, at the lights, at the whole rotten arrangement, and somewhere in the dark a voice called out—not a rescue, not yet, just a voice, an old man's voice saying her name like a question—and other voices answered it.

The flare died. The guards remembered their rifles. But by then the skiff was sliding out of the canal mouth, and by the time anyone thought to look at the water, the gray tarp and the rust and the two fugitives were gone into the channel dark, and Sefa was being hauled to her feet by dockworkers who had decided, all together, without discussion, that no consortium man was taking her anywhere.

"They put me in a dinghy," Sefa said now, from under the net rack, while Ilya held a cracked mirror so she could check the dressing. "Rowed me up here themselves. Wouldn't take payment. One of them cried, which was embarrassing for everyone." She paused. "His father worked my sister's boat. Before."

Nobody said anything for a while. Out on the flats, the tide was coming back in, filling the channels with small busy sounds, the world quietly repairing itself around them the way it always did, indifferent and generous at once.

"Courage as inheritance," Ilya said softly, almost to himself. He was staring at the flare gun, the empty one, turning it over in his hands. "That's what that was. Her sister's. All those people on the docks tonight—they've been carrying it twenty years, waiting for someone to light it."

"Don't get poetic at me," Sefa said. "I'm wounded. It makes me sentimental enough already."

* * *

They stayed under the net rack through the worst of the morning, because moving a wounded woman in daylight past a harbor full of search warrants was suicide with extra steps, and Ada had done enough math for one week.

Sefa's wound was ugly but not mortal—a bullet through the meat above her hip, in and out, missing everything that mattered by what the old river surgeons called "a prayer's width." Ada cleaned it, packed it, stitched it with the curved needle from her kit, and did her best field surgery while Sefa narrated criticism throughout, a service she provided free of charge.

"Tighter."

"It needs slack for swelling."

"Tighter, ranger. I've been sewn up by drunk fishermen with twine. You can afford to be ambitious."

"You've been shot before?" Ilya asked, from his post at the edge of the rack, watching the shore road.

"Twice. Three times if you count the pig." At their looks: "Different story. Different country. Ask me when there's rum." Then, quieter, watching Ada tie off the last stitch: "You're good at this."

"My brother bled a lot," Ada said. "I got practiced at not losing people." She heard how it came out a beat too late, and Sefa heard it too, and mercifully neither of them made it a thing.

By late afternoon the tide was high enough and the searchlights had swung south, chasing rumors of them in the wrong direction—which meant the dockworkers were feeding the consortium false sightings, which meant an entire waterfront had quietly chosen sides, and Ada sat with that knowledge the way you sit with a coal in your cupped hands, careful and warmed at once. Twenty years of fear, and it had taken one old woman lying in a searchlight to tip it over.

"We can't take the main channel," she said, when the light went amber and it was finally time to move. "Hale will have the narrows wired by nightfall. He knows we have copies of the ledger, and he knows copies are only worth something if they reach the coast."

"So we don't take the coast," Ilya said. "We go up."

"Up is the camp. Up is Hale's front door."

"Up is Toma." Sefa said it from where she lay, flat and certain, and pushed herself up on one elbow despite Ada's glare. "Toma's cove. His ferry's burned, but his arms work, and he owes this family more than he owes his own skin. And above the cove—" she tapped the air, tracing a line only she could see, the line her sister had probably traced once, twenty years ago, going the same direction for the same reasons— "above the cove is the glacier road. Calving window opens in five days. Maybe six."

"That's a two-day crossing for a healthy crew," Ada said. "You can't walk it with a hole in your hip."

"I can't walk it," Sefa agreed. "But I can steer it. There's a way up the shoulder of the valley by water, if you know the rocks, and nobody alive knows those rocks better than me except a dead man, and I'm faster than he is." She looked at Ada, and for the first time since the dock, since the flare, since the cigarette, something moved behind her face that wasn't fury or iron. Something tired, and old, and asking. "My sister died in that valley because nobody carried the truth out. I'm not dying in it. I'm going in, and I'm coming out, and the difference between me and her is the two of you and what's sewn into your coat. So don't you dare leave me on these mudflats to be sensible."

The tide lapped at the racks. Somewhere inland a bird complained about the evening.

"Fine," Ada said. "But you ride in the middle, and you hold onto things, and if you tear those stitches I'm sewing them again without the rum story."

"Barbarian," Sefa said, with approval.

* * *

They launched at full dark, the skiff riding low with three aboard and supplies, and slid north up the eastern channel where the water ran shallow and silvered with moonlight and no searchlight had ever bothered to look, because no map showed the channel as navigable. Sefa navigated it anyway, from a nest of blankets in the stern, calling depths and hazards in a low flat voice—rock, current, deadfall, rock—like a woman reciting a psalm she'd learned before she could read.

An hour up, Ilya sat down beside Ada at the tiller. He didn't say anything for a long time. The banks slipped past, dark and breathing.

"On the dock," he finally said. "When she stood up in the lights. I kept thinking—I signed the manifests that paid the men who hunt us. And she stood up in the lights anyway. For us. For a ledger she wants burned." He shook his head slowly. "I keep waiting for the arithmetic to balance. What I owe against what I've done. It doesn't. It just gets bigger."

"Maybe that's what a debt is," Ada said. "Not a number. A direction."

He looked at her then, moonlight and riverlight moving on his face, and whatever he saw in hers made him nod once and look away, and neither of them said anything else, because some things are better steered than spoken.

Ahead, far up the black shoulder of the valley, lightning flickered inside a cloud with no thunder following—heat lightning, monsoon lightning, the sky clearing its throat. Five days, maybe six, until the glacier calved and the pass opened. Behind them, Hale's boats hunted an empty channel. Above them, the storm was gathering the way debts gather, patient and enormous.

"Hold course," Sefa said from the stern, her voice rough but steady, carrying the weight of every woman who'd ever stood up in a searchlight so someone else could run. "And somebody find me that rum. I've got a story about a pig, and I think we've earned it."
