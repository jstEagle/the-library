## 30. Into the Ice Field

The whiteout came down on them like a lid.

One moment Ada could see the glacier's spine stretching ahead of them, a gray river of ice running between black walls of stone; the next, the world shrank to a circle ten feet across, and then to less than that, until the only things that existed were her own boots, Ilya's back three steps ahead, and the wind screaming through the gap in her hood like something with opinions.

"Rope!" she shouted, and didn't know if he heard her until his arm shot up over his shoulder, holding out the coil they'd taken from Toma's burned ferry. She tied it around her waist with fingers she couldn't feel, biting the knot tight because her teeth still worked even if nothing else did.

They had been running for two hours. Maybe three. Time had gone soft and unreliable, the way it did when your body was burning through its last reserves and your mind started making deals with itself. *Just to that boulder. Just to that blue shadow in the ice. Just one more ridge.* The calving window was closing early — Sefa had said so through gritted teeth before they'd left her at the moraine camp, propped against a supply crate with her hand pressed to the wound in her side, refusing with the whole force of her personality to lie down.

"The pass narrows by tomorrow noon," she'd said. "After that, no boat, no sled, no prayer gets through. And Hale's men will know it too. They'll stop chasing and start waiting."

So they ran. Up-glacier, into the white, with duplicate ledger pages sealed in oilcloth against Ada's chest and the original wrapped in Ilya's coat, and behind them — somewhere back there in all that howling nothing — six men with rifles and a director's money.

Ada counted her steps. It was an old ranger trick, one of the few things from her training that had ever felt like a friend instead of a taskmaster. Count to a hundred, check the ground, count again. On step sixty-one, the snow under her right boot made a sound she'd been dreading since they'd stepped onto the ice field.

Not a crunch. A *hollow* sound. Like knocking on a door.

She threw herself sideways.

The bridge of snow held for half a second longer — long enough for her to roll clear, arms windmilling, heart slamming — and then collapsed into a crevasse so deep that the darkness below didn't end so much as *continue*, a blue-black throat going down farther than any rope they owned would reach.

Ilya hauled her up by the rope before she'd finished sliding, both hands fisted in the line, dragging her away from the edge with a strength she hadn't known was in him. They ended up sitting in the snow ten feet from the hole, breathing hard, white fog pouring out of them.

"You found that with your feet," he said finally.

"I found it with my ears." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "It sounded hollow."

"It sounded like the end of the world."

"That too."

He laughed — a short, cracked sound, more shock than humor, but real. Then he crawled forward on his belly, the way Toma had taught them, and peered into the crevasse. "How deep?"

"Don't find out."

"Fair." He backed away and got up slowly, and when he turned to face her, the wind ripped the words half apart but she caught them anyway: "We can't see anything. We're going to walk straight into one of these if we keep going blind."

Ada looked back the way they'd come. Nothing. White on white on white. Hale's men could be fifty yards behind them or five miles; the storm erased everything equally, which cut both ways. It hid their pursuers. It also hid every crevasse, every icefall, every drop-off between here and the pass.

"We need the probe," she said. "Toma's pole."

Ilya unslung the pack, dug past the food tin and the flare gun Sefa had pressed into Ada's hands at the last minute — *"Point it at whatever you want to not die"* — and pulled out the nine-foot bamboo pole with the steel tip, salvaged from the wreck of Toma's ferry. Twenty years that old man had poled his ferry through these waters. Now his pole was going to walk them across a glacier. Ada thought there was probably a lesson in that, about what survived and what didn't, but she was too cold to find it.

"Probing formation," she said. "You take point. Pole every step, left-center-right. I follow in your tracks, exactly, no improvising. We move at the speed of certainty."

"And if we hear Hale's men?"

"Then we move faster and pray louder."

* * *

They moved for another hour like that — Ilya stabbing the pole ahead of him in a patient rhythm, *thock, thock, thock*, solid, solid, solid, and once, horribly, *nothing*, the pole plunging through a hidden bridge with a soft sigh, and both of them freezing while Ilya eased backward foot by foot until they could skirt the gap in a wide arc, Ada marking the route with little cairns of stone she built as fast as her numb hands allowed.

The cairns were for Sefa, if Sefa lived, and could follow. Ada didn't say that out loud. Neither did Ilya. But when she stacked the third one, he stopped and added a flat stone to the top of it, carefully, like laying a flower on a grave.

"She's tougher than both of us," he said.

"Sefa could argue with a blizzard and win."

"She's already doing that, probably. Down there on the moraine, telling the storm exactly where it can put its snow."

Ada smiled despite everything. That was the thing about Ilya she kept rediscovering, the thing that kept ambushing her in the worst moments: he made the dark smaller. Not by denying it — he'd signed off on enough death to never deny it again — but by standing next to you inside it and talking, quietly, stubbornly, as if the two of you were the reasonable ones and the apocalypse was being dramatic.

They stopped in the lee of a serac — a leaning tower of ice the size of a chapel, groaning under its own weight — and shared the last of the dried fish and a quarter liter of water, taking turns sipping because drinking too fast in this cold made your teeth ache down to the roots. Ada checked the oilcloth packet against her ribs. Dry. Intact. Forty pages of names and numbers and signatures, each one a person the Consortium had spent like currency.

"My brother's crew is page twelve," she said. She hadn't meant to say it. It just came out, the way truths do at altitude, where there's not enough air for pretense.

Ilya went still. "I know."

"You read it."

"I read all of it. Twice." He looked at his hands, at the frost-nipped fingertips he'd been flexing all day. "I signed the authorization that moved the freight contract your brother died on. Not my signature on the accident order — that was Hale's office, three levels up, someone whose name isn't even in the ledger. But my signature was underneath it. Certifying the route as 'economically viable.' Which is auditor language for *the insurance payout exceeds the loss.*"

Ada watched him. The wind filled the silence for a while.

"When we get down," she said, "you're going to say all of that again. To the magistrate. To the press. To anyone who'll print it."

"I know."

"Not because it clears you. Because the dead deserve to hear it said out loud, by the man who signed."

He nodded. And then, because he was Ilya, he said, "Your brother's name was Oscar."

Something went through her chest like a pick through lake ice. "How do you—"

"Page twelve. Manifest list. You've never read it aloud, have you? You read around it. I've watched you check the packet four times today and skip the same page every time." He wasn't accusing her. His voice was almost gentle. "Oscar Nystrom. Deck boss. There were eight others."

"Nine," Ada said. "Nine including him." She stared out into the white. "I named the book in my head after him years ago. *Oscar and the Summit.* He always wanted to see a glacier. Said the desert had everything except something that moved slow enough to trust." She laughed, and it hurt. "He would have loved this stupid ice field."

"Then let's get across it," Ilya said, "so he can watch us do it."

* * *

The storm thickened toward late afternoon, if the gray dimming could be called afternoon — light and time had divorced somewhere back at the serac and weren't speaking. Visibility dropped to arm's length. The probing rhythm slowed. Every step became a negotiation.

And then, through the roar, Ada heard something that wasn't wind.

Voices. Distant, directionless, bounced and shredded by the storm — but voices. More than one. Shouting to each other over the gale, the way searchers do.

She grabbed Ilya's sleeve and pulled him down behind a pressure ridge, and they lay flat against the ice, breath held, listening. The voices drifted. Closer, then farther, then — worse — seeming to come from everywhere at once, the way sound lies in a whiteout.

"Left or right?" Ilya breathed.

Ada closed her eyes and listened like a ranger. Wind from the northwest, steady. Voices moving *with* the wind, slightly east of them, upslope. Hale's men were sweeping a line across the glacier, driving toward the narrowing pass, herding anything alive ahead of them like dogs working sheep.

"They're pushing us," she whispered. "They don't need to catch us. They just need to funnel us into the bad ice and let the glacier do the work."

"So we go where they're not."

"Which is directly into the wind, upslope, through the icefall." She opened her eyes. "Where nobody sane goes."

Ilya considered this. Snow accumulated on his shoulders and the back of his head until he looked like something the mountain had grown. "Toma told me once," he said, "that his father crossed this ice field in a storm like this. Said the trick was to stop trusting your eyes entirely. Eyes lie in a whiteout. Trust your feet, your ears, and the rope."

"His father sounds like he was fun at parties."

"He ferried people across a killing river for forty years. I imagine fun was low on his list."

They moved into the wind.

It was the hardest hour of Ada's life, and she'd once walked out of the Salt Reach with a broken ankle and a dead horse. The wind hit them full in the faces like a wall of gravel, ice crystals scouring every inch of exposed skin, and the probing had to be doubled because the icefall ahead was a chaos of shattered seracs and leaning slabs, a frozen shipwreck the size of a city. Ilya stabbed the pole. Ada followed. Cairn, pole, step. Cairn, pole, step. At some point her thoughts narrowed down to a single repeating sentence — *one more, one more, one more* — and she understood that this was what survival actually was, stripped of everything: not courage, not cleverness, just the refusal to stop putting one foot in front of the other, maintained past the point where the body filed formal objections.

Once, the pole went through and Ilya dropped to his waist before the rope snapped taut and Ada threw herself backward, boots skidding, and held him — ninety seconds of pure screaming effort, her shoulders tearing, her heels carving twin trenches, until he got an elbow onto solid ice and wormed out, gasping, white to the skin.

They lay together afterward, tangled in the rope, shaking.

"Okay," he wheezed. "New rule. No more new rules."

"Agreed," she gasped.

But they got up. One more, one more, one more.

* * *

The storm broke at dusk with the suddenness of a curtain falling.

One moment: white chaos. The next: the wind simply quit, as if embarrassed by its own performance, and the clouds tore open along the eastern horizon, and the whole ice field lit up gold and rose and impossible — mile upon mile of fractured ice glowing like embers, long blue shadows pooling in every crevasse, the black rock walls of the valley flanking them, and far ahead, catching the last direct light like a signal fire, the summit pass: a notch of open sky between two peaks, with the glacier flowing through it in a smooth, deceptively gentle tongue.

Ada stood up, legs trembling, and forgot for a full thirty seconds that she was being hunted.

"Oh," she said softly.

"Yeah," Ilya said beside her.

It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it was trying to kill her, and both facts were equally true, and she thought: *this is what Oscar wanted.* This exact light. This exact terrible gorgeous indifference.

Then she saw the smoke.

Three threads of it, rising thin and straight in the dead-calm air, from the mouth of the pass. Campfires. Someone was already there, settled in, waiting. Hale's men had split — some sweeping the ice field, some holding the exit — and now, in the clear air, Ada could see the whole trap laid out with perfect clarity: the narrowing pass ahead, blocked; the sweep lines behind, closing; and the ice field itself, riddled with crevasses, offering a hundred ways to die and none to escape.

Ilya saw it too. He was quiet for a long moment. Then he unslung the pack and took out Sefa's flare gun and weighed it in his hand.

"There's a fourth option," he said.

"Enlighten me."

"Toma said the pass closes when the glacier calves. Big calving event, the whole tongue shifts, nothing crosses for days." He pointed, not at the pass, but at the glacier's flank — at a vast, unstable-looking wall of ice halfway up the valley, hanging over the route like a loaded question. "What if we *made* it close? Behind us. With Hale's men on the wrong side of it."

Ada stared at the hanging ice, then at the flare gun, then at the man who had once certified death for a living and now stood proposing to bring down a glacier with a signal flare and audacity.

"That's insane," she said.

"Yes."

"The concussion could trigger it anywhere. Including under *us*."

"Also yes."

"The timing would have to be perfect."

"Which is why," Ilya said, and there was that quiet stubbornness again, that smallness-that-was-not-small, "we should start walking toward it now, while you figure out the part where we live."

Ada looked at the pass, at the fires, at the beautiful murderous light dying off the ice. She thought of page twelve. Of nine names she'd never said aloud. Of a debt owed not to a company but to the dead, payable in truth, collectible only if she lived to speak it.

She checked the flare gun. Two cartridges.

"All right," she said. "Show me where we stand when the mountain answers."

Behind them, faint on the wind, came the first shout of the searchers who had spotted their tracks in the clearing storm. Ahead, the hanging ice waited, patient as everything else on the glacier, for someone foolish enough to knock.

Ada Nystrom started walking.
