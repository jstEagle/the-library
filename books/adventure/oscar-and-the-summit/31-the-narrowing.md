## 31. The Narrowing

The mountain told them the truth before anyone said it aloud.

Ada heard it first as a change in the wind—a new note in it, high and thin, like a wire singing. Then the light changed. For three days they had climbed through the calving window under a sky the color of pewter, and now that sky was lowering itself over them like a lid.

"The window's closing," Ilya said. He didn't shout it. He didn't need to. The glacier had gone quiet around them in a way that felt less like peace and more like held breath.

They stood on a shelf of blue ice above a field of seracs—those leaning towers of glacial ice that looked, Ada thought, like a city built by something with more ambition than patience. Beyond them, the pass narrowed to a corridor between two walls of ice, and somewhere in that corridor was the bridge Toma had promised: an arch of old ice spanning a crevasse deep enough to swallow a church.

Behind them, smaller now but unmistakable, were four figures in Consortium grey, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew the window would hold for them because their employer had paid for it to hold.

"Four of them," Sefa said. She sat on a supply crate they'd hauled up from the boat wreck, her left arm bound tight against her chest, her face the color of old candle wax. She'd been shot two days ago at the dock and had described the wound, in her laconic way, as "a scratch with opinions." Now she was breathing in short, careful sips, the way you drink scalding tea.

"Four," Ada confirmed, glassing back down the slope. "And they're not hurrying."

"Why aren't they hurrying?" Ilya asked. There was a genuine, wounded puzzlement in his voice that made Ada want to laugh and also hug him, which was becoming an inconvenient habit.

"Because they know something we don't," she said. "Or because they think we'll do their work for them and fall into a crevasse out of politeness."

"I would never," Ilya said. "I'm very impolite. Ask anyone at the Consortium."

"They did," Ada said. "That's why there are four men with rifles walking up a glacier after us."

* * *

They moved.

There was no other verb for it. The ice underfoot was old and groaning, threaded with meltwater channels that ran silver and quick beneath surfaces thin as windowpane. Ada went first, reading the glacier the way she'd once read salt flats—by texture, by tilt, by the small telltale hollows where snow bridged over nothing. Ilya came second, carrying most of the weight because he refused, with a stubbornness Ada found both infuriating and quietly moving, to let Sefa carry anything heavier than the flare gun.

"You're favoring your left side," he told her for the fourth time that morning.

"And you're favoring nagging," Sefa said. "We all have our burdens."

The humor was thin, but it was theirs, and Ada had learned over these weeks that thin humor on a glacier is like a small fire in a wet forest: not much, but everything depends on keeping it lit.

By midday the light had gone flat and sourceless—the dreaded whiteout light, where sky and ice surrender their border and the world becomes an unwritten page. Shadows vanished. Distance became a rumor. Ada roped them together at twenty-foot intervals and taught Ilya, again, the probe-and-step rhythm: pole, tap, listen, step.

"What am I listening for?" he asked.

"Hollow," Ada said. "Ice over air sounds different. Like knocking on a door versus knocking on a wall."

"And if I knock on a door?"

"Then you've found a crevasse."

"And if I knock on a wall?"

"Then you've found Hale, and we have a different problem."

He laughed—a short, startled bark—and Ada felt it land somewhere under her ribs and stay there. Two days ago she'd kissed him against the storm wall of Toma's cove, and since then every joke between them carried a faint electric charge, like wire near a storm. They had agreed, without quite saying so, that whatever this was might cost everything. That agreement did not appear to have reduced anything. If anything it had raised the stakes, the way naming a thing always does.

Sefa brought up the rear, tied to the rope but mostly pulling herself along the line hand over hand, conserving her legs. Twice Ada looked back and caught her resting her forehead against the rope, eyes closed, counting breaths. The third time, Sefa opened one eye and caught her looking.

"I'm fine," Sefa said.

"You're lying."

"I'm laconic. It's different. Lying takes more words."

* * *

The bridge appeared out of the white like a verdict.

One moment the page was blank; the next, the fog thinned for perhaps thirty seconds, and there it was—an arch of ancient ice spanning the crevasse, maybe forty feet from lip to lip, its underside dripping threads of meltwater that fell away into a darkness so complete it seemed less like depth than like subtraction. The bridge was beautiful the way certain mushrooms are beautiful: invitingly, fatally.

"Oh no," Ilya said.

"It holds," Ada said, though her voice came out flatter than she intended.

"It looks like a meringue."

"It's held for years."

"So has my aunt's marriage, and I wouldn't walk across that either."

Ada knelt at the lip and studied it properly, because fear deserved respect but not obedience. The arch was thick at its shoulders, thinner at its crown. Meltwater had carved a channel down its spine. The day was cooling, which helped; ice that would sag at noon could hold at dusk. But the drip-threads told her the core was going soft, and the low, intermittent groan from the crevasse depths told her the whole structure was negotiating with gravity and losing ground slowly.

"We cross one at a time," she said. "Rope stays on. Sefa last, and I go ahead of her to take the strain if—"

"If," Sefa said. "My favorite word."

But even as she said it, the sound came from behind them.

Not thunder. Not the glacier's usual complaints. A voice—carried up the corridor of ice by the wind-funnel of the pass, distorted but legible:

"Nystrom! Voss!"

Hale's men. Closer than they should have been. The whiteout that had blinded Ada had apparently not slowed men who knew this route, who had perhaps walked it before, who had perhaps been waiting for the calving season the way a spider waits at the center of its web.

Ada turned. Through the shifting fog she made out shapes—three, no, four—fifty yards down-slope and closing with the terrible economy of professionals. No shots yet. Shots on a glacier are a stupid man's tool; every bullet that misses becomes a hazard, and every bullet that hits means hauling a body or leaving evidence. They wanted to walk up and take the ledger and take Ilya and probably take Ada too, tidily, at arm's length.

"Bridge," Sefa said. Just the word. Just the fact.

"I know."

"The window's closing early." Ilya's voice had gone quiet and precise, the auditor's voice, the one that emerged when everything else fell away. "Look at the temperature. Look at the water. We have hours, maybe less, before that arch won't hold a sparrow. And they know it. That's why they're not running. They don't need to run. They just need us to hesitate."

Ada looked at the bridge. Then at the men. Then at Sefa, whose breathing had gone shallow, whose face had gone from candle-wax to something closer to ash.

Trap closing from both sides. Behind, men. Ahead, a bridge deciding whether to exist. Below, a darkness with no floor that any of them had seen.

"Okay," Ada said, and heard her own voice steady itself the way it did on the worst nights of her brother's absence, when steadiness was the only thing left to give. "New plan. We cross together. All three, spaced on the rope, fast, no stopping. If the bridge starts to go, we spread the load and keep moving—weight distributed is weight survived. Sefa, you stay on the rope no matter what. Ilya—"

"Yes?"

"When we get across, you cut the rope anchor on our side. Not before. Not early. I know how you feel about cutting things."

"I cut one thing," Ilya said, wounded. "One time. And it was a mooring line that needed cutting."

"It was attached to a dock," Ada said. "With Sefa's boat on it."

"The boat was leaving anyway."

"The boat was sinking, Ilya. That's not the same as leaving."

"Can we argue about boats," Sefa said, "after the murder bridge?"

* * *

They crossed.

Ada went first, and the bridge spoke to her through her boots—a creak, a shiver, a sensation like walking on the deck of a ship in a long swell. Halfway across, the fog closed again and she was crossing on faith alone, arms out, each footfall placed by memory of the thirty seconds she'd been allowed to see. Behind her, the rope paid out in small tugs: Ilya's weight joining the span, then Sefa's, three heartbeats strung along forty feet of dying ice above a hole in the world.

At the far side her boots hit solid shelf and she nearly wept with the ordinariness of it. She hauled the rope, hand over hand, calling encouragement she didn't fully believe—"Come on, come on, come on"—and Ilya came stumbling out of the white with his hair full of frost and his eyes enormous, and then Sefa, gray-faced, gasping, alive.

Alive. Ada let herself have exactly one second of that word. Then she turned to the anchor.

That was when the first shot cracked off the ice walls.

It went wide—deliberately wide, a warning, or a test—but the sound in the corridor was apocalyptic, doubled and redoubled, and somewhere below them in the crevasse something answered. A deep, structural groan. The kind of sound a building makes in an earthquake movie right before the audience starts grabbing their coats.

"They're shooting," Ilya said, incredulous. "On a glacier. In a whiteout. Next to a crevasse. Do they want to die?"

"Some people," Sefa said, sliding down against an ice block, "have never bailed out a leaking boat in a monsoon, and it shows."

The second shot was not a warning. It snapped past close enough that Ada felt the wind of it, and struck the bridge's shoulder with a crack like a rifle hitting a church bell—which, acoustically speaking, it essentially was.

The bridge rang. And then, horribly, it began to sing—that same high wire-note Ada had heard in the wind that morning, except now it was coming from the ice itself, from the fracture racing invisibly through the arch's crown.

Across the gap, through a tear in the fog, Ada saw one of the grey figures stop dead. Even from here she could read the body language: the sudden stillness of a man realizing his employer's plan had a flaw in it, namely that the plan required the bridge to survive being shot at.

"Anchor!" Ada shouted, and dropped to the ice, driving the piton home with the flat of her axe, three blows, four, tying off the rope in a knot her hands knew better than her mind did. "Get behind me—all of you, behind the shelf—"

Another shot. Another ring. The note changed pitch, dropping, deepening, the way a bell goes when the crack reaches its heart.

And then, from the far side, a voice. Not shouted this time. Carried. Calm. Almost gentle, which was the worst part.

"Ms. Nystrom."

She knew that voice. Everyone in the Consortium's orbit knew that voice. Director Hale had come up his own glacier himself—not trusting the window, not trusting his men, wanting to look her in the eye for the final offer. He stood at the far lip of the crevasse, fifty feet away across the failing arch, a small neat figure in a grey coat, hands clasped behind him like a man admiring architecture.

"The bridge will not hold another hour," Hale called. "Perhaps not another ten minutes. My men can wait. Can you? Your friend needs a doctor—I can see that from here. The temperature is falling. The window is closing. And on your side of that gap, Ms. Nystrom, there is nothing but ice, and distance, and eventually, the same offer I made you in the camp—except colder."

Ada knelt behind the shelf, rope in her fist, and looked at her people. Sefa, breathing in sips. Ilya, watching her with an expression that was half terror and half something else—something that had been there since the kiss, since the bilge confession, since the night he'd told her what he'd signed and she'd decided, silently, not to hate him for it.

Trust, she realized. He was looking at her with trust. After everything. After the counteroffer he'd overheard, after the betrayal he'd felt coming off her like heat, he was still looking at her like she was the fixed point.

The bridge groaned. The note dropped again.

"Ada," Ilya said quietly. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking," she said, "that he's right about one thing. The bridge is going down."

"And the other things?"

"The other things he's wrong about." She tightened her grip on the rope and looked at the arch—cracked, singing, sagging at its crown like a tired shoulder—and felt the plan assemble itself in the place below thought where rangers live. "Because a bridge that's already falling doesn't need to hold us. It just needs to fall *on schedule*. And he's standing on the wrong end of it."

The ice sang. The fog tore open. And the bridge began, at last, to break.
