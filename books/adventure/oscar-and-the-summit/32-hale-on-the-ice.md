## 32. Hale on the Ice

The whiteout broke all at once, the way a held breath breaks.

One moment Ada was navigating by the tug of Ilya's rope in her fist and the memory of Sefa's voice in her ear—*left of the serac, count your steps, don't trust anything that looks solid*—and the next the wind simply stopped, peeled back like a curtain, and the world arrived all at once: blue ice to every horizon, the sun a pale coin behind gauze, and ahead of them, maybe four hundred meters, the bridge.

It was not a bridge the way bridges are bridges. It was a tongue of glacier that had flowed across a crevasse and frozen there, arched and sagging, its underside hollowed by meltwater into something like the ribcage of an enormous animal. It spanned perhaps thirty meters. Beyond it, the far side rose toward the pass—a notch in the black rock, still open, still breathing out that faint steam of warmer air from the valley beyond. Still possible.

Ada counted steps without meaning to. It was a habit from the salt flats, where distance was the only honest thing. Four hundred meters of ice between them and the bridge. Behind them—

Behind them, she heard the dogs.

Not dogs, exactly. She'd learned that much on the moraine two days ago, when the first pursuit party had appeared as smudges on the white and then resolved into men on snow machines, their engines whining a note too high for comfort. But the sound they made coming over the ice—the eager, carrying clamor of them—would always be dogs to her. The sound of something that had caught a scent and intended to keep it.

"They're close," Ilya said. He was beside her, roped to her at three meters, his face raw with windburn, one glove missing since the crevasse field so that his bare hand was a mottled thing he'd stopped complaining about somewhere around dawn. "How close?"

"Close enough that we stop talking about it." She looked at the bridge. Then at the sky, which was doing something ominous and slow in the west, clouds stacking themselves like plates before a dinner no one wanted to attend. "And we start talking about that."

"The bridge."

"The bridge."

"It'll hold," Ilya said, in the voice of a man who needed to hear himself say it.

"Everything holds right up until it doesn't." Ada started forward anyway, because the mathematics were simple and ugly: the men behind them were faster on machines than she would ever be on foot, and the only variable left was whether the ice under her feet decided to be loyal. "Stay off my rope. Short steps. If I go through, you drop flat and crawl backward, and you do *not* come after me. Say it back."

"I won't come after you."

"Liar. Say it anyway."

"I won't come after you," he said again, softer, and she let it go, because some lies are load-bearing.

They made two hundred meters before the engines came around the shoulder of the icefall behind them and stopped pretending to be weather.

* * *

There were six of them. Ada made the count without turning fully around, which was a skill she'd perfected across ten years of deserts where turning your back on anything was a hobby for corpses. Six figures in Consortium grey, snow machines idling in a loose crescent, and in front of them, walking the last stretch on foot because some men never trusted any ground but their own boots, came Director Hale.

He looked terrible. That was the first thing she noticed, and she held onto it like a handrail. The man who had sat across from her in the camp office three days ago—all pressed collar and reasonable voice, offering her a clean record and a cleared debt like a man dealing cards he knew were marked—that man had been groomed by professionals. This one had chased them up a glacier for two days. His coat was crusted with rime. There was a cut above his eyebrow that someone had taped badly. He was breathing hard, and he was smiling, and the combination made Ada's skin try to leave her body.

"Nystrom." He spread his hands, palms out, the universal gesture of *I am unarmed, please notice how reasonable I am.* "You're a hard woman to have a conversation with."

"You've been having it with snow machines and flares," Ada said. "I'd call that a conversation."

"That was my people being enthusiastic." He glanced past her at Ilya, and something in his face tightened—not anger, Ada thought. Something more like disappointment, which was worse. "Voss. You look awful."

"I've been told I photograph better," Ilya said.

Despite everything—despite the engines and the ice and the bridge groaning somewhere in its hollow chest—Ada felt the corner of her mouth move. That was the trouble with him. Even now. Especially now.

Hale took another step, and the ice under him made a sound like a knuckle cracking, and everyone pretended not to hear it. "Here's where we are," he said. "The window's closing. My meteorologist says eighteen hours, maybe less, before this front comes down and the pass shuts for the season. Behind me is warmth, food, a helicopter pad at the lower camp, and a future. Ahead of you is a piece of ice that has been telling anyone who listens that it intends to fail." He paused. "And beyond it, nothing. A valley of farmers and ghosts. Ledger copies that will take years to matter, if they ever do."

"You forgot the part where I burn the ledger," Ada said. "You always forget that part. It's my favorite part."

"Nystrom." Her name in his mouth had changed weight since the camp. Back then it had been a key he was offering to fit into a lock. Now it was a handle he was trying to turn. "I'm going to say this once, plainly, because I respect plainness and we haven't got time for anything else. Your brother's crew. The manifest. I know what it means to you. So here it is: deliver Voss, surrender the ledger, and every name in that book gets a settlement. Real money. To the families. Public acknowledgment of 'operational failures.' Not a confession—but compensation. More than they'd get from ten years of magistrates and newspapermen. You know how courts work. You've watched them work your whole career. They grind fine and they grind slow, and most of these families will be dead of old age before the grinding finishes." He opened his hands wider. "Or. You walk across that bridge, and you get your truth, and it arrives slowly or never, and meanwhile I go home and the machine keeps running, because the machine always keeps running. One auditor goes down. Another signs the forms. You know this. You've *lived* this."

The wind had begun again, low, worrying at the edges of things. Somewhere beneath them, deep in the glacier's throat, water moved with a sound like distant traffic.

Ada found that she wasn't angry. That surprised her. She'd expected fury—had carried it up the whole ice field like a second pack—and instead what she felt was something quieter and much more dangerous: clarity. The salt-flat kind. The kind where the horizon is flat in every direction and there is nowhere to hide, including from yourself.

"My brother's name was Oscar," she said.

Hale blinked. Whatever script he'd prepared, that wasn't in it.

"Oscar Nystrom. And his crew—" she said the names slowly, the way you set stones on a cairn, one at a time, each one placed so it stays—"was Marta Okonkwo. Denny Ferreira. Old Sam Ilic, who was sixty-one and should never have been on that river and went anyway because the pay was good and his granddaughter needed teeth. And a kid called Tomas Reyes who was nineteen and wrote postcards to his mother in block letters because he thought cursive looked like showing off."

The wind moved. Nobody spoke. Even the snow machines seemed to idle lower.

"They died on a run I booked," Ada said. "I signed the contract. My signature is on the same paper as theirs, in a way. I spent nine years thinking that was my debt—to the company, because the company held the paper. And then I read your ledger, Director, and I learned that the company had already decided how much their lives were worth before they ever launched. Line items. *Operational adjustments.* Sam Ilic fit in a column, Hale. Tomas Reyes fit in a margin note." She shook her head. "So no. No settlements. No operational failures. Their names said out loud, in full, by you, in a courtroom, or I will spend the rest of my life making sure somebody says them for you. That's the offer. From me. Once."

For a moment—one long, glittering moment—she thought it might actually land. Hale's face did something complicated. Grief passed through it like weather; she watched it come and watched him fight it, and understood suddenly that this man had signed those columns himself, years ago, younger, and had built the rest of his life as a wall against the sound of names like hers.

Then the wall held. It always held. That was what walls were for.

"You're romanticizing arithmetic," Hale said quietly. "That's a luxury of people who aren't responsible for payroll."

"And you're doing accounting with people," Ilya said. "I know. I invented the spreadsheet."

Hale turned to him, and for the first time all the reasonableness drained out of his face, and what was underneath was just tired. Deeply, humanly tired. "You were the best of us, Voss. That's what I can't forgive. Not the theft. The *waste*. You could have changed it from inside."

"I did change it from inside," Ilya said. "From inside a boat, going upriver, with the ledger. Turns out inside was bigger than your building."

Something cracked underfoot. It was not a footstep. Everyone froze. Far below, in the blue dark under the bridge, something vast shifted its weight, and the whole arch sang—a low, resonant tone, like a wet finger on a glass rim, rising and refusing to stop.

"The window," Sefa's voice crackled in Ada's earpiece, thin and static-chewed from the ridge where she lay covering the retreat route with a flare gun and, knowing Sefa, probably several opinions. "Whatever you're doing, do it faster. The far side's calving early. I can see dust off the ice wall. You have minutes, not hours."

"Director," Ada said. "Last chance to be a witness instead of a defendant."

Hale looked at the bridge. At the pass beyond it, steaming faintly, closing by degrees. At the sky going the color of dirty wool in the west. And Ada watched him do the same arithmetic she had done, the cold kind, the kind that counts machines and windows and odds—and she saw the exact moment he concluded that he could afford to wait, that the ice would do his work for him, that patience was cheaper than conscience.

"Take them when the bridge drops," he said to his men, turning away. "If they make the far side, fall back to the lower camp. We'll be waiting when the season turns."

"There isn't going to be a season turns," Ada said. "Not for you. Not after the press gets—"

"Nystrom." He didn't even look back. "Nobody reads anymore."

He walked toward his machines. And that was when the glacier, which had listened to all of it with the patience of something twenty thousand years old, decided it had heard enough.

The sound began low, under everything—a groan that came up through Ada's boots and into her teeth, deeper than thunder, older than language. The bridge shivered along its whole length. Rime fell from its arch in glittering sheets. And down at its far end, where it anchored to the opposite wall, a crack opened with a report like a rifle shot and ran toward them across the surface, quick as a thought, splitting the ice into a jagged Y directly beneath their feet.

"Go!" Ada shoved Ilya toward the arch. "GO GO GO—"

Behind them, Hale spun, shouting orders that dissolved into the roar, and the first snow machine lurched forward onto the ice after them, engine screaming, and the bridge—hollow-boned, exhausted, done pretending—began to come apart.
