## 33. The Bridge Breaks

The whiteout had thinned to a gray murk by the time they reached the bridge, which was the only mercy the mountain had offered all day, and like every mercy on this glacier it came with a bill attached.

Ada saw it first because she was in front, breaking trail with her ice axe and pretending her hands weren't shaking. The crevasse below them — the same one that had swallowed Sefa's flare smoke an hour ago, breathing it back up in slow gray ghosts — narrowed here to perhaps four meters across, and someone, sometime, years ago maybe, had laid a slab of old blue ice across the gap like a plank over a cellar door. It sagged in the middle. Water wept from its underside in long glassy threads that refroze as they watched.

"That's not a bridge," Ilya said behind her. "That's a rumor of a bridge."

"It's a bridge."

"It's a rumor with delusions."

She turned to look at him. He was roped three meters back, hood crusted white, lips cracked and bleeding, frostbite blooming gray-yellow along his jaw where he'd refused to keep his collar up because, he claimed, he couldn't hear her over the wind otherwise. He looked terrible. He looked, she thought with a lurch that had nothing to do with altitude, like the best thing she'd ever seen.

"Can you cross it?" she asked.

"I can cross anything if you don't give me time to think about it first."

"Then don't think." She turned back to the span. "Crawl. Distribute your weight. Axe flat, use it like a sled. And whatever you do—"

"Don't look down. I know. I've been audited before; it's the same principle."

Behind him, further down the slope, the wind carried something else up through the murk: voices. Shouts, flattened by snow into something almost gentle, almost conversational. Hale's men. Four of them left after the crevasse field took their patience, moving fast, unroped, arrogant, closing the distance they'd lost when Toma's old ferry trail had fooled them for twenty minutes.

Sefa would have made a joke about arrogance being heavier than gear. Ada's chest did the thing it had been doing since the dock — a hard clench around a name she couldn't say yet — and she shoved it down where it could wait, the way everything waited now, in a room behind her ribs that grew more crowded by the hour.

"Crawl," she said. "Now."

* * *

He went first because she ordered it, and he crawled beautifully, which surprised no one who knew how carefully Ilya Voss moved through the world — a man who had spent years signing documents that killed people and then one day decided to stop, and whose whole body still carried the memory of tiptoeing past consequences. Flat axe, spread knees, chin up. Halfway across the slab groaned, a deep structural complaint, and he froze.

"You're fine," Ada called. "The groan means it's holding. Ice screams when it works."

"Everything screams when it works," he muttered, and kept crawling.

She followed him out onto the span, weight low, axe flat under her chest, and the world beneath her was blue light and falling water and a depth her eyes refused to measure. The slab flexed. Somewhere far below, the glacier shifted its enormous shoulders, and the sound that came up through the ice was less like cracking than like a door opening very slowly in another room.

Three meters. Two. Ilya reached the far lip and got his boots onto solid ground and turned, reaching back for her, and that was when the shouting behind them resolved into shapes.

Hale came out of the whiteout first.

Not running — walking, which was worse. Director Hale, whom Ada had last seen in a heated office offering her a clean record like a man offering a napkin, stood at the near edge of the crevasse with snow in his silver hair and his coat open and his men fanning out behind him, and he looked at the two of them strung across his collapsing bridge with the expression of a landlord surveying tenants who've let the place go.

"Nystrom!" His voice carried flat and clear across the gap. "You're a ranger. You have training. You can see what I see — that slab has minutes. Come back to this side and we all get off this mountain alive. You, me, your thief. Everyone."

"He's lying," Ilya said quietly. "You know he's lying."

"Of course he's lying," Ada said. "But listen to him. Listen to how good he is at it."

Hale spread his hands. "I'm not a monster, Nystrom. I'm a businessman. There's a difference, and the difference is that monsters don't make offers twice." He took a step onto the slab. It held. He smiled. "Your brother's crew — you want their names honored? Fine. A memorial. A settlement for the families. I'll fund it personally. All I need is the ledger and the man who took it, and you walk down this mountain with your badge and your conscience both intact."

And there it was again — the offer, the third offer, each one tailored a little finer than the last, each one cut to fit the exact shape of the hole in her. Ada lay on the ice with the crevasse breathing cold against her stomach and felt the temptation arrive one final time, punctual as a train.

Her brother's name was Oscar. She hadn't said it aloud on this mountain. She hadn't said it aloud in six years, because saying it made him dead in a way that paperwork didn't, and she had built her whole life on the difference between those two things.

"Hale," she called back, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "Their names were Oscar Nystrom. Denna Kwei. Marcus Oyelaran. Petra Ilves. Sam Okafor. Junie Brandt."

Something flickered across his face — not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt, but recognition, the flicker of a man realizing the ledger wasn't paper anymore, it was a person, and persons were harder to buy.

"They were a survey crew," she went on, louder, over the wind, over the groaning ice. "They mapped the upper valley in good faith for a company that had already sold their route to a claim-jumper. Your company. You didn't kill them with your own hands, so you think you didn't kill them. But I signed the contract that booked their run, Hale, and I've spent six years doing arithmetic about whose fault that makes me, and I am done. Done doing your math. The ledger goes to the press. Ilya goes free. And you—" she pushed herself up onto her knees on the swaying slab, small and absurd and utterly unafraid, "—you can stand there and be a businessman until the mountain files its own audit."

For a moment nobody moved. The wind turned over. Somewhere below them, vast and patient, the glacier began, very quietly, to let go.

* * *

It started as a sound like a rifle shot — one sharp crack from deep in the span, directly beneath Hale's feet — and then the whole slab shrugged.

Later, Ada would piece it together in fragments, because that was how the body saved things: in pieces, out of order, each one too bright. The crack. Hale's arms pinwheeling, his composure gone all at once, the businessman revealed in half a second as just a man on bad ice. The slab tilting. Ilya screaming her name. Her own hand shooting out sideways along the rope line — not toward Hale, toward Ilya, because the tilt was throwing him backward off the far lip, his boots skidding on the fresh verge, his arms wheeling exactly the way Hale's had, and some part of her brain that had never once done arithmetic correctly made its final calculation and chose.

She threw herself flat and caught his wrist.

The world went sideways. The slab tore loose beneath them with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and then it was simply gone — a whole bridge, decades old, dropping into the blue dark without ceremony, taking Hale's shout with it. Not a scream. Something shorter. A syllable. The kind of sound a man makes when the arithmetic finally finishes.

Ada slid. The verge gave way under her knees and she went down the broken slope on her belly, Ilya's wrist locked in both her hands, the rope between them snapping taut and useless at an angle, the crevasse mouth rising up to meet them with its soft blue breath. Snow sprayed. Her axe skipped away. She dug her elbows in, found a rib of old ice, wrapped herself around it, and held.

Ilya's weight swung her half off the ledge. Below him was nothing. Below him was the place where names went.

"Look at me," she gasped. "Ilya. Look at my face, not down. Look at me."

His eyes found hers and held on like a man gripping a rail in a storm, and she hauled, elbow over elbow, inch by screaming inch, her shoulders burning, the rib of ice creaking, until his free hand slapped down beside hers and then his other boot found purchase and then, all at once, gravity changed its mind and he was up, both of them collapsed together on the solid side, tangled in rope and snow and each other, breathing like bellows.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The crevasse breathed its cold breath. Far below, faintly, water moved.

"You dropped your axe," Ilya said finally, voice shredded.

"You dropped yourself."

"We're even, then."

"We are absolutely not even." She sat up, and her hands were shaking so hard she had to look at them, fascinated, like they belonged to a stranger. "I had a whole speech planned for after. About how you're not allowed to die before I finish being furious with you."

"Is the speech long?"

"Extremely."

"Good." He leaned his forehead against hers, snow melting between them, and closed his eyes. "I'll stay for all of it."

* * *

Across the gap, the world had changed shape.

Two of Hale's men stood at the broken edge, staring down into the blue. One of them — young, red-faced, the one who'd recognized Ilya at the camp and set off the alarm, the one whose conscience had visibly been at war with his paycheck ever since — had his hands on his knees and was being quietly, thoroughly sick over the edge. The other just stared at the gap where his director had been, and where the bridge had been, and where the road forward had been, and Ada watched the understanding land on him in stages: the pass was closed. The calving window was shutting. Behind them lay the crevasse field, the storm, the burned cove, and a consortium that had just lost its director to a hole in the world.

The man stared a long time. Then he shouldered his rifle — slung it, actually, slung it properly, muzzle down — and looked across the gap at Ada, and something passed between them that needed no radio and no ledger and no words at all.

"Bridge is out," he called across, flat and loud. "Nobody's crossing today. Nobody's crossing this season." He turned to the sick young one. "Come on. We're going down to the coast. We're going to find a magistrate."

"And say what?" the young one croaked.

"The truth, son. I hear it holds up better than ice."

They turned and went, two small figures shrinking into the murk, and the mountain closed behind them like a book.

Ada stayed on her knees at the edge a moment longer, looking down into the blue. She felt — she searched for the feeling and was surprised by what she found — she felt almost nothing. No triumph. No grief either. Just a strange, ringing quiet, like the silence after a bell stops, when the air is still shaped like the sound.

Somewhere down there was a man who had signed her brother's death with a fountain pen and offered her a memorial for it. The mountain had taken the pen. That was all the justice there was, and all the justice there needed to be, because the rest — the real rest, the part that mattered, the part with names in it — was wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, and it was going downstream come morning.

"Ada." Ilya's hand on her shoulder, gentle. "Come away from the edge."

"In a minute." She looked down once more, into the patient blue. "Oscar," she said aloud, to the glacier, to the dark, to the boy who used to steer their father's boat with his elbows and laugh at the wake. "Junie. Sam. Petra. Marcus. Denna." The wind took the names and carried them down into the ice, and the ice, for once, seemed to hold still and listen. "I've got them now. Go home."

She stood. Turned her back on the crevasse. Took Ilya's hand — his frostbitten, ridiculous, stubborn hand — and faced up-slope, where the pass narrowed to a bright seam of sky between walls of blue-green ice, already beginning, at the edges, to shift and whisper and close.

"How long do we have?" he asked.

She judged the light, the wind, the groan of the seracs above. "Less than yesterday. More than enough." She squeezed his fingers. "Walk fast, auditor. And don't look down."

Behind them, with a sound like a door finally shutting, the mountain began to close the road — and ahead, past the narrowing seam, the descent waited: the moraine, the river, the coast, the press, and every name in the ledger, ready at last to be read out loud.
