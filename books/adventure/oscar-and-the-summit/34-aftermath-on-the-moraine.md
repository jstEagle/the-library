## 34. Aftermath on the Moraine

The wind stopped sometime in the night, and when Ada woke the silence was so complete she could hear her own blood moving.

She lay on the moraine with her cheek against gravel and frost, one arm still curled around Ilya's pack strap, and for a long moment she didn't move at all. Moving meant deciding what came next. Not moving meant the world could stay exactly as it was: three people alive on a ridge of broken stone below a glacier that had tried to kill them and mostly failed.

Then Sefa coughed, wet and wrong, and the world started again.

Ada sat up. Her joints reported in one by one, each with its own complaint. The sun was up — actually up, a fat yellow disc sitting clean on the eastern ridgeline, the first honest sunlight she'd seen in six days. It turned the ice field above them into something almost beautiful. Almost. The collapsed bridge was visible from here, a white scar across the crevasse where Hale had gone down into the blue dark with his offers and his gun and his men. The glacier had closed over him the way water closes over a stone. No one had even screamed for long.

She looked away and found Ilya already awake, sitting propped against a boulder, watching her with the careful expression of a man who wasn't sure yet whether he'd dreamed the last two days.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm inventorying," he said. "You. Me. Sefa. One flare pistol, no flares. Half a rope. Your left glove is missing." He paused. "I'm trying to decide if we count as rich or poor."

"Rich," Ada said. "We're alive, and we've got paper the whole Consortium would burn a valley to get back." She flexed her fingers inside her remaining glove. They worked, mostly. "That makes us the wealthiest people on this mountain."

"Spoken like someone who's never been wealthy."

"Spoken like someone who has been poor enough to know the difference."

He smiled at that — small, cracked-lipped, but real — and then his eyes went past her shoulder to the boat-shaped shadow of canvas under which they'd dragged Sefa last night, and the smile went out like a lamp.

* * *

Sefa was worse than she'd let anyone believe, which surprised Ada not at all. The woman had taken a bullet through the meat of her right shoulder during the escape from the dock, and had then rowed, poled, climbed, and hauled herself up a glacier for two days on it, complaining only about the weather and once, memorably, about the quality of Consortium bullets ("Cheap jacketing. Doesn't even expand properly. No craftsmanship anymore.").

Now she lay on the spread tarp with her back against a boulder, gray around the mouth, and when Ada peeled back the improvised bandage — a shirt of Ilya's, boiled in snowmelt over their single surviving burner — the wound told its own story. The entry was ugly but survivable. The exit, low near the armpit, was angry red and weeping, and the skin around it had gone a color that skin should not go.

"When were you going to mention the fever?" Ada asked, keeping her voice level.

"What fever," Sefa said.

"That fever." Ada pressed the back of her hand to Sefa's forehead. Hot as a stove lid. "Sefa."

"It's a mountain," Sefa said. "Everything's cold except me. I'm the warmest thing here. That's just efficiency."

Ilya crouched on her other side, holding the water bottle, and said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement.

"We have to drain it," Ada said. "Or open it, anyway. Let it run. And you need to stay off the arm entirely, which means—"

"Which means nothing," Sefa said. "Which means you two carry everything and I sit in the boat and steer with my knees like I've done since before either of you could swim. Sit down, ranger. Do your cutting. And if you tell me it won't hurt, I'll take the knife away and do it myself, badly, out of spite."

Ada got out the knife. There was no alcohol left; there was barely anything left. She boiled what water she could, washed the blade twice, and made Ilya hold Sefa steady by the good shoulder while she talked — about the pass, about the descent, about the magistrate in Kell's Landing who owed Toma a favor and the press office on the coast that owed the truth a favor — an unbroken stream of ordinary words to give Sefa somewhere else to be while the blade went in.

Sefa did not cry out. What she did was grip Ilya's wrist hard enough to leave bruises and recite, through gritted teeth, the names of rivers. "Vell. Ostra. Karst proper. The Reach. The Miren cut—" each name landing like a footstep, like a way of walking herself through the pain along a road she knew by heart. When it was done and the wound ran cloudy and then clearer, she slumped back against the boulder, soaked and shaking, and said:

"The Vell. I forgot the Vell. Start over."

Ada laughed — a rough, unexpected sound that hurt her own ribs — and started over, and by the third river Sefa was asleep, truly asleep, her breathing shallow but even, her face finally slack.

* * *

They buried what they couldn't carry in the afternoon.

It was strange work, grave-digging without bodies. Hale's men had been left to the ice, and the ice kept what it took; there was nothing of the expedition's dead to bring home either, nothing of the survey team, nothing but the things they'd carried in life. But the moraine gave them a hollow between two boulders, sheltered from the wind, and Ada found herself unable to simply walk away and leave a surveyor's brass instrument case or a dead man's coat to the ravens.

So they dug. Or rather, Ada and Ilya dug, taking turns because the ground was half-frozen gravel and every inch was argued for, while Sefa slept and the sun crawled.

Into the hollow went the expedition's spare instruments — too heavy, too delicate for the climb down. A coil of rope too rotten to trust. The ransacked station's flag, folded small. A tin cup that had belonged to someone whose name none of them knew, and which Ada held for a long moment before setting down.

"We should say something," Ilya said.

"You knew them?"

"No. You didn't either."

"No." Ada looked at the little pile of surrendered things. "But they had names. Everyone in this valley had names until the Consortium decided they were line items." She thought of the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, full of columns where people used to be. "Delmar Ostrow. That was the station chief — I read it off the door plaque before they smashed it. His crew I don't know. My brother's crew I do." She swallowed. "Kesh Nystrom. And Brannic Fell, and Odile Marsh, and Tomas Reyes, and the boy — the deckhand, Petri, he was nineteen."

The wind moved through the boulders, making a sound almost like assent.

"They died on a river run I booked," Ada said quietly. "Company contract. I signed the manifest myself. I've spent four years telling myself it was an accident of weather." She set the cup down gently among the rest. "It wasn't. So this is for all of them. The named and the unnamed."

Ilya stood beside her with his head bowed, and after a moment he said, "Ansel Voss. My father. He signed off on safety reports for the same company, twenty years ago, and believed them. He's not dead, but some part of him died signing, and I've been carrying that part around ever since." He crouched and placed a flat stone on the pile, the way river people mark a grave. "And Toma's ferry. And everyone in the drowned villages. We can't carry all the names down the mountain. But we can carry the paper that says them."

They filled the hollow with stones, and Ada marked it the ranger's way — a cairn, small but true, each rock chosen and seated so it would stand through storms. When it was done it didn't look like much. A meter of stacked stones on a ridge of the world's debris. But it would be there next season, and the season after, long after Meridian's claims had rotted, and somehow that mattered more than any monument she could imagine.

"There," she said. "Now the mountain knows them too."

* * *

Ilya's hands were the next problem.

She'd noticed it the night before, in the whiteout — the way he'd fumbled the rope, the way he'd stopped using his right hand altogether somewhere on the ice field and pretended he hadn't. Now, in the honest light, there was no pretending. The last three fingers of his right hand were waxy white and hard as candle tallow, and the fingertips of his left weren't much better. Frostbite, deep, nursed silently through a day and a night because he hadn't wanted to slow them down.

"How long," Ada said, when she saw it.

"It's fine."

"Ilya."

"A day. Maybe two." He wouldn't meet her eyes. "It didn't hurt, that was how I knew it was bad, and by the time it started hurting we were on the ice and there was nothing to do anyway, so."

"So you decided to lose the fingers quietly so as not to inconvenience anyone."

"I decided to keep moving," he said, with a flash of the stubbornness she'd first seen across a desert well, a thief with a stolen ledger and nowhere to run. "You had Sefa bleeding. You had the pass closing. Someone had to not be a problem."

Ada sat down heavily on the boulder beside him. For a moment she was too tired to be angry, and what was underneath the anger frightened her more — a tenderness so large and so inconvenient that she had to look at the mountains to keep it off her face.

"You are a problem," she said finally. "You have been a problem since the well. You are the single most expensive problem of my career. And if you ever hide an injury from me again I will personally throw you in the first crevasse we find, thawed or otherwise. Do you understand?"

"Yes," he said, and then, softer: "Yes, Ada."

Warming frozen flesh was slow, painful, humbling work. She couldn't build a real fire — no wood up here, and smoke might draw the wrong eyes — so it was body heat and snowmelt and patience. She stripped off her remaining glove and took his right hand between both of hers, and the first contact made him hiss through his teeth.

"I know," she said. "I know. It gets worse before it gets better. That's how you know it's working."

"You're enjoying this."

"Immensely."

He laughed, and then gasped, and then settled into breathing the way she coached him through it — slow, in through the nose, out through the mouth — while the feeling came back into his hand in waves, each wave worse than fire and better than losing it. She worked the fingers gently, bending them, keeping the blood moving, and somewhere in the middle of it he stopped looking at his hand and started looking at her, and she pretended very hard not to notice, and neither of them said anything, because some things are easier to hold in silence than in words.

When the hand was pink and throbbing and unmistakably alive, she wrapped it in dry cloth and moved to the left one, and he said, quietly, "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank yourself for having ten stupidly stubborn fingers."

"My father used to say hands remember what the head wants to forget," Ilya said. "That's why he liked auditors' work. Everything important goes through the hands." He flexed them experimentally, wincing. "I think he'd have liked you."

"He'd have hated me. I arrested his son."

"He'd have loved you," Ilya said. "He always said the best people are the ones who arrest you for the right reasons."

Ada snorted, and busied herself with the bandage, and did not say what she wanted to say, which was that she'd stopped thinking of him as an arrest somewhere around the monsoon, maybe earlier, maybe the night they bailed water together while Sefa sang river-chants to keep time, and that the debt she'd chased onto this mountain had quietly changed currency somewhere along the way, and she no longer knew whom she owed or how much, only that she intended to pay all of it.

* * *

Evening came down gold and cold. They ate the last of the smoked fish Toma had pressed on them, divided with a fairness that had become automatic, and Ada checked Sefa's wound by lantern light and found it — mercifully, improbably — looking less angry than it had at noon. The fever still burned, but lower. The woman slept in genuine rest, one arm cradled against her chest, muttering river names in her sleep like a litany.

"She'll make it," Ilya said. It wasn't quite a question.

"She'd better. If she dies on this mountain I will never forgive her, and she knows it, and that's probably why she's fighting so hard." Ada banked the burner and sat back on her heels. Above them the glacier glowed faintly in the last light, blue-white, enormous, indifferent. Somewhere beyond it, days down, were the coast, the press, the magistrate, the end of everything. Somewhere behind them, the Consortium would already be counting what Hale's failure cost and deciding how many men to send next.

"We can't stay here past tomorrow," she said. "Sefa can't walk far, but she can't stay here either. The moraine drops to the river valley on the south side — Toma said there was an old shepherd track. Two days down to the water. Three, carrying her."

"Two," Ilya said. "I'll carry her."

"With those hands?"

"With these hands," he said, and held them up, pink and bandaged and trembling slightly, and grinned at her with such sheer mulish warmth that Ada felt something in her chest come loose that had been tight since the salt flats.

"You're impossible," she said.

"I'm an auditor," he said. "I balance things."

She laughed — really laughed, the first time since the bridge broke, since Hale fell, since the ice closed its white book — and the sound went out over the moraine and came back off the glacier smaller, and colder, and true.

And then, from down-slope, from the direction of the river valley they'd have to cross tomorrow, came a sound that stopped the laughter in her throat.

A whistle. Thin, high, deliberate. Then another, answering it, farther east.

Not birds. Birds didn't whistle in patterns. Men did — patrol men, signal men, men spreading out along a shepherd track in the last light because someone upstream had finally guessed where the survivors of the ice field would come down.

Ada put out the lantern with her bare hand and lay flat against the stones, and beside her she felt Ilya go still, and above them the glacier held its breath.

Three whistles now. Closing.
