## 35. Downriver

The whistles came no closer.

They circled, instead — the way hawks circle a thing they can see but haven't decided about. Ada lay against the cold stone and counted them out: one to the east, one below on the shepherd track, one that wandered and doubled back like a man second-guessing his orders. Three. Maybe four. Hale's men had come down off the glacier faster than she'd thought possible, which meant they'd come down angry, which meant someone had survived the ice bridge to report it.

She let herself feel exactly one second of satisfaction about that, and then put it away with the rest of the things she couldn't afford to carry.

"They'll sweep the moraine at first light," Ilya breathed against her ear. "It's what I'd do. It's what I *did* do, twice, for them."

"And then?"

"Then they widen. We're not hard to find, Ada. We're two people and a stretcher made of oars, walking downhill in a valley with one road through it."

Sefa, laid out between them under the tarp, said nothing. She'd stopped saying much two days ago, when the fever first climbed into her voice and started rearranging her sentences. But her eyes were open, fixed on the dark where the whistles had been, and her hand — the good one — lay flat on the ledger satchel as if it were a dog that might bolt.

"We go tonight," Ada said.

Ilya turned his head. In the starlight she could just make out the shape of his disbelief. "Tonight. Down the shepherd track. Past three patrols. With her." He didn't say *with her feverish and half-delirious*, because Sefa was right there, but the sentence arrived anyway, fully dressed.

"Tonight," Ada said again, "because tomorrow there'll be four patrols, and the day after six, and by then we're a story they tell new hires. The monsoon's breaking — you felt it today, the air's turning. When it breaks, the river comes up fast and every checkpoint on it gets busy checking. That's our crowd. That's our noise. We hide inside the weather."

"That's either brilliant or the stupidest thing—"

"It's both," Sefa rasped, and coughed, and the cough went on long enough that Ada pressed her palm flat against the woman's sternum until it eased. "Both," Sefa finished, thinner. "That's how the river does everything. Go."

* * *

They went.

The shepherd track was a rumor of a path, a suggestion the mountain had once made and mostly taken back. Ada led with the compass — Ilya's repaired compass now, its needle steady as a held breath — and behind her came Ilya with Sefa lashed to his back in the oar-stretcher rig they'd built on the moraine, and behind him Ada's own pack, and the satchel, strapped across his chest so that if he fell forward the ledger would land on top of him rather than under.

"Sentimental," he'd called it.

"Practical," she'd said. "You're replaceable. The paper isn't."

He'd grinned at that, which had been the point, though she hadn't told him so.

They moved through the last hours of dark the way water moves through a crack — patiently, without any wasted argument with the shape of things. Twice Ada heard voices on the wind, low and bored, men talking about food. Once a lantern swung somewhere below them, and she took Ilya's collar and pulled them all flat into a fold of rock while the light wandered past close enough to read the stitching on their sleeves, if anyone had been close enough and foolish enough to look.

Nobody looked. The world was busy ending its rainy season, and the rain's last tantrum came down around three in the morning, a final furious hour of it that soaked them to the skin and, Ada realized somewhere in the middle of it, was also the best thing that could have happened. Rain killed scent. Rain killed tracks. Rain made every patrol man alive think about his fire and his tea and nothing else.

By dawn they were below the snowline, in the green throat of the valley, and the river was already talking.

* * *

It talked all day.

They followed it downstream through country that changed by the hour — glacier-gray boulder fields giving way to pine, pine giving way to birch scrub, birch giving way to terraced slopes where somebody's ancestors had wrestled farms out of the mountainside and lost, mostly. The flood marks on the trees told the story in rings: high water, higher water, highest. Every year the monsoon came up this valley like a debt collector, and every year the valley paid.

"Familiar," Ilya said, when Ada pointed one of the marks out. His voice was rough with tiredness. Sefa rode his back with her arms looped over his shoulders, burning quietly, occasionally murmuring names Ada didn't recognize — a sister's name, mostly. *Mira. Mira.* Ada pretended not to hear, and Ilya pretended not to hear, and Sefa probably knew they were pretending, and that was its own kind of mercy.

They made camp that night in the shell of a drowned village.

Not drowned in water — drowned in time. The Consortium had done its work here years ago; Ada could read it the way she'd learned to read anything on the Reach: the empty window sockets, the doorways bricked with fieldstone in too much of a hurry, the well capped with a slab that still bore a chiseled family name some clerk had ordered ground off and someone had only partially obeyed. Half a name remained. *KELL-* something. A whole village reduced to a fragment of a word.

Ada stood at the well a long time.

"I used to think my job was protecting places like this," she said, when Ilya came to stand beside her. "Ranger. Keeper of the map. I used to walk the flats and think, nobody erases anything while I'm watching."

"You were watching the wrong thing. So was I. We watched the law. They bought the law." He touched the half-name on the well slab, lightly, the way you'd touch a scar. "Toma says the magistrate in Corren's Landing still owes him. Still remembers."

"Toma's ferry is ash and his name is on a list now. If we bring him trouble—"

"He gave us the name anyway. He said—" Ilya closed his eyes, finding it. "'Tell Magistrate Oduya that Toma Vess kept the ferry twenty-two years and never once carried a man who didn't pay his fare. And tell her the fare for this one has been paid.' Something like that. Old man poetry."

Ada almost smiled. Almost. "Old man poetry," she agreed, and went to check on Sefa.

The fever had opinions of its own now. She was shivering under the tarp despite the mild night, and when Ada pressed the back of her hand to Sefa's forehead the heat came off her like a stove door left open. The wound in her shoulder had gone from angry to quiet, which was worse; quiet wounds were the ones that got ideas.

"Sefa." Ada put water to her lips. "Sefa. Drink."

Sefa drank, mostly. Then her eyes found Ada's and focused with an effort that Ada could see cost her something. "Corren's Landing," she said. "Three days' walk if the river's kind. Four if it isn't. Don't wait for me to be well. I've been carried enough for one lifetime."

"Noted," Ada said. "Overruled."

A ghost of the old Sefa surfaced then — the laconic boat-woman who'd run rapids blind and bribed checkpoints with a straight face. "Bossy ranger."

"Thieving passenger."

"Fair." Her eyes drifted shut. "Wake me if the river gets loud. I hate missing the loud parts."

* * *

The river got very loud on the second day.

The monsoon broke the way a door breaks — all at once, with the sky tearing open along the western ridgeline and the pressure dropping out of the air like a held breath finally released. And the river, freed from weeks of rain-capture up in the high valleys, came down to meet them.

They saw it from a bend above a gorge: brown, muscular, running bank-full, carrying entire trees like matchsticks. Where three days ago there had been gravel bars and slow channels, there was now a single boiling artery.

"No boat," Ilya said flatly. "No boat in that. No boat in that even if we had a boat, which we don't."

"No boat," Ada agreed. They walked.

But the flood did one thing for them that no plan could have: it emptied the valley of everyone else. The shepherd track became theirs alone. The one road — such as it was, a mud shelf cut into the gorge wall — showed no traffic but deer and, once, a bear that regarded them from across a slide with professional indifference before ambling off. The Consortium's patrols, Ada guessed, were pinned upstream, waiting out the same flood, fuming. Rivers didn't take sides. Rivers just made everything equal.

On the third evening they came down out of the gorge and the valley opened, and there — tucked into a bend where the current went gentle and wide — was a fishing hamlet that the maps had missed and the Consortium, apparently, had too.

Six houses. Smoke from chimneys. Nets drying on racks. A dog that announced them at two hundred yards and then, having announced them, lost interest entirely.

An old woman mending a net looked up as they came down the bank, took in the stretcher, the feverish woman, the mud to their thighs, and said, "Flood refugees?"

"Something like that," Ada said.

"Huh." The old woman bit her thread. "We get those. There's soup. Don't touch the dog's bowl, he's sensitive about it."

Her name was Petra, and she asked no questions, which Ada understood by nightfall was the local currency. This stretch of the Reach had been passed over by the consortium money — too far downriver, too poor, nothing worth claiming — and so it had kept the old habits: feed strangers, ask nothing, remember everything. Over soup that tasted like onions and survival, Petra listened to Sefa's breathing from across the room and said only, "Fever's in the shoulder, is it? My mother packed wounds with honey and moldy bread. Smelled worse than the wound. Worked better than most doctors." A pause. "I have honey."

By morning Sefa's fever had broken — not gone, but broken, the way a wave breaks, spending itself. She woke clear-eyed and furious about being horizontal.

"The ledger," was the first thing she said.

"Safe," Ada said.

"The copies?"

"Safe. On my body. On his body. Split three ways with Petra's oilcloth and a lot of optimism."

"Good." Sefa tried to sit up, failed, glared at her own shoulder as if it had betrayed her personally. "Then we're ahead. For the first time since Kell's Landing, we are actually *ahead* of these people, and I intend to enjoy it for at least a full minute."

She enjoyed it for about forty seconds, and then fell asleep again, and Ada sat by her and let herself, for one minute, do the same thing — count what they had instead of what they'd lost. Two people walking. One wounded captain alive. A ledger duplicated in triplicate, hidden in oilcloth against three separate bodies. A name: Magistrate Oduya, Corren's Landing. A debt: Toma's ferry, Toma's fare, paid.

And a brother's crew, named now, written in Ada's own hand on a page she'd copied out at the mining camp and carried against her ribs ever since. *For the reading aloud,* she'd written at the top, because some documents weren't evidence. Some were eulogies waiting for an audience.

* * *

Petra's son-in-law had a boat.

"It's a terrible boat," he said cheerfully, showing it to them on the fourth morning. It was a flat-bottomed skiff, patched in four places with five different materials, and it smelled like fish and tar and honesty. "Leaks a little. Rides high when loaded light. Current's doing all the work anyway — you're not rowing to Corren's Landing, you're falling there with style."

"How much?" Ada asked.

He waved the question away. "Petra says you're flood refugees. Petra's been right about people for seventy years. Take the boat. Bring it back if the river lets you." He squinted at Sefa, propped on a fence post and refusing all help with the dignity of a cat in a bath. "You're the one who ran the Narrows? People talked about that even down here. Said a woman took a gunboat's wake through the teeth of it with a holed hull."

"People exaggerate," Sefa said.

"People say you laughed."

"I might have laughed."

He grinned and shook her good hand like she was a returning general.

They launched at midday, riding the flood's long brown shoulder downriver, and it was — Ada had to admit it, somewhere over the first hour — the easiest travel they'd done since the salt flats. The current carried them past terraces and ruins and stands of birch going gold early in the cold snap; Ilya steered with a plank while Ada bailed the leaks on a schedule ("a little," the son-in-law had said, turned out to mean "constantly"), and Sefa lay in the bow issuing navigation advice that was fifty percent useful and fifty percent pure nostalgia.

"There," she'd say, pointing at a bend. "There's where I ran cargo in 'nineteen. Before. When the papers were honest." Or: "That village had a ferry. Gone now. Everything's gone now except us, apparently, and the river." And once, quietly, looking at the water: "Mira would have liked this stretch. She liked water you could trust."

Nobody answered that. Nobody needed to. The river answered for them, steady and enormous, carrying them down.

They camped on gravel bars at night, three points of a triangle around a small fire, and on the second night Ilya sat down next to Ada on watch and didn't say anything for a long time, which by now was its own language between them.

"Corren's Landing," he said eventually. "When we give Oduya the ledger — she'll want testimony. Names. Dates. She'll want *us*, on the record, in a courtroom the Consortium's lawyers will turn inside out."

"I know."

"You understand what that means. You were the law, Ada. You know what testifying costs a person."

Ada fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "I spent a long time thinking the law was a thing outside me. A badge. A contract. A company that paid me to find a thief." She glanced at him. "Turns out the law is just what you do after you know better. That's the whole thing. That's all it's ever been."

Ilya was quiet a moment longer. Then he reached over and, with great ceremony, adjusted the compass hanging at her neck so it hung straight.

"Steady needle," he said.

"Steady needle," she agreed.

* * *

Corren's Landing announced itself by smell — woodsmoke, tar, frying fish, and under it all the particular green rot of a river town in flood season — and then by sound: gulls, hammering, a bell buoy clanging off the breakwater. They came around the last bend on the third afternoon and there it was, a real town, docks and warehouses and cranes, the biggest collection of human beings Ada had seen since the desert.

It should have felt like safety. Instead it felt like walking into a lit room after weeks in the dark — every eye potentially on them, every face potentially bought.

"Rules," Ada said quietly, as the current swept them toward the public landing. "We're flood refugees looking for work. We don't know each other's business. The satchel stays wrapped. We find Magistrate Oduya's office before we find food, sleep, or conversation."

"I'm wounded," Sefa said. "I'm allowed to be visible. Wounded boat captains attract sympathy, not suspicion."

"You're also on a list."

"I'm *flattered* to be on a list. Do you know how long I worked before anyone noticed me?"

They tied up at the public landing among a dozen other flood boats, and stepped onto solid ground that swayed underfoot like a deck, and stood for a moment in the noise and the ordinary life of it — a boy selling roasted chestnuts, a woman arguing about rope prices, two dockhands laughing about something — and Ada felt the strangest sensation, that the world down here had simply continued, unbothered, while up in the high valley it had been remade.

Then she saw the notice.

It was nailed to the customs post beside the landing, fresh paper, fresh ink, and it stopped her mid-stride the way a tripwire stops a runner. A reward notice. Two faces rendered in indifferent engraving: a man with hollow cheeks, a woman with a ranger's jaw. Below them, in official type:

*MERIDIAN CONSORTIUM RECOVERY DIVISION — INFORMATION LEADING TO RECOVERY OF STOLEN PROPERTY AND APPREHENSION OF FUGITIVES. SUBSTANTIAL COMPENSATION OFFERED.*

Below that, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*REPORT ALL SIGHTINGS TO THE HARBORMASTER.*

The harbormaster's office stood thirty feet away. Its windows faced the landing. Its windows faced *them*.

Ilya had gone very still beside her. Sefa, leaning on his arm, read the notice twice and then did the most Sefa thing imaginable: she snorted.

"They got your jaw wrong," she muttered. "Makes you look trustworthy."

"Harbormaster," Ada breathed. "Oduya's office is on the harbor square. Which means the harbormaster—"

"—is either hers or theirs," Ilya finished. "And we won't know which until we're inside."

Down the dock, the harbormaster's door opened. A clerk stepped out, cupping a match to light a pipe, and stood there surveying the landing — the boats, the flood crowd, the strangers — with the mild, cataloguing gaze of a man whose job was noticing things.

His gaze crossed the three of them. Paused. Moved on.

Paused again.

And came back.
