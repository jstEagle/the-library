## 36. Publication

The press office on Coil Street smelled of ink and old rain, which was to say it smelled like every honest building in Saltmere after the monsoon broke. Ada stood in the doorway a long moment before going in, the duplicate ledger wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, heavier than any bundle of paper had a right to be.

Inside, a woman with ink-stained fingers and spectacles pushed up into gray hair looked up from a composing table littered with slugs of type.

"We're closed for repairs," the woman said. "Roof leaks. Everything leaks."

"I'm looking for Magistrate Oren Vell."

"You've found what's left of her." The woman—Vell herself, apparently, in shirtsleeves and an apron that had once been white—wiped her hands and came around the table. "And you'd be the ranger who's been asking after me from every river town between here and the Karst. Ada Nystrom. You have a talent for making yourself known."

"I had help." Ada set the oilcloth bundle on the composing table, right on top of a half-set column about flood damage. "Toma sent word ahead. He said you owed him."

"Twenty-two years ago he pulled my son out of the Kess current when I couldn't." Vell looked at the bundle without touching it. "He didn't say what it was. He only said: *when she comes, print everything.*"

"That's the instruction, yes."

"Everything," Vell repeated. She unwrapped one corner of the oilcloth, saw the first page of the ledger—the neat auditor's columns, Ilya Voss's own hand, the accident reports cross-referenced against payout schedules—and went very still. "How many copies?"

"Three. One here. One hidden with dockworkers at Kell's Landing. One—" Ada hesitated. "One goes back to the valley families, eventually. That one isn't mine to place yet."

Vell pulled her spectacles down onto her nose and began to read. Ada watched her read for what felt like an hour. The magistrate's face did not change much; she had clearly been a woman who expected the worst of powerful men for a long time and had stopped being surprised by it somewhere around the second decade. But her hands shook slightly as she turned each page, and at one point she took off her spectacles entirely, pressed the heels of her palms against her eyes, and said, quietly, "My brother crewed a supply run out of Halvatt. Nineteen years back. They called it weather."

Ada said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't in the ledger already.

When Vell finished, she straightened, re-inked her fingers, and asked the only question that mattered. "Can you prove it holds? In front of a tribunal, can you stand behind these pages?"

"I signed some of the contracts they used," Ada said. "I carried warrants for them. I'll testify to all of it."

Vell nodded slowly, then smiled—a dry, tired, genuinely dangerous smile. "Then we'd best start setting type. The Consortium owns two of the three papers on this coast. Fortunately for us, they don't own this one, because nobody's wanted to own a leaky building on Coil Street since before the war." She cracked her knuckles. "Print runs by Friday. You'd better sit down, Ranger. This is going to be loud."

* * *

It was louder than anyone predicted.

The first edition ran under a headline Vell had fought her own typesetters to keep simple: **THE LEDGER: WHO PAID FOR THE ACCIDENTS IN THE KARST REACH.** Beneath it, three columns of names—crews, pilots, survey teams, ferry families—each name paired with a date, a payout figure, and the initials of the officer who authorized it. No editorializing. Vell insisted. "Let the arithmetic do the shouting," she said, and the arithmetic shouted.

By the second day, the story had jumped the coast. A paper in Corvane picked it up, then one across the strait, then the wire services, who loved nothing so much as a document with numbers in it. By the fourth day there were magistrates in three provinces issuing subpoenas, and by the fifth, the Meridian Consortium's stock—which Ada did not understand and refused to pretend to understand—had done something dramatic that made Sefa laugh for the first time since the dock.

She laughed from a chair, though. That was the part Ada kept coming back to. Sefa sat propped in a rented room above a chandlery near the harbor, bandaged from shoulder to elbow where the guard's round had torn through, refusing painkillers on the grounds that they made her stupid, and reading every edition cover to cover with a pencil in her teeth.

"They got Director Hale's deputy in Corvane," she reported on the sixth morning, tapping the page. "Arrested trying to board a freighter with two trunks of documents he was planning to *lose at sea*, which is the oldest trick in the book and he still botched it. And look—" she turned the paper around— "the harbormaster at Kell's Landing. The one who waved us through after taking Sefa's bribe money and then sold our route to the gunboats anyway. Arrested Tuesday."

"He'll name names," Ada said.

"He'll sing like a kettle." Sefa tossed the paper aside and studied Ada over the rim of her tea. "You sleep at all?"

"Some."

"Liar. Your brother's grave is upcountry, isn't it. Halvatt way."

Ada didn't answer, which was an answer.

Sefa nodded slowly, unsurprised. "Go when the tribunal's done. Not before. Finish what you started here first—dead brothers are patient, but tribunals aren't." She said it gruffly, the way she said everything, but her good hand found Ada's wrist and squeezed once, briefly, before retreating to its teacup. It was the most affection Sefa permitted herself in daylight hours, and Ada had learned to treasure it accordingly.

* * *

The tribunal convened in the assize hall in Saltmere, a cavernous room built by people who believed justice required bad acoustics and uncomfortable benches. It was packed anyway. Word had traveled, and half the coast seemed to have come to watch a ranger turn on her employers.

Ada testified on the third day.

She wore her ranger's uniform—not out of loyalty, but because Vell had advised it, shrewdly: *"Let them see exactly what they're losing."* She walked the tribunal through it in order, voice level, the way she'd track a trail: the bounty contract she'd signed, the delivery order for Ilya Voss, the agents at the desert well who had been instructed to bring him in "processing-ready," the checkpoint at Kell's Landing operating on Consortium pay, the ransacked survey station, the smashed radio, the survivor delirious with a phrase no one should ever have to hear—*the valley that eats names*—and finally the ledger itself, entered into evidence in triplicate.

"When did you first understand what you were carrying?" one of the tribunal examiners asked. He was a thin man with a kind face, and he asked gently, which nearly undid her more than hostility would have.

Ada considered lying, or at least softening it. Then she thought of Ilya in the bilge of a leaking boat, saying *I signed off on the accidents, Ada. Every one. I told myself it was bookkeeping* — and decided he deserved better than her comfort.

"The moment I read the payout schedule," she said. "But if you're asking when I should have understood—earlier. Much earlier. My brother died on a run I booked through a Consortium broker. I was told it was weather. I wanted it to be weather, because weather doesn't require anything of me. So I didn't ask. That's how it works. That's how it worked on me, and I'm supposed to be the one who notices things."

The hall was very quiet.

"And your badge?" the examiner asked. "You surrendered it this morning, I'm told. Voluntarily."

Ada reached into her pocket and set the brass badge on the witness rail, where it caught the light and threw a small crooked gleam across the judge's bench. She'd polished it the night before, which surprised her; she'd meant to let it go tarnished, but her hands had done it without consulting her, some old habit of care asserting itself one last time.

"I took an oath to the law," she said. "For eleven years I believed the law and the Consortium were the same animal. They paid me, they gave me warrants, they told me who was a criminal. When I finally saw they weren't the same animal—that the law was something older and quieter that they'd been feeding on—I had a choice. I could keep the badge and stay useful to them, or I could put it down and be useful to the truth." She looked at the examiners, then past them, at the packed benches, at Sefa sitting stiff-backed with her bandaged arm cradled like a sleeping child, at Ilya in the third row wearing borrowed clothes and the expression of a man watching his own confession get vindicated in public. "I put it down. I'd do it again. Some debts you pay to companies. Some you pay to the dead. Only one of those receipts is worth keeping."

She stepped down. The hall stayed quiet a beat too long—and then someone in the back, a woman with a river accent Ada half-recognized from the drowned villages, began to clap, and the sound rolled forward through the benches like water finding its level.

* * *

The arrests rippled upstream for weeks, exactly as promised. Deputies first, then officers, then the comfortable middle layer of magistrates and harbormasters and brokers whose signatures appeared in the margins of the ledger like a trail of oily footprints. Two of the agents from the desert well were identified by Ada from photographs and taken without incident; the third had fled upriver and was rumored to be hiding in the Karst Reach itself, which Ada found fitting and others found ominous.

Director Hale himself remained unaccounted for. Officially, he had resigned his directorship citing health. Unofficially, per Vell's sources, he had boarded a private vessel the night the first edition hit the streets and vanished into the shipping lanes. Warrants went out in four jurisdictions. His assets were frozen. His name began appearing in headlines next to words like *fugitive* and *at large.*

"He's gone," Ilya said one evening, on the chandlery steps, watching the harbor lights come on. "Hale doesn't run. Running is admitting. He's *repositioning*. He'll be back under another name, another consortium, same ledgers, new ink. Men like him don't end. They rebrand."

"Then we'll be here when he does," Ada said.

Ilya glanced at her. "We?"

Ada looked out at the water rather than at him. "Old habits. I say 'we' now. It snuck up on me."

He smiled—not the quick deflector's smile she'd first met at the desert well, but the slower one she'd earned somewhere between the bilge and the ice field. He didn't push it further. Neither of them did. There would be time, or there wouldn't; they had both learned the difference between promises and direction, and direction was enough for now.

* * *

On the last day of the tribunal, the verdicts came down like a long-awaited storm finally breaking clean: convictions, forfeitures, the Consortium's coastal charters revoked pending dissolution. Vell read the summary aloud from the steps of the assize hall to a crowd that had gathered without being summoned, and when she finished, she folded her papers and added, off the record, in her dry dangerous voice:

"For the record, none of this happened because of a tribunal. It happened because a thief stole a book, a ranger chose wrong and then chose right, a boat captain ran rapids she should have portaged, and an old ferryman kept a debt for twenty-two years. Institutions printed the results. People made them possible. Remember the difference."

The crowd cheered. Sefa, beside Ada on the steps, snorted. "She's going to be insufferable now."

"She's earned it," Ada said.

Afterward, in the emptying hall, a clerk approached Ada with a small tray and an apologetic expression. "Ranger Nystrom. Your badge, if you want it back. The tribunal ruled your surrender was... symbolic. Your commission technically stands. There are those who'd like to reinstate you. Quietly. With back pay."

Ada looked at the badge on the tray—her badge, eleven years of it, salt-scoured and dented, the brass worn bright where her thumb had worried it through a thousand cold watches. She thought about how it had felt in her pocket during the testimony, lighter than she'd expected, and how it had felt the night she'd first pinned it on, heavier than she'd expected, and how both of those weights had been lies of a kind.

"No, thank you," she said. "It did its work. Let it retire with honors."

The clerk blinked, shrugged, and carried the tray away, and Ada felt the loss and the relief arrive together, indistinguishable, like two currents meeting.

Outside, evening was coming down gold over the harbor. Ilya waited at the bottom of the steps with his hands in his pockets and a question he was visibly deciding whether to ask.

"Say it," Ada said.

"The original ledger," he said. "The valley copy. The families upriver—the ones whose villages got erased—they should hear their own names read back to them. From the pages. By someone who..." He stopped, restarted. "Sefa can't make the climb, not yet. And it should be you. You're the one who carried it down. Carrying it back up—that closes the circle."

Ada looked north, toward the high country, where the Karst Reach disappeared into blue distance and beyond it the glacier sat in its white silence, calving season turning over in its slow mind. She thought of Toma's burned ferry and his uncomplaining shrug. Of the drowned villages. Of a manifest she hadn't yet been able to read aloud.

"There's someone I have to see first," she said. "Halvatt. My brother's grave. There's a list of names I owe him, and I've been practicing."

Ilya nodded. "I'll come as far as you want me."

"That," Ada said, and found she was smiling, "might be quite far."

They walked down the steps together into the warm noise of the harbor, and behind them the assize hall bell rang the hour—just a bell, just a sound, but for the first time in a long while, it sounded to Ada like something beginning rather than something ending.
