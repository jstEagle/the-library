## 37. What the Debt Was For

The cemetery sat on the shoulder of a hill above the river town of Marrow's Bend, where the water ran slow and brown and honest after the monsoon had wrung itself out. Ada Nystrom climbed the path in boots that still smelled faintly of glacier silt, carrying her hat in one hand and a folded paper in the other, and she thought—not for the first time—that cemeteries were the only places where the dead got to keep the high ground.

Oscar's grave was the fourth from the fence, under a crooked ironwood tree that dropped its leaves early every year as if it couldn't be bothered with the pretense of autumn. The marker was simple: his name, two dates, and the word *RIVERMAN* carved by a stonemason who had clearly known him, because the letters leaned slightly downstream.

Ada hadn't been here since before the Karst Reach. She'd meant to come a dozen times. She'd stood at the edge of the path once, two years ago, gotten as far as the gate, and turned around. You couldn't say sorry to a stone. You couldn't explain to dirt. And she had not yet been able to say what needed saying without her voice doing something humiliating.

Today her voice would do whatever it wanted. Today she had names to read.

Ilya waited by the gate, hands in his pockets, radiating the particular awkwardness of a man who has been told he may come along and is now deeply unsure whether coming along was wise. He'd shaved. He'd put on a shirt with buttons. He looked like a man attending a funeral for someone he'd never met, which, in a way, he was.

"You can wait at the gate," Ada said. "This part's mine."

"I know." He didn't move. "But Sefa told me if I let you go up there alone, she'd have my other arm."

"How is she?"

"Complaining. Which the doctor says is the best possible sign." He smiled a little. "She's already reorganized the infirmary. Two nurses have resigned."

"Good," Ada said, and meant it. Sefa Voss, shot through the shoulder holding a dock against Hale's men, alive and tyrannical—there were worse outcomes. There had almost been so many worse outcomes.

She walked the last stretch alone.

The grass around Oscar's grave needed cutting. She knelt and pulled weeds instead, because her hands wanted work, and talked while she worked, the way you talk to family when nobody living is listening.

"I brought it," she said. "Took me long enough."

She smoothed the paper on her knee. It was a copy—the original was with the magistrate's clerk now, entered into evidence, Exhibit A in a trial that was going to eat the Meridian Consortium from the feet up—but she'd copied it herself, in her own hand, because some documents deserved to pass through fingers that cared about them.

It was a manifest. Dated four years back. The crew list of the *Marrow Belle*, a supply run upriver into the Reach, nine men and one woman, contracted through a shell company called Bluewater Freight, insured by an underwriter who had paid out promptly and asked no questions.

Her brother's last run. The one she'd booked him on. The one the ledger said had never been meant to come home.

*Accident,* the Consortium's books called it. *Rockfall, seasonal channel shift, regrettable.* Eleven thousand crowns, settled quietly. One line item among forty-three.

Ada had spent four years believing the river took him. Rivers did that. She'd made her peace with rivers, or thought she had, the way you make peace with weather. Then a thief with careful eyes opened a ledger in a leaking boat and showed her that the river had been given help.

She cleared her throat. The ironwood dropped a leaf onto the marker, obligingly, like punctuation.

"They caught them," she said. "Hale's people. Most of them. The ones who signed the papers are singing so loud the courthouse echoes. Turns out everybody's brave when the alternative is prison." She pulled another weed. "Your boat's name is in the testimony. The *Marrow Belle*. They wrote it down wrong twice—called it the *Belle Marrow*, then the *Marrow Bell*—and both times somebody corrected them. A clerk corrected them. Some kid who'll never know why it mattered." Her voice wobbled; she let it. "It mattered."

She unfolded the paper fully and held it flat against her thigh.

"There's a thing I should've done four years ago," she said. "At the funeral. Everybody stood up and said words and I couldn't get past 'he was my brother.' So I'm going to do it properly now. Late. But properly."

She took a breath the way Sefa had taught her to take a breath before running rapids—all the way down, hold nothing back.

"The crew of the *Marrow Belle*," she read, "as contracted on the fourteenth of Thaw-month, Bluewater Freight, outbound from Kell's Landing."

And she said the names.

"Oskar Nystrom. Pilot and captain."

His own name sounded different out loud here, in this place, spoken not as grief but as record. She kept going.

"Denna Marsh. Engineer."

Denna, who had taught Ada to field-strip an engine at fourteen and sworn at her affectionately the whole time.

"Piet Olem. Deckhand."

Piet, who cheated at cards so badly everyone let him win anyway.

"Sefa Voss. Navigator."

Ada stopped. Read the line again. Looked up at the ironwood tree as if it might explain.

Sefa Voss. The name she'd been saying for weeks, the laconic captain of a rotting riverboat who avoided checkpoints and owed no explanations. The woman whose sister, she'd said, was one of the Consortium's accidents—and Ada had assumed a sister lost in some other year, some other valley.

Sefa hadn't lost a sister on the *Marrow Belle*. Sefa had *been* on the *Marrow Belle*. Navigator. Contracted, same date, same run. And somewhere between that manifest and the wreck, she had walked away—or crawled away, or been dragged—and lived, and carried it, and never once, in all those weeks of bailing water and running narrows and getting shot on a dock, had she said *your brother died beside me.*

Ada sat down hard in the grass.

"Well," she said to the headstone, when she could speak again. "That explains a lot about her."

It explained the look on Sefa's face the first time Ada mentioned her surname aboard the boat—a flicker, gone in a second, filed away. It explained why Sefa had taken them upriver at all, why she'd named her price as the ledger's destruction and then, later, quietly agreed to copies instead. It explained the night in the bilge during the monsoon when Sefa had listened to Ada talk about her brother and said only, "He sounds like he was good at his work," in a voice like a door closing.

She had been standing next to the answer the entire time, and she'd let Ada find it on her own, at her own pace, in her own place. That was Sefa all over. Grace delivered like cargo: no fuss, no invoice, just set down gently where it needed to be.

Ada laughed, wetly, and wiped her face with her wrist.

"You'd have liked her," she told Oscar. "You'd have liked her too much. She'd have run your whole crew inside a month."

She finished the manifest. "Corim Tass. Rigger. Ansel Boyce. Cook. Marta Ilves. Deckhand. Jorun and Hallam Pike. Twins, deckhands—they came as a pair, wouldn't sign otherwise. Old Teodor Brandt. Pilot's mate." Ten names. Ten lives, each reduced by the Consortium to a settlement figure, each restored now to a full syllable of sound in the open air.

She said them again, slower. All ten. Because the ledger had counted them as losses, and losses were numbers, and numbers were how companies apologized. But names were how people mourned, and Ada Nystrom was done letting companies do the counting.

When she finished the second reading, the hill was very quiet. Somewhere below, the river went on doing what rivers do, indifferent and essential, and Ada found she didn't mind it anymore. The river hadn't killed her brother. Men had. Men were being dealt with.

"I booked you that run," she said. "My signature got you on that boat. I've been paying that debt to the wrong people for four years—worked their contracts, wore their badge, hunted their thieves. I thought if I was useful enough, it would balance out." She shook her head. "It doesn't work like that. Debts to the dead don't clear through the company office. They clear here." She touched the warm stone. "So. Consider it paid. Not because I say so. Because they're arrested, and the ledger's public, and every one of those ten names got read into the court record yesterday by a prosecutor who pronounced Piet Olem's name right on the first try."

She stood, brushed off her knees, and set the copy of the manifest at the base of the marker, weighted with a river stone she'd carried down from the pass—from the summit, from the place where the glacier opened its road once a year for whoever came next. It seemed right. Oscar had always liked high water country.

Footsteps on the path. Ilya, having judged the moment survivable, came up carrying two cups of something hot from the tea stall by the gate. He handed her one without asking what it was. It was terrible. It was perfect.

"You found it," he said quietly, nodding at the manifest. "The connection."

"She was his navigator." Ada stared down the hill toward the town, toward the infirmary where a one-armed tyrant was terrorizing nurses. "Four years. She knew me before I ever knew her."

"That's not quite true," Ilya said. "She told me once—back in Toma's cove, when you were sleeping—that she'd been looking for you. Since the accident. She said she wanted to see what kind of person the pilot's sister turned out to be." He paused. "She said she wasn't disappointed. She said it annoyed her."

Ada laughed again, and it hurt, and it healed, both at once. "Of course she did."

They stood together while the light went long and gold across the graves. Downriver, a boat horn sounded—some freighter hauling goods on a river that belonged to itself again, its checkpoints abandoned, its tollbooths empty, its magistrates suddenly, urgently honest.

"You know what happens next," Ilya said. Not a question. He'd seen the letter too—the one that had arrived that morning on official Consortium remnant stationery, thick cream paper, addressed to *Ranger A. Nystrom (ret.)* with an offer inside so generous it was almost funny. Reinstatement. Back pay. A settlement. And one small condition, printed in the fine print where cowards live: discretion regarding ongoing matters.

"I know what happens next," Ada said.

"It's a lot of money."

"It's hush money wearing a nice coat." She drained the terrible tea. "They think I did all this to clear my name. They think everybody's for sale because everybody they ever met was for sale." She smiled, and it was the smile Ilya had first seen across a desert well, aimed down a rifle barrel, except warm now, aimed at the horizon instead. "I'm going to go tell them no. Publicly. In writing. With witnesses."

"Want company?"

"Always." She picked up her hat, settled it on her head, and gave the leaning letters one last look—*RIVERMAN*, leaning downstream, going with the current at last. "Come on, Voss. Let's go disappoint some very rich people."

They walked down together, two figures on a hill path, and behind them the ironwood finally, grudgingly, began to turn.
