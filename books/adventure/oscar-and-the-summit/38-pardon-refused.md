## 38. Pardon, Refused

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Ada would later decide was the Consortium's first mistake. Tuesdays were market days in the lower valley, and market days meant witnesses.

She was haggling over the price of dried apricots—losing, cheerfully—when a boy in a pressed gray uniform threaded through the crowd and held out an envelope with both hands, as if it might bite him. It probably had been trained to. The paper was heavy cream stock, the kind that cost more per sheet than Ada made in a week of honest work, and it bore the Meridian Consortium's seal: a mountain peak inside a circle, stamped in silver foil that caught the sun and threw it back like a knife.

"Miss Nystrom?" the boy said. His voice cracked on the second syllable. He couldn't have been more than sixteen, and he looked like he'd rather be delivering anything else—to anyone else—in any other valley on earth.

"That depends who's asking."

"Director—" He stopped, swallowed, started again. "The Consortium requests your presence. There's a matter of... settlement."

Ada took the envelope. The apricot seller, a wiry grandmother named Petra who had been holding firm at four coppers a bag out of pure principle, leaned across her stall to peer at the seal.

"Meridian," Petra said, loud enough for three neighboring stalls to hear. "They still exist? I thought the magistrates ate them."

"The magistrates are *chewing*," Ada said, and the market laughed, and the boy in gray went pale in a way that suggested he'd been raised on stories about what happened to Consortium couriers in valleys that had read the ledger.

She opened the envelope right there, because she'd learned something in the last year about doing things in front of witnesses. Inside was a single sheet, typed, unsigned by hand but signed in spirit by every dead man whose name had ever appeared in a Meridian margin note:

*The Consortium, in its reorganized capacity, extends formal apologies for past administrative irregularities. Ms. Ada Nystrom is invited to discuss immediate reinstatement to her former position with full back pay, restoration of rank, and a discretionary settlement in recognition of her discretion regarding matters now under legal review.*

*Discretion regarding matters now under legal review.*

Ada read it twice. Then she folded it along its crease, very carefully, the way you fold something you intend to keep as evidence, and put it in her coat pocket next to the compass Ilya had fixed—the needle steady now, pointing true north with the smug confidence of a machine that had finally been forgiven.

"Bad news?" Petra asked.

"Worst kind." Ada handed over five coppers for the apricots. "It's good news wearing bad news's coat."

* * *

Ilya found her that evening on the porch of the ranger station, which was no longer officially a ranger station because Ada had surrendered her badge, but which everyone in the valley called the ranger station anyway, the way people call a river by its old name long after some official has renamed it after a duke.

He came up the path carrying two cups of tea and the particular walk of a man who has heard news and is pretending he hasn't so he can watch how you carry it.

"You've heard," Ada said.

"I've heard." He handed her a cup and sat on the step beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was how they talked about large things. Small distances for large things; they'd learned that in the bilge of Sefa's boat, in Toma's cove, on the moraine where they'd buried what couldn't be carried. "Back pay. Rank. A settlement for your discretion."

"My discretion." She turned the phrase over like a stone with something alive under it. "You know what my discretion is worth to them? Everything. If I take their money, then every arrest that comes out of the ledger becomes a grudge instead of justice. Every magistrate who rules against them gets to hear, someday, that the star witness was bought. My silence would be the last accident they purchase."

"I know." Ilya sipped his tea. He had been the Consortium's auditor once; he knew precisely what a line item was worth, and he had spent months teaching himself to hate his own fluency in that arithmetic. "I did the numbers before I walked up the path. They're offering you maybe two years of wages, all told. What your testimony is worth to them, in avoided judgments alone—"

"Don't."

"—is forty times that," he finished anyway, because he was Ilya, and honesty was the wound he kept picking at, the penance he performed daily. "Which means they think you can be bought cheap. Which means they still don't understand you at all."

"They understand money," Ada said. "It's the only language they ever learned. So we'll answer in it."

* * *

The reorganized Consortium sent a representative this time—not a boy but a woman named Corvel, silver-haired, immaculate, with the patient smile of someone who had dissolved companies before breakfast and attended funerals before lunch. She arrived in the valley on a Thursday, in a hired coach, and requested a public meeting, because the new Meridian was very concerned with transparency. The old Meridian had been concerned with it too, in the way a thief is concerned with locked doors.

They met in the valley hall, and half the valley came, because there was nothing else happening and because everyone wanted to see the face of the thing that had eaten names off maps. Petra sat in the front row with her arms crossed. Toma would have come but his knees had opinions about stairs now, so he sent his ferry pole instead—a symbolic gesture he refused to explain, and nobody asked him to.

Corvel stood at one end of the hall with her documents arranged in a fan. Ada stood at the other end with her hands in her pockets, wearing the same coat she'd worn upriver, patched at the elbow where the ice field had taken its tax.

"Ms. Nystrom." Corvel's voice was warm as banked coals. "On behalf of the reorganized Meridian Consortium, I want to express our sincere regret for the irregularities of the previous administration."

"Say the word," Ada said.

A pause. A flicker. "I'm sorry?"

"'Irregularities.' Say the actual word. You mean *murders*. Your predecessor's administration purchased them the way it purchased timber rights—one signature at a time, entered neatly in a ledger. My brother was an irregularity. Sefa's sister was an irregularity. Twenty people in a high valley twenty years ago were irregularities. I'll accept your regret when it uses the right noun."

Something moved behind Corvel's eyes—the brief recalculation of a professional encountering an amateur who refuses to play professionally. She adjusted her fan of papers.

"Ms. Nystrom. The Consortium offers reinstatement to your former post. Full back pay. And a discretionary settlement—an acknowledgment of the hardships you endured during your service."

"My service." Ada laughed, and it wasn't a warm sound, and the hall was quiet enough that it carried to the back wall. "Let me tell you about my service. I tracked a man across the salt flats to deliver him to people who would have killed him, because a contract told me to. I watched those people put a boot on his throat. And I stayed, because the contract said alive, and I mistook the letter of a thing for its soul. That was my service. I'm not asking for it back."

"The settlement, then—"

"No."

"Ms. Nystrom, the amount is substantial—"

"*No.*" She said it plainly this time, without heat, and somehow that was worse for Corvel than the laugh had been. Ada pulled the cream-colored letter from her pocket and held it up. "This offer, and every offer like it, is declined. Publicly. Permanently. And here's why, so there's no confusion in whatever office drafts the next version."

She unfolded the letter and read from it, her voice flat and clear, giving each word its weight.

"'Discretion regarding matters now under legal review.' You wrote it down. You put it in writing, on Consortium stock, with the seal attached. You offered to pay me for my silence while your officers are under indictment. Do you know what that makes this letter?"

Corvel's smile had gone the way of snow in the calving season.

"It makes it evidence," Ada said. "Exhibit whatever-number-we're-on. I'm not selling my discretion. I'm entering it into the record."

* * *

The hall erupted—not angrily, not all at once, but the way a river goes when the ice lets go. Petra was on her feet shouting something about apricots and justice in the same breath. Someone started clapping and couldn't stop. In the third row, a young clerk from the magistrate's office was already copying down the exact wording of the letter, tongue between his teeth, because he understood what he was looking at: not a refusal, but a confession the Consortium had mailed to its own execution.

Corvel gathered her papers. To her credit—and Ada granted her the credit grudgingly, later, over tea—she didn't bluster. She stood in the noise and looked at Ada across the hall, and for one unguarded moment her expression was almost human: tired, and old, and faintly envious.

"You could have been comfortable," Corvel said. Not accusing. Almost wondering.

"I was comfortable once," Ada said. "It nearly killed a lot of people I love. I'm trying uncomfortable for a while. So far the company's better."

Corvel left on the afternoon coach. The letter stayed behind, in the custody of the magistrate's clerk, who handled it like a relic and washed his hands afterward, ceremonially, to the delight of the market children.

* * *

That night, Ada and Ilya walked up to the ridge above the station, where the valley spread out below them lamplit and small and stubbornly itself. The monsoon was long gone; the stars had that washed, hard-edged look they only got after a season of rain.

"They'll try again," Ilya said. "Different wording. Maybe through intermediaries. Maybe they'll stop offering you money and start offering you absolution—that's more expensive and people fall for it more often."

"Let them draft it." Ada turned the compass over in her pocket. The needle swung and settled, swung and settled, always finding its way home. "Every offer they make in writing is another nail. They can't help themselves. Money is the only sentence they know, and they keep writing it down for me."

"And when they run out of sentences?"

Ada looked down at the valley—at Petra's darkened stall, at Toma's window with its lamp still burning, at the river running silver under the moon toward the coast where the presses waited and the trials were beginning and the names were being read aloud, one by one, back into the world.

"Then they'll have to learn a new language," she said. "And I hear the lessons are brutal."

Ilya laughed—a real one, startled out of him—and put his arm around her shoulders, and for a while neither of them said anything, because some things are settled better in silence.

But down in the valley, a lamp was still burning in the magistrate's clerk's office, and a list was being drawn up—witnesses to summon, depositions to take—and at the top of the list, in careful handwriting, was a name that made Ada smile when she heard about it the next morning:

*Ilya Voss, auditor, defector, witness for the valley families.*

His turn to testify. His turn to say the word *murders* into a record that would never let it go. And somewhere upriver, in a high valley that ate names and was learning, slowly, painfully, to give them back, the original ledger was traveling from family to family—being read, being wept over, being remembered.

The road to the summit would open again soon. Calving season was coming.

But first, there was testimony to give.
