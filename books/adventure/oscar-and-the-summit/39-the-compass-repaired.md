## 39. The Compass Repaired

The monsoon broke on a Tuesday, which Ada only knew because Sefa told her, and Sefa only knew because the dockmaster's calendar had washed up against her hull and she'd decided, in her laconic way, that the river owed her a date.

"Tuesday," she said, standing at the rail of the mended boat with her arm still bound to her chest, looking out at a sky scrubbed clean for the first time in weeks. "Never trusted them myself."

Ada was sitting on the dock with the broken compass open in her lap like a patient she'd finally agreed to examine. It had been broken since before the salt flats—since before Ilya, since before the valley that ate names—and she had carried it anyway, the way you carry a dead man's watch. The needle floated free in its housing, spinning whenever it pleased, pointing sometimes north, sometimes at nothing, once memorably at a goat.

"You know," said Ilya, dropping down beside her with two cups of tea he'd charmed out of the dock canteen, "most people would have replaced it by now."

"Most people don't have my luck with replacements." She took the tea. It was terrible. She drank it anyway.

He didn't laugh right away, but when he did it was quiet, and it settled between them the way warm things settle into cold hands. Three weeks since the ice. His frostbitten fingers were healing crooked—the little finger of his left hand would never quite straighten again—but they worked. He'd spent the morning helping the dockworkers copy manifests, and now he sat with his shoulder just touching hers, not leaning, not pulling away, occupying exactly the space a person occupies when they've stopped pretending they don't want to be there.

Below them, the river ran high and brown with snowmelt, carrying the last of the calving season's ice downstream in slow white rafts. Somewhere upriver, magistrates who owed Toma their careers were reading documents that had been photographed by lamplight by people with trembling hands. Somewhere upstream of that, men in good coats were being woken by telegrams. Ada could feel it all moving beneath the surface of the ordinary morning, the way you can feel current under calm water.

"The hearing's Thursday," she said.

"I heard." He turned his cup slowly. "You'll do fine. You're terrifying under oath. I've seen it."

"That was a bluff at Kell's Landing."

"It worked, didn't it?"

"It worked because Sefa bribed a man while I was doing it."

"And yet," Ilya said, "history will remember you as the terrifying one. That's how history works. It remembers whoever was talking."

Ada looked at him. He was thinner than when she'd cornered him at the well—a thief then, or so she'd believed; an auditor, actually; a defector, actually; a man who had signed his name to other people's deaths and carried it the way she carried her brother's manifest, folded small and read nightly. They had that in common now, among other things. Among quite a number of other things, some of which had happened in Toma's cove during a storm, and none of which either of them had yet found words large enough for.

"When are you leaving?" she asked, because asking directly was the only kind of courage she'd ever been any good at.

Ilya set down his tea. "Tomorrow," he said. "Early. Before the river traffic picks up. Toma's taking me as far as the drowned villages, and after that—" He shrugged. "After that there are forty-one families in the Reach who were told their people died in accidents. The original ledger belongs to them. Copies went to the press. The truth goes home."

"Forty-one families," Ada repeated. "And you're going to knock on forty-one doors."

"I'm going to try." His voice did something complicated. "Some of them will want to hit me. Some of them should. A few of them—" He stopped, started again. "A few of them have been waiting twenty years for someone to say their father's name out loud without the word 'accident' attached. I can't give them anything else. But I can give them that."

Ada nodded slowly. She knew about names. She'd read thirty-two of them aloud at her brother's grave three days ago, kneeling in wet grass while the monsoon tried to drown the words and refused to succeed. Her voice had held until the last name, and then it hadn't, and that had been right too.

"I could come with you," she said, and then immediately: "No. No, I can't. The hearing. And the valley needs—" She gestured vaguely at herself, at the badge-shaped absence where her ranger's star used to be, at everything. "There's work here. Toma says the valley's going to need a ranger who isn't owned by anyone. Apparently I'm the only applicant."

"Terrifying under oath," Ilya agreed solemnly. "Strong résumé."

She laughed despite herself, and he smiled, and then the smile faded into something more careful, and they both looked at the river for a while, because some silences are easier side by side than face to face.

* * *

He fixed the compass that evening.

She hadn't asked him to. She'd left it open on the table in the room over the dock canteen where they'd both been sleeping—separately, mostly, in the way of people conserving a certain kind of fire—and when she came back from the magistrate's office with her deposition notes, she found him hunched over it under the lamp with a jeweler's loupe he'd borrowed from somewhere, his crooked fingers working with a delicacy that seemed impossible for a man who'd spent a decade falsifying shipping manifests.

"Don't move," he said, without looking up. "There's a screw the size of an eyelash involved and I've already dropped it twice."

"What are you doing?"

"Committing crimes against horology." He adjusted something infinitesimal. "Your pivot bearing is corroded. Salt got in—salt flats, obviously—and the needle's been floating in its own private ocean this whole time. That's why it pointed at the goat."

"The goat was real," Ada said. "The goat was going somewhere."

"Everything's going somewhere. That doesn't make it north." He held the compass up to the lamp, squinted, made another adjustment so small she couldn't see it happen. "Hold still. This is the part where I find out if I'm a genius or a vandal."

Ada held still. Outside, the river murmured its endless commentary, and somewhere below them Sefa was arguing with the dockmaster about mooring fees with the serene confidence of a woman who intended to win.

"There," Ilya said softly.

He turned the compass in his hand, and the needle swung—not wildly, not drunkenly, but with a smooth, decisive sweep, like a dog that has finally been let off the leash—and settled. North. True, steady, unbothered north.

Ada stared at it. Something in her chest did a thing she wasn't prepared to name in front of witnesses.

"How long has it been since it worked?" Ilya asked quietly.

"Four years." She took it from him. The needle held. "It broke the week after Torben's run. I never—" She stopped. Started over. "I kept meaning to fix it. Then I kept meaning to mean to fix it. Then it seemed like it would be dishonest somehow. Like fixing it would be saying I'd forgiven myself, and I hadn't earned that yet."

Ilya was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way it always was when they got near the center of things.

"Do you think you've earned it now?"

"No," Ada said honestly. "But I think maybe that's not what earning is for. Maybe you don't fix the compass because you deserve to know where north is. Maybe you fix it because other people need you to know, and your feelings about yourself aren't actually load-bearing."

Ilya blinked. Then he laughed—a real one, surprised out of him—and rubbed his eyes with the back of his wrist. "God. Four years of carrying a broken compass out of guilt, and you arrive at healthy emotional processing through *cartography*."

"It's how I process everything. Ask Sefa. I once resolved a grief spiral by reorganizing a tackle box."

"I believe it completely." He reached across the table and closed his hand gently over hers and the compass together. "Keep it working," he said. "That's all. Keep it working, and point it at true things."

* * *

They said goodbye at dawn, because Ilya Voss was constitutionally incapable of dramatic farewells and had announced, the night before, that he was leaving "early, before anyone gets sentimental," which everyone understood to mean *before I get sentimental*, which everyone further understood to mean *before I change my mind*.

Toma's ferry was already loaded—supplies for the drowned villages, oilcloth-wrapped bundles, and one leather ledger, the original, heavy as sin and heavier as memory. Toma himself stood at the tiller with the patience of driftwood, nodding once at Ada in greeting, which from Toma was a eulogy-length speech.

Sefa came down the dock with her arm still slung, moving carefully, and pressed something into Ilya's hand. Whatever it was, he looked at it for a long moment and then put it away without showing anyone, and his jaw did the thing jaws do when a man refuses to cry in front of colleagues.

"River owes you," Sefa said.

"River doesn't owe anybody," Ilya said. "That's the whole problem with rivers."

"Then I owe you. Which is worse. Don't make me say it twice."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

Ada stood a few feet back with her hands in her pockets, holding the moment at arm's length the way you hold a hot pan. They had agreed—explicitly, verbally, like adults—that there would be no promises. Promises required futures you could schedule, and his future was forty-one doors upriver and hers was a hearing and a badge-less valley and a summit pass she intended to walk every calving season until her knees filed a complaint. Promises would have been a kind of lie, and they had spent the whole journey learning, painfully, expensively, what lies cost.

So instead of promises, Ilya walked over to her and stood close, and the dawn came up gray-gold behind him on the water.

"True north," he said.

"Every time," she said.

He kissed her then—not doomed-feeling at all, actually, though neither of them said so, because some things are truer unsaid—and when he pulled back his eyes were bright and steady and entirely unashamed.

"Come find me," he said. "When the valley's settled. When the families have their names back. Come upriver in spring, when the pass opens. I'll be the one at the first door, probably getting hit."

"Noted," Ada managed. Her voice was annoyingly rough. "I'll bring the compass. You can check my bearings."

"Wouldn't trust anyone else's."

He stepped onto the ferry. Toma pushed off. The current took them—first slowly, then with the river's old indifferent efficiency—and Ilya raised one crooked-fingered hand, and Ada raised hers, and then the bend of the river took them both away, and she stood on the dock until the sound of the engine faded into the sound of the water, which is to say, into no sound at all.

"Well," said Sefa beside her, having materialized with the specific gravity of a woman who has decided someone needs company and will deny it forever. "That was excruciating."

"Thank you."

"You cried."

"I did not."

"Your face did. Your face is a bad liar. Good thing you never went into crime." Sefa handed her a cup of tea. It was terrible. It was, Ada thought, watching the empty river hold the whole sunrise, exactly what she needed.

* * *

Thursday, she testified. It went the way Ilya had predicted: she was terrifying, and history would remember her for the talking, and by Friday the telegrams had become warrants, and by the following week the Karst Reach had a name on maps again for the first time in twenty years.

But that was later. On Tuesday morning—the real Tuesday, the one Sefa's calendar had promised—Ada climbed alone to the ridge above the dock with the repaired compass in her hand and let the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, testing it against landmarks she knew: the smokehouse, the bend, the white scar of the glacier pass far upstream, open now, waiting, the road it opened each year already filling in her mind with next spring's possibilities.

North, said the needle. Steady. Unbothered.

Ada Nystrom stood in the wind with her debt discharged and her record ruined and her heart inconveniently full, and began, very quietly, to plan a trip upriver.
