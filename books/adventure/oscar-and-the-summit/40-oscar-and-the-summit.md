## 40. Oscar and the Summit

The valley had a name again.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, Ada thought—how long a name could stay missing, and how ordinary it felt when it came back. Not triumphant. Not loud. Just a name on a sign at the head of the ferry landing, painted by Toma's grandnephew in careful block letters that dripped a little at the bottom of the S: KARST REACH — UPPER VALLEY, POP. 214 AND COUNTING.

The "and counting" had been Ilya's suggestion. He'd stood there with the paintbrush behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil and said, "Every valley should be allowed to grow," and Toma had grunted the way he grunted at everything that was either very stupid or very true, and Ada had never learned to tell the difference.

Three years, now. Three springs since the ledger went to the coastal press, since the arrests rippled upstream like a stone dropped in still water, since Ada stood in a magistrate's courtroom with her badge on the table in front of her and said, "I'm not here to testify for the Consortium. I'm here to testify against them, and I'm also here to tell you I worked for them, and you should weigh everything I say accordingly."

The magistrate—an old woman with ink-stained fingers and a face like a closed ledger herself—had looked at Ada for a long moment and said, "Noted. Continue."

Ada had continued. For eleven days.

* * *

She climbed to the summit pass on the same morning every year now, the way some people went to church. It wasn't a rule anyone had written down. It was just that the calving season came, and the glacier opened its road with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and Ada had learned that if you wanted to understand a thing, you had to stand next to it while it happened.

She started before dawn, as she always did, from the cabin she'd built herself—badly, at first, with a roof that leaked in exactly the way the old boat had leaked, so that the first monsoon she spent under it felt less like shelter and more like a reunion. She'd gotten better. The roof didn't leak now. She almost missed it.

The trail went up through the birches first, then the pines, then the scrappy nothing-zone above the treeline where the wind did all the talking. She carried the repaired compass out of habit, though she no longer needed it to find north. North was easy now. North was home.

The debt was paid. That was the phrase she'd carried for so long—*the debt, the debt*—a stone in her chest she'd stopped noticing the way you stop noticing a bad tooth. Her brother's crew, their names read aloud at the graveside in chapter after chapter of her own voice, hoarse by the end. The manifest recovered, the accidents renamed murders, the company's remnant dissolved into smaller, quieter companies that she watched the way a ranger watches smoke on the horizon: not panicked, just attentive.

And the pardon they'd offered—reinstatement, back pay, silence money in a plain envelope—she'd refused on the courthouse steps with a reporter's camera running, and the footage had gone further upriver than any boat she'd ever piloted. *Ranger refuses blood money* was the headline. She'd kept the clipping. Not out of pride. Out of proof. Some mornings you needed proof that you'd been the person you intended to be.

The sun came up over the eastern shoulder of the pass as she crested the last rise, and there it was: the glacier, blue-white and ancient and groaning, the whole valley spread below it like a hand opened palm-up.

She sat down on her rock—the rock, hers by three years of squatter's rights—and waited for the ice to speak.

It didn't keep her waiting long.

* * *

The calving, when it came, was never the same twice. Some years it was a groan and a shrug, a shed shoulder of ice slipping into the meltwater with a sound like a door closing gently. Other years it was thunder. This year, she could tell from the deep tremor in the rock under her boots, it was going to be thunder.

"Go on, then," she said to the glacier, conversationally. She talked to it every year. It had never once answered, which she respected. "Nobody's rushing you."

But the glacier was rushing, in its way, because glaciers are always rushing, just at a speed that makes patience look like sloth. The face of it shuddered. A crack ran up the ice like a sentence being written too fast, and then—there it was, the thunder, rolling down the valley and bouncing off the far walls and coming back changed, and a house-sized piece of the glacier's shoulder let go and fell into the lake with a splash that reached her face as a fine cold mist a full minute later.

The pass was open. The road through, the ice-choked channel that would widen day by day until it closed again, was open for whoever came next.

That was the part she'd come to love best, in these three years. Not the thunder, though the thunder was good. Not the view, though the view was the kind that made you forgive the world its worst behavior. The part she loved was the *whoever came next*. Because they came. Every year, they came.

The first season after the trials, it had been surveyors—honest ones, this time, with instruments they calibrated in daylight and maps they drew with the drowned villages put back in, names restored like bones set straight. The second season, families. Farmers, mostly, and a schoolteacher, and a woman who opened a bakery at the ferry landing whose cinnamon rolls had done more for regional morale than any magistrate's ruling. This year, the advance word said, there was a crew coming up to rebuild the old footbridge at the Narrows, and a botanist studying what grew in soil that had been lied about for twenty years, and—

"Ada!"

She turned. Ilya was coming up the trail behind her, breathing hard, which he always pretended not to do. He carried a thermos in one hand and a rolled paper under his arm, and he was wearing the ridiculous hat Sefa had given him before she shipped out south for the winter—wide-brimmed, sun-faded, with a feather in it that Ilya insisted was ironic and that everyone else insisted was not.

"You started without me," he said, arriving at the rock.

"You were late."

"I was saying goodbye to Toma. He made me promise to bring him back a stone from the pass. Every year the same stone. Every year I bring it and every year he says, *that's not from the pass.*"

"Is it from the pass?"

"It's from the trailhead. He knows it's from the trailhead. It's our ritual." Ilya sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and unscrewed the thermos, and poured two cups of coffee that was terrible in the specific way she had come to prefer over all other coffee. "How's the glacier?"

"Talkative."

"They usually are, this time of year." He handed her a cup and unrolled the paper across both their knees, weighting the corners with stones. It was a map. Of course it was a map. Ilya Voss, former auditor, defector, thief of nothing and signer of everything, had discovered in his middle years that what he loved most in the world was drawing maps of places that had been erased, and he was very good at it, and the valley had adopted his maps the way a body adopts a transfusion.

"The footbridge site's here," he said, tapping. "And I've marked the old village plots under the new ones—dotted lines, so nobody builds on a grave without knowing it. And look." His finger moved to the summit pass, to a small careful notation in his small careful handwriting. "I've named the lake."

Ada looked. The lake below the glacier, the one that had swallowed the calved ice and would swallow a hundred more before the season turned, had a name in ink where for three years it had had only a blank.

*Oscar.*

She was quiet for a moment.

"You named the lake after a dog," she said.

"I named the lake after *the* dog," Ilya corrected. "Oscar. Toma's dog. The one who found the survey team's survivor in the reeds after the station was ransacked, and who once ate an entire Consortium manifest, which I maintain was an act of archival justice, and who is, at this moment, asleep in front of Toma's stove with his legs going like he's running, because even in his dreams he's working." He shrugged. "Valleys should be allowed to grow. Lakes too. And nobody was using that name."

"Oscar," Ada said, testing it. Below them, the lake lay blue and new under the morning light, icebergs drifting in it like slow white boats, and it did look, now that she looked, like an Oscar. She couldn't have said why. Some names just fit a thing, the way a key fits a lock, and you knew it the moment the tumblers fell. "It suits him."

"I thought so."

"He's going to be insufferable. The dog. He'll find out."

"Dogs always find out."

They drank their terrible coffee and watched the glacier work. Another calving came, smaller, a growl and a slide. Somewhere below, faint on the wind, a boat horn sounded from the ferry landing—the first arrivals of the season, early, eager. Ada could picture them without looking: the bakery woman waving, the schoolteacher's kids running the dock planks, Toma on his porch pretending not to watch everything, which was how he watched everything.

"Letter came," Ilya said, after a while. "From Sefa."

Ada turned. "Is she—"

"She's fine. She's in the southern ports. She says the scar itches when it rains, which she claims is a lie she tells to get out of hauling rope, and which her doctor claims is true." He smiled. "She says she'll come up for the anniversary. She says—" he paused, and read from memory, because of course he had it memorized, "—*tell Ada the river remembers who ran it honest, and tell her also that her coffee was always an abomination and I expect it has not improved.*"

"She's right on both counts," Ada said, and felt the warmth of it go all the way down, past the coffee, past the cold morning air, into the place where the stone in her chest used to sit. The place was not empty, exactly. It never would be. But it was a garden now instead of a grave, and things grew there, and some of them had Sefa's laugh in them and some of them had her brother's name and some of them, on good mornings, had the sound of ice calving into a lake that was named after a good dog.

* * *

They stayed on the summit until the sun was high, because there was no reason not to. That was the luxury the valley had given her, the one she still caught herself marveling at: time that wasn't owed to anyone. She'd spent so many years with her hours mortgaged—to the Consortium, to the debt, to the version of herself that thought duty meant obedience. Now her hours were hers, and she spent them like a woman who had learned the hard way what they cost.

A party of hikers came up the trail around midday—new arrivals, the botanist's team, maps out and faces full of that hungry first-week wonder. Ada watched them from the rock and saw one of them, a young man with sunburned ears, stop at the pass's crest and just stand there, staring down the long blue length of the valley, his mouth slightly open, his whole body forgetting what it was doing.

She remembered that feeling. Standing at the top of a road you'd bled to keep open, and seeing how far it went.

"First time?" she called over.

The young man startled, then grinned, embarrassed. "Is it that obvious?"

"Everybody stands like that, the first time. Even the ones who pretend they don't."

"Is it always like this? The pass opening?"

"Every year. Different thunder, same door." She nodded at the map tube under his arm. "You'll want Ilya's map, not the government one. The government one still has a blank spot where the upper village should be. Bureaucracy heals slower than glaciers."

The young man laughed and thanked her and moved on down the trail with his team, and Ada watched them go and felt the thing she felt every year at this moment, the thing that had taken her two full seasons to name.

It wasn't pride, exactly, though there was some. It was more like the feeling of a hand unclenching. For years her job had been to close things—close cases, close borders, close her heart around the debt and carry it. And now her work was to keep a road open. To stand at the top of it every calving season and watch the ice let go and the water run and the people come, and to know—really know, in the scarred and mended place where her certainty lived—that when the next thief came up this river, or the next runaway, or the next ranger with a broken compass and a debt she couldn't name, they would find a valley that had learned better than to eat names.

"Ranger Nystrom." Ilya said it formally, raising his terrible coffee in a toast. "Of the free valley."

"Ranger Voss," she returned, "of the maps."

"I'm not a ranger."

"You draw the boundaries. That's most of the job. The rest is coffee and stubbornness, and you're two for three."

They clinked cups. Below them, the lake named Oscar caught the light and threw it back, and the glacier groaned its long slow sentence, and the road stood open.

Ada finished her coffee, and shouldered her pack, and started down the trail toward home—past the birches, past the pines, past the sign with the dripping S, toward the smoke and the cinnamon and the dog asleep by the stove, toward all of it, the whole loud ordinary miracle of a valley that had gotten its name back and meant to keep it.

The needle, had she checked, would have pointed north.

She didn't need to check.
