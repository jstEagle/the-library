## 1. Miles, Not Years

The dredge coughed up its hundredth load of river muck and Kestrel Whitlock read the bottom of it like a letter.

Silt first — gray-brown, fine as flour, the signature of slow water. Then a seam of clay the color of old brick, which meant the channel had cut through an old bank somewhere upstream. And threaded through it all, glinting when the sun caught it right: flecks of mica that didn't belong in this stretch at all. Mica came from granite country. Granite was three days' travel north.

Someone upstream had moved earth recently, and a lot of it.

Kestrel tipped the pan, let the slurry slide back into the gray water, and wiped their hands on their thighs out of habit rather than need. The overalls had given up on cleanliness sometime around the second week of the contract.

"You reading tea leaves down there?" Halvard called from the dredge platform, his voice half-swallowed by the engine's idle. He was a barrel of a man with a beard full of sawdust and no memory of how it got there.

"River's telling me stories," Kestrel said.

"River's telling you we're behind schedule."

Kestrel climbed the ladder to the platform, boots ringing on wet steel. The dredge *Marrow* sat anchored mid-channel in Port Sallow, a town that had earned its name honestly: a smear of mudflats and tin roofs where the Sallow River widened into something too lazy to clean itself. The contract was simple — deepen the shipping lane before the autumn barges came down heavy with ore. Simple work. Honest pay. The kind of work that asked nothing of a person except their hands and their silence.

Kestrel was good at both.

They took the ledger from Halvard and made notes in the margin — *clay seam, mica trace, check north bend* — because even a dredging contract deserved attention, and because reading riverbeds was the one language Kestrel had ever learned fluently. Not letters. Not the ones that came in envelopes, anyway.

Seven years since the last envelope. Seven years since the last anything.

"Boat coming in," Halvard said, nodding downstream.

Kestrel looked up. A survey launch was picking its way through the moored barges, riding low, paint scabbed, a radio mast bent like a finger someone had stepped on. It flew no colors. Working boats rarely did. But there was something in the way it moved — hurried, unsteady, a boat whose pilot wasn't looking at the water but at some point far ahead of it — that made Kestrel set down the ledger.

The launch tied off at the mud-stairs below the chandlery. A woman climbed out alone: sixty if she was a day, silver hair cropped short, wearing a field coat that had been patched so many times the original fabric was more memory than material. She carried a leather case under one arm like it was a sleeping child.

She scanned the waterfront once, quick and professional, and her eyes stopped on the *Marrow*. Stopped on Kestrel.

Kestrel had learned long ago what it meant when strangers looked at them that way — not at the face but at the build, the stance, the way they stood with weight forward like they were always about to move. It meant someone had described them to someone else. It meant they'd been talked about by people who shouldn't know they existed.

The woman crossed the mud-stairs without slipping once. That told Kestrel something too.

"You're the one they call when a job needs doing quietly," she said. No greeting. Her voice had the clipped economy of someone who'd spent decades shouting over engines or into wind.

"I'm the one they call when a job needs doing," Kestrel said. "Quiet costs extra."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. "Dr. Imelda Voss-Rennick. River Authority, Survey Division." She didn't offer a hand; her grip was occupied with the case. "I'm told you know the upper Sallow better than our own charts do."

"The charts are wrong above Grayfall Narrows. Everyone knows that."

"Which is why I'm here." She opened the case. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a radio handset — scorched, its casing split along one side. Beside it, a logbook swollen with water, its pages fused into a single gray block. "Three weeks ago, an expedition went upriver past Grayfall. Six people. Archaeological salvage — a temple site, pre-dynastic, possibly flooded for centuries. They had a relay buoy every ten miles. The last transmission came from mile forty-one." She tapped the dead handset. "Then nothing."

"Dredging season," Kestrel said. "Storms take buoys."

"Not three in a row. Not with a backup array." Voss-Rennick closed the case with a click that sounded final. "The Authority wants a recovery team. Officially, it's a welfare check. Unofficially—" she looked out at the brown water, and for a moment the clipped voice frayed at the edges, "—unofficially, I want to know what happened to my people."

Kestrel should have said no. The arithmetic was simple: the dredge contract ran another month, the pay was steady, and steady was the whole religion of Kestrel Whitlock's life. Steady work. Steady meals. Steady distance between themselves and everything that had ever hurt.

"How much?" they asked instead.

Voss-Rennick named a figure. It was triple the dredge money. It was enough to buy out the last of the debt on the *Marrow*, enough to put a year between Kestrel and the next time they'd have to take a job they hated.

Enough to be free, maybe, if freedom could be bought in installments.

"When do you leave?"

"Tomorrow's tide, if you'll come."

Kestrel looked at the dead radio, the drowned logbook, the woman who held them like relics. Somewhere upriver, six people had gone silent, and the river had swallowed the sound the way it swallowed everything — patiently, completely, without malice.

"I'll want my own gear," Kestrel said. "And I pick the route. We go up the east channel, not the main stem. Main stem floods this time of year."

"Done."

They shook on it. Voss-Rennick's hand was dry and strong, calloused in the pattern of someone who still worked sites herself despite the title.

* * *

That night, in the rented room above the chandlery, Kestrel packed by lamplight: line, hooks, the good knife, waterproof wraps for the flares. The routine of it steadied them. Packing was a language too — each item a word, each bundle a sentence that said *I will be ready for whatever the water does.*

It was only when they lifted the mattress to stow the spare coil that the paper slid out.

Kestrel froze.

They knew that paper. Cream-colored, heavy stock, watermarked with a crest from a university two thousand miles south. They knew the handwriting on the address panel before they read a single word — the sharp downstrokes, the way the *W* leaned like it was hurrying somewhere else.

*To Kestrel Whitlock, c/o the Chandlery, Port Sallow.*

Postmarked eleven years ago. Never delivered — the chandlery had changed hands, the letter lost in a drawer until the new owner found it during inventory and, being honest in the lazy way of small towns, simply left it where Kestrel would eventually sleep on top of it. Which is exactly what Kestrel had done for four years. Slept on top of it. Kept it beneath them like a stone in a riverbed, letting the current of ordinary days wear it smooth.

They had never opened it.

Kestrel sat on the bunk's edge now, the letter in both hands, the lamp hissing. Eleven years. Seven since the last contact of any kind — a wire transfer, no note, the account number written in that same leaning hand. Before that, letters that came thinner each year, then stopped. *I'm sorry. The work matters. You'd understand if you'd see what we're finding.* Kestrel had stopped writing back somewhere around the fifteenth birthday, when it became clear that Dr. Mara Whitlock — field archaeologist, celebrated excavator, the person whose name appeared in journals Kestrel couldn't afford to buy — had chosen miles over years, distance over daughter.

Over child. Kestrel had never much cared which word fit.

They turned the letter over. Turned it back. The wax seal had cracked years ago, a hairline fracture running through the crest like a fault line through bedrock.

They did not open it. They slid it back beneath the mattress, face-down, and blew out the lamp, and lay in the dark listening to the river slap against the pilings.

Tomorrow they'd be paid triple rate to go find six missing strangers.

That was all this was. Pay. A job. A route they knew better than anyone alive.

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of rain that hadn't arrived yet. Kestrel loaded the *Marrow*'s tender with gear while Voss-Rennick's remaining crew assembled on the dock — a young cartographer with ink-stained fingers, a medic who kept checking a kit that was already checked, two riggers who looked like brothers and argued like them.

Six missing. Seven going after them, counting Kestrel. Counting Voss-Rennick, who stood at the gangway with the leather case and a face arranged into official calm.

"One thing before we cast off," Voss-Rennick said, and handed Kestrel a folded sheet. "The team roster. You'll be responsible for knowing faces, names, specialties. If we find anyone—"

Kestrel unfolded the list.

*Expedition personnel, Upper Sallow Temple Survey:*

*Tomas Reyes-Ibarra, field director.*

*Petra Osei, geotechnical.*

*Dov Anselm, dive lead.*

*Halla Brandt, conservator.*

*Jun Okafor, instruments.*

*Mara Whitlock, senior archaeologist.*

The dock tilted. Or the world did. Kestrel's hand stayed perfectly steady, which took everything they had, and their voice came out level, which took more.

"Mara Whitlock," they read aloud. Flat. Professional. "Any relation to the excavator? The one who wrote the Grayfall papers?"

Voss-Rennick watched them for a moment too long. "Her sister, I believe. Why?"

"No reason." Kestrel folded the roster along its crease, precise as a surgeon. "Names are names. Faces are what matter. Let's cast off."

The tender pulled away from the dock and the town began to shrink behind them — the tin roofs, the mudflats, the dredge *Marrow* sitting fat and patient in the channel like a promise Kestrel was breaking. Ahead, the river narrowed and darkened, and the fog was already standing up out of the water in columns, the way it did above Grayfall, the way it always did, like the narrows were breathing.

Eleven years of silence, and now Kestrel was being paid to go find the source of it.

*Just pay,* they told themselves, watching the fog lean closer across the water. *Just a job. Just miles.*

But their hand, resting on the gunwale, had drifted without permission to the inside pocket of their coat — where, that morning, without deciding to, without admitting anything, they had slipped the cream-colored letter.

Sealed still. Unread. Riding upriver toward its author like an answer traveling backward through time.

The fog swallowed the last of Port Sallow, and the river took them north.
