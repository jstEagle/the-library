## 2. The Silent Reach

The fog came up the river like a held breath.

Kestrel stood at the bow of the *Marrow's Luck* and watched it swallow the banks whole — first the reed beds, then the leaning alders, then the low brown hills that had been their only landmarks since dawn. By midmorning there was nothing left of the world but the boat, the water sliding black and slow beneath her, and the sound of the engine working too hard for the current it was pushing against.

"Visibility's down to a boat-length," Bo called from the wheelhouse, one hand on the throttle, the other shielding his eyes as if squinting would help. It didn't. Nothing helped. "Maybe less."

"Then we go slow," Kestrel said.

"We were supposed to reach the survey camp by noon."

"The camp's been silent for nine days. It'll still be silent at one." Kestrel kept their voice flat, unhurried, the way they'd learned to talk to nervous clients. "Rivers don't reward rushing. You taught me that yourself, first day you hired me."

Bo grunted, unconvinced, but eased back on the throttle anyway. The engine note dropped to a mutter. Somewhere behind them, Marisol was coiling line in the well deck with the particular violence of someone who hated fog, hated rivers, and had signed onto this job anyway because the pay was good and the story would be better.

Kestrel hadn't told her the real reason they'd taken it.

They unfolded the expedition manifest again — third time that morning, fourth, they'd stopped counting honestly — and read the names by rote. Dr. Emeric Voss-Adler, lead archaeologist. Two graduate students, Okafor and Lindqvist. Site technician, cook, boat pilot. And at the bottom of the personnel list, in the cramped bureaucratic type that made even a name look like an inventory item:

*Dr. S. Halloran — ceramicist & stratigraphy consultant.*

Not *mother*. The contract didn't say mother. Contracts never did. But Kestrel had known the handwriting on the field authorization before they'd known the name — that leftward slant, the way she crossed her sevens like she was striking them through, killing them, one stroke at a time.

Seven years since the last letter. Seven years since Kestrel had stopped waiting for the next one and started reading riverbeds instead — gravel bars and cutbanks, the grammar of silt and stone, a language that never lied about where it had been or what it intended to do next. Rivers told you everything if you knew how to listen. People signed contracts and vanished upriver and left nothing but a crossed-out seven.

"You're doing the thing," Marisol said, appearing at their shoulder without sound, which was a skill Kestrel had stopped trying to explain.

"What thing."

"The folding-and-unfolding thing." She nodded at the manifest. "Paper doesn't change, Kess. Reading it harder won't make it say something new."

"It's not paper I'm worried about." Kestrel folded it away. "It's what took the radio offline."

Ahead, the narrows closed around them like a throat.

* * *

The Silent Reach, the charts called it — six miles of channel where the river pinched between shale bluffs and the current ran strange, doubling back on itself in eddies that could spin a loaded barge sideways. Old pilots swore the fog pooled here year-round, that the gorge breathed it out like something sleeping. Kestrel had run worse water. They'd also never run it looking for a missing crew, and the difference sat in their chest like swallowed ballast.

The radio had been crackling since dawn. Not words, exactly. Fragments. Every few minutes the set in the wheelhouse would hiss awake, catch half a syllable, and die again — a rhythm almost like breathing, almost like someone keying the transmitter from the far end and getting nothing through.

"—copy—"

Static.

"—down, repeat, all—"

Static.

"—can't hold the—" A burst of tone, high and warbling, then silence deeper than before.

Bo killed the engine entirely and let the boat drift, and in the sudden quiet the fog seemed to press closer, beading on the rail, running off the canopy in fat drops. "That's a live signal," he said quietly. "Somebody's transmitting. Or somebody's transmitter is."

"There's a difference?"

"A transmitter can keep sending after nobody's left to send." Bo said it gently, the way you tell a client their cargo's gone. "Dead man's switch. Timer. Faulty relay. Nine days is a long time for gear to sit powered in river damp."

Kestrel looked at the radio. Looked at the fog. Did the arithmetic they'd been refusing to do since the port town: nine days silent, six miles of narrows, a camp somewhere past the bend where the bluff shelved down to a gravel terrace — the only place flat enough to land a survey crew, which meant the only place anything could have happened to them.

"Marisol," they said. "Get the boarding lines ready."

* * *

They heard the camp before they saw it.

A sound carried strangely across still water — thin, rhythmic, mechanical. A ticking. As the *Marrow's Luck* ghosted around the final bend and the fog thinned just enough to show the gravel terrace, Kestrel picked it out: the soft metronome tick of an instrument still running.

The camp sat exactly where the survey plan said it would. Two wall tents. A drying rack hung with labeled ceramic sherds, each bagged and tagged. A coring rig collapsed but not stowed. A cook fire burned down to gray ash, ringed with stones, a kettle sitting beside it — and here Kestrel's stomach tightened, because the kettle was full. Rain-full, algae-green at the rim, days and days full. Someone had filled it, set it to boil, and never come back for the tea.

They grounded the bow and stepped onto the terrace, and the ticking grew louder until Kestrel found its source inside the larger tent: a sediment logger bolted to a folding table, stylus tracing its slow line across a paper drum, recording layer after layer of a riverbed nobody was there to read. Beside it, a second instrument — a magnetometer, its needle swinging in small agitated arcs, still hunting for metal in the earth below.

Mid-meal. That was the phrase that came, unbidden, and refused to leave. The camp was abandoned mid-meal. Three tin plates on the mess table, two of them scraped clean, one still holding rice gone to concrete. A card game abandoned — Marisol found the hand later, fanned face-down where someone had dropped it. A boot by the tent door, its mate nowhere. A jacket over a chair.

And no people. No signs of struggle either, and that was somehow worse. No blood, no scuffle-marks in the mud, no dragged tracks. Just absence, total and tidy, as if the river had reached up and lifted seven adults off the gravel between one bite of rice and the next.

"They walked out," Bo said, but he didn't believe it; Kestrel could hear the doubt under the words. "Or they were moved out."

"Moved out how? There's one boat landing and it's empty. Their skiff's still tied." Kestrel crouched at the mud edge of the terrace and read it the way they read any bank — grain by grain, mark by mark. The ground told its usual honest story: footprints, many, all heading one direction. Inland. Toward the bluff.

Toward the wreck site.

"Here." Marisol's voice came tight from the smaller tent. She was standing over the field desk, and on it lay a ledger open to the last written page. Kestrel crossed to her and read over her shoulder, and the world went narrow and ringing.

The handwriting was unmistakable. Leftward slant. Crossed sevens struck through like kills.

*Day 41. Confirmed the siphon schedule — six usable tides per season, next window opens in eleven days. Voss-Adler wants to push regardless. I've told him what's down there and what it will cost us if we're wrong about who else knows. He says patrons don't wait. I said patrons don't dive.*

*If anyone reads this: we are going down ahead of schedule because we have no choice. Do not follow the main channel. Whatever happens, the keystone stays buried until the right hands come for it.*

The entry stopped there. No date closed out. No signature.

Below it, in different ink, in a hand Kestrel didn't know — Voss-Adler's, presumably — a single line:

*She's taken the notes. Find her.*

Kestrel stood very still. The magnetometer ticked. The logger traced its patient line. Rain dripped off the tent fly in a rhythm almost like counting.

"She sealed it," Marisol said softly. "Your mother sealed the wreck. Those collapse-lines we briefed on — the charges in the survey photos — those were hers."

"I know."

"Do you?" Marisol turned, and her dark eyes were kind in a way Kestrel hadn't braced for. "Because you've been reading that manifest like it owes you money, and now you're standing in her tent, and you haven't said her name once. So I'm asking. Is she yours?"

The question hung in the wet canvas air. Kestrel could lie — had built a career on the clean professional lie of *it's just pay* — but the tent smelled of camphor and pencil shavings and a particular brand of ink that seven years hadn't scrubbed from memory, and lying suddenly felt like drowning.

"She's mine," Kestrel said. "Was. Is. I don't know what tense applies to a woman who leaves."

Marisol nodded slowly, accepting it the way she accepted everything — as load-bearing fact. "Then let's find out which tense we're in."

Bo appeared in the tent flap, rain-slicked, grim. "Found the skiff's fuel cans. Empty. All three, drained into jerry jugs and carried off. And Kess — tracks on the far side of the terrace. Boat tracks. Something beached here after they left, heavy, deep draft, and launched again headed downstream."

Downstream. Toward them. Toward the port, toward traffic, toward anywhere.

"So somebody took the crew," Kestrel said, "and your mother went to ground with the notes." They looked down at the ledger, at the crossed sevens, at the sentence that kept snagging like a hook: *the keystone stays buried until the right hands come for it.*

Eleven days from Day 41. Kestrel counted backward from the silence, forward from the tide tables folded in their breast pocket, and felt the number land in their chest like a dropped anchor.

Three tides. Whoever held the crew, whoever hunted the notes, the window was already opening.

Outside, the fog began to lift off the water in ragged sheets, and far downstream — so faint it might have been imagination — came the low horn of a ship that shouldn't have been there, answering nothing.
