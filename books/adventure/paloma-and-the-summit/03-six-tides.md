## 3. Six Tides

The field notes were written in a cipher I knew by heart, though I'd never admitted it to anyone. Not the formal academic shorthand my mother had taught me in another life — this was her private code, the one she'd invented for me when I was nine, so we could pass messages across a dinner table full of visiting patrons without them knowing. River-words for people. Current-words for danger. A whole language built out of water, because even then she'd understood that everything important in our lives would flow through it.

I sat in the abandoned camp with the notebook open on my knees while Marisol and Bo did what they could with the instruments that still hummed on their tripods. The tide gauge's stylus had run out of ink hours after whoever left — after *she* left — and the last legible line it drew was a jagged spike, like a heartbeat interrupted.

"Kestrel." Marisol crouched beside me, dripping river water onto the canvas floor. She'd been down twice already, working the shallows where the channel dropped off into blackness, and her lips were still blue at the edges. "There's something down there you need to see. But first — tell me what those pages say, because you've been staring at them like they're going to bite."

"They might." I turned the notebook toward her. "She was mapping something. A structure under the channel floor — big one, older than anything the river authority has on record. And she wasn't just mapping it. She was—" I stopped, because the next part sounded like madness spoken aloud.

"She was what?" Bo had come over too, wiping grease from his hands onto trousers that had given up being any other color. He was a broad, patient man who talked to engines more gently than he talked to people, and right now his voice had the careful tone he used when an engine was about to do something interesting.

"She sealed it." I traced the diagram: a series of collapse-lines drawn in red, choke points marked along a submerged passage. "These aren't survey marks. They're demolition charges. She brought down parts of the approach tunnel herself. On purpose."

Marisol went very still. "Sealed it against whom?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I closed the notebook. The answer was sitting in my chest like swallowed stone: against someone. Against the patron whose letters I'd burned unread. Against whatever she'd found down there that was worth collapsing a tunnel over — worth, maybe, worth walking away from her own child over, if you believed the worst version of the story. I didn't say any of that. "Against everyone. Including us, probably."

Bo grunted. "Then why leave the notes?"

That stopped me. I looked back down at the notebook, really looked, and saw what grief and anger had kept me from seeing: the cipher was the childhood one. The private one. The one exactly two living people could read, and one of them had been missing for seven years.

*She wrote it for me.*

* * *

We spent the afternoon turning the camp inside out, and what we found rearranged the shape of everything.

Her instruments hadn't been abandoned; they'd been *left running*, deliberately, a message in the only language guaranteed to reach another salvage crew. The tide gauge records went back eleven months. When Bo plotted them against the depth soundings Marisol had pulled from the deep channel, a pattern emerged that made the hair on my arms stand up.

"There," Bo said, tapping the overlay. "See it? Eleven times a day the channel reads shallow, then deep, then shallow again. Normal tidal breathing. But every—" he counted, lips moving, "—every ninth day, there's an anomaly. A drawdown. The whole channel drops four meters below predicted tide for about forty minutes, then surges back hard enough to bend the gauge needle."

"A siphon," Marisol said quietly. She said it the way sailors say *storm*. "A tidal siphon. There's a cavern system under the channel — the wreck sits inside it — and on certain tides the pressure differential opens a passage. Water gets sucked through, drops the surface level, then slams back when the siphon chokes."

"How long does it stay open?" I asked.

Marisol shook her head. "Depends on the geometry. Could be minutes. Could be less. You dive it on the drawdown, ride the pull through, and you pray the surge doesn't catch you in the throat of it." She looked at me, and there was no bravado in her face at all, which from Marisol was more frightening than fear would have been. "Divers die on siphons, Kestrel. Good ones. Ones who've done it fifty times. The river doesn't care how good you are. It cares whether the tide is feeling generous."

Bo had gone back to the gauge records, jaw working. "Every ninth day," he repeated. "And the season turns in—" he did arithmetic on his fingers, then on the camp's wall calendar, where my mother had marked days with small precise crosses. "Sixty-three days. That's seven openings left this season. Seven chances."

"Six," Marisol said. "You never dive the last window of a season. Pressure patterns get erratic near the turn. Six tides, if we're perfect. Five, if we're smart."

Six tides. Fifty-four days. To find a way through a passage my mother had deliberately collapsed, into a wreck she'd booby-trapped against intruders, and raise whatever was worth all of it — before the river closed its throat for the winter.

I should have said no. Every competent instinct I owned said no. I'd taken this job as pay, as a simple escort contract upriver and back, and now I was holding a dead woman's — a *missing* woman's — notebook, planning a dive that had killed better divers than me, on behalf of a crew I'd known for nine days.

"Kestrel." Marisol's voice cut through. "Whatever you're deciding — decide fast. Because there's a second problem."

* * *

The second problem was anchored three miles downstream, and it had not been there yesterday.

Bo spotted it first from the ridge above the narrows: hulls, black as tar, riding low and heavy in the evening current. Three ships, maybe four, masts bare, no lights showing. No flag either, which on a salvage vessel meant one thing — they weren't here to file claims with the river authority. Legitimate crews flew their letters. These ships were dressed for work they didn't want witnessed.

"Salvage fleet," Bo said, passing me the glass. "Deep-draft. Those are lifting cranes on the foredecks. Heavy ones."

I put the glass to my eye and counted hulls in the failing light. Four. And at the bow of the lead ship, catching the last orange strip of sunset, a figure stood watching the narrows — watching *us* — with the stillness of someone who had been watching for a long time.

"They know about the siphon," I said. It wasn't a guess. Nobody anchored four crane-ships in a dead reach of river for the fishing. "They've known longer than we have."

Marisol took the glass, studied the fleet, and swore softly in Portuguese. "That's Vess. The black hulls — that's Vess's fleet. He worked the drowned quarter of Corvessa ten years back. They say he lifted a cathedral bell out of sixty feet of floodwater and didn't lose a man. They also say what happened to the men who questioned his claim on it."

"You believe that?"

"I believe he didn't lose a man," she said, handing the glass back. "I believe nothing else anyone says about him."

Below us, the river ran dark and quick between the cliffs, carrying cold meltwater toward the sea, past the black ships, past the drowned temple none of us had seen yet, past the camp where my mother had eaten a meal she never finished and walked — or been carried — into the fog.

Bo sat down heavily on a rock, and for a moment the three of us just looked at each other in the blue dusk. We were not a promising company. A dredger with a fake name and a chip on my shoulder the size of a cargo hold. A diver who'd been fired from two crews for diving drunk — her word, offered up front, which is why I'd hired her. An engineer who talked to winches. Nine days together, most of them spent arguing about fuel rations.

"We can't out-lift them," Bo said. "Four crane-ships against our one tug with a patched winch. If they get to the wreck first, it's over. Whatever's down there, they'll strip it and sink the evidence, and nobody upstream will ever know."

"And if we try to beat them through the siphon," Marisol said, "we die tired."

"So we don't beat them through the siphon." I heard my own voice come out flat and certain, and realized I'd already decided somewhere between the tide gauge and the black hulls. "We let them watch us fail. We let them think the siphon's impassable — that the crazy dredger crew tried it and got nothing. Meanwhile we work the real approach." I tapped the notebook. "She collapsed the main tunnel, but look — these marks here. She left one route intact. Narrow. One diver, no rig support. She left it open *on purpose*, the same way she left the notes. For someone who could read this."

"For you," Marisol said.

For me. Seven years of silence, and the first thing my mother ever sent me was a death-trap with my name on it.

"The fleet will make its move soon," I went on. "Ships like that don't sit idle. So here's how it stands. Six tides this season. One usable approach. Four hostile ships downstream, and whatever drove my mother to seal a temple and vanish somewhere in the middle of it." I looked at them both — at Marisol with her salt-cracked hands, at Bo with his gentle engine voice — and asked the only question that mattered. "You're both free to walk. There's a road out of this valley. Nobody would call you cowards."

Bo snorted. "Walk where? Back to hauling dredge spoil for port bosses who short the tally?" He shook his head slowly. "I came upriver because the pay was strange and the radio went quiet. I'm staying because the pay's still strange and now I'm curious. That's as good a reason as any man needs."

Marisol smiled, and it was the first real smile I'd seen from her since the fog. "I've died on worse odds," she said. "Usually on purpose."

* * *

That night we rigged the camp for defense — not much of one, a tripwire and a flare rack, but Bo took pride in it — and I sat up with the notebook while the others slept. By lamplight I decoded the rest of the intact-route markings, and near the end of the final page I found something I hadn't expected: a date, recent, and beneath it a single line in plain handwriting, no cipher, as if she'd finally grown tired of hiding even from herself.

*If the siphon gives six, take the sixth. Never the fifth.*

No explanation. Just that, in her careful archaeologist's hand — the same hand that had signed seven years of letters I'd returned unopened.

Outside, the river breathed against the banks, counting toward its next opening. Downstream, the black hulls rode at anchor, patient as debts. Fifty-three days. Six tides. One narrow door through the throat of the world, left open by a woman who had always been better at leaving instructions than apologies.

I closed the notebook and went to wake Marisol, because tomorrow we'd need to sound the approach channel before the fleet's scouts moved upriver — and because the tide tables said the first window opened in four days, and I intended to be standing at the edge of it when the water began to pull.
