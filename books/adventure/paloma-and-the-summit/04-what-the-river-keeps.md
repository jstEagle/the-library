## 4. What the River Keeps

The siphon opened at slack water, and for eleven minutes the river forgot how to kill you.

Marisol had dived wrecks from the Sable Coast to the drowned quarries of Vell, and she'd never seen anything like the throat of it: a limestone funnel where the current peeled away from itself, leaving a column of dead-calm green water ringed by a spinning collar of white. Bo called it the river's eye. Kestrel hadn't called it anything. They'd just stood at the rail that morning with the tide tables in their fist and said, *Eleven minutes. Maybe twelve if we're lucky. Don't be greedy.*

So Marisol wasn't greedy.

She went down through the eye at the turn of the tide, when the pull slackened and the siphon's hunger paused like a held breath. The water below the collar was cold in a way that felt deliberate — not the cold of depth but of something kept. Her lamp cut a cone through the murk, and the walls of the funnel closed around her, ribbed stone slick with a century of silt, and she thought: *this isn't geology. This is architecture.* Someone had shaped this throat. Someone had wanted the river to swallow things whole and deliver them here, gently, to the dark.

Her gauge read forty feet when the funnel spat her out into open water, and Marisol forgot, briefly, how to breathe on the regulator.

The temple lay on its side across the basin floor like a fallen god.

It was enormous — a stone body longer than the *Cormorant*, its colonnade snapped at the waist, its roof slabs fanned out across the silt like the feathers of a broken wing. Carvings covered every surface she could see: processions of figures, rivers rendered as serpents, a sun disc held aloft by hands that repeated down the length of the frieze until they became a pattern, then a texture, then a kind of music made visible. The silt had taken everything soft and left only the bones, but the bones were *whole*. No dynamite scars. No salvage cuts. No anchor drags.

In thirty years of this work, Marisol had never once found a wreck that nobody had gotten to first.

She kicked along the flank of the thing, her lamp sweeping, cataloguing out of habit — and that was when she saw the lines.

They ran across the temple's spine in taut, deliberate rows: braided cable anchored to the columns, threaded through carved pulley-stones older than any rope attached to them, and rigged — she followed one with her fingers, tracing its logic — rigged to *drop*. Each line terminated in a counterweight wedged into the superstructure above the main vault. Cut them wrong, pull them wrong, come at the vault from the wrong angle, and the weights would fall, the columns would fold, and the whole roof would come down like a jaw closing.

Collapse-lines. Dozens of them. A minefield woven out of archaeology.

Marisol hung there in the green dark, heart hammering against her regulator, and understood what she was looking at. This wasn't ancient engineering. The pulley-stones were old, yes, but the rigging was new — new-ish, anyway, weathered by maybe two or three seasons of current. Someone had come down here, into a siphon that opened six tides a season, and spent those precious minutes not taking treasure but *building traps*.

Someone had sealed this place on purpose.

And there was only one person on the river who could have done it. The same person whose field notes were spread across Bo's workbench right now, annotated in a hand so precise it looked printed. Dr. Elara Whitlock had found the temple first — months ago, maybe a year — and instead of stripping it, she had turned it into a locked room and swallowed the key.

*She wasn't running an expedition,* Marisol thought. *She was running a defense.*

The realization should have frightened her. It did, a little. But underneath the fear was something else, something that made her want to laugh into her regulator: wonder. Because whoever rigged these lines hadn't just been clever. They'd been *careful*. Every counterweight sat clear of the friezes. Every anchor point was drilled into dead stone, never into the carvings. Even sealing the temple shut, even preparing to bring it down around intruders, Whitlock had protected the art.

That told Marisol more about the woman than seven years of silence ever could.

Her timer buzzed against her wrist. Four minutes to make the ascent window. She turned back toward the funnel throat — and her lamp caught a glint near the temple's prow, where the bow-stone rose out of the silt like the stem of a sunken ship.

A cairn. Small, human-sized, deliberate. Three flat stones stacked at the base of the prow-carving, and tucked beneath the topmost stone, a shape that didn't belong to the temple's geometry.

Marisol knew she shouldn't stop. Kestrel's voice was already in her head — *don't be greedy* — and the timer was burning, and the siphon's patience was not infinite. But the object was small, palm-sized, and it had been *placed*, not lost, and some instinct older than training said: this is a message, and messages don't wait for the next tide.

She took it.

The ascent was a blur of counting breaths and watching the collar of white light grow overhead. She shot up through the eye as the current began to wake, the river's hunger returning like a slow-closing hand, and broke the surface into gray daylight with the token clenched in her fist and Bo hauling her over the gunwale by the tank straps.

"Eleven minutes flat," Bo said, checking his watch. "You're getting cocky."

"I'm getting *good*," Marisol said, spitting out her regulator, "and I found something."

Kestrel came off the winch housing like they'd been launched. They'd been pretending to work all dive — coiling rope that was already coiled, checking a gauge that didn't need checking — and now the pretense fell off them entirely. "The temple? Is it—"

"Intact." Marisol watched their face do something complicated. "Completely intact. And trapped. Collapse-lines across the whole vault approach — rigged fresh, professional work. She sealed it, Kestrel. Your mother found it first and she *sealed it*, and every trap is built to spare the carvings. Whoever she was keeping out, she wasn't willing to break the site to do it."

Kestrel stood very still. The wind moved the fog past them in slow rags. "That sounds like her," they said finally, and their voice was strange — flat on top, shaking underneath. "Everything precise. Everything protected. Everyone kept at arm's length."

"That's not all." Marisol opened her fist.

The token was river-stone, worn smooth, no bigger than a flattened plum. One face bore a carving — crude compared to the temple's friezes, done with a knife or a nail by someone working fast, working by feel. But the strokes were confident, and Marisol had spent enough evenings watching Kestrel annotate charts to recognize the hand.

It was a bird. Wings spread, riding a current.

Kestrel took it without touching her palm, the way you take something hot. They turned it over. On the reverse, cut shallow but unmistakable, was a single word.

*Kestrel.*

"No," they said. Not to her. To the token. To the word. "No, that's not—" They stopped. Started again. "My mother didn't call me that. Nobody calls me that. That's not a name anyone uses."

"It's a name *someone* used," Marisol said quietly. "Someone who knew you before you decided who you'd be."

Bo leaned in, squinting. "Kid's name's Kestrel. That's just—" He stopped too. His eyebrows climbed. "Oh. Oh, that's not your given name, is it. That's a *bird*. Somebody named you after a bird that hunts on the wing."

"My father did," Kestrel said. Barely audible. "Before he left. Before she—" Their thumb moved over the carved wings, back and forth, a small compulsive motion. "Nobody's called me that in twenty years. She never used it. Not in the letters. Not once. She always wrote my full name like she was filing a report."

"And yet," Marisol said, "she dove into a tidal siphon six times a season, alone, to seal a temple against whatever's chasing her — and she left a stone under the prow with your childhood name on it."

The fog thickened around the boat. Somewhere downstream, faint as a memory, the black hulls of Vess's fleet would be waiting out the same tide tables they were. Marisol thought about saying so and decided the news could keep for one more hour.

Kestrel closed their fist around the token. When they spoke again, the flatness was gone, and what was underneath it was worse and better at once.

"She left this *for me*. Not for the expedition. Not for the report. For—" They stopped, jaw working. "Seven years of nothing, and the first thing she sends me is a message I have to dive through a drowning machine to find."

"Maybe," Marisol said, "that was the message."

Kestrel looked at her then — really looked, the way they'd been carefully not doing since the camp — and Marisol saw the exact moment the arithmetic changed behind their eyes. The missing team list. The abandoned meal. The instruments still recording. All of it re-sorting itself around a single carved stone.

"She's alive," Kestrel said. "She has to be alive. You don't leave a message for someone unless you believe they'll come."

Downriver, low and wrong, a sound rolled up through the fog — engines, many of them, running at a speed no honest survey vessel needed at slack tide.

Bo straightened. "That'll be our friends in the black paint."

Kestrel put the token inside their shirt, against the skin, and turned to face the sound.

"Then we've got five tides left," they said, "and a temple to open before they do."

* * *

That night, Marisol found Kestrel at the stern with the field notes spread under a lantern, the token sitting in the lamplight like a small smooth heart. They weren't reading the notes anymore. They were holding the stone up to the flame, turning it, studying the knife-strokes as if the angle of each cut might tell them where in the world their mother had gone next.

Marisol settled onto the rail beside them and said nothing, because some questions you answer by staying.

But she'd seen the cairn's placement, and she'd counted the stones, and she knew what three stacked markers meant in every river tradition from the Sable Coast to the Vell quarries: *follow me deeper.*

Whatever Whitlock had left under that second stone — or beneath the vault her collapse-lines guarded — it wasn't treasure.

It was a trail. And it led straight through the trap.
