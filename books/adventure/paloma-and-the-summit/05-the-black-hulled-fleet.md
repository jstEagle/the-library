## 5. The Black-Hulled Fleet

Bo had rebuilt the winch motor twice in her life, and both times she'd sworn on her father's wrench that she'd never do it a third time. She was elbow-deep in the housing, knuckles greased to the wrist, when Marisol's shout came down from the crow's nest — flat, clipped, the way she only spoke when something was actually wrong.

"Bo. Smoke downstream. Three columns."

Bo wiped her hands on a rag that was already more grease than cloth and climbed the ladder two rungs at a time. From the nest, through the morning haze off the water, she saw them: three ships running dark, hulls painted the black of river mud, no lights, no colors, moving against the current with the lazy confidence of something that had never once been told no.

"They've been holding station since before dawn," Marisol said. "Kestrel spotted them an hour ago. Didn't want to say anything until they committed."

"Committed now," Bo muttered.

The lead ship turned toward their anchorage with the unhurried grace of a knife being laid on a table. As it closed, Bo made out the figure at its rail — broad-shouldered, silver-haired, wearing a coat too fine for the river and boots too clean for it. A parley flag ran up the halyard: white, bordered in green, the old river code for *I come to talk, not to take.*

"The last man who flew that flag at me," Marisol said quietly, "took my boat."

"Then we watch his hands, not his flag."

Kestrel came up from below deck already dressed, hair tied back, face arranged into that careful blankness Bo had learned to read as anything but calm. They looked at the black hulls for a long moment.

"Vess," Kestrel said. "Salvage fleet out of Corren's Landing. I've heard the name on three different docks, and none of them said it kindly."

"You know him?"

"I know the shape of him. Every river has one." Kestrel rolled their shoulders. "Let him board. If he's flying parley, he wants something, and a man who wants something shows you where he's soft."

* * *

Captain Aldous Vess came up the ladder like a man boarding his own vessel. He was taller than he'd seemed from distance, with a voice like warmed honey poured over gravel, and he surveyed their little expedition boat — the patched rigging, Bo's jury-rigged compressor lines, the dredge scars along the hull — with an expression of polite, surgical appraisal.

"A survey vessel," he said. "How charming. I haven't seen honest equipment in years."

"We're not selling," Kestrel said.

"No. You're digging." Vess smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Captain Whitlock."

Bo watched it land. Watched Kestrel go very still — not flinching, exactly, but locking, the way a door locks. The name hung in the air between them like a dropped tool nobody would pick up.

"That's not my name," Kestrel said.

"No? Strange. Halloran is the name on the missing-persons filing. But the harbor master at Tallow Bend keeps excellent records, and a certain dredger working the mud flats there signed on as *Whitlock* seven years ago, right around the time Dr. E. Halloran stopped answering her university's letters." Vess folded his hands behind his back. "You'll forgive a captain his curiosity. Family resemblance is a powerful thing. I served briefly under your mother, years ago. She has her father's jaw. You have hers."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere below, their compressor coughed and settled.

"You've been looking for us," Kestrel said.

"I've been looking for *her*. You were simply standing in the place where she'd been." Vess began to walk the deck slowly, trailing one finger along the rail, and Bo understood suddenly that this was a performance rehearsed on many decks. "Here is what I know, and I offer it freely, as a gesture of good faith. Dr. Halloran found the temple wreck four months ago. She mapped it. She sealed it — collapse-lines, deliberately set, which tells us she wanted it kept from someone. And then she and her team vanished into the narrows, leaving instruments running and tea going cold." He paused. "Someone else wanted it kept from *me*, Captain. And she paid for that opinion with her freedom, I suspect."

"If you think we'll help you find her—"

"Help me?" Vess laughed, warm and genuine-sounding, and somehow that was worse. "Child, I'm offering you command. My fleet, my rigs, my divers — all of it pointed at that wreck, with you at the wheel. Your name on the salvage claim. Full partnership shares. All I require is the route through the siphon, and whatever your mother left behind by way of maps." He spread his hands. "She sealed that site to keep it from men like me. But you're not a man like me, are you? You're her blood. The claim would be *rightful*. Nobody could touch you. Nobody could touch her, once she's recovered — my patron's interest evaporates the moment the site is legally yours."

"And your patron gets—" Bo started.

"Compensated. Handsomely." Vess's eyes flicked to her, and for just a moment the honey went cold. "Which is more than can be said for people who obstruct legitimate salvage operations. I'd hate for this crew's fine equipment to suffer... regulatory seizure."

There it was. Bo felt her stomach drop through the deck plates. Not a request. An audit with a smile.

Kestrel hadn't moved. They stood at the rail with the stillness of someone reading a riverbed — reading *him* — and when they finally spoke, their voice was level as stillwater.

"You said she has her father's jaw. You knew my grandfather."

"I dined with him twice. A brilliant, foolish man who thought knowledge belonged to everyone." Vess's smile thinned. "Look how that ended for him."

"My mother sealed that wreck," Kestrel said, "because of people like you. And you're standing on my deck asking me to hand you the key."

"I'm standing on your deck," Vess said softly, "offering you everything you've spent seven years too proud to ask for. A name. A claim. A family legacy instead of a family wound." He straightened his coat. "You have until the next tide to decide. I'll send my launch for your answer. Do think carefully about your crew, Captain. Ambition is survivable. Loyalty to the wrong people is not."

He descended the ladder without waiting for a reply, and the black ship pulled away with the same unhurried grace, back toward its station downstream.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a full minute after the launch disappeared into the haze. Then Bo broke it, because somebody had to.

"He knew your name before he ever boarded."

"He's known it longer than that." Kestrel sat down hard on the dredge housing, and for the first time since Bo had known them, they looked tired. Not defeated — tired, like a rope bearing weight it was built for but resented. "Tallow Bend harbor master. Seven years of records. He's been building a file on me since before I ever heard of this expedition."

"Why show us his hand?" Marisol crouched nearby, arms on her knees. "A man like that doesn't reveal leverage unless he thinks he doesn't need to hide it anymore."

"Or unless the leverage isn't the point." Bo wiped her hands again, uselessly. "Did you catch it? 'Regulatory seizure.' His patron isn't some collector. It's an authority. Maybe *the* authority. He doesn't need our permission — he needs our cooperation so the paperwork comes out clean. He wants us to make it legal for him."

"So we refuse," Marisol said.

"We refuse," Kestrel agreed, "and he takes the refusal as an obstacle to remove." They looked up, and something in their face had shifted — the tiredness burning off, leaving something harder underneath. "He gave us a deadline because deadlines make people stupid. So first thing: nothing we say or do between now and the next tide should tell him we've decided anything. Second thing—"

The compressor below deck shrieked.

Not coughed. *Shrieked* — a metal-on-metal scream that rose and died in the same breath, followed by a sound Bo knew in her bones: the particular groan of a hydraulic line letting go under load. She was down the ladder before anyone could speak, and what she found in the engine bay turned her stomach to iron.

Their spare winch cable — the good one, the one they'd rigged from the *Paloma*'s stores at Tallow Bend — was gone. Cut. Not frayed, not worn: cut clean near the drum, the severed end polished bright, and coiled neatly beside it, deliberate as a signature, lay a length of black-painted chain. Fleet issue. She'd seen a hundred coils of it on Vess's decks.

"He boarded us under parley," Bo said flatly, when the others crowded into the bay, "and robbed us while he smiled."

Marisol picked up the chain and turned it over. "His diver took it during the tour. While we were watching his hands, he was watching ours."

"He never intended to wait for our answer." Kestrel took the chain, weighed it, and set it down with terrible gentleness. "This wasn't theft. It was a message. *You need me more than I need you.* Without that cable, we can't lift anything heavier than a lantern. No keystone. No salvage. Nothing."

Silence. Bo stared at the empty drum and did the math she didn't want to do: six days to the nearest port, two days back, and Vess's fleet sitting fat between them and every cable merchant on the river. He hadn't just taken their wire. He'd taken their timeline and folded it into his own.

"So that's it," Marisol said quietly. "We're done."

"No." Kestrel picked up the severed end of the old primary cable — the frayed, kinked, half-dead line they'd replaced a week ago — and held it up to the lamplight. "That's what he thinks. Bo. How much life is left in this one?"

Bo took it, ran her thumbs down the strands, feeling for the story in the wire the way Kestrel read a riverbed. Frayed at the shoulder. Kinked twice. Corroded at the core. Dead for heavy lifting.

Dead for *heavy* lifting. But they weren't lifting stone. Not yet. They were lifting a map out of a siphon, and a person out of a vault, and after that—

After that, they'd need a miracle, or a splice, or both.

"It'll hold maybe eight hundred pounds if I re-lay the strands and bind the weak points," she heard herself say. "Maybe. Once. Possibly twice if the river's merciful, and the river has never once been merciful to me."

"Then we have a winch," Kestrel said.

"We have a *prayer* with a hook on it."

"Same thing, on this river." Kestrel turned to the others, and Bo saw it settle over them — not confidence, exactly, but resolve, the kind that comes from having nothing left to lose but each other. "Three tides left before the siphon opens wide enough for the deep dive. Vess thinks he's taken our choice away. Good. Let him think it. Because while he's waiting for us to crawl to him and beg, we're going to dive, and map, and get my mother — and when the final tide turns, Captain Aldous Vess is going to learn exactly what this river does to men who rob the wrong crew."

Marisol grinned, sharp as a filleting knife. Bo just picked up her tools.

Eight hundred pounds. One shot. She'd rebuilt the motor twice and sworn never a third time.

Time to start swearing.
