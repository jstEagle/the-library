## 6. Letters Never Sent

The storm came off the ridge like something with a grudge.

Kestrel heard it before they saw it — a low rolling thunder that didn't sound like thunder at all but like the river itself turning over in its sleep, annoyed at being disturbed. They had been checking the upper survey stakes when the sky went the color of wet slate, and by the time they'd coiled the last line, the first fat drops were hitting the mud hard enough to leave craters.

"Storm shelter!" Bo's voice crackled through the handheld, shredded by static. "Cave on the north cliff — Marisol found it yesterday — get inside before this washes the whole bank into the channel!"

Kestrel ran. There was no dignity in it; there never was with storms on the Reach. The rain came sideways, needling through their oilskin, and the world shrank to a gray smear of water and stone and the pale blur of their own hands grabbing at roots. Behind them, the camp vanished. Ahead, the cliff rose out of the murk like the hull of a ship run aground a thousand years ago and left to rot upright.

The cave mouth was a black seam in the limestone, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging vine. Kestrel shouldered through it and stood dripping in the dark, chest heaving, listening to the storm arrive in earnest outside — a wall of sound, the river rising, the whole world hammering.

A match flared. Marisol's face appeared above it, sharp-boned and calm as always, her hair plastered flat to her skull.

"You look like something the river spat back out," she said.

"It tried." Kestrel wrung out their sleeves and looked around. The cave went deeper than a shelter needed to. Deeper than a natural seam usually bothered to go. The floor was dry sand, swept clean — not river-swept. *Broom*-swept.

They both noticed it at the same time.

Marisol raised the match higher. "Somebody lived here."

"Somebody *camped* here," Kestrel said, and their voice came out flatter than they intended. Because they knew the sweep pattern. They knew the way gear was stowed against the left wall, weight low, tied down against tremors. They knew the little cairn of stones marking a fresh-water seep in the back wall, each stone placed clockwise. Their mother used to build those cairns at every site, every dig, every temporary life she assembled and then abandoned. Kestrel had spent seven years trying to forget the habit and failing.

"Kestrel." Marisol's voice was careful. "Whose camp is this?"

The storm answered for them, slamming the cave mouth with a gust that snuffed the match. In the sudden dark, Kestrel stood very still and let the truth settle over them like the water dripping from their coat.

"This is hers."

* * *

They waited out the worst of it with the lantern lit low, because there was nothing else to do. Bo arrived soaked and swearing twenty minutes later, hauling a satchel of instruments he'd refused to leave behind, and together the three of them explored the cave by lamplight while the storm screamed at the entrance like a creditor at a door.

It went back forty feet, maybe more, the walls sweating mineral shine. And at the very back, behind a fall of limestone that someone had carefully wedged to look natural, there was a cavity. And in the cavity, wrapped in waxed canvas and sealed with pitch, there was a strongbox.

Not a treasure box. Kestrel knew treasure boxes now — they'd spent two weeks thinking about nothing else. This was an archive box: shallow, iron-strapped, the kind survey offices used for paper records. The kind that survived floods.

The latch was stiff with age but not locked. Of course not locked. It had been waiting.

Inside: a field journal, water-stained at the corners. A bundle of instruments — a hand lens, a compass with a cracked bezel, a pencil worn to a stub. And beneath all of it, tied with cord gone soft as cloth, a stack of envelopes.

Dozens of them. All addressed. None stamped.

All addressed to *K. Whitlock*, care of a dozen different post offices up and down the river country — the harbor town where Kestrel had turned fourteen, the dredging camp where they'd turned sixteen, the port where they'd learned to read current-scars on a riverbed well enough to make a living from other people's wrecks. Every place Kestrel had ever been. Every address their mother could possibly have known.

"She wrote," Marisol said quietly. "She just never sent them."

"No." Kestrel's hands were steady on the stack, which surprised them. Inside, nothing was steady. Inside, seven years of anger were rearranging themselves into some new shape they didn't have a name for yet. "No, she did worse than write. She kept them. She carried them. She probably moved this box three times." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's not forgetting someone. That's *curating* them."

Bo cleared his throat. "You want us to give you room?"

Kestrel almost said yes. Almost took the box and the hurt and the letters into the far dark of the cave to open alone, the way they'd done everything alone since the day a neighbor's letter arrived saying *your mother's taken a post upriver, don't know when she'll be back,* and no letter ever followed it.

But Marisol was looking at them with that level, unflinching patience she brought to everything, even drowning things, and Kestrel thought about the dive through the siphon, and the token carved with a name nobody living should have known, and thought: *she meant these for me, but maybe she also meant me to not be alone reading them.*

"Stay," Kestrel said. "If I'm going to do this, I'm doing it sober, and you two are my sobriety."

* * *

The earliest letter was dated seven years back. The handwriting was small and slanted and ruthlessly neat — field-notes handwriting, handwriting trained to survive rain and haste.

*Kestrel. I've started this letter eleven times. That's not an apology, it's a confession of cowardice, and you deserve better than apologies anyway. You deserve reasons. Here is the only one I have that's true.*

*I found something in the temple wreck. You'll have heard the stories — everyone tells the stories wrong. It isn't gold. Gold would be simple. It's records. Ledgers, correspondence, sealed contracts — the patron class of this river, forty years of them, buying salvage rights, buying silence, buying people. Names. Dates. Sums. Enough paper to hang half the families that own the shipping lines.*

*My patron funded this expedition to retrieve it. He believes it will be his leverage. He is correct, which is why I can't let him have it. So I've collapsed the access channels. I've made the wreck a tomb again. He thinks the river took it. Let him think that.*

*The cost is that the river has taken me too, for a while. If I surface, if I file so much as a survey claim, he will know the wreck still lives and he will come with machines and lawyers and men whose jobs are not legal. So I stay buried alongside it. I'm sorry. I keep writing that word and hating it.*

*You learned to read riverbeds instead of my letters, and I want you to know I understood exactly what that meant, and I deserved it.*

Kestrel stopped. The lantern hissed. Outside, the storm had settled into a long, grinding roar, the sound of the river eating the banks.

"Read riverbeds instead of my letters." They said it aloud, tasting it. "She knew. She *knew* that's what I—" Their voice snagged. They pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. "I spent seven years telling myself she didn't think about me at all. That was easier. That was a *load-bearing* belief, Marisol. You don't just pull that out."

Marisol said nothing. Bo studied the ceiling like it owed him money. Both of them stayed.

Kestrel read on.

The middle years of letters were shorter, harder. Reports without a recipient. *I saw a dredge crew in Harrow Bend flying your old company's colors and I stood in the rain for an hour trying to decide whether to ask after you. I didn't. He watches the harbors.* Once, devastatingly: *I taught a girl on the survey team to read stratigraphy the way I would have taught you. She's better at it than I was. I think you'd have liked her, or hated her, or both.* And once, in a hand shakier than the rest: *I sealed the lower vault with the records inside and the keystone map in my head, and some nights I dream the river opens it without me and I wake up tasting rust. If anything happens to me, the map dies with me, and that is the right outcome. Remember that if you're angry. Be angry at the right person.*

"I was angry at the right person," Kestrel said. "I just picked the wrong one."

The last letter was recent. Weeks old. The ink was barely faded.

*Kestrel. If you're reading this, then either the river gave you up or you gave yourself to it, and knowing you, it's the second. My team and I are going into the vault to move the records somewhere he can never reach — deeper, under the collapse line, past the point where recovery stops being profitable even for men like him. It's dangerous. I won't pretend otherwise. But I find I'd rather die finishing this than live guarding it forever.*

*One more thing, and then I'll stop hiding behind paper. I named you for a bird that flies into storms on purpose. I told myself once that I left so you wouldn't inherit my war. That was a lie I liked. The truth is I left because I couldn't bear for you to inherit it, and staying was how wars get inherited. Whatever you decide to be — Whitlock, or nothing, or something with no name yet — decide it yourself. Not from my letters. Not from my ghost. From the riverbed, if that's what you trust. You always read it better than I did.*

*— M.*

Kestrel set the letter down on their knee and looked at their own hands, scarred and rope-callused, hands that had spent seven years signing nothing, claiming nothing, belonging nowhere on purpose.

"Her name's Maren," they said. It came out strange, like a word in a language they'd stopped speaking. "I haven't said it out loud in six years."

"We know," Bo said gently. "It's on the missing-team manifest. Been staring at you the whole trip."

"I know it's on the manifest." Kestrel folded the last letter along its original crease, careful, careful. "I mean I know *her*. Now. Seven years of her, in her own hand, no one filtering it." They looked up. The anger hadn't vanished — anger that deep doesn't vanish, it just changes address. It had simply packed its bags and moved downriver, toward a patron with machines and lawyers and men whose jobs were not legal. Toward whoever had taken her team mid-meal and left the instruments running.

"She sealed the wreck to starve him of leverage," Kestrel said. "And Vess is anchored downstream with a fleet full of winches, and he knows my name, and he knows whose child I am, which means somebody told him, which means *he* isn't working blind either." They stood, and the cave felt suddenly too small for what was building in their chest. "She chose the river over him. Seven years ago. Alone."

Marisol tilted her head. "And you?"

Kestrel looked at the letters, at the compass with the cracked bezel, at the broom-swept floor of a home their mother had kept in a hole in a cliff within a day's walk of the daughter who hated her.

"I choose the crew," they said. "And then we go get her back."

Outside, the storm broke apart as suddenly as it had gathered. And through the thinning rain, from the direction of the river, came a sound that froze all three of them where they stood — the deep, patient horn of a black-hulled ship, coming up the narrows.
