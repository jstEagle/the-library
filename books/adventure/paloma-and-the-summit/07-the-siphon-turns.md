## 7. The Siphon Turns

The third tide of the season came in gray and mean, the kind of water that made even the gulls sit down and sulk. Marisol stood at the rail of the *Cormorant's Rest* in her dive skin, watching the siphon breathe.

That was the only word for it. The channel below the cliff narrowed to a throat of black stone, and twice a day the river poured into it and vanished — swallowed whole, roaring down into the flooded vaults beneath the temple wreck. Then, hours later, it exhaled. The water came back up in a slow, shuddering column, thick with silt, carrying things it had no business carrying: branches stripped of bark, a fisherman's net rotted to lace, once — Bo swore on his mother's grave — an entire canoe, unbroken.

Six tides per season, Halloran's field notes said. The window when the pull slackened enough for a diver to slip through the throat and reach the temple intact.

Three tides left.

"You're going to want to check my math again," Bo said behind her, "because I've checked it four times and I hate all four answers."

Marisol turned. Bo stood over the patched rig like a man standing over a sick animal he'd nursed too long to put down. The compressor housing was lashed with wire and sealed with resin; the air lines ran through three spliced joints, each one wrapped in rubber cut from an old pontoon. It looked less like diving equipment than like a prayer with valves.

"The math's fine," Marisol said. "It's everything else I'm worried about."

"Rude. Accurate, but rude."

Kestrel came up from the hold carrying the keystone map case wrapped in oilcloth, strapped flat against their chest. They'd barely slept — Marisol could see it in the raw rims of their eyes, in the way they moved like someone walking on a frozen river, listening for cracks. Since the cave, since the letters, something had shifted in them. Not softened. Settled. Like sediment finally given time to sink.

"Air reserves?" Kestrel asked.

"Last of it," Bo said. "Two full tanks and the emergency bottle. After this dive we're breathing river water and good intentions."

"Then we make it count." Kestrel looked at Marisol. "You don't have to—"

"If you say 'you can stay topside' one more time," Marisol said, pulling her hood up, "I'm going to drown you myself and save the siphon the trouble."

Kestrel almost smiled. Almost. "Copy that."

* * *

The descent began at slack water, in the forty-minute hush between the siphon's exhale and its next inhale. Marisol went first, Kestrel on the line above her, the map case between them like a shared heartbeat.

Below ten meters the light died. Below twenty, her lamp found the throat — a chimney of black basalt polished smooth by centuries of tidal violence, wide as a wagon at the top and narrowing to barely a body's breadth where it kinked leftward into the vaults. The notes had called it *the swallow*. Halloran had underlined the name twice.

Marisol hated whoever had named it. She hated how right they'd been.

She felt the pull before she saw anything wrong. A faint tug at her fins, gentle as a hand testing a doorknob. Then stronger. The slack window was closing early — the storm system offshore, Bo had warned, dragging the whole tidal cycle out of rhythm.

*Turn back,* said every protocol she'd ever learned, every rule drilled into her during five years of salvage certification. Slack windows were calculated, not guessed. You did not enter a siphon on a shrinking margin. You especially did not enter one with a spliced air line.

She kept going. Kestrel was right behind her, and ahead, somewhere in the dark, lay the answer to why Halloran had sealed a temple with her own hands — and whether she could be gotten out alive.

The throat kinked. Marisol hooked her elbow around the guide line and pulled herself through the narrow bend, and then the passage opened like a held breath released, and her lamp swept across the impossible.

The temple hung inverted in the flooded vault, columns pointing down toward her like the roots of some drowned tree, gold leaf still clinging to their capitals in ragged constellations. Carved figures lined the walls — processions of priests, kings, a goddess with her arms flung wide. And there, wedged in the great doorway they'd seen in the sonar images, sat the collapse: deliberate, precise, a mason's work done in fury or fear. Halloran's seal.

The keystone socket was set in the lintel above the door, exactly where the notes promised. All they needed was the map's confirmation of the release mechanism, and Bo could rig the lift on the final tide.

Marisol swam toward the lintel, unwrapped the map case, and held the oilcloth pages up to her lamp.

That was when the river inhaled.

She felt it in her teeth first — a pressure change, a deep subsonic groan rolling through the stone. Then the water *moved*. Not around her. Toward the throat. Every loose particle in the vault swung like iron filings toward a magnet, and the guide line snapped taut and sang, and Marisol's fins left the bottom without her permission.

"No—" The word came out as a burst of bubbles. She grabbed a carved figure's outstretched arm, a stone priest frozen mid-blessing, and held on while the current tried to peel her away like bark off a branch. Her lamp spun wild. Somewhere above, the throat was swallowing — she could hear it now, a vast wet roar, the sound of a mouth opening.

The map case tore free of her grip.

She watched it tumble end over end into the dark, pages fanning, and thought: *three years of decoding, gone in four seconds.* Then the priest's arm cracked along a fault line, and Marisol stopped thinking about maps at all.

Her grip failed. The current took her.

* * *

Later — much later, when her hands had stopped shaking — Marisol would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. What she remembered was sound: the roar of the throat, the scream of the guide line through its fairlead somewhere far above. What she remembered was cold, total and absolute, folding around her like a fist. And what she remembered, most of all, was the moment the current slammed her against the lip of the throat's narrow bend and held her there — pinned, spinning half-control, her tank banging against basalt, her regulator hose stretched toward snapping.

Through the roar, a second lamp appeared above her. Descending. Into the siphon. Against every protocol ever written, against the shrinking slack window, against sense.

*Kestrel.*

They came down the throat like they'd been born in it — reading the water the way Marisol read charts, finding the dead-water seam along the bend's inner curve where the pull weakened, and sliding down it straight to her. Their hands closed on her harness. Their eyes behind the mask were calm in a way that Marisol would think about for the rest of her life: not fearless, but *decided*. Someone who had already done the arithmetic — two lives against one air supply, one way out against none — and chosen the answer that cost them most.

Kestrel pressed their own backup regulator into her hands.

Marisol shook her head violently. *No.* One diver sharing one bottle, ascending a live siphon — the numbers didn't work, everyone knew the numbers didn't work—

Kestrel's grip tightened. Their eyes said what their mouth couldn't: *I read rivers. Trust me.*

So she breathed.

They went up together, tangled harness to harness, Kestrel reading each shift in the current a half-second before it hit — banking them into the seams, riding the throat's rhythms like a boatman running rapids blindfolded. At the narrow bend Kestrel turned sideways, hauling her through by main strength while the siphon sucked at their fins hard enough to tear one away entirely. Marisol's vision sparked at the edges. The gauge on the shared bottle bled down like a wound.

Thirty meters. Twenty. The roar faded above them, replaced by the gray-green shimmer of open channel, and then they broke the surface together into rain and shouting, and Bo's hands were on the line, and the world was winches and canvas and air, blessed ordinary air.

* * *

Marisol sat wrapped in blankets by the stove, a mug of tea she wasn't drinking cooling in her hands, and watched Kestrel cough river water onto the deck planks.

They'd surfaced on fumes — literally. Bo had cracked the valve on the emergency bottle and gotten maybe sixty seconds of flow before it died. Sixty seconds. Kestrel had come up the last stretch of the throat on nothing but will and a lungful, and had handed Marisol the last working breath without hesitation, without calculation, without even seeming to register it as a choice.

"That was," Bo said, kneeling beside Kestrel with a blanket, "the single worst violation of dive protocol I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a man in Port Sable dive after a dropped wrench."

"Noted," Kestrel rasped.

"You had no reserve. No line. No plan past 'grab her.'"

"I had a river." Kestrel wiped their mouth. "I know how rivers lie."

Bo stared at them for a long moment, then laughed — a cracked, half-hysterical sound — and thumped them on the shoulder hard enough to stagger them. "You're going to be the death of me, Whitlock. Both of you."

Marisol looked down at her hands. They were still trembling, but underneath the tremor was something else, something warm and unfamiliar. She had worked salvage for five years alongside people who would calculate the odds of saving you and usually decide against it. In ninety seconds underwater, Kestrel Whitlock had never once done the math.

"We lost the map," she said quietly.

Kestrel shook their head. They reached into their dive vest, stiff with cold, and drew out a single oilcloth page — crumpled, water-spotted, but whole. "Tore it free before the case went. The mechanism diagram. It's enough for Bo to build the lift."

Marisol took it. Her eyes caught on the margin, where a note in Halloran's small, slanted hand ran alongside the diagram — a note meant for whoever decoded the map, but written, Marisol realized with a chill, in words only one person alive would understand:

*To the kestrel who reads rivers: the door opens from mercy, not force. Tell them I chose this. Tell them I'm sorry. Tell them—*

The rest was blotted, drowned in ink.

Kestrel read it twice. When they looked up, their face was composed, but Marisol had spent a week learning to read them the way they read water, and she saw the crack running through the calm.

"She's in the lower vault," Kestrel said. "The letters said the seal was built around a living space. Air pockets. She designed it so anyone sealed inside could survive." A breath. "One tide left. Vess is coming upriver tonight — Bo spotted his running lights at the bend."

Marisol set down the tea. Around her, the crew was already moving — Bo toward the engine room, the others toward the winch — drawn together by the same gravity that had pulled her through black water and out the other side.

"One tide," she said. "Then we raise the stone, and we go get your mother."
