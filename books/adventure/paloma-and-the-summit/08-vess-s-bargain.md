## 8. Vess's Bargain

They came at slack water, when the river went glass-still and every sound carried for a mile.

Kestrel heard them first as a wrongness in the water — the absence of the herons that usually worked the shallows at dusk, the absence of frogs, the absence of anything that had the good sense to be afraid and act on it. Then the running lights resolved out of the dark: three black hulls sliding upstream without a single lamp showing, running on muffled engines, the way a knife slides between ribs.

"Kill the lanterns," Kestrel said. "Bo, get below. Marisol — the cache, take it into the cave. Now."

There wasn't time to do more than that. There was never time. Vess's lead ship came alongside the *Cormorant* with a soft bump of fenders, and before anyone could reach the winch controls or the radio, grappling lines arced across the deck and men in river-rubber came over the rail the way water comes over a gunwale — everywhere at once, patient, inevitable.

Kestrel stood in the middle of the deck and let them.

That was the calculation, made cold and quick: fight now and lose everything, or stand still and keep the one thing Vess actually wanted, which was not the camp, not the boats, not the winch. It was the route. The siphon gates. The knowledge sitting behind Kestrel's teeth like a stone.

Floodlamps bloomed. And there he was, Captain Vess, stepping aboard his own ship like a man walking into a room he'd already measured for furniture. He was smaller than Kestrel remembered from the parley — a compact, tidy man with a surveyor's squint and hands that looked soft until you noticed the old rope burns across the knuckles. He'd earned those burns pulling men out of water, once, maybe. Or putting them in.

"Whitlock," he said, and the name landed like a hook set in flesh. "Or should I say Halloran? Your mother's name opens doors, you know. It opened this one."

"You broke it open," Kestrel said.

"I knocked." He smiled. He had a good smile, the kind that had been practiced on investors. "Where is she?"

"The river has her."

"The river," Vess repeated, tasting it. He walked the deck slowly, trailing a gloved finger along the rail, along the patched winch housing, along everything Bo had bled to keep running. "I've read Dr. Halloran's early papers. Brilliant woman. Sloppy about people. She thought institutions were structures. They're organisms, Whitlock. They eat." He stopped. "My patron wants what's in that temple. My patron always gets what he wants. The only variable in this equation is you."

"Then I'm the variable you should be polite to."

Something flickered behind the squint — respect, maybe, or the recognition of a familiar animal. Vess gestured, and his men eased back a half-step, which on a captured deck was as good as a drawn curtain.

"Here is my offer," he said. "You take command of this salvage. Full authority. Your crew stays on, paid at fleet rates, triple, with bonuses. Your name goes on the recovery manifest — *Kestrel Halloran-Whitlock, salvage master* — and when the keystone comes up, your mother comes up with it. Alive. She's in the lower vault, you said so yourself to your diver, and my hydrophones confirmed the air pocket this afternoon. She is alive, and she stays alive exactly as long as this operation stays cooperative."

The words went through Kestrel like cold water through a wetsuit — finding every seam.

*She's alive.* He'd said it like a threat, because for Vess everything was a threat wearing a gift's clothing. But underneath the threat: confirmation. Confirmation Kestrel hadn't dared to fully believe since the cave, since the letters, since seven years of silence had cracked open into seven years of explanation. Her mother was down there in the dark, breathing, waiting, and this tidy man with rope-burned hands had just made her a bargaining chip.

Kestrel felt the old thing rise — the anger, the riverbed anger, the kind that had learned to read currents instead of writing letters back. And beneath the anger, something new and colder and more useful: clarity.

Power offered is power corrupted. The letters had said that too, in her mother's careful hand — a warning about the patron, about men who offer you command of a thing so they can own the hand that steers it.

"Command," Kestrel said slowly. "Of the salvage."

"Of the salvage, the crew, the site. My patron doesn't want to manage details. He wants results."

"And the temple contents? The artifacts?"

"Documented, divided, dispersed. Standard salvage law." A lie so smooth it was almost a kindness.

Kestrel looked at Marisol, standing by the mast with two of Vess's men flanking her, her face giving away nothing but her eyes giving away everything — *I trust you, whatever you're about to do, I trust you.* Bo was visible through the engine room hatch, hands raised, jaw set like poured concrete.

The crew. The misfits. The family made rather than given. If Kestrel said no here, Vess would simply put a gun against the arithmetic and solve it another way. If Kestrel said yes—

If Kestrel said yes, there was one tide left, and a siphon, and gates that Bo had spent two days rigging, and a false channel on the survey charts that Kestrel had drawn with their own hand, waiting, like a letter never sent, for exactly this reader.

"Show me your charts," Kestrel said.

* * *

They laid them out in the *Cormorant*'s cabin under a swinging lamp, Vess's navigator and Vess's second officer crowded at the edges while Kestrel stood over the survey of the narrows and became, for one long hour, the thing they were offering to make official: salvage master. Commander. The person whose word moved ships.

It was the easiest lying Kestrel had ever done, because almost all of it was true. The siphon did open on the final tide. The approach channel did run along the north bluff. The keystone could be raised with the winch configuration Bo had already built — that part Kestrel gave him whole and accurate, because the keystone *had* to come up, had to come up on Vess's winches if necessary, because their mother was below it and one tide would not wait.

The lie was a single line on a chart. The false channel — the one Kestrel and Bo had sounded as a decoy, the one that dead-ended against an undercut bluff where the river ran deep and fast and hungry — sat on the survey looking exactly as real as the true one. Two lines. One led to the temple. One led to the river's teeth.

Kestrel put a finger on the false line. "Approach here. The north bluff channel. It's deeper, it stays out of the siphon's surface turbulence, and your draft will clear by two meters."

Vess studied the chart. Then he studied Kestrel — that long, weighing look, the look of a man who has been lied to by professionals and has learned to hear the frequency of it.

"Your mother taught you to read rivers," he said.

"My mother taught me nothing. I taught myself."

"Mm." He tapped the false channel once. "Here, then. Fleet moves on the final tide, two hours before the siphon opens. You'll ride my lead ship and walk us in."

"Done," Kestrel said.

And shook his hand, and felt the rope burns against their own palm, and thought: *this is what she meant.* Not that power corrupts like rot corrupts wood — slowly, from inside. It corrupts like the river corrupts a bank. It takes exactly what's offered, and a little more, every tide, until one day the ground you're standing on is gone and you're in the current and you can't remember choosing to fall in.

Vess believed them. Kestrel could see it — not fully, not cleanly, but enough. Enough because the offer had been real, and people believe most easily in the things they've already decided to want.

* * *

Afterward, under guard, walking the deck on the pretext of checking the winch lashings, Kestrel passed close to Marisol and spoke without moving their lips, the way river people talk in front of customs men.

"North bluff channel is the lie. True channel stays in your head. Gates on my signal — three long pulls on the winch cable."

Marisol nodded once, slow, a diver's economy of motion. "And your mother?"

"Comes up with the keystone. Whatever else happens, she comes up."

"And you?"

Kestrel looked out at the black hulls riding at anchor, at the floodlamps turning the river the color of old bone, at the crew of the *Cormorant* moving among armed men with their hands loose and their eyes doing arithmetic.

"I ride his lead ship and walk him in," Kestrel said. "Somebody has to be standing on the bank that floods."

Marisol's hand found their shoulder, brief and hard, and was gone before the guards could read it.

Below, in the flooded dark of the lower vault, a woman who had sealed a temple against exactly this kind of man was breathing recycled air and waiting. Above, the moon was going down on the last tide of the season. And somewhere between the two, between the vault and the moon, Kestrel Whitlock stood on a captured deck wearing a borrowed title, and understood, with the cold clarity of deep water, that they had already made the only choice that mattered.

The river would take Vess's fleet at dawn.

Kestrel just had to make sure they weren't standing in its way when it did.
