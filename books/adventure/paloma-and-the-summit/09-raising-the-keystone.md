## 9. Raising the Keystone

The last tide of the season came in gray and mean, the river running high with storm-melt from the headwaters, and Bo Halvorsen stood at the winch controls with his hands shaking so badly he had to press them flat against the console to make them stop.

He was an engineer. Engineers did not shake. Engineers calculated load tolerances and safety margins and failure points, and every calculation Bo had run since dawn told him the same thing: what they were about to do would break something. The patched rig. The siphon gates. Possibly all of them.

"Talk to me, Bo." Kestrel stood at his shoulder, hair tied back tight, eyes on the water where Marisol's dive light was a green ghost forty feet down. "Numbers."

"Winch drum's holding at eighty percent rated load," Bo said. "Cable splice is the weak point — Vess took our good line, so we're running on the salvage cable I spliced with ferrules meant for half this weight. If it parts under strain, the keystone drops back into the vault and takes the rig with it."

"And if it holds?"

Bo watched the readout climb. Sixty-one percent. Sixty-three.

"Then we've done something no engineer should ever be asked to do," he said, "and I'll buy everyone drinks for a year."

Kestrel almost smiled. Almost. Then the radio crackled and Marisol's voice came through, breathless and tinny: *"I'm at the collar. Keystone's seated in its cradle — she built it to lift, Bo. Counterweights are still true after seven years. Your mother-in-law-to-be was good."*

"She's not my—" Bo started, and gave up. There wasn't time. There had never been enough time since they'd found the camp with the tea still warm in the pot.

"Lift on my mark," Kestrel said. "Marisol, clear of the collar?"

*"Clear. And Kestrel — the lower vault. I can see movement down there. A light."*

Everything on the deck went still. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

"That's her," Kestrel said quietly. Not a question. A person who had spent seven years learning to read riverbeds instead of letters knew how to read a light in the dark.

"Then we don't miss this tide," Bo said, and threw the switch.

* * *

The keystone came up out of the dark like a sleeping animal waking.

It was a single carved block the size of a fishing skiff, granite shot through with quartz veins that caught their floodlights and threw them back in fractured stars. The old counterweight system groaned as it took the load, and Bo rode the winch with one hand on the brake lever and the other on the pressure gauge, calling numbers like a prayer: sixty-eight, seventy-one, seventy-four —

The splice held. He could hear it holding — a faint singing note in the cable, metal stretched to the edge of its nature and refusing, out of sheer stubbornness, to fail. Bo decided in that moment that he loved that cable more than he had ever loved any machine, which was saying something.

"Seventy-nine percent," he called. "She's moving clean. Kestrel — the gates."

Kestrel turned to the second console, the one they'd spent three nights building from salvaged hydraulics and Bo's own retooled gate mechanisms. Upstream, half-hidden in the narrows, the false channel waited — a dead-end cut that looked, on any sonar screen, exactly like the main salvage route. They'd spent two tides carving it wider, deepening it just enough that a loaded salvage barge would read it as safe harbor.

And Vess was coming. They'd heard the fleet's engines before dawn, three black hulls running lights-out up the channel, riding the same final tide because Vess was too greedy to wait for the next season and too proud to believe a crew of misfits could beat him to it.

"He'll commit when he sees the keystone break the surface," Kestrel said. "He won't be able to help himself. Seven years of patron money sunk into this site, and the prize floating right in front of him."

"And when he commits—"

"The gates open. The siphon does the rest." Kestrel's jaw was set. "The river keeps what the river keeps."

Bo thought about saying something — about the fact that opening those gates while men were still aboard was drowning, plain and simple, whatever else it was. But then he remembered the parley flag, and the way Vess had smiled when he mentioned Kestrel's mother like she was a ledger entry, and the cut cable lying in the mud where his fleet had left it. Vess had come here to strip the temple and silence witnesses. Every soul on this boat was a witness.

"Marisol," Kestrel said into the radio. "Get to the surface. Now. Once the keystone clears the collar, everything downstream of the gates goes."

*"Copy. On my way up."* A pause, static chewing at the edges. *"Kestrel — the light in the lower vault is moving toward the shaft. She knows. She's trying to reach the air pocket before the flood hits."*

Kestrel went white around the mouth. Bo saw it happen — saw seven years of guarded nothing crack open all at once.

"How long?" Kestrel asked him.

"If the keystone clears and the gates open on schedule? Ninety seconds before the surge reaches the lower vault. Maybe less."

"And the air pocket?"

Bo ran it fast, ugly math. "Big enough to survive in. If she reaches it."

"Then I'm going down."

"You're *what*—"

But Kestrel was already stripping off their jacket, already hauling the spare rig — the one with Bo's patchwork regulator, the one rated for exactly none of this — off its rack. Marisol broke the surface thirty yards out, coughing, waving off Bo's reaching hand.

"Kestrel, the protocol—" Bo started.

"To hell with protocol." Kestrel stepped onto the dive platform, mask in hand. "You raised me by numbers, Bo. Tell me one number that says she makes it without me."

Bo ran it. He couldn't find one. The air pocket was reachable; the timing was survivable; but the flooded shaft between here and there was a labyrinth of collapse-lines and seven-year-old sediment, and a stranger to that maze would drown in it nine times out of ten.

Unless the stranger had spent their whole life reading water.

"Ninety seconds," Bo said hoarsely. "Go."

Kestrel went.

* * *

What happened next, Bo would reconstruct afterward from instruments rather than memory, because memory failed him completely.

The keystone broke the surface at 14:41, streaming water and quartz-light, and the moment it cleared the collar the whole wreck shuddered beneath them — a deep structural groan rolling up through the hull of the boat, through the soles of their boots, as if the temple itself had exhaled after seven held breaths. His mother-in-law-to-be — fine, fine, Marisol could call it whatever she wanted — had engineered her seal to open outward. Lifting the keystone hadn't broken the temple. It had unlocked it.

At 14:42, the lead ship of Vess's fleet entered the false channel. Bo watched it on the sonar overlay: a black wedge driving confidently into water that got shallower and narrower with every length, committed now, turning back costlier than pressing on. Behind it, the second ship. Behind that, the third.

At 14:43, Kestrel's dive light vanished into the shaft.

"Open the gates," Marisol said. She was dripping on the deck beside him, hand on his shoulder. "Bo. Open them."

His finger hovered over the switch. Three ships. Dozens of crew. Men who had chosen to follow a captain who cut cables and burned camps and talked about witnesses like problems to be solved.

"They'll have seconds to get clear," Bo said. "The channel bends before the gates. Anyone who reads the water—"

"Vess doesn't read water," Marisol said. "He reads ledgers. Open them."

Bo opened them.

The sound was enormous. The siphon gates — ancient stone valves older than any nation on the map, reawakened by Bo's jury-rigged hydraulics — swung wide, and the river changed its mind about where it wanted to go. The false channel, starved of current for centuries, took the full weight of the flood tide all at once, and the water in it dropped six feet in ten seconds, then twelve, then it simply stopped being a channel and became a throat.

On the sonar, Bo watched the first black wedge slew sideways, engines screaming against a current no engine could argue with. It struck the channel wall and rolled. The second ship tried to reverse and was dragged backward past the bend, into the narrows, into the rocks. The third — smaller, faster, captained apparently by someone with better instincts than loyalty — actually made it out, a black shape fleeing downstream with its wake standing straight up behind it.

The rest went into the throat. The river swallowed them whole, and kept swallowing, and then the surge hit the wreck itself and the whole valley floor shook.

"Kestrel," Bo said into the radio. Nothing. Static. "Kestrel, come in."

Nothing.

He stared at the depth gauge, at the clock, at the place where the dive light should have been, and for the first time in twenty years of engineering Bo Halvorsen found himself with no numbers at all — just a name, and a prayer he didn't believe in, and his thumb hovering over the reserve air release that would spend their last bottle on a maybe.

Then the radio crackled.

*"Air pocket,"* Kestrel said, ragged, half-drowned, laughing or crying or both. *"We're in the air pocket. Both of us. Bo — Bo, she's here. She's real. She's asking if you're the one who fixed the regulator, because she says the workmanship is terrible and she wants to know who to yell at."*

Bo sat down hard on the deck plates. Marisol was shouting. Somewhere upstream, the river was still roaring through the gates, carrying Vess's ambition out to sea in splinters.

"Tell her," Bo managed, "that the workmanship is beautiful, and she can yell at me when she's topside."

* * *

They came up an hour later, on the slack water, hauled hand over hand up the guide line by every soul on the boat. The woman who surfaced first was thin and sun-browned and wore seven years of cave dust like a second skin, and she paused at the ladder to look up at the sky as if confirming it still existed.

Dr. E. Halloran. Bo knew the name from a hundred footnotes. He'd never expected to meet her hanging off a dive ladder, coughing river water and glaring at his regulator.

"This is your patchwork?" she rasped, holding it up.

"Yes, ma'am."

"It's held together with hope and ferrules."

"Yes, ma'am."

She looked at him a long moment, and then — impossibly — she smiled, and it was the same smile, the exact same crooked smile, that Kestrel wore whenever they pretended not to be pleased.

"Good," she said. "Hope and ferrules. That's the only thing that's ever held this family together."

Kestrel surfaced behind her, hauling their mother's arm over their shoulder, and for a moment nobody spoke. The keystone floated beside the boat in its sling, quartz-veined and dripping, the treasure of a drowned temple bobbing in the gray light like a piece of the moon that had fallen in the river and decided to stay.

Bo looked at it, and at the empty channel where a fleet used to be, and at the two Hallorans clinging to each other on the dive platform, and he understood suddenly that they had lost nothing today. The treasure was ceded, the patrons would burn, the report would be filed by people whose names deserved to be on it.

"Bo," Kestrel said, looking over their mother's shoulder at him. Their face was wrecked and lit from inside. "Get us home."

Bo put his hands on the wheel — steady now, perfectly steady — and turned the bow downstream.

Behind them, the river closed over the temple like a book, keeping what it kept, and giving back, for once, more than it had taken.
