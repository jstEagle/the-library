## 10. A Name of Their Own

The river authority's office in Corren's Landing smelled of ink, mildew, and the particular staleness of rooms where important things were filed and forgotten. Kestrel had been in buildings like it before — signing dredging contracts, thumbing receipts for salvage rights they'd never intended to honor. The paper always won. The paper always told you what you were worth before you'd said a word.

But this morning the paper was theirs.

It lay on the long oak table in three bound copies, each thick as a brick: *Report of the Upriver Salvage Expedition, Site Designation Paloma-1*. Four hundred pages. Bo had spent two weeks typesetting it in a fever of precision that bordered on religious, correcting decimal points at two in the morning by lamplight while Marisol slept curled in a chair beside him like a cat who had decided, permanently, that this was her hearth now. The diagrams alone were worth framing — cross-sections of the temple wreck rendered in Bo's immaculate hand, every collapse-line Kestrel's mother had laid seven years ago drawn in red ink and annotated: *intentional. see appendix L.*

Appendix L was her.

Dr. Sera Whitlock sat at the far end of the table, thin as a winter reed still, her hands steady only when they held a pen. The doctors said the flood-water exposure would take months to fully shake; the river had nearly kept her, and everyone in the room knew it. She wore a borrowed cardigan that hung off her shoulders and she had not once, in six weeks, tried to fill the silences between herself and her child with anything but patience. It was, Kestrel was learning, the hardest thing she could have done.

"Page one," Bo said, sliding the first copy toward Kestrel. "Authorship block. We left it blank."

Kestrel looked down. Under the expedition title, under the site designation, under the formal recitation of findings — temple wreck intact, keystone recovered and ceded, salvage channel collapsed per contingency plan Delta — there was a line of white space and beneath it, printed small:

*Filed by the undersigned crew.*

Four lines. Four signatures waiting.

"You're the digger," Marisol said. She was sitting sideways on her chair, boots hooked on the rung, a bruise-yellow scar still fading along her forearm where the siphon had taken its toll. "Tradition says the digger signs first. I read it somewhere. Possibly I invented it just now."

"It's true," Bo said solemnly. "I have a book."

"You do not have a book."

"I will acquire a book to support this claim."

Kestrel laughed — short, surprised out of them — and picked up the pen.

The nib scratched. That was all. Ink on paper, the same motion they'd made ten thousand times on manifests and wage ledgers, contracts signed with someone else's name for someone else's profit. But their hand hesitated above the line anyway, because the name mattered now in a way it never had before.

Whitlock. They had hated the word for seven years. It had meant an empty chair at supper, letters returned unopened, a childhood spent learning riverbeds instead of a mother's handwriting because the riverbeds at least stayed where you left them. When Vess had spoken their parentage aloud across the parley table — *Kestrel Whitlock, daughter of the woman who found Paloma* — he had thought it a lever. He had never understood it was a wound he was pressing.

And then the cave. Seven years of unsent letters, bundled in oilcloth, each one beginning *To my child, wherever the current has taken you.* Each one explaining, badly and beautifully, why she had gone, why she had sealed the wreck, why love sometimes looks like leaving and silence sometimes looks like cowardice until you read enough of it to know it was neither.

Kestrel wrote the name.

Not the careful, impersonal capitals of a contract. Their own hand — the slanted, quick script learned from tallying dredge loads, the letters slightly too tall, the crossbar on the *t* slashing through like a channel cut through mudflat. *Kestrel Whitlock.* It looked different written this way. It looked like it belonged to them.

Below it, Marisol signed with a flourish that required most of the margin. Bo signed twice by accident and crossed one out. And at the end, after a pause long enough that the rain against the window became the loudest thing in the room, Dr. Sera Whitlock added her own name — smaller than Kestrel remembered it, but unmistakably the same hand that had written seven years of letters into the dark.

"There," Marisol said softly. "Now it's real."

* * *

The hearing itself took less than an hour, which felt like an insult to six tides of terror, but bureaucracy moves at the speed of paperwork and paperwork does not care how deep you dove or what you lost to the water.

The assessor was a dry man named Ondrel with spectacles that magnified his eyes to owl-like proportions. He read the report's summary aloud in a voice like gravel settling: the temple wreck documented in full, photographed, mapped, its contents catalogued. The keystone — the carved heart of the structure, worth more than every building on this street combined — ceded without condition to the river authority, to be housed in the public collection at Corren's Landing, viewable by anyone who cared to make the trip upriver and look at what the water had kept safe for four hundred years.

"And the salvage fleet," Ondrel said, looking over his spectacles at Kestrel. "Captain Vess."

"Captain Vess is dead," Kestrel said. "His ships are at the bottom of the false channel. Three of his crew survived and are cooperating." They slid a second folder across the table — thinner, but dense. "Those are the ledgers we recovered from his flagship before she went down. Names, dates, payments. His patrons weren't buying artifacts for museums. They were buying provenance forgeries and export routes, and at least two of the names sit on your own oversight board."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere outside, gulls argued over the fish market.

Ondrel removed his spectacles, polished them slowly, and put them back on. "You understand," he said, "that naming those names will end careers. Possibly begin prosecutions."

"That was the point."

He studied Kestrel for a long moment — the salt-scoured jacket, the diver's scar at the hairline, the hands of someone who worked for a living — and something in his owl eyes shifted from assessment to respect. "The authority accepts the report," he said. "In full. Including the recommendations." He tapped the last page. "You're proposing a survey of the upper reaches. Beyond the narrows. There are, according to your appendix M, no fewer than eleven unrecorded sites."

"Twelve," Kestrel said. "I found another one while writing it."

Marisol made a sound like a kettle starting to boil, which was her way of not cheering in a government office.

"The authority can fund a proper expedition," Ondrel said. "Vessel, crew wages, equipment. What it cannot do, Miss Whitlock, is find someone to lead it whom we trust. Everyone qualified is either retired, corrupt, or dead." He almost smiled. "You appear to be none of those things, though I'm told you were briefly all three."

Kestrel looked down the table. Bo had stopped breathing. Marisol had stopped pretending she wasn't listening. And at the far end, her cardigan sleeves pulled over her knuckles, Sera Whitlock watched her child with an expression that asked nothing and offered everything — the expression of a woman who had learned, at terrible cost, that you cannot hold what you love by gripping it.

"We'd want the *Paloma* refitted," Kestrel heard themselves say. "New winch cable. Bo gets final say on all rigging — he's earned that. Marisol leads dive operations. And my mother consults on site interpretation. She knows these ruins better than any living person, and the authority would be fools to waste that."

"Done," Ondrel said, "pending board approval, which given recent events may be easier than usual."

* * *

They walked out into the late afternoon together, all four of them, and the rain had stopped and the light came off the river in long hammered sheets of gold. The *Paloma* rode at anchor in the harbor road, patched and repainted, her hull still bearing the pale seam where the black fleet's grapnel had torn her — Bo refused to sand it out. "Scar tissue," he'd said. "Proof the old girl heals."

Marisol peeled off toward the fish stalls, negotiating loudly for eel. Bo followed, arguing about whether eel constituted proper expedition provisions, which it did not, which he would eat anyway. Their voices faded down the quay, easy and bickering and certain of each other, the way voices get when people have pulled one another out of deep water enough times.

Kestrel and Sera walked to the harbor wall and stood looking at the water.

"I used to come here," Kestrel said. "When the letters stopped coming. I'd stand right about here and decide I hated you." A breath. "Took me a cave full of paper to figure out I didn't."

"I know." Her mother's voice was quiet. "I read the ledger of your life in that report, Kestrel. Every contract, every port, every riverbed you learned to read instead of me. You built yourself out of everything I wasn't there to give you." She turned. "I've spent six weeks trying to find a way to apologize for that, and I've concluded there isn't one large enough. So instead I'll say: whoever raised you did it better than I could have, and I intend to spend whatever years I have left learning who you are. Not reclaiming. Learning. If you'll allow it."

Kestrel watched the water. The tide was turning — the sixth tide since the survey approval, though nobody was counting anymore except maybe all of them, always.

"The upper reaches," Kestrel said. "Past the narrows. Six weeks out, maybe more. Sites nobody's recorded. You'd be coming?"

"If you'll have me."

"You'll be sleeping in a hammock."

"I've slept in worse caves."

A laugh escaped before Kestrel could stop it, and it startled them both — warm, unguarded, a sound with seven years of rust scraped off it. Down the quay, Marisol shouted something about eel prices being robbery, and Bo's answering laugh carried across the water, and the *Paloma* swung gently at her mooring like a dog waiting to be let off the lead.

Sera touched Kestrel's shoulder — brief, light, asking permission even now — and went to join the others.

Kestrel stayed a moment longer. In their coat pocket was a folded page torn from the report's spare copy: the authorship block, four names in four hands. They unfolded it once more, just to look. *Kestrel Whitlock*, slashed t and all, sitting above the others like a keystone holding an arch.

Seven years ago they'd learned to read riverbeds because rivers don't leave. Now the river had given back more than it took — a wreck documented, a patron exposed, a mother learning their face again, a crew that had become the thing the word *family* had always promised and never delivered.

Kestrel folded the page away, turned from the gold-lit water, and walked up the quay toward the voices calling their name — their actual name, said like it meant something — already counting the days until the *Paloma* turned her bow upstream.
