## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The storm came in off the flats like a debt collector — which, I suppose, is exactly what it was.

I had her on the scope by four in the morning, a fat green smear crawling northeast across the Kessume basin, spinning up lightning the way the Meridian Optimists spun up interest: steadily, cheerfully, and with total indifference to who got hurt. By five I had the chase rig loaded. By six I was doing eighty on a road that wasn't technically a road anymore, just a suggestion the county had made once and then abandoned, same as it abandoned everything else out here.

Salvage work runs on weather the way loan sharks run on desperation. A storm rolls through, knocks over comm towers, drowns relay stations, peels roofs off automated weather buoys, and somebody somewhere wants that hardware back before the rain finishes rusting it. The fees aren't glamorous. But they're real, and real was the only currency I had left.

I did the math at every stoplight, because there were no stoplights, so I did it against the mile markers instead. Outstanding balance to the Meridian Optimists: eleven thousand, two hundred and forty credits, not counting the "goodwill assessment" Voss had tacked on last month for my "attitude." This run would clear maybe nine hundred after fuel and the cut my insurer took for insuring a woman who chases storms for a living, which he considered either a joke or a calling, depending on how his week was going.

Nine hundred. Against eleven thousand two hundred forty.

That's thirteen more runs. Thirteen storms, minimum, assuming the sky cooperated and nothing broke and nobody else added a fee to my account for breathing wrong. Thirteen more times watching lightning walk across the flats on legs of white fire while I crouched under a salvage rig calculating whether today's take moved the needle or just kept the needle from moving backward.

People think debt is a number. It isn't. Debt is a weather system. It has pressure fronts and seasons. It sits on your chest at night with its whole gray weight and hums. And the Meridian Optimists — God, the name alone should have been my first warning — understood this better than any bank ever had. They didn't want your money early. They wanted you tired. A tired debtor doesn't run. A tired debtor keeps driving toward the next storm, forever, because the alternative is stopping, and stopping means thinking, and thinking means understanding what you actually signed.

* * *

I caught the tower at noon.

It was an old relay mast off the flats near Dry Wren Crossing, folded double by a microburst, its guts spilled across a quarter mile of saltbush. The client wanted the signal array intact. The array was intact. The problem was the array's new neighbors: a colony of scorpions who'd taken up residence in the housing during the night, and a storm cell behind me that had decided it liked the scenery and wanted to stay.

I worked fast. Gloves, pry bar, the little ritual of unscrewing a machine from the earth while the earth reconsidered its position on the matter. Lightning hit the ridge two miles east and the thunder arrived late, embarrassed, like everything else in this valley.

That's when the groundcar pulled up.

It was a Meridian Optimists vehicle, because of course it was. They always found you at work. That was policy, I suspected — never catch a debtor at rest, when she might be dangerous or desperate. Catch her mid-task, hands full, kneeling in the dirt. Catch her owing.

Voss stepped out into the flat white light wearing a linen suit that cost more than my rig's suspension, and he smiled the way he always smiled, which is to say completely, warmly, and without a single muscle above his cheekbones participating.

"Petra Dunmore," he said, like we were old friends at a wedding. "Look at you. Productive as ever."

"Voss."

"You know, most people in your situation are home by now. Resting. Planning." He strolled over, hands clasped behind his back, surveying the fallen tower like a man appraising a garden. "But not you. You're out here wrestling hardware out of a lightning zone. There's something almost admirable in it. Almost."

"What do you want?"

"I want to talk about your future." He crouched beside me, careful to keep his trousers out of the saltbush, and picked up one of the array's mounting bolts, turning it over like a curious child. "Thirteen runs. That's your current trajectory, yes? I've done the arithmetic too. You're very consistent. Consistency is a virtue, Petra. But consistency assumes the sky keeps cooperating. And skies—" he let the bolt drop, "—are famously unreliable."

"I'll make my payments."

"You will. Of course you will. But here's what worries my colleagues." He stood, brushed off his knees, and smiled down at me with all the warmth of a heat lamp. "My colleagues worry that you're the sort of person who might, someday, decide the math doesn't work. And people who decide the math doesn't work do unpredictable things. So we've prepared an alternative. A consolidation. Your debt, transferred to a longer schedule, secured against—"

"My rig."

"Your rig," he agreed pleasantly. "And, well. Everything else, if it comes to that. We find collateral is healthiest when it's comprehensive."

He left the offer sitting there between us like the bolt in the dirt, and drove off waving through the window, cheerful as a man delivering good news.

I sat in the saltbush with the array in my arms and did the math again. Thirteen runs. Or sign the rig away and become one of those people you see in the crossing towns, walking everywhere, working everywhere, owning nothing, smiling at everyone because the Optimists taught them that smiling keeps the assessments down.

I put the array in the truck and drove back toward Dry Wren with the storm grumbling behind me like a creditor of its own.

* * *

Teo was waiting at the fuel depot, which meant Teo had heard Voss was in town, which meant Teo had been listening for it. My brother-in-loyalty — not blood, but the other thing, the thing that's rarer and holds better — sat on the tailgate of his own battered hauler with two cups of something hot and a face like a closed door.

"He was at my shop Tuesday," Teo said, before I'd even parked. "Asking about your routes."

"They do that."

"He asked about your routes, Petra. Not your payment history. Your routes." He handed me a cup. "That's not collection behavior. That's asset-tracking behavior."

I drank the whatever-it-was. It tasted like boiled tires and kindness. "I'm fine, Teo."

"You're thirteen runs from fine. You told me that yourself, three weeks ago, and since then you've added a goodwill assessment and lost half a week to a busted axle. You're fifteen runs from fine and falling backward." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and for once he didn't perform his usual calm. "Run. Take the hauler south. I'll drive the first shift. By the time they organize, you're past the border ranges and they can't—"

"And then what? Live in a border town doing contract freight until the Optimists' friends down there find me? Their reach isn't shorter than my legs, Teo. It's longer."

"Longer legs get tired too."

"Not theirs." I looked out at the flats, where the storm was finally dying, unraveling into long purple curtains of rain over nothing at all. Even the sky out here couldn't afford to keep what it carried. Everything fell eventually. Everything got spent.

Teo was quiet for a while. Then he reached into his cab and pulled out a slate, thumbed it awake, and turned it toward me.

"There's this," he said. "Came through the broker board this morning. Government contract, private channel. Somebody's offering serious money for a recovery job."

I read it twice. *Recovery of equipment and materials belonging to a state hydrographic survey team, last known position: Kessume River headwaters. Team unaccounted for. Equipment presumed intact. Fee negotiable upward.*

"The Kessume headwaters?" I frowned. "Nobody contracts work up there. Half those reaches aren't even surveyed."

"That's what makes the fee what it is." He tapped the number and I felt it land somewhere under my ribs like a dropped tool. It was nearly half the debt. One job. One trip up a river nobody went up anymore, to collect gear from a team that hadn't come back.

"Unaccounted for," I said. "What does that mean, exactly?"

"It means the government doesn't know where its own people are, and it's paying strangers to go look instead of looking itself." Teo watched me carefully. "You know what that usually means."

I knew. It usually meant the government knew exactly where its people were, and the answer was embarrassing, and the contract was less a recovery than a cleanup with extra steps. But it was also half the debt in a single line item. Fifteen runs became one. The math didn't whisper this time; it shouted.

"Petra." Teo's voice went low. "Don't."

"Somebody has to bring their gear home."

"Somebody has to keep *you* home." He caught my arm — gently, the way you hold someone back from a ledge without startling them. "I've watched you trade pieces of yourself for years now. Hours. Sleep. That shoulder. Your pride, when Voss started adding fees. Every piece you trade makes the next one cheaper to sell. This one—" he nodded at the slate, "—this one's priced like it costs a whole person. Because it does."

I looked at the number again. I thought about Voss's smile, and the bolt dropping into the dirt, and the word *comprehensive*, and the way the storm behind me had spent itself completely on empty ground, giving everything to a landscape that never said thank you.

"I'm taking it," I said.

Teo exhaled through his nose, slow, the sound of a man filing something away to argue about later. "Then I'm coming."

"You don't have to."

"No," he agreed, climbing into his hauler and firing the engine. "But I'm coming anyway. Somebody has to carry you home when the math finally breaks."

We rolled north out of Dry Wren Crossing as the light went long and gold, two vehicles and one bad decision, following the river road toward the Kessume headwaters. Behind us the storm's last rain fell on the flats and vanished into salt. Ahead of us the river ran thin and silver in its canyon, going up into country no map bothered to name.

I told myself it was just a salvage run. Gear with no owners, fee with no complications, in and out before the season turned.

I was very good at telling myself things. It was, after all, how I'd signed with the Optimists in the first place.
