## 2. Gear With No Owners

My sister-in-loyalty has a gift for making bad decisions look like navigation. I've watched her do it for eleven years — since the day she married my brother, and then since the day she buried him, which is when "in-law" stopped being a legal technicality and became a covenant neither of us signed but both of us keep. Petra doesn't run from trouble. She walks toward it at a brisk, debt-servicing pace and calls it a shortcut.

So when she said the survey camp was two bends upriver and we'd reach it by midafternoon, I said nothing, because saying nothing is the only argument she's never won.

The Kessume ran low and glassy beside us, the color of old bottle glass, carrying that faint mineral smell of snowmelt that never quite made it downstream. The trail was less a trail than a rumor — cattle paths braiding apart and rejoining, willow stands thick enough to swallow a man if he stopped paying attention. Petra moved through it like she'd been born in it, which she hadn't; she'd been born in a floodplain town with one road out, and maybe that's the same thing.

"You're quiet," she said, without turning around.

"I'm conserving energy for the part where this goes wrong."

"It's a government contract, Teo. Paperwork. We find their gear, we log it, we get paid."

"Government paperwork," I repeated. "From a team that stopped answering its radio six weeks ago."

She shrugged, that economical shrug that meant *I've already done this math and you haven't seen my work.* That was the thing about Petra. She showed her work to storms, not to people.

We came around the third bend an hour early, and I knew before I saw it. You can smell a lived-in camp — woodsmoke, coffee grounds, the sour ghost of human sweat in fabric. This camp smelled like nothing. Like a room where someone had just finished explaining something important and then stepped out.

The tents were zipped. All four of them. Zipped from the outside.

Petra crouched at the first one and ran her thumb along the zipper pull, and I watched her face do the thing it does when the numbers stop adding up — not fear, exactly. Recalculation.

"People don't zip out," she said quietly.

"No," I agreed. "People zip in."

* * *

Here is what I have never told Petra, and what I am telling you now because someone should know: I think she's trying to die with her books balanced.

Not suicide. Nothing so honest as that. But ever since Marek drowned — since the river took him on a salvage run she'd insisted was safe, a run she still calls *the last good contract* — she has been conducting an audit of herself, and every line item comes back owing. She owes the Meridian Optimists money, sure. That's the ledger they show her. But underneath it there's another ledger, the one she keeps in the dark behind her eyes, and in that one she owes a dead man a life, and no collector on earth carries paper for that debt.

So she takes contracts like this one. Contracts with wrongness baked into the terms. A survey team vanishes, and instead of asking *why does the state want its own equipment found by strangers*, she asks *how much does it pay*. Because if the answer is enough, she gets to keep walking upstream, and walking upstream feels like doing something, and doing something is the only prayer she knows.

I love her the way you love a bridge that's been condemned and keeps holding anyway. And I am tired, down in the marrow, of watching trucks cross it.

I stood in that silent camp while she worked, and I did my own math. Four tents, zipped from outside. Four bedrolls, rolled tight. A mess kit drying on a flat rock. A pot of rice, half-eaten, sitting under a mesh screen against flies — and here was the detail that put ice in my stomach, though I didn't say it yet: the rice had gone to mold. Not dried out. Molded. Soft and gray-green at the edges. Which meant water had gotten to it, which meant it had been sitting through rain, which meant nobody had come back for it in weeks.

Nobody walks away from their dinner and stays gone. Nobody zips the tent behind them like a polite guest leaving a hotel.

"Teo." Petra was at the instrument table under the tarp shelter. "Look at this."

A data logger sat bolted to the table, solar panel angled at the sky, screen glowing with a patient green cursor. Still running. Six weeks unattended, and it was still logging — flow rates, turbidity, timestamps scrolling past like a clock that had outlived its owners.

"It's been recording the whole time," she said. "Whoever left left in a hurry, but they set it up to keep working."

"That's not 'left in a hurry,'" I said. "That's 'expected to come back.'"

She looked at me. For a second — just a second — I saw her actually consider it. Consider the camp the way I was seeing it. Then she turned back to the logger, because considering things all the way through is a luxury for people whose debts are denominated in money.

* * *

We searched the rest of the afternoon, methodically, the way you search when part of you hopes to find something terrible just so the terrible thing has a shape. Satellite phone in its charging cradle, battery dead. Boots by the fire ring — three pairs, sized for at least two different people, laces tucked in neat. A shirt on a line, dry as paper. Everything arranged for return. Nothing carried away.

And no tracks.

That was the part that got me. I'm not a tracker — I fix engines, I read weather, I count money I don't have — but even I could see the ground told no story at all. The soil around the camp held deer sign, coyote, the cracked hoofprints of feral cattle. Around the tents, around the fire ring, around the path to the water: nothing. Not scuffed. Not swept. *Absent*, like the ground had agreed to forget them.

"Rain?" Petra offered, but she didn't believe it, and I could hear that she didn't. There'd been rain, but not enough, and rain writes smears, not blank pages.

By late light we'd turned up one more thing, and it was the thing that changed the temperature of everything.

It was wedged under the instrument table — a field journal, canvas-bound, swollen with damp. Torn. Not damaged: torn, deliberately, a ragged vertical rip through the middle pages, the way you tear something when tearing is the point. Petra eased it open on her knee, careful as a surgeon, and read by the last orange coin of the sun.

I watched her lips move. Then I watched them stop moving.

"'Day forty-one,'" she read aloud, slow, like each word cost something. "'Flow readings confirm it. The Kessume is losing volume above the gorge and gaining nothing below. There is a source that isn't on the map.'" She paused. "That's it. That's where the tear starts."

"Source that isn't on the map," I repeated. Rivers have sources. Sources are the one thing about rivers maps never get wrong — cartographers have been arguing about headwaters since before there were cartographers. You don't lose track of where a river starts. Unless somebody very large and very well-funded has decided you should.

"And the rest?"

"Torn out." She turned the ruined stub of pages toward me. "Somebody took the middle of this book and left the beginning and the end. You don't do that to hide information. You do that to keep it."

We sat with that while the dark came up the canyon walls like water filling a basin. Somewhere upstream, too far to see, the river kept running its reduced, rationed course, and the little green logger kept writing down the theft, hour after hour, faithful as a church mouse, waiting for someone to come collect the testimony.

"Petra." I said it carefully. "Walk me through the exit again. Four people, no vehicles, no tracks, meals abandoned, instruments left running, journal torn and hidden under a table. What kind of departure is that?"

She was quiet long enough that the crickets got loud.

"The kind where you don't leave," she said finally. "The kind where you're taken, and whoever takes you tidies up after, except they missed the journal, or they wanted it found, or—" She stopped. Rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Or the person who tore it tore it fast, in the dark, and hid it from both sides."

"Both sides of what?"

"I don't know yet." She closed the journal and held it against her chest the way you hold a kid in a crowd. "But I know the wardens who posted this contract aren't looking for equipment, Teo. Equipment doesn't need finding. It's right here, logging away. They're looking for whatever isn't."

Above us the first stars came out, indifferent and clean, and I thought about how the valley had felt all day — that nothing-smell, that absent ground — and how wrongness works on a person. It starts as curiosity. Curiosity is warm. It feels like adventure, like the opening chapter of something. And then you notice the details that don't fit, one by one, like lights going out in a house you thought was empty, and somewhere in there curiosity curdles into dread, and dread is cold, and dread says *turn around.*

Petra stood up, slung her pack, and faced upstream, where the canyon narrowed into blackness and the stolen river whispered over its stones.

"Camp's here," she said. "We sleep, we move at first light. Somebody tore those pages out because they matter, and I want to know who's missing them."

I looked at the zipped tents, at the molded rice, at the ground that had forgotten four men on command, and I thought: *she's trading her life for pride, and she'll call it navigation.*

But I picked up my pack and followed her anyway. That's the covenant. Neither of us signed it. Both of us keep it.

We'd walked perhaps twenty minutes in the dark when Petra froze, arm out, and I heard it too — a sound from downstream, back the way we'd come. An engine. Low, throttled way down, moving slow along the track we'd used that morning.

Headlights off.

Nobody drives the Kessume track at night with their lights off unless they already know the road — or already know who's on it.
