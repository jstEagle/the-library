## 3. The Cartographer Who Jumped

The woman came out of the reeds at dusk like a rumor made flesh, and I nearly put a bolt through her.

I'd been sitting on the overturned skiff with my crossbow across my knees, doing the thing I do when the light goes long and gold and lying — counting. Not money, for once. Distances. The camp was two miles behind us, its zipped tents and half-eaten meals still logging data to nobody. Teo was thirty feet downstream, rinsing pots in the shallows, humming that tuneless hum he does when he thinks everything is fine, which is how I know he's worried. And the river ran past us, brown-green and patient, carrying whatever it carries toward towns I'd never thought about until two days ago.

So when the reeds parted and a figure stood up out of them — mud to the thighs, a canvas satchel clutched to her chest like a child, hands raised before I even leveled the bow — my first coherent thought was not *who are you* but *how long have you been watching us*, and my second was that the answer was probably *long enough*.

"Don't," she said. Her voice was hoarse, like she'd been swallowing river water for days. "Please don't. I've been following your smoke since morning."

"Following our smoke." I kept the bolt trained on the center of her chest. Center mass; Teo taught me that, back when we were family instead of whatever we were now. "That's either very brave or very stupid."

"It's hungry." She said it flatly, no self-pity in it, and something in me — some stupid soft hinge I keep meaning to oil and never do — creaked. "Three days. I ate my last ration yesterday. I saw the fire and I thought: either they're surveyors, or they're wardens, or they're collectors, and of those three, surveyors are the only ones who might feed me before they decide what I am."

"And if we're none of those?"

Her eyes went to the crossbow, then to my face, and she did the arithmetic faster than anyone I'd ever met. "Then you're salvage contractors on the Kessume contract, because you're the only ones dumb enough to take it."

Teo chose that moment to come around the bend with the pot dripping in his hand, took in the scene — strange woman, raised hands, my bow — and said, with the impeccable timing that has defined his entire life, "Is there soup?"

* * *

Her name was Ilsa Vannik, and she was, by her own account, the cartographer for the Meridian government's Ninth Hydrographic Survey Team. Which meant she was the person the dead men had trusted to draw the truth, and the reason the truth had a shape at all.

She ate like someone who had forgotten food was possible — slowly at first, then with a terrible efficiency, while Teo hovered with the pot and I sat across the fire doing the other thing I do, which is watch people lie. She wasn't. That was the unsettling part. Every detail checked against the journal we'd pulled from the abandoned camp: the team roster, the handwriting on the supply manifests, the little cartographic shorthand she used without thinking, sketching the bend of the river in the dirt with one finger while she talked, the way some people drum their fingers.

"You're the one who jumped," I said.

"I'm the one who didn't stay to be disappeared." She set down the bowl. "There's a difference, and the difference matters to me. The others stayed. They believed the state wouldn't touch its own survey. They were wrong by about six hours."

"Six hours?"

"The interval between Dr. Amara sending his report to the regional office and the warden patrol arriving at our second camp." Her voice didn't shake. It got quieter, which is worse. "They told the team they were being relocated for their safety. The team went. All except me. I'd seen the patrol's vehicles before — same make, same plates as the ones that escorted a land-claims delegation through the reserve two years ago. I knew what 'relocated' meant. So I walked into the marsh with the field journal and I tore out the pages that mattered, and I burned the rest so it would look like nothing was worth taking, and I have been walking south ever since, eating things I'd rather not describe."

"The pages that mattered," Teo said carefully. "Being what?"

She looked at him, then at me, and I watched her make the decision — watched it happen, actually, the way you watch someone step off a ledge. She reached into the satchel and drew out a waterproof sleeve, and inside it, folded twice, were eight sheets of survey paper gone soft at the creases from handling.

"Being this," she said. "The Kessume doesn't start where every map says it starts. We proved it. The headwater gauge readings, the flow differentials, the sediment cores — the river's true source sits inside the Karavelle Closed Reserve. Military ground. Off every civilian chart. And somebody has been building inside it for two years. Intake works. Pumping infrastructure. Enough capacity to—" she paused, choosing the word, "—to redirect a river."

The fire popped. Downstream, an owl asked its idiot question and got no answer.

"That's why the camp was zipped up," I said slowly. "That's why the instruments were still running. They weren't lost. They were—"

"Silenced. Yes." Ilsa folded the pages away. "And now you know, and I'm sorry for that, because knowing is the dangerous part. There are people paid to make sure these pages never reach a printing press or a magistrate, and you've just spent an evening feeding the woman who carries them."

I should mention here that I owed the Meridian Optimists eleven thousand four hundred marks, that their collector Voss had smiled at me three weeks ago and said the word *final* in a way that made my teeth ache, and that the salvage contract in my pocket — recover the survey team's equipment, deliver it intact, payment sufficient to clear the debt with room left over for a new alternator and maybe, someday, sleep — did not specify what I should do if the equipment turned out to be evidence of murder.

It also did not specify what I should do if the woman carrying the missing half of that evidence looked at me across a fire with the particular exhausted clarity of someone who had already decided to die walking rather than live kneeling, and asked me nothing at all. That was the part that got under my skin. She didn't ask for help. She'd just told us the truth and let it sit there between us like a blade on a table, point toward whoever wanted to pick it up.

"We should sleep in shifts," Teo said, which was his way of saying *this changes everything and I love you and I'm frightened*, and we did.

* * *

The collectors came at moonrise.

I heard them before I understood them — a sound like the river clearing its throat, but wrong, rhythmic, mechanical. Then the far bank lit: two long-range lamps sweeping the reed beds, and beneath them, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who are paid whether the work takes an hour or a week, four figures in gray weather-cloaks.

I knew the walk. You spend enough years owing money and you learn to recognize the walk of the people who collect it. That was Voss's walk. That was the Optimists' walk, all the way out here, eighty miles past the last place they had any legal business being.

"Petra." Teo's hand closed on my shoulder in the dark. He'd seen them too.

Ilsa was already awake, already rolling the pages into the sleeve, already moving with the economy of someone who had practiced this exact moment alone in the marsh for three days. "Skiff?" she said.

"Leaking."

"Then we swim the narrows and take the old towpath downriver. They'll expect us to run upstream, deeper into the country they control. Nobody runs downstream, toward the towns, toward witnesses."

"Nobody runs downstream," I said, "because downstream is where the debt lives."

"Downstream," Ilsa said, "is where the proof lives too. Pick one."

What I want to tell you, honestly, is that in that moment I weighed it. I want to be the kind of narrator who says she didn't hesitate, but I did. Four collectors, one leaking skiff, a stranger with stolen government property, and my brother-in-loyalty looking at me in the dark waiting to find out what kind of person I'd decided to be this month. Eleven thousand four hundred marks sat on one side of the scale. Eight soft-folded pages sat on the other. And the truly obscene thing — the thing I still haven't fully forgiven myself for — is that for almost three full seconds, the scale balanced.

Then one of the lamps swung across the water and caught the reeds where we'd been sitting, and the arithmetic stopped being abstract.

"Narrows," I said. "Now."

We went into the water together. It was colder than the season justified, the kind of cold that feels personal, and the current took us sideways immediately, the way rivers do, indifferent to your plans. Teo swam strong and silent beside me. Ilsa swam like she'd been born in floodwater, which I later learned was approximately true. Behind us the lamps swept, and a voice — amplified, cheerful, horribly familiar — carried across the water with perfect clarity:

"Petra Dunmore! Meridian Optimists! We just want to talk about your account!"

Voss. Of course it was Voss. The man probably slept with the amplification horn.

We came out on the towpath side soaked to the sternum, and Ilsa hauled us both up the bank with hands like mooring cable, and then we ran — not the panicked sprint of prey but the grinding, meter-eating jog of people who understand that tonight is going to be long. The towpath followed the river through cut rock, old haulage country, and the dark was total except for the lamps behind us blooming and fading with every bend.

At the first mile marker Ilsa stopped, pressed herself into the rock shadow, and held out the waterproof sleeve.

"Take it," she said.

"What? No—"

"If they catch me, they catch the pages. If they catch you, they catch a debtor with a salvage contract and nothing incriminating. Split us up and the evidence survives one of us. That's not trust, that's engineering."

And there it was again — that ledger-brain of hers, turning loyalty into load-bearing structure. Teo looked at me. I looked at the sleeve. Somewhere behind us, the cheerful voice called my name again, closer now, and I heard in it the exact tone Voss had used over my kitchen table: *we can make this easy, Petra.*

"Keep your pages," I said. "If we split the evidence, we split the story, and a story split in half convicts nobody. We stay together, we move together, and when this goes somewhere that matters, all three of us are standing there saying it." I started jogging again. "Besides, you eat like a starving woman and navigate like a born one. I'm not giving that up."

Behind me, in the dark, I heard Ilsa laugh once — short, surprised, rusty, like a hinge finally oiled.

We ran the towpath until the lamps fell behind the last bend, and then we ran longer, because none of us believed in them yet. When we finally stopped in the lee of an old lockhouse, lungs burning, Teo wrung out his shirt and said what we were all thinking:

"They knew where we were. Tonight, exactly. That's not luck."

"No," Ilsa said quietly. She was looking back upriver, toward the black line of the valley, and her face in the starlight had gone very still. "Someone told them. Someone who knew our route." A pause. "Or someone who's been ahead of us the whole way — and there's only one place ahead of us now."

"The reserve," I said.

"The reserve," she agreed. "Where the river really comes from. Where the answers are. And where, if my reading of these people is right, they're hoping we'll run — because a grave dug on military ground stays dug."

The river muttered past in the dark, heading downstream toward towns that drank last and knew it not, carrying our secret along with everything else it carried. I looked at the two people flanking me — one who'd lied to me for a decade and come back anyway, one who'd known me four hours and handed me the most dangerous paper in the province — and understood that whatever happened next, the knife-edge we were balanced on had just become the only solid ground available.

"Upstream rules, then," I said, and turned toward the valley.
