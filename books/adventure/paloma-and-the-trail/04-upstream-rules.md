## 4. Upstream Rules

The road ended at a rusted gate with a sign that had been shot more times than it had been read, and Ilsa said, "We walk now," in the tone of someone announcing the weather.

Petra looked at the canyon ahead — a brown seam in the earth where the Kessume went to think about its problems — and then at her boots, which had opinions about this plan. "How far?"

"Six days to the reserve boundary. Seven if we're careful."

"And if we take the road around—"

"The road has checkpoints," Ilsa said. "The river doesn't."

I'd been listening from the truck with my arms crossed, doing the math I always did: fuel, food, distance, odds. The math said the road was faster. The math also said faster meant seen, and seen meant Voss's collectors or the wardens, and both of those meant the same thing wearing different uniforms. So I uncrossed my arms and started repacking the bags into something a human spine could tolerate, because apparently that was my job now: making bad plans survivable.

"Teo," Petra said, watching me work. She does that — watches me instead of helping, like supervision is a contribution.

"I heard her. Checkpoints. I'm not arguing."

"You look like you're arguing."

"I'm always arguing. It's just usually internal." I cinched a strap. "Get your pack. We're leaving the truck for whoever steals it next."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about walking a river through canyon country: the river is the only honest thing down there, and even it lies by omission. The walls rise on both sides until the sky is a rumor. The water talks constantly — over gravel, against stone, under ledges — and after two days you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as speech. That was Ilsa's doing. She'd appointed herself our translator.

"Stop," she said on the second morning, holding up a hand before Petra took another step into the shallows. "What do you see?"

"Water."

"Congratulations. What else?"

Petra squinted. I watched her actually try, which for Petra is an act of enormous discipline. "It's... fast there. Slow along the far bank."

"Why?"

"Because—" Petra stopped. I could see the machinery turning, the storm-chaser brain engaging, the one that reads pressure gradients and cloud bases the way other people read menus. "Debris fan. Something came out of that side canyon and pushed the channel left."

"So what does the water know that you don't?"

"That the side canyon floods." Petra crouched at the edge of the wet gravel, touching the stones. "And recently. These are still sorted — fine stuff on top. A week? Two?"

Ilsa smiled like a teacher whose worst student had just done long division. "That's how we mapped it. You don't survey a river. You interview it. Every riffle is testimony. Every cutbank is a grudge."

"She's been waiting two days to say that," I muttered.

"I have," Ilsa admitted cheerfully. "It's a good line."

But here's the thing I noticed, walking behind them while they did their water-witching act: they were becoming a unit. Not friends — nothing so comfortable — but a unit, the way people who've shared a fire and a threat become. Petra would point at a seam in the current and Ilsa would nod and they'd pick a crossing without a word spent on it. Three days ago Ilsa was a stranger Petra was deciding whether to trust. Now Petra trusted her with route-finding, which from Petra is roughly equivalent to handing someone your wallet and your firstborn.

Me, they trusted with the heavy end of the pack. I've made peace with my role. Somebody has to be the mule with opinions.

* * *

On the fourth night the canyon opened into a bowl of red stone, and we made camp early because Ilsa wanted light enough to read the walls. She traced the flood lines with her finger — dark mineral stains banding the rock like tree rings — and narrated years of water history off them the way Petra reads storms. High line, low line, the strange new low line that shouldn't exist.

"This is wrong," she said quietly, on the third band up. "The river's been dropping. Steadily. Two years of it." Her finger stopped. "Right when the survey started asking why."

Petra didn't say anything. Neither did I. Some facts you let sit in the air until everyone's had time to taste them.

We ate cold rice and dried meat, and Ilsa told us about the survey team without being asked — small things, human things. Okafor, the hydrologist, who sang off-key and kept a photo of his daughter taped inside his instrument case. Brandt, who complained about everything and worked harder than anyone. The kid, Reyes, fresh out of training, who'd never seen a canyon before and cried the first night from sheer size of it.

"They were good," Ilsa said, poking the fire. "That's the part people get wrong about why it matters. They weren't heroes. They were just thorough. Thoroughness is what got them killed."

"Is that supposed to make me feel better?" Petra asked.

"No. It's supposed to make you accurate."

I lay back and looked at the strip of stars between the walls and thought about my sister — Petra's late wife, the reason Teo is brother-in-loyalty and not just brother-in-law, though honestly the loyalty outlived the law by years. She'd have liked Ilsa. She'd have told Petra exactly what I keep not saying: that pride is a currency too, and Petra keeps trying to pay her debts with it, and pride doesn't clear accounts. It just accrues interest.

I didn't say it. I'm saving it. There'll be a night when it's the only thing left to give her.

* * *

We smelled the second camp before we saw it.

Day five, mid-afternoon, the wind shifted and brought us char and something underneath it — chemical, sweetish, wrong. Ilsa went flat against the canyon wall and put her hand out, and we all stopped, three statues in the noon heat, while she listened to the river the way she'd taught Petra to.

"No smoke," she whispered finally. "Old burn. Days old. But stay off the skyline anyway."

We came around the bend single file, hugging the shadowed wall, and there it was: the second survey camp, tucked into a gravel bench above the waterline, burned to its skeleton. Tent frames gone black and brittle. Melted aluminum pooling in little silver tears where instruments used to be. A cooking pot fused to a rock. Everything that burns, burned; everything that doesn't, smashed.

Nobody spoke. There wasn't anything to say that the place wasn't already saying louder.

Petra walked the perimeter the way she walks a storm cell — reading edges, looking for the rotation, the thing that tells you how bad it really is. I followed, because following Petra into places that should scare me has become a hobby I never chose.

"No tracks out," she said finally. Same words as the first camp. "No drag marks. No vehicle ruts past the bench." She turned a slow circle. "They didn't leave, Teo. They were taken, and the taking was tidy."

"Tidy people leave paperwork," I said, because gallows humor is my love language.

As it happened, the tidy people had. It was Ilsa who found it — or rather, found what was left of it. At the center of the ash, where a mess table had stood, someone had built a small fire of a different kind. Paper ash, dense and curled, the ghost of documents burned in a pile. And sitting on top of the ash heap, placed there — placed, not dropped, the placement deliberate as a signature — was a metal badge the size of a coin.

Warden insignia. The river-and-ridge crest of the state survey authority, stamped in brass, scorched black on one face.

Ilsa picked it up with a stick, turned it over twice, and set it back down with a care that frightened me more than the badge itself. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite.

"That's Marlowe's," she said. "Deputy warden. He visited our second camp for 'safety inspection.' He ate dinner with us. He asked Okafor about his daughter." She looked up, and I saw what two days of canyons hadn't shown me: she wasn't running from the wardens anymore. She was hunting them, quietly, the way water hunts the low ground. "He left this so anyone who came looking would understand. It's not evidence. It's a message."

"What's the message?" Petra asked, though I could hear in her voice she already knew.

"That thoroughness gets answered." Ilsa wiped her hands on her trousers like the badge had made them dirty. "They want whoever follows this trail to know exactly who did it and exactly what happens next. They're not hiding. Hiding is for people who think they might be caught."

The wind moved down the canyon, carrying river-smell and ash-smell together, and I understood something with total clarity: we were being invited. Upstream. Toward whatever the wardens guarded hard enough to kill for. Every step we'd taken since the gate had been a step someone expected us to take.

"We can still turn around," I said. "River forks below the narrows. Downstream's four days to open country."

Petra picked up the badge. Turned it over the way Ilsa had. Then she pocketed it — of course she pocketed it, she pockets everything, evidence, grief, other people's fates — and looked upstream to where the canyon narrowed into a slot of blue shadow.

"Okafor's daughter is going to grow up thinking her father ran out on her," she said. "Unless somebody finishes his survey."

And that was it. No vote taken, no argument won. Ilsa shouldered her pack. I shouldered mine, heavier than hers by design, and we started up toward the narrows, three people who owed each other nothing, giving each other everything, walking into a message that had been left for us like bait on a hook.

Behind us, the burned camp kept its silence. Ahead, the river climbed toward its source, and somewhere up there, past the narrows, past the reserve line, men with brass badges and tidy habits were waiting to see if we were thorough too.

By nightfall I'd stopped hoping we weren't.
