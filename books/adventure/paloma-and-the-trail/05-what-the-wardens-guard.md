## 5. What the Wardens Guard

They took us at the narrows, where the canyon pinched down to a throat of red rock and the river ran loud enough to swallow footsteps. I'd like to tell you we put up a fight. The truth is that Teo was filling a canteen, Ilsa was sketching the confluence on the back of a ration wrapper, and I was doing what I do best — staring at water and doing math — when four figures stepped out of the shadows wearing gray field uniforms and the patient expressions of men who had been waiting all day for exactly this.

"Petra Dunmore," said the one in front. He had a warden's insignia stitched at his shoulder and a face like a man who'd been told to smile in a training seminar and had studied the instructions carefully. "You've been hard to find."

"That's generally the point of running," I said.

My hands went up without my brain's permission. Some part of me — the part that had spent three years calculating interest on borrowed money — understood before the rest of me did that this was not a moment for cleverness. Behind him, two wardens took Teo's canteen out of his hand with a courtesy so precise it felt like violence. The fourth checked Ilsa's satchel, found the journal pages wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, and looked at his commander with an expression that said *found it* without wasting breath.

The commander — his name tag read HALVERSEN, which struck me as far too Nordic for a man about to ruin my life — gestured up-canyon. "Walk," he said. "Talk later."

We walked. That's the thing nobody tells you about capture: it's mostly hiking, just with worse company and a growing certainty that the trail ends somewhere you won't like.

* * *

The warden camp sat in a box canyon that didn't exist on any map Ilsa had ever drawn or stolen, which by now I understood was the whole theme of this valley. Two canvas structures, a solar array, a comms mast disguised as a dead tree — someone had spent real money making sure no satellite would ever get curious. There was even a cold box with actual ice in it, which in canyon country is a bigger flex than any weapon.

Halversen sat me down at a folding table across from him and had someone pour me water from a steel pitcher. Actual clean, cold water. In a valley where people downstream were drinking mud, they gave me a glass of it like it was nothing. Like it grew on trees.

"You're in debt," he said. Not a question.

"To the Meridian Optimists," I said. "Everyone's in debt to somebody. What's yours?"

He smiled the seminar smile. "Mine is to the state. It's a cleaner arrangement. The state doesn't charge interest; it charges obedience." He folded his hands. "Here's what happens next, Ms. Dunmore. You give us the pages. You give us the cartographer. You walk back downriver, and on your way, a call gets made. Your account with Meridian is settled. Every cent. You wake up tomorrow owning your own life again."

I want to be honest with you, because Petra Dunmore being honest with herself is the only kind of honesty she's ever managed: for about four seconds, I was gone. Four seconds of pure arithmetic. No more Voss smiling at me across tables. No more storm runs until my bones filed formal complaints. No more lying awake doing division in the dark. Four seconds where the answer was *yes* and my mouth was already shaping around it.

Then I thought about the survey camp. Tents zipped shut. Meals half-eaten. Instruments still logging data to an audience of nobody. Eight people who had also, presumably, been offered something reasonable once, and who had declined it, and who were now a line item in some ledger I hadn't seen yet.

"What happened to the survey team?" I asked.

Halversen's smile didn't move but something behind it did, like weather behind glass. "An administrative matter."

"They left their dinner on the table."

"Ms. Dunmore." He leaned forward, and for the first time he dropped the seminar voice and used his real one, which was quieter and much worse. "Do you know what you've been carrying up this river? Do you understand what those pages describe?"

"A diversion," I said. "Somebody's drinking the Kessume upstream and letting the towns downstream go dry."

"Somebody." He almost laughed. "The Kessume feeds eleven municipalities, four agricultural zones, and a power grid serving two million people. Two years ago, flow allocations were revised. Quietly. Legally. At the highest level of government there is — above the level that signs things, into the level that decides things. The reserve you've been sneaking toward isn't hiding a crime, Ms. Dunmore. It's hiding a *policy*. There is no one to report this to. Reporting it would be like reporting gravity."

"So why send wardens after a cartographer?"

"Because policy requires maintenance." He spread his hands, reasonable as a banker. "A map is a small thing. A map with the true source marked on it is a crack. Cracks propagate. All we ask is that you stop being a crack."

I looked at the water in my glass. Cold, clear, stolen. Somewhere downstream a kid was probably drinking what was left over after the pumps had their fill, and here I was holding the good stuff in my hand, being offered a second good stuff: a debt erased, a life returned, in exchange for handing over a woman who trusted me approximately as far as she could throw me and had still handed me her life's work anyway.

"How do I know you'll honor it?" I asked. "The debt clearing."

"You have my word."

"Your word. From the administration that erased eight people."

Something flickered across his face then — fast, quickly smoothed — and I realized I'd guessed wrong. They weren't dead. Or not all of them. Or maybe he genuinely didn't know, and that was its own horror: a man administering a theft so large he'd never had to look at its teeth.

"The offer stands until morning," he said. "Think about what your pride costs. It cost your brother-in-loyalty a great deal already, if I'm reading the man correctly. And it will cost the cartographer everything, because the cartographer is not part of the offer. She's part of the cleanup."

* * *

They put us in separate tents. Mine had a cot, a blanket, and a guard outside whose boots I could hear shifting weight every few minutes. Through the canvas I could hear Teo arguing with his guard in a low, relentless voice — not angry, just persistent, the way water is persistent — and I lay there listening to the river I couldn't see and thinking about Halversen's word *policy*.

That's the trap of it, you understand. A crime has a criminal. You can hate a criminal. But a *policy* — a policy is a smooth wall with no handholds. You can't punch a policy. You can't shame a committee. Somewhere above both Voss and Halversen, in rooms with better chairs, people had decided that two million people with power mattered more than eleven towns with water, and they'd done it with signatures and flow charts, and the river had simply... changed its mind, the way the elders downstream would say it, because rivers don't know they've been repossessed.

And the worst part — the part I keep circling like a storm cell — is that the offer was fair. That's what I couldn't stop chewing on. Voss would have smiled through the same deal and charged me a finder's fee. Halversen just wanted the pages and the woman and wanted me gone, and he'd pay my debts to do it. Clean trade. Everyone walks. Except the eight people who walked into a camp and never walked out, and except Ilsa, who wasn't offered walking at all.

I heard Teo's tent flap open. Boots crossing gravel toward mine. A pause. The guard's voice, low. Then Teo's voice, closer: "Five minutes. She's family. You can watch me say goodnight."

The flap opened. Teo stood there in the lantern light with his hands visible and his eyes doing that thing they do when he's terrified and refusing to announce it. He crouched by the cot like a man checking a fence line.

"Take the deal," he whispered.

"Teo—"

"Petra. Listen to me. Take the deal. Walk out. I'll stay with Ilsa, we'll go north over the ridge, we'll—" He stopped, because we both knew how that sentence ended. Nobody goes north over the ridge. North over the ridge is where maps stop pretending.

"You'd hand yourself back to them to save me from a debt," I said.

"I'd hand myself anywhere to save you from anything. That was the vow. Yours too, remember? Before you decided vows were interest-bearing instruments." His jaw worked. "Four seconds, Petra. I watched your face at that table. You wanted it."

"Four seconds," I agreed. "Then I remembered what happened to the last people who sat at that table."

"And what did happen? Because I've been asking myself that since the first camp. What actually happened to them, Petra? Because if they're alive somewhere — if they signed something, if they took a deal like this one —"

"Then they're living with it," I said. "And I don't think they are. I think the deal changes after you sign. I think the fine print is a shallow grave."

Teo closed his eyes. When he opened them, he was older, the way he got older in single moments instead of gradually, like everyone else.

"The guard changes shift at second bell," he said quietly. "There's a gap. Twenty seconds, maybe less, while they swap the lantern. Ilsa's already working the tie-down on her tent stake — she's been working it since they brought us in. If you're taking the deal, tell me now, and I'll sleep, and in the morning we walk out and I never think about this valley again as long as I live."

I looked at him. My brother-in-loyalty, offering to carry my freedom on his back into a country with no maps, if I'd only let him.

"Halversen said the cartographer is part of the cleanup," I said.

"I know what he said."

"He said it like a man reading a supply list."

"I know." Teo exhaled slowly through his nose. "So that's it, then. That's the answer."

"That's the answer."

He squeezed my shoulder once, hard enough to mean it, and stood. At the tent flap he paused, silhouetted against the lantern, and said, so softly the guard outside probably heard nothing:

"Second bell. Don't be the reason I break another vow."

The flap fell. I lay back on the cot with the stolen-cold taste of that water still in my mouth and listened to the river running past a camp built to hide it, and I counted the minutes until second bell the way I used to count the miles between me and solvency — except this time the math finally came out simple.

One debt. One cartographer. One twenty-second gap in the lantern light.

I'd run tighter margins than that for less.
