## 6. The Town That Drinks Last

I have spent my professional life making maps, which is to say I have spent it lying in a very disciplined way. A map says: here is the river, here is the road, here is the town, all of it fixed and certain and permanent. The truth is that everything moves. Rivers wander. Roads rot. Towns die slowly enough that no one draws the line around the corpse.

So when we turned downstream instead of up — against every instinct I had earned in twenty years of reading water — I told myself it was reconnaissance. Due diligence. Petra called it "checking our six," Teo called it "a waste of two days," and all three of us were lying, because none of us could stop thinking about what the wardens had said to Petra behind that checkpoint tent, and none of us wanted to say it out loud yet.

The village was called Sarnholt, if it appeared on maps at all, which mostly it didn't. It sat where the Kessume widened into braids — or used to widen. We came down off the benchland in the late afternoon and I stopped walking before I understood why.

The channel was wrong.

I don't mean low. Low I've seen. Drought does that, seasons do that. I mean *wrong* — the way a face is wrong when someone you know has been ill for months. The braids had collapsed into a single thread of water barely wide enough to wet your boots, running gray-green over mudflats cracked into hexagons like old varnish. Dead reeds stood in rows along banks that had clearly once held a proper current, marking where the waterline used to live, a good meter and a half above where it did now. Willows along the cutbank, dead from the top down, their crowns bare while their trunks still tried to sprout green at the root collar — drowning in reverse, dying of thirst with their feet in mud.

Petra crouched at the edge of the flat and pressed her palm into the silt. "This isn't seasonal," she said. Not asking me. Telling me, so I wouldn't have to be the one to say it.

"No," I said. "Seasonal drop comes back."

Teo stood a little apart, arms crossed, looking upstream as though he could see through forty kilometers of canyon to whatever was eating the river. He'd been quiet since the checkpoint. Quiet in the specific way of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

We walked into Sarnholt past a pump house with its door standing open and its machinery silent, and I want you to understand that an open door in a working settlement means something. Closed doors mean privacy. Open doors mean nobody expects anyone to come, including the people who own them.

* * *

They fed us anyway. That's the thing about places like Sarnholt — hospitality outlasts abundance by years, the way a lamp burns after the oil should be gone. An old woman named Maret set out flatbread and tea made from something that wasn't quite tea, and she apologized twice for the saltiness of the bread, and I realized only later that the apology wasn't about baking. Wells go brackish when the aquifer drops. Salt is what thirst tastes like when it's been living in the ground a while.

There were maybe sixty people left. There had been four hundred. Nobody said this directly; you read it in the empty foundations, in the schoolhouse with grass growing through the floorboards, in the fact that Maret's guest cups were dusty until she wiped them, meaning guests were rare enough to require dusting but common enough to keep cups for.

It was the elders who talked, in the end. Three of them by the well head in the evening — Maret, and a stooped man called Dorin who had been the pump operator, and a third whose name I never caught because he spoke only twice and both times everyone went quiet.

"We used to argue about the river," Dorin said, with the fondness men reserve for dead friends. "Flood year, dry year, where the cutbank would go next. Whole evenings. Now there's nothing to argue about." He turned his cup in his hands. "Two years ago, in the spring, the river changed its mind."

"Changed its mind," Petra repeated carefully.

"That's how we said it at first. Like it was thinking something new." He shrugged. "Dropped a hand's width overnight. Then again. Then the wells started going salty, one by one, from the east side first. We sent to the district office. They sent a survey team." His mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "Nice young people. Measured things. Asked good questions. Said they'd come back with answers."

Maret looked at her hands. "They never came back," she said, "and the answers never came either. But the letters did. Official ones. Saying the decline was natural variation. Saying we should consider relocation assistance." She said *relocation assistance* the way you'd say the name of a disease.

I kept my face still, because I knew exactly when the river had changed its mind. Two years ago, spring. The diversion intake went operational — I had drawn the cover documents myself, before I understood what I was covering, before I understood that a cartographer can be an accessory. The date matched to the season. To the month. Sitting there in the dust of Sarnholt, hearing an old man describe my own paperwork as weather, I felt something in my chest that I'd been carrying since I ran, finally take on weight and heat. Abstract injustice is a phrase you write in reports. This was an old woman apologizing for salty bread.

Petra asked them questions — good ones, gentle ones, the kind that get people talking without feeling interrogated. How many wells still worked. What they planted now versus before. Whether anyone downstream of them had it worse. (Worse, they said. Much worse. The tail-end towns drank last; Sarnholt at least had the aquifer's memory.) And through it all Teo stood at the edge of the firelight saying nothing, watching Petra with an expression I couldn't read, and I thought: *he's counting too.* Everyone in this story counts. The syndicate counts money. The wardens count bodies. Teo counts the cost of loving someone reckless.

* * *

It was near midnight when he finally said it. I was half-asleep against my pack on Maret's porch when I heard him shift, and then his voice, low, pitched for Petra alone — except porches carry sound, and I have excellent hearing, a professional habit.

"The deal," Teo said. "You're going to tell me about the deal."

"There's nothing to tell."

"You spent forty minutes behind that canvas with a warden captain. You came out smiling. You haven't smiled since the flats. So either he told you a joke that ruined your life, or you made a bargain, and I know which one, because I've watched you make bargains your whole life and you always come out of them smaller."

Silence. A nightbird somewhere down in the dead reeds, making a sound like a hinge.

"They offered debt clearance," Petra said at last. Flat. The voice she uses for weather reports. "Full discharge. Meridian Optimists wiped clean, Voss out of my life, Teo, *out*. All I have to do is give them the pages and point them at Ilsa."

I didn't move. I'm not sure I breathed. Somewhere inside me a small cold voice noted that she had just said it aloud — *point them at Ilsa* — and that the warden captain would be very pleased to know how cheaply that sentence had been purchased.

"And you said?"

"I said I'd think about it."

"*Petra.*"

"What do you want me to say? That I refused?" Her voice cracked upward, and for the first time since I'd met her I heard the exhaustion under it, the whole exhausting math of her survival laid bare. "You know what my number is, Teo. You've known it longer than I have. Fourteen more runs. Fourteen more storms, fourteen more salvage fees, fourteen more chances for Voss's collectors to find me in some drainage ditch with my throat open. They offered me zero. Zero runs. Walk away whole. And I stood there in that tent and I wanted it so badly I couldn't speak, and the worst part — the *worst part* — is that it's a fair offer. It's fair. That's what makes it filthy."

Teo was quiet a long moment. When he spoke again his voice had gone soft, which with Teo is more dangerous than loud. "You know what I did today? I sat with those elders and I listened to a man describe his river dying and I thought: this is what the number looks like. Your fourteen runs. Their four hundred people. Somebody did the same arithmetic about them that Voss did about you, and decided they were worth less than the sum. And you're sitting here telling me you might sell the only proof that exists — the thing those surveyors died holding — to the men who killed them, so you can walk out of a debt."

"It's my *life*, Teo."

"It's your pride wearing your life's clothes. You've been trading pieces of yourself since Halloran died and calling it survival. Well, I've done the math too, and here's mine: you take that deal, and in five years you'll be clear-eyed and solvent and you'll hate yourself so thoroughly that being debt-free won't matter, because you'll owe something worse than money."

I heard Petra exhale, long and shaking. "And if I refuse? If I keep the pages and push upstream into a military reserve with a defector and a brother-in-loyalty who's already given up everything for me once? What do I owe then?"

"Everything," Teo said simply. "But the right things."

Nobody spoke after that. The nightbird sang its hinge-song. I lay in the dark with my pack under my head and the journal pages — *my* pages, the ones they'd kill me for, the ones they'd buy Petra's freedom with — pressed flat against my chest inside my coat, and I understood that I had just heard the terms of my own life debated over my sleeping body, and that the woman arguing to save me had also just admitted she wanted to trade me.

And I found — this is the strange part, the part I keep turning over — that I wasn't afraid of her. Because wanting a thing honestly, out loud, in the dirt, and then choosing against it, is worth more than never wanting it at all. I've known powerful men who never once admitted what they wanted. They're the ones who built the intake.

* * *

We left Sarnholt at dawn. Maret pressed a cloth bundle into my hands — flatbread, and a clay flask of well water, the last of the sweet well, she said, the east one having gone salty. "For the trail," she said. "And if you find where our river went—" She stopped. Elders don't finish sentences like that. Hope is expensive and they've learned to budget.

Dorin walked us to the edge of the dead reeds. He pointed upstream, to where the Kessume thread vanished into the canyon country, toward the closed reserve marked on no map I ever drew honestly.

"Two years ago it changed its mind," he said. "Rivers don't change their minds. Something changed it. Find the something."

We climbed the benchland as the sun came up, and behind us Sarnholt shrank to a scatter of roofs around a silver thread too thin to be called a river. Petra walked fast, jaw set, not looking back. Teo watched her, and I watched both of them, and I thought about the flask of sweet water riding against my hip, heavy as testimony.

We were ten kilometers out, cresting the first ridge back into canyon country, when Petra stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into her.

Down in the draw below, half-hidden in the shadow of an overhang, sat a vehicle. Syndicate make. Clean tires. And leaning against its fender with his hands in his pockets, smiling like a man arriving at a friendly lunch, was Voss.

"Well," he called up to us, cheerful as morning. "I do hope you enjoyed the sightseeing. Because I've driven a very long way to talk numbers."
