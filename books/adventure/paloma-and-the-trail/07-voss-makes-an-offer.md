## 7. Voss Makes an Offer

Voss caught us at the worst possible moment: when we were comfortable.

That's how he works. I'd learned that much in three years of dealing with the Meridian Optimists. They don't strike when you're desperate — desperation makes people stupid and fast. They wait until you've eaten something warm and slept six hours and started to believe, just for a morning, that things might work out. Then they sit down across from you like old friends at a funeral, and they make everything sound reasonable.

We'd come down off the canyon rim to a place where the Kessume spread wide and shallow over pale stone, a mile of braided water that looked almost gentle if you didn't know what it was supposed to be carrying. Ilsa said the surveyors had called it the Table because from above it looked set for dinner. We made camp early for once. Teo actually fished — actual fish, actual fire — and for about two hours I let myself forget that I was a woman with a price on her head being chased by two separate organizations with overlapping interests in my silence.

I was rinsing the pan in the shallows when I heard him.

"Petra Dunmore." That voice. Warm as bathwater, patient as erosion. "You look well. Debt agrees with you."

He was standing on the bank with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring scenery, his two collectors flanking him at a respectful distance — close enough to matter, far enough to seem casual. Voss himself never carried anything heavier than a pen. He didn't need to. The pen was for contracts, and contracts were worse than guns, because guns only kill you once.

Teo was on his feet with the fishing rod still in his hand, which would have been funny under other circumstances — my brother-in-loyalty, defending me against an accountant with a stick. Ilsa had gone very still by the fire, her hand drifting toward the satchel where the pages lived. I saw Voss see it too. He saw everything. It was his whole personality.

"Voss," I said. I kept rinsing the pan. Petty defiance, but you take your victories where the river gives them. "You're a long way from the office."

"I'm a long way from a great many comfortable things," he said, stepping down onto the stones, choosing his footing with the care of a man who considered wet rock a personal insult. "But then, so are you. Shall we skip the part where we pretend this is a coincidence? You know why I'm here."

"The pages aren't for sale."

"They are," he said pleasantly, "for exactly what they cost you. That's my offer. That's always been my offer. I confess I don't understand why we're having this conversation in a river valley instead of my office, but I'm adaptable."

He sat down on a flat stone near the fire like he owned it. Maybe he did. At this point I wouldn't have been surprised to learn the Meridian Optimists held the mineral rights to the entire watershed.

"Here is the arithmetic," Voss said, and I hated him a little for using arithmetic, because arithmetic was *mine*, it was the language I thought in, and hearing it in his mouth felt like someone reading my diary aloud. "Your principal stands at forty-one thousand, six hundred marks. Interest accruing. Salvage fees, storm runs, the contract you took from the ministry — none of it has moved the number meaningfully in fourteen months. You know this. You do the math every night; I've seen your ledger pages when we repossessed the truck." He smiled at whatever my face was doing. "The pages in that satchel are worth more than the number. To certain parties, considerably more. So: you give me the pages, I tear up the ledger, and everyone walks away dry. No violence. No collections. No more of this—" he gestured at the canyon walls, the cold river, all of it, generously including the sky "—unpleasantness. Your brother goes home. The cartographer can go wherever cartographers go. Everyone eats dinner tomorrow night in a warm room."

"And the surveyors?" Ilsa said quietly. She hadn't moved from the fire. "Do they eat dinner?"

Voss turned his head toward her with the mild interest of a man noticing weather. "The surveyors," he said, "made different choices."

* * *

Here's the thing about Voss's offer, the thing that made it dangerous: it was fair.

Not generous — Voss had never been generous in his life, generosity was a rounding error he refused to carry — but *fair*. The pages were worth more than my debt. Anyone honest could see it. And the deal came with no hooks that I could find, no interest deferred into new debt, no clause where I owed him a favor later. Just clean paper. He even had the paper with him. Of course he did.

I stood there in the shallows with a dirty pan in my hands and did the math I'd been doing since the flats, the math that had organized my whole life: how many runs to clear the debt, how many years, how many storms, how much of me left over at the end. And here was a man offering to zero the column. One signature. Walk away whole.

Forty-one thousand reasons to say yes.

And Teo — I could feel him behind me, wanting it. Not for himself. That was the awful part. Teo had never wanted anything for himself in his life except maybe that I'd stop trying to buy my freedom one storm at a time. He wanted it *for me*, and love makes people stupid in ways that greed never manages, and I watched him start to open his mouth—

"Don't," I said, without turning around.

"Petra—"

"You were going to offer him a side deal." I said it flat, not angry, because anger would have been a lie. "Don't. Whatever it was. Don't."

Silence behind me. The river doing its patient work on the stones.

"He was going to take the pages and let you go," Teo said finally. "Just you. He told me before you came down to the water. He said — he said you'd die out here, Petra. For paper. For other people's dead. And he was right, wasn't he? Look at where we are. Look at what's chasing us. If I trade him the pages while he's got them distracted, you live. That's the whole transaction. You *live*."

"And the town downstream drinks dust," Ilsa said.

"And the town drinks dust," Teo said, and his voice cracked on it, and I understood that he knew, that he'd known since the burned camp, that he'd been carrying both ledgers — mine and theirs — and mine weighed more to him because mine had a name he loved on it. "Yes. And I am allowed to want my sister more than I want justice. That's the one thing I get to want."

* * *

Voss watched all of this with the contentment of a man whose plan is working. Because it was working. That was the real design of it — not the offer to me, but the offer to Teo, seeded earlier, alone, the way these things are always done. Divide the loyal. Make the rescue itself the betrayal. I had to give him credit; somewhere in the Meridian Optimists' onboarding materials there must be a seminar.

"Let me tell you what I know about those surveyors," I said.

"Petra," Voss sighed, "you know nothing about them. You found their laundry."

"I know their tents were zipped." I set the pan down on the stone. My hands were steady, which surprised me. "I know their meals were half-eaten. I know their instruments were still logging, because whoever took them didn't care enough about the equipment to turn it off — or wanted it to keep recording, so anyone who came looking would think they'd walked out. I know there were no tracks leading away, which means they didn't walk out. I know the second camp was burned with a warden's insignia in the ash. And I know that the man who wrote the field journal — the one whose pages your employers want destroyed — wrote *source that isn't on the map* in his own handwriting, and then he died for it. Or worse than died. Do you know what worse than died is, Voss? It's being erased. It's your government deciding you were never there. Every map, every record, every name struck. These weren't mercenaries. They were surveyors. They measured water for a living. And the last thing they ever did was measure something somebody powerful didn't want measured."

Voss's smile hadn't moved. It was bolted on. But something behind his eyes had gone very still, the way water goes still over a deep hole.

"That's very moving," he said. "It doesn't change the number."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't change the number. Here's what changes the number." I looked at him straight on. "For three years I've been paying you in storms. In salvage. In blood pressure and broken gear and nights sleeping in the truck. And I used to think that was the price of being stupid at twenty-six, signing with cheerful men in good suits. But standing here, doing the final arithmetic — I finally understand what those pages cost. Not to sell. To *carry*. Nine people carried them until it killed them, and the carrying was the whole job. Nobody paid them. Nobody thanked them. The pages just needed someone to hold them long enough to matter." I wiped my hands on my trousers. "So no, Voss. I'm not selling them. And it's not heroism, so don't put it in your report as heroism. It's bookkeeping. Somebody has to balance that account, and everyone else who tried is dead."

For the first time in our entire acquaintance, Voss looked at me like I was a line item he couldn't categorize.

"The offer expires," he said, rising, brushing stone dust from his coat. "Tonight. After tonight, the collectors stop being polite, and I should tell you — the wardens have independently concluded you're worth more silent than talking. You'll have two hunts on you instead of one. Think about the math, Petra. You always think about the math."

He turned to go, and that's when Ilsa stood up from the fire and said, in a voice like a blade coming out of a sheath:

"The intake is real. We've seen it. And by tomorrow night, so will everyone else."

Voss paused. And I swear on the Kessume itself, for half a second, the smile fell off his face like a sign in a storm — and underneath it was not anger.

It was fear.

Then it was gone, smoothed over, filed away, and he walked up the bank between his collectors without looking back, and the river went on pretending to be gentle over the Table.

Teo wouldn't meet my eyes. I didn't make him. There'd be time — upstream, in the dark, in the reserve we were about to break into — for the account between us too.

I picked up the pan. "Pack it up," I said. "We move tonight. Ahead of them."

Because Voss had just taught me something, free of charge, the last lesson of my debt: scared men check their watches.

And his had started counting down.
