## 8. The Forbidden Source

The fence was the first honest thing we'd seen in weeks. It didn't pretend to be anything but what it was: government property, keep out, by order of the Interior Directorate. No smiling Meridian Optimists letterhead, no friendly payment schedules. Just wire and warning and a hundred kilometers of nothing behind it.

"You'd think they'd want people to know," Ilsa said, crouching at the base of the post with her clippers. "All this effort to hide it. It's almost flattering."

"It's not flattery," Petra said. "It's an admission."

I'd been listening to them trade lines like that for six days, since the burned camp, since the warden patrol let us walk away with Petra wearing a lie like a coat she couldn't take off. Teo's job — my job — was to carry the water and watch the ridgelines and say nothing about how every hour we walked deeper into the reserve, the odds of walking back out got worse in ways I could feel in my teeth, the way you feel weather coming.

We cut the fence at dawn because Ilsa said dawn was when the night shift got stupid and the day shift hadn't arrived. She'd been right about everything else on this river. Water reading, route finding, which canyons were dead ends dressed up as shortcuts. A cartographer who jumped ship rather than be silenced turns out to know exactly where the silencers live.

* * *

We came over the last ridge an hour after sunrise, and I stopped thinking in words for a while.

You have to understand what we expected. Some pump shed. A few pipes. Something you could point at and say, there, that's the crime, that's the wound. What we found instead was a city that had been taught to hold its breath.

The Kessume came down out of the high country the way it had come down for ten thousand years, wide and green and loud with snowmelt — and then it simply stopped being a river. It dropped into a concrete throat the size of a cathedral nave, grated over, fenced, floodlit, and vanished underground. Below the intake, the riverbed continued for two hundred meters, damp and embarrassed, before it gave up entirely and became cracked stone going nowhere.

Beyond it, built into the canyon wall like it had grown there, stood the pumping station. Six stories of raw concrete disguised as geology, painted the exact brown-gray of the cliffs, vent stacks hidden behind false rock formations. I'd spent my life around storms, around weather that rearranged horizons, and I knew awe when it hit me. This was awe. And underneath it, rising slow and cold, was the other thing.

"They buried a river," Petra said quietly. "They buried a whole river."

"Two years ago," Ilsa said. Her voice had gone flat, which I'd learned meant fury. "The date matches. The elders were right. The river didn't change its mind. Someone changed it for them."

Through my binoculars I counted contractors moving along the catwalks above the intake — maybe thirty, forty, in gray coveralls with no insignia at all, which is its own kind of insignia. Armed. Relaxed. Men who had never once worried about being seen.

One of them was laughing at something. That was the detail that stuck. Not the guns, not the scale, not the sheer engineering arrogance of stealing a river inside a mountain. The laughing. Because somewhere downstream, an old woman was hauling water in buckets from a hole in the ground that gave less every week, and up here a man was laughing on his coffee break atop the thing that was killing her town.

Awe curdles fast, if you feed it right.

* * *

Ilsa photographed everything. That was the plan, such as it was: get close, document, get out, broadcast. She worked down the ridge on her belly through the scrub with a camera older than Petra's debt, shooting the intake, the station, the pipe corridor running south toward the closed valley where all that water actually went — some agricultural concern, some syndicate-backed empire of green fields in a desert that had been told to stay a desert.

Petra covered her. I watched the road.

And I'll tell you what nobody tells you about witnessing a crime this size: it makes you small. Voss and his cheerful arithmetic, the wardens and their tidy offers — I'd hated them for hunting us. Up here I understood they were fleas. The thing itself didn't care about us at all. You could erase three surveyors off the face of the earth and the pumps wouldn't stutter. You could erase four more of us and the river would keep flowing into that concrete throat, obedient as a paid employee, forever.

Unless somebody made it stutter.

"Teo." Petra's voice, low, close. "Road."

Dust plume, south approach. A supply truck, and behind it a warden-green SUV moving too fast for routine. Ilsa slid the camera into its case and we went to ground, and that was when I heard it — faint, mechanical, wrong — a sound from the station like a door that had never been opened deciding to open.

The alarm wasn't for us. Not yet. But the SUV stopped at the gate anyway, and men got out fast, and fast men mean information moving faster than you.

"We're blown," I whispered.

"Not yet," Petra said. "But we're done being careful. New plan. We go down the maintenance cut on the north side, get inside the pipe gallery, shoot everything Ilsa needs from under their feet, and we're gone before they finish whatever meeting they're having."

"That's not a plan," I said. "That's a dare with extra steps."

She looked at me, and there it was — the thing I'd been afraid of since the abandoned camp, the thing I'd never said out loud. She wasn't doing this to survive anymore. Somewhere between the burned tents and the thirsty town, surviving had become secondary, and pride had gotten promoted, or maybe it wasn't pride, maybe it was the other thing, the thing that looks exactly like pride from the outside and isn't. I still don't know what to call it. I just know she smiled, and I followed her, because that's what I do, and because some part of me had decided that if she was going to spend her life, I was going to be standing next to her when she spent it.

* * *

The maintenance cut was a slot in the rock barely wider than my shoulders, descending past humming transformer banks into the guts of the station. The noise down there was enormous — the whole stolen river roaring through conduits overhead, pressed into pipes, forced to go where it was told. Standing inside it felt like standing inside a lie so big it had its own acoustics.

Ilsa shot the pipe manifolds. She shot the control room through a window — rows of consoles, screens showing flow rates in numbers so large they looked like typos. She shot the nameplate on the primary pump housing, and when she read it she went very still.

"This manufacturer," she said. "It's the same supplier as the survey team's instruments. Same procurement chain. They bought the equipment that mapped the river and the equipment that killed it from the same catalog." She took the picture. "That's a thread someone can pull in a hearing."

Then the lights changed.

Not off — color. Every fixture in the gallery shifted from white to a deep amber, and somewhere far above us a siren began, patient and unhurried, the sound of an institution that has all the time in the world and all the guns.

"Lockdown," Petra said. "They know."

We ran.

* * *

You don't fight in a place like that. You survive it. The gallery became a corridor became a maze of junction chambers where the river thundered past behind grating, and the contractors came down the access stairs in pairs, calm and methodical, and the first shots rang off concrete and screamed away into the roar of stolen water.

Petra returned fire the way she does everything — economically, without drama, buying meters. I hauled Ilsa by the pack straps through a low service tunnel while she kept the camera case strapped tight to her chest like a child. A round spalled concrete an inch from my face and I tasted dust and old stone and kept running.

At a junction chamber, Petra pulled us behind a pump housing thick as a chapel pillar and did her math out loud, because math calms her the way prayer calms other people.

"Two exits. South stair is where they came from. North overflow channel runs back toward the riverbed — it'll be gated but gates are just fences with opinions." She reloaded. "Ilsa. Did you get enough?"

Ilsa checked her camera with shaking hands. "Enough to end careers. Enough to end the syndicate, if anyone downstream lives long enough to read it."

"Then we leave."

We made the overflow channel under covering fire, Petra putting rounds into the stairwell with a calm that scared me more than the shooting did. The gate at the channel mouth was chained, and I put my crowbar against it and thought, absurdly, of Voss's smile, of interest compounding, of every debt I'd ever watched Petra carry, and I broke the chain on the second swing because some debts you settle with tools.

Daylight. The dry riverbed. We came out of the mountain into sun so bright it felt like a different jurisdiction, and behind us the sirens kept their patient time and vehicles were already moving along the rim roads, cutting off the canyon mouths one by one.

Ilsa clutched the camera case and laughed once, high and ragged. "I have it. All of it. It's real, it's documented, it exists now—"

"Then they can't let us leave," Petra said. She was looking at the rim, at the dust of the converging vehicles, doing her terrible arithmetic one more time. "Every gun in this valley is about to be pointed at three people carrying the only copy of the truth."

"There's more than one copy," Ilsa said slowly. "There will be. If we reach a relay."

Petra turned east, toward the high country, toward the ridge relay station we'd marked on Ilsa's old maps days ago and prayed we'd never need.

"Then we climb," she said. "Right now. Before they finish closing the box."

We ran up the dry throat of the dead river, three small figures fleeing a cathedral of theft, and everywhere along the rims, dust plumes turned to follow us home.
