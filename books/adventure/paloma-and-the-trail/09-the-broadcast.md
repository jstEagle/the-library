## 9. The Broadcast

The relay station sat on the ridge like a tooth that had outlived its jaw — a concrete box with a lattice tower bolted to its spine, solar panels furred with dust, and a door whose lock had rusted into decoration sometime before I was born. Ilsa had found it on her own maps, the ones the state didn't know she'd memorized. *Every valley has a throat,* she'd said back in the canyon country, teaching me to read water. *And every throat has a way to scream.*

We climbed to it at dusk, Teo carrying the data drive wrapped in a sock inside his boot because he trusted no pack strap, no pocket, no place a knife could reach without waking him. Below us the Kessume Valley went gold and then bruise-purple, and somewhere down there in the dark was an intake station big enough to swallow a river, and somewhere behind us were wardens, and somewhere ahead of us — because Voss's people were never where you wanted them, only where it hurt — were collectors.

I put my hand on the relay station's door and it swung open like it had been waiting for me.

"Charming," I said.

"Don't jinx it," Teo said.

Too late. The interior light came up on a man sitting on a supply crate with his ankles crossed and his hands folded, wearing the Meridian Optimists' idea of field attire: a good coat, clean boots, and a smile you could bank against. Voss himself. He'd come up the other side of the ridge while we were climbing this one, which told me everything about how long he'd known where we were going.

"Petra Dunmore," he said warmly, as if we were old friends at a funeral. "You look terrible. Altitude agrees with nobody."

Behind him, filling the doorway of the equipment room, stood two of his collectors with the patient posture of men being paid by the hour. Ilsa stopped in the entrance behind me, and I felt Teo shift his weight — one step from running, one step from fighting, the eternal Teo dilemma.

"How did you—" I started.

"The wardens sold your route for a favor," Voss said. "Everyone sells something, Petra. It's the one honest market left." He gestured at the transmitter console with its dead amber screens. "That's why I'm here instead of them. The wardens want pages burned. I want something better. I want this to end *cleanly*."

"Cleanly," Teo repeated, flat.

"For everyone." Voss stood, unhurried, brushing dust off his coat. "Here is my final offer, and I want you to hear all of it, because I respect you enough not to bury the terms. You give me the drive and the journal pages. Your debt — all of it, every run, every fee, every interest calculation my accountants have been enjoying — is erased. Not paid. *Erased.* You walk out of this valley with nothing owed to anyone. Your brother-in-loyalty walks free. And the cartographer—"

"No," Ilsa said from the doorway.

"—the cartographer comes with us," Voss finished, gently, the way you'd mention weather. "She defected from a government survey. There are questions only she can answer. She'll be treated well. Better than the wardens would treat her, certainly."

"And the surveyors?" I asked. "The ones who didn't get to be treated well? Okafor. Lindqvist. The kid, Reyes, who was twenty-three and logged water temperatures because he thought someone would read them someday."

Voss's smile didn't move but something behind it did, a small recalibration. "Those were decisions made above my level. I collect debts, Petra. I don't dig graves."

"You just know where they are."

"Everyone in this valley knows where they are. That's the tragedy of it." He spread his hands. "Think about what you're holding. A drive full of numbers. Numbers don't feed you. Numbers don't clear six years of salvage runs at ruinous rates. Numbers got four people killed already, and the only thing they'll get you is what they got the surveyors — except you won't even get the dignity of vanishing, because I'm standing here offering you the exit first. That's generosity. That is me, standing in a cold room at the top of a mountain, being generous."

He was right, and that was the worst part. That was the whole rotten worst part. Six years I'd done the math — how many storm runs to cover principal, how many salvage fees to cover interest, how many seasons left in my body and my rig before the math stopped working. I'd taken this contract because the number finally exceeded my courage. And here was a man offering to zero it out. All I had to do was hand over the only thing in the world worth more than my freedom, and hand over the woman who'd carried it through canyon country on blistered feet.

Teo touched my elbow. Just that. In our family's whole vocabulary of loyalty, that was the sentence: *whatever you choose, I'm standing in it.*

I looked at Ilsa. She wasn't begging. She'd watched her team disappear tent-by-tent, meal-by-meal, and she'd run anyway, carrying paper, because paper was the only weapon nobody could confiscate from her hands fast enough. She met my eyes and gave me the smallest nod — not *save me*. Something else. *Whatever you do, mean it.*

And that's when I understood it, really understood it, standing in a room with my debt on the table: the pages hadn't cost the surveyors their lives because they were valuable. They'd cost the surveyors their lives because they were *true*, and truth is the one commodity that appreciates when you try to bury it. Every person who died for these numbers had made them heavier. If I traded them now, I wouldn't be erasing a debt. I'd be spending four lives to buy my own comfort, at a discount.

I'd spent six years doing survival math. This was the first equation that actually balanced.

"Voss," I said. "Do you know what the difference is between you and me?"

"Several million credits."

"I owe money. You owe *people*." I turned to the console. "Teo. The drive."

* * *

The transmitter woke up grudging, the way old machines do, screens stuttering from amber to green while Voss talked — and he did keep talking, that was the thing about Voss, he believed every human being had a price and that the price was always negotiable downward under pressure. "Petra. Petra. Think about the interest alone. Think about what happens to Teo if you're wrong. Think about—"

"Broadcasting," Ilsa said, and started reading coordinates off the journal pages in a voice like she was reciting scripture, because to her, maybe she was.

The relay station's antenna array was built to push agricultural telemetry and river-gauge readings to downstream towns. It had range. It had bandwidth. What it did not have was any security whatsoever, because who hijacks a water gauge? I slotted the drive. Ilsa had spent three nights in the second camp's ashes organizing it: intake schematics, pump capacity logs, diversion volumes dated to the day two years ago when the river "changed its mind," contractor rosters, warden correspondence, photographs of a tunnel mouth big enough to drive a truck into, cut into the reserve's bedrock where no map admitted anything existed.

"Last chance," Voss said, and for the first time since I'd known him, the cheerfulness thinned enough to show the machinery underneath. "You broadcast this, and there's no deal anywhere that covers you. Not mine, not the wardens'. You will be the most expensive loose end in the province."

"Yeah," I said. "I've done the math."

I pressed transmit.

* * *

It takes a few seconds for a signal to become a story. Long enough for Voss to say, very quietly, "Take the drive," and for his collectors to move, and for Teo to put himself between them and the console with the particular stillness he gets when he's decided how a fight ends before it starts.

But the signal was already gone. That's the beautiful, terrible physics of broadcasting: you can't recall light. Somewhere downstream, in a newsroom in Corren Falls, an editor's queue lit up with attachments. In Halloway Bend, where the wells had failed and the elders remembered exactly when the river changed its mind, a duty clerk's terminal began printing documents nobody had sent her. On three networks and two independent feeds, files with government headers began unpacking themselves like eggs hatching.

Voss's collector reached past Teo — or tried to. Teo caught his wrist and introduced it to the console edge, and the room became briefly, professionally violent, two hundred kilos of hired muscle discovering that a cornered brother-in-loyalty is a fixed obstacle, not a variable. I kept transmitting. Ilsa fed me page after page. Voss stood in the middle of it with his coat dusted and his composure cracking along fault lines he'd never let anyone see, watching his leverage turn into headlines in real time.

"You stupid woman," he said. "You've destroyed your own ransom. Nobody will ever pay for you now. Do you understand? You've made yourself worthless."

I hit send on the last file — the contractor roster, names and all — and turned around.

"That's the point," I said. "You can't buy silence anymore. And you can't kill me quietly either, because in about twenty minutes every outlet in the province knows my name, my face, and what I'm claiming, and if I vanish, I *become the story*. I'm too famous to bury, Voss. You should have killed me back when I was cheap."

His collectors had Teo pinned against the rack, and the door banged open behind us — wardens, four of them, rifles first, the patrol lieutenant's face going through recognition, then horror, then the specific blankness of a man whose orders just became evidence. Outside, engines. More than one set. Down the ridge, lights converging from both directions, collectors and wardens racing toward the same little concrete tooth, each side suddenly needing to not be seen next to the other.

Voss looked at the console, at the still-transmitting antenna, at me. And he smiled — smaller now, realer, almost admiring.

"Well," he said. "Now it's interesting."

The lieutenant raised her rifle at all of us equally, which felt about right. The first headlights crested the ridge road. Teo worked his arm free and came to stand beside me, and Ilsa closed the journal like a book finishing itself.

Somewhere below, the Kessume ran dark and stolen through a valley that was about to learn its own name.

"Hold position," the lieutenant radioed, and then, quieter, into the sudden roar of engines coming up the switchbacks: "Everybody's coming. *Everybody's* coming."
