## 10. Walking Out

Ilsa Vann kept lists. It was the cartographer in her — a life spent recording where things were, so that when they vanished, someone could say: *here, they stood here, and this is what it cost.* So she made a list on the third morning after the broadcast, sitting in the hearing room's overflow corridor with her boots still crusted red from Kessume clay.

*What we lost:* two tents, one rifle, Teo's good knife, Petra's storm rig (she'd left it as collateral for the relay station's generator, then blown the generator anyway), any pretense of anonymity.

*What we gained:* forty-one minutes of footage, three hundred photographs, the intake schematics, the names of nine contractors, and the sound of a warden captain saying *sanctioned at the highest level* into a microphone he didn't know was live.

The hearings had been going for six days. Ilsa attended every one, because someone had to sit in the room and watch powerful men learn that water has witnesses. The diversion scheme unfolded in testimony like a wound being unpacked: eleven years of planning, buried under a closed military reserve, feeding irrigation canals to syndicate-backed agro estates while the river downstream forgot its own name. Two years ago the Kessume "changed its mind," the elders had said. It hadn't changed its mind. It had been robbed.

And the survey team — Okafor, Lindqvist, the Maren twins, young Pell with his water-quality kits — they hadn't vanished. They'd been *managed*. That word appeared in an internal memo read aloud on day four, and Ilsa watched a junior clerk in the front row go gray, because everyone understood at once what management meant when the asset was a person who knew too much.

Petra Dunmore testified on day five.

Ilsa had expected her to be hard in that room, all flint and debt-collector arithmetic. Instead Petra sat very straight in a borrowed jacket and told the truth plainly, the way she read weather — no drama, just pressure systems moving in. She described the empty camp with the meals half-eaten. She described Voss's smile. She described standing at the ridge relay with her thumb over the transmit key, knowing that pressing it erased everything she owed and everything she owned in the same motion.

"The Meridian Optimists held my debt," she said. "They offered to clear it for the pages. I want the record to show the offer was fair. That's what made it obscene."

A senator asked her if she understood she'd destroyed her own leverage. Petra almost smiled.

"Senator, my whole life was leverage. Somebody else's. This was the first trade I ever made where I wasn't the thing being sold."

Ilsa wrote that down too. Some lines you map so you can find your way back to them.

* * *

The Optimists fractured faster than anyone predicted. Voss had built his syndicate on cheerful inevitability — the smiling man at your door, the friendly terms, the gentle reminder that everyone pays eventually. But inevitability only works in the dark. Once the hearings started broadcasting his collection methods alongside the water thefts, once the same backers' names surfaced in both ledgers, the whole architecture came apart like wet paper. Three of the estate families disavowed. One fled the country with impressive speed. A fourth hired lawyers who immediately began describing their client as "a passive investor with limited visibility."

Voss himself did not testify. He sent a statement expressing confidence in the process. Ilsa found this funnier than she should have — the collector, collected at last by something he couldn't negotiate with.

But here is what Ilsa's list didn't capture, what no ledger captures: the men Voss had employed didn't all evaporate. Syndicates fracture; collectors scatter like roaches with severance packages. And some of those men had lost pensions, reputations, futures — all traceable to one woman with a transmit key. Petra Dunmore was famous now, which made her too visible to kill quietly, but fame is a riverbank, not a fortress. It erodes. There were already men in the hearing gallery taking notes of a different kind than hers.

* * *

On the ninth morning, the three of them walked out.

It was Teo's idea — "before anyone decides to make us permanent exhibits" — and the state, eager to look magnanimous, provided transport to the trailhead below the canyon country, the same trailhead where Petra had signed the salvage contract that started everything. The irony was noted by all parties and appreciated by none.

The morning was cold and clean, the kind of air that makes you feel briefly solvent. Downstream, past the last ridge, you could hear the river again — really hear it, because two days ago a judge had ordered the intake gates opened pending review, and the Kessume was remembering its width. Ilsa stopped once on the switchbacks just to listen to water running where water was supposed to run. She'd mapped a lot of rivers. She'd never before heard one come back.

Teo walked beside her, quiet in the way he'd been quiet for three hundred kilometers, carrying everything without complaint. At the bottom, where the trail flattened out and the government vehicle waited with its apologetic driver, he stopped.

"So this is it," he said.

"This is it."

He looked up the trail, back toward the valley, then at Ilsa. "You'll send me the corrected maps? When they let you publish?"

"I'll draw you originals. Nobody else gets the first printing."

He nodded, satisfied, and then did something Ilsa hadn't seen him do in all their weeks together: he laughed, short and real. "She's going to keep walking, you know. She told me last night. Says she doesn't trust doors that lock from the inside anymore."

"Where will she go?"

"Upstream, probably." He shrugged. "It's the only direction she trusts."

* * *

Petra was waiting at the vehicle, arms crossed, watching the trail behind them like she expected the past to come around the bend at any moment. Maybe she did. Ilsa had learned that about her — Petra never assumed a threat was finished, only postponed.

"You're staying for the rest of the hearings?" Petra asked her.

"A while. They need someone who can point at places on a map and say *this is what they took.*" Ilsa paused. "Then maybe north. There are other rivers nobody's counting."

"There always are." Petra looked at her for a long moment, and whatever passed between them didn't need narrating — two people who'd extended trust under a knife's edge and found, against all odds, that it held. "Don't lose the habit of jumping," Petra said. "It saved your life twice."

"It saved ours three times. I counted."

"Of course you did." And Petra smiled — the real one, rare as clean water in a dry season.

Then it was just the goodbye, which neither of them made sentimental. Teo hugged her like a brother-in-loyalty, which is what he was, whatever the paperwork said. Ilsa shook Petra's hand, and Petra held it a beat longer than necessary, and said, "You gave up a career for this."

"I gave up a lie for this. Careers I can get again."

Petra climbed into the vehicle alone. Through the window, as it pulled away, she raised one hand — not waving exactly, more like acknowledging a bearing. *Heading known. Course set.*

Ilsa stood in the road until the dust settled, then turned back toward town, toward the hearing rooms, toward the work of making sure the valley stayed on the maps from now on.

Behind her, upstream, the river ran loud and free through country that had learned its own name again.

And somewhere ahead of it, walking by choice, went the woman who'd paid for that freedom in full — hunted, broke, and, for the first time in her life, owing nothing to anyone.
