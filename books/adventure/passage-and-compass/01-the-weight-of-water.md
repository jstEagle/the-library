## 1. The Weight of Water

The Kestrel ran shallow and mean against the current, the way she always did in October, when the river dropped and gave back every lie it had swallowed all summer. Zephyrine Hartwell stood at the wheel with her collar up and her hands bare, feeling the boat talk to her through the wood—every snag, every sandbar shifting since spring, every place where the water had grown tired of carrying its own weight. She had learned to read the river the way other people read faces. Lately it had been telling her nothing but trouble.

The cargo was salt pork, nails, and a crate of lamp oil for the trading post at Miller's Bend, forty miles of gray water upstream. Not much. Enough. She'd been running smaller loads all season, keeping to water that drew less than six feet, water too shallow for the company boats with their deep keels and their paid pilots. The company owned the main channel now, lock and toll and all, but nobody had figured out how to tax a river that didn't want you on it.

She was thinking about this—about the particular pleasure of being somewhere no one wanted to look—when she heard the coughing.

It came from aft, from the cargo deck, a wet ratcheting sound like a pump drawing air. She'd taken no passengers. She'd taken no one at Coyle's Landing except a sack of mail and a boy who'd jumped off again before the lines were cast, white-faced, having remembered something urgent on shore. So there should have been no one aboard but her and the river.

Zephyrine took the wheel spoke in one hand and the boathook in the other and went aft along the gunwale, boots quiet on wet planking, because twenty years on the water had taught her that quiet was worth more than courage.

He was lying behind the stacked cordwood, folded into the shadow between the wood and the rail like something the river had decided to return. An old man in a coat that had been good once, bearded, gray as driftwood, with a canvas satchel clutched to his chest the way a drowning man clutches a plank. His eyes found her the moment she leaned over him. They were clear eyes. That was the worst part. Whatever was killing him hadn't gotten around to his mind yet.

"Kestrel," he said. "You're the Kestrel."

"I'm the captain," she said. "Who told you my boat's name?"

"Everyone knows the Kestrel." He coughed again, and brought his hand away red, and looked at it with mild annoyance, as though the blood were an inconvenience arranged by someone else. "Woman captain. Hartwell. Runs the shallows. I've been asking after you for two months."

"Then you've wasted two months. I don't carry passengers."

"I'm not asking to ride." He worked at his coat with fingers that shook, and she saw he meant to open the satchel, and some instinct older than courtesy made her crouch down beside him rather than stand over him. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and sickness and, beneath both, faintly, of silver—the metallic tang that hung around assay offices and stamp mills, a smell she knew better than she wanted to. "I'm asking to arrive."

"Where?"

"Over the mountains. Silver Pass town. Before the snow closes the road." He got the satchel open. Inside: a letter sealed with plain wax, and a square of oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth something flat and folded that he handled like it was alive. "There's a girl there. My daughter. Her name is Adela Vane. This letter goes to her. You carry it. That's the whole of the bargain."

"And what do I get?"

"Passage," he said. "Safe passage. Anywhere you ever need to go, after this, you'll have safe passage." He laughed, which turned into more coughing. "I know how that sounds. A dying man selling weather. But I mean it differently than it sounds. There are men looking for what I'm holding. If they find me, they'll take it and put me under the ground quietly, and they'll keep looking afterward, because they'll know I talked to someone. The only way you stay invisible, Captain Hartwell, is to become visible first. Do one thing so loud and so public that hiding would look strange. Carry that letter through the pass in front of God and everybody, and no one will believe you're the woman they want, because the woman they want wouldn't be so foolish."

"That's a terrible plan," Zephyrine said.

"It's the only one I've got left." He pressed the letter into her hands. His grip was still strong; dying men are often strong, the body spending everything at once. "And this—" the folded thing, the map, heavy paper crackling inside its oilcloth, "—this goes wherever the letter goes. Don't open it. Don't sell it. Don't show it to anyone with clean fingernails."

"What is it?"

"A map," Elias Vane said, and smiled with blood on his teeth. "You asked what it is. It's a map. I never said what it maps."

* * *

She buried him at Miller's Bend, because the trading post had a spade and the ground was soft near the willows and because, whatever else he'd been, he had died on her deck, and a captain owes her dead that much. The post's factor watched from his porch and asked no questions, which was why she traded there. She marked the grave with river stones and stood over it longer than she meant to, the satchel hanging from her shoulder heavier than any cargo she'd carried all year.

That night, moored in the willows downstream, she held the map over her lamp and broke her promise to a dead man in under six hours, because she was who she was.

It was drawn in a surveyor's hand—fine, precise, confident, the kind of hand that had measured half the territory. It showed the upper Silver River country, the mine claims staked along the canyon, and one vein marked in red ink running deeper than anything the company's public charts admitted to. Rich enough to buy a territory. Rich enough to kill for.

But it wasn't the vein that stopped her breath. It was the margin. In the same fine hand, small and neat, a note: *Collapse of 14 March — not accidental. W. Hartwell present. Blame assigned.*

W. Hartwell. William. Her brother.

Willie, who had come home from the mines three years ago with his hands shaking and the company's law on his heels, who had signed a confession written by men he was afraid of, who had sat in the territorial jail at Fort Alder ever since while she ran the shallows and sent money to lawyers who took it and did nothing. She had spent three years believing her brother weak. Looking at the note—in a stranger's handwriting, in ink that predated his confession—she understood suddenly that he had been made into the scapegoat the way a river makes a channel: patiently, deliberately, by forces that knew exactly where they wanted the water to go.

She sat until the lamp burned low, and the dread she felt was not the old dread, the running kind. It was something with edges. Something that pointed.

* * *

Coyle's Landing was a mudbank and a warehouse and a saloon, and she tied up there at noon the next day because the mail had to go and because hiding had just become a habit she intended to break carefully, one landing at a time.

They came aboard within the hour. Three men in good coats gone wrong at the seams, dust on their boots from a long overland walk, and the leader wore his smile the way a man wears a borrowed hat. He showed her papers she didn't ask to see. Silver Consolidated Mining Company, the seal said. Special investigators.

"We're looking for a woman captain," he said. "Runs a shallow-draft boat. Might have given passage to a prospector. Older fellow, sick."

"This is a freight run," Zephyrine said. "I don't take passengers."

"No?" He wandered her deck, touching things—her coil of line, her lamp housing, the spot aft where the cordwood was stacked. "Name of Vane. Elias Vane."

"Doesn't ring."

"Mm." He turned, and for a moment the smile slipped and she saw the thing underneath, which was patient and cold and very good at arithmetic. "Funny thing, Captain. Every landing from here to the forks, somebody's seen a woman pilot running the shallows. Nobody can tell us her name. You'd think a woman running a boat alone would be memorable." He tipped his borrowed hat. "If you hear of such a person, the company pays well for information. Pays better for silence, if you follow me. Pays best of all for certain items returned. Paper items."

She followed him. She followed him all the way down the gangplank and watched them walk off toward the saloon, and then she went below and put her hand flat on the oilcloth bundle and felt her own heart going hard and steady, like a good engine.

They hadn't searched the boat. They were fishing. Which meant Elias had died before he could be made to talk—but it also meant they knew the map existed, and they would not stop fishing until the whole river was dragged.

Zephyrine stood in the dim of her cabin with the letter in one hand and the map in the other, and thought about Willie in his cell at Fort Alder, three years of winters, signing his name to a lie because he'd been made small enough to hold it. She had run from that truth so long she'd mistaken the running for freedom.

Upstream, past the last navigable water, the mountains were already wearing their first snow. Beyond them, a daughter named Adela waited for a letter. And beyond even that, a court, a trial, a chance to say aloud what the map said in ink.

She cast off at dusk, alone at the wheel, and turned the Kestrel's bow toward the head of navigation—and past it, toward the trail, toward the pass, toward everything she'd spent three years steering away from.

The river ended ahead of her. For the first time in her life, that felt less like a wall than a door.
