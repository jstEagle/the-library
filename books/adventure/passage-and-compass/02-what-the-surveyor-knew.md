## 2. What the Surveyor Knew

The map had a smell, Zephyrine decided. Old paper and lamp oil and something underneath—iron, maybe, or the memory of iron. She'd been carrying it three days now, folded against her ribs where the Kestrel's ledger used to sit, and she had begun to think of it less as paper than as a passenger. A quiet one. The kind that watched.

She'd left the boat moored at Tallow Bend with a boy named Ferro paid to sleep aboard her and lie about it if asked, and she'd taken to the overland trail with a pack, a rifle, and a stranger who walked like a woman measuring the world even when she wasn't being paid to.

Odile Marchand did not talk on switchbacks. Zephyrine had learned that by noon of the first day. She talked on flats, when her hands were free to gesture at ridgelines and drainage cuts, and she talked at night, when the fire was small and the dark was large, but on the steep places she kept all her breath for the mountain. Zephyrine respected that. It was more honesty than most people managed.

It was on a flat, two days out from Tallow Bend, that Odile finally said what she'd been circling since the river.

"Show me the map again."

"You've seen it."

"I've seen you not show it to me." Odile stopped walking. She was a compact woman, weathered brown at the hands and face, gray coming in early at her temples like frost that had decided to stay. Her eyes were the pale, patient color of a winter sky, and they did not move from Zephyrine's face. "I surveyed these ranges for eleven years, Captain Hartwell. I know every fold of them. I'd like to know why a dying prospector's map makes you walk like it's loaded."

Zephyrine weighed it. Three days of shared trail, shared fire, shared silence when the enforcers' sign appeared—a brand burned into a stump, fresh, no more than a week old—and still she'd held the thing close. But the brand meant the company was ahead of them too, or behind, and either way the arithmetic of trust had changed.

She unslung her pack, worked the oilcloth loose, and unfolded the map on a flat stone between them.

Odile went very still.

Zephyrine had seen men look at gold like that. She had never seen anyone look at ink that way—with recognition, and grief, and something that might have been guilt wearing recognition's coat.

"That's my linework," Odile said quietly. "That's my hand. Contour interval's forty feet—I argued for twenty, they said paper costs money." Her finger hovered above the ridge without touching it. "I drew this in the spring of '89. Every line of it. And then it was taken from me, along with everything else, and I was told to forget I ever held a transit on that mountain."

"The mine," Zephyrine said. "Vane's silver."

"There is no mine." Odile's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "There's a shaft the company drove into a fault they knew about because *I told them*, in writing, with this survey attached. They drove it anyway because the ore body ran rich on the wrong side of the fault line. And when it came down—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Eleven men, Captain. Eleven men came down with it, and the company wrote it up as ground slippage due to unauthorized timbering by one Elias Vane and his partner, whose name they needed a scapegoat for. They found one. A riverman with debts and no family local enough to make noise."

The wind moved through the pines above them. Zephyrine did not move at all.

"Thibault," she said. "My brother's name is Thibault Hartwell."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the tree line, and neither of them pretended to sleep.

"He signed nothing," Zephyrine said. She sat with her back against her pack, knees drawn up, the rifle across them like a sleeping animal. "Thibault never touched a contract in his life he didn't read twice. He wouldn't have timbered a birdhouse without checking the grain."

"I know." Odile fed the fire a stick, watching it catch. "I read the report they filed under his name. The handwriting wasn't his. The timber counts were wrong—the numbers were copied from a different claim entirely, I checked, I spent two years after they dismissed me checking, which is how a surveyor ends up walking freight trails with a theodolite nobody will insure." She looked up. "You should understand what I'm telling you. I didn't just recognize your brother's name on that map margin. I recognized my own signature on the report that buried him. They kept me on long enough to sign it. Then they took my field books and put me on ditches."

Zephyrine studied her across the flames. There were a hundred things to say and most of them were accusations, and none of them would land on the right person. The company had built a machine for distributing blame, and it had delivered a share to everyone in this hollow—her brother in a cell, this woman in exile, an old man dying on a riverboat deck with a letter to a daughter he hadn't seen in nine years.

"Vane said the map was proof," Zephyrine said at last. "I thought he meant the vein. Silver enough to buy lawyers."

"It's better than that." Odile reached into her own pack and brought out a battered notebook, its cover scarred with water damage. "My original field notes. Fault readings, dated, cross-referenced to the survey grid. If that map and these pages sit in front of a magistrate together, the company's story doesn't just crack—it comes apart at the seams. They can't have both documents exist. Which means they can't let either of us reach the pass."

"Then we're agreed on the destination."

"We're agreed on the direction." Odile's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but the ground where one might form. "You're going to the pass for the letter. I'm going for the testimony. After that, Captain, our roads may diverge."

"Roads usually do."

"Yes," Odile said, and looked into the fire instead of at her. "Usually."

* * *

Two days later, at the Charcoal Fork waystation, they learned what the company did about roads.

The station keeper, a wide woman named Bess with forearms like dock rope, took their coin and asked no questions, which was the custom of the place. They ate beans and salt pork and slept four hours in bunks that smelled of other travelers, and they were on the trail again before first light, climbing toward the notch where the Charcoal Fork met the high route.

It was Odile who turned around.

She stopped mid-stride on the switchback, and Zephyrine, ten paces ahead, felt the stop like a change in pressure and turned too. Below them, small with distance, the waystation stood in the gray dawn—and smoke stood above it, straight and black in the still air.

No chimney smoke stood that straight. No chimney smoke stood that black.

"Oil," Odile said. Her voice had gone thin. "They fired it with oil. Bess keeps lamp stock in the cellar—that's lamp oil burning."

Zephyrine counted the minutes in her head, doing the arithmetic she hated: how long since they'd left, how fast a man could ride, how long a woman like Bess would argue before she stopped arguing forever. The smoke did not waver. Nothing else rose from the buildings. No second column, no voices carrying up the cold air. Just the one black pillar, standing in the dawn like a signature.

"They're not chasing us anymore," Zephyrine said. "They're closing doors behind us."

Odile was still looking down. When she spoke, her voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of a woman reading instruments in bad weather. "I filed reports for that company for eleven years. I told myself the worst thing they did was waste good data." She turned. In the light of the burning station reflected faintly in her eyes, her winter-sky gaze had gone the color of steel before a storm. "I was wrong about the worst thing. Let's find out if I'm wrong about anything else. Move."

They moved. Up through the pines, past the snowline's first white warnings, the fire shrinking behind them until it was only a smudge, then a memory, then a reason. Neither of them spoke of turning back, because there was no back to turn to. The company had made sure of that, and in doing so had done the one thing neither bribes nor bargains nor three days of wary distance had accomplished:

It had made the two of them, finally and completely, allies.

By late afternoon the trees thinned and failed altogether, and the world opened into bare rock and old snow, and far above them the pass waited under a sky the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges. Weather was coming. So, Zephyrine knew, was everything else.

At the last stand of pines, Odile paused, looked back once at the valley they'd climbed out of, and said quietly, almost to herself: "Whatever happens up there—thank you for showing me the map."

Zephyrine adjusted her pack straps and did not answer, because the true answer was not one she was ready to give aloud: that showing it had felt less like surrendering a secret than like setting down a weight she'd carried alone so long she'd forgotten it had another side.

Above them, on the ridge, something glinted. Once. Like light off glass, or a lens, or a rifle scope finding its range.

Odile saw it too. Her hand found Zephyrine's sleeve.

"Ambush," she breathed. "Creek bed. Now."
