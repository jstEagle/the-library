## 3. Snowline

The trees gave out two days past the burned waystation, and Odile felt the loss like a wound.

She had spent eleven years reading country—timber, grade, water, stone—and she knew what it meant when the spruce thinned to krummholz and then to nothing at all. It meant exposure. It meant wind with nothing to break it. It meant that from here to the pass, every step they took was a step in full view of anything that cared to look, and something had been looking since Coyle's Landing.

"Snow by tomorrow night," she said, crouching at the edge of the last stunted pines, testing the air the way an old man tests his knee. "Maybe sooner. The light's gone flat."

Zephyrine stood beside her with the satchel strap crossed over her chest, the way she'd worn it since the creek below Vane's claim, since Elias had pressed it into her hands with fingers already going gray. "Then we don't stop."

"We should rest while we can. Eat something hot."

"I've been resting my whole life, Odile." Zephyrine's mouth did the thing it did—not quite a smile, more like a door opening a crack on a lit room before slamming shut again. "I'm not doing it anymore."

Odile wanted to argue. She was good at arguing; it had been her profession once, arguing with men who thought a woman's figures were decoration. But she looked at Zephyrine's face—wind-scoured, jaw set, eyes fixed on the white shoulder of the pass as if it owed her money—and she swallowed the argument whole.

They climbed.

* * *

The frozen creek came at midafternoon, a seam of ice stitched down between granite shoulders, running silver toward the valley they'd left behind. Odile knelt at its edge to fill the water flask, chipping through the skim ice with her knife, and that was when she heard it—the sound she had been listening for since the waystation burned: not a sound at all. An absence. The birds had stopped.

"Down," she said, and didn't wait to see if Zephyrine obeyed, because Zephyrine never obeyed, and because there wasn't time.

The first shot took the ice six inches from Odile's hand and threw chips of it into her cheek like thrown gravel. The second went wide, singing off rock somewhere above them. She rolled behind a granite boulder furred with lichen, cold biting through her coat, and saw them then—three figures on the far rim of the creek's cut, dark against the sky, rifles working. Company men. She knew the silhouette of company men the way she knew her own handwriting.

"The map," one of them shouted across the ice. Not a name. Not a warning. Just the map, as if it were cargo to be claimed, as if they hadn't already killed for less.

Zephyrine had gone to ground behind a fallen slab twenty feet downstream, and Odile saw her do the thing that would live in Odile's memory longer than any scar: instead of shrugging the satchel off to run faster, instead of throwing it into the creek to lighten herself, she wrapped both arms around it and folded her body over it like a mother over a child, making herself smaller around the thing she carried.

A third shot cracked. Odile heard it find flesh—the wet, dull sound of it—and heard Zephyrine cry out, a short sharp sound bitten off at the teeth.

"Zeph!"

"I'm hit—" Through gritted teeth, almost furious about it. "It's nothing—it's the arm—I'm fine—"

Bullets chewed the slab. Stone dust bloomed white. And Odile lay behind her boulder with her heart slamming and did the arithmetic she would spend the rest of her life doing: the satchel was twenty feet downstream, exposed, half-sliding toward the open ice where the next shot could put a round through the oilskin and the letter and the evidence all at once. Zephyrine was bleeding. The enforcers were already moving down the near slope, patient, closing like weather.

She could not carry both.

The thought arrived whole and terrible, the way surveyors' truths arrive: you cannot carry both. The map and the woman. The promise and the person. Eleven years ago she had stood in a company office with a report in front of her and been asked to choose, and she had chosen the safe thing, the small thing, the thing that let her sleep badly but sleep, and a man named Corrigan Vane—no, that was wrong, the partner's name had been scrubbed even from her memory—had died in a collapse that never officially happened, and her brother-in-law of no one had taken the blame, and she had signed her name at the bottom of the lie in her own neat surveyor's hand.

She had chosen the document once. She would not do it twice.

Odile broke cover.

She ran the twenty feet with bullets stitching the snow around her boots—one kicked up so close she felt its heat pass her calf—and dropped to her knees beside Zephyrine, whose left sleeve was soaked black to the elbow, whose arms were still locked around the satchel, whose eyes found Odile's with a ferocity that had nothing of surrender in it.

"Take it," Zephyrine gasped. "Take the satchel and go. I'll draw them. I'm fast when I'm angry."

"You are insufferable when you're angry."

"Odile—"

"Shut up." Odile got a shoulder under Zephyrine's good arm and hauled. "We go together or not at all. That was always the terms."

"It was never the terms."

"It is now." She got them both upright, staggered three steps, four, the ice groaning under their combined weight, and then the world offered her the only mercy it had: the far bank's snow shelf, loaded and rotten, chose that moment to calve. It went down into the creek with a boom like a cannon, taking half the near bank's lip with it, and the enforcers on the slope threw themselves flat against the concussion, and Odile ran them both up the cut and into the rocks beyond while the mountain's own noise covered theirs.

* * *

She got them two miles before she let them stop—a hollow under an overhang, wind-shadowed, invisible from below. Zephyrine sat with her back to stone while Odile peeled back the ruined sleeve, and neither of them spoke while Odile worked: the bullet had gone through the meat of the forearm, clean in, clean out, bleeding hard but not arterial. She washed it with brandy, packed it, bound it with strips of her own shirt, her hands steady in the way hands get when steadiness is the only thing holding a person together.

"You saved me," Zephyrine said at last, watching her work. "Over the map. I told you to take it."

"I know what you told me."

"You should have taken it. One of us carrying it gets through. Two of us bleeding don't."

Odile tied off the bandage, and then, because hiding had become impossible somewhere on that ice—because the open cold had stripped them both down to whatever was true—she said it.

"I signed the report."

Zephyrine went still.

"The collapse. Nine years ago. The company sent a survey team after the Vane-Quist disaster, and we wrote what they told us to write—unstable ground, operator error, no fault of the company's. My instruments said otherwise. I knew otherwise. I'd measured the timbering myself; it was underspec, it was criminal, and I signed anyway." Her voice came out level, which surprised her; she had expected it to break. "They ruined your brother with my signature, Zeph. When those men ask who I am, the honest answer is: the reason you've spent five years running."

The wind moved through the hollow. Somewhere above, snow had begun to fall—the first flakes, fat and slow, the vanguard of the blizzard.

Zephyrine looked at her a long time. Odile braced for the fury, the recrimination, the cold she deserved—and watched instead as Zephyrine lifted her bandaged hand and pressed it, clumsy, against Odile's cheek.

"You came back for me," Zephyrine said. "That's the only ledger I keep."

"Zeph—"

"I loved you before you told me. I love you now. Whatever you signed, you're the one who chose me over the map today, and I—" Her breath caught, and Odile saw, for the first time since the Kestrel, the woman underneath the captain: young, grieving, terrified, and certain. "I'm done running from things. Maybe I start with that."

Odile kissed her then, there in the hollow with the snow coming down and the blood drying on both their hands, and it was not gentle and it was not brief, and when they broke apart Zephyrine laughed—a raw, cracked sound, half sob.

"They'll follow the blood," Odile said against her hair. "We have maybe an hour before the storm covers us, and then the pass, and then—"

"Then the town. Then the daughter. Then the truth." Zephyrine pulled back, eyes bright, and Odile saw the resolve there sharpen into something that frightened her more than any rifle. "And then, Odile—we don't cross the border. We stay."

Through the falling snow, far below along the creek line, Odile caught the glint of lanterns. Three of them. Still coming.
