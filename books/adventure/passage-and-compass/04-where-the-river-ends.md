## 4. Where the River Ends

The town of Meridian Pass announced itself the way a drowning man announces land: desperately, and all at once. One moment Zephyrine was watching Odile's back move ahead of her through the last stand of wind-bent pines, and the next the trees fell away and there it was—two dozen rooftops furred with snow, a church steeple, a scatter of lamplight going amber against the coming dark. Below it all, the river that had carried her whole life appeared one final time, sliding out of the mountains in a ribbon of gray ice, spreading flat, and simply ending. A river with an end. She had not known such a thing existed.

"Storm's maybe two hours behind us," Odile said, breath tearing white from her lips. Her left hand had not left Zephyrine's since the creek—since the bullet, since the cold that had nearly taken everything—but she squeezed it now like a woman testing rope. "Maybe less."

"Then we don't waste them." Zephyrine looked down at herself: blood frozen stiff along her side where the ball had torn through flesh and not bone, the bandage Odile had bound there with strips of her own shirt, her own hands shaking and steady by turns. The satchel hung across her chest, dry. Dry, because Odile had chosen her over it, and then somehow saved both anyway, hauling satchel and bleeding pilot out of the creek together with a strength Zephyrine would remember until she died.

They came down into Meridian Pass as the first flakes arrived—not the soft heralding kind but hard grains that ticked against the boards of the walkway like gravel thrown by a furious hand. The town smelled of woodsmoke and horse and tallow. A boy splitting kindling on a porch stared at them, two women gray with trail dust, one bleeding through her coat, and said nothing, which was its own kind of welcome.

The daughter's name was Adelise Vane, and she kept the town's post office, which struck Zephyrine as a joke Elias would have appreciated—the man who had spent his last years carrying nothing but ore samples and guilt entrusting his final letter to the mail.

She stood behind the counter with her sleeves rolled, sorting letters into pigeonholes by lamplight, and when Zephyrine laid the oilskin-wrapped packet on the counter before her, Adelise Vane went very still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"He made it to the river," Zephyrine said. "He didn't make it past it. I'm sorry. He asked me—" Her voice caught on the old promise, worn smooth from three hundred miles of repetition. "He asked me to bring you this before the snows closed the pass. We ran the storm to do it."

Adelise did not open the letter at once. She held it in both hands, turning it, reading the outside of it the way other people read the inside. Then she broke the seal with her thumbnail and read standing up, and Zephyrine watched her face do what faces do when they receive both a wound and a gift in the same envelope—the flinch and the softening arriving together, inseparable.

"He writes about my mother," Adelise said finally. Her eyes were dry but her voice wasn't. "He never talked about her while he lived. And he writes about a mine. About a collapse. He writes names." She looked up, and her gaze went straight to the satchel, as if she could see through oilskin and canvas to the folded map inside. "He writes that the proof is with the woman who carries this. That the proof is worth more than the silver."

Outside, the storm arrived in earnest. The windows went white. Somewhere a door banged, and the lamplight shivered.

* * *

They took a room above the saloon because it was the only room, the pass closing behind them with a sound like the whole mountain exhaling. By morning the drifts stood higher than the hitching posts, and the telegraph line was down, and whatever road out of Meridian Pass had ever existed belonged now to spring.

Zephyrine woke to find Odile already dressed, sitting at the window with the map spread open on her knees, studying it not as a surveyor studies a survey but as a woman studies a scar.

"I know what you're thinking," Zephyrine said.

"You don't."

"The border's forty miles south even in snow. There are men in this town who run sleds across for less than we'd pay them. By thaw, we're in another territory, under other names, and the company can write whatever reports it likes about two women who vanished in the mountains." She sat up, wincing at the pull along her ribs. "I've been running since I was nineteen years old, Odile. I'm good at it. It's the only thing I've ever been good at."

Odile turned from the window. Lamplight lay along the map's creases, and along her face too, and Zephyrine saw that she had been crying or close to it, though her voice when it came was level as a transit line.

"When I signed that report," Odile said, "I told myself the numbers were wrong, not the mine. That the collapse was bad luck, not bad timber, not the corners the company cut because cutting corners was cheaper than killing fewer men. Eleven men, Zephyrine. And your brother's name put on it because he was there and he was poor and someone had to hang for it." She folded the map along its old lines, precise, gentle. "I have spent six years being sorry in private. Sorry is a currency the company accepts at any bank. It costs them nothing."

"So we stay."

"So we stay." Odile crossed the room and knelt beside the bed so their eyes were level, and her hands found Zephyrine's, the bandaged one and the whole one alike. "Not because staying is safe. Because running kept us alive and it will keep us alive forever, and I am done confusing those things. I want—I want to be somewhere on purpose. With you. Even if 'somewhere' turns out to be a courtroom."

Zephyrine laughed, and it hurt, and she let it hurt. "You understand what you're asking. The moment I hand that map to the magistrate, I stop being nobody. Every debt, every name I've sailed under, every mile of river I fled—they'll all come looking. My brother gets his name back and I lose mine entirely. That's the trade."

"That's the trade," Odile agreed. "And I've done the survey. The ground holds."

* * *

The magistrate's office occupied the front half of a building whose back half was a feed store, and Magistrate Corliss was a broad, slow-moving woman with spectacles and the unhurried air of someone who had waited out worse storms than this one. She listened to all of it—the mine, the collapse, the eleven men, the brother, the enforcers, the burned waystation, the bullet—without interrupting once, her pen moving in a small dry script, snow melting off Zephyrine's shoulders onto her floorboards.

When Zephyrine finished, Corliss looked at the map for a long moment without touching it.

"You understand," she said, "that if I take this, it becomes a matter of record. The company has lawyers in the territorial capital with longer arms than mine. You'll be named. Both of you. There will be a hearing when the passes open, and you'll be called to testify, and they will do everything lawful and some things otherwise to make certain neither of you is alive to do it."

"I know," Zephyrine said.

"And your brother? Where does he sit today?"

"In a cell in Fort Alder, waiting for a trial that keeps getting postponed because the company keeps buying the postponements."

Corliss nodded slowly, and reached out, and took the map. "Then this buys him a trial that can't be bought. Miss Hartwell—for the record, and so there's no confusion later—state your name. Your true one."

The office was warm and smelled of paper and lamp oil. Through the frosted window, the storm had begun to slacken, great slow flakes descending now instead of driven ones, and beyond the glass Zephyrine could see the place where the river ended, buried in white, patient, waiting for spring the way everything here waited for spring.

She thought of the Kestrel tied up somewhere far downstream, and of every dawn she had ever raised steam and slipped away before the town woke, and of how the moving had never once felt like freedom—only like falling, indefinitely postponed.

"Zephyrine Hartwell," she said. "Of the riverboat Kestrel. And I'd like it entered plainly, please. No aliases."

"Miss Odile Marchand," Odile said, stepping forward to stand shoulder to shoulder with her. "Formerly of the Silver Ridge Survey Office. Witness for the document, and witness for her."

Corliss wrote it all down, each word landing on the page like a small bell being rung, and when she finished she blotted the ink and looked up over her spectacles.

"Hearing's set for the first week the pass opens. April, God willing." She almost smiled. "You'll want somewhere to winter."

* * *

They found it three streets up from the river's end: a cabin with a stove that drew well and a window that faced east, rented from a widow who asked no questions beyond whether they could chop wood. Zephyrine's wound knit slow and clean under Odile's hands. They learned the town—the post office where Adelise Vane had pinned her father's letter inside the counter lid, face out, like an icon; the saloon where the piano player knew four songs; the long white silence of the afternoons.

On the evening of the winter solstice, the shortest day, they sat at the east window with tea going cold between them, watching the light fail over the buried river.

"My brother used to say," Zephyrine murmured, "that a boat isn't free just because it moves. He said a boat is free when it has somewhere it means to arrive."

"And do you?" Odile asked. Her shoulder rested against Zephyrine's, warm through wool. "Have somewhere you mean to arrive?"

Zephyrine considered the question honestly, the way she'd once read water—looking beneath the surface for what was actually there. "April," she said at last. "A courtroom. A trial. My brother walking out of Fort Alder with his own name in his mouth. And after that—" she turned her head, found Odile watching her with an expression she had once seen on the face of travelers sighting land, "—after that, wherever you're standing. That'll do for arrival."

Outside, the snow went on falling, deepening, keeping faith with the season. But under it, unseen, the river was already moving—thin threads of water working patiently beneath the ice, feeling toward the sea, and toward spring, and toward everything they had chosen to stay and face.
