## 1. The Wreck They Don't Name

The water off Thursday Island was the color of old bottle glass, and Bram Kavanagh went down into it the way a man walks into a church he no longer believes in — out of habit, out of debt, and because there was nowhere else to go.

Forty feet down, the wreck resolved out of the green murk in pieces: a hull line first, then the skeletal suggestion of superstructure, then the detail that made his chest tighten behind the regulator. She was a navy auxiliary, one of those vessels that existed on no public registry and answered to no port authority, and she had been sitting on this shelf of coral since before Bram learned to hold his breath properly. The chart in the wheelhouse of the *Mako* called it nothing at all. The coordinates had come to him folded inside a beer mat from a man who was now either dead or very good at pretending to be.

Bram checked his gauge. Two thousand two hundred psi. Enough for forty minutes if he didn't work too hard, and he never worked hard on this wreck. You didn't rush a place like this. You moved through it the way you'd move through a house where someone had died recently and might not have finished dying.

The current came in long, slow pulses off the Torres Strait, tugging at him like a hand on his sleeve. He let it carry him along the starboard side, past a gun mount that had been stripped so thoroughly it looked like a rusted flower, past a hatch that hung open on its hinges like a jaw. Fish had colonized her. A groper the size of a door watched him from the shadow of the bridge wing with the flat, proprietary stare of something that had outlived everyone's claims.

He'd been diving this site for eleven days. The job, as presented by the man with the beer mat, was simple: survey for recoverable metal, photograph nothing, speak to nobody. Cash on completion, cash in advance for fuel. It was the kind of job that came looking for men like Bram precisely because men like Bram had learned not to ask questions. He'd taken it anyway, because the *Mako* burned diesel whether he ate or not, and because Thursday Island was as good a place to be invisible as any in the Coral Sea.

But he'd known within the first five minutes on the bottom what kind of wreck this was. He'd known the way you know a face across a crowded room — instantly, painfully, with your whole body.

It wasn't the same ship. That was the thing he kept telling himself, down here in the cold green silence. Different hull, different decade, different stretch of water. But the way she'd been stripped told him everything. Not salvaged — *sanitized*. Salvage takes what's valuable. Sanitizing takes what's true. Someone had gone through this vessel with a list in their hand and removed every plate, every fitting, every document case that could tell a story, and they'd done it with government efficiency, and then they'd let the reef grow over the scars.

Bram knew that signature. He'd seen it once before, on a wreck he was ordered to forget, in water he was ordered to stay out of, and the last crew who talked about it had stopped talking about anything at all.

He found the case at twenty-eight minutes into the dive, wedged under a collapsed section of deck plating near the aft hold, where whoever had done the stripping had missed it or hadn't bothered. It was an equipment case, olive drab, maybe eighteen inches long, and when he worked it free of the coral that had grown through its handle, it came up light — empty, or nearly. His torch beam found the stencil on its face and his heart did something complicated and unwelcome.

A stamp. The broad arrow and the serial number beneath it, six digits, in paint that time hadn't touched because the case had been face-down in the sand all these years.

He knew that format. He had held cases just like it, once, in another life, and passed them hand to hand down a chain of silent men on a night he still dreamed about with his eyes open.

Bram hung in the water above the wreck and let the current rock him, and for a long moment he considered putting the case back exactly where he'd found it. That would be the smart play. That would be the play of a man who intended to keep breathing. Take nothing, report nothing, remember nothing — the three commandments of the trade he'd been conscripted into without ever signing anything.

But his hands didn't put it back. His hands stowed it in his mesh bag, because some part of him — the part that had been drowning slowly for years — needed proof. Needed something solid to hold in the small hours when the dreams came. Proof that he hadn't imagined it. Proof that the thing he'd been ordered to forget had a shape, a weight, a serial number.

He ascended slow, watching his computer, doing his safety stop in the featureless blue where a man can pretend he's nowhere at all.

* * *

The *Mako* rode at anchor in a back channel off the island's western shore, tucked between two mangrove cays where the chart showed shallow water and only a fool would take a boat. Bram was, increasingly, that fool. He came up the ladder and hauled himself over the transom, and the boat took his weight with a familiar complaint.

"You're late." Deke Rourke sat in the wheelhouse doorway with a rifle across his knees, cleaning it with the unhurried attention of a man who had cleaned ten thousand rifles and expected to clean ten thousand more. Deke was the one who'd brought him the job — a former navy man himself, though neither of them ever said which navy or when, and the silence between them on that subject was the closest thing to friendship either of them had.

"Current was running," Bram said. He peeled his hood back. "Bottom's clean. Nothing worth lifting."

Deke's eyes went to the mesh bag. "Nothing?"

"Conch shells. A porthole with more rust than brass." Bram carried the bag below decks without hurrying, which was the hardest thing he'd done all day. Hurrying was a confession. He dumped the bag's contents into a bucket, set the case beneath a false panel under his bunk, and screwed the panel down with fingers that wanted to shake and didn't.

When he came back up, Deke was watching him with that flat patience of his. "The man who hired us radioed. Wants a progress report tomorrow."

"He'll get one."

"And if he asks about the aft hold?"

Bram met his eyes. "There's nothing in the aft hold."

Deke held the look for a moment longer than was comfortable, then nodded once and went back to his rifle. That was the arrangement between them: each man's ignorance was his own property, and trespassing on it was the one unforgivable sin. It was a thin kind of trust. It was the only kind Bram had left.

That night he cooked rice and tinned fish on the single burner and ate sitting on the foredeck with his back against the mast, watching the lights of Thursday Island shimmer across the water like something drowned and still glowing. Music drifted out from the pubs on the strand. Somewhere a generator thumped. It was, by any reasonable measure, a peaceful evening, and Bram sat in the middle of it feeling like a man standing in a field the day after a battle, waiting for the ground to tell him where the bodies were.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape. Eleven days ago he'd started diving a wreck that officially did not exist, hired by a man whose name he'd been given secondhand, paid in cash that smelled of the same institutional drawers as the stamp on that case. And the last time he'd stood on a deck with this feeling in his stomach — the feeling of being a small gear in a machine much larger and colder than himself — four other men had stood there with him. Ruiz. Callahan. The Okafor brothers. Good divers, good men, ordinary in their vices and their hopes. They'd stayed in Cairns afterward, because Cairns was comfortable and the money was steady and staying is what people do.

Bram had left. Not because he was smarter than them — he'd told himself it was luck, for years, that it was luck — but because some animal part of him had smelled the machine and run.

Ruiz drowned in a marina slip, alone, at night, with a life vest hanging on the rail six feet away. Callahan fell from a hotel balcony in Darwin. The Okafors' trawler went down with both brothers aboard in weather so mild the coroner had struggled to find language for it. Four accidents in fourteen months, spread across a thousand miles of coast, and not one investigation that went anywhere, because the men asking questions kept getting transferred, retired, promoted.

Bram had gone to the funerals. All four. He'd stood at the back in a borrowed jacket and listened to priests talk about the sea taking its own, and he'd understood, with a clarity that had never once lifted since, that the sea hadn't taken anyone. The sea had been *used*. The way a knife is used. And the hand holding it belonged to the same institution that had stamped the case now hidden under his bunk.

So he kept moving. Port to port, job to job, never more than a few months anywhere, never a lease, never a woman who asked questions he'd have to answer, never a barstool he could be found on twice. People called men like him restless. Drifters. Men who couldn't settle. Nobody guessed that settling was the dangerous thing — that a man who stays puts down roots, and roots are how they find you, and how they find the people standing next to you.

He finished his rice and sat a while longer in the dark, and then he went below, unscrewed the panel, and took the case out into the lamplight.

* * *

Up close, under the swinging bulb, the case was worse than it had been underwater. The stamp was crisp. The serial number — 447193 — was crisp. And stenciled smaller beneath it, half worn but legible, was a code prefix that Bram recognized the way you recognize the voice of a man at your funeral.

He turned it over. On the base, scratched with a knife point in handwriting he didn't know, someone had written a date and two words: *NOT LOST.*

Not lost. Meaning: this vessel was not lost. Meaning: whatever had put her on the bottom had a name other than accident, and someone aboard had known it, and had spent their last minutes above water scratching a message into a case they hoped someone would someday find.

Bram sat with the case in his lap for a long time. The lamp swung. The boat breathed beneath him. Out in the channel, the tide turned with a sound like a long exhale.

He thought about reporting it. There were channels — supposedly. Journalists, ombudsmen, ministers, the whole bright machinery of accountability that civilians believed in the way children believe in the tooth fairy. He thought about walking into the police station on Thursday Island with the case under his arm and saying the words *I have evidence*, and he pictured how it would go, because he'd played it forward a hundred times in a hundred variations. The polite interest. The phone call made while he waited. The transfer of custody. And then the quiet, and then the waiting, and then either an accident with his name on it or a cell with his name on it, and either way the case disappearing into the same drawer that had swallowed everything else.

You cannot hand evidence to the people it implicates. That was the first lesson of the wreck he didn't name, and he'd paid four funerals' worth of tuition to learn it.

He thought about keeping it. Selling it, even. There were men in this part of the world who'd pay well for a serial number that embarrassed the right people, and Bram was not a rich man, and the *Mako* needed a new propeller shaft, and virtue was a luxury priced beyond his means most months. But selling it meant showing it, and showing it meant being traceable, and being traceable meant the machine would find him the way it had found Ruiz, patient as tide.

Which left the third option. The one his hands were already moving toward while his mind catalogued objections.

He took the case up on deck. The night was windless; the mangroves stood black and silent along the cay. He built a small fire in the steel drum they used for burning trash — driftwood kindling, a splash of diesel — and when the flames had settled into that orange roar that eats everything, he set the case on top of them.

Olive-drab steel doesn't burn easily. It took twenty minutes. Bram fed the fire and watched the paint blister and curl, watched the stencil darken, watched the serial number go soft and then illegible and then gone. At some point the case split open along a seam, and out of the cavity inside slid a sealed oilskin packet, small as a cigarette pack, that the stripping crews had missed along with the case itself.

Bram froze.

The packet lay on the deck planks beside the drum, firelight crawling over it. He should burn it. Of course he should burn it. Whatever was inside — a document, a note, a name — was the same species of poison as the serial number, only concentrated. Burn it and go back to being nobody, moving on, alive.

His hand hovered over it. The fire crackled. Somewhere across the water, the island music had stopped, and the silence felt less like peace than like listening.

He picked up the packet instead. It was heavier than it looked. He weighed it in his palm, turning it, and told himself he was only checking whether it was waterproof enough to survive the fire — that he was being thorough, not curious, that thoroughness was different.

Then he put it in his pocket, raked the coals until the case was a shapeless slag of melted alloy, and drowned the fire in a bucket of seawater that hissed like something angry.

Dawn was still hours off. Bram sat on the foredeck with the packet in his pocket and the taste of diesel smoke in his throat, and he made himself a promise in the old grammar, the only grammar that had never failed him: *first light, we move.* Fuel, water, north. Past Cape York, past the Gulf, anywhere the machine's reach grew thin. He'd been running for years and he'd run for years more, and if that was the whole of his life, then it was the price, and he'd pay it.

The packet pressed against his hip like a second heartbeat.

He didn't open it that night. He almost wished, later, that he never had — because whatever small mercy ignorance had left him, he spent it three weeks later in a tavern in Port Moresby, when a dying prospector with blood on his shirt pressed an oilskin map into Bram's hands and asked for a promise, and Bram, who trusted no harbor and no man and least of all himself, heard himself give it.
