## 2. A Dying Man's Bargain

Port Moresby smelled the way all ports smell when the rain has just stopped — diesel and salt and rotting fruit, a sweetness gone wrong somewhere beneath it all. Bram Kavanagh came down the gangway of the *Kestrel* with his pay in an envelope inside his shirt and no plans beyond a cold beer and a berth on whatever boat was heading north next week. That was the whole of his ambition these days: north, or east, or anywhere that wasn't where he'd been.

The tavern was called the Anchor's Rest, which was a joke, because nothing about it rested. It was a low concrete block with a tin roof sweating in the heat, ceiling fans turning slow as tired gulls, and a bar top scarred by forty years of bottles set down hard. The air was blue with cigarette smoke and loud with the argument of half a dozen languages. Bram took a stool at the far end, near the wall, back to the corner — old habits, navy habits, habits he told himself he'd shed and never did — and ordered what passed for beer.

He'd been there twenty minutes, watching the door without seeming to, when the man came in.

Bram noticed him the way you notice weather. Something in the pressure of the room changed. The stranger was white-haired, sun-cured to the color of old rope, wearing a canvas jacket too heavy for the climate and walking like each step had been negotiated separately with his legs. He stood in the doorway a moment, blinking against the dimness, and his eyes went across the room — not searching for a face but counting exits, same as Bram would have done. Then they found Bram, and stopped.

There was a moment, then, that Bram would think about for years afterward. The moment before the hook goes in. The old man crossed the room with the terrible deliberation of someone spending strength he couldn't afford, and sat down on the stool beside Bram without asking, and said:

"You're Kavanagh."

"I'm nobody."

"You're Kavanagh." The voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Diver. Ex-navy. Thursday Island last month, before that Darwin, before that who knows. You move around." He smiled, and the smile cost him something; Bram saw the effort of it, the way a man's face tightens around pain like a fist around broken glass. "I've been asking after you in six ports. Thought I'd die of old age before I found you, the way you travel."

"Then you should have saved your money." Bram turned back to his beer. "I don't know you."

"No." The old man signaled the bartender with two fingers. "But I know you. Or I know of you, which is nearly as good and considerably cheaper." His drink came — rum, neat, no glass wiped twice — and he wrapped both hands around it like it was warmth he was borrowing. Up close Bram could see the details now: the yellow tinge at the edges of his eyes, the sweat that wasn't from the heat, the way his left side didn't quite participate in his breathing. "Elias Wren," he said. "Prospector. Pearl-shell, once. Guano, later. Lately, salvage. And lately of that, nothing at all, because lately I'm a dead man walking around in his own body waiting for it to catch up."

"People say things like that in bars."

"This isn't talk." Wren shifted on the stool, and Bram heard the wet sound of it, and understood. "This is arithmetic."

Under the jacket, high on the left side, was blood. Not fresh — dried black in a wide stain, and above it a smell Bram knew from bad nights in worse places, the sweet-rot smell of a wound going septic. Someone had shot Elias Wren, and sewn him up badly or not at all, and he had walked into this tavern anyway because there was nowhere else left to go.

Bram looked at his own beer. He thought about the envelope of pay against his chest, and the ferry north, and the clean empty weeks ahead of him with nothing in them but water and work. Every instinct he owned — and he owned them the way a miser owns coins, counted nightly, guarded jealously — every one of them said *stand up, walk out, let the old man die alone, it's cheaper.*

He stayed on the stool.

"Why me?" he said.

Wren drank, a small careful sip. "Because of what you did at Thursday Island. Because word travels among divers the way current travels under a calm surface — you can't see it, but everything floating moves with it. Because three years ago a navy diver named Kavanagh pulled something off a wreck he was ordered to leave, and instead of selling it or trading it or even keeping it, he burned it. Burned it and walked away from a pension doing it." The old eyes came around, bright and feverish and terribly steady. "A man who burns evidence instead of selling it is either a fool or an honest man. I've watched you long enough now to bet you're the second kind. I can't afford a fool."

The room seemed to recede. The noise of the tavern went distant, the way sound does underwater. Bram set his glass down with great care, because his hand wanted to shake and he wouldn't permit it.

"What wreck?"

"The one they don't name." Wren smiled again, and this time there was no humor in it at all. "Yes. I know about that too. Don't look like that — I haven't come to blackmail you, Kavanagh. Blackmail needs a living man to collect. I've come to spend my last coin, and I mean to spend it well."

* * *

They talked for two hours while the light through the doorway went from gold to grey to the blue-black of tropical dusk, and the tavern filled and emptied around them like a tide. Wren talked in pieces, the way dying men do — a flood of detail, then a silence where he fought his own body, then more. Bram bought him water when the rum was finished and made him drink it, and Wren obeyed with the sullen gratitude of a man who knows the doctor's right and hates him for it.

His name had been real once, the prospecting. Twenty years of it across the Torres Strait and the Coral Sea coast, islands and shoals and the drowned margins of the war. And salvage — honest salvage mostly, and some that wasn't, because a man eats either way. Five years ago he'd taken a contract. A recovery job, private, well paid, run out of Brisbane by a shipping company whose name he wrote on a beer mat and pushed across the bar, and Bram looked down at the four letters — *HALLORAN* — and felt the temperature of the evening change.

"They dressed it up nice," Wren said. "Cargo lost in a storm, 1947. The *Meridian Star*, general freighter, Sydney to Manila. Insured, written off, forgotten. Except she didn't go down where the record says. She went down ninety miles east of that, in deep water off a reef nobody charts, and she didn't go down in any storm, because there wasn't one." He tapped the beer mat with one cracked fingernail. "And her hold wasn't carrying general freight. She was carrying bullion. Company bullion, moved quiet, moved off the books — and the company that owned her decided, somewhere between Sydney and the deep water, that the insurance payout plus the secret was worth more than the cargo plus the crew."

Bram said nothing. The fan turned overhead. Somewhere behind them a bottle broke and laughter followed it.

"There were eleven men aboard," Wren went on, quieter now. "Scuttled with scuttling charges while they slept, most like. Or maybe awake. I've wondered about that for five years and I'll wonder about it however long I've got left, which isn't long." He coughed, a deep wet tearing cough, and when it passed he sat very still with his eyes closed, gathering himself like a man gathering spilled grain, grain by grain. "I found her. Two years ago. Found her and dove her and came up with proof — manifests, bills of lading, the company's own stamps, serial numbers matching the bullion bars still chained in her hold. Enough paper to hang Edmund Halloran from his own crane in Brisbane harbor. And I got shot for it in a alley in Rabaul three weeks ago by men who took everything except—" his hand went inside the canvas jacket, moving slowly, wincing, "—except this."

He brought it out and laid it on the bar between them.

An oilskin packet, tied with tarred twine, soft with age and handling. It was smaller than a book and heavier than it looked, the way important things are.

"The chart," Wren said. "Coordinates, soundings, the reef line, the approach. Everything I know. Everything I bled for." His hand stayed flat on it, though, and his eyes came up, and Bram saw what was underneath the fever now, underneath the pain — a will like a keel, worn thin but true. "It's not for sale. Before you say anything — it's not for sale, because I've got nothing left to buy with and we both know it. It's for trade. One thing only."

"Which is?"

"A promise."

The word hung there. Bram looked at the packet, and at the old man's hand on it, and thought about walking out. He thought about it seriously, the way you think about a door in a burning building. He had spent three years learning to carry nothing — no debts, no anchors, no names people could use. A promise was a debt with interest accruing forever.

"Wren—"

"Hear it first." The old man leaned close, and Bram caught the smell of him — salt and sickness and rum — and the heat coming off his skin like a stove. "See it found, or see it destroyed. Those are your choices. Not sell it, not sit on it, not hand it to the police or the press or the navy, because the navy—" and here his voice dropped to almost nothing, and his eyes were suddenly fierce, "—the navy already knows. The navy helped bury her. You know how those orders work, Kavanagh. You've signed one. So it comes down to you and me and the dead. See it found — bring the truth up into daylight where it can't be drowned again. Or see it destroyed — go down yourself and put the charges in and end it, so no more men like me get shot in alleys over it, so Halloran can't ever lift it and make it disappear clean. Either one. But one of them, sworn, on your honor, tonight. Because I am not giving this to a maybe."

Outside, the rain started again, sudden and enormous, drumming on the tin roof until conversation everywhere else in the tavern became impossible. Inside their small space it was just the two of them and the drumming, like the world had narrowed to a barrel.

Bram stared at the oilskin packet and saw, unbidden, a steel case in a gloved hand, thirty meters down in green dark water, stamped with serial numbers he could still recite. He had burned that. He had stood on a beach at three in the morning and fed it page by page into a fire and watched the truth curl up as smoke, because the truth had gotten eleven good men silenced before him and he'd been a coward in the only currency cowards understand — survival. He had told himself it was wisdom. He had told himself it for three years, moving port to port, and the telling had almost taken.

Almost.

"If I swear this," Bram said slowly, "and I find out you're wrong — if this is a madman's treasure map, if you're conning me—"

"Then you'll have wasted a dive." Wren shrugged, and the shrug hitched halfway and became a grimace. "I'm a prospector, son, not a novelist. Check my soundings yourself. They'll be true to the fathom." He paused. "The rest of it — the ship, the company, the men — check that too, if you live long enough. You'll find it truer than the soundings."

"And if I say no? Walk out that door?"

"Then I'll give it to the next diver, and the next, until somebody swears or somebody kills me for it, and either way it stops being my problem, because I'll be past problems." He said it without self-pity, flat as a depth reading. "But I came looking for you particular. I've read your service record — the parts that aren't blacked out, which is to say your name and your birthdate and nothing else worth having. And I've heard the parts that are, from a man who was there, who owed me money." A ghost of the smile. "You're the right kind of stubborn, Kavanagh. The kind that keeps breathing."

The rain hammered. The fan turned. Bram picked up his beer and found it empty and set it down again, and understood that he had already decided, had probably decided the moment the old man sat down, the way a hull decides about ice long before the sound reaches the bridge.

"Say it exactly," he said. "What I'm swearing to."

Wren straightened as much as the wound allowed, and his voice took on a strange formality, the cadence of a man laying a keel.

"See it found, or see it destroyed. Swear it on your honor as a diver and a sailor, and keep it past convenience, past fear, past sense. Keep it the way the sea keeps what it takes." He held out his hand — steady now, steadier than it had been all night, as if the oath were the only medicine left that worked. "That's the bargain. That's the whole price. The map costs nothing. The promise costs everything. Most valuable thing I own."

Bram took the hand. It was hot and dry as driftwood.

"I swear it," he said. "Found or destroyed. Past convenience, past fear, past sense."

Something went out of Elias Wren then — not life, not yet, but the tension that had been holding the life in, like a line finally made fast. He slumped forward onto the bar, laughing softly, a dry rustling laugh with no wind behind it, and pushed the oilskin packet across the wood with two fingers.

"Then it's yours," he said. "God help you. It was the death of me, but it might be the making of you. Funny, the currents." His eyes were closing. "Funny, the currents..."

Bram got him to a room above the tavern — carried him, actually, up stairs built for smaller men, while the old man cursed him with what energy remained and thanked him in the same breath. He sent for a doctor with the envelope of pay, and the doctor came, and looked, and shook his head, and gave Wren something for the pain and told Bram what Bram already knew: hours, not days. Maybe less.

* * *

Wren died a little after dawn, with the rain gone and the harbor turning pink and gold outside the shutters, the light coming through the slats in bars across the bed like something out of a church. Bram was sitting beside him because he hadn't been able to make himself leave, and at the end the old man surfaced once from wherever he'd gone, and found Bram's wrist with fingers light as gull bones, and gripped it.

"Meridian Star," he whispered. "Ninety miles east. Deep water. Remember the name — Halloran. Say it back."

"Halloran."

"Good lad." The grip tightened, one last pulse of that iron keel. "They'll come for the map. Men like the ones who shot me. They always come. Trust no harbor, Kavanagh. Trust no—" and the sentence went out like a lamp, gently, mid-word, and the hand on Bram's wrist weighed nothing at all.

Bram sat there a long time while the light climbed the wall. Then he closed the old man's eyes, took the oilskin packet from inside his shirt where he'd kept it all night without deciding to, and went downstairs into the noise and heat of the waking port.

At the bar, the night bartender was sweeping glass. On the counter where Wren had left it, the beer mat with the four letters was gone. Bram stood very still, remembering — certain, now, with the sick certainty of hindsight — that they had not been alone in the tavern as often as he'd thought. That the door had opened more times than the traffic warranted. That a man in a grey shirt had sat too long over one drink, facing the room.

He walked out into the morning with the map against his chest and the oath in his mouth like a swallowed hook, and three days later, in a Cairns newspaper over eggs he couldn't finish, he would read a paragraph about an insurance investigation opened into Halloran Shipping's wartime losses — and an investigator's name that he would not yet know was already on a plane north, looking for a diver named Kavanagh.

But that morning, all he knew was this: he had given his word to a dead man, and somewhere behind him in the waking city, footsteps had begun to follow.
