## 3. The Investigator

The rain came down on Brisbane like a verdict — steady, gray, indifferent — and Mara Voss watched it from the fourth-floor window of the Meridian Mutual Assurance building while the city below turned its umbrellas up and hurried on with the business of not drowning.

She had been at her desk since six. The claim file lay open in front of her, and she had read it four times now, each pass finding something new to dislike.

*Salvage recovery, vessel* Cormorant Maid, *position 14°41′ south, 147°12′ east. Cargo recovered: general freight, machinery, specie. Claimant: Halloran Shipping Consolidated. Loss date: eleven months prior.*

The numbers were neat. That was the first thing wrong with them. In eleven years of marine claims, Mara had learned that honest salvage was never neat. Honest salvage came in waterlogged and contradictory, with manifests that didn't match the hold and divers who disagreed about what they'd found. It arrived smelling of diesel and rot, wrapped in affidavits signed by men who couldn't spell *fathom*. This file arrived pressed and dry as a hymnal, every figure reconciled to the cent, every witness statement worded in the same careful bureaucratic cadence, as though all three of Halloran's divers had been issued a single tongue.

She turned to the position report. Fourteen forty-one south, one forty-seven twelve east. She'd charted it twice on the wall map behind her desk, and both times her pencil had stopped over open water well inside the reef belt — deep water, too deep for the* Cormorant Maid'*s draught, which raised the question of what she'd been doing there when she supposedly struck an uncharted shoal. And it sat squarely inside the monsoon corridor, in a season when no prudent master took a loaded ship anywhere near the outer reefs without cause.

Ships don't sink where they can't float. Her old instructor at the maritime academy used to say that, tapping the blackboard with his pipe. *When you find a wreck in impossible water, stop asking how it sank. Start asking who put it there.*

Mara pulled the loss record again and compared it against the Bureau of Meteorology's archived synoptic charts. On the night the* Cormorant Maid* went down, the nearest gale was four hundred miles east. The sea state in that quadrant, by every record kept, had been moderate. Two-foot swell. A millpond, by Coral Sea standards.

And yet the claim cited "catastrophic weather event" three separate times, in three documents prepared by three different hands, all using the same phrase. *Catastrophic weather event.* Not storm. Not squall. The phrase had the flavor of something drafted once, in a solicitor's office, and distributed downward like communion wafers.

She wrote the observation in the margin of her working notes, small and precise, in the blue-black ink she favored because it didn't smear when wet. Then she did what she always did with a claim that smelled wrong: she built a second file, the quiet one, the one that lived in her bottom drawer under a folder labeled GARDEN SUPPLIES — RENEWALS, because nobody in the history of insurance had ever wanted to look at garden supplies.

Into the quiet file went photocopies. The position reports. The weather archives. A list of every vessel Halloran Shipping had declared lost or constructively lost in the past six years — nine ships, she found, counting backward through the bound registers, nine ships in six years for a line that size, which was roughly four more than actuarial sense allowed. She charted them all on the wall map in red pencil, and when she stepped back and looked at what she'd drawn, the little red crosses strung themselves across the Coral Sea like beads on a string, all of them clustered along the same two hundred miles of reef shelf, all of them in seasons when the weather bureau's records showed nothing worse than rain.

Nine wrecks. One patch of ocean. One company.

Mara stood very still in front of the map, the rain ticking against the glass, and felt the particular cold pleasure that was the closest thing she had to joy: the moment when scattered facts stopped being scattered and became a shape. It was the same feeling other people got from church, she suspected, or from falling in love. The world resolving into pattern. Chaos admitting it had been lying.

Then she thought about what the shape meant — nine crews, potentially, if the losses hadn't been as clean as the paper claimed — and the pleasure went out of it like heat out of an opened oven, and what remained was just work. Necessary work. Hers.

She squared the quiet file, returned it to the drawer beneath the garden supplies, and went to find Aldous Crane.

* * *

Crane's office occupied the corner of the floor with the best light, though you'd never have guessed it from the way he kept the blinds half-drawn, as if daylight were a concession he grudgingly permitted. He was a long, dry man, sixty or thereabouts, with a face like a folded map and a voice he kept pitched just below the level of command so that people leaned in to hear him and mistook their own leaning for respect. He had been at Meridian Mutual for thirty-one years. He knew where every body was buried, and in at least two cases, Mara privately suspected, he had held the shovel.

"Voss." He didn't look up from the paper he was reading. "Sit."

She sat. She laid the original claim file on his blotter, squared its corners out of habit, and waited until he deigned to finish his page.

"The Halloran salvage claim," she said. "The* Cormorant Maid*. I'm declining to certify it."

That got his eyes up. Crane's eyes were pale and patient, like a fisherman watching a float. "Basis?"

"Position is inconsistent with the vessel's draught. Weather records contradict the stated loss conditions. Three independent witness statements use identical phrasing, which suggests common authorship. And the recovery manifest lists specie bullion in quantities that—" she paused, choosing precision over drama, "—that exceed anything the* Cormorant Maid*'s registered cargo profile could plausibly have carried. Either the ship was carrying undeclared gold, Mr. Crane, or the recovered gold came from somewhere else entirely."

"And your recommendation?"

"Refuse the payout. Refer to the marine board. Possibly refer to the police."

Crane set down his newspaper with great care, aligning its edges with the blotter's edge, and regarded her for a long moment. Outside, the rain intensified, drumming on the blinds like fingers.

"You've been with us four years," he said at last. "Good years. You have a nose, Voss. I'll grant you that. Better than any investigator I've had in this chair, and I've had some fine ones." He laced his fingers together on the desktop. "Which is why I'm going to say this to you plainly, and you're going to hear it plainly. The Halloran account is worth more to this firm than any single claim against it. Edmund Halloran insures forty-one vessels through our marine division. He sits on two boards that this firm would very much like to sit on. He is, in the commercial sense, a friend of the house."

"A friend of the house is filing fraudulent claims."

"I said the account was valuable. I didn't say the man was honest." Something moved behind the pale eyes — not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "The world runs on arrangements, Voss. Firms like ours survive not by being right but by being useful. Now." He drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, unhurried, and made a show of thinking. "You believe there's fraud in the Halloran book. Very well. Prove it — quietly. No referrals, no police, no marine board. Find me the leak before anyone else finds it, and we'll decide internally how it gets handled. If you're right, you'll have done this firm a service it won't forget. If you're wrong—" the almost-smile surfaced fully now, thin and dry as old varnish, "—then you'll have learned something about certainty, which is also a service."

"That isn't an investigation," Mara said. "That's a private errand."

"It's discretion. There's a difference, and the difference is your career." He uncapped his pen. "One more thing. There may be a diver involved. An outside man — someone who worked the recoveries for Halloran's survey contractors, or who knows too much about them. Name of Kavanagh. Bram Kavanagh. Ex-navy clearance diver, if the gossip is worth anything. Find him. Find out what he knows, what he's seen, who he's talked to. Quietly, Voss. Off the books. Nothing in writing beyond what stays in your drawer."

The name landed somewhere in her chest and sat there, unexplained. "Why a diver? The fraud is on paper. Paper doesn't dive."

"Paper is written by people," Crane said, signing something at the bottom of the blank sheet, which Mara found herself noting as a small masterpiece of style, "and people talk. Especially frightened ones. Kavanagh may be nothing. He may be everything. You won't know until you've shaken his hand and looked in his eyes, and neither will I. That's why I pay you."

He slid the blank-signed sheet across the desk. Letterhead authorization: expenses approved, travel approved, access to the Halloran correspondence files approved. Everything she needed. Nothing that named the purpose.

"Off the books," she repeated.

"Completely. If Halloran hears this firm is sniffing at him before we're ready, forty-one policies walk out the door and take their premiums with them. So you'll travel as yourself, but not as ourselves. Marine surveyor, consultant, whatever suits the port you're standing in. You're good at faces, Voss. Use one."

She took the sheet. It felt lighter than paper should feel, the way a key feels light until you understand how many doors it opens and how few of them you'd want to walk through.

"One question," she said, standing. "If I find what I think I'll find — crews, Mr. Crane. If those nine ships went down with people aboard, and the paper says otherwise — do I bring that to you, or does it go to the board?"

Crane aligned his pen with the blotter's edge, a small, exact motion, and gave her the full weight of his patience.

"You bring everything to me," he said. "Everything goes through this office. That is not negotiable, and it is not a test. It is how this firm has kept its name clean for eighty years — by deciding, deliberately and in private, what the world needs to know about us. Go home, Voss. Take the morning train north tomorrow. And Voss—" he had already picked his newspaper back up, eyes descending, voice dropping into that register just below command, "—the drawer file. Keep it locked."

She was three steps from his door when he added, without looking up:

"Kavanagh. If you find him, don't spook him. Men who dive on wrecks they shouldn't are either running toward something or away from it, and either way they startle easy."

* * *

Her flat above the chandlery on Boundary Street was small and smelled of salt and old varnish, which she liked, because it meant the river was close even when she couldn't see it. She packed that night by lamplight: two practical skirts, oilskins, the good boots, a canvas case of instruments — sextant, parallel rules, the brass depth gauge her father had left her, still keeping honest time after twenty years. Into the false bottom of the instrument case went the photographs she'd had copied that afternoon from the Halloran correspondence files: survey contracts, crew rosters, a payment schedule to a salvage contractor whose registered address turned out to be a laundry in Townsville.

And one photograph that wasn't a document at all. It had been tucked into the back of a contract folder, unexplained — a press clipping, eight years old, yellowed at the fold. *NAVY DIVER CITED FOR VALOR IN WRECK RECOVERY,* the headline ran, above a photograph of a young man squinting into a sun that had nothing to do with the camera. Broad shoulders already going soft around the middle with the particular strength of men who work underwater. Dark hair cut short. Eyes that looked at the lens the way a man looks at a current he's decided to swim anyway.

The caption gave his name. Bram Kavanagh, leading seaman, clearance diving branch. Commended for recovering bodies from a naval wreck whose name the article never printed — *a restricted site off Thursday Island*, the text said, *details withheld.*

Details withheld. She'd read enough redacted files in four years of insurance work to know what that phrase cost, and who usually paid it.

Mara sat on the edge of her narrow bed with the clipping in her lap while the rain worked the tin roof, and told herself the story she was supposed to believe: that she was an investigator doing her job, that Crane's caution was ordinary corporate cowardice, that finding Bram Kavanagh was simply the next line item in a methodical inquiry. It was a good story. It had the advantage of being mostly true.

But underneath it, in the place where she kept the things she knew and didn't yet say, another thought moved slowly, like something big turning over in deep water: Aldous Crane had given her a signed, blank authorization and told her to keep nothing in writing. Aldous Crane, who counted paperclips. A man like that didn't loose an investigator off the books to protect an account. He loosed one to control what she found — or to make sure that whatever was found, only one person ever heard of it.

And he had known the diver's name before she'd brought him hers. She was nearly certain of it. He hadn't reached for a file, hadn't asked her to spell it. *Kavanagh* had come off his tongue smooth as ballast, already loaded.

She put the clipping face-down in the instrument case, on top of the false bottom, where a thing could be carried without being looked at.

The morning train went north through country steaming under the last of the rain — cane fields, drowned paddocks, rivers the color of milk tea running high and fast toward the coast. Mara rode with her forehead against the window and the Halloran file open on her knees, and somewhere past Gympie she caught herself doing arithmetic she hadn't meant to do: nine ships, six years, one stretch of reef. And a navy diver commended for pulling bodies off a restricted wreck, walking free in the world with details withheld stitched into his service record like a wound sewn shut from the inside.

Whatever connected those facts, she intended to be the one who found it first.

The train whistled for a crossing. Somewhere ahead, past the sugar and the rain, the Coral Sea waited with its nine red crosses strung along the reef like a rosary, and at the end of the string, a man who trusted no harbor, who did not yet know that a woman with a forged smile and his photograph in her bag was already on the road to find him.
