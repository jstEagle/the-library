## 4. Salt and Small Debts

The Pelican looked like she'd been built by committee and finished by argument.

Bram stood on the Cairns wharf at first light and studied her across the gap of grey water, hands in his pockets, the oilskin packet a flat weight against his ribs where it rode inside his jacket. Forty feet of trawler, maybe fifty years old, hull patched in three different colors of paint that had never been introduced to each other. Her wheelhouse listed a degree to port the way old men lean into a wind they've stopped fighting. Nets hung from a gallows aft like the rigging of a ship that had given up fishing and taken up hanging things.

She was perfect. Nobody with money looked twice at a boat like that.

He'd been watching her for two days. Not her, exactly — the traffic around her. The way a wiry woman with close-cropped grey hair and a forearms-like-cable handshake had argued with a chandler over an invoice until the man's ears went red. The way a broad Islander in an engineer's coverall had crawled out of her engine bay black to the elbows and laughing at something only he could hear. The way a kid — a girl, sixteen maybe, all elbows and suspicion — had appeared on deck only after the chandler left, like a cat that knew which visitors were safe.

No company flags. No survey gear. No men in clean shirts with clipboards. Just a working boat running on stubbornness and small debts, which was the only kind of economy Bram trusted anymore.

He bought two bags of ice and a carton of engine oil from the chandler — the same chandler, still red-eared — and carried them down the wharf like a man with somewhere to be. When he stepped onto the Pelican's gangway, the girl materialized at the rail above him as if she'd been poured there.

"You can't come aboard."

"I'm carrying ice."

"Then leave it and go."

He set the bags down on the planks, deliberately, where they'd start sweating in the sun. "They'll be water by noon. Your call."

The girl looked at the ice, at him, back at the ice. Her face performed a brief, furious negotiation with itself. "Wait there," she said, and vanished.

* * *

The wiry woman came down the wheelhouse stairs wiping her hands on a rag that had once, in some earlier geological age, been white. Up close she was shorter than she'd seemed from the wharf and harder than she'd seemed from anywhere — a face like a knuckle, eyes the flat brown of channel water at low tide. She looked at Bram the way a customs officer looks at a suitcase.

"You're the one who's been standing on the wharf for two days counting my rivets."

"Checking your waterline," Bram said. "You're carrying a list to starboard. Fouled bottom on that side, or you're storing heavy."

Her eyes narrowed a fraction, which he suspected was the equivalent of another man raising his eyebrows in surprise. "And you'd know."

"I dive. Bottoms are my business."

"Uh-huh." She came to the rail and looked down at the ice, sweating patiently. "Nell Okonkwo. This is the Pelican. She's not hiring, she's not selling, and she's not going where you want to go."

"You don't know where I want to go."

"Everyone who looks at this boat wants to go north, and everyone who wants to go north is either running from something or chasing something, and both kinds cost more than they pay." She said it without heat, the way you'd recite a tide table. "So. Which are you?"

Bram had prepared answers to that question in three languages and none of them came. What came instead was the truth, or a slice of it, which was the most dangerous thing he'd done in weeks. "Running," he said. "But I pay my way. I'm a salvage diver, eleven years, navy before that. Your hull's fouled, your prop's probably nicked — I heard it in your idle yesterday, a flutter on the third beat. I can clean and inspect wet, no slip, no yard fees, no questions. That's worth more than ice."

Nell Okonkwo studied him for a long moment. Behind her, at the top of the stairs, the big engineer had appeared without sound, wiping his hands, watching with the open, unhurried interest of a man who had all day and intended to use it.

"Tui," Nell said without turning. "You hear a flutter?"

"On the third beat," the engineer agreed comfortably. "Been telling you for a month it's the prop."

"You told me it was the shaft bearing."

"It could also be the prop. I contain multitudes."

Nell's mouth did something that was almost a smile and then thought better of it. She looked back down at Bram. "Wet dive on my hull. Today. If you find what you say you'll find, we talk about passage. If you drown, that's your own affair, but you'll sign nothing that makes you my affair first. I've got enough paper problems."

"I'll want a waiver anyway," Bram said. "I write them myself."

That got the almost-smile all the way out. "Pili," she called, and the girl leaned over the rail, glaring. "Get the man a tank. And move the ice before it's soup."

* * *

The bottom of the Pelican was a confession.

Bram hung in the green half-light under her keel, regulator hissing, running his palms over plating that told her whole history in rust and repair. A grounding scar along the port strake, old, sanded and filled by someone who cared. Newer patches — two, three years — welded in a hurry by someone who didn't. A trail of barnacles thick on the starboard side, thin to port, confirming the list. And there, just aft of the propeller aperture, the nick he'd promised: a crescent bitten out of one blade, smooth-edged, probably a piling or a submerged log, catching water on every third revolution like a stutter in a sentence.

He scraped the fouling off in long strokes, working methodically, letting the work quiet the part of his mind that was always, always listening. That was the thing about being under: the sea didn't care what you were running from, and there was a mercy in that. Down here there were no harbors to be turned out of, no names on manifests, no stamped cases with serial numbers that you burned in a drum at three in the morning and still saw when you closed your eyes. Down here there was just the next handhold.

He'd been ordered to forget a wreck once. He'd learned that the sea never forgets anything; it just holds it where no one looks. He'd been trying to live the same way for six years, and it was working about as well as you'd expect.

He surfaced by the swim step, mask up, and called it. "Nick's about eight centimeters, trailing edge, blade three. I can fair it wet with a file and it'll hold you till you find a yard. Bottom's clean now. You'll pick up half a knot."

Tui's head appeared over the transom, upside down, grinning. "Half a knot! Nell, we're rich."

"We're a half-knot less late," Nell said, appearing beside him. She crouched at the step, looking at Bram treading water, and something in her face had shifted — not warmer, exactly. Recalibrated. "You came up on your reserve?"

"Two hundred pounds."

"You counted while working?"

"I always count."

She grunted, which he was beginning to understand was her highest form of praise. "Breakfast's at seven, work's at eight, and the only free thing on this boat is opinion. You want passage north, you dive, you haul, you fix, and you stay out of my chart table. That last one's not negotiable."

"Fair," Bram said, and thought: *she has no idea how much I needed to hear that last part.*

* * *

The Pelican went north the next morning on a falling tide, and Bram learned the boat by learning its people, which was the same thing.

Tui was the engine — literally and, it seemed, temperamentally. He was from somewhere in the Solomons by his accent, built like the concept of load-bearing, and he talked to the main engine the way other men talked to horses, low and coaxing, his big hands moving over the block with a gentleness that would have surprised anyone who'd only seen him swing a spare cylinder head one-handed. He'd taken Bram down into the engine room the first afternoon, shown him every system twice, and said only, "If I'm asleep and she makes a sound you don't know, you wake me. If you make a sound I don't know, I wake you. That's the whole religion." Bram, who had spent six years believing that the whole religion was trusting nobody, found something in his chest go loose and didn't know what to do about it.

Pili he couldn't figure, and the not-knowing itched at him. She was sixteen at most, island-born, quick as a deck ape and twice as jumpy. She worked like she was proving something to a jury that wasn't aboard, and she watched Bram constantly — not the open staring of the curious but the flat surveillance of someone cataloguing exits. He recognized it because he wore the same expression in mirrors. Stowaway, Nell had said, in the tone of someone describing weather that had blown in and never quite left. He caught fragments: a father in Honiara with a temper and a fishing boat of his own; a mother gone; an aunt who'd looked at Pili like a debt. Nell had found her in a locker in Cairns with three days of stolen biscuits and had, instead of calling the harbor police, put her on the payroll at a wage that was an insult and a kindness in the same figure. Pili had never once said thank you. She'd never once been late, either.

On the third evening, running up the coast with the sun going copper behind them, she cornered him at the rail.

"You checked my knots," she said. Accusation.

"Your bowline was a granny knot wearing a costume."

"I fixed it."

"You did. Faster than most deckhands twice your age." He kept his eyes on the water. With jumpy things, you didn't look straight at them.

She was quiet for a while. The wake talked to itself below them. "Nell says you were navy."

"Nell says a lot of things."

"Were you?"

"Eleven years."

"Doing what?"

"Diving."

"What kind of diving?"

Bram turned his head and looked at her then, and whatever she saw in his face made her take half a step back, though her chin came up to cover it. "The kind you get ordered to do," he said, "and then get ordered to say you didn't." He softened it with the only currency he had. "Your knots are better. Your steering could use work. Ask Nell to teach you the wheel when she's in a good mood."

"She's never in a good mood."

"She's in a good mood right now. Can't you tell?"

Pili looked aft to where Nell stood at the wheel, jaw set, scanning the horizon like it owed her money, and Bram watched something happen in the girl's face — the corner of a smile, quickly imprisoned. "That's her good mood?"

"That's her best one. I've seen the archive."

The imprisoned smile escaped for almost two full seconds. Then Pili remembered herself, scowled, and went forward to coil a line that didn't need coiling. But the surveillance had changed. She still watched him. She just wasn't only cataloguing exits anymore.

* * *

The map he kept where he'd kept it since Port Moresby: sewn into the lining of the jacket he never let out of his sight, washed around, slept on. At night, in the fo'c'sle bunk with the boat's sounds all around him — Tui's snore like a slow pump, the sea on the planks, the creak of a vessel talking in her sleep — he would put his hand flat against his ribs and feel the oilskin's edge and do the arithmetic he'd been doing since a dying man had gripped his wrist with blood-cold fingers.

*See it found, or see it destroyed. Promise me, diver. Promise me on something you still believe in.*

He'd promised on the sea, because it was the only thing left on the list. He hadn't known then that the name Wren had choked out with his last breath — *Halloran* — would follow him north like a shark's fin. He hadn't known anything except that a man shouldn't die holding a secret, and that Bram Kavanagh had already failed one crew by surviving them, and his hands couldn't hold a second failure.

So he kept the map sewn against his heart like a debt, and he hauled nets and scraped bottoms and fixed knots, and he did not go near Nell's chart table, and every hour the warmth of the Pelican's crew crept further into him like water into a hull, slow and below the waterline, in the place you don't check until you're taking on too much to pump.

He knew the danger of it. Warmth was how they got the last crew — not through the hull, but through each other. You silence men one at a time, but you silence a crew by making them a crew first, so that the silencing of one pulls the rest in like a net. He'd watched it happen from a distance of two hundred miles and a navy security clearance, and he'd run ever since, and running had kept him alive and kept him nothing, and he'd told himself those were the same thing.

But on the fifth night, Tui woke him — the one sound Bram didn't know, a knocking from forward, irregular — and it turned out to be the anchor chain chafing its pipe, a ten-minute fix, and afterward Tui didn't go back to his bunk. He sat on the fo'c'sle hatch in the moonlight and split a green coconut he'd been saving and handed half to Bram without asking, and they ate it in silence like two men who'd known each other long enough to need no words, and when Tui finally stood and stretched and said, "Good to have hands aboard who count," Bram lay back in his bunk and admitted it to the dark overhead:

He was in trouble. Not the kind with guns in it. The worse kind.

* * *

It was Nell who almost caught it, on the ninth day, and it was an accident of weather.

A squall came down at dusk out of the northeast, the first real taste of the season, and Nell took them into a mangrove anchorage behind a spit with the ease of a woman who'd been hiding from weather in this exact water for thirty years. While the rain hammered the wheelhouse and Pili whooped from the bow like the wild thing she was, Nell spread a chart on the table and started penciling the next leg north, and Bram stood at the shoulder of the doorway — deliberately at the doorway, deliberately the shoulder — and watched her pen move.

She was good. Better than good. Her courses hugged water most skippers wouldn't trust, shortcuts through passes that only came from years of local knowledge, and he found himself reading ahead of her pen out of pure habit, the navigator's reflex the navy had ground into him, and that was his mistake.

Because her pen was heading north, and his eyes were already north, and his eyes stopped — just for a fraction, just the flicker of a man who has seen a familiar face in a crowd — at a set of coordinates that sat maybe forty miles off the line she'd drawn. Coordinates he could plot in his sleep. Coordinates a dying man had pressed into his hands in oilskin and blood.

Nell's pen stopped.

She didn't look up. She finished her pencil line, squared the chart, rolled it, and said, in a voice as level as a spirit gauge, "Interesting."

"I was reading the coast."

"You were reading a spot of empty ocean forty miles east of the coast, and you read it like it owed you money." She looked up then, channel-water eyes, and Bram understood that he had badly underestimated how much a woman like Nell Okonkwo saw while appearing to see nothing. "I've carried runners and chasers and one government man who cried, Kavanagh, and every one of them had a spot of ocean like that. So here's how it goes. You can tell me it's nothing, and I'll believe you, because it's your business and I keep out of my crew's business. Or you can tell me it's something, and I'll decide whether it's the kind of something that rides on my boat."

Rain drummed on the roof. Aft, faintly, he could hear Pili laughing at something Tui had said, and the sound went through Bram like a blade finding a seam.

"Nothing," he said.

Nell held his eyes for three full seconds — long enough to tell him she didn't believe a word, and long enough to tell him she'd let it stand — and then she rolled the chart the rest of the way and said, "Breakfast's at seven," and went out into the rain to check the anchor.

Bram stood alone in the wheelhouse with the map a flat weight against his ribs and the laughter of a family he hadn't asked for drifting aft through the rain, and he understood that the arithmetic had changed. It wasn't just his secret anymore, and it wasn't just his life. Whatever Wren had chained him to, the Pelican was already towing it, whether he told them or not.

And two days later, when the trawler came up out of the southeast with her working lights off and her silhouette wrong — too clean, too new, too many antennas for a boat with no name painted on her bow — and Nell lowered her binoculars and said, "Tui, kill the galley fire, we've got company," Bram took the glasses and looked at the stranger's stern and felt the bottom drop out of his stomach.

He knew that silhouette. He'd seen it riding at anchor off a Port Moresby tavern, the night a dying man had made him promise.

They'd found the current he was swimming in. And they were following it north.
