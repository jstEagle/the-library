## 5. Following the Paper Trail

The ledgers smelled of dust and money, which was to say they smelled of nothing at all.

Mara Voss had been in the Halloran Shipping archive for six days, and she had learned that the truth about a company rarely lived in its documents. It lived in the gaps between them. The paper told you what the company wanted remembered; the silence told you what it wanted forgotten. She had made a career of reading silences.

The archive occupied the third floor of Halloran House on Eagle Street, a room of grey steel cabinets and green-shaded lamps that some designer forty years ago had decided should look like a bank vault and had succeeded too well. A clerk named Ferro brought her boxes on a trolley and watched her work with the flat patience of a man paid to notice nothing. He brought tea at eleven. He never asked what she was looking for. That, more than anything Crane had said to her, told her this assignment was not an ordinary one.

Ordinary assignments came with ordinary paperwork. This one had come as a verbal instruction, delivered across Crane's desk on a Tuesday afternoon while rain walked down the windows of the fourteenth floor like fingers counting them.

"A diver named Kavanagh," Crane had said. "Find him. Quietly."

"Quietly how?"

"The way water's quiet." He had smiled then, and Aldous Crane's smile was a famous thing around the office — warm, creased, entirely without information. "A claim's gone sideways. Salvage fraud, we think. Someone's been filing losses against policies they shouldn't hold. Kavanagh's name keeps surfacing near the edges of it. I want to know if he's a fraudster or a witness before anyone upstairs decides he's a problem."

"Upstairs being?"

"Being upstairs," Crane said pleasantly, and turned back to his window, and she had understood that the conversation was over.

Now, six days later, she sat under a green lamp with a ledger open in front of her and the first real shape of something wrong rising out of the columns like a hull coming up through dark water.

* * *

It began, as these things did, with weather.

Insurance companies kept weather the way churches kept saints — reverently, exhaustively, everywhere. Every marine loss filed against a Halloran policy carried with it the meteorological record of the sea it had happened in: bureau reports, barometric traces, ship-shore radio logs where they existed. You could not lose a vessel in a storm that no one else had noticed. The ocean was large but the records were larger.

Except that four ships in seven years had managed exactly that.

The *Corvina*, lost 1998, position logged two hundred miles east of Bougainville. The bureau's synoptic charts for that week showed a high-pressure ridge sitting over the Coral Sea like a fat cat on a cushion. No gale. No swell event. The master's final report, dictated by radio before the ship went down, described "sudden structural failure aft, taking water fast." The report was signed by a relief master whose name appeared nowhere else in Halloran's crew rosters.

The *Anselm*, lost 2000, same region. Same story: failure, flooding, gone. Cargo manifest heavy with machinery — dredging equipment, compressors, things with serial numbers. The salvage claim was filed by a company called Meridian Recovery Partners, which shared a registered address with a Halloran subsidiary and, when Mara pulled the corporate filings, shared three directors with Halloran Shipping itself.

She sat very still for a long moment after that, the lamp humming above her, Ferro somewhere behind her shelving boxes with the soft scrape of cardboard.

Ghost salvage. The term surfaced from her training days like a body coming up out of a lake. It wasn't common — it required either extraordinary luck or extraordinary control — but it existed in the case studies, always in past tense, always elsewhere. A company insures its own cargo through layers of subsidiaries. Then it loses the ship. Deliberately. On paper to weather or misadventure; in fact to a prepared scuttling in deep water, away from casual divers. The insurers pay out. The cargo — often bought cheap, insured dear — is lifted later by a recovery firm that is also yours. You collect twice on the same steel: once from the policy, once from the market. And the ship itself, the inconvenient hull with its crew and its maintenance costs, simply ceases to exist.

The case studies never mentioned the crews. That was the part the textbooks left in a footnote, if they mentioned it at all: that ships have people aboard, and people are not cargo, and a scuttling done properly is done without witnesses.

Four ships in seven years. Four payouts. Four recoveries by Meridian Partners. And every loss clustered in the same corridor of the Coral Sea, a stretch of water so consistently calm that the bureau's own climatological summary described it as "among the most benign operating environments in the South Pacific."

Ships do not drown in benign water. Not four times. Not with the same signature.

Mara turned pages until the light outside the archive windows went from gold to violet, and Ferro cleared his throat and said, "We close at six, miss," and she realized her tea had gone cold hours ago.

* * *

Crane found her at her desk the next morning, which was unusual, because Aldous Crane did not walk to other people's desks; other people's desks were summoned to him.

He stood at the corner of her workstation with his hands in his pockets and looked, with an expression of mild regret, at the printouts fanned across it. Bureau charts. Payout schedules. The Meridian Partners filings.

"You've been busy," he said.

"I've been thorough. There's a difference."

"There usually is." He picked up one sheet — the *Corvina*'s final radio log — and read it the way a man reads a menu he has already ordered from. "You want to know what I see here?"

"I imagine you'll tell me."

"I see an investigator finding patterns in noise. Ships sink, Voss. It's the oldest story in our business. Old hulls, tired welds, masters who panic and pump the wrong ballast. You put four losses on a chart and draw lines between them and suddenly it looks like a conspiracy, because human beings can't help drawing lines. It's why we love constellations." He set the page down and aligned it, precisely, with the others. "But this isn't astronomy. It's actuarial science. And the science says these are within normal variance for a fleet that size."

"Normal variance doesn't include a recovery partner owned by the insured."

Something moved behind Crane's eyes — not anger, nothing so legible. A shutter adjusting. "Meridian is an arm's-length contractor. Standard practice. You know that."

"I know that arm's length is measured differently depending on who's holding the tape."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Down on Eagle Street the traffic made its river-sound, and the rain had stopped and the glass towers were doing their morning trick of pretending to be clean.

"Mara," Crane said, and now his voice changed, dropped into the register he used for performance reviews and condolences, "I'm going to give you some advice, and I'd like you to take it in the spirit it's given. Drop the pattern. Find the diver. Kavanagh is the file. Kavanagh is what you were asked for. Whatever you think you've found in those ledgers, it predates your employment and it will postdate your departure, and the only thing your digging will change is the shape of your career when you leave this building. Do you understand me?"

"Not entirely."

"No," he agreed. "I don't suppose you do." He smiled — the warm, creased, informationless smile — and patted the edge of her desk twice, like a man closing a lid. "Kavanagh. Find me the diver."

He walked away, and Mara looked at the aligned papers, and understood that she had just been shown the exact boundary of what she was permitted to know, which meant she had been shown where the knowledge lived.

Aldous Crane had been at Halloran for nineteen years. He knew where everything was filed. And a man does not warn you off a scent unless he already knows what you're tracking.

* * *

She dug anyway. Of course she dug anyway. Her mother had once told her, exasperated, that Mara had been born with a hand already reaching for whatever was on the highest shelf, and her father had said worse things than that about her stubbornness, and both of them had been right, and here she was.

She went home that night instead of going up — home being a rented room in a Queenslander in West End with a sagging veranda and a landlady who left tomatoes on the rail — and she worked from her personal terminal, because whatever she did next, she would not do it inside Halloran's walls.

The four lost ships gave her their corridors. The corridors gave her the dates. And the dates gave her something she hadn't expected: a fifth entry, older than the rest, buried not in Halloran's records at all but in the public registry of naval incidents — a government wreck, declassified, listed with a coordinates block that fell squarely inside the same quiet water.

The listing was thin. Date, general position, cause of loss given as "structural failure during exercises," and a single line that made her sit forward on the sagging chair with her heart going oddly:

*Salvage and recovery contracted: Meridian Recovery Partners.*

A navy wreck. Recovered by the same private firm. Seven years before the *Corvina* went down.

So it went further back. So it wasn't a shipping company's vice — it was a system, and the system had learned its trade somewhere, and the somewhere had a naval prefix.

And threaded through the incident report's annexes, in the lists of civilian contractors and survey personnel attached to the recovery operation, was a name.

*Kavanagh, B. — diver, contract.*

She pulled his service record next, expecting the usual — postings, commendations, discharge. What she got was a document that had been eaten. Whole paragraphs replaced by black bars. Dates excised. His final two years of service reduced to a sequence of redactions so dense the page looked like a window painted shut. And beneath the redactions, a stamp she recognized from her regulatory training, the kind of stamp that made lawyers go quiet:

*Sealed by order. Access restricted. Ref: [excised].*

Sealed service records were rare. Sealed records for an enlisted diver were rarer still. Sealed records for an enlisted diver who then turned up, years later, drifting through the exact waters where five ships had drowned — that was not a coincidence. That was a thread, and someone had tied it off and melted the end.

Mara sat on the veranda after midnight with the tomatoes cooling beside her and the city murmuring below, and she made herself think like an investigator instead of like a woman who wanted the answer to be simple.

Two possibilities. One: Bram Kavanagh was part of it. A contract diver who'd worked the ghost operations, who knew where the bodies and the bullion lay, and who was now — what? Blackmailing? Running his own recovery? Filing fraudulent claims of his own, as Crane suggested?

Or two: he had seen something on one of those dives that he was never supposed to see, and he had run, and he had been running ever since, and the reason his record was sealed was that the people who sealed it could not afford what he knew.

She thought about Crane's smile. About *find me the diver*. About the way he had aligned her papers, edge to edge, like a man tidying a scene.

If it was possibility one, Crane wanted Kavanagh found to close a nuisance file.

If it was possibility two, Crane wanted Kavanagh found for altogether different reasons, and she had just been handed the shovel.

Either way, there was only one direction the trail ran: north. The last confirmed sighting of a vessel associated with Kavanagh put him in Port Moresby three weeks ago. After that, nothing — except a trawler's movement report she'd pulled from the coastal traffic listings, a leaky old working boat called the *Pelican*, northbound, licensed out of Thursday Island, with a skipper named Okonkwo who had a reputation for hiring strangers.

Mara went inside and took a bottle of beer from the fridge and drank half of it standing at the kitchen window, watching moths batter themselves against the porch light, and admitted the thing she had been not-admitting since Tuesday.

She wasn't going to stop. Whatever Crane wanted, whatever the seal on that record protected, she was going to follow this to the bottom, because four ships and a navy wreck and a man painted shut inside his own history was not variance. It was a grave with a ledger attached, and she had spent ten years learning to read ledgers, and nobody — not Aldous Crane, not Edmund Halloran, not whoever held the stamp — was going to tell her to look away and have it work.

She finished the beer. She booked herself onto the Cairns flight for Friday morning, under her own name, because forged papers were a line she hadn't crossed yet, and told herself the cover story she'd use at the dock — marine surveyor, routine hull inspections, nothing to see — was merely efficient rather than dishonest.

Then she opened the file one last time and looked at the photograph clipped to the front of it: a service portrait, years old, of a man with a jaw like a mooring bollard and eyes that had already learned not to trust the camera.

*Bram Kavanagh,* she thought. *What did you bring up out of the water?*

Three thousand miles north, on a dark sea, a trawler with her galley fire killed was watching an unnamed ship with too many antennas come up out of the southeast — and the current that would carry them all was already running.
