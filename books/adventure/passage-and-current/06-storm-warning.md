## 6. Storm Warning

The charter came to them at Thursday Island, which should have been the first warning. Nothing good came to the Pelican at Thursday Island; the place was a magnet for men with too much money and too little patience, and Nell Okonkwo had a standing rule about both.

But we were broke. The trawler's fuel bill alone would have sunk a smaller vessel, and Tui had been muttering about the port-side engine mount for a week in that quiet way of his that meant it was worse than he was letting on. So when a well-dressed man in deck shoes came down the pontoon with a contract already typed and folded in his breast pocket — a survey job, two weeks' work running hydrographic lines off a reef system three hundred miles northeast — Nell looked at the money, looked at me, and looked at the money again.

"Two weeks of honest work," she said, signing. "God knows we're due some."

I didn't say anything. I'd learned long ago that the words *honest work* and *typed contract* rarely shared a page, but I was four days into knowing these people and I had no standing to object. I was the diver they'd picked up to pay my passage north, sleeping in a bunk that smelled of diesel and old fish, eating Pili's terrible rice, and telling myself every morning that I'd be gone at the next port. I hadn't left yet. That worried me more than the contract did.

* * *

The man's name was Callis, and he arrived on the second day with two assistants and enough electronic gear to outfit a small university. He was pleasant in the way of a man who has never once needed anything from anyone: crisp shirts pressed by someone else, a smile calibrated to the millimeter, hands soft as a bureaucrat's. He called me "the dive contractor" and shook my hand for exactly one and a half seconds.

"Kavanagh, is it?" he said, reading from a clipboard he didn't need. "Any experience with side-scan interpretation?"

"Enough."

"Good. Good." He smiled again. "We're mapping approaches for a proposed shipping corridor. Insurance requirements, you understand. Very routine."

I understood. I also understood that shipping corridors were not surveyed three hundred miles from any shipping lane, in water no freighter drew, over reef systems that appeared on no commercial route. But I said nothing, because saying nothing was a skill I'd polished for eleven years, and because a small cold thing had begun to form behind my sternum that I recognized the way you recognize a change in barometric pressure before the sky shows it.

We ran lines for five days. Callis sat in the wheelhouse with Nell, charming her with stories about Sydney, while his assistants lowered the towfish and watched the returns scroll across their screens. I dove the transect buoys, checked moorings, did the grunt work a charter expects, and kept one eye always on what the survey was actually looking at.

On the fifth night, unable to sleep in the wet heat, I climbed to the wheelhouse where Tui stood the midnight watch and asked him, casually, to show me the track chart. He slid it across without comment. Tui was a man who missed nothing and remarked on less.

The survey grid lay over open water — and beneath that open water, if Wren's map was half what the dying man had sworn it was, lay the Meridian Star.

I stood there a long time, watching the inked line crawl across the chart, and felt the cold thing behind my sternum crack open and spread.

"Problem?" Tui asked.

"No," I said. "Bad dream."

It wasn't a bad dream. It was memory, and memory is worse, because it doesn't fade when you wake.

* * *

I had seen Callis before. Not him specifically — I'd have remembered the deck shoes — but his kind, and worse than his kind, his *ship*. Three nights before Elias Wren bled out against my shoulder in a Port Moresby tavern, I'd sat in that same tavern's corner nursing a beer I couldn't afford, and I'd watched a table of six men talk in the low, economical way of professionals. They weren't locals. Their kit bags carried the flat, expensive look of contracted equipment, and one of them wore a jacket with a faded company crest I'd clocked without thinking: a stylized wave over a star.

Halloran Shipping. I hadn't known the name then. I knew it now, because Wren had gasped it into my ear along with the coordinates, pressing the oilskin map into my hands like a man passing a live coal. *Halloran,* he'd said. *They killed the Meridian's people and they'll kill whoever finds her. Promise me. See it found or see it destroyed.*

And now here was a Halloran survey vessel's shore team, chartered aboard the same trawler carrying the same man who held the map, running sonar lines directly over the wreck they'd murdered a shipload of sailors to bury.

I told myself it was coincidence. I was very good at telling myself things. Eleven years of practice. But coincidence doesn't put a survey grid over an unmarked grave in the middle of nowhere, and coincidence doesn't hire a crew whose shore party drinks in the same tavern where a prospector gets shot two days later.

I went aft, past the galley where Pili was supposed to be asleep and was instead reading a battered paperback by torchlight, and I stood at the stern rail and watched the phosphorescence stream away in our wake, and I made myself think it through like a man defusing something.

Option one: coincidence. Halloran Shipping was large; its survey contracts were legion; the Meridian Star was a legend every salvage man in the Coral Sea had chased at least once. Possible. Option two: they were hunting the wreck on their own intelligence and the Pelican was simply a convenient platform. Uncomfortable but survivable. Option three —

Option three was that Wren had talked before he died. That someone had seen an old prospector press a package into a stranger's hands, had reported it up whatever chain connected a Port Moresby tavern to a boardroom in Brisbane, and that the stranger had been found. That the stranger was currently under contract to the very people looking for what he carried, diving every day above the thing they wanted, sleeping eight feet from their technicians.

Option three explained why Callis had asked for my name off a clipboard he didn't need. Option three explained why one of the assistants photographed the Pelican's registration number on the first morning, casually, like a tourist. Option three explained the satellite phone Callis used every evening at 1900, standing at the bow with his back to the boat, speaking in a voice pitched below the wind.

I had run option three eleven years ago, off a navy wreck whose name I still couldn't say aloud, and I had waited too long then. The last crew who stayed got silenced — all four of them, drowned in a harbor with clean bilges and dry lungs, ruled accidental by a coroner who'd been paid in ways no ledger would ever show. I'd burned the thing I recovered rather than report it, and I'd burned the report too, and I'd been moving ever since, harbor to harbor, trusting no pier, no paper, no face. Moving was the only country I held citizenship in.

And now I was anchored. Now I had a bunk that smelled of diesel and old fish and a girl reading paperbacks in the dark and a skipper who'd signed a contract in good faith, and I was anchored directly over the grave of everything I'd spent eleven years outrunning.

* * *

I found Nell at dawn on the sixth day, drinking coffee on the port wing of the bridge while the sun came up the color of old brass. She was a broad, weathered woman with grey threading her braids and hands that had hauled line since before I was born, and she took one look at my face and set down her mug.

"Out with it," she said. "You've got the look of a man about to ask for something he thinks I'll refuse."

"I want us to break charter."

She didn't laugh, which I gave her credit for. "That's a breach. That's a black mark on this boat's record in every port from here to Suva. That's money we don't have." She studied me. "So make me believe it."

I'd rehearsed a dozen versions on the way up the ladder — mechanical trouble, weather concerns, a family emergency invented for a man who had none. All lies, and Nell Okonkwo could smell a lie the way a shark smells blood in water. So I gave her the truth, or the shape of it.

"That survey isn't mapping a shipping corridor," I said. "There's no corridor. There's no lane within four hundred miles. They're looking for a wreck, and it's not theirs to look for, and the people behind it have killed to keep it lost. I know because a man died in my arms in Moresby telling me so, and I know because I've seen how these operations end. Everyone near them ends badly. Us included."

Nell was quiet for a long moment. The wind moved her braids. Below us, I could hear Pili scrubbing the deck, and the rhythmic knock of Tui's hammer somewhere in the engine room, the small domestic sounds of a floating home.

"You're asking me to throw away a contract on your word," she said finally. "You've been aboard nine days."

"Yes."

"And this dead man. This prospector. What exactly did he give you?"

My hand moved toward my chest before I could stop it — the oilskin packet rode inside my shirt, against my skin, everywhere I went, even in the water. Nell saw the motion. Her eyes narrowed, and I watched her decide, visibly, not to push. Not yet.

"He gave me a promise to keep," I said. "That's all you need to know right now. What you need to know is that monsoon season is building — you've seen the pressure charts, same as I have — and in four days this window closes for weeks. And in those four days, either we're far from here, or we're in the middle of whatever these people do when they find what they're looking for."

"You think they'll find it?"

"I think they've been looking longer than we have. And I think—" I stopped. Started again. "I've seen what happens to crews that stay too close to this kind of secret. I'm not going to watch it happen to this one."

Something shifted in her face. Not belief — I don't think she believed me yet, not wholly — but recognition. She'd been at sea forty years; she'd seen men carry things they couldn't set down.

"One condition," she said. "You tell me the rest. All of it, before we're done running. No half-truths on my boat, Kavanagh. I've buried friends who died of other people's half-truths."

"All of it," I said. "Soon."

"Then get Tui. Tell him to bring her up to full revs and check the fuel. We'll run out the last of the charter's advance on supplies at the next anchorage and be gone before Callis finishes his breakfast." She stood, joints cracking, and looked east at the horizon where the sky had begun to stack itself into towers, blue-black and patient. "And Kavanagh? If you're wrong about this—"

"I'm not wrong."

"If you're wrong," she repeated, "you'll be swimming to the next port. Understood?"

"Understood."

* * *

We told Callis at 0800 that the Pelican had developed an engine fault requiring immediate return to Thursday Island. Tui, magnificent liar that he was, pulled apart a perfectly functioning fuel line on the aft deck and displayed the results to Callis with the mournful expression of a doctor delivering bad news. Callis was displeased. He made calls. He offered a bonus to wait for a relief vessel. Nell declined with the immovable courtesy of a woman who had outlasted cyclones, customs officers, and three marriages.

He accepted it gracefully. That was the part that frightened me most. A man who suspected nothing argues; a man who suspects everything smiles.

By noon we were clear of the survey ground and making nine knots northeast, riding ahead of a front that had begun organizing itself on the horizon like a slow thought turning ugly. The barometer had fallen twelve points since dawn. Tui whistled tunelessly in the engine room. Pili stood at the bow rail with her face into the wind, seventeen years old and gloriously unaware of how close she'd come to being cargo in someone else's war.

I stood beside her a while. She glanced up.

"You look like you've seen a ghost," she said.

"Worse. I've seen myself."

She laughed, thinking I was joking, and went forward to help Tui lash the deck cargo. I stayed at the rail and watched the storm build, and I let myself feel, for the first time in nine days, something adjacent to relief.

Then, at 1540, Tui's voice came over the intercom, flat and careful. "Bram. Bridge. You want to see this."

The radar showed a contact twenty-two miles astern, closing at better than twenty knots. Fast for a trawler. Fast for most things. I watched the blip crawl toward us across the screen and felt the cold thing behind my sternum close its fist.

At nineteen miles, the contact altered course — not toward Thursday Island, not toward any port, but directly onto our track, matching our heading with the patience of something that knew exactly where we were going and intended to arrive when we did.

Nell stood beside me, arms crossed, watching the screen. She didn't ask who it was. She'd read the same arithmetic I had: a survey team's support vessel, a fast hull, a course that mirrored ours through empty ocean.

"Well," she said quietly. "Either you're wrong about all this, or we're in it now."

Ahead of us, the monsoon's leading edge had come down over the sea like a dropped curtain, rain falling in grey sheets, lightning walking the horizon on legs of fire. Behind us, the contact closed another mile.

"Which is it, Kavanagh?" Nell asked. "Because I need to know what I'm steering into."

I watched the radar, and the radar watched us back, and somewhere south of us a man named Halloran was learning that his divers had come home empty.

"We're in it," I said. "Run."
