## 7. Two Currents Meet

The trouble with lying for a living, Mara Voss had learned, was that you got good at it in exactly the ways that made it dangerous. You learned to hold a stranger's eyes a half-second past comfort. You learned to let small truths do the work of large lies — *yes, I'm from Brisbane; yes, that's a survey case; yes, the weather's turning filthy* — until a person walked away feeling they'd known you all their lives and couldn't remember a single thing you'd told them.

She'd been good at it for six years. She was good at it now, standing on the fuel dock at Cairns with a forged marine surveyor's ticket in her jacket pocket and rain starting to spot the planks around her boots, watching a battered trawler named *Pelican* come up the channel like something dragged out of a wreck itself.

Aldous Crane's voice was still in her ear, though the call had ended two days and eleven hundred miles ago. *Find the diver. Quietly, Voss. No warrants, no paperwork, no ripples. A man named Kavanagh. He'll be moving north ahead of the season.* And beneath the words, the thing she hadn't said aloud to anyone, least of all herself: *why does an insurance company want a diver found off the books?*

The *Pelican* came alongside with more confidence than her hull deserved. Mara watched the deck through the rain: a broad woman at the helm with grey threading her braids and hands on the wheel like she'd been born holding one; a young islander coiling lines with quick economy; a kid — fifteen, maybe sixteen, all elbows and defiance — scrambling onto the dock to catch a mooring line before anyone asked her to.

And then a man stepped out of the wheelhouse shadow, and Mara forgot, briefly, to breathe in the practiced rhythm of her cover.

He wasn't what she'd expected. She'd built him from the file: navy diver, dishonourably separated, records sealed, last seen working salvage out of Thursday Island. She'd imagined someone harder-edged, coarser, a man shaped by whatever he'd done to earn a redaction. But Bram Kavanagh came down the gangway slowly, rolling his shoulders like the sea still lived in them, and his face was quiet the way deep water is quiet — no telling what moved underneath. His eyes went over the dock the way hers did over a ledger: reading it. Finding what didn't belong.

Which meant finding her.

She didn't flinch. She'd rehearsed this. She crossed to the trawler's rail with her survey case in hand and her badge of authority — the forged one, the one Tui would later swear he'd clocked in under four seconds — sitting easy in her posture.

"Morning," she called up. "Voss. Marine surveyor, Cairns office. Your skipper flagged a hull inspection request last week?"

"We did not," the woman at the rail said. Nell Okonkwo. Mara had her file too; everyone had a file if Crane wanted one. "We don't flag anything. We don't trust anything."

"Then your broker did," Mara said smoothly. "Standard pre-monsoon survey. Insurers are twitchy this time of year, and a vessel your age running north—" She shrugged, letting the sentence die where a real surveyor's would. "I'm here anyway. Might as well look at her while I'm dry-ish."

The kid on the dock snorted. The engineer — Tui, lean and watchful, wiping grease from his hands with a rag that had given up being a rag years ago — said nothing at all, which Mara noted, and filed, and worried about slightly more than she'd admit.

It was Kavanagh who answered. He came down the last two steps of the gangway and stood close enough that she could smell salt and diesel and, underneath it, something clean like rain on hot metal.

"Surveyors carry clipboards," he said. "You carry a case like it's got a body in it."

"It's got instruments in it."

"Mm." He looked at her hands, then her face, then back to her hands, and something almost like amusement touched the corner of his mouth and left again. "Ultrasonic thickness gauge?"

"Among other things."

"Then you won't mind showing me."

*There it is.* The first probe, delivered gently, like a hand testing ice. Mara smiled and opened the case, turning it toward him: gauge, torch, sounding tape, a laminated tide table she'd bought that morning from a chandlery that would remember her only as a woman who paid cash. He studied it longer than courtesy required, and when he looked up, his eyes held hers, and the spark jumped before either of them had agreed to strike it.

It was inconvenient. It was immediately, professionally inconvenient — the kind of recognition that happens between two people fluent in the same dishonest language, each hearing the other's accent. He knew she was performing. She knew he knew. Neither of them blinked, because blinking was losing, and because underneath the performance something else was happening that neither of them had scheduled: interest. Plain, human, unwelcome interest.

"Well," Kavanagh said, closing the case lid with one finger. "Hull's below. Mind the third plank. She bites."

"I've been bitten before."

"I bet you have," he said, and led her aboard.

* * *

The engine room smelled of oil and heat and old iron holding on by habit. Tui watched her from beside the main engine with the rag draped over his shoulder and his arms folded, saying nothing while Kavanagh pointed out frames and seams, and Mara ran her gauge along the hull plates and read numbers aloud in the flat tone of a woman doing a job.

The numbers were bad. She wasn't pretending about that part. Corrosion had eaten into the strakes above the turn of the bilge, and a weld near the stern gland had been repaired by someone with more courage than training.

"She leaks," Mara said, straightening. "Not dangerously. Yet. But you're pointing her north into monsoon season with plating thinner than my patience."

"You've got opinions," Kavanagh said.

"I've got a job."

"So do we." He crouched beside her at the bilge stringer, close enough that she caught the line of old scarring along his forearm — rope burn or cable burn, healed rough. "And our job doesn't stop for weather. So tell me honestly, Ms. Voss of the Cairns office: is there any version of your report that lets us sail?"

Mara held his gaze. This was the moment, she understood, where the whole architecture of her cover balanced. A real surveyor would hedge. A fake one would reassure too fast.

"A conditional pass," she said. "Pumps serviced, watch kept on that stern weld, and you don't push her hard in the worst of it. That's the honest answer. Whether it's the one you wanted—"

"It's the one I expected." Something shifted in his face — not warmth exactly, but a recalibration, a man adjusting a bearing. "You know what I do?"

"Diver. Word gets around the docks."

"Word does." He stood, offering his hand to help her up, and she took it, and the contact lasted one beat longer than the transaction required. His grip was careful, like he was used to handling things that broke. "Thanks for looking at her. Invoice the office."

"There's no office," Pili said from the engine room hatch, with the devastating clarity of the young.

"Pili," Nell's voice carried down from above, a warning wrapped in a name.

"What? She's got a laminated tide table for *Cairns* with *Thursday Island* tides printed on the back. Who laminates a mistake?"

Silence. Tui's rag stopped moving. Mara felt the floor tilt under her professional feet — not the ship, the situation. She turned, unhurried, and looked at the kid, who stared back with the fearless insolence of someone who has nothing yet to lose.

"Chandlery error," Mara said. "They refunded me five dollars and my dignity."

Pili's mouth twitched. It was, Mara realized with genuine alarm, possible she liked this child.

Kavanagh walked her up to the deck, and at the rail, with the rain thickening and the harbour lights coming on early through the murk, he paused.

"Your gauge," he said quietly. "Serial number's been ground off."

"Factory recall. They reissue them."

"They don't."

"No," Mara agreed. "They don't."

The rain fell between them. Somewhere across the harbour a ship's horn sounded, long and low, like the sea clearing its throat before speaking.

"Why are you really here?" he asked. Not hostile. Worse than hostile — genuinely asking, the way a man asks when he's decided the answer matters to him more than his safety should.

And Mara Voss, who had lied to customs officers, lawyers, widows filing claims on husbands lost at sea, and one very memorable archbishop, heard herself say: "I needed to see the boat."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I've got today."

He studied her for a long moment, rain running off his jaw, and whatever he saw made him nod once, slow, like a man marking a chart.

"Conditional pass," he said. "Mind how you go, Ms. Voss."

* * *

She watched the *Pelican* from the end of the pier until the rain took her, which took embarrassingly little time. The trawler didn't leave that evening — Tui had parts to chase, Nell had decided the barometer could keep its opinions to itself another twelve hours — but Mara had seen enough. The way Kavanagh had positioned himself between her and the wheelhouse. The way Nell's eyes had gone to him before answering any question, a crew already orbiting its newest gravity. The way the kid had watched her case like it might open and show teeth.

Back in her rented room above the chandlery, she laid out her notes and did what she always did: built the ledger. Facts in one column. Inferences in the other. In the facts column: Kavanagh was aboard, bound north, nervous in the specific way of a man expecting pursuit. In the inference column, growing darker by the hour: *he knows who's behind him. Which means he knows what Wren gave him. Which means Crane's real question isn't where the diver is — it's where the map is.*

Her phone buzzed. Crane.

"Cairns?" he said, without greeting.

"He's there. Aboard a trawler called *Pelican*. Outbound within a day, likely north."

"And the claim documents?"

"Not with him. If they exist."

"They exist." A pause, and when Crane spoke again his voice had the smooth weight of ballast shifting. "Voss. Bring me certainty, not surveillance. Halloran's people are also interested in Mr. Kavanagh, and I would prefer our firm's interests settled before theirs. Understood?"

Understood. She set the phone down and sat a long while listening to the rain hammer the tin roof, thinking about the word *interests*, and about a man's careful grip on her hand at a rail, and about a laminated lie that a teenager had undone in one sentence.

In the facts column, she wrote one more line, and hated how steady her handwriting was:

*He asked why I was really there. I wanted to tell him.*

* * *

Tui found Bram at the rail after midnight, when the harbour had gone quiet except for water slapping pilings and the *Pelican*'s pumps keeping their patient argument with the sea.

"She's not a surveyor," Tui said.

"I know."

"The gauge is decommissioned police-issue. The tide table's a prop. And she watches the wheelhouse, not the hull." Tui leaned beside him, passing a thermos of coffee that tasted like engine coolant and salvation. "Insurance?"

"Worse. Someone sent her. Question is who." Bram drank, watching the dark water. "Halloran's people come loud. Guns, boarding parties, men who ask questions with crowbars. She's not that. She's paper and patience." He was quiet a moment. "She's the kind they send when they want to find a thing without breaking it."

"Then we run."

"We run anyway — monsoon's coming regardless. But we don't run scared, Tui. We run smart." He handed back the thermos. "And nobody says a word about cargo, course, or charts where she can hear. If she comes aboard again—"

"She will," Tui said. "Did you see her look at you? That wasn't surveillance. That was a woman arguing with herself."

Bram said nothing. He was remembering the spark at the rail, the half-second too long, the way her defences had flickered — just once — when the kid had caught the tide table. He was remembering that he'd spent six years trusting no harbor, no face, no kindness offered without invoice, and that none of that discipline had prevented one inconvenient, unprofessional, entirely unwelcome thought:

*I hope she comes aboard again.*

Out in the channel, lightning walked the horizon on legs of fire, and the leading edge of the monsoon came down over the sea like a dropped curtain — and somewhere south of them, Mara Voss packed her case, ground the serial number off a second thought she should have abandoned, and booked herself passage on the next vessel heading north.

The *Pelican* sailed at dawn.

So did she.
