## 8. What the Map Shows

The Pelican lay at anchor off Snapper Island, riding a swell that came in long and lazy from the southeast, and Bram Kavanagh did not sleep.

He'd learned the trick of it years ago — sleeping with one ear open, one hand curled around whatever was nearest that could cut or crush. On navy ships they'd called it shark sleep. Half the brain awake, circling. He lay in the fo'c'sle bunk with his boots on and the oilskin packet flat against his belly under his shirt, and he listened to the boat talk to herself: the tick of cooling steel, Tui's generator muttering below, Pili's breathing from the opposite bunk — quick and light as a bird's, the way teenagers breathe when they haven't yet learned the world is heavy.

The swell changed just after two.

It was nothing a landsman would have caught. The rhythm went from long-and-lazy to short-and-chopped, the way water does when something big has come between it and the shore. Bram was already sitting up before his mind finished the arithmetic. A boat between them and the island. Running dark. Running quiet.

He slid out of the bunk without a sound and stood at the portlight, wiping the salt haze with the heel of his hand. Moonless night, overcast, the black water barely distinguishable from the black sky. And there — a shape where no shape should be. Low, wide-beamed, no running lights. An inflatable or a fast tender, coming on at a crawl, engine muffled to almost nothing.

They weren't fishermen. Fishermen ran lights and generators and noise, because out here darkness was death and silence was suspicion.

Bram moved. He woke Pili with a hand over her mouth first — she came up swinging, all elbows and terror, until her eyes found his in the dark and she stilled. He put one finger to his lips, then pointed at the portlight, then made a fist. *Stay.* Her chin set stubbornly, but she nodded.

Nell Okonkwo slept in the wheelhouse, half-sitting in the helm chair the way she always did, claiming a bad back and refusing the bunk aft. Bram climbed the ladder slow, testing each rung, and leaned close to her ear.

"Nell."

Her eyes opened instantly. That was the thing about Nell — she slept like a woman who'd been ambushed before and expected to be again. She read his face in the dark and didn't waste breath on questions. Her hand went to the shotgun racked above the chart table.

"How many?" she breathed.

"One tender so far. Maybe more behind."

"Course?"

"Coming up the western side. They'll board amidships if they're any good."

"They any good?"

"I don't know yet." He took the shotgun down, broke it, checked the shells by feel. "But nobody runs dark and silent at two in the morning for anything good."

Nell swung her legs down and stood, rolling her shoulders, a broad dark silhouette against the faint gray of the wheelhouse glass. "Tui," she said. "Wake him. Quietly. And Pili stays below — I mean it, Kavanagh. Whatever this is, she's cargo, not crew tonight."

"She's more crew than I am," Bram said, and meant it, and saw Nell register that.

Then the tender's engine coughed once — a single unmuffled bark, someone losing their nerve or their grip — and the whole careful choreography of stealth collapsed into speed.

* * *

They came aboard amidships, exactly as Bram had guessed, four of them in dark wet gear with balaclavas rolled down, moving with the crisp economy of men who'd done this before. Not pirates. Pirates would have been drunker, louder, greedier. These men boarded like auditors. One stayed with the tender, two came forward, one went straight aft toward the engine hatch — toward Tui.

Bram met the first pair at the foot of the wheelhouse ladder.

He didn't announce himself. There was no time for the theater of warnings, and anyway he'd learned long ago that the man who speaks first gives away the most. He simply stepped out of the shadow of the wheelhouse door as the lead man reached the third rung and drove the butt of the shotgun into the side of his head. The man dropped back onto his partner and both of them went down in a tangle of limbs and salt water on the deck plates.

The third man was better. He came around the starboard rail instead of up the ladder, and he had a pistol, and he wasn't shy about it. The first shot went through the wheelhouse window and sprayed Nell with glass; she answered with the shotgun and blew the rail apart a foot from his head, and the man decided discretion was the better part and dropped back over the side.

Which left the fourth man. The one who'd gone aft.

Bram heard it happen before he understood it: a short cry from below, high and young and furious, then a crash of tools, then Tui's voice roaring words in Samoan that Bram didn't know but understood completely.

By the time Bram got down the aft ladder, the fourth man had Pili.

She'd come up out of the engine room despite everything — of course she had, she was sixteen and immortal — and she'd armed herself with a pipe wrench, and she'd gotten one good swing in that would have felled an ox. But the man was bigger and faster and he had her wrist twisted up behind her back and a knife at her throat, and he was backing toward the rail with her held in front of him like a shield.

"The map," the man said. His voice was flat, professional, faintly accented — South African, maybe, or Australian worn thin by years elsewhere. "The oilskin packet. The old man gave it to you in Moresby. Give it here and the girl keeps her throat."

Everything in Bram went very still and very cold.

They knew about Wren. They knew about the tavern. Which meant they hadn't just been hunting the map — they'd been hunting *him*, since Port Moresby, maybe longer. The survey vessel, the charter job, the whole patient dance of the last week — it had all been a net drawn slowly around a leaking trawler and the man aboard her who carried a dead man's promise in an oilskin wrapper against his skin.

"Tui," Bram said quietly. "The fuel line to the generator. Now."

Tui didn't ask. That was the thing about Tui — he never asked, he just *did*, and thirty seconds later the generator died and the deck lights went out and the world went black as the inside of a boot.

In the dark, everything changed. The man holding Pili couldn't see; Bram couldn't either, but Bram knew this deck the way he knew his own hands — every cleat, every coaming, every trip-hazard — and the man didn't. There was a scuffle, a grunt, the ring of the wrench dropping on steel, and then Pili's voice, ragged: "Go! *Go!*"

Bram went. He hit the man low and hard, driving him back against the rail, and felt the knife score a hot line across his forearm before the man lost his grip on both girl and blade. Pili scrambled clear. The man came up swinging the knife and Bram took the stroke on the shotgun barrel, twisted, and put him down with a knee and an elbow and the cold iron certainty of a man who had once been trained by professionals to end fights in under three seconds.

The man lay groaning among the fish boxes. From the water came the sound of the tender retreating at full throttle now, no more pretense, and the two men on the foredeck hauling themselves over the rail after it, leaving their bleeding colleague behind without a backward glance.

Bram stood in the sudden silence, chest heaving, blood running warm down his arm, and listened to the tender fade into the dark.

Then Pili made a small sound, and he turned, and the light from Nell's emergency lantern showed him what the fight had cost.

She was sitting against the engine housing with Tui crouched over her, and her left forearm was laid open from elbow to wrist — not the knife, the wrench, dropped and skidded, its edge catching her as she'd scrambled free. It wasn't deep, but it was long, and it bled the way young blood bleeds, generously and with drama, and her face was white as bone above the dark stain.

"It's fine," she said, before anyone asked. "I'm fine. Did you see me get him? I got him with the—"

"Hush," said Nell, and knelt, and began tearing a shirt into strips with the unhurried competence of a woman who had dressed worse wounds in worse places.

* * *

They stitched Pili up in the wheelhouse under the emergency lantern, Tui holding her hand while Nell sewed, because Pili refused to look at the needle and Tui refused to let go of her. Bram sat on the chart bench with his own arm bound in a pressure dressing, watching the girl's jaw stay clenched against the pain, and felt something in his chest turn over slowly, like ballast shifting in a hold.

They had come aboard his boat. His leaky, debt-ridden, honest little boat. For him. For what he carried. And a sixteen-year-old stowaway had taken a pipe wrench to a hired killer rather than give him up.

"You knew," Nell said.

It wasn't a question. She'd finished with Pili and now she stood over him, arms folded, the lantern throwing her shadow huge across the chart table. Glass from the shot-out window glittered on the floorboards. "You knew they were coming. You've known since Cairns, maybe since Moresby. The way you watch the horizon. The way you sleep in your boots. The way you asked me about radar signatures off the survey ship like a man asking about weather he could already smell."

Bram said nothing.

"And now there's blood on my deck and a hole in my wheelhouse and a man trussed in my fish hold, and you're going to tell me what it's all about, Kavanagh. Right now. Or you can swim ashore at Snapper and take your chances with whoever sent that lot."

"Nell—" Tui started.

"No." She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; she didn't need to. "He'll tell us. Because he owes us that, and because we've earned it, and because Pili bled for whatever's in that packet you keep pressed against your ribs like a second heartbeat. I've watched you do it for weeks. I'm not a fool, diver. I'm just a patient one."

So Bram told them.

He unwrapped the oilskin and laid it on the chart table, and the lantern light fell on Elias Wren's map — the coastwise sketch, the reef markings in faded ink, the coordinates written in a dying man's shaking hand, and beneath them, in letters gone wobbly at the edges: *MERIDIAN STAR — HALLORAN SHIPPING — SEE IT FOUND OR SEE IT DESTROYED.*

He told them about the tavern in Port Moresby. About Wren shot and fading, propped in a corner booth with his life leaking out of him faster than anyone could stop it, pressing the packet into Bram's hands with fingers cold as the deep. About the name he'd gasped — *Halloran* — and the price he'd named: not money, not passage, but a promise. *See it found, or see it destroyed. Swear it, diver, or die knowing you didn't.*

"I swore it," Bram said. "I didn't know what it would cost. I still don't. But I swore it."

"And the Meridian Star?" Nell asked. "What is she?"

"A bullion ship that went down forty years ago with a fortune aboard and a story nobody tells. Halloran Shipping collected the insurance and buried the questions." Bram looked down at the map, at the ink-brown coordinates sitting in the middle of empty blue water. "Wren spent his whole life chasing her. He found her. And somebody killed him for it."

Silence in the wheelhouse. The lantern hissed. Below, faintly, the man in the fish hold moaned against his gag.

Pili spoke first, from her bunk in the corner, arm bandaged thick, face still pale but eyes bright and hard as new coins. "We go there. To the coordinates. We find her first."

"Pili—" Tui began.

"No!" She sat up, wincing, furious. "They came on *our* boat. They cut *me*. They think we're nothing — a leaky trawler and a diver and an old lady and—" she caught herself, glanced at Tui, "—and an engineer. They think we'll run. Well, I say we don't run. I say we get there first."

Nell hadn't moved. She stood looking down at the map for a long moment, and Bram watched her — watched the play of thought across that seamed, unreadable face, the weighing of debts and dangers, the arithmetic of a skipper deciding what her boat was for.

"You could put me ashore," Bram said quietly. "I'd understand. This isn't your fight. It stopped being mine the moment I took Wren's hand, but it was never yours at all."

"No," Nell agreed. "It isn't my fight." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and dark and absolutely certain. "But you're wrong about one thing, Kavanagh. It stopped being only yours the moment those men boarded my boat and put a knife to my crew. My crew." She tapped the chart table once, decisively. "That girl bled for you. Tui killed our lights for you. That makes it ours, whether you like it or not. Nobody hunts my people and walks away clean."

She turned to the chart drawer, pulled out the regional charts, and began sorting through them with quick, sure hands.

"Coordinates," she said, holding out her palm.

Bram read them off the map. Nell marked them, measured, frowned at the distance, measured again.

"Three days," she said. "Four if the weather turns. We'll need fuel, water, and that man in your fish hold gone before we make open water — I won't carry a prisoner into a storm season." She glanced at Bram. "And Kavanagh? When this is done — whatever's down there, whatever Wren wanted found or destroyed — you and I are going to have a longer conversation about what else you're not telling me. I can smell a sealed record on a man the way I smell rain coming. But that's for later."

She bent over the chart, and began to plot the course.

Bram stood in the wheelhouse of the Pelican and felt something he hadn't felt in fifteen years of moving, and burning, and trusting no harbor: the strange, terrifying weight of being *kept*. Not tolerated. Not used. Kept — chosen by a gruff skipper and a silent engineer and a bleeding girl who'd swung a wrench at the dark for him.

Outside, the sea lay black and waiting, and somewhere north of them, in three days' sailing, the Meridian Star sat in her grave with her secrets chained beside her.

And somewhere behind them, Bram knew, the men who'd boarded them were reporting to someone far more dangerous than themselves — someone who would not send four men in a tender next time.

Nell looked up from the chart, read his face, and almost smiled.

"Get some sleep, diver," she said. "Tomorrow we run."
