## 10. Running Before the Monsoon

**Bram**

The sky to the north had gone the color of a bruise by dawn, and Nell stood at the wheel of the *Pelican* squinting at it like a debt collector at a bad ledger.

"That's not weather," she said. "That's a promise."

Bram stood beside her, a mug of Tui's terrible coffee going cold in his hand. Behind them, twenty miles back and closing by degrees, the Halloran gunboat had appeared at first light — a grey wedge on the horizon with no flag showing, which told him everything he needed to know about whose flag it would show if it got close enough.

"How's she handling?" he asked.

"Leaking like a politician and pulling to port like a drunk." Nell spat over the rail. "Tui's patched the worst of it, but we're bailing more than I'd like and I'd like none. You want to tell me again why we're running north into a monsoon instead of south into a friendly port?"

"Because friendly ports have telegraphs, and telegraphs have ears, and every ear between here and Brisbane is paid for by the man in that boat."

Nell grunted. It was her way of agreeing without surrendering anything.

Bram looked aft. Mara Voss stood at the stern rail, hair whipping, watching the gunboat with the flat, assessing stare of a woman reading a document she'd written herself. She'd been exposed for two days now — the investigator, the hunter, the woman with the forged surveyor's papers — and she hadn't once apologized, which Bram found himself respecting more than he wanted to.

She caught him looking. She didn't look away.

He crossed the deck to her, the *Pelican* rolling under his boots. Up close she had salt crusted in her eyebrows and a fading bruise along her jaw from the night Halloran's men had boarded, and she was, he thought with some irritation, still the most composed thing on this boat.

"Your friends are gaining," he said.

"They're not my friends."

"Your employer's, then."

"My former employer's." She turned to face him fully. "I burned my report, Kavanagh. Crane will have my warrant by now, if he doesn't have worse. Whatever I was when I boarded this boat, I'm not that anymore. I'd like to stop being treated like it."

"Would you." He leaned on the rail beside her. "Old habit. The last woman who lied to me on a deck did it with better papers."

"And you've never lied on a deck? Never hidden a chart, a name, a promise?" Her voice was even, but something moved behind her eyes. "I read your service record, Bram. The parts that weren't blacked out. I know what a man who keeps moving looks like, because I've spent three years chasing the paper trail he leaves. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to use me."

"Use you."

"I spent six years at Halloran Marine underwriting hulls and investigating losses. I know how Edmund Halloran thinks, how his operations run, what his captains are ordered to do when they catch a fugitive trawler in open water. You have a leaking boat, a teenage stowaway, an engineer with a bad shoulder, and a monsoon coming. I am, whether you like it or not, the most valuable cargo on this deck."

Bram looked at her for a long moment. The wind pulled at them both. Somewhere below, the bilge pump coughed and resumed.

"Nell!" he called. "Come hear this."

Nell came, wheeling the *Pelican* one-handed, which was a thing she did to remind everyone she could.

Mara laid it out plainly. The gunboat would not fire first — Halloran's operation lived in shadows and could not afford an incident with witnesses. It would run them down, board, and "detain" them as salvage-jumpers on a wreck Halloran held paper claims to, all perfectly legal-looking. Which meant the *Pelican*'s only weapons were weather and paperwork.

"You can't outrun them in this hull," Mara said. "But you don't have to. You have to outrun them *to* something. A shipping lane, a fishing fleet, a patrol — witnesses. Or you run them into weather so bad they have to break off, and you do it on a course they can't predict. I've read Halloran's standing orders. His captains are cowards about their hulls. Insurance premiums. I signed some of them myself." Her mouth twisted. "I can tell you exactly how much weather that boat will tolerate before her master turns away."

Nell and Bram exchanged a look over her head.

"And the leak?" Bram asked.

"I'm not an engineer. But I've written the survey reports on a hundred hull casualties. I know where old trawlers fail, and yours is failing exactly where I'd have predicted. If Tui will have me, I'll bail, I'll hold, I'll hand him tools, and I'll shut up about it."

Nell chewed on this. "You'll bail."

"I'll bail."

"Everyone bails on this boat eventually." Nell thrust out her chin toward the bow. "Forward. There's a bucket with your name on it. If you're still bailing when we clear this weather, maybe you're crew. If not, you were cargo."

Mara took the bucket.

Bram watched her go forward, roll her sleeves, and start hauling seawater like she'd been born to it, and felt something in his chest loosen a degree he hadn't authorized. He'd spent years keeping people at the length of a boat-hook. This one kept stepping inside it, and the strange thing, the dangerous thing, was how little he minded.

* * *

**Mara**

The bucket was heavier than it looked, and the sea it drew from was determined to return.

Mara bailed in the waist of the *Pelican* with spray coming over the rail in cold slaps, and she bailed, and bailed, and thought about how she had once signed off on the survey that declared this class of vessel "fit for coastal service," and how the universe had apparently decided to make her eat the report.

Tui found her an hour in. The engineer was a broad, quiet Samoan with hands like mooring line and a shoulder that hung wrong since the boarding fight. He crouched beside her, watched her technique with the bucket, and said nothing for a long time.

"You held the pump valve wrong yesterday," he said at last.

"I've never touched a pump valve."

"I know." He took the bucket, dipped, poured, a metronome. "You did it wrong like someone who'd read about pump valves. That's how I knew your surveyor papers were forged. Real surveyors get their hands dirty. You had clean nails and a book in your bag about claims litigation." He handed the bucket back. "Your nails are ruined now. Good."

"Is that approval?"

"That's an observation." He looked at her with mild, unreadable eyes. "The skipper's deciding what you are. So's Bram. So am I. Buckets help."

She bailed. The *Pelican* rolled. And somewhere in the rhythm of it — dip, lift, heave, the sea's cold argument against her arms — she understood that this was the first honest work she'd done in six years, and that she'd been more fraudulent with her real papers than she'd ever been with the forged ones.

Bram appeared in the afternoon, when the swell had begun to organize itself into long grey ranks and the wind had found its voice. He took the bucket from her without asking and nodded aft.

"Come look at this."

At the stern, he handed her his binoculars. The gunboat had closed to perhaps fifteen miles — close enough to see her bow wave, white and confident.

"Now watch," Bram said. "She's been holding a straight pursuit course all day. Efficient. Confident." He watched. "There. See her come up two points? She's just gotten new weather. Her master's reading the same barometer Nell is."

"And Halloran's standing orders say?"

"Say nothing about weather. That's the point. But her insurance survey — the one you probably signed a cousin of — restricts her to sea state five. That's a sea state six building out there, and by midnight it'll be a seven with ambitions."

Mara lowered the binoculars. "So we don't run from her. We run *ahead* of the front, on a heading that keeps the worst sea between us and her, and let the weather do the fighting."

Bram looked at her sideways. "You've done this before."

"I've *paid for* this before. There's a difference, but it's not nothing." She paused. "Bram. I need to say a thing and then never say it again. I came aboard to find a scapegoat for Edmund Halloran. I was the weapon. I'm not going to apologize, because apologies are cheap and I'd rather pay in work. But I'm done being his. Whatever's at the end of that chart — I want to see it found, or I want to see it destroyed. Same as you."

He was quiet a long moment, the wind pulling at his collar.

"Wren made me promise that," he said finally. "See it found, or see it destroyed. How do you know those words?"

"Because I've spent three years reading Halloran's losses," she said, "and every one of them reads like a promise somebody made and somebody else paid for. I just didn't know whose promise until I met you."

Something passed between them then that had no paperwork attached — no case file, no cover story, no careful lie. Just two people on a rotten deck, agreeing on the shape of the truth.

"Nell," Bram called. "Come up two points north. Let's put the front between us and that boat."

* * *

**Bram**

They ran the whole night ahead of the monsoon's leading edge, and it was like driving a cart ahead of a landslide.

The *Pelican* groaned and rolled through building seas, green water coming aboard in solid slaps, Tui below decks cursing at the pump in three languages. Bram took the wheel from midnight, Nell beside him with a mug of something medicinal, and Mara — soaked through, hair plastered flat, hauling on the wheel lines when the sea knocked the helm heavy — worked beside him without complaint and without fear, and he found himself watching her hands more than the compass.

"You're staring at the wrong instrument," she said, not looking at him.

"The compass lies in this swell anyway."

"That's not what a compass does."

"That's what it does when you're holding the wheel," Nell said from her stool, and cackled.

Behind them, the gunboat's masthead light had vanished at dusk and not returned. Mara's reading had been right — the grey wedge had turned away at dusk, running for shelter with her premiums intact, and the sea between the two vessels had filled up with weather and emptiness.

Which left only the leak.

It came for them at three in the morning, in the trough between two grey hills of water. Tui's voice came up the companionway, stripped of all its usual calm: "Bram! I need hands! Now!"

Below, the engine room was a chaos of swinging lamps and spray. The patch Tui had set two days ago had let go along its whole length, and seawater came through the seam in a silver rope, arcing into the bilge. Tui had his shoulder against a timber, face grey with pain.

"I can't hold it and set it," he said. "I need a second pair of hands that doesn't shake."

Bram took the timber. "Mara. There's a canvas kit on the shelf — red handles. Bring it."

She brought it. And then she stayed, and Bram, wedged against the straining frame, watched her take instruction — *hold there, press there, don't let go until I say* — and follow it with the exact, unflinching precision of a woman who had decided to be useful and would not be moved from it. When the patch went in and Tui called the hold, her hands were shaking, and she didn't hide it, and she didn't stop.

"Good," Tui said, and it was the longest word of praise he'd issued in Bram's hearing.

Above them, the storm found its full voice. The *Pelican* climbed and fell, climbed and fell, a small stubborn animal on the back of something vast and indifferent. And in the lamplit engine room, three people leaned against a patched seam and breathed.

Mara sat down hard on a crate, salt water dripping from her chin, and started — to Bram's astonishment — to laugh.

"What?" he said.

"Six years of offices," she said. "Six years of memos and ledgers and men in good suits telling me what the truth was. And it turns out the truth is a rope of seawater in an engine room at three in the morning, and you hold it down or you drown." She looked up at him, eyes bright and exhausted. "I think I've been running before this monsoon my whole life, Bram. I just didn't know which direction."

He crouched in front of her. Up close, in the bad light, she looked young and old at once, and he understood that whatever he'd been holding at boat-hook's length was no longer at that length, and had not been for some time.

"Where's it running now?" he asked.

"North," she said. "With you. Apparently."

He took her hand. It was cold and wrinkled from the water and it closed around his like a decision.

Above them, Nell's voice came down the companionway, dry as a chart: "If you two are done bonding, the front's got a clear patch behind it and I'd like us alive to sail through it. And Bram — radar's got a contact, north-northeast, holding station. Been there an hour."

Bram was on his feet. "Halloran's?"

"No," Nell said, and Bram heard it in her voice before she said it, the thing that would turn the weather cold again. "It's grey, it's armed, and it's flying the white ensign. It's the *navy*, Bram. And it's between us and wherever we were going."

Mara rose beside him, and her hand found his in the dark.

"Then we'd better decide," she said quietly, "what story we're sailing in with."

Bram looked up the companionway toward the wheel, toward the grey dawn and the white ensign waiting in it, and felt the old silence order — the one he'd burned his whole life around — tighten like a hand around his throat.

"They already know the story," he said. "They wrote it. The only question is whether they've come to read it to us — or to make sure we never read it at all."
