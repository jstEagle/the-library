## 11. The Lagoon

The reef opened for them like a secret kept too long.

Nell found it the way she found everything — by instinct and a lifetime of reading water — easing the Pelican through a gap in the coral no wider than the trawler's beam, engines at idle, Tui calling depths from the bow with a lead line and a voice gone hoarse from shouting soundings. On either side of us the coral rose in blue-and-bone ramparts, and beyond it the monsoon stacked itself against the sky like a wall being built all day by invisible hands.

Then the gap closed behind us, and there was silence.

A lagoon perhaps half a mile across, ringed by reef, sheltered so completely that the sea inside it lay flat as poured glass. The water went from green to turquoise to a blue so deep it looked lit from below, which of course it was — coral, sand, the white bones of old wrecks catching the sun. A scatter of terns lifted off a sand spit and resettled, unbothered. Somewhere under our keel, I knew, fish were going about their business as if the world above hadn't spent three days trying to kill us.

"Drop anchor," Nell said quietly, as if speaking loudly might break whatever spell we'd sailed into.

The chain rattled out and the hook bit into sand, and the Pelican swung her head to the wind she couldn't feel, and for the first time since Cairns nobody was holding onto anything.

I stood at the rail and let the stillness come into me the way water comes into a dry hold — slowly, then all at once. My hands were shaking. I noticed it the way you notice weather: distantly, without surprise. Three days of running before a storm, three days of pumps and bailers and watching the horizon for a gunboat's superstructure, and now my body wanted its bill paid.

"You look like something the tide rejected," Nell said beside me. She had a mug of coffee in each hand and passed me one without ceremony.

"I feel like it."

"Hull?"

"Tui says four hours to patch if the weather holds. It'll hold." I nodded at the reef wall, standing between us and the black line of the monsoon. "That's the best shipwright in the Pacific out there."

Nell grunted. She drank her coffee and watched the light move on the water, and after a while she said, not looking at me, "You gave up your bunk to Pili two nights running. And you haven't slept since Moresby worth the name."

"I'll sleep."

"You'll sleep when you've decided whether to tell that woman what's eating you," Nell said, and walked away before I could answer, which was her way of ending conversations she'd already won.

* * *

They patched the hull through the morning, and I dove the patch.

It was simple work, honest work — clean the split seam, seat the steel plate, drive the bolts home while Pili worked the surface pump and Mara ran the air hose, keeping the turns even, calling depth and pressure in a voice that grew steadier every hour. The water inside the lagoon was warm and clear enough that I could see her face distorted above me, framed in the bright coin of the surface, hair drifting loose from where she'd tied it back.

There are worse things than looking up at a woman through ten feet of perfect water. There are worse things than having her be the one holding your lifeline.

By noon the plate was seated and the leak was dead, and Tui declared the Pelican seaworthy "for any weather short of the end of the world," which given what was queued up over the reef felt like a fair assessment.

We ate on deck — rice and tinned corned beef and a green papaya Pili had traded off some part of herself I didn't ask about — and afterward the afternoon opened up in front of us like an empty road. Nobody said the word monsoon. Nobody said Halloran. We let the lagoon keep its promise of safety a few hours longer, the way you let a fever patient sleep.

Tui sat aft with a length of rope and a knife, and Mara settled cross-legged beside him with the particular hunger she brought to anything she didn't yet know how to do.

"Show me," she said.

Tui looked at her sidelong. He was a small man, quick and brown, with hands that seemed to operate independently of his attention; he could splice line while telling a story, argue about rugby, and drink his tea, all at once, and never lose a strand. "Splicing? You're an investigator. When will you need to splice?"

"The same day the pump belt goes," Mara said. "Or the towline parts. Or someone throws me a rope and my life depends on knowing what to do with it. I don't like depending on other people's competence, Tui. It's a character flaw."

He grinned, showing teeth gone yellow from tobacco. "All right then. Watch."

He unlayed the strands, three of them fanning out like the petals of some mechanical flower, and began weaving them back into the standing part with movements so economical they looked lazy. "Eye splice. Oldest trick on the water. The rope wants to hold — you're just giving it a shape to remember."

Mara watched, then took her own length and unlayed it, and her first attempt came out a drunken tangle that made Pili laugh from the wheelhouse steps until Nell told her to go find work. Her second attempt was better. Her fifth was nearly right, and Tui made her undo it anyway.

"Nearly right is how people drown," he said, without cruelty. "Again."

And she did it again. I sat against the rail with my coffee and watched her work the strands with her strong fingers, tongue caught between her teeth, and thought about what Nell had said, and looked away.

* * *

Pili found me at dusk.

I'd taken the first watch — habit more than necessity, because no gunboat was coming through that reef wall, but habits are the only cargo I've never lost overboard. The sun was going down behind the western rim of the lagoon, turning the water to hammered copper, and the monsoon beyond the reef had lit itself up with internal lightning, silent from this distance, a private storm in a glass case.

Pili climbed onto the rail beside me, which is a thing fifteen-year-olds can do and forty-year-old divers shouldn't try. She was small and sharp-faced, with her hair cut short with a fishing knife and a way of sitting perfectly still that reminded me of the terns on the sand spit — ready to leave at the first wrong movement.

"You're brooding," she said.

"I'm keeping watch."

"You're brooding *while* keeping watch. It's inefficient. You should pick one."

I almost smiled. "What do you want, Pili?"

She was quiet for a while. Out on the spit, the terns were settling down for the night, a soft gray drift of them. When she spoke again her voice had changed — dropped the performance, dropped the deckhand bravado, become the voice of an actual girl, which I realized I'd heard maybe twice since she'd stowed away in Cairns.

"My father sold me," she said.

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say yet, and she wasn't finished.

"Not like — not money, exactly. Not all at once. He's a copra boat owner, small-time, always in debt to somebody bigger. And there's a family on our island, the Faleolo family, they've got money and no daughters. And my father owed them. So it was arranged. I was fourteen." She picked at the paint on the rail, flaking little curls of it loose. "Nobody asked me. That's the thing everyone gets wrong about it. They think the worst part is the man. The worst part is that nobody asked me. My mother cried and did nothing. My father shook hands. There was a feast. A *feast*, Bram. They killed a pig."

The lightning flickered out past the reef, lighting the clouds from within, and went dark again.

"So I left," she said. "Swam out to a yacht anchored off the point one night and hid in their tender until they towed me to the next island, and then I did it again, and again, working passage, stealing bread, lying about my age. Until I got to Cairns and saw the Pelican and thought — that boat looks like it leaks. Leaky boats need crew who don't ask questions." She glanced at me sideways. "Worked out pretty well."

"Nell would take you back to your island if you asked."

"I know." She said it simply, without fear. "But I'm not asking. I'm telling you why I won't ever ask. There's a difference."

"There is," I agreed.

"And you?" she said, with the directness of the young, which is either cruelty or kindness depending on how it lands. "Why do you keep moving? Nell doesn't know. Tui thinks you're hiding from a wife. I've seen how you sleep — facing the door, boots on. I've seen how you watch the horizon. You're not hiding from a wife."

I looked out at the copper water and the darkening reef and the storm in its glass case, and I felt the truth rise in me like a diver coming up too fast — the navy wreck, the stamped case, the serial numbers, the order to forget, the last crew who stayed and got silenced, Elias Wren bleeding out against a tavern table pressing an oilskin map into my hands. All of it crowding up my throat, wanting out, wanting to be said to someone, anyone, and here was this girl who'd told me her worst thing first, offering me the trade.

"Mara," I started. "When I was—"

And then I stopped. Because Mara came up the companionway at that moment with two mugs of tea, and because the words that almost came out were not mine to give away — they were the navy's secret, and Wren's, and the dead crews', and saying them aloud on an open deck in a lagoon that might not be as hidden as it looked was the kind of mistake that got people silenced. I'd learned that lesson written in other men's blood.

"What were you going to say?" Pili asked.

"That the tea smells burnt," I said.

"It does," Mara said, handing us the mugs. "Tui made it. He claims it's an art form." She leaned on the rail next to me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and the three of us watched the last light die on the water.

After a while Pili yawned hugely, theatrically, and said she had the middle watch, and went below, leaving us alone at the rail with the burnt tea and the silent lightning.

"She told you," Mara said. It wasn't a question. The whole ship knew Pili's story by now except the parts Pili hadn't told anyone; apparently tonight she'd handed me another piece.

"She told me."

"Are you going to tell me yours?"

I drank the tea. It was terrible. "Which one?"

Mara turned and looked at me, and in the dying light her face was all shadow and outline, and her eyes held the last of the sky. "Bram. I've read your service record. Or tried to. It's redacted so heavily it might as well be written in Sanskrit, and then sealed by an authority that doesn't officially exist. I've spent six weeks chasing you across the Coral Sea, and I've stopped pretending it's about the insurance claim, and I think you've stopped pretending you believe that's why I'm here. So." She shrugged, deliberately casual, and missed casual by a mile. "Which one?"

The truth was right there. One sentence. *I dove a wreck the navy ordered forgotten, and I pulled out proof of something that got men killed, and I burned it, and I've been running ever since.* One sentence, and she'd understand everything — the vigilance, the moving, the boots-on sleeping, the whole architecture of a man built to leave harbors before dawn.

One sentence. And if Crane or Halloran or whoever else was listening ever heard it repeated, it would paint a target on this boat that no reef would hide.

"I dove a wreck once," I said, "that I shouldn't have."

Her breath caught. I heard it.

"And?" she said softly.

"And it's not mine to tell. Not yet. Maybe not ever." I set down the mug and turned to face her fully, because she deserved at least that much honesty, the shape of the wall if not the door in it. "Mara — there are things I carry that aren't secrets for my sake. They're secrets because the last people who talked about them are dead. I'm not keeping them from you. I'm keeping them *off* you. There's a difference, and it matters, and I need you to trust me on it a little longer."

She studied me for a long time. The lightning pulsed beyond the reef, once, twice, a slow heartbeat in the clouds.

"You know what the worst part is?" she finally said. "I believe you. Completely. And that frightens me more than the secrets do, because I've spent my whole career believing nothing anybody tells me, and it's kept me safe, and you've ruined it in about five weeks."

"That sounds like a character flaw," I said.

"It is," she said, and laughed, low, and leaned her shoulder into mine, and we stayed like that while the sky went fully dark and the stars came out over the lagoon, reflected so perfectly in the flat water that the Pelican seemed to hang suspended between two heavens.

* * *

I woke before dawn to the sound of Tui swearing at the radio.

Not the ordinary swearing — Tui swore at engines the way other men prayed, constantly and with great feeling. This was different. This was short, clipped, frightened swearing, and it brought me up the ladder with my boots in my hand before I was fully awake.

He was hunched over the set in the wheelhouse, headphones clamped on, and Nell was already there, arms crossed, watching him with a face like carved wood. Mara appeared behind me. None of us spoke.

Tui pulled the phones off. "Weather traffic," he said. "Cairns relay, rebroadcasting a notice." His eyes went around the wheelhouse and settled somewhere near the compass. "Halloran Marine has filed an emergency salvage claim. Vessel *Meridian Star*, position—" he read the coordinates off the telex flimsy in his hand, and my stomach dropped through the hull, through the sand, through the whole floor of the lagoon, "—declared a hazard to navigation. Recovery operation commencing within seventy-two hours. Salvage vessel *Proteus* en route from Thursday Island."

"They found it," Mara whispered.

"No," Nell said. She was looking at the chart table, at the coordinates, at the lagoon's position marked in pencil a bare ninety miles south. "They didn't find it. *Someone told them.*" She straightened up and looked at each of us in turn, and her voice went flat and hard as the reef outside. "Seventy-two hours. Storm breaks tomorrow night. Which means if we mean to reach that wreck before Halloran's divers do — we sail *today*."

Outside, the first light was coming up gray over the reef wall, and beyond it the monsoon waited, patient as a debt.
