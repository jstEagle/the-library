## 12. Crane's Net

The message came through on the Pelican's aging radio at dusk, in a burst of static and clipped code that Tui decoded with a pencil stub and a frown. Mara read the transmission twice before her hands stopped shaking.

It was addressed to *Voss, M.* Not to the marine surveyor she'd been pretending to be for three weeks. Not to the forged papers Tui had quietly clocked back in Cairns. To Mara Voss, Senior Investigator, Meridian Marine Insurance — a name she had buried somewhere south of Cairns along with her career.

*Your position is known. Your warrant is filed. Crane sends his regards.*

She stood in the wheelhouse doorway with the paper in her hand while the sun went down over the reef like something being drowned slowly, and she understood, with the cold clarity of a woman reading her own obituary, exactly what had happened. She had burned her report. She had gone dark, off the grid, running north with the very man she'd been sent to find. And Aldous Crane — patient, meticulous Aldous Crane, who never raised his voice because he never needed to — had answered her betrayal the only way he knew how.

He had opened the net and dropped it over both of them.

* * *

Bram found her there an hour later, sitting on the foredeck with her knees drawn up, watching the phosphorescence wake behind the hull. The Pelican was running dark, no lights, threading the outer reef passages by Nell's memory and Tui's charts, putting distance between themselves and the port where Pili had gone ashore for parts. They wouldn't know until morning whether the girl had made it back aboard or been taken.

"You got a message," Bram said. It wasn't a question. News traveled fast on a small boat.

Mara didn't turn around. "Crane knows where we are. Or close enough." She held up the paper without looking at it. "There's a warrant out now. Not just for you anymore."

"For what?"

"Everything, probably. Obstruction. Theft of company property — my report, my files. They'll dress it up however it needs dressing." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, brittle as dried coral. "I spent eleven years building a reputation for finding people who lie about ships. Now I'm the liar. There's a certain symmetry to it. Crane would appreciate the symmetry."

Bram lowered himself onto the deck beside her, his back against the rail, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him in the cooling air. He didn't say anything for a long time. That was one of the things she had learned about him in the weeks since Cairns — since the night the Halloran strike team boarded them and Pili took a gash across her arm and Bram finally showed Nell the oilskin-wrapped map. Bram Kavanagh did not fill silence with comfort. He let silence do the work.

Finally he said, "Tell me the rest of it."

"There isn't a rest of it."

"Mara." Her name in his mouth, low and even. "You've been circling something since the lagoon. Since before the lagoon. Whatever's in that message, whatever Crane's holding over you — I've watched you carry it for two hundred miles. So tell me the rest of it, and then we'll deal with it the way this crew deals with everything. Together."

She looked at him then. His face was half shadow, half the last grey light off the water, and there was nothing accusatory in it. That was almost worse. She could have handled anger. Anger she knew how to survive.

"My original orders," she said. "From Brisbane. You should hear them from me before you hear them from anyone else."

"I'm listening."

"Crane didn't send me to investigate Halloran. Not really. The fraud pattern — the ships lost where no storm was logged — I found that myself, digging through the ledgers. But when I brought it to Crane, he didn't want the pattern. He wanted a name. One name, specifically." She drew a breath. "He sent me to find a diver named Kavanagh. Quietly. Off the books. I was meant to locate you, establish contact, and build a case file. Salvage fraud, they said. Stolen bullion from the Meridian Star. Enough to put you away for twenty years."

Bram's expression didn't change. "I never touched the Meridian's cargo."

"I know that now. I didn't know it then. All I knew was that a senior investigator doesn't get handed an off-the-books assignment unless someone very powerful wants a very specific result." Her voice steadied as she went, the way it always did when she laid out facts — old habit, the armor of the profession she'd just lost. "I was the scapegoat mechanism, Bram. Crane wasn't hunting you to catch you. He was hunting you to *blame* you. When Halloran's ghost-salvage operation finally surfaced — if it ever surfaced — there would be a ready-made criminal standing in the wreckage. A diver with a sealed navy record and no family and no one to ask questions. You weren't a suspect. You were an *exit strategy*."

The water hissed against the hull. Somewhere aft, Pili's hammock creaked — no. Pili wasn't aboard yet. The hammock creaked empty, and Mara felt the girl's absence like a missing tooth.

"And the report?" Bram asked. "The one you burned."

"I burned it the night I confronted Crane. I told him I knew what Halloran had done — scuttled his own ships, crews aboard, insurance collected on every hull. I told him I'd documented it. He looked at me the way a man looks at a ledger entry that's come in wrong, and he said—" She stopped. Swallowed. "*'Miss Voss, you have confused your function with your judgment.'* As if I were a piece of equipment that had malfunctioned. Then he offered me a transfer and a raise, and when I refused, he thanked me for my service and walked out. By morning, my access codes were dead and my flight home was booked. I ran instead. I chose the Pelican." She turned to face him fully. "I chose you. And I've been waiting since Cairns for you to ask me why an investigator with a spotless record threw it all away for a stranger, because the honest answer is that I don't entirely know. Duty pointed one way. Something else pointed north. I followed the something else, and I've never once regretted it, and I am so tired of pretending that's a simple thing."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. The stars were fully out now, hard and white over the black water, and the Pelican rolled gently through the swells like a tired animal.

"When I was in the navy," he said at last, "they gave us a briefing before every restricted dive. Same speech every time. *What you see down there belongs to the Crown. What you recover belongs to the Crown. What you remember is classified.* We used to joke about it. Say it back and forth in the mess like a catechism." His jaw tightened. "Then a crew I served with stayed too long in one harbor, and started asking questions about a wreck we weren't supposed to name, and within a month two of them were dead and the third had recanted everything in writing and disappeared. I learned my lesson. I kept moving. Twelve years of keeping moving." He looked at her. "You're asking whether I forgive you for following orders. Mara, I spent twelve years not trusting anyone enough to follow orders *with*. You burned your report. You chose. That's more than most people ever do. So yes. I forgive you."

"*Mostly,*" she said. "You said mostly."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face. "Mostly. Because you circled it for two hundred miles instead of telling me in Cairns, and I'd have liked to know then, when it might have mattered differently. And because you're still holding something back — I can see it. There's a shape under the words."

Mara exhaled slowly. He was right, of course. There was always a shape under the words; that was what made Crane dangerous, and what made her good at her job, and what made lying to Bram Kavanagh impossible even when she tried.

"The message," she said. "It wasn't only a warning. There was a second line, coded deeper. Crane's personal cipher — I'd know it anywhere, I used to draft his correspondence. It said: *The girl is in our custody. Her cooperation depends on yours.*"

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the sea seemed to hold its breath.

"Pili," Bram said.

"Pili." Mara's throat was tight. "They took her ashore. Which means they know we'll come for her, which means wherever they're holding her is a trap, and Crane knows I know that, and he's counting on the fact that we're the kind of people who walk into traps for a teenage stowaway." She met his eyes. "Are we?"

Bram was already on his feet, moving toward the wheelhouse, and his answer came back over his shoulder, flat and certain as a depth sounding:

"We're exactly that kind of people. Wake Nell. Plot me a course to Port Douglas — Crane's office has a satellite relay there, and if they're holding her anywhere in Queensland waters, it'll be through that relay. And Mara—" He paused in the doorway, silhouetted against the wheelhouse light, and for a moment he was not a fugitive but something older and harder, a man who had finally stopped running. "Get me everything you remember about how Crane thinks. If we're going to spring his net, we're going to do it from inside it."

Behind them, the monsoon horizon flickered — once, twice — like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

* * *

Nell Okonkwo listened to all of it without interrupting, arms folded, one boot propped on the chart table, her scarred face unreadable as driftwood. When Mara finished, the skipper was silent for the space of ten slow swells.

"So," she said finally. "Insurance woman. Navy diver. And now they've got our girl." She unfolded herself and bent over the chart, her thick finger tracing the coast north from their position. "Port Douglas relay puts them either at the marina compound or the old cannery wharf. Marina's got security cameras and a gate. Cannery's got nothing but rust and rats." Her finger stopped on the cannery. "Which means that's where they'll keep her, because men like Crane always choose the place nobody looks."

"Tui," Bram called forward. "How fast can you make her?"

Tui emerged from the engine space wiping grease from his hands, and something in his face had changed since the news broke — a hardness Mara hadn't seen before, the look of a man recalculating everything. "If you give me four hours and don't ask what I'm doing to the injectors, I'll give you nine knots into a head sea. Maybe ten."

"Four hours," Nell said. "Course laid for the cannery. We go in before first light, when the watch is stupidest." She straightened and fixed Mara with a look that had ended arguments on this boat for years. "One condition, insurance woman. From here on, no more papers, no more covers, no more half of anything. You're crew now. Crew means you bleed where we can see it. Understood?"

Mara understood. She looked around the wheelhouse — at Nell with her chart and her dead-eyed certainty, at Tui already disappearing back into his engine, at Bram watching her from the doorway with that still, measuring gaze — and she felt the last wall inside her come down, quietly, like a bulkhead giving way to water.

"Understood," she said. "Crew."

Outside, the first fat drops of the monsoon began striking the deck, and far to the north, past the rain and the dark, a girl was waiting in a rusted cannery for people who had no reason to come for her except that she was theirs.

Mara went to find a splicing knife and learn the lines. There was work to do, and for the first time in eleven years, every hour of it belonged to her.
