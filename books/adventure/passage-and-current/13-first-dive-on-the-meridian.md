## 13. First Dive on the Meridian

The coordinates put them over open water that looked like every other stretch of the Coral Sea — blue-green, featureless, the kind of water that kept its secrets well. But Wren's map had been drawn by a man who'd spent thirty years reading reefs, and he'd marked it with more than numbers. There was a bearing line to a seam of broken coral on the southern headland of an atoll that didn't appear on the Pelican's charts under any name but "unsurveyed," and when Nell brought the trawler around to that bearing and throttled down, the bottom came up to meet them.

"Twenty-two fathoms," Tui called from the sounder, his voice carrying that particular flatness he got when he was trying not to be excited. "Then eighteen. Then—" The needle jumped and held. "Hard return. Big hard return. That's not reef."

Bram stood at the rail with the oilskin map folded in his jacket, feeling the paper against his chest like a second heartbeat. Below him, somewhere in the green dark, lay the Meridian Star — a Halloran freighter listed lost with all hands in '51, one of eleven ships the company had mourned in its ledgers while quietly collecting the insurance. He'd known that much since Cairns. What he hadn't known, what no ledger would ever say, was whether the ship had died honestly.

He was about to find out.

"Bottom's clean," Nell said, squinting across the water. "No mooring buoys, no salvage markers, no survey stakes. Either nobody's found her, or nobody's supposed to." She looked at Bram. "Which is it?"

"Ask me after," Bram said.

Nell grunted. She'd been different since the night of the boarding — quieter, harder-edged, the way steel gets after tempering. Pili's arm was healing in a sling Tui had rigged from a bicycle inner tube, and she'd taken to sitting watch with a shotgun across her knees like she'd been born to it, which none of them liked and none of them commented on. The monsoon was three days behind them and closing. They had this window or they had nothing.

Mara came up from below deck already dressed for the water: wetsuit half-zipped, knife at her calf, hair tied back wet from a bucket rinse. She'd been diving since she was nineteen, she'd told him once — insurance investigators who worked marine fraud learned early that the truth lived underwater, and you went down and got it yourself or you took somebody's word for a lie. She checked her gauge, checked his, checked the shot line coiled on the deck, and only then looked up at him.

"Nervous?" she asked.

"No."

"Liar."

"Yes," Bram said, and something in her face eased, as if honesty were the thing she'd actually been checking for.

They went down the shot line together into water that turned from green to blue to a darkness the sun couldn't quite reach, and at forty feet the Meridian Star rose out of it like a cathedral.

* * *

She was bigger than Bram had expected — four hundred feet of freighter lying on her port side in the sand, masts still standing, cargo booms swung out at angles like the arms of men caught mid-gesture when the end came. Coral had claimed her in the way coral claims everything, patiently and without malice: soft orange fans growing from the rail, plate coral shingling the boat deck, a forest of black coral trees along the promenade where passengers had once walked. Fish moved through her in schools that parted around the divers and closed again behind them, indifferent as weather.

But the damage — the damage told its own story.

Bram swam aft along the starboard side, following the hull's curve, and Mara fell in beside him because she'd seen it too. There was no grounding scar. No reef strike, no shoal gouge, none of the long ragged wounds a ship leaves on herself when the sea takes her by accident. Instead, low near the turn of the bilge, there were three holes. Clean-edged. Evenly spaced. Each one large enough to drive a truck through, each one blown outward — from inside.

Scuttling charges. Placed by someone who knew exactly where to put them to make a big ship go down fast and straight, in deep water, far from any shipping lane where a passing crew might see her die.

Mara touched his arm and pointed. Beside the largest breach, wedged in the torn plating, a shape hung that didn't belong to the wreck: a steel case the size of a sea chest, bolted to the hull itself with brackets and chain, its surface furred with a decade of growth but its padlock housing still bright beneath the weed. Someone had wanted that case to stay with the ship. Someone had wanted it found only by whoever knew it was there.

Bram pulled his pry bar and worked the hasp while Mara hovered watch, her eyes moving along the dark length of the hull out of habit neither of them discussed anymore. The lock gave with a screech of corroded metal that sounded enormous underwater and wasn't, really. Inside, wrapped in oiled canvas that had done its job perfectly, were documents.

He brought them up slowly, carefully, cradling them like something alive, and they hung in the water between himself and Mara and turned in the current: heavy paper, ship's stationery, a manifest header he could read even through the blur of his mask.

MERIDIAN STAR — CARGO MANIFEST — HALLORAN SHIPPING LINE.

And below the cargo list, in the column marked DECLARED LOSS, figures that made no sense until they made complete sense. Gold bullion, declared. Insured, declared. And in the margin, initialed twice, a handwritten note in a businessman's tidy hand:

*Hull value secondary. Cargo primary. Confirm crew manifest does not exceed minimum safe complement.*

Minimum safe complement. Eleven hands had gone down with the Meridian Star. Bram had read the loss notice once, years ago, in a stack of maritime archives he'd been mining for anything about another ship. *Lost with all hands in heavy weather.* Heavy weather. Three holes blown from the inside, in calm water, on a clear day according to the log pages wrapped in the same canvas — log pages that recorded fair seas right up to the final entry, in a hand that stopped mid-word.

The last entry read: *All secure. Mr. H— sends his reg*

And then nothing.

* * *

They surfaced at the shot line and climbed aboard in silence, and it wasn't until Bram had the documents spread on the chart table under the lamp, salt water beading off them onto Nell's charts, that anyone spoke.

"That's Halloran's hand," Mara said. Her voice was steady in the way he'd learned meant the opposite. "I've spent two years reading his correspondence. The initials, the phrasing — *minimum safe complement* — that's him. That's Edmund Halloran planning, in writing, how few men he needed aboard to sail his own ship into a hole in the ocean."

"He insured the gold," Bram said. "Collected on the loss. And the manifests prove the cargo existed, which means if anyone ever lifted it, they'd have to know it was stolen goods — which means nobody could lift it without exposing him. It wasn't treasure down there. It was a vault. He sank his own evidence and his own crew together, and chained the proof to the hull so it would keep."

Tui had come up from the engine room halfway through and stood wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since before Pili was born. "Eleven men," he said. "You're saying eleven men."

"I'm saying the log stops mid-sentence," Bram said. "I'm saying the last entry says *all secure*. I'm saying whoever wrote it trusted the man who killed him."

Nobody said anything for a while. The Pelican rolled gently on the swell. Down the companionway, faintly, they could hear Pili singing to herself in the galley, some island song with a rhythm like rowing, and the sound of it sat against the words on the table like a hand laid on a wound.

It was Nell who broke it. She'd been standing at the far end of the table, arms crossed, looking at the manifest without touching it, and now she reached out and turned the top page over with one finger, careful as a surgeon.

"There's more," she said. "Look at the stamp."

Bram looked. In the lower corner of each page, in violet ink faded to gray, was a circular stamp he'd catalogued the moment he saw it, because his body had catalogued it before his mind did — the way a man flinches from a stove he touched years ago. A circle. An anchor crossed by a lightning bolt. Around the rim, letters too small to read at a glance but burned into his memory at full size:

NAVAL SURVEY OFFICE — SPECIAL ASSETS DIVISION — CLEARED FOR DISPOSAL.

Cleared for disposal. That was the phrase. That was the phrase stamped on the case he'd brought up from the navy wreck off Thursday Island six years ago, the case with serial numbers he'd burned in a coffee can on the beach at Horn Island while his hands shook and the tide came in. Cleared for disposal. The navy's own tidy language for making things disappear — ships, cargoes, crews, records, men.

"It's the same stamp," Mara said quietly. She was watching him, and he knew she'd seen it — seen the recognition land on him like a blow, seen whatever happened in his face that he couldn't stop happening. "Bram. You've seen this before."

He had promised himself he wouldn't tell her. Not yet, maybe not ever — the story was a door he'd kept locked so long he'd forgotten what was behind it except the weight. But she was standing across the chart table with Halloran's murder written under her fingers, and she had burned her own report and her own career rather than carry Crane's water, and outside the porthole the monsoon was coming for all of them equally.

"Thursday Island," Bram said. "Six years ago. A navy site off Horn — restricted water, no charts, no explanation. They hired contract divers through a broker who never gave names. We were told to recover sealed cases from a wreck at depth and deliver them unopened. Nobody asked what was in them. The men who asked weren't hired back." He paused. "The crew before mine asked. All four of them. Their boat was found burned to the waterline off Boigu, and the finding was accidental fire, and the file was closed in nine days."

"And the cases?"

"One of them cracked on the lift. Manifests. Shipping schedules. Serial numbers matching cargo that had been declared lost — Halloran-flagged losses, going back years. And the stamp." He tapped the violet circle on the page. "That stamp. On navy paper, authorizing disposal of commercial cargo. The navy wasn't investigating Halloran's sinkings. The navy was handling them. Taking the heat off, losing the paperwork, clearing the wrecks of anything inconvenient." He looked down at the manifest, at the mid-sentence log entry, at the initials of a man who had signed away eleven lives with a fountain pen. "Wren didn't just give me a map to gold. He gave me the other half of a story I've been running from for six years. This isn't his crime alone. It's the same operation. It's always been the same operation."

Mara's hand found his across the chart table, over the dead men's paperwork, and held on.

"The promise," she said.

"The promise," Bram agreed. His voice came out rough. "See it found, or see it destroyed. I thought I was promising to find a treasure." He shook his head slowly. "I promised a dying man I'd dig up his grave and tell him what killed him. And now I know. And knowing it doesn't feel like winning anything. It feels like the water rising."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the daylight. Mara photographed every page with the camera she'd carried since Brisbane — the good one, the investigator's camera, the one tool of her old life she'd never managed to leave behind — shooting duplicates of everything, sealing the originals in oiled bags against the damp. Tui ran the generator to charge batteries and said nothing, but he checked the bilge pumps twice as often as usual, and once Bram caught him standing in the engine room doorway looking at the manifest with an expression like a man reading his own death warrant on behalf of strangers.

Pili came up at dusk, sling and all, and read the pages upside down with the fierce total concentration of the young, and then looked up at Bram.

"Mr. H," she said. "That's the man who sent the boat that hurt my arm."

"Yes," Bram said.

"Good," said Pili, and went back down to the galley, and no one laughed, because it wasn't funny, and it also wasn't wrong.

Nell plotted their position on the chart and drew the monsoon's likely track in pencil, a great curving arrow of bad news aimed directly at them. "Two days," she said. "Maybe three. After that we're diving in a washing machine, assuming we're afloat at all." She capped the pencil. "So we do it tomorrow. First light. Both of you down, everyone else topside, engines warm, and if anything comes over that horizon that flies a Halloran flag, we run and we don't look back and neither do you."

"And the bullion?" Tui asked. It was the question under all their questions, the one nobody had said aloud. Four hundred thousand pounds of gold sitting in a hold twenty-two fathoms down, enough money to buy a fleet of Pelicans, a life ashore, a whole other existence.

Bram looked at Mara. She looked back at him, and whatever passed between them passed without words, the way it had begun to lately — two currents meeting, agreeing on a direction.

"We'll decide down there," Bram said.

But he already knew. He'd known since the log entry stopped mid-sentence. Some things you didn't take; some things you witnessed. Elias Wren had traded his dying breath for a witness, and tomorrow Bram intended to keep the bargain — though what form the keeping would take, with the monsoon closing and Halloran's reach longer than any of them guessed, was a question the sea itself would have to answer.

That night Bram stood his watch alone at the bow while the others slept, and the stars wheeled overhead, indifferent and beautiful, and somewhere below his boots the Meridian Star held her dead in the dark, waiting a dozen years for someone to come down and finally, finally say their names out loud.

At 0300, Tui's radio crackled once in the wheelhouse — a single burst of static, a fragment of carrier wave, gone. Tui slept through it.

Bram didn't know that yet. But the sea did, and the sea was keeping count.
