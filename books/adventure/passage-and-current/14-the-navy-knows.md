## 14. The Navy Knows

The grey shape came out of the rain squall at dawn, and Mara knew before the hail crackled over the radio that it was trouble wearing a white ensign.

"Unidentified vessel, this is HMAS *Warramunga*. Heave to and prepare to be boarded."

Nell's hand found the wheel and held it, knuckles pale against the salt-cracked wood. "We're a licensed trawler out of Cairns," she said into the handset, her voice flat as a millpond. "Running from weather. Request permission to continue."

"You'll continue nowhere, *Pelican*. Reduce speed. We are coming alongside."

Mara stood at the rail with the monsoon drizzle beading in her hair and watched the patrol boat grow out of the murk — ninety metres of naval steel cutting a bow wave that would swamp their leaking hull without ceremony. She had spent eleven years reading ships the way other people read faces, and everything about this one read wrong for a routine intercept. It had come too fast, too directly, out of open water where no shipping lane ran. Someone had told it exactly where to look.

Someone ashore. Someone with Crane's voice or Halloran's money behind the telling.

"They're launching a boat," Pili said from the masthead, binoculars pressed to her eyes. "Six men. Rifles."

"Nobody moves," Nell said. "Nobody speaks unless spoken to. Tui—" she caught herself, and something flickered across her face like a shadow under water, "—stay below. Keep the pumps quiet."

Tui went down the companionway without meeting anyone's eyes, and Mara watched him go and said nothing. There would be time enough for that reckoning later, if there was a later.

* * *

They came up the side in boarding gear, six of them in life vests and webbing, and the officer who climbed over the rail last was not dressed like the rest. Older. Crisp despite the spray. He carried no rifle, only a leather folder under his arm, and when he stepped onto the Pelican's fouled deck he did so with the particular care of a man who considered small boats beneath him.

"I'm looking for Bram Kavanagh," he said.

Not *who is your master*. Not *papers and cargo*. Mara felt the words land on the deck between them all like a dropped tool.

"And you'd be?" Nell asked.

"Commander Aldous Reeve. Royal Australian Navy." He opened the folder, unhurried, and Mara saw the flash of a photograph inside — grainy, years old, a younger man with shorter hair and harder eyes standing beside a diving bell. "This man served aboard the tender attached to my command. He was present at an incident I am authorised to discuss with no one. He was given certain instructions at that time." Reeve looked up, and his gaze swept the deck until it found Bram standing by the winch, arms folded, saying nothing. "It appears he has forgotten them."

"He hasn't," Mara said.

Every head turned toward her. She hadn't planned to speak; the words had simply arrived, the way her best ones always did — in the gap between what was safe and what was necessary.

Reeve studied her. "And you are?"

"Mara Voss. Marine insurance investigator, Meridian Assurance Group." The lie came easily now, worn smooth by weeks of use. "I'm conducting a survey of vessels operating in the Coral Sea basin. Mr Kavanagh is contracted to me as a dive consultant."

"A consultant." Reeve smiled without warmth. "How convenient. Mrs Voss—"

"*Ms*."

"—Ms Voss. Your employer has been in correspondence with my office. They express concern about unlicensed salvage activity in these waters." He closed the folder. "I'll take Mr Kavanagh aboard the *Warramunga* for questioning. A formality. He'll be returned within the day."

"The hell he will," Nell said, and moved — one step, shoulders squared, a woman who had spent thirty years hauling men twice her size out of bars and storms putting herself bodily between the navy and her diver.

Bram put a hand on her arm. "It's all right, Nell."

"It is not all right."

"It's all right," he said again, quieter, and Mara heard what lived underneath the words: *I've done this before. I know how it ends.* He looked at Reeve. "I'll come aboard. Nobody else needs to."

"Bram." Mara stepped forward. "Don't."

He met her eyes, and for a moment — there in the rain, with the navy boat rising and falling against the trawler's flank and Pili watching wide-eyed from the rail — something passed between them that had nothing to do with maps or gold or the law. *Trust me*, his face said. Or perhaps: *Forgive me if I can't.*

Then he swung his leg over the rail and went down the pilot ladder, and the navy took him away.

* * *

The interrogation room aboard the *Warramunga* was a converted storeroom with a bolted table and a bulkhead light that buzzed like a trapped insect. Mara sat on one side of it because she had insisted, loudly and with forged credentials, on being present as the diver's contracting officer — and because Reeve, to her surprise, had allowed it. That allowance itself was information. Men confident of their position permitted witnesses.

Bram sat opposite Reeve, hands flat on the table, and said very little.

"Fourteen April, nine years ago," Reeve began. "The tender *Kestrel* was tasked to recover material from a wreck in restricted waters. You were one of four divers on rotation. On your final descent you recovered items not listed on the manifest. You were ordered to surrender them. You did not."

"I surrendered them," Bram said.

"You surrendered some of them."

The light buzzed. Rain drummed on the deck above them, and somewhere aft a petty officer's voice called depths and bearings, the ship going about its lawful business around them.

"I burned the rest," Bram said. "You know I did. You signed the order telling me to forget the whole thing. Why are we pretending otherwise?"

Reeve laid a single sheet of paper on the table, turned it so Bram could read it. Mara, angling her head, saw a typed directive, a date nine years old, and two signatures at the bottom. One she didn't recognise. The other was Reeve's own, still sharp after all this time.

"You were instructed to forget," Reeve said, "because forgetting was the condition of your continued breathing. Do you imagine that order protected the Navy? It protected *you*. Four divers made that recovery. Three of them talked. You may confirm for yourself how long they remained talkative." He leaned back. "So understand me, Kavanagh. When I say forget, I am not threatening you. I am performing the last courtesy anyone in uniform will ever extend to you. There is a wreck at those coordinates you've been steaming toward for three days. There is bullion in it. And there are documents in that hold that certain parties have spent nine years and a great deal of money ensuring stay fathoms down. If you bring those documents up — if you so much as *photograph* them — the people I answer to will not send a commander with a folder. They will send something else."

"Who do you answer to?" Mara asked.

Reeve turned to her with the patience of a man addressing a child who has wandered onto a firing range. "Ms Voss. Your employer, Meridian Assurance Group, filed a query regarding the loss of the vessel *Meridian Star* eleven years ago. The claim was settled. The file was closed. Yet here you are, eleven hundred miles from your jurisdiction, aboard a trawler with a falsified survey licence, anchored over the exact coordinates of that closed file." He let that sit. "Does your employer know where you are? Or shall we ask which employer?"

The buzzing light filled the silence. Mara kept her face still, but inside her chest something cold turned over. He knew about the forged papers. Which meant Crane had told him — or Halloran had — which meant her cover wasn't just blown to the navy. It had been *sold*.

"You buried the Meridian," she said quietly.

"I recovered a wreck in accordance with maritime law."

"You buried it with its crew's manifests chained in the hold. Manifests proving the scuttling orders came from her own owners." She leaned forward. "Eleven years ago Edmund Halloran sank his own ship with forty-three souls aboard and collected the insurance, and the Navy helped him keep it quiet, and you—" she pointed at the signed order, at his name, "—you were the pen they used."

For the first time, Commander Reeve looked tired.

"You think I don't know what's in that hold?" he said. His voice dropped, and the buzzing light seemed suddenly loud. "I was a lieutenant then. I did what I was told, and the men who gave the orders are admirals now, and the man who paid them owns half the shipping in the Pacific, and every year I sign another silence order and every year I tell myself it keeps someone alive." He straightened his cuffs. "Perhaps it does. Perhaps it's kept *him* alive." He nodded at Bram. "Consider that, diver. Consider whether your promise to a dead prospector is worth more than the lives your curiosity will spend. You have until we make port. Then I hand you to civilian authorities, and after that, nothing I say matters."

He gathered his folder and left. The bolt on the door did not slide home — Mara noted that, filed it away — but a shadow crossed the porthole glass: a sentry, posted outside.

* * *

They had twenty minutes alone, maybe less, and Mara spent none of them on comfort.

"He told us everything," she said, keeping her voice below the hum of the light. "That's what you understand, isn't it? He didn't come to threaten you. He came to warn you off because warning you off is the last legal thing left to him."

Bram rubbed a hand over his jaw. "Or it's a trap dressed as a conscience."

"Both. Both things at once — that's how men like him survive." She pulled her chair around to his side of the table, close enough that their knees nearly touched. "Listen to me. He said 'until we make port.' The *Warramunga* isn't making port today — I heard the bridge traffic, we're riding out the squall line. That gives us hours. And he didn't lock the door, because officially this is a voluntary interview, and if we're gone when he checks, he can log it as released pending inquiry instead of escaped detainees. He's leaving us a door because part of him wants us through it."

"And the part that doesn't?"

"That part is why we go now." She reached into her jacket and set the copied manifest pages on the table — the photographs she'd taken in the Meridian's hold, sealed in plastic, the serial stamps sharp and legible. "These are already off this ship. Pili has them, sewn into her jacket, and she's been sitting on the foredeck in plain sight since before you boarded, because nobody searches a teenager twice. Whatever happens next, the evidence doesn't live or die with us."

Bram looked at the pages, then at her, and something in his face eased — not softening exactly, but the settling of a weight he'd carried alone for nine years. "When did you become the dangerous one?"

"About the time I started reading Halloran's ledgers." She stood, pressed her ear to the door, heard the sentry's boots shift aft — turning, checking the weather cloths, a rhythm of perhaps ninety seconds. "On his return. Ready?"

"No," Bram said honestly. "But I've stopped needing to be."

* * *

They went over the side on the sheltered lee of the patrol boat while the rain thickened to a curtain, dropping the last four feet into the grey chop where the Pelican's dinghy waited with Tui at the oars, face greyer than the water. No one spoke until the Warramunga's lights had dissolved into the squall astern and Nell had them running north-northwest, engines pushed past prudent, the leak gaining on the pumps by inches.

In the wheelhouse, Mara spread the chart and weighted its corners with coffee mugs.

"Reeve said 'the people I answer to,'" she said. "Past tense, plural, institutional. But the money — the money runs one direction, and it runs to Halloran. The Navy didn't bury the Meridian for honour. It buried her for a share." She tapped the coordinates. "Which means there is no authority left to appeal to. No court that isn't paid for, no office that isn't warned. If those manifests see daylight, it's because we put them there ourselves — every port authority, every newsroom, all at once, so fast and so wide that no one can buy it back."

"That's not justice," Nell said, steering. "That's a bomb."

"Yes," Mara said. "It is."

Bram stood at the chart table, and she watched him look down at the coordinates — at the grave where a promise and a horror lay tangled together on the seabed — and she saw the last of the drifting man go out of his posture, replaced by something with a heading.

Behind them, unnoticed at first, Pili had appeared in the doorway, soaked and silent, one hand pressed flat over the pocket where the copies rode against her ribs. When she spoke, her voice was steady in a way that made Mara's throat tighten.

"There's a boat following us," Pili said. "Been holding station on our wake since the squall. Not navy. No lights." She swallowed. "And there's smoke on the horizon ahead. Big smoke. Like a working barge."

Mara met Bram's eyes across the chart.

Halloran wasn't waiting for them to find the wreck.

He was already lifting it.
