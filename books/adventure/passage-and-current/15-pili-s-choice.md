## 15. Pili's Choice

The dinghy bumped against the fuel dock at Port Moresby's outer wharf like a dog nosing at a door it wasn't sure was still home.

Pili tied off the painter with a bowline Tui had taught her — twice around, under, through, *again*, because once was for people who trusted docks — and stood up in the rocking boat to get her bearings. The Pelican lay at anchor two hundred meters out in the roadstead, gray-green water slapping her rusted flanks, looking exactly like what she was: a trawler too old to be worth stealing and too stubborn to sink. Nell had refused the marina berth. Marinas had cameras, and cameras had memories.

"Parts list," Nell had said that morning, pressing the folded paper into her hand along with a roll of kina notes soft as cloth from handling. "Impeller for the raw-water pump, gaskets, and whatever Tui wrote on the back. You haggle. Don't let them see you're young. And Pili—" she'd caught her chin, calloused thumb rough under her jaw, "—you come straight back. No wandering."

"I'm fifteen, not stupid," Pili had said, and Nell had said, "Fifteen is exactly when stupid gets clever," and they'd both pretended that settled it.

Now, standing on the dock with salt drying white on her forearms, Pili counted the town. Fuel depot shuttered for lunch. Chandlery open, its owner asleep in a chair behind the counter with a newspaper over his face. A police launch at the far pier, two officers fishing off its stern, not even pretending otherwise. And a white four-wheel drive parked where no tourist would park, engine ticking as it cooled, tinted windows holding the harbor in their black glass like something swallowed.

She made herself walk past it slowly. Made herself look bored. She'd gotten good at bored. It was the first thing the sea taught you — how to look like nothing was happening when everything was.

* * *

The chandlery had the impeller. It didn't have the gaskets, but the sleeping man, woken by a coin flicked onto his counter, jerked his chin toward a yard two streets back where a Tongan woman named Sefina sold salvage out of three shipping containers. Pili haggled the way Nell had shown her: pick up the part, look disgusted, put it down, start to leave. Sefina laughed and knocked a third off the price and threw in a bag of split cotter pins "for the pretty face," which Pili decided not to take personally.

It was coming back out of the container yard, canvas parts-bag over her shoulder, that the white ute pulled alongside her.

The window came down. The man behind it was islander, big through the shoulders, with the kind of neatness that didn't belong to fishermen — pressed shirt, hair cut sharp, hands clean. He smiled like he'd practiced it in a mirror.

"You're off the trawler," he said. Not a question. "Pelican. Out of Thursday Island originally, though she's changed hands so many times the registry can't decide what she is."

Pili kept walking. The ute crawled beside her at her pace, tire sidewalls whispering on hot asphalt.

"Nothing to be scared of," the man said. "Name's Roka. I work for Halloran Shipping. We've got an interest in finding a vessel matching your description. That's all this is."

"Then find her," Pili said. "She's at anchor. Everyone can see her."

"At anchor now." Roka's smile widened by one tooth. "Where she goes next is the interesting question. Where she goes next is worth money to somebody who has a lot of it."

She stopped then. Not because she meant to — because her legs decided for her, some animal arithmetic running underneath her thinking: keep walking and he keeps talking, and every meter of this street has corners, and corners are where men like this stop being polite.

"How much?" she said.

Roka laughed, delighted, and reached across to push the passenger door open. "Get in. Let's talk like professionals."

* * *

She got in. Later, lashed to the wheel of a dying ship in the tail of a monsoon, she would try to remember whether there had been a moment — a breath, a heartbeat — where she could have turned and run instead, and whether choosing not to was bravery or just curiosity wearing bravery's clothes. In the moment itself there was no such clarity. There was only the cold leather seat, the smell of air conditioning, and the flat brown envelope Roka laid on his knee like a card player showing his hand.

Inside were notes. Kina, Australian dollars, US hundreds. Enough to buy the Pelican outright. Enough to buy a life ashore, a real one, with walls and a door that locked and a bed that wasn't a bunk in a compartment that smelled of diesel and other people's dreams.

"Here's the situation," Roka said, driving nowhere in particular, looping the waterfront. "Your skipper is sitting on top of coordinates she has no legal right to. The man who gave them to your diver was a thief and a murderer, and the property he stole belongs to our principal. All we want is the location. Nobody gets hurt. Your boat sails away rich, you sail away richer, and everyone goes home." He glanced at her. "You're the youngest aboard. They'll never suspect you. That's not an insult, it's tradecraft. I was the youngest once too."

"My family's dead," Pili said. "I don't have a home."

"Even better," Roka said, without a flicker. "No one to spend it on but yourself."

She looked out the window at the harbor. The Pelican sat at her anchor, small and crooked and leaking, and Pili thought about the first night aboard, when Nell had found her curled in the fish hold among the ice packs and hadn't called the police, hadn't put her ashore, had just said, *Can you cook? Because Tui burns water,* and that had been the whole interview. She thought about Bram teaching her knots on the aft deck while Mara watched from the rail with an expression she was only now learning to read. She thought about Pili's own name being shouted across a deck — *Pili, watch your line! Pili, mind the boom!* — a name being used, which was a different thing than a name being called.

"Ten thousand now," Roka said. "Twenty when we have the position confirmed."

He counted ten thousand into her hands. She felt the weight of it, crisp and real, and understood with terrible adult clarity that this was the moment the rest of her life would be measured against. Every story she'd ever been told about herself would bend around this minute in this car.

She took the money. She saw him register it, saw the satisfaction settle into his face like silt in still water.

"The coordinates," Roka said. "You've seen the chart. Your diver doesn't hide things as well as he thinks — not from someone who sleeps in the passageway outside the chart room."

That landed harder than he knew. Because it was true. Pili slept in the passageway. Pili heard everything. Three nights ago she'd heard Bram and Mara arguing in whispers over the table, heard the paper rustle, heard Mara say a latitude and Bram say it back wrong on purpose and Mara correct him, and Pili had lain in her bunk with her eyes open doing the math of what knowing was worth.

She recited the numbers. Roka wrote them down, read them back, and she nodded confirmation, and the lie went out of her mouth so smoothly it frightened her — because it *was* a lie, she'd flipped two digits and shifted the minutes, moving the wreck twelve miles northeast into open water near a seamount marked on every chart as *unsurveyed, foul ground*. If Halloran's divers went down there they'd find rock and current and maybe their own deaths, and no evidence of anything.

Roka tucked the notebook away and shook her hand, his grip dry and businesslike. "Smart girl. You did the right thing. When this is done, look us up. People who help Halloran Shipping don't regret it."

People who help Halloran Shipping, Pili thought, watching the ute pull away. Like Elias Wren. Shot twice in a tavern in Port Moresby, dying on a floor sticky with beer, trading a map for a promise because there was nobody else left to give it to.

She walked back toward the dock at a normal speed. She did not run until she rounded the fish market and was hidden behind the stalls, and then she ran like the devil himself had her name.

* * *

The dinghy felt slow as syrup. She rowed standing up, full strokes, the canvas bag of parts banging her shins with every pull, and the whole time her eyes went between the Pelican and the water around her — the empty channel, the treeline, the white ute nowhere in sight but *somewhere*, always somewhere now.

Bram met her at the rail, hauling the bag up before she could pass it, and something in her face must have shown, because his eyes narrowed the way they did when the barometer dropped.

"What happened?"

"Halloran knows we're here," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "A man named Roka stopped me. He gave me money for the coordinates." She dug the envelope out of her shirt and held it up, and saw Nell appear in the companionway behind Bram, and Mara behind Nell, and Tui's head poking up from the engine hatch like a periscope.

"You took it," Nell said. Flat. Not a question either.

"I took it," Pili said. Her heart was going hard enough that she could hear it in her ears, but she made herself stand still under Nell's stare, because running from this — running from *them* — was the one direction she refused to go. "And I gave him numbers. Wrong ones. Twelve miles northeast, foul ground off the seamount. He'll have divers on bare rock by tomorrow morning if he believes me." She swallowed. "I'm sorry I didn't come tell you first. There wasn't time to do both."

Nobody spoke. The harbor slapped against the hull. Somewhere ashore a dog was losing an argument with a scooter.

Then Nell crossed the deck, took the envelope out of her hand, weighed it the same way Roka had, and put it in Pili's pocket instead.

"Keep it," Nell said. "Blood money spends the same as wages, and you just earned both. Tui — get the pump going. We weigh anchor inside the hour." She turned for the wheelhouse, then stopped, and looked back at Pili, and for half a second the gruffness fell off her face like a mask slipping, and what was underneath was fierce and bright and terrible. "You lied to a killer's face with a straight eye and came back anyway. Fifteen years old." She shook her head. "God help the man who tries to buy you twice."

Tui whooped from the hatch. Bram squeezed her shoulder once, hard, saying nothing, which from Bram was a speech. Mara just looked at her for a long moment and then nodded, investigator to — whatever Pili was now.

They raised the anchor with the old manual winch because the windlass had died a month ago, and the Pelican came about and stood out of the roadstead with her decks awash and her heart beating in the engine room, heading north into deepening weather.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the squall line had found them, rain coming down in gray ropes, visibility down to a cable's length. Pili took the wheel for the middle watch, which Nell only allowed in weather bad enough that nobody else wanted it, and steered by compass and feel, the way Tui had drilled into her: *the sea will tell you what the boat's doing before the instruments do, little sister, listen with your hands.*

It was during that watch, hunched and streaming, that she saw it.

First the wake — a smudge of disturbed water holding station far astern, no navigation lights, no shape, just a presence the way a shark is a presence. Then, as the squall tore a hole in the horizon ahead, the smoke: a great dirty column rising off the sea to the north, black at the base, flattening into cloud, the unmistakable signature of heavy machinery working — generators, cranes, compressors burning diesel around the clock.

A barge. A working barge, exactly where the false coordinates she'd fed Roka said *nothing* should be — which meant the true coordinates, which meant Halloran hadn't waited for her betrayal at all. He'd known where the Meridian Star lay all along. Everything on that dock, the envelope, the whole theater of it, had been about one thing only: confirming where *they* were.

Her hands went cold on the wheel spokes. She thought about shouting for Bram, and didn't; she thought about what her grandfather used to say, hauling nets off Madang — *when you find the snake in the canoe, first you paddle, then you scream.*

Behind her, unnoticed at first, the wheelhouse door opened, and Mara came in dripping, chart case under her arm, and stopped when she saw Pili's face.

"There's a boat following us," Pili said. "Been holding station on our wake since the squall. Not navy. No lights." She swallowed, and pointed north through the wet glass at the smoking horizon. "And there's smoke ahead. Big smoke. Like a working barge."

Mara unrolled the chart across the wheelhouse table with hands that weren't quite steady, and Pili watched her fix the barge's bearing against the penciled cross that marked the Meridian Star, and watched her go pale in the green instrument light.

Halloran wasn't waiting for them to find the wreck.

He was already lifting it.
