## 16. Betrayal at Anchor

The lagoon had been kind to us for six days, and I should have known better than to trust it.

Kind water is a liar's water. It lies flat and green-gold in the morning light, so clear you can count the spines of the coral forty feet down, so still the Pelican's reflection sits beneath her hull like a second ship sailing a drowned world. We'd patched the worst of the leak — Tui driving copper nails with a patience he showed nowhere else, Pili bailing and singing, Nell supervising from a deck chair with her ribs strapped and her opinions freely given. Mara had learned to splice. The monsoon was still two days south by Tui's barometer. For six days we were almost happy, and happiness at anchor is a debt that always comes due.

I felt it first as a wrongness in the fish.

The reef lagoon held a population of sergeant-majors and parrotfish that had grown bold around us, rising to scraps off the galley scraps bucket like gulls with better manners. On the sixth morning they were gone. Not hiding — gone, every one of them, out through the western passage in a silver stream I watched from the rail while my coffee went cold in my hand.

Fish don't evacuate a lagoon for weather. Weather comes slow to them, pressure changes they ride out in the coral. Fish leave when something big moves through the deep water outside. Something with engines.

"Tui," I said. He was below, elbow-deep in the bilge pump. "How far does your radio reach?"

He looked up at me from the engine pit, grease to his elbows, and something crossed his face too fast to name — a flicker, a shutter drawn and reopened. "Depends on the set and the sky," he said. "Why?"

"Because I want to know who else might be listening."

* * *

We ran the drills anyway. Nell thought I was being paranoid until I told her about the fish, and then she wasn't laughing either; she'd spent thirty years reading water the way other people read newspapers. We shifted the Pelican deeper into the lagoon's eastern pocket, behind a coral head that would break radar clutter, rigged the awning down, moved the fuel drums to trim her low. Mara sat on the cabin roof with binoculars she'd bought in Cairns with money she claimed came from survey work, watching the western passage where the reef opened to the sea like a door left ajar.

It was near dusk when Pili, up in the rigging because she went up there the way other kids climbed trees, called down: "Smoke! West-nor'west, past the reef edge!"

Not smoke. Haze — the heat-shimmer of engines working hard, hanging over the horizon like breath on glass. Through the glasses I found it: a gray shape, low and fast, running parallel to the reef line. Not a patrol boat. Patrol boats carry their guns in plain sight. This one carried its teeth behind false deckhouses, the way Halloran's people did everything — honest shapes over dishonest cargo.

The *Corvus*. I'd seen her silhouette once before, standing in the doorway of the tavern in Port Moresby while Elias Wren bled out under my hands. I'd hoped never to see it again.

"Nell," I said. "Night run. Out the east passage, north along the deep water. We can be a hundred miles away by dawn."

She looked at the sky, at the last light going amber behind the storm line to the south, and I watched her do the arithmetic every skipper does — sea state against hull condition, fuel against distance, the known devil against the unknown one. "East passage hasn't been sounded since the war," she said. "If we hit coral in the dark—"

"If we stay, we don't need charts. We need a priest."

She held my eye a moment longer, then nodded once. "Tui. Light the boiler. Pili, get the anchor in. Mara—" She stopped, because Mara was already moving, coiling lines, and because Nell Okonkwo did not give orders to people who were already doing the right thing.

Tui didn't move.

He stood at the engine room hatch with his hand on the frame, and in the failing light I saw his face properly for the first time all day, and what I saw there made my stomach drop through the deck plates. Not fear. Shame. Shame has a look all its own; I'd seen it on men about to confess and men about to run, and Tui wore both at once.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Nell. Bram. I'm sorry."

Nobody spoke. The wind moved the awning canvas overhead with a sound like a held breath.

"They have my brother." His voice came out flat, rehearsed, a speech he'd been carrying too long. "Suva. He owes money to people who broke his hand the last time he couldn't pay, and they came to me in Cairns — before Cairns, at Thursday Island, they found me at Thursday Island — and they said all they wanted was a position report now and then. Just where the Pelican was. Nothing else. Nobody had to get hurt." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "They said nobody had to get hurt."

"How long?" Nell asked. Her voice was very quiet, which anyone who knew her understood was worse than shouting.

"Since you hired me on at Thursday Island." He couldn't look at her. "Every port. Every anchorage. I sent it from the radio shack when you were asleep."

Pili made a small sound and Mara put a hand on her shoulder without looking away from Tui.

"The *Corvus*," I said. "How far out?"

"Six hours when I reported this morning. Maybe less now. They told me — they said if I warned you, if I ran, they'd go back to Suva." His jaw worked. "Bram, they said they only wanted the map. They said give them the map and let them take it and the rest of you could sail on. That's true, isn't it? That could be true?"

"It could be true," I said, "the way a hook could be bait. Tui, they shot Elias Wren for less than what's in my chart locker. They sank ships with their own crews aboard — I've seen the manifests. There is no version of this where they take the map and wave goodbye."

He closed his eyes. And then, God help him, he moved — not toward the radio, not toward the boat, but toward the engine room, and threw the fuel valve open, and lit the burner, and began doing the work of getting us underway as fast as his hands knew how. Whatever else he was, whatever he'd sold, he was still trying, in the last hour he had, to buy some of it back.

Nell watched him for a long moment. "After," she said. "We'll settle what you are after. Right now you're the only man who can keep this old girl's heart beating, so beat it."

* * *

We nearly made it.

The east passage was exactly as bad as Nell feared — a winding lane between coral walls that showed green and white in the last light, water going from thirty feet to eight in the space of a boat-length. Nell conned her from the bow, Pili at the wheel relaying depths, and I stood ready with the lead line feeling like a man walking a tightrope in the dark. Twice the keel kissed coral with a shudder I felt in my teeth. Twice we scraped free. Behind us the lagoon emptied astern, and ahead the passage bent north toward open water, and for perhaps four minutes I believed.

Then the searchlight came on.

It swept the passage mouth from seaward, a white blade that turned night into noon and turned our little ship into something pinned to a board. Beyond it, filling the gap between reef and horizon, lay the *Corvus*, come around the northern end of the reef while we threaded the southern passage — because Tui hadn't just given them our position, he'd given them our heading, our intentions, everything but our souls, and maybe those too if they'd asked.

"Hard aport!" Nell roared. "Back her down!"

Too late. The gunboat's launch was already inside the passage, a black shape riding the edge of the light, six men aboard with weapons that caught the glare. A voice came across the water through a loudhailer, pleasant as a shipping forecast:

"*Pelican*, stand fast. Stand fast and no one is harmed. Mr. Kavanagh — we only want a conversation."

"A conversation," Nell muttered. "With machine guns."

"We could run for it," Pili said, knuckles white on the wheel. "Turn south, back into the lagoon—"

"And spend the monsoon in a fishbowl waiting for them to get bored or hungry. No." Nell looked at me, and I looked at her, and we had the same conversation without words, the way you do with someone you've shared a deck with long enough. Fight and die here, or talk and maybe live. Neither good. One survivable.

"Kill the engine," I said. "Tui — kill it."

He killed it. The silence after was enormous, broken only by water slapping the hull and the idling growl of the launch coming alongside. Grapnels bit our rail. Boots hit our deck.

There were six of them, and they were professionals — spread out, weapons low but present, faces blank in the way of men paid not to have any. Their leader was a big islander with a scarred jaw and a Halloran Shipping cap worn like a joke he was in on. He looked at each of us in turn, cataloguing, and stopped on me.

"Bram Kavanagh."

"That's me. You have the advantage."

"Mr. Sefo." He smiled without warmth. "Mr. Halloran sends his regards. He understands you've been through an ordeal — the storm, the damage, the company of strangers. He wants you to know none of it was personal. There's an item in your possession that belongs to the estate of a deceased prospector, and the estate has outstanding obligations to certain parties. Hand it over, and Mr. Halloran will see you compensated. Fuel. Repairs. Passage anywhere in the Pacific. Cash, even, if you prefer cash."

"And if we don't?"

Sefo sighed, genuinely regretful, which was somehow worse than menace. "Then I have orders I'd rather not follow, and you have a boat full of people I'd rather not hurt. The young lady especially." His eyes touched Pili and moved on. "The map, Mr. Kavanagh."

Behind me I heard Mara shift her weight — she'd been edging, all this time, one careful inch at a time, toward the chart locker where the oilskin roll lay wrapped in a spare shirt. I'd rewrapped it there myself that morning. Sefo's men would find it in ninety seconds whether I handed it over or not, and the difference between handing it over and having it taken was the difference between a story we might survive and a fight we wouldn't.

So I did the hardest thing I've done in a life that hasn't lacked for hard things. I walked to the chart locker, took out the oilskin roll, and held it out.

"One condition," I said. "You take it and go. Now. Tonight. Nobody follows us, nobody touches these people again. Wren's debt dies with the map."

"Generous of you to offer terms," Sefo said, taking it. He weighed the roll in his hand, and something in his face made me look closer — a flicker of doubt, a professional's instinct that a gift comes too easy. He turned it over. Felt the weight.

And that was when Tui moved.

I'll never know everything that went through his head in that moment — the debts, the brother, the six months of small betrayals stacking up into one large one. But I know what I saw. I saw Sefo's doubt, and I saw Tui read it the same instant I did, and I saw Tui understand that the map was wrong — that whatever was in that oilskin, it wasn't heavy enough, wasn't stiff enough, wasn't the thing they'd been promised, and that when Sefo discovered the substitution the price would be paid in blood, and that the blood would start with Nell, because Sefo's eyes kept coming back to her, to the woman whose trust he'd sold.

"Run!" Tui shouted, and threw himself at Sefo.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way violence always does — too fast for sense, each fragment burned separately into memory. Tui's shoulder hitting Sefo's chest. The oilskin flying free, splitting as it landed, a rolled chart — nothing but a tide table from Suva — unrolling across the deck like a confession. Sefo going down under Tui with a roar. A rifleman swinging his weapon up. Nell stepping forward, arms out, putting herself between the muzzle and Pili — Nell, who never once in all the time I knew her stepped back from anything — and the shot.

The sound was enormous in the night. Nell folded, not falling but folding, a hand pressed to her side, and Pili screamed a word I won't repeat, and Mara came off the cabin roof onto the nearest gunman like a hawk on a rabbit, and I went for Sefo.

But Tui got there first, in the way that mattered. He'd wrapped both arms around Sefo's weapon arm, dragging him down, pinning him, holding him — and the second shot came from the rifleman wild, swinging wide as Mara took his legs, and the round took Tui high in the back, between the shoulders, as he lay across the man he'd betrayed us for.

The fight went out of it then, strangely, all at once. Sefo shoved Tui's body off — gently, I remember that, gently, as if respect had arrived too late to matter — and got to his feet, and looked at the bleeding skipper, the dead engineer, the map that wasn't a map, and made the calculation men like him are always making.

"Mr. Halloran will find you," he said. "The coordinates are in your heads now, and heads can be opened." He backed toward the rail, his men covering, and dropped into the launch, and the searchlight swung away, and the *Corvus*'s engines built to a growl beyond the reef, and then there was only the dark, and the water sounds, and Pili crying, and Nell swearing at everyone in three languages while she pressed her shirt against her ribs — a flesh wound, a miracle, a groove torn along her side that would scar like a signature.

And Tui lying on his own deck, looking at the stars, still breathing.

Mara reached him first. She was the steadiest of us in that moment — later I'd learn why, later she'd tell me about the accident reports she'd read, the bodies pulled from water, the years of learning to be calm beside the worst thing in the room. She knelt, and took his hand, and he turned his head with terrible effort and found Nell with his eyes.

"Nell," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Tell my brother—" He coughed, and there was blood in it. "Tell him the debt's paid. Tell him I paid it."

"You tell him yourself," Nell said, kneeling on her wounded side, ignoring it entirely, taking his face in both hands the way you'd hold something breakable. "You hear me, Tui? You're not done. You don't get to buy your way out with one good act and leave me short-handed. You're going to live, and you're going to be sorry for a very long time, and that's an order."

His mouth twitched. Almost a smile. "Aye, Skipper," he whispered.

He lived. Barely — the bullet had missed his spine by the width of a prayer and lodged against a rib, and Mara and I cut it out of him by lamplight in the galley with Pili holding the lamp and her whole body shaking, and Nell directing the surgery from the bench because she'd stitched worse on fishing crews in the Bering Sea and wasn't shy about saying so. By dawn Tui was sleeping, feverish but breathing, and the Pelican rode at anchor in a lagoon that no longer felt like sanctuary, and the real map — the true one, which I had never kept in the chart locker, because a man who trusts no harbor trusts no locker either — hung in its oilskin from my neck, warm against my chest like a second heartbeat.

We gathered in the cockpit as the sun came up. Nell, gray-faced, propped against the wheel. Pili red-eyed and silent. Mara with blood on her sleeves she hadn't washed off. Me, with the map.

"They'll be back," Nell said. "Sefo knows we're here, and he knows we know it. But he also knows the monsoon's coming, and even Halloran doesn't argue with a monsoon. Which means we have maybe a day, maybe two, before the weather closes the window — for them and for us."

"So we dive the Meridian before the storm," Mara said. "Find the bullion hold. Find the manifests. Get it done."

"With a wounded engineer, a wounded skipper, and a gunboat sitting between us and the wreck site." I looked at the chart, at the coordinates Wren had died for, at the little penciled cross that had cost so much already. "Or we wait out the storm here, and come back after, and hope Halloran's divers haven't lifted everything worth taking."

"No," Nell said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Halloran thinks he's hunting a map," she said slowly, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before — not the gruff skipper, but the woman underneath, the one who'd lost crew before and buried them and kept sailing. "He doesn't know there's no map anymore. He doesn't know what's in Bram's head and Mara's head. And he doesn't know his own engineer—" she glanced toward the cabin where Tui slept, "—his own diver, his own salvage master, is about to make the worst mistake of his life, because he thinks he's already won." She pushed herself upright against the pain. "We don't run, and we don't wait. We let him think he's won. And while his gunboat watches this lagoon for us to bolt — we go out the west passage, quiet, on the flood tide, tonight, straight into the tail of the monsoon. Straight to the Meridian. Let him chase us into the storm. I've sailed worse than Edmund Halloran, and I've sailed worse weather than this one's going to be."

Pili stared at her. "That's insane, Skipper."

"Yes," Nell said. "Get the boiler lit."

Beyond the reef, far to the south, the sky had begun to bruise — a great violet darkness building along the horizon like a wall being raised, and the first long swell of it was already reaching us, lifting the Pelican gently, patiently, like a hand opening to receive her.
