## 17. Eye of the Storm

The eye came for them at dusk.

Mara felt it before she understood it — a change in the pressure behind her ears, the way the rain slackened from a hammering to a whisper, the sea flattening into something obscene and glassy. For six hours the lagoon had been a cauldron, white water screaming over the reef lip, the Pelican bucking at her anchors like a horse fighting the bit. Now the wind simply stopped, and the silence that rushed in was worse than the storm. It had the quality of held breath. Of something leaning over them to look.

She climbed out of the engine room with Tui's wrench still in her hand and her arms shaking from the pumps, and she stood on the wet deck and looked up.

The sky above the lagoon was open. Not clear — nothing about this night would ever be clear — but open, a ragged circular window of violet-black cloud with stars showing through it, cold and indifferent, as if the heavens had peeled back an eyelid to watch what they would do next. All around the rim of that window the storm wall stood like a cliff face, turning slowly, lit from within by lightning that never quite reached the ground.

"Beautiful, isn't it," Nell said.

Mara turned. The skipper was propped in the doorway of the wheelhouse with one arm braced against the frame, and even in the strange half-light Mara could see how much that small posture cost her. The bandage under her shirt had been changed twice since the boarding fight; the third time, Pili had done it, her hands steady and her jaw set, refusing to look at the blood on the old dressing. Nell's skin had a grey undertone now, the grey of metal left too long in salt water. But her eyes were the same as they'd been the day Mara first boarded the Pelican under forged papers: measuring, unfooled, awake.

"You should be lying down," Mara said.

"I should be a great many things." Nell eased herself onto the wheelhouse bench, moving her left arm carefully, like something borrowed. "Come here. You'll want to see this while it lasts."

Mara climbed the two steps and sat beside her. Through the salt-streaked glass they watched the eye drift across the sky, vast and slow, a cathedral of rotating cloud with a hole bored through its heart. Somewhere beyond the eastern reef, muffled by distance, thunder rolled without end.

"How long?" Mara asked.

"An hour. Maybe less." Nell spat over the side, an old habit that seemed to cost her something. "Then the back wall comes down on us and we get the second half of it. Storms are like debts, Voss. They always come round again, and the second visit is meaner than the first."

In the cockpit below, Pili had fallen asleep sitting upright, her back against the coaming, one hand still curled around the signal lamp she'd refused to put down since dark. She'd rigged it facing the reef passage, Mara had noticed. Facing out. As if she expected someone to come looking for them — or expected to have to signal someone not to find them. The girl hadn't spoken more than ten words since Tui. Since they'd buried him at sea that morning, sewn into canvas because there was no time and no ground deep enough, and read whatever words Bram could remember, which weren't many, and didn't need to be.

Mara looked away from the girl and found Bram standing at the bow rail, alone, his silhouette cut sharp against the flat silver water. He'd been there most of the hour. She knew why. She'd stood at rails like that herself, in another life, after closing a file that had ended someone's livelihood, and she knew the particular weight of a body that doesn't want to come inside yet.

She went to him.

He heard her boots on the deck and didn't turn, but his shoulders came down half an inch, the way they always did when she got close — a loosening he probably didn't know he did, and that she had decided, somewhere over the last hundred miles of running, not to mention.

"The pumps are holding," she said. "Tui patched the intake before—" She stopped. There was no way to finish that sentence that wasn't a wound. "They're holding."

"For now." His voice was rough. "Everything holds until it doesn't."

Mara leaned her forearms on the rail beside him. Below them the lagoon lay mirror-still, and in it the Pelican floated on her own reflection, two ships keel to keel in the impossible calm, riding inside the storm like a secret inside a lie.

"Bram." She said his name quietly, and waited until he glanced at her. "I've been doing the arithmetic all afternoon. I want you to tell me if I'm wrong."

"Go on."

"Halloran's barge is anchored off the Meridian site — forty miles northeast, give or take. The eye passes over us, then the tail of the system swings through on a northeasterly track. If we leave at the back edge of the eye—"

"No."

"—we'd have the wind quartering astern the whole run. Forty miles in these seas, running before it instead of beating into it—"

"I said no." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "You want to take a leaking trawler with a shot skipper, a dead engineer, and a crew of three into the trailing edge of a monsoon, to arrive at an armed salvage barge with no plan except arriving. That's not arithmetic, Mara. That's a eulogy with extra steps."

"It's the only window we'll get." She kept her voice level. She'd learned that from him, or maybe she'd always known it and he'd taught her to trust it. "Once the storm clears, Halloran lifts the bullion, and the manifests go down with the hold when he scuttles the wreck over it — same as he's done before, same as the navy helped him do before. We have until the weather breaks. After that, it's gone. Wren's promise is gone. Everything those men died for is gone, and Halloran walks away clean, and Crane's warrant stays live on both our heads until one of us dies inconveniently."

Bram stared out at the water. Lightning crawled along the underside of the storm wall, far off, silent, like veins of fire in a black mountain.

"And if we miss the window," he said finally, "if the tail catches us in open water, we don't die clean either. We die scattered. Pili's sixteen years old."

"Pili already voted."

That made him turn. "What?"

"She, Nell, and I took a vote while you were brooding at the bow rail." Mara allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Three to nothing. You were the only one not consulted, on the grounds that you'd say no."

For a long moment he said nothing at all. Then he laughed — a short, cracked sound with no real humor in it, but real everything else. "Christ. Two weeks aboard and she's organizing mutinies."

"She organized one the day she stowed away, from the sound of it. We just gave it a flag."

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse, all four of them, because there was nowhere else and no reason anymore to pretend there was a hierarchy larger than necessity. Nell sat at the chart table with the plot spread out under a swinging lantern, one hand pinning the corner flat. Pili perched on the engine cover, still holding the signal lamp, her eyes red-rimmed and ferocious. Bram stood at the door with his arms crossed, which Mara had learned was not refusal but surrender being negotiated.

Nell tapped the chart with a blunt finger. "Here's us. Here's the reef passage — we go out on the north channel, and we go out *now*, in the eye, while the swell's down, because once the back wall hits, that channel is a washing machine and we're the laundry." Her finger slid northeast across the blank blue paper. "Forty-one miles to the Meridian site. The barge will be riding it out on dynamic positioning or a heavy moor — Halloran doesn't do anything cheap. Which means when we come out the back side of the storm, we come out fast, low in the water, running dark, and they'll have their whole attention on their lift operation and their own decks tearing themselves apart."

"They'll have lookouts," Bram said.

"They'll have lookouts who can't see past their own bowsprits in that mess. And they won't be expecting company, because no sane person puts to sea in the tail of a monsoon." Nell looked up, and there was blood under her fingernails and exhaustion carved into every line of her face, and her voice did not waver at all. "Good thing nobody on this boat has ever been accused of sanity."

"Tui would've said the engines aren't ready," Pili said suddenly.

Everyone went quiet. The girl stared at the deck, her knuckles white around the lamp handle.

"He'd have said the number two injector's fouled and the raw water pump's on borrowed time and you'd be mad to push her past eight knots in a following sea." Her voice cracked, and she pushed through it the way she pushed through everything, head down, fists closed. "And then he'd have fixed it anyway, because he always did, so I did what he'd do. Injector's cleaned. Pump's repacked. She'll make nine knots if we ask nice and ten if we ask ugly." She lifted her chin, daring anyone to say it wasn't enough. "I checked his notes. He wrote everything down. He wrote down *everything*."

Mara's throat tightened. She crossed the wheelhouse and put a hand on the girl's shoulder, and Pili didn't shrug it off, which was its own kind of permission.

"Then we ask ugly," Nell said softly.

Bram uncrossed his arms. Mara watched him do it — watched the last of the resistance go out of him like tide leaving a shoal. He came to the chart table and bent over it, and when he spoke, he was already somewhere else, forty miles northeast, already underwater.

"If we reach the barge, we can't board her. Six of them at least, armed, and we're four with a rifle and a flare gun. So we don't board." His finger touched the chart where the wreck lay. "We dive. While they're fighting their own weather topside, we go down to the hold. I rig the collapse charges on the aft bulkhead — the same trick I used on survey jobs, reversed. Bring the hull section down onto the evidence vault, bury the manifests under sixty tons of steel and coral. By the time their divers can get down there, there'll be nothing to lift but rubble."

"And the gold?" Pili asked.

"Goes with it." Bram said it flatly, and Mara saw him glance at her — a quick check, the old reflex, the man who'd burned a stamped case rather than report it, still waiting to learn whether the woman beside him was the kind who flinched at burning things. She met his eyes and didn't flinch. Something in his face settled. "Wren made me promise to see it found or see it destroyed. Found means Halloran finds it. So we destroy it. The manifests are the treasure. Everything else is bait he's been fishing with for twenty years."

"There's a problem," Mara said. "The copies."

Everyone looked at her.

"I copied the manifests when we recovered them. Photographed every page, every stamp, every serial." She patted the waterproof case slung across her body — the one she'd slept with, dived with, carried through the boarding fight with a bullet hole punched through its corner an inch from her ribs. "If we sink the originals and something happens to me, the evidence dies with the wreck and none of it was worth anything. So the plan needs a second half. When we're clear — or if we're not — those photographs go out. Every port authority, every shipping registry, every newsroom from Brisbane to Singapore. I built the distribution list months ago, back when I thought I was building it for Crane. It's ready. It just needs a transmitter and five minutes."

"A radio broadcast in the middle of a monsoon," Nell said. "From a trawler running dark."

"Storm static cuts both ways. Nobody's listening for us, and nobody can triangulate us in that noise. And if the Pelican doesn't survive—" Mara shrugged, and found that the shrug cost less than she'd expected. "Then the case goes down with me, and Bram sinks the originals, and Halloran keeps his gold and loses his sleep for the rest of his life, wondering. It's not perfect justice. It's the justice that's on offer."

Nell grunted. From her, Mara had learned, a grunt was a standing ovation.

* * *

It was while they worked — rigging the deck cargo, charging the pumps, Pili in the engine room speaking quietly to Tui's ghost of a maintenance log — that Nell called Mara into the wheelhouse alone.

The skipper was sitting very straight on the bench, both hands flat on the chart table, and Mara recognized the posture. It was the posture of a woman spending strength she couldn't afford, on purpose, because there was a thing that needed saying and a limited window in which to say it.

"Sit down, investigator."

"I'm not—"

"You were. You might be again someday, if the world turns honest. Sit."

Mara sat.

Nell studied her for a long moment, and outside the wheelhouse glass the eye was beginning to close, the western wall of the storm creeping inward across the stars like a lid coming down.

"When that bullet went through me," Nell said, "I was on my back on the deck looking up at the sky, and I thought, well. This is what it costs. I've spent thirty years telling myself the sea takes what it takes and holds no grudges, and there I was learning different." Her voice stayed even, but her hands pressed harder against the table. "Tui wasn't so lucky. And I've had two days to decide whether I'm angry at you for bringing Halloran's war to my boat."

"You'd be right to be."

"I know I'd be right to be. That's not the question." Nell's eyes were steady and terrible. "The question is what you're going to be, going forward. Because I've watched you this week, Voss. I watched you splice line wrong on purpose so Pili could correct you and feel useful. I watched you give up your bunk without being asked. I watched you stand between Bram and that gunboat's searchlight knowing what they'd do to you if they caught you. People show you what they are when the bullets start. Bullets started. So here's mine." She leaned forward, slow and deliberate, grey-faced and immovable. "After tonight, whatever happens — if we live, if we don't — you're crew. Not a passenger running from a warrant. Crew. And crew doesn't get to die politely keeping secrets. Whatever else is locked in that case of yours besides photographs, you'll tell Bram before we clear the reef. All of it. He's bled enough for other people's silences."

Mara held her gaze. "There's nothing else. The photographs, the list, my orders from Crane — he knows all of it."

"Not the orders." Nell sat back. "He knows you *had* orders. He doesn't know what Crane promised you if you brought Kavanagh in quiet. Your old life back. Your name cleared. A promotion with a view of the harbor." She let that sit in the air between them. "Pili reads faces. So do I. You told him the shape of it, not the price of it. Tonight, before we clear the reef — you tell him the price. Because if he hears it from the sea instead of from you, in the middle of what's coming, it'll gut him. And I need that man's whole heart on the end of a dive line tomorrow, not the three-quarters version."

Mara looked down at her hands. They were rope-burned and salt-cracked and shaking slightly, and she realized she was no longer sure whose hands they were — the investigator's, or the crew member's. Perhaps that was the answer. Perhaps that had been the answer since Cairns.

"All right," she said. "Before the reef."

"Good." Nell reached under the chart table and produced a bottle — rum, cheap, half gone, the label worn to ghosts. She poured two measures into tin cups and pushed one across. "To Tui," she said. "Who wrote everything down."

"To Tui," Mara echoed, and drank, and the rum burned all the way down like a small bright coal, like something that intended to keep burning.

* * *

The back wall hit at 0300.

It came without ceremony — one moment the eye's strange starlit calm, the next a solid blow that laid the Pelican over fifteen degrees and sent everything not lashed across the deck in a single sliding avalanche. The wind returned all at once, from the new quarter, screaming through the rigging in a register Mara felt in her teeth. Rain came horizontal, hard as gravel. The lagoon, so long a mirror, became a boiling white field in seconds.

They cleared the north channel at first light — or what passed for light, a bruised grey suggestion of dawn under cloud so low it seemed to comb the masthead. Nell stood at the wheel with Pili lashed beside her on a double harness, the girl's hands on the spokes and Nell's resting over hers, guiding more than steering. The skipper's face was the color of wet ash and her lips were pressed thin against pain, but her voice came out clear and unhurried over the roar, calling the sets, reading the sea the way other people read print.

"Big one on the port bow — ease her — *ease* — now bear away, ride her stern, let her carry us—"

The Pelican went out through the reef gap into the open Coral Sea and turned her bow northeast, and the storm took her like a hand taking a leaf.

Mara stood at the wheelhouse door with the waterproof case strapped tight against her body and watched the world become water. There was no horizon. There was no up, sometimes, for whole seconds together. The trawler surfed down wave-faces tall as churches, buried her bows in green blackness, shook herself free, and clawed up the next one while the engine howled below at full ugly throttle and Pili sang — actually sang, some high broken fragment of a song from home, pitched against the wind like a challenge.

Before the reef, as promised, Mara had found Bram in the lee of the wheelhouse and told him the rest. The promotion. The cleared name. The apartment Crane had described, and the quiet transfer, and the understanding that Kavanagh would simply disappear into a naval prison and no one would ask questions. She'd told him all of it in a flat rush, watching his face, waiting for it to close.

It hadn't closed. He'd listened all the way to the end, and then he'd taken her rope-burned hand in both of his and said, "You gave it up. That's the part that matters. The rest is just the size of what you gave up." And then the back wall had arrived, and there'd been no more time for anything but survival.

Now, forty miles of hell stretching ahead of them, she watched him forward on the deck checking the dive bottles lashed under the rail, moving with the ship's motion like a man born to it, and she understood that Nell had been right about something else too: whatever happened today, on the barge or in the water or in the collapsing dark of the Meridian's hold, she was no longer investigating Bram Kavanagh. She was crew. And crew went where the ship went.

"Voss!" Nell's voice cut through the gale. The skipper jerked her chin forward, toward the churning grey east, and shouted the words that turned Mara's blood to ice water and rocket fuel at once:

"Smoke on the horizon! Flare stack — that's her, that's the barge!" A grin split the grey ruin of her face, fierce as a wound. "We beat the storm there, people! Now let's see if we can beat *Halloran!*"

Ahead, through a tear in the rain, a smudge of smoke rose off the sea, and beneath it — glimpsed and lost and glimpsed again between the wave crests — the pale superstructure of the salvage barge, riding its moorings over the grave of the Meridian Star, its cranes already swinging out over the water.

The lift had begun.
