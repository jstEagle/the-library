## 18. Into the Tail

The eye passed over us at dusk and gave us forty minutes of terrible mercy — a sky like a held breath, a sea gone flat and greasy, the whole horizon ringing with the black towers of the storm we'd have to cross to reach Halloran's barge. In that silence I could hear every sound the Pelican made: the bilge pumps working in their arrhythmic stutter, Tui's hammer somewhere below patching what couldn't be patched, Nell's breathing from the wheelhouse doorway, shallow and wet where the bullet had clipped through her side and out again.

She stood there with one hand pressed to the bandage and the other pointing at the southern wall of cloud.

"There," she said. "That's our door."

It wasn't a door. It was a cathedral of falling water two hundred feet high, lit from inside by lightning like something alive behind frosted glass.

"Nell," Mara said quietly, "the forecast puts the trailing edge right across the barge's position. If we go now, we ride it in. If we wait for it to pass—"

"Halloran lifts the gold." Nell finished it for her. She turned her head slowly, and even gray-faced with blood loss she managed a smile that had teeth in it. "Then we don't wait."

* * *

We went into the tail at full dark, and I will tell you honestly that part of me believed we were all going to die, and another part of me — the diver's part, the part that had learned long ago that panic is just air spent too fast — noted that belief, set it aside, and got on with the work.

The first hour was the worst thing I've ever been inside of. The wind came not in gusts but in sustained screams, and the rain didn't fall so much as travel horizontally, hitting the deck like gravel flung by an angry god. The Pelican climbed waves the size of buildings and fell down their far sides with a shudder that came up through my boots and into my spine. Twice she broached almost to her beam ends, and both times Pili hauled her back.

Pili was lashed to the wheel.

Nell had done it herself, threading line under the girl's arms and around the helm stanchion, knotting it with the quick economy of a woman who'd spent fifty years tying things down so they wouldn't be lost. "You steer," Nell told her, shouting over the wind with her mouth close to Pili's ear, "and if you're thrown clear, the rope throws you back. That's the whole trick of it. Same as life."

So Pili steered. Fifteen years old, soaked to the bone, knuckles white on spokes that bucked like live animals, and she steered. Every time the bow buried and the green water came over the top of us, she rode the wheel up out of it, correcting before Nell even called the course, and I watched Nell watch her, and I saw something in the old woman's face that I hadn't seen since I'd come aboard — pride without any fear mixed into it.

"Two points to starboard!" Nell roared. "Ride her down the face — don't fight the drop, use it!"

"Aye!" Pili's voice cracked on it and she didn't care.

I was on the foredeck with Mara, running lifelines forward to check the lashings on the dive gear. There was no reason to check them — I'd rigged them myself, doubled everything, and the gear was going nowhere — but there was also no reason to stand still, and standing still in that kind of weather is how a man starts thinking. So we moved along the rail hand over hand, clipped in, shouting things neither of us could hear, until we reached the pulpit and hung there together looking into the boiling dark ahead.

Mara's face was stripped bare by the spray. No investigator's composure left, no careful lies, no careful truths either. Just a woman lashed to the bow of a dying trawler because she'd chosen to be here, and when she caught me looking at her she didn't look away.

"How much air we carrying?" she shouted.

"Enough for the job. Not enough for mistakes!"

"Then we won't make any!" And she grinned — actually grinned, salt water streaming off her jaw — and something in my chest that had been clenched since Port Moresby let go half a turn.

We went aft. Below, in the engine room, Tui fought his own private war. The pumps were losing. I could hear it in the rhythm — the bilge alarm cycling faster each hour, the water in the hold coming up past the floorboards, cold around our ankles, then our shins. Tui had the auxiliary pump torn open on the workbench, rebuilding it with parts cannibalized from God knew what, and every time we rolled he threw himself over the machinery to keep it from walking off its mounts.

"She'll hold!" he yelled up at me once, through the engine room hatch, his face black with grease and lit by the work lamp swinging above him. "She has to! I owe her!"

He didn't say who he owed. He didn't look at Nell's door when he said it. But I saw his hands shake on the wrench, and I understood that whatever deal he'd made with Halloran's men, whatever debt he'd tried to drown in their money, he was paying it now in the only currency that mattered — staying below, in the dark, keeping us alive.

I didn't forgive him yet. But I stopped counting what he owed me.

* * *

Past midnight the storm began to spend itself, though spending itself for a monsoon's trailing edge still meant seas no sane man would cross voluntarily. The rain thinned from gravel to needles. The wind dropped from scream to roar. And through a tear in the cloud cover, for maybe thirty seconds, we saw stars — hard, cold, indifferent — and then the sky closed again.

Nell navigated by dead reckoning and stubbornness. The radar was dead; the GPS antenna had been carried away in the first hour; but Nell had been running these waters since before satellites were anything but military secrets, and she counted wave sets and timed the swells and read the current the way a blind man reads a face.

"Bearings say the shoal water starts in forty minutes," she told us in the wheelhouse, her voice hoarse down to a rasp. "Barge should be anchored just past the twelve-fathom line, riding it out same as us. Halloran's not stupid. He'll have picked his anchorage. But he'll think nobody's crazy enough to be moving tonight."

"He doesn't know Nell Okonkwo," Pili said.

"No," Nell said, and put her hand briefly on the girl's shoulder, above the lashings. "He surely does not."

At 0230 by the clock, which had become more ritual than instrument, Mara spotted the light.

It came and went behind the swells — a white strobe, high up, mounted on something tall. Then, lower, a cluster of work lights glowing yellow-green through the rain-haze, like a carnival seen through dirty glass.

"There." Mara's arm was steady as she pointed. "Range and bearing match Wren's coordinates. It's the barge."

And beneath the work lights, faint but unmistakable even at two miles, the sound reached us across the water: compressors. The diesel-throb of surface-supplied diving equipment, running flat out.

"They've started," I said.

"They started hours ago, most likely," Nell said. "Storm kept their topside crews battened down, but a lift operation runs from the barge, not the boats. They'll have divers down on the wreck this whole time, or they'll have sent the ROV. Either way—" she spat over the rail, an old habit that cost her a wince, "—they're not waiting for fair weather, and neither are we."

The last three miles took an hour. We came in with the sea behind us, surfing down faces that would have broached a lesser hull, Pili fighting the wheel the whole way, Nell calling depths off the echo sounder as the bottom rose toward us — twenty fathoms, sixteen, fourteen — and then the swell changed character, steepening, shortening, the sea-floor reaching up to grab at the storm's energy, and Nell said, "Anchor's got to be close. Watch for chain. Pili — ready to go astern on my word."

We found the barge the way you find a reef at night: too late to avoid seeing how close you'd come. She loomed out of the rain like a cliff that had decided to float — two hundred feet of steel deck cranes and container modules and floodlights, anchored bows-on to the swell, rising and falling with a slow majesty that made our little trawler feel like a dinghy beside a cathedral. Her name ran along her stern in letters ten feet high: MERIDIAN SALVOR II.

Halloran hadn't even bothered to hide it. He'd named his salvage barge after the ship he'd murdered.

"Go astern," Nell said quietly. "Take us under her stern quarter, into her lee. Slow as you can."

Pili brought us in like a thief. Under the barge's leeward side the sea flattened abruptly, and we slid into that shadowed pocket of relative calm, engines ticking over, and looked up at eighty feet of steel hull streaked with rust and running lights.

A voice came down out of the dark above us, amplified, bored: "Vessel alongside, identify yourself."

Mara looked at me. I looked at Nell. Nell looked at Pili.

"Pelican," Nell called back, pitching her ruined voice into something cracked and harmless. "Fishing vessel, disabled, requesting shelter in your lee till daylight!"

A pause. Somewhere above, a man's voice speaking to another man, indistinct. Then: "Hold your position. Do not come alongside. Someone will inspect you at first light."

"Aye," Nell called. "Much obliged."

The loudspeaker clicked off. Above us, unseen, the compressors kept their steady heartbeat. Divers down. Right now, at the bottom of twelve fathoms of storm-churned water, Halloran's men were working on the Meridian Star's bullion hold, cutting, rigging, preparing to raise what Elias Wren had died trying to show me.

We drifted in the barge's lee, and the crew of the Pelican looked at one another across a deck slicked with rain, and nobody said the obvious thing, which was that we were half-drowned, shot through, leaking, and about to dive into flooded wreckage guarded by armed men.

Tui came up from below, wiping his hands on a rag that only spread the grease around. He looked at the barge towering over us, then at Nell, and his jaw worked twice before anything came out.

"The pumps'll keep her afloat till dawn," he said. "After that, I make no promises."

"Nobody's asking for promises," Nell said. "Just work."

"I know." His voice broke on it. "That's why I'm still here."

* * *

We rigged in the dark, by feel and by the spill of the barge's own work lights filtering down over the water. Mara checked my gear and I checked hers, the old ritual, each regulator tested twice, each buckle, each knife. Pili pressed a spare torch into my hand and closed my fingers over it.

"Bring them back," she said. Meaning Mara. Meaning me. Meaning, underneath it, all of us.

"We'll bring back more than that," I said, and wished immediately that I'd said something simpler, because promises have weight, and I was already carrying Wren's.

We slipped over the Pelican's port side at 0340, away from the barge's watch lights, and swam the hundred yards to the wreck site underwater, surfacing only once, briefly, between swells, to take a bearing. Twelve fathoms down, the Meridian Star waited exactly where she'd waited for sixty years, and the first thing I saw when the silt cleared was the work of Halloran's divers: guide lines strung taut to the bullion hold, cutting torches' scorch marks bright as fresh scars on the hull plating, and a lifting frame already bolted over the hold coaming, its slings hanging slack, waiting for cargo.

They'd be back at first light. Maybe sooner.

Mara's torch found mine in the green dark. She pointed at the hold, then at the evidence vault compartment aft of it — the one we'd found on the first dive, where the manifests sat chained in their watertight case — and then she looked at me through her mask, and I nodded.

Tonight we learned the ground. Tomorrow, while Halloran's divers lifted gold, we would do the other thing. The thing Wren had asked for.

We hung there in the drowned ship's shadow, two small lights against all that dark, while overhead the barge groaned at its anchor and the tail of the monsoon dragged its last fury across the Coral Sea — and above us, faint through sixty feet of water, we heard the winches begin to turn.
