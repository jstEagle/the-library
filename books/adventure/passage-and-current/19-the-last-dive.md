## 19. The Last Dive

**Bram**

The winches were still turning when we went down.

I felt them through the water more than heard them — a low, patient vibration in the bones of the Meridian Star, sixty feet of green-black sea pressing down between me and the barge's floodlights. The storm's tail had spent itself in the night, but the swell was still running, long and heavy, and every time it rolled past, the whole drowned ship seemed to breathe. Hull plates ticking. Corrosion sighing through seams that hadn't seen air in forty years.

Mara was at my shoulder. I could see her light swinging in the murk, steady, the way she did everything. We'd spent the last hour of darkness going over the plan until Nell told us to shut up and sleep, and now there was no more planning to do. Only the doing.

The bullion hold was aft, below the old cargo deck, behind a watertight door that had been watertight for the last time sometime in 1963. We'd found it yesterday. We'd found the other thing yesterday too — the steel vault welded into the forward bulkhead of the hold, the one Wren's map had circled three times in a hand gone shaky with morphine. The manifests were still inside. I'd put my palm flat against the cold of that door and felt the whole weight of the story settle onto me: Halloran's ships, Halloran's scuttling charges, Halloran's crews locked below with them. The same stamped serials I'd burned off Thursday Island. The same operation, running twenty years before I was old enough to drown in it.

The gold was a lure. It had always been a lure. Halloran hadn't come back for bullion — he'd come back because the one thing that could hang him had been sitting in a steel box on the sea floor, waiting for someone curious enough to open it.

So we weren't going to open it.

We were going to bury it.

* * *

**Mara**

The hold swallowed our lights the way the sea swallowed everything — greedily, without echo. Bram went first through the cargo deck, past the collapsed mast that had killed the Meridian's last mate, and I followed, watching the silver shimmer of his exhaust bubbles climb toward a surface I could no longer see.

I had signed papers, once, that said wrecks like this belonged to insurers. That salvage was a matter of liens and percentages and due diligence. I had believed, in the way you believe things before you've held them in your hands, that the law was a floor beneath the world — that as long as you stood on it, you couldn't fall.

Then I'd read the manifests through the porthole glass, and the floor had turned out to be a trapdoor.

Bram stopped at the hold entrance and turned, and even through the mask I could read his face: the question he asked every time before we committed to something. *Still with me?*

I tapped his forearm twice. *Yes.* Then I pointed down, into the dark, and we went in together.

The bullion hold was a steel room the size of a chapel, and the vault was its altar — a riveted box bolted to the forward bulkhead, its lock already sprung by Halloran's advance divers, its door hanging open like a mouth. Inside, wrapped in oiled canvas gone to leather, the manifests waited. I'd seen them yesterday. I'd photographed every page with the little waterproof camera Tui had rigged, and the copies were already lashed under the Pelican's wheelbox, and by tonight they would be in a hundred hands across the Pacific.

But the originals had to stay. Wren had been clear about that, or as clear as a dying man can be: *See it found, or see it destroyed.* Found, the copies would do. But the originals — the originals had to go somewhere no lawyer's writ, no navy diver, no quiet cheque could ever reach them.

Bram had worked it out on the chart table, chalk and string and Nell's old cargo tables. The after bulkhead of the bullion hold was already failing — we'd seen the crack running corner to corner, weeping rust like an old wound. Above it, wedged into the cargo deck, lay the Meridian's spare boiler: nine tons of iron, held off the deck by two stanchions and forty years of luck.

Cut the stanchions. Drop the boiler. The deck goes, the bulkhead goes, and nine tons of iron comes down on the vault like a headstone.

We had four charges. Tui had built them from the Pelican's flare canisters and a prayer. We had perhaps ninety minutes before Halloran's dive team started their lift on the bullion room two compartments forward — and perhaps less than that, because men like Halloran's did not keep schedules; they kept guns.

* * *

**Bram**

The first stanchion was the easy one.

I set the charge low, where the steel had already thinned, and packed it with mud from the deck so the shock would go into the metal and not out into the water. Mara held the light for me, both hands, rock steady, and I thought — not for the first time and not for the last — how strange it was that I'd spent six years trusting no one, and now my life hung on the steadiness of a woman who'd been paid to find me.

She wasn't paid anymore. None of us were paid anymore. That was the whole point.

We backed into the companionway, and I thumbed the detonator, and the stanchion went with a sound I felt in my teeth — a soft, enormous *whump* that rolled through the wreck like a whale's call. When we came back in, the stanchion was a twisted stub and the boiler had shifted, settling an inch, groaning like something waking up.

One left. Then the far one.

Mara was rigging the lift bags on the vault door when I saw her light stop moving. She hung in the water, frozen, and I came up beside her and followed her beam to the vault's open mouth.

The manifests were gone.

For three full seconds my heart stopped, and the sea rushed in to fill the space. Then I saw it — the corner of canvas, folded, tucked into the shadow at the back of the vault, behind a steel plate that had been pried loose and set back in place. A hiding place. Wren's hiding place, maybe, or the last survivor's, someone who'd known what was coming for the paper even if he hadn't known what was coming for him.

Mara looked at me. I looked at her. We didn't have the air to waste on the conversation, but we had it anyway, in the shorthand divers use. She pointed at the canvas. Then at her chest — *I'll get it*. Then she made a fist and pressed it against the vault door — *and it stays*.

I nodded. She worked the canvas free, wrapped it tight against her chest harness, and buckled it down like a mother strapping in a child.

Then the far stanchion, and after that, nothing left to do but the thing itself.

* * *

**Mara**

The last charge went against the after bulkhead itself, at the head of the crack, and Bram set it with the care of a man laying flowers on a grave. Which, in a way, he was. We both were. Somewhere under that steel were men who'd been locked in with the scuttling charges in 1963, men whose names were on the manifests against my ribs, men whose deaths had been signed for in a Brisbane office by a man who was, at this moment, somewhere overhead in the storm-light, waiting for his gold.

I checked my gauge. Sixty bar. Enough. Not enough for mistakes.

Bram took my hand — a clumsy, gauntleted grip, nothing like the way he'd taken it on the Pelican's deck two nights ago, when the rain had finally stopped and neither of us had gone below. Through the masks, his eyes were calm. That was the thing about Bram Kavanagh that no one who hadn't dived with him would ever understand: the man was afraid of everything on land and afraid of nothing down here. The sea was the only place his ghosts left him alone.

We backed out through the cargo deck, into the open water above the wreck, and I looked down at the Meridian Star one last time as a whole — the great broken length of her, masts down, funnel collapsed, lying on her side in the sand like a woman asleep. Forty years she'd kept the secret. We were about to make sure she kept it forever.

Bram raised the detonator.

And that was when the floodlights above us went out.

* * *

**Bram**

Not all of them. Two stayed burning — but the work lights over the bullion lift died all at once, and through sixty feet of water I heard the change in the barge's voice: engines flaring, men shouting, the winches stopping mid-turn.

Then a new sound. A chop-chop-chop, distant, coming down through the water from a long way up.

A helicopter.

Halloran. He'd flown in ahead of his own schedule, riding the storm's last edge, because men like Edmund Halloran trusted no one else to hold their treasure — and because he was the kind of man who needed to watch.

I looked at Mara. She looked at me. Sixty bar each, and the choice we'd already made hanging in the water between us like a third diver.

We could surface. We could hide in the wreck and let the moment pass. Or we could finish it now, in the next sixty seconds, while every man on that barge was looking up at the sky instead of down at the sea.

Mara pointed down. Then she held her fist against her heart — the manifests, safe, against her ribs — and pointed down again.

I thumbed the detonator.

The bulkhead went. Even at sixty feet, even through the hull of a dead ship, the sound reached us — a deep, final concussion, like the sea clearing its throat. The boiler came down the way nine tons of iron comes down, which is to say like judgment. I felt the shockwave shove past us, saw the silt leap off the wreck in a vast brown bloom, and then the Meridian's after section folded, slowly, almost gently, the bullion hold crushing closed around the vault like a hand closing around a secret.

The gold went with it. Every bar. Every coin. Down into the dark, under the iron, under the sand the collapse was already pulling down like a shroud.

Mara's hand found my arm and squeezed, hard, and through the murk her mask was a pale blur and I knew she was crying, because I was.

*See it found, or see it destroyed, Elias.* I'd done both. God help me, I'd done both.

Then the bloom of silt began to settle, and the two remaining floodlights carved cones through the green water, and I saw the shapes coming down the anchor line from the barge — divers, four of them, descending fast, lights swinging, looking for their gold and finding a graveyard instead.

And above them, faint through the water, the helicopter settled onto the barge's pad, and somewhere in my chest, under the regulator's hiss and the hammer of my heart, I heard Elias Wren's last breath, and I made myself one promise more:

Whatever happened next — whatever Halloran did, whatever the navy did, whatever the sea itself decided — we were not coming up without the truth.

Mara took my hand. We turned, two small lights against all that dark, and started for the surface — where Edmund Halloran was waiting.
