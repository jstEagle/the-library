## 20. Halloran's Reckoning

The barge rose out of the rain like a lit cathedral.

Floodlights blazed from her deck rails, cutting white cones down into the churning water where Halloran's divers worked the lift lines. Generators thundered. A crane swung its hook in slow, patient arcs, and every arc brought up another length of chain from the Meridian Star's grave. Bram stood at the Pelican's rail with rain hammering his shoulders and watched the thing he had been running from for eleven years finally catch him.

"There," Nell said. Her voice was gravel and blood loss, but her hands on the wheel were steady as bedrock. "Port side, amidships. That's their boarding ladder."

Pili had lashed herself to the helm beside Nell, both of them soaked through, both of them grinning like this was a fair-day race instead of the tail end of a monsoon that had already tried to kill them twice since midnight. Tui was below with the pumps, keeping the old girl alive by main force and profanity.

And Mara stood at Bram's shoulder, her hair plastered to her face, one hand pressed flat over the waterproof case strapped to her chest — the copied manifests, scanned frame by frame on the bottom of the Coral Sea while Halloran's men slept.

"You don't have to go up there," she said. "I can send it all from here. The radio—"

"They'd jam you before the second transmission." He said it without heat, because it was simply true, the way the tide was true. "He'll want to talk. Men like Halloran always want to talk. That's our door."

"And if he offers you enough?"

Bram looked at her. Rain ran between them like a curtain, and through it her eyes held his — not afraid, not pleading. Asking him to be what she had already decided he was.

"He will," Bram said. "That's how I know exactly what to do."

* * *

They came up the boarding ladder into light so hard it blinded. Two of Halloran's men took their arms — professional, almost gentle, the way you handle cargo you've been paid to keep intact — and walked them across a deck awash with rain and generator stink toward the wheelhouse superstructure, where a man in a dry oilskin coat stood beneath an awning with a cup of coffee steaming in his hand.

Edmund Halloran was older than his photographs. Silver at the temples, a face built for boardrooms and funerals, eyes the flat grey of deep water. He looked at Bram the way a man looks at a horse he is considering buying.

"Kavanagh." He said the name like he was tasting it. "Eleven years I've wondered what happened to you. The navy file says drowned. Off Thursday Island." He smiled faintly. "Files are such obedient things."

"The navy writes what it's told," Bram said.

"Yes." Halloran sipped his coffee. "It does. And it tells me what I pay it to tell. Which brings us to business, because the storm won't wait and neither will I." He nodded at Mara. "Miss Voss. Or should I say Miss Voss, formerly of Crane & Associates, currently wanted in three jurisdictions? You've had an eventful season."

"She's with me," Bram said.

"I can see that. It's rather the point." Halloran set down the cup. Somewhere below them, a lift line groaned and the crane took up slack; the whole barge shivered. "Here is what happens now, Kavanagh. In about forty minutes my divers reach the bullion hold. In about fifty, four hundred thousand ounces of gold start coming up in slings. And in about an hour, you and I are going to be very rich men, or one of us is going to be a very dead one, and I have honestly stopped caring which. But before we get there — because I am, despite everything, a reasonable man — I'm going to make you an offer."

"You scuttled your own ships," Bram said. "With crews aboard. The Meridian Star. The Coromandel. Half the losses in your ledgers weren't losses at all — they were murders with insurance payouts attached. Wren knew. The manifests prove it."

"They do," Halloran agreed pleasantly. "Which is why they're coming out of that hold and going into a furnace, and why everyone who has seen them is standing on this deck in the rain. But here's the offer." He stepped closer, and the floodlight caught the silver in his hair and nothing human in his eyes. "The gold. A full share — call it ten percent, call it more, I don't haggle with dead men's promises. A new name, papers, a house somewhere the monsoon never reaches. And the navy's protection — the same protection that sealed your service record, that buried Thursday Island, that has kept Edmund Halloran untouchable for twenty years. You've seen what happens to men who cross me, Kavanagh. Your friend Wren did. The crew of the Meridian did. Walk away rich and protected, or stay and join them. There is no third door."

For a moment — just a moment — Bram let himself feel the weight of it. Dry land. A life with no harbor to distrust. Mara safe. Pili in school. Nell's hull patched with new money instead of prayer.

Then he thought of Elias Wren bleeding out against a tavern wall, pressing an oilskin map into a stranger's hands because there was no one else left to trust. *See it found, or see it destroyed.* Not: get rich. Not: get even. *See it found.*

"There's a third door," Bram said quietly. "You just can't afford to look at it."

Halloran sighed, genuinely regretful. "Take them to the rail, then. Let them watch the lift come up. It's educational."

* * *

They put them at the starboard rail under two rifles. Below, the floodlit water boiled around the crane's hook, and divers' bubbles broke the surface in silver strings. Forty minutes. Maybe less.

Mara's shoulder pressed against Bram's. Her lips barely moved. "Radio room. Port side of the wheelhouse. One operator, headphones on. The transmitter's keyed to the mast antenna — I saw it when they walked us past."

"I saw it too."

"Bram." Her hand found his behind the rail, hidden from the guards. "Whatever you're planning — the charges are on a timer. Twenty minutes. If you go into that water and something goes wrong—"

"Nothing's going wrong."

"Something always goes wrong. That's the sea." She turned her head, and rain ran off her chin, and she was smiling — fierce and unafraid. "So we do it anyway. Together. Say it."

"Together," he said.

She slipped her hand free and moved — casual, unhurried, a woman walking to the rail to be sick — until she stood beside the younger of the two guards, and then her elbow went into his throat and his rifle went over the side and Mara Voss, wanted in three jurisdictions, was running for the wheelhouse with the rain covering her like a blessing.

The second guard turned. Bram hit him once, hard, and followed her.

* * *

The radio room door burst open under Bram's boot. The operator came up out of his chair reaching for a pistol on the desk; Mara got there first, swept the pistol away, and shoved the man bodily into a locker, slamming the latch. Then she dropped into the chair, tore off her sodden jacket, and started pulling the waterproof case open with shaking fingers.

"Give me two minutes," she said. "Every port authority from Brisbane to Singapore. Every newsroom with a shortwave desk. Reuters, AAP, the lot. Precedence priority, weather emergency cover — nobody questions traffic during a monsoon." Her hands flew over the key. "Go. Get your timer started. I'll keep this room."

Bram hesitated half a heartbeat — long enough to see her set her jaw, long enough to understand that this was who she had become out here, someone who kept rooms and held lines and did not need saving — and then he was gone, down the aft ladder into the rain, toward the crane and the open water beyond it.

Halloran's voice cracked across the barge's PA system, furious now, the boardroom polish burned away: *"Stop them. Stop the diver. Nothing leaves that wreck but what I say leaves it."*

Rifles opened up along the rail, muzzle flashes strobing through the rain. Bram ran the gunwale low and fast, the way he'd run decks under fire a lifetime ago, and dove off the stern into black water.

* * *

Down was silence.

His torch cut a cone through the murk, and the Meridian Star rose to meet him out of the dark — her plates furred with coral now, her superstructure collapsed into itself, a cathedral of rust and bone. His charge lines were where he'd left them on the last dive, taped along the bulkhead above the bullion hold, wired back to the main pack at the hold's mouth. Above them, rigged separately, sat the evidence vault — the strongroom where the manifests had lived for thirty years, where the copies in Mara's hands had been born.

Not to steal it. To bury it. Hold onto vault, vault onto seabed, seabed over everything — a tomb no dredge would find and no lawyer could subpoena. Wren's bargain, kept the only way it could be kept.

Bram checked the wires. Checked them again. His hands were steady. Eleven years of running, and here at the end of it his hands were finally, perfectly steady.

Through the water, muffled, he heard the crane stop. Heard the PA garble. Heard, distantly, the first alarm bell.

They knew. They'd send divers down. Minutes, maybe less.

He armed the pack. Twenty minutes on the clock — no. He spun the dial down. Ten. The storm would cover the sound topside; the collapse would cover everything else. He pressed the last connection home, kissed two fingers and touched them to the cold steel of the strongroom door — *for Wren, for the crews, for the ones whose names were redacted* — and swam for the surface with the monsoon roaring overhead like the whole sky coming down.

* * *

He surfaced in the lee of the Pelican, and Pili's arm hauled him over the gunwale before he'd finished coughing.

"Timer?" she shouted.

"Ten minutes! Get us clear!"

Nell had the engine already thundering. The Pelican wallowed away from the barge through rain like falling glass, and behind them the floodlights still blazed, the crane still swung, and figures on deck ran in circles like ants whose hill had been kicked. Ahead, the storm's tail waited — black water, black sky, and somewhere in it the only road home.

In the wheelhouse doorway stood Mara, soaked, hoarse, holding a printout flapping in the wind.

"It's gone out," she shouted over the engines. "All of it. Brisbane acknowledged. Sydney acknowledged. Singapore's asking for confirmation — they'll have it in an hour when the wire services wake up. Bram—" She grabbed his arm, and her eyes were blazing, and she was laughing and crying at once. "It's done. It's *out*. There's no calling it back. Every port in the Pacific is going to read those manifests by morning."

Bram pulled her against him, rain and salt and diesel between them, and looked back once at the shrinking lights of the barge.

"Halloran doesn't know yet," he said.

"He will."

"He'll come at us with everything he has left. A drowning man grabs at whatever floats." He turned forward, into the storm, into the dark. "Ten minutes, Nell. Then we'll see what the sea gives back."

Behind them, right on the clock, the horizon flashed white — a silent bloom of spray rising off the water where the Meridian Star lay — and then the thunder of it rolled over the Pelican like a verdict, and Edmund Halloran's empire began, in daylight and in front of witnesses, to drown.

Nell spat over the side. "Now we run," she said, and put the wheel hard over.
