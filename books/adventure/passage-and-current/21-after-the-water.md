## 21. After the Water

The news came in on a borrowed radio, crackling through the static like a voice rising from a drowned ship.

"—Halloran Shipping shares suspended on the Sydney exchange this morning after leaked manifests—"

Mara stood in the wheelhouse of the Pelican with a mug of tea going cold in her hands and listened to the world rearrange itself. Outside, Cairns harbour lay flat and glittering under a washed sky, the monsoon gone north to spend its fury on empty water. Gulls argued over the fish market. A ferry chugged past trailing diesel smoke. Everything ordinary. Everything changed.

"—authorities have confirmed the documents are authentic. Edmund Halloran was taken from his Point Piper residence at six a.m. and is assisting police with inquiries into the deaths of no fewer than eleven crewmen aboard vessels scuttled between 1968 and—"

Eleven. She closed her eyes. Eleven names that had been cargo ledgers once, line items, write-offs. Eleven families who had been told their men were lost to weather.

Behind her, Nell Okonkwo shifted in the bunk they'd rigged for her in the wheelhouse, because Nell had refused — absolutely, profanely refused — to be carried ashore to a hospital while there was still a radio to listen to. The bullet had gone through her shoulder and out the back, missing everything that mattered by what the doctor in Thursday Island called "a sailor's luck and no more." She'd been stitched, splinted, and stuffed full of antibiotics, and she'd spent three days glaring at the ceiling of a ward before demanding a boat ride home.

"Turn it up," Nell rasped. "I want to hear them say his name."

The announcer said it. *Edmund Halloran.* Said it twice more, in two different sentences, each time stripped of the deference that had always wrapped it before. Mara realized she'd been holding her breath and let it go slowly.

"There," Nell said softly. "That's for Elias Wren. And for every poor devil who went down with his ships and got called an act of God."

* * *

The warrants dissolved the way ice melts — not all at once, but inevitably, from the edges inward.

Mara watched it happen from the inside, which was stranger than watching it happen from outside would have been. A solicitor retained by the newspaper that first ran the manifests contacted her through the harbormaster's office, polite and careful, asking if she would confirm her role in obtaining the documents. She thought about it for a long time, sitting on the Pelican's rail with her feet dangling over water the color of hammered tin.

In the end she confirmed everything. Her name, her position, her original assignment from Aldous Crane, the night she'd burned her report in the galley sink while Bram stood watch at the companionway. The solicitor had gone very quiet on the line, and then he'd said, "Ms. Voss, you should know that the federal police have opened a file on Mr. Crane. Your statement would be... material."

"Then they can have it," she said. "All of it. I'm done deciding what the truth can afford."

Crane was arrested nine days later, trying to board a flight to Singapore under a passport that wasn't his. She read about it in a week-old paper Pili brought back from town, folded to the relevant column, held out wordlessly with an expression that managed to be both solemn and delighted. *Insurance executive charged with obstruction, conspiracy, and accessory to murder in the Halloran matter.* There was a photograph of him being walked to a car, one hand raised against the cameras, and in the photograph he looked smaller than she remembered. Older. Like a man who had spent thirty years building a wall and only now discovered it was made of paper.

She felt nothing when she saw it. That surprised her most. She'd expected rage, or grief, or at least the sour satisfaction of vindication. What she felt instead was the particular lightness of a debt discharged — not forgiven, because some things couldn't be forgiven, but paid. He had taught her the trade. He had also aimed her at an innocent man like a weapon and asked her not to look down the sights. Both things were true, and both things were finished, and she set the paper down and went to find Bram.

He was in the engine room with Tui.

That was its own kind of wound, and she'd learned to move carefully around it. Tui hadn't left the Pelican since the night of the boarding — hadn't gone ashore except for parts, hadn't eaten with the rest of them more than twice, took his meals standing at the workbench with his back to the room. He'd confessed everything the same night, kneeling in Nell's blood with Halloran's men still shouting on deck: the debts in Apia, the money wired to his sister's creditors, the coordinates passed in a moment of weakness that had cost Nell her shoulder and nearly cost her life. He'd turned on them the instant he understood, and he'd taken a rifle butt across the face for it, and none of that had bought him absolution from the only judge whose verdict he cared about.

Nell had forgiven him in four words — "Get back to work" — delivered from the stretcher with her good hand gripping his wrist. But Tui hadn't forgiven himself, and so he worked. He worked like a man bailing a boat that would never quite float, replacing stringers, re-caulking seams, rebuilding the engine that had nearly died in the storm's tail with a devotion that looked, from the outside, a great deal like penance.

Bram didn't preach at him. Mara loved him for that. Bram Kavanagh knew better than anyone alive what it was to carry a thing you couldn't put down, and he seemed to understand that the carrying was the point — that Tui needed the weight until the weight became something he could live inside rather than under.

She stood in the engine room doorway and watched the two of them bent over the main bearing, Bram's hands guiding Tui's, saying nothing, and thought: *this is what salvage actually is.* Not gold. Not bullion. This.

* * *

The navy's silence order collapsed more slowly, and less gracefully, than anything else.

It began with a journalist — a persistent woman from a Brisbane daily who had gotten hold of the phrase *Meridian Star* from the leaked manifests and started pulling threads. It turned out there were threads everywhere. A wreck site sealed by order in 1971. A salvage survey conducted and then buried. A diver's service record redacted so heavily it read like a ransom note. And beneath it all, the quiet arrangement by which certain naval officers had looked away from Halloran's ghost fleet for twenty years in exchange for — what? Favors. Information. The comfortable fiction that some wrecks were better left unexamined.

Mara gave the journalist three hours of interviews in a Cairns hotel room, speaking carefully but leaving nothing out. When the story ran, it ran above the fold, under a headline she read twice because she couldn't quite believe it: *THE NAVY'S DROWNED SECRET.*

Bram read it standing at the rail, and his face did something she'd never seen it do. It didn't soften, exactly. It *unclenched*. Like a fist opening finger by finger after years shut.

"They'll want statements," he said. "Hearings, maybe. They'll want me to stand up in some room in Canberra and say what happened off Thursday Island."

"Will you?"

He was quiet a long moment. A trawler went by, low and heavy-laden, and the Pelican rocked gently on its wake.

"Yeah," he said. "I think I finally can. Turns out it's easier to tell the truth when nobody's holding a silence order over your head." He glanced at her, and something wry moved through the seriousness. "You'd know about that."

"I would," she admitted. "Occupational hazard. Falling in with criminals."

"Divers," he corrected. "We prefer *divers*."

* * *

Pili came back from the chandlery with more than parts.

Mara found her on the foredeck that evening, cross-legged in the last of the light, surrounded by the wreckage of a cardboard box. Out of it had come: a case of brass fittings, two coils of rope, a textbook titled *Coastal Navigation and Seamanship, Second Edition*, and — this was the thing Pili kept touching, the way you'd touch a scar to check it was real — an application form. *Certificate of Competency, Deckhand Class.* Her name was already written on it in careful, school-taught cursive: *Amelia Okonkwo-Pili.*

She'd added the surname herself. Nobody had suggested it. Nobody needed to.

"She's studying," Nell reported later, from her bunk, with the gruffness that was as close as she ever came to open weeping. "Got the chart symbols down already. Asked me what a transit bearing was and then corrected my answer." A pause. "Kid's going to have my ticket inside two years, mark me. Then she'll be skipper and I can retire and drink rum in peace."

"You'll never retire," Mara said.

"No," Nell agreed comfortably. "But it's nice to lie about."

Outside, dusk came down over the harbor like a hand closing gently over a flame. Mara sat with Nell and listened to her talk — about the Pelican's history, about the charter trade, about a hundred small stories that filled in the bones of the family Mara had fallen into sideways, at gunpoint, running. Somewhere in the middle of it she realized she wasn't listening anymore. She was just *staying*. Sitting in the warm dark of a wheelhouse that smelled of diesel and salt and bandages, staying, without anywhere else to be and without wanting anywhere else to be.

For seven years she had been a woman in motion — cases closed, flights caught, doors opened onto other doors. She had mistaken movement for purpose the way some people mistake noise for music. Now the motion had stopped, and underneath it she could hear the actual tune: the slap of water on a hull, Tui's wrench ringing in the engine room, Pili's voice below decks reciting buoyage systems like a rosary.

It sounded like home. She hadn't known she'd been listening for it.

* * *

They poured the rum on a Tuesday.

Not for ceremony — Nell insisted it wasn't ceremony, it was just what you did — but everyone came anyway, and everyone dressed as though it were church. Even Tui, scrubbed and combed, his bruise faded to a yellow shadow. Even Pili, wearing the one clean shirt she owned and looking terrified of it.

They anchored over the Meridian Star's grave at slack water, in that impossible green-gold light the reef makes when the sun gets low, and Nell stood at the rail — standing now, though she leaned hard on Bram's arm — and said the words. Short ones. Names where she had them. Elias Wren. The eleven from the manifests. The ones from the navy wreck whose names nobody would ever print, spoken anyway, into the wind, because somebody ought to say them even if the sea was the only witness.

Then Bram uncorked the bottle and poured it out in a long amber ribbon, and the current took it, and the water closed over it without comment, the way water does.

Mara watched Bram's face during the pouring. He wasn't grieving anymore — or rather, he was, but the grief had changed density, settled like sediment, become part of the seabed of him instead of the storm above it. When the bottle was empty he held it a moment, then handed it to Pili. "Keep that," he said. "First thing in your sea chest. Every diver needs one empty bottle and one full promise."

"What's the promise?" Pili asked.

"That you'll come up," he said. "Every dive. No matter what you find down there. You come up."

* * *

Afterward, with the anchor watch set and the others gone below, Mara climbed to the quarterdeck and found Bram already there, leaning on the rail, looking south toward the open water.

She stood beside him. For a while neither of them spoke. Below them the hull breathed against the swell; overhead the first stars were coming out over the reef, sharp and unhurried.

"I got a letter today," she said finally. "From the insurance board. My old certification's been reinstated. They're offering me a position. Investigations, Sydney office. Full authority, public mandate — everything I was doing illegally, only legal this time." She turned the letter over in her hands without opening it further. "It's a good offer. It's the job I wanted my whole life, basically. The honest version of it."

"And?"

"And I keep thinking about what investigation actually means. Sitting in offices. Reading other people's losses in triplicate. Flying home every second Friday to an apartment where nobody knows what kind of tea I drink." She laughed, quietly, at herself. "I spent three months learning to splice line and gut fish and sleep in a bunk that smells like diesel, and do you know what I discovered? I'm good at it. All of it. Better than I ever was at paperwork."

Bram said nothing. He was very good, she'd learned, at letting people arrive at their own landings.

"The Pelican needs an investigator," she went on, before her nerve failed her. "Half the salvage claims in this ocean are frauds, and Nell's too honest to profit from it and too smart not to notice. Somebody has to read the wrecks before you dive them. Somebody has to know which harbors are crooked and which charters are traps." She made herself look at him. "I'm asking if there's a berth. Officially. Not running, not hiding. Just — aboard."

He turned then, and in the starlight his face was open in a way it had never been in Port Moresby, or Cairns, or any of the careful lying places between. Whatever walls he'd built after the navy wreck, after Wren, after years of trusting no harbor — they weren't down, exactly. But the gates were open, and he was standing in them.

"Nell already cut a berth tag for you," he said. "Three weeks ago. It's hanging by the chart table. She just didn't want to presume."

Mara laughed — really laughed, the sound going out over the water startling a gull off the rigging — and then, because the moment deserved better than laughter alone, she stepped into him and kissed him, and the quarterdeck tilted very slightly, or perhaps that was the swell, or perhaps it was neither.

When they broke apart, his forehead resting against hers, he said, "There's one more thing. Before you sign on permanent."

"Mm?"

"Wren's map. The original. I've been thinking about what to do with it." He reached into his jacket and drew out the oilskin packet, soft with handling, and held it between them. "The Meridian's found. The evidence is either public or buried where it belongs. This is just coordinates now. Paper." His thumb moved over the oilskin. "I could burn it. Close the whole account."

"But?"

"But Pili's studying navigation," he said. "And there are other wrecks out there. Honest ones. Ships that went down to weather and bad luck, carrying cargo that belongs to somebody's grandchildren." A slow smile, the first easy one she'd ever seen on him. "And I know a diver who trusts exactly one investigator to tell her which ones are worth the risk."

Mara looked at the map — at the dying man's handwriting, the promise that had dragged them both through storm and gunfire and the bottom of the sea — and then at the man holding it, and understood that the bargain hadn't ended. It had only changed hands. Changed shape. Become, at last, what it was always meant to be: not a debt, but a heading.

"Keep it," she said. "We'll need somewhere to sail."

Below them, faint through the deck planks, came the sound of Pili reciting chart symbols, Tui's wrench keeping time, Nell's voice telling both of them to pipe down — the ordinary music of a ship at rest, a family at anchor, a passage finally earned.

Bram folded the map away, and together they stood the evening watch, with nothing left to run from and the whole of the current ahead.
