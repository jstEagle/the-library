## 22. Passage and Current

The reef off Wreck Bay had a name on no chart, just a number in Nell's logbook and a reputation among the fishermen of Thursday Island as a place where the coral grew thick enough to hide a truck. The *Pelican* sat over it now, riding a slack tide, her new paint catching the morning sun — a proper job this time, done in Cairns by men who charged honest rates, the hull patched where Tui's welding had held her together through the monsoon and would hold her together until someone with more patience than money got around to replacing plates outright.

Bram came up the ladder with a bag of oyster shells in one hand and his mask in the other, and stood dripping on the deck while Pili brought the trawler around in a slow, careful arc.

"Port side down another fathom," he called. "You're drifting off the bed."

"I'm not drifting," Pili said, not turning from the wheel. "I'm holding station. There's a difference."

"There is," Bram agreed. "And you're doing it."

She grinned without looking at him. Sixteen now, or seventeen — she'd stopped being precise about it somewhere between Cairns and the lagoon, the way kids do when they've decided their age is nobody's business but their own. She wore the wheel like she'd been born to it, feet planted, hips loose, eyes flicking from compass to water to compass again. On the chart table behind her lay her coastal ticket study guide, swollen with saltwater and annotated in three colors of pencil. She'd take the exam in Townsville in the spring. Nell had already told the examiner, in writing, that if he failed her he'd have to explain to Nell Okonkwo why, and the examiner had reportedly gone pale before she'd finished the sentence.

The salvage below was nothing dramatic. A pearling lugger gone down in '61, cargo of shell still in her hold, owner dead and heirs scattered and the insurance long since paid out. Honest work: raise what was worth raising, leave the bones for the coral. No manifests chained beside anything. No serial numbers stamped into steel that made Bram's chest go tight when he saw them. Just shell, and silt, and the slow patient work of bringing things back into the light.

He'd learned to love work like that. It had taken him most of a year.

* * *

They poured the rum at noon.

It was Nell who kept the calendar — she kept everything, in that ledger mind of hers, every debt and every date — and she came up from the galley carrying the bottle they'd bought in Port Moresby eight months ago and hadn't touched since. Dark rum, cheap, the kind Elias Wren would have drunk and complained about and drunk anyway.

"Anniversary," she said simply, and set it on the deckhouse roof.

Nobody needed it explained. They gathered the way families gather for such things — loosely, without ceremony, each of them finding their place as if they'd rehearsed it. Mara came up from the forepeak where she'd been re-stowing the dive gear, hair still damp from a swim. Tui appeared from the engine room, wiping his hands on a rag he never seemed to put down, and stood a little apart, the way he always did at moments like this, as if he wasn't sure yet whether grief was something he was allowed to share or something he still owed. Pili killed the engine and let the *Pelican* drift, and the silence that filled the space where the diesel had been was enormous and clean.

Bram took the bottle.

He'd thought about what to say for weeks, and in the end he said almost none of it. Words felt like the wrong currency for a man like Wren, who had traded in coordinates and promises and died owing nothing but a debt he'd chosen well.

"He told me once," Bram said, "that gold doesn't rust but men do. He said it like it was funny." He worked the cork free. "I didn't laugh then. I do now."

He tipped the bottle over the rail. The rum went out in a dark ribbon, spreading and thinning, swallowed by the blue. Somewhere under them, forty fathoms down and half a mile east, the *Meridian Star* lay in the tomb they'd made for her — bullion hold collapsed onto the evidence vault, concrete and steel and silt sealing the manifests away forever, the whole grave of it invisible from above, indistinguishable from any other piece of drowned history. The navy had sent divers down twice in the first months, Bram knew. They'd come back up with nothing, because there was nothing left to find, and eventually the file had been closed with the word *inconclusive*, which was the closest thing to justice the sea ever gave a man in an official document.

Mara's copies had done the rest. Every port authority from Darwin to Suva. Every newsroom that would take the transmission. Edmund Halloran's empire had come apart the way rotten hulls come apart — slowly, then all at once, planks springing loose in a dozen harbors at once until there was nothing left to float. He was awaiting trial in Brisbane. Crane was already convicted, testifying against everyone he'd ever served in exchange for a sentence he might survive. The navy's silence order had dissolved under daylight like sugar in rain, and Bram's own name had surfaced from the redactions — not cleared, exactly, but *explained*, which after all these years felt like the same thing.

"To Elias Wren," Nell said.

"To Wren," they said, and Tui said it loudest, and Bram understood that too.

Pili poured a capful over the side herself, for reasons she didn't explain and nobody asked about — the girl had her own dead, her own debts, her own private ceremonies — and then the moment passed the way such moments do, dissolving into the ordinary business of a working day. Nell went back to the galley. Tui dropped into the engine room. Pili restarted the engine and brought them back over the wreck site, and the *Pelican* settled onto station with a sigh, and the sea went on being the sea.

But Bram stayed at the rail a while longer, and Mara came and stood beside him, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

* * *

"You could have taken it," she said finally. "The gold. Nobody would have known. Half the Pacific wouldn't have believed it existed."

"The other half would have," Bram said. "That's the problem with treasure. It only stays secret until somebody needs it not to be."

"And you don't regret it? Even now?"

He considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because he'd asked himself the same thing on quiet nights, lying in the bunk they shared, listening to the hull tick and breathe.

"When I was twenty-two I pulled a case off a wreck I wasn't supposed to touch," he said. "Serial numbers. Stamped steel. And I burned it, because the last crew who reported things like that got silenced, and I figured staying small and staying moving was the only way to stay alive." He watched the water slide past the hull. "I spent fifteen years running from that fire. And the thing is — burning the case didn't save anybody. It just saved me. This—" he nodded down, toward the tomb, toward the ribbon of rum already dispersed into the endless blue "—this saved somebody. Wren's granddaughter, maybe. Some crew on some ship Halloran hasn't scuttled yet. I don't know who. But it's the first time in my life I've done the thing right instead of the thing safe."

Mara leaned her shoulder into his. "For what it's worth," she said, "the investigator in me thinks you made the correct call."

"And the part of you that isn't an investigator?"

"She thinks you're standing too close to the rail and should come have lunch."

He laughed — really laughed, the kind that came up from somewhere below the ribs, rare as fair weather in the monsoon season — and let her pull him away from the edge.

* * *

The afternoon passed in the rhythm that had become their life. Tui ran the winch while Bram made two more dives, bringing up baskets of pearl shell crusted white with fourteen years of growth. Mara worked the sorting table, knocking the shells clean, setting aside the nacre that would pay for fuel and food and Pili's exam fees and the new propeller shaft Tui had been hinting at since Cairns. Nell supervised all of it from the deckhouse with a mug of tea and the expression of a woman who had waited years to run a boat honestly and intended to enjoy every minute of it.

At one point Pili called down from the helm, casual as anything: "Bram. There's a boat coming. Small. Out of Thursday Island, I think."

His hands went still on the basket. Old reflexes. They'd fade someday, maybe, but not yet.

Nell had the glasses up before he did. "Fisherman," she said. "Alone. Flying nothing." A pause. "He's showing us his catch. That's all this is."

It was. The man waved as he passed, held up a string of mackerel in salute, and was gone south toward the island. Bram exhaled slowly and found Mara watching him with an expression that was equal parts sympathy and amusement.

"It gets easier," she said.

"Does it?"

"No idea. Ask me in another year." She bumped his hip with hers. "But you're not checking the horizon every ninety seconds anymore. I've been counting. You're down to maybe every ten minutes."

"That obvious?"

"To me. I love you. I notice these things."

She said it easily now, the way she said most true things, and it still landed in him like sunlight through water. He'd almost told her about the navy wreck in the lagoon, all those months ago, and stopped himself — and then there had come a night in the eye of the storm when there was nothing left between them but truth, and he'd told her all of it, the case and the fire and the silenced crew and the fifteen years of motion, and she had listened without flinching and then said, *well, you're stuck with me now, so you'll have to stop*. And he was. Stuck with her. Happily, permanently, in the way of people who had both learned that the world takes things from you without warning and therefore held on tight to what it gave back.

* * *

They anchored that night in the lee of the reef, and after supper Bram climbed to the bow alone, as he sometimes did, and sat with his back against the windlass and watched the stars come out over the Coral Sea.

This was the hour that used to be hardest. For fifteen years, dusk had meant decisions: which harbor next, which name on which paper, how far to stay ahead of whatever was behind. He had worn out passports and friendships and one marriage, all in service of the simple animal project of not stopping. A moving target, he used to tell himself. Keep moving and you keep living.

But the last crew who stayed got silenced, and the man who kept moving got hollowed out, and it had taken a dying prospector and a leaking trawler and a woman with forged survey papers to teach him the difference between hiding and living.

He heard footsteps on the deck and didn't turn. He knew the weight of everyone aboard now — Tui's rolling gait, Pili's quick light ones, Nell's deliberate tread, Mara's, which he knew better than his own heartbeat.

"Pili's asleep over her charts again," Mara said, settling beside him. "Third night running. Nell covered her with a blanket and pretended to be annoyed about it."

"She'll pass her ticket."

"She'll pass it top of the class, and then she'll captain something bigger than this old tub inside five years, and Nell will pretend to be furious and be prouder than she's ever been in her life." Mara leaned into him. "What are you thinking about?"

"Wren," Bram said. "And the fact that I can't remember the last time I looked at a harbor and started calculating exits."

She was quiet a moment. "Is that frightening?"

He turned the question over the way he'd turn a shell in his hand, feeling its weight, its shape.

"A year ago it would have been," he said. "Now it feels like putting down a load I forgot I was carrying." He looked at her, at the dark outline of the deckhouse where Nell was making her final rounds, at the faint glow of the chart table lamp where Pili slept among her penciled courses. "I spent my whole life thinking salvage meant pulling valuable things out of the water. Turns out that's just the job description. The actual work is different."

"What is it?"

"Deciding what's worth keeping," he said. "And then holding on."

Out over the reef, the moon was rising, laying a silver road across the water — the same sea that had taken the *Meridian Star* and given back a family; the same current that had carried a dying man's map into a tavern and delivered it, by ways no one could have plotted, here. Somewhere beneath that silver, the wreck they didn't name rested at last in the dark, her secrets sealed, her debt paid, her dead — Wren among them now, in whatever way the sea keeps its own — finally allowed to sleep.

Bram watched the moonlight on the water and did not calculate anything at all.

Tomorrow they'd raise more shell. Next week, Townsville, and Pili's exam, and a new propeller shaft, and whatever honest trouble the Coral Sea cared to offer. The *Pelican* rocked gently at anchor, patched and painted and paid-for, riding easy on her moorings like a creature that had finally found its depth.

He stayed on the bow a long time, Mara warm against his side, and when at last he went below, he slept without dreaming of wrecks — and woke, for the first time in fifteen years, without wanting to be anywhere else but exactly where he was.
