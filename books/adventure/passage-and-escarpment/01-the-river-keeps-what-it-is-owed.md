## 1. The River Keeps What It Is Owed

The Kestrel had spent the winter eating at its own banks, and by the first week of thaw it was spitting them back out again—mud, fence posts, half a sheep, and once, memorably, an entire outhouse that Odile claimed on sight as salvage. Jonah Iversen stood on the trestle at Mile Forty-Seven with her notebook open against the wind and watched the water run brown and muscular beneath the pilings, and she thought, as she thought every spring, that rivers were honest in a way people were not. They took what they took. They gave back what they gave back. There was no pretending otherwise.

"Third piling from the north end," Odile called up from the bank, cupping her hands around her mouth. "You want to come look at this before I send Tam down."

Jonah closed the notebook and went down the ladder two rungs at a time.

The third piling had taken a beating—the flood had scoured around its footing, leaving it standing in a bowl of churned gravel like a man in a hole up to his knees. Not fatal. Not yet. But the surveyor's eye in Jonah saw the lean of it, the way the water curled greedily around the base instead of sliding past, and she knew they'd be shoring it before the first freight came through in three weeks. She said so, and Odile grunted, which from Odile was agreement.

"It's not the piling I wanted you to see," Odile said. "It's what Tam found in the scour."

Tam was crouched at the edge of the gravel bar, dripping to the elbows, looking like a boy who had hooked something too big for his line. He was twenty-two and built like a fence post and he grinned at Jonah with the uncomplicated joy of a man who has never once wondered whether he is who he says he is.

"You're going to want the crane," he said.

What they'd dredged up, snagged in the roots of a fallen alder forty yards downstream, was an iron chest. It took four of them and a block-and-tackle rigged off the winch truck to get it onto solid ground, and even then it sat in the mud like something that intended to stay there. It was perhaps three feet long, banded in iron gone the orange-black of old blood, its wood swollen nearly black with ten years or more of river. A chain had been wrapped twice around it and padlocked through itself, and the lock had rusted into a single piece, so that chest and chain were now one object, indivisible, like a fact and its consequences.

Jonah might have logged it as salvage and thought no more about it. Crews pulled strange things out of the Kestrel every spring; the river kept a museum nobody visited. But then Tam, impatient, took a wire brush to the lid to see if there was a maker's mark, and the mud came away in sheets, and Jonah stopped breathing.

Stamped into the iron lid, deep enough to have survived the flood, the silt, the years, was a seal: a mountain split by a road, crowned with seven stars, ringed by letters in a script she had not seen since she was nineteen years old and burning papers one by one in a stove while her mother wept in the next room.

*Vessany.*

The word arrived in her mind without permission, the way a name does when you've trained yourself never to say it. Vessany, the Grand Concession of, dissolved eleven years ago in a season of tribunals and border redrawings, its rail charters voided, its ministries scattered, its maps reprinted with the country simply *gone*, the way a tooth goes from the mouth—you keep finding the space where it was.

She knew the seal because her father had stamped it on his letters. He'd had a brass stamp, worn smooth at the handle, and he would press it into the wax with a kind of ceremony that had embarrassed her as a girl, as though his correspondence were state business. *From your father, servant of the mountain and the road.* She could still feel the wax cooling under her thumb when she broke the seals open. She could still smell the stove, the paper curling, her mother saying *not the photographs, Jonah, leave me the photographs.*

"Ma'am?" Tam said. "You know it?"

Jonah realized she had been silent long enough to be strange. The wind moved the alder branches along the bank. Somewhere upstream the dredge crew was laughing about the outhouse.

"Old freight," she said. Her voice came out level, and she was proud of that, proud the way a drowning woman is proud of touching bottom. "Pre-war. Probably washed out of some warehouse collapse upstream. Log it, tag it, put it in the shed."

"Should we open it?"

"No." Too fast. She made herself shrug. "Company property rules. Anything sealed gets opened in front of the section chief, not on a riverbank. You know that."

Tam didn't know that, actually—there was no such rule—but he was twenty-two and the crane needed moving and Odile was already shouting about the piling, and the moment passed the way moments do, gratefully, irretrievably.

* * *

That night the camp at Mile Forty-Seven smelled of woodsmoke and wet canvas, and Jonah lay in her bunk listening to the river and doing arithmetic she hated.

Three years. Three years since she'd stepped off a freight car at Corven Junction with a forged journeyman's card and a surveyor's kit bought with the last of her mother's jewelry, and signed her name—not her name, a name, *J. Iversen*, common as gravel—and let the railway swallow her. Three years of measuring life in miles: the miles of line surveyed, the miles between postings, the miles she told herself put distance between her and everything she had been. Miles were clean. Miles could be counted, verified, entered in a ledger. Years were treacherous. Years accumulated. Years asked questions like *who are you now* and *what did your father do* and *why are you still running from men who are almost certainly dead.*

Her father had been a concessions clerk. That was the true and terrible shape of it—a clerk, a penman, a man with good handwriting and a brass stamp, who had spent the last decade of Vessany's existence making documents for men whose names Jonah had only ever heard whispered. When the tribunals came, the clerks did not get trials. They got lists. And the families of the clerks got the same list, one line lower.

So: miles. And now the river, which keeps what it is owed, had reached three years up the line and handed her back a piece of the country that no longer existed, stamped with her father's seal, and set it in a shed forty feet from her bunk.

She got up. She told herself she was checking the lanterns, which was even true.

* * *

The equipment shed stood apart from the sleeping tents, a long low building of rough-sawn planks that smelled of creosote and cold iron. Jonah let herself in with the key she wasn't supposed to have and lit the lamp and stood in front of the chest for a long time without touching it.

It looked smaller indoors. Things dragged out of rivers usually did—they lost the drama of the water and became just objects, just weight and volume. But the seal stared up at her from the lid, seven stars over the split mountain, and she thought: *someone chained this shut before it went into the water.* People don't chain things shut to throw them away. People chain things shut to make sure they sink, or to make sure that whoever finds them understands they were meant to stay shut.

Or to make sure they arrive.

The thought came unbidden and unwelcome: couriers chained strongboxes. Her father's world had been full of strongboxes—she remembered the leather cases, the wax, the men who came to the house at odd hours and left with their collars up. If this chest had been *in transit* when it went into the Kestrel—if it had been on the ferry at Wrenlow Crossing, the one that went down in the ice breakup eight years ago, the disaster everyone in the valley still measured time by (*before the ferry, after the ferry*)—then somewhere, someone had been waiting for it. Had maybe still been waiting. Eight years was a long time to wait for a drowned box, but Jonah had learned that certain kinds of patience outlasted nations.

She should burn it. The thought arrived fully formed, practical, almost gentle. Drag it to the slag heap, crack the lock, feed it to the boiler, log it as unsalvageable. Whatever was inside belonged to a dead country and a dead life, and the living ones—Odile, Tam, the thirty-one souls of the Mile Forty-Seven crew, her own borrowed name—were better off with it ash.

Her hand was on the lid. The iron was cold and faintly gritty, river-touched.

But she was a surveyor. Her whole trade was the refusal to guess when you could measure, the discipline of opening the instrument and looking. And underneath the fear, in the place she kept locked even from herself, curiosity had already curdled into something worse: recognition. Because her father's stamp had been pressed hard into that wax, always, harder than necessary, and the stamp on this lid had been pressed hard too, and some stupid animal part of her had already decided it was his work, his hand, his iron, and she could not burn her father's hand without knowing what it carried.

"Not tonight," she said aloud, to the shed, to herself.

She turned down the lamp. And that was when she heard the footsteps outside—two sets, unhurried, coming up the track from the direction of the junction, though no train was due until Thursday and no one walked nine miles of muddy grade for nothing.

A knock. Then Odile's voice, carefully neutral in the way it got when Odile was watching someone: "Iversen. There's a gentleman here asking after the dredging crew. Says he's a courier." A pause, the length of a held breath. "Says he lost something in the river."

Jonah stood in the dark beside the chest, her heart going like a signal lamp, and reached for the door.
