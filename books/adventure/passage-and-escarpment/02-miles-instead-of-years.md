## 2. Miles Instead of Years

The chest sat in the survey tent like a guest no one had invited, and Jonah Iversen found she could not stop looking at it.

She had tried, in the three days since the dredge brought it up. She had thrown herself into the bridge inspection with a ferocity that made the younger crewmen exchange glances—re-torquing bolts already sound, re-sounding pilings that had answered clean the first time, walking the trestle twice in an afternoon when once was company policy and twice was compulsion. The Kestrel River had dropped back into its banks, brown and patient, and the bridge stood, and the work was done, and still her eyes kept returning to the tent flap, behind which the iron box waited with its stamped crest half-scoured by a decade of silt.

*Vessany.*

Even thinking the word felt like stepping off a platform edge. She did it anyway, the way she always did, testing the drop.

Her father's letters had come twice a year, then once, then not at all. They were written in a small, upright hand on paper that smelled faintly of pipe smoke and sealing wax, and every one of them carried the same device at the top: a double-headed eagle standing on a mountain, wings spread over a river fork. The seal of the Chancellery of Vessany. As a girl she had traced it with a fingertip until the ink ghosted onto her skin, and her father had laughed and said, *Careful, little wren. That bird marks everything it touches.* She had thought he meant it as a joke about smudges.

She knew better now.

Jonah left the tent at dusk and walked the line east, past the last switch, past the ballast shoulder where the gravel gave way to bunchgrass, out to the mile marker they'd set that morning. Mile 214 from the coast terminus. She stood beside it while the light went copper and then gray, and she did what she had done every night for three years: she counted.

Not years. Never years. Years required a self to measure them against, a continuous person accumulating time like debt. Miles were cleaner. Miles could be walked away from. In three years she had laid or surveyed or inspected one hundred and ninety-one miles of rail, and if you asked her how long she had been Jonah Iversen, she would have to think; but ask her how far she was from the ferry landing at Serevan, and she could tell you without pausing: six hundred and forty miles, give or take the meanders of the line.

Serevan. Where the chest had been loaded, most likely, before the ferry went down. Where the chest had been *going*.

She pressed her palm flat against the mile marker's cold face until her fingers ached, and let the memory come, because out here, alone, it always came.

* * *

She had been nineteen the winter Vessany died, and she had spent that winter believing her father was a clerk.

That was the family fiction, maintained with the same care her mother maintained the good china—brought out for occasions, polished, never examined too closely. Aldous Iversen, senior registrar in the Office of Concessions and Grants, a man who shuffled papers for the Chancellery and came home smelling of ink. It explained the long hours. It explained the sealed dispatches he sometimes carried. It explained, though nothing could truly explain it, why men in plain dark coats came to the house after midnight and spoke to him in the study with the door shut.

The collapse came not as a war but as an accounting. That was the thing no one outside understood: nations did not die of cannons, they died of ledgers. For eleven years the Chancellery had been selling what it did not own—mountain concessions, river rights, timber grants along the northern escarpment—signing away the same valleys two and three times to different syndicates, taking payment in gold and promises, until the promises came due simultaneously and there was nothing beneath them but paper. When the foreign creditors called their claims, Vessany had no answer. When the border provinces seceded rather than inherit the debt, the Chancellery had no army willing to defend it. Within fourteen months the eagle was struck from the maps, the borders redrawn by tribunal, and the country Jonah had been born in existed only in the memories of people who were scattering across three new nations like seed before wind.

And the papers—the forged permits, the duplicated grants, the paymaster's books that proved which hands had signed what—those had vanished. Officially burned in the Chancellery fire. Unofficially, everyone who understood such things assumed they had been carried out first, because documents like that were worth more than gold to anyone with a claim to press.

Jonah learned what her father was on a Tuesday, from a stranger.

The man came to the door in a railway coat—not a uniform, just a coat—and said he represented the Northern Extension Survey, and that they were hiring instrument-men and chainmen, no experience necessary, and that a young woman with her aptitude ought not waste herself in a dying city. He said it kindly. He said it looking slightly over her shoulder, at the house behind her, and she understood only later that he had been sent. That her father, in the last week of his life, had arranged his daughter's escape the way he arranged everything: on paper, through intermediaries, with a signature that would hold up under scrutiny.

He was arrested nine days later. The tribunals wanted the missing ledgers. Aldous Iversen either could not or would not say where they were, and the question stopped being asked when he died in custody—officially of fever, officially. Her mother followed within the year, not of grief exactly, but of the particular exhaustion of a woman who had spent thirty years polishing the good china for a household built on forgery.

Jonah signed her railway contract with her own name, because at nineteen she had not yet learned to be afraid of it. Then she learned. Then she became careful. A junior surveyor moved between crews so often that names blurred; she shortened hers, flattened her vowels, let the foremen assume she was from wherever they themselves were not. She took the jobs nobody wanted—flood inspections, washout surveys, lines through country so rough the company paid hazard rates—and she kept moving, and somewhere in the third year she noticed that she had stopped counting birthdays and started counting miles, and she understood that this was not thrift. It was amputation. You cannot lose what you refuse to keep track of.

The chest in the tent changed all of it. The chest said: *the past has a weight, and water is patient, and everything you buried floats up eventually.*

* * *

"You've re-inspected that bridge three times."

Odile Brannoc's voice came from behind her without ceremony, the way it always did. Jonah turned. The foreman stood with her hands jammed in the pockets of her canvas coat, gray-streaked hair escaping its braid, watching Jonah with the flat, unhurried attention she gave to suspect pilings.

"Four," Jonah said. "You missed one."

"I don't miss anything on my line." Odile came to stand beside her at the mile marker. Below them the river ran dark, carrying snowmelt down from the escarpment toward the coast, toward Serevan, toward everything Jonah had walked away from. "Three days ago you pulled a box out of that river, and since then you've been wound tighter than a hoist drum. You sleep in the survey tent. You've stopped eating with the crew."

"The chest is company property. I'm cataloguing it."

"You're guarding it." Odile said it without accusation, which was worse. "There's a difference, and I've watched enough people guard things to know it. So." She turned, and her eyes in the last light were steady and shrewd and not unkind. "Either you tell me what's rattling you, or you don't. But understand something, Iversen. I've run crews for twenty-two years. I know what a person looks like when they're running, because I've hired them, and fed them, and occasionally lied to inspectors for them. You run quieter than most. But you run."

The words landed in Jonah's chest like a spike driven home. She looked out at the water and said nothing, and the silence stretched, and Odile—mercifully, maddeningly—let it stretch.

"Whatever's in that box," the foreman said finally, turning back toward camp, "it came up out of the river for a reason. Water doesn't give things back for free." She paused at the edge of the lantern light. "Supper's at seven. There'll be a plate for you whether you eat it or not. That's how it works here—you don't have to want the plate. It's there."

She went, and Jonah stayed, and the first stars came out over the escarpment, black ridgeline against deepening blue, the pass she would have to cross someday visible even now as a pale notch in the rock.

A plate. Set for her, whether she ate or not. Three years she had worked beside these people, and she had never once let them do that—never let the plate be *for* her, always taken hers last, always eaten standing, always positioned herself at the edge of the firelight where the names couldn't stick. And she had told herself it was prudence. Caution. The discipline of a fugitive.

Standing alone at mile 214, Jonah admitted what it actually was. It was loneliness, chosen daily, maintained like any other piece of equipment. It was the cost of invisibility, paid out in small coin every single day—a plate refused, a story withheld, a birthday unmarked—and she had told herself the price was cheap because the alternative was the tribunal cell her father had died in. But the price was not cheap. The price was that when Odile said *you run*, no one else in the world had known it. Three years, one hundred and ninety-one miles, and not one living soul who could say what she was running from.

The chest knew. The chest had crossed the water and the years to find her, or someone like her, and tomorrow—she could feel it coming, the way you feel weather in old breaks—tomorrow someone would come asking about it.

She was right. She saw the dust first, far down the valley road, a rider moving fast toward the camp, and behind him, later, a second traveler on foot with a courier's stride.

Two of them. Coming for the box. Coming, she suspected, for whatever the box meant.

Jonah put her hand on the mile marker one last time, then turned toward the lanterns, toward the plate that would be waiting, toward the questions she could no longer outrun.
