## 3. A Man Who Claims the Water

The stranger came up the river road on foot, which was the first thing wrong with him.

Nobody walked the river road in April. The thaw had turned it to a brown ribbon of mud and meltwater, and anyone with business at the camp arrived by rail or by barge or not at all. But this man came walking out of the alder scrub in the gray light before breakfast, coat dark to the shoulders with spray, boots ruined past saving, and he stopped at the edge of the work yard as though he'd been standing there all night and had only just decided to be seen.

Jonah noticed him from the trestle, where she was checking the soundness of the third pier with a sounding hammer. The river ran high and loud beneath her, still carrying the winter's wreckage downstream—branches, fence rails, a drowned door that turned over and over like something trying to remember how to swim. She struck the pier twice, listened, and watched the man watch her.

He didn't ask for Odile. He didn't ask for the foreman's tent or the paymaster or anyone whose name would have told Jonah what he wanted. He stood with his hands loose at his sides and his face tipped up toward the bridge, and when their eyes met across forty yards of cold air, he said nothing at all. He simply waited, patient as the water, until she climbed down.

"You're the surveyor," he said. Not a question. His accent sat strangely on the words—not northern, not company-trained. Somewhere south and east, softened by years of travel. "Iversen."

"And you're wet," Jonah said.

"Elias Marchetti." He offered a hand. His palm was rough, a working man's hand, but there was ink on his fingers too, faded blue stains in the creases—the kind that came from handling cheap paper and cheaper carbon copies. "Courier. Independent line out of Corvane. I had a shipment on the *Merrilee* when she went down at the Narrows three weeks back."

Jonah kept her face still. The *Merrilee* was real; everyone within fifty miles had heard about the ferry breaking up against the ice run. "Condolences," she said. "You'll want the company office. Lost freight is their department."

"The freight wasn't theirs." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at one corner, and Jonah hated how immediately she registered that fact, the way a compass registers north without being asked. "It was mine. And I think your crew pulled it out of the river two days ago."

* * *

She made him say it plainly, sitting on an overturned crate outside the mess tent while the morning shift clattered around them, close enough to overhear and far enough to pretend they weren't. Elias Marchetti did not look at the tool shed where the chest sat under a tarp behind a padlocked door. He looked at Jonah instead, which was worse.

"An iron strongbox," he said. "Sealed. Stamped with the crest of Vessany."

The word went through her like the sounding hammer through bad timber. She heard the hollow note behind it. Three years she had gone without hearing that word spoken aloud in this valley, and now a stranger said it over a tin cup of Odile's terrible coffee as easily as he might say *Tuesday*.

"There's no crest on anything we dredged up," she said. "We pulled out fence posts and a dead mule."

"I know what I shipped." He drank his coffee without flinching at it. "Documents. Sealed under diplomatic courier seal from a government that doesn't exist anymore, carried by a man who technically doesn't exist either, bound for a tribunal that has been waiting ten years for them to arrive. The river took the ferry. Apparently the river also took its time giving the rest back."

"If such a chest existed," Jonah said carefully, "and if my crew had found it, protocol would be to report it to the railway company. Which owns these works. Which would then handle it."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger, exactly. Recalculation. "Would it," he said. "And is that what you've done?"

"That's my business."

"It is," he agreed, mildly, and that mildness frightened her more than any threat would have. "Miss Iversen. May I tell you why I walked sixty miles through the mud rather than telegraph ahead?"

"You're going to regardless."

He set down the cup. For a moment the easy warmth dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road. "Because whoever else comes asking about that chest will not be a courier. They'll come by rail, in a clean coat, with papers signed by men who never get mud on their boots. And when they leave, the chest will go with them, and so will every person who touched it, and the paperwork will show they were never here at all." He stood, unhurried. "I'm the honest version of trouble, Miss Iversen. It's the only kind I can offer. Think on it."

He left her the cup, empty, and walked out of camp the way he'd come in—and then, she noted, did not leave at all. He took a room at Callum's boarding house downriver, paid in coin, and was seen on the bluff that evening doing nothing more suspicious than watching the water.

* * *

Harrow arrived on the four o'clock train.

Jonah saw him first from the survey office window: a broad, neat man in a gray traveling coat stepping down onto the ballast with a leather case in one hand, pausing to speak to the conductor, laughing at something—a man who made himself agreeable as a professional discipline. She had met his type before, in another life, in another country. They were always polite. That was the whole horror of them.

He introduced himself at the foreman's tent as Mr. Harrow, claims and correspondence division, and apologized for arriving unannounced. "There was a ferry lost at the Narrows," he told Odile, who had summoned Jonah with a look that said *come listen*. "Company property aboard. We're conducting a recovery sweep along the banks. Routine."

"River gave most of it back already," Odile said. "Fence wire, planking, a dead mule."

"Mule," Harrow repeated, with a small smile. "Yes. We heard about the mule." His gaze traveled, unhurried, around the camp: the trestle, the tool shed, the dredge lines still coiled on the bank. It rested on Jonah last, and stayed. "You're the surveyor. Iversen. You found the mule, I understand."

"My crew found the mule."

"But you signed the salvage report." He produced a paper from his case, smooth as a card trick. "Itemized. Thorough work. Though I notice—" he tilted it toward the light, as if reading it fresh, "—no mention of iron."

"The river gives back what it wants," Jonah said. "It kept the rest."

For a moment neither of them blinked. Then Harrow folded the report away and thanked them both for their time, and asked, casually, whether the camp had many visitors this season, and whether any of them had been asking after freight. Odile said no. Jonah said nothing, and Harrow seemed to find her silence more interesting than an answer.

He took the last room at Callum's too.

* * *

That night Jonah couldn't sleep. She lay in her bunk listening to the river, which had not quieted since the flood, running high and dark past the pilings, carrying things nobody had claimed. Twice she got up. The second time she dressed fully, took the lamp, and let herself into the tool shed, because the chest was there and she needed to stand near it the way you stand near a grave to confirm it hasn't opened.

The padlock was untouched. The tarp was dry. She knelt anyway, put her palm flat on the cold iron, and felt the raised ridge of the crest under her hand: the double mountain of Vessany, the crossed keys beneath it. Her father had pressed that same seal into wax on letters she'd burned one by one, six years ago, reading each by candlelight until the words were part of her and then feeding them to the flame.

A floorboard creaked behind her.

She turned with the lamp raised, and Elias Marchetti stood in the doorway with his hands visible and empty, rain-wet again, looking less like a trespasser than a man arriving somewhere he'd been expected.

"How did you get in here?" she whispered.

"Callum's back step sticks. So does yours." He nodded at the chest. "I'm not going to touch it. I just needed to see that it was still here, and that you were still the one keeping it."

"You don't know anything about me."

"No," he agreed quietly. "But I know that seal. I've been carrying it, or carrying news of it, for half my life. And today a man from the railway asked me, very politely, over dinner, whether I'd ever handled diplomatic freight." His jaw tightened. "Harrow knows I'm here. He knew before I reached your camp. Which means someone told him the chest surfaced before I even knew it myself—and that, Miss Iversen, is the part that should worry you. Because I was the only one who was supposed to know it existed."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the river went on saying the only thing it ever said, over and over, taking and taking and taking.

"Then we're both marks," Jonah said at last.

"Or bait," Elias said. "For each other." He held her gaze in the low light, and she could not read him—ally, mark, trap, and no instrument she owned that could sound the difference. "Ask me which tomorrow. Ask me anything. Just don't decide tonight, and don't decide alone."

He left the way he'd come. Jonah stood a long time with her hand on the cold iron, feeling the crest bite gently into her palm, and thought: *the river keeps what it is owed.* She had believed, for three years, that she had paid everything. Now two men had arrived in a single day—one offering truth, one offering courtesy—and the water between them was rising fast, and somewhere in the dark it was already carrying her name downstream.
