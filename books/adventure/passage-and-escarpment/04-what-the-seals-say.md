## 4. What the Seals Say

Jonah waited until the camp slept.

She had learned the discipline of waiting in another country, under another name, and it had never left her: the way a body learns to be still while the world moves on without it. Now she lay in her bunk listening to the ordinary music of the yard—the settling of timber, the last cough of the cookfire, Odile's slow breathing through the canvas wall—and she counted the hours the way she counted everything, in distances that could not betray her. Four miles to the tool shed where the chest was locked. Six miles to the river bend. A hundred and forty miles, if she were foolish enough to run, to the nearest border station.

She was tired of running. She just hadn't admitted it yet.

At two o'clock she rose, dressed in the dark by touch, and let herself out into the cold. The moon had gone down behind the escarpment and the stars were hard as nailheads. Frost had come early this year; her breath hung around her like something she'd shed. She carried no lamp. She knew every step of this ground—she had measured it, staked it, argued with it—and she walked the four miles to the tool shed with the ease of a woman walking through her own memory.

The chest sat where they'd dragged it from the dredge bucket three days ago, still crusted with river silt, iron gone the color of old bruises. The padlock was hers now; Odile had given her the key without asking why, which was its own kind of question. Jonah set the key in the lock and stood a moment with her hand on the cold hasp, listening—not to the camp behind her but to the silence ahead, the silence inside the chest, the way a sealed thing holds its breath.

Her father's letters had borne this seal. She had been nine years old when she first traced the double-headed wren stamped in violet wax, and he had laughed and said, *That's the old bird, little one. It watches both directions at once.* She hadn't understood then that the Vessany crest watched both directions because it trusted neither—that a nation built on watching is a nation already falling.

She turned the key.

The lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with river water, lay documents. Dozens of them. She lifted them out one bundle at a time and laid them on the workbench beneath the single lantern she'd allowed herself, and for a long while she simply looked at what the river had kept for ten years and given back to her like a debt called due.

The concession permits came first. Three of them, each bearing the seal of the Vessany Ministry of Works, each granting the Northern Escarpment Railway Company exclusive right-of-way through the pass—rights that had passed, through some legal alchemy she'd never bothered to learn, into the hands of the company that now owned her labor and her silence. She read the first permit twice before her eye caught what it was trained to catch, because surveyors are trained to catch exactly this: the surveyor's registration number in the lower margin. She knew the numbering system. Every licensed surveyor in the old territories did. And these numbers belonged to men who had died or fled before these permits were supposedly drawn—or, in one case, to a man who had never existed at all. She checked the registry cross-references in her head, the way she checked a chain measurement against itself, and the permits did not close. They did not close at all.

Forgeries. Old ones, careful ones, but forgeries.

Beneath them, paymaster ledgers. She opened the first and the columns swam up at her: sums paid out quarterly to officials whose names meant nothing to her and everything to a tribunal, disbursements marked only by initials, and—one entry, dated eleven years ago, circled in faded ink—a payment authorized under a signature she would have known anywhere, even half-dissolved by water, even after a decade of drowning.

*Iversen.*

Her father's hand. Not his best hand—he'd written fast when he was frightened, and the letters leaned forward like runners—but his. She had spent three years telling herself that whatever he had done, she didn't know the shape of it, and here was the shape, drawn in ledger ink: he had certified the surveys. He had put his name beneath the forged numbers and made the lies load-bearing. The whole railway rested on his signature the way the trestle bridge rested on her pilings, and she was the one who had pulled it out of the river.

Jonah sat down on the workbench stool because her legs informed her they were no longer interested in standing.

* * *

The lantern burned low. Outside, an owl worked the treeline, and somewhere far off a freight train moaned through the cut, hauling other people's cargo toward other people's futures. She thought about burning it.

That was the honest truth of the next hour, and she made herself look at it straight, the way she looked at bad ground before crossing it. The ledgers would burn. Oilcloth takes flame beautifully; iron holds heat like a grudge. By morning there would be nothing but ash and a story about river rot, and Harrow's questions would find no answers, and Elias Marchetti—whoever he truly was—would go home empty-handed, and her father's name would stay buried deeper than the chest had ever been. She could go back to being nobody. Nobody was warm. Nobody was safe. Nobody had kept her alive for three years.

She even gathered the permits together. Held them over the lantern glass until the paper began to sweat.

And then she saw the date.

Not on the permits—on the concession's renewal clause, the final page, the fine print that lawyers love and surveyors skim. The company's claim to the line did not run forever. It ran on the life of the original grant, and the grant expired—by its own terms, by the dead nation's own stubborn arithmetic—six weeks from that night. When the permits lapsed, the legal claim lapsed with them, unless renewed by a tribunal empowered to examine their provenance. Unless someone stood up in front of such a tribunal and said: *these papers are false, and I can prove it, and I know why.*

Six weeks. The snows would come in five.

Jonah lowered the papers away from the flame and set them down very carefully, as if they might go off. Her hands were shaking. Not from cold.

Because she understood now what the choice actually was. Burning the chest wouldn't return her to invisibility—it would only make her complicit in a new forgery, the forgery of her own silence, signed and sealed and dated tonight. And keeping the documents meant more than carrying paper across an escarpment. It meant carrying names. It meant standing in front of strangers and saying *my father helped build this lie,* and letting that sentence do what sentences do: change everything it touches. The crew. Odile. Whatever Elias Marchetti thought he wanted from her. Herself, most of all, whom she had spent three years carefully not including in any accounting.

She repacked the chest slowly, wrapping each bundle the way she'd found it, and she noticed—as a surveyor notices, because noticing is the trade—that the bottom layer held one item apart from the rest. A letter, folded small, addressed in French-inflected script to a name she didn't recognize: *E. Marchetti, care of the ferry.* It had never been delivered. The river had taken it instead, along with everything else, and kept it ten years for reasons rivers keep their own counsel about.

She should have left it sealed. She told herself that later, and she believed it, and it didn't matter, because her fingers were already opening it.

* * *

*My friend,—*

*If you are reading this then the ferry has failed us both, and I am sorry, because I know what you risked to carry what I could not. The chest must reach the border tribunal before the grant expires—they will tell you there is time, and there is not, there has never been time, that is how they win, by owning the calendar if nothing else.*

*A word about the woman you may meet. They say a surveyor named Iversen works the northern line. If it is who I fear it is, be gentle with her. Her father was not a wicked man, only a frightened one, and frightened men sign things. She will hate the papers and need them in equal measure. So will we all.*

*Do not trust the company's fixer. Trust the river, which at least keeps honestly what it takes.*

*—A.*

Jonah read it three times. Then she sat with the lantern guttering and the frost climbing the shed windows, and she felt the strange vertigo of a map redrawing itself around her. Someone had known. Ten years ago, someone had known she might be here, working this line, dredging this river—and had written about her the way you write about weather, gently, as a condition to be endured.

Be gentle with her. As if she were the fragile thing in the chest.

She closed the letter. She locked the chest. And she stood in the doorway of the tool shed looking north toward the escarpment, black against the stars, six weeks of road and weather between her and a room full of strangers who could unmake her with a gavel—and she caught herself doing the old arithmetic, measuring the distance, pricing the miles.

Then she stopped. Deliberately, the way you set down a heavy load.

It wasn't miles anymore. It was weeks. Time, not distance. And time was the one thing Harrow owned and she didn't—which meant, she realized as the first grey light touched the ridge, that she needed allies, and she needed them before morning muster, and there was exactly one person in this valley who claimed to know what was in the chest without being told.

She blew out the lantern and started back toward camp, toward the stranger sleeping in the bunkhouse, carrying a dead man's letter against her ribs like a second heartbeat.
