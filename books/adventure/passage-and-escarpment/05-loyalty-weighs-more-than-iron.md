## 5. Loyalty Weighs More Than Iron

Odile Marchand had buried three husbands' worth of grief in the gravel of this valley—none of them men—and she knew every sound a camp made when it was lying to her.

The sounds of lying were subtle. A fire that burned too low because someone didn't want light spilling from their tent. Boots that went quiet past a certain canvas wall. The way conversation died and restarted at the wrong pitch when she walked through it. For eleven days since the river gave up its iron chest, the camp had been making those sounds around Jonah Iversen, and Odile had let it go on because she wanted to be wrong.

She was not often wrong about her crew. She had picked them the way a mason picks stone—not for beauty but for how they bore weight. Tam with his ruined ear who heard vibration better than anyone alive. Sefa, who could read water like scripture. The Kessler twins, who quarreled in two languages and fought as one animal. And Jonah, quiet Jonah, who measured the world in chains and links and never once asked to be measured back. Three years Odile had watched her walk the line with that folded leather case under her arm like a psalter, and three years Odile had thought: *there is a woman running from something, and whatever it is, it is heavy enough that she carries it standing straight.*

Now the chest sat under the floorboards of the survey shed, and Jonah stood over it at midnight looking like a woman deciding whether to dig her own grave or someone else's.

Odile had not meant to catch her at it. She'd come down for the spare theodolite because the fog on the upper cut had swallowed their morning sightlines and she wanted to re-shoot the grade before the company's man arrived. That was the other thing—the company's man. Harrow. He'd been in the valley nine days now, asking questions with the flat courtesy of a man reading names off a list he already knew by heart. He'd taken tea in Odile's own cookhouse and complimented her bread and asked, so gently, whether any of her crew had family connections east of the border. She'd said no. It was the first lie she'd told the company in nineteen years, and it had tasted like iron, and she had understood then that she was already in whatever this was, whether she chose it or not.

So: the theodolite, the fog, the shed. And lamplight where there should have been none.

She didn't announce herself. Nineteen years of running crews taught you that you learned more from what people did unwatched than anything they performed for you. She stood in the shadow of the doorframe and watched Jonah kneel on the cold boards with the chest open before her, papers spread out in careful rows like a surgeon laying out instruments, and watched her copy something—a name, an amount, a date—into the back of her leather case with a hand that shook only at the very end of each stroke, when the pen lifted.

There were seals on those papers. Odile couldn't read them from the doorway, but she knew the color of old wax, and she knew the shape of a thing being hidden, and she knew that Harrow had asked about exactly this kind of paper over tea and honeyed bread.

She stepped inside and let the door swing wide behind her.

Jonah came up off her knees like a struck animal—no, not like. Like nothing Odile had ever seen, because most people in that moment would have reached for a lie, and Jonah reached instead for the papers themselves, gathering them toward her chest as if she could carry them all out through the wall if she had to.

"Foreman," she said. Her voice was level. Her hands were not.

"Put them down, girl, before you crease something important." Odile pulled the second stool from under the bench and sat, which took longer than it used to, and let the sitting say what she wanted said: *I am not leaving, and I am not afraid, and you may as well stop bracing.*

Jonah did not put them down. But she stopped gathering them.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Odile said, "and then you're going to decide how much of yours to give me, and either way, at dawn we're both going to still be here, and Harrow is going to drink my bad coffee and learn nothing. That's the offer. It doesn't expire."

For a long moment the lamp hissed and the river spoke its one long sentence below the bank. Then Jonah set the papers down—all of them, even the ones Odile could see she hated letting go of—and turned the top sheet so Odile could read it.

Odile read. She was slow at reading; she'd been a field woman before she'd been anything else, and letters had come late to her. But she read it twice anyway, because the first time she thought her eyes had made a mistake.

It was a concession permit. Grant of right-of-way, mineral access, timber rights, the whole spine of everything—the legal root of the line they'd spent three years building and the company had spent thirty years owning. Issued under the seal of Vessany. Dated the year the border courts had ruled Vessany's instruments void. Which meant—

"This is ash," Odile said. "This is worth nothing."

"The others aren't." Jonah's voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet when she was most awake. "These are the originals. The company filed copies—copies dated four years later, reissued under the successor state's registry. Someone forged the succession. The whole claim is built on paper that was never signed by anyone with authority to sign it." She tapped a ledger page. "And these are the paymaster records showing who paid for the forgery. There are names."

"There are always names."

"One of them is my father's."

The lamp hissed. Odile looked at the girl—woman, thirty if she was a day, though she still held herself like someone waiting to be sent away—and saw the whole architecture of it suddenly, three years of timetables and silence and never once a letter home.

"And you've known since the river," Odile said.

"Since the seal." Jonah's jaw worked. "I was going to burn it. For eleven days I've been telling myself I was going to burn it."

"But you copied the names instead."

"I'm a surveyor," Jonah said, and it wasn't quite a joke. "I don't destroy evidence. I record it."

* * *

Odile told her story the way she did everything: plainly, without ornament, while she cleaned the lamp glass because her hands wanted work.

"In 'ninety-one I had a farm. Not much—one hundred and twenty acres on the south slope, apples and a milk cow and a stream that never froze. My husband Tomas ran a dray team for the company when they first came through, hauling rail. Good money. Better than farming." She set the glass down. "You know what a right-of-way agent is? Company man, comes around with maps and a fountain pen, tells you the line will pass a mile east of your land, no trouble to anyone. Then the line moves. Maps get redrawn. And when you go to the land office with your deed, the clerk can't find your parcel. It's been absorbed. Some filing error. Some *error* that happens to forty parcels in a row, all of them owned by people too poor to sue and too stubborn to leave, until they're not stubborn anymore."

"They took the farm."

"They took the farm, and then they took the appeal, because the judge hearing appeals owned shares—I found that out years later, found it out the way you find out everything, too late and in writing." She laughed, short and without humor. "Tomas drank himself into the river two winters after. I stayed on as a cook at the grading camps because it was the only work that fed me, and I worked up to chainman, and then to foreman, because I am very good at telling strong men what to do and having them thank me for it. Nineteen years. And in nineteen years I have watched that company eat this valley one parcel, one wage, one widow at a time, and I have told myself that at least my crew eats, at least my crew is paid, at least—" She stopped. Set the rag down. "At least is a coward's arithmetic, Jonah. I've been doing it half my life."

Jonah hadn't moved. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you've spent three years thinking nobody in this valley would bleed for you, and I want you to hear how wrong you are from someone who's been bleeding here longer than you've been hiding." Odile leaned forward. "You think you discovered something tonight. You think you walked in here alone with your father's sins and your sealed chest. Girl, I have been watching you carry that chest's weight since before the river gave it up. Whatever's in those papers, it isn't just yours. It's mine. It's Tomas's. It's every parcel that got *absorbed*. You didn't dredge up your past. You dredged up ours."

"That's why I was going to burn it." Jonah's voice cracked on it, finally, the level tone failing. "Because it isn't just mine. Because if I run with it, Harrow follows it here, to all of you. If I testify, everyone who helped me is named. I've read enough ledgers to know how reprisal works."

"Yes," Odile said. "And?"

Jonah stared at her.

"And," Odile repeated, "you are not the only person in this room who has done that arithmetic. Here is what I know about the escarpment crossing that you don't, because you've only been here three years and I've been here nineteen. The old pack road above the slide chutes is closed on paper but open on foot until the first hard snow—call it five weeks, six if the weather's gentle. There's water at the cairn spring year-round. The Kesslers ran contraband tobacco over that road during the embargo and can still recite every switchback in their sleep. Tam knows which trestles will bear a mule and which will bear a rumor. And Sefa—" she smiled, thin and fierce, "Sefa has been telling me for a week that you've been sleeping badly and eating worse, and Sefa does not notice things unless they matter."

"You knew."

"I suspected. Now I know. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm in it." Odile stood, joints announcing themselves, and looked down at the spread of papers—at the forged permits, the paymaster's neat columns, the dead nation's seal gone brown as an autumn leaf. "Six weeks. The permits lapse when the snows come, you said. So we cross before the snows, we reach the tribunal at the border, and we put this paper in front of judges while it still means something. That's the plan. It's a bad plan. Plans made against companies usually are."

"It'll cost everyone something," Jonah said. "Maybe everything."

"Loyalty weighs more than iron, girl. I learned that carrying chain up this valley for nineteen years. Iron you can set down. Loyalty you carry or you were never really holding it." She gathered the papers with more care than Jonah had, squaring the edges, and passed them back. "Now. Tell me about the man who came off the ferry claiming to be a courier, because Harrow asked me about him too, and I want to know whose side of the tea he was sweetening."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low and the sky past the shed window went the gray of old pewter. Jonah talked; Odile listened and interrupted, which was her way of loving things. The courier—Elias—who knew the chest's contents without being told. The fixer—Harrow—who had posted extra men at the yard office and begun walking the line at hours no inspector keeps. The plan assembled itself out of both their knowledge: documents split between carriers, the rails used as noise while the pack road carried the truth, timing set against the permit expiry like a fuse cut to length.

When it was done, Jonah sat back on her heels and looked at Odile with an expression the foreman had seen maybe twice in three years—unguarded, young, astonished.

"Why?" Jonah asked. "You barely know me."

"I know you returned a dead man's wages to his daughter in Corren's Ford, out of your own pay, and told no one." Odile snuffed the lamp. "I know you re-surveyed the Miller cut on your own time because you thought the company's numbers were cheating the blast crew, and you were right. I know exactly who you are, Jonah Iversen. I've known for three years. You're the one who's been keeping yourself a secret."

Outside, the fog was lifting off the upper cut, and down the valley, at the yard office, a lamp was burning that had no business burning at that hour. Odile saw it and felt the old cold move through her, the cold of 'ninety-one.

Harrow was awake early. Which meant the clock on all of them had started sooner than six weeks.

"We move the chest tomorrow night," she said.
