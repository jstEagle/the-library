## 6. The Escarpment Road

They left before dawn, in the blue hour when the river still held the night's cold and the rails ran silver with frost. Jonah stood at the edge of the yard with the map spread across a crate of spikes, weighting the corners with stones, and watched her crew divide itself around her like water around a stone.

Odile had drawn the lines herself, but it was Jonah's hand that marked them. The overland party—Odile, Tam, and the Vessany chest broken down into three oilskin-wrapped bundles that no longer looked like anything but survey equipment—would take the shepherd track above the Kettle River and climb toward the escarpment through country so broken that even Harrow's best riders would need four days to cover what they could do in two. The rail party—Jonah, Elias, and young Piet with his gift for engines—would run interference: a maintenance run on the north spur, loud and legitimate, pulling Harrow's watchers east while the real cargo went west.

"You understand," Odile said quietly, not for the first time, "that if they stop you, you know nothing. You're an inspector. You have papers. You are boring."

"I've been boring for three years," Jonah said. "I'm very good at it."

Odile's mouth twitched. Then she gripped Jonah's forearm, hard, the way rail men did instead of embracing, and held it a moment longer than the gesture required. "The pass closes when it closes. Don't be clever about the weather. The weather always wins."

"Then I'll be fast instead."

Elias was waiting by the handcar with Piet, and something in the set of his shoulders made Jonah's chest tighten—not suspicion anymore, or not only suspicion, though she had learned in the last week that trust was not a door you walked through once but a room you kept re-entering, checking the windows. He wore a company coat he'd acquired from somewhere, and he wore it like a man wearing a stranger's skin.

"The north spur," he said as she came up. "You've ridden it?"

"I mapped it." She said it flatly, and watched him decide not to ask why a surveyor who'd been in the valley three years had already walked every closed line on the escarpment. That was the arrangement between them now: questions banked like coals, to be fed later.

Piet pumped the handcar onto the main line and they were moving, the valley unspooling behind them, birch and scree and the gray thread of the river. By midmorning they passed the first of Harrow's watchers—a company man in a telegraph shack, pretending to mend wire, whose eyes tracked them with the diligence of a man being paid by the question. Jonah raised two fingers in lazy salute. Boring. Legitimate. Gone.

* * *

The trouble came at Miller's Bend, where the escarpment shouldered down close to the river and the rock above the cut had been shedding since the spring flood.

Jonah heard it before she saw it—a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, deep and grinding—and threw herself off the handcar, dragging Piet by the collar, shouting at Elias to jump. They hit the gravel ballast as the first boulders came down through the cut, tons of it, a white roar of dust and stone that buried the rails ahead of them and shook the ground like a struck bell.

When the dust thinned, the line simply wasn't there. Forty yards of track gone under rubble, and above it, the slope still ticking and shifting, deciding whether it was finished.

"Well," Piet said faintly, from where he lay in the ballast. "That's the north spur done."

Jonah got up slowly, brushing grit from her palms, and looked at the slide, and then at the mountain above it, and then west, where the escarpment rose in its long gray wall. There was another way. There was only one other way, and she had found it two winters ago, alone, on a survey nobody had asked for, walking a line that the company had abandoned twelve years before because the tunnel through Sorrow Notch had cost more than it returned.

Nobody knew that line. Nobody living. She had kept it the way she kept everything—folded small, hidden, hers.

She could feel the old reflex rising in her throat like bile: don't tell them, don't show them, keep the map inside yourself where it can't be used against you. Three years of practice. Three years of miles instead of names.

But Odile's grip was still on her forearm, phantom-warm. *Belonging offered freely.* And Elias stood in the settling dust watching her with those patient dark eyes, not asking, waiting to see what she would do—as if he already understood that whatever she chose next would tell him more than any interrogation.

"There's a closed line," she heard herself say. "Through Sorrow Notch. It joins the high road six miles past the slide. The tunnel's unsound—I surveyed it myself—but it'll hold for a handcar if we walk the load light and check the crown as we go." She pulled the map case from under her coat, and her hands were steadier than she expected. "It's not on this copy. I'll draw it."

"You surveyed a dead line alone in winter," Elias said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Somebody had to know where we were," Jonah said. "Even if it was only me."

He held her gaze a moment longer, and then he nodded, once, and began hauling the handcar up the embankment path without needing to be told how. Piet followed. And Jonah knelt in the gravel with her pencil out, drawing the line she had sworn never to show anyone, and felt the strangest sensation—like setting down a weight she had forgotten she was carrying, and discovering her spine straighter for its absence.

* * *

Sorrow Notch took them the rest of the day and gave them nothing back.

The approach grade was choked with alder grown through the ties, and they cleared it by hand, Piet singing low under his breath in the old work rhythm, Elias swinging a brush-hook with the competence of a man who had done hard labor somewhere in his buried history. Inside the tunnel the air was mineral-cold and absolutely black beyond the reach of their lanterns, and twice Jonah stopped them all with a raised fist and climbed the ladder of fallen timber to check the crown, running her lamp along cracks in the rock, listening with her whole body.

"Cracked like this two years ago," she called down. "Hasn't moved since. Rock tells you the truth if you ask it often enough."

"And you?" Elias said from below, quietly, in the dark. "Does it work both ways?"

She let the lantern light find his face for just a moment—the shadowed jaw, the eyes that never demanded anything—and did not answer. But she smiled, and she knew he saw it, and in the black throat of the mountain that seemed like enough confession for one day.

They came out the far portal at dusk into a bowl of high meadow going gold and violet, and below them, six miles on, the high road wound toward the pass with the evening sun striking fire along its length. From here they could see everything: the switchbacks, the way-station chimney smoking, the thin dark line of riders coming up the valley road behind them—too far to be certain, too organized to be chance.

"Harrow," Piet breathed.

"Or mail carriers," Elias said.

"We're not that lucky." Jonah studied the line of the road, the angles, the distances, translating landscape into time the way she translated everything. "We can't make the way-station before they do. But there's a cutoff—" and there it was again, the old reflex, the hoarded knowledge—and she pushed through it, deliberately, saying it aloud: "A cutoff, half a mile back along the tunnel rim. Old mule trail. It drops us onto the high road below the last switchback. We'll come out behind them instead of ahead."

Elias looked at her, and something moved across his face that was not surprise but recognition—the look of a man watching someone become, in real time, exactly who she had always been underneath the timetables.

"Lead on, Inspector," he said.

* * *

They came down the mule trail in the last light, leading the handcar in pieces on their shoulders, and gained the high road in the blue dusk with the wind already carrying the first iron smell of snow. Behind and above them, the riders' lanterns crawled along the upper switchbacks, committed to the long way, hours behind.

Piet laughed out loud, giddy with relief, and clapped Jonah on the shoulder hard enough to stagger her. "You witch," he said fondly. "You absolute witch of the mountains."

And Jonah laughed too—actually laughed, the sound strange and rusty in her own ears—and for one moment, standing on the high road with the escarpment looming black against the stars and snow-smell thickening in the wind, she felt the truth of it settle into her bones: that competence was a kind of intimacy, that every mile she led these people safely was a word in a language of belonging she was only now learning to speak.

Then the wind shifted, and brought down from the pass the smell of woodsmoke and horses, and Elias went still beside her, head cocked, listening the way he listened.

"Lanterns," he said softly. "Below us. On the road ahead."

Jonah turned. Far down the descending grade, where the high road bent around the shoulder of the mountain toward the pass, lights were moving. Not crawling. Waiting.

Someone had known about the cutoff after all—or known her well enough to guess.

"They're not chasing us," Elias said quietly. "They're already there."
