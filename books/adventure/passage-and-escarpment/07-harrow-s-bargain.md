## 7. Harrow's Bargain

The trap was set at a water stop, which Elias thought showed a certain poetry in the man. Harrow had chosen the place the way he did everything—without flourish, without waste. The siding at Kilnwater was nothing: a rusted tank on a timber cradle, a plank platform gone silver with weather, one lamp burning green against the dusk because the company had never paid to fix it to white. Elias came down off the freight car expecting an empty platform and forty minutes of cold, and found Harrow sitting on the edge of it instead, hat in his lap, boots crossed at the ankle, as though he'd been waiting for a friend.

"Mr. Marchetti." He said the name the way a customs officer reads a passport aloud—checking each syllable for forgery. "You'll forgive the theater. I find stations make men honest. Everyone at a station is either leaving something or chasing something."

Elias stayed where he was, one hand on the iron ladder, letting the train's heat drain out of him into the evening air. Behind Harrow, two of the company's road agents stood by the water crane with the studied idleness of men who were not idle at all. Beyond them, the escarpment rose black against the last light, its ridgeline already dusted with the season's first threat of white.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Elias said. "You know my name. I only know your reputation."

"Harrow. Company man, in the way that a knife is a kitchen implement." He patted the plank beside him. "Sit. Or don't. Standing suits you—I can see why she likes you."

The name went through Elias like a fishhook set too deep. He kept his face still, but he saw Harrow register the stillness, catalog it, file it away. That was the thing about the man that no one had warned him properly about: he didn't threaten. He *noticed*. Threats were for people who needed volume; Harrow worked in margins.

"I'm a courier," Elias said. "Between jobs."

"You're a courier who lost his shipment in the river at Vessany Bend, yes, we've established that. A tragic ferry accident. Very sad. The company paid out on that ferry route for years, you know—we know exactly what it carried, because we were the ones who arranged—" He stopped himself, smiled thinly, corrected course like a man adjusting a ledger entry. "What I mean to say is that you are a courier whose shipment is currently sitting in a surveyor's tent at Camp Nine, under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, being read by a woman who should have burned it six days ago."

*Six days.* So they had eyes inside the camp. Elias let that settle into him, cold as the river had been. Not Odile—the old woman guarded her crew like a wolf guards a den. One of the laborers, then. Someone small, someone frightened, someone whose debts Harrow held the paper on. There was always someone.

"She hasn't read it," Elias said. "She's deciding whether to."

"And that," Harrow said, standing now, brushing off his coat with two precise strokes, "is the entire problem, isn't it. An undecided woman with a chest full of dead nation in her tent." He began to walk, slowly, along the platform, and Elias fell into step beside him—not because he wanted to, but because refusing would look like fear, and fear was the one currency Harrow accepted at any exchange rate. "Let me tell you what happens next, Mr. Marchetti, in the version where you're sensible. In six weeks, those permits expire. Legally. Cleanly. The concession renews itself like winter follows autumn, and every name in that chest becomes a historical curiosity instead of a scandal. Nobody hangs. Nobody loses anything. The world simply continues, which is the most anyone can hope for."

"And in the version where I'm not sensible?"

"In that version, the documents reach the border tribunal, there is a hearing, there is testimony, and the railway loses the line. And do you know what else happens?" Harrow turned to look at him directly, and up close his eyes were the color of weak tea, patient and faintly amused. "Every hand that touched those papers gets named. Every courier, every smuggler, every exile who financed the crossing. The tribunal doesn't distinguish between the forger and the man who carried the forgery. It can't afford to. Justice at a border is a blunt instrument, Mr. Marchetti—it has to be, or nobody respects it."

He let that sit. Somewhere down the line a locomotive sighed, long and low, like something settling into sleep.

"The exiles will vouch for me," Elias said quietly.

"The exiles," Harrow said, "are eleven hundred miles away, scattered across three countries, and half of them would sell the other half for passage money. I've bought from them before. They give good rates." He reached into his coat—not fast, deliberately slow, showing his palm the whole way—and produced a folded paper, sealed with plain wax, no crest. "Amnesty. Full protection. Your name cleared in this country and the next; the warrant from Vessany's successor government dissolved; safe conduct anywhere the rails run. You walk away a free man with money in your pocket, and the chest goes into the company furnace at Kilnwater, and by Thursday it's smoke."

"And what does she get?"

Harrow's smile didn't move so much as deepen, the way a crack deepens in ice. "Jonah Iversen keeps her job. Keeps her anonymity. Keeps her little life of timetables and bridge inspections. All she loses is—" he consulted the paper as if reading from it, though Elias knew he knew it by heart, "—her credibility. A quiet note in the record. *Surveyor Iversen, found in possession of falsified documents, believed to be acting as agent for foreign interests.* She becomes unreliable. Unpromotable. Watched. But employed." He refolded it. "That's the mercy, Mr. Marchetti. I'm offering you her ruin in the gentlest available form. Other men in my position would offer you nothing at all."

The green lamp hissed. A moth threw itself against the glass, again and again, with the devotion of the damned.

Elias looked out at the escarpment. Somewhere over that ridge, in a camp lit by cookfires, Jonah was probably awake—she slept badly, he'd learned that, slept in snatches like a soldier or a fugitive—and somewhere in her tent, under the floorboards, lay the proof that had cost her father everything and might yet cost her more. He thought about the amnesty paper. He thought about the exiles who had sent him, the old men in the boarding houses of Marrowgate who had pooled their pensions to fund a courier, who had made him swear on the dissolved seal of Vessany that the truth would cross the border if it cost him his skin. He had sworn. He could still feel the wax of that oath under his thumb.

And he thought about her hands, ink-stained at the knuckles, measuring the world inch by inch because she trusted measurements more than she trusted people.

"You've done your arithmetic," Elias said at last. "Ledgers for amnesty. A clean trade. But you've made one error in the calculation."

"Enlighten me."

"You think I'm choosing between her and the documents. You've priced it all out—her ruin against my freedom, the ledgers against the warrant. But that's not the choice." He turned to face Harrow fully, and felt, strangely, calm—the calm of a man stepping off a known height. "The choice is whether the truth arrives whole or arrives broken. And I'm not going to help you break it. Not for amnesty. Not for her safety. Not for anything you can put on paper."

Something flickered behind Harrow's tea-colored eyes—not anger, nothing so crude. Recalculation. "You understand," he said softly, "that stalling is also a choice. And it's the one that gets people hurt. The crew at Camp Nine. The foreman. Her." He said her name without emphasis, which was somehow worse than emphasis. "If the documents move before the permits expire, I'm authorized to treat the crossing as smuggling. Armed, if necessary. I would prefer it not be necessary. I suspect you would too."

"Then we have until the snows," Elias said, "to both be disappointed."

He walked back to the freight car and climbed the ladder without looking at the road agents, without looking at Harrow, keeping his spine straight through sheer expenditure of will. As the train groaned back into motion, he pressed his forehead briefly against the cold iron and allowed himself, for exactly the length of one breath, to feel the terror he'd been carrying since the platform: that he had just signed away his own pardon, that the exiles' oath and the woman at Camp Nine were now bound together in him like fuse powder, and that Harrow had gotten precisely what he'd wanted from the meeting after all—a measure of the man, and a hook set in him that no honest man could pull free.

Because Harrow had been right about one thing, and Elias knew it as the rails began their long climb toward the escarpment: stalling was a choice. He had bought time—days, perhaps a week—but time was the one commodity Harrow couldn't print, and he would come to collect it with interest.

Through the window, the first flakes were starting to fall. Early. Far too early. And Elias understood, with the certainty of a man watching a timetable fail, that whatever Jonah meant to do, she would have to do it now.

* * *

The snow thickened as the train crawled north, and Elias spent the night in the vestibule between cars, smoking nothing, chewing the end of a cold cigarillo, doing the arithmetic he'd refused to do on the platform. If the documents moved overland, they'd need three days good weather minimum. The forecast the stationmaster had grumbled about gave them one. Which meant Jonah already knew, which meant she was already moving, which meant Harrow's men would already be watching the passes—which meant the crossing would happen in weather bad enough to kill them all, and his job, the only job left to him now, was to be wrong about that.

At dawn the train stopped at a water tower below the escarpment's southern shoulder, and Elias dropped from the vestibule into knee-deep drift, leaving his berth made up, his ticket punched through to Marrowgate, and his old life neatly arranged on the seat like evidence.

He started walking toward Camp Nine, and the snow erased his footprints behind him faster than he could make them.
