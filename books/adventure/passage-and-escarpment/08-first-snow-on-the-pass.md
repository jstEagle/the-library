## 8. First Snow on the Pass

The snow came three days early, the way debts do.

Jonah felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind the ears, a hush in the pines that had nothing to do with wind. She stopped at the head of the switchback and turned her face up, and the first flakes came down fine as sifted flour, catching in her lashes, melting against the heat of her skin. Behind her, the mule train snorted and stamped. Ahead, the escarpment road climbed in long hairpin bends toward the pass, each turn a sentence in a language only she could read fluently.

"Storm's here," Odile said, coming up beside her. The older woman's breath plumed white. "You said we'd have until Thursday."

"I said the almanac said Thursday." Jonah pulled her collar higher. "The mountain didn't sign the almanac."

Odile grunted—the sound she made instead of laughing—and turned to bellow orders down the line. *Tarp the loads. Shorten the traces. We move.* Six of them now: Odile, Jonah, the Kessler twins with their rope and their silence, Marta who could drive anything with legs, and young Piet whose hands shook but whose memory was iron. The documents rode in oilcloth inside the false bottom of a grain chest, strapped to the lead mule, and every one of them knew what they carried even though none of them spoke it aloud. Forty miles to the border tribunal. Five days if the weather held. The permits expired at midnight on the sixth day, and after midnight, the railway's claim became law again, and everything they had bled for became paper.

Jonah checked the chest strap twice, then a third time, and hated herself for it. Three years of measuring life in miles had taught her this much: you can count a distance, but you cannot count on it.

They climbed.

* * *

The storm found its full voice by midday. What had been flour became gravel, then fistfuls, then a white roar that erased the road ten feet ahead and turned the world into a shaking gray room. They moved by feel and by Jonah's voice—*bear left, the drop is on your right, trust me*—and they did trust her, which was the thing that kept catching in her chest like a burr. She had spent three years making herself untrustable, unknowable, a woman made of timetables. And these people had simply decided otherwise, without asking her permission.

The first switchback was a shelf cut into the mountainside, wide enough for two mules abreast, with a drop below that the snow had kindly hidden but that Jonah's map held in perfect detail: ninety feet of scree ending in frozen water. She remembered surveying it in June, when the drop had been a green blur and the danger had seemed theoretical. Nothing was theoretical anymore.

She heard the horses before she saw them. Under the wind's howl, a wrongness—the particular clop of shod hooves moving too fast for this road, coming from below.

"Piet." She caught his sleeve. "Count them."

He tilted his head, listening the way he listened to rails, and his face went gray. "Eight. Maybe nine. Coming hard."

Harrow. It could be no one else. The fixer had let them think they'd slipped him at the river junction; now she understood he had simply been letting them climb, letting the mountain narrow the options until there was only one place left to make a stand. A switchback is a trap built into the land itself—you cannot go around it, only across it, exposed, slow, visible.

"Marta, take the lead mule. Cut off the trace and bring the chest to me." Jonah was already pulling the map case from inside her coat, already unrolling it against her own body to shield it from the wet. Her fingers were steady. She noticed that from a strange distance, the way you notice someone else's hands. "Everyone else, listen once, because I'll only say it once."

Below them, through the white, shapes resolved: riders in company oilskins, and at their head a figure she knew by posture alone, upright and patient as a fence post. Harrow did not draw a weapon. He never needed to. He simply raised one gloved hand, and his men fanned out across the lower bend, cutting the road behind them.

"You're carrying company property," Harrow called up, his voice carrying flat and clear through the snow. "I have a bill of seizure signed by the district magistrate. Hand over the chest and walk down, and everyone eats hot food tonight."

"He's lying about the magistrate," Odile said quietly. "But he's not lying about the hot food. Or the alternative."

Jonah looked at the chest. Ninety pounds of paper that could void a concession, free a valley, name her father a forger in front of God and the tribunal both. She looked at her people—Piet's shaking hands, Marta's set jaw, the twins flanking Odile like bookends. And she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like relief, that Harrow had built the trap correctly. He had offered her exactly the choice she feared: the truth or the people. The mission or the family. One or the other, and either way she lost something she could not replace.

That was the moment she stopped being afraid.

Because Harrow had read the terrain, but he hadn't read her map. Nobody had. Not all of it.

"No," Jonah said aloud, to Harrow, to the mountain, to the girl she used to be. Then, to her crew: "Marta, the chest stays with you and the twins. Take the lower branch."

"There is no lower branch," Odile said. "It's closed. It's been closed since the slide."

"It's been closed since the slide," Jonah agreed, "and I surveyed the closure. There's a maintenance cut, forty yards past the second cairn, that drops to the old haul road. It's choked with rock, but it's passable on foot with mules if you take the west wall. I mapped it in June. It's on no chart the company has." She met Odile's eyes. "That's where the documents go. You go with them."

"And you?"

"I'm the decoy." She rolled the map closed and thrust it into Marta's arms along with the route she'd traced that morning in grease pencil. "Harrow wants the chest and he wants *me*. He thinks I won't split from the papers because I'm the kind of person who chooses. So I choose. Loudly. Up here, on the open road, where every eye he has stays on me."

"Jonah—" Odile's hand closed on her arm, grip like a vice. For a moment the foreman's face was stripped bare, and Jonah saw in it everything Odile had lost to this same company, everything she was refusing to lose again. "You don't get to buy our lives with yours. That's not how a crew works."

"That's exactly how a crew works," Jonah said. "It's just never been tested before." She covered Odile's hand with her own. "Take them down the cut. Get to the tribunal. If I'm not there by midnight tomorrow, testify anyway. Tell them everything. Tell them my name."

* * *

What followed took four minutes and lasted a season.

Marta and the twins went first, mules and all, easing sideways into the whiteout toward the cairns while Jonah walked the lead mule—her mule, a placid gelding named Bors, saddled and blanketed to look burdened—straight up the open switchback, in plain view, making no effort at stealth. Behind her, Odile stayed.

Jonah realized it too late. Odile was supposed to descend with the others. Instead the foreman had stepped back onto the road, planted herself between Jonah and the riders below, and pulled the long coil of blasting fuse from her pack—the fuse they'd carried for clearing rockfall, and the small charge kit they'd carried for worse.

"Go," Odile said, not turning around. "Both of you. Bors and the girl."

"Odile—"

"They'll want to talk first. Harrow always talks. That's his whole trick—he makes you think words are safer than actions." Now she glanced back, and she was smiling, and it was the worst thing Jonah had ever seen. "So I'll give him words. And while he's giving them, you'll be gone, and so will the papers, and this old woman will have done the last useful thing of her life."

"That's not the last useful thing," Jonah said fiercely. "Rebuilding the line under honest charter—that's the last useful thing. You promised. You're supposed to be there."

"Then I suppose I'd better survive." Odile shoved her, hard, toward the upper trail. "Ride, surveyor. Ride like the mountain owes you money."

Jonah went. She hated every stride of it. She put Bors up the switchback at a scrambling trot, snow driving sideways, and heard behind her the rise of voices—Harrow's smooth baritone, Odile's gravel—and then, cutting through everything, a crack like the sky splitting: the charge, placed not to kill but to collapse the lip of the road behind her, sealing the switchback, buying minutes with tons of fallen stone.

And then a shot. Then another. Then Odile's voice, high and furious and alive—*alive*—shouting something the wind tore to pieces.

Jonah reined up at the top of the climb, torn between the pass and the road below, between the mission and the woman, and understood that this was the false choice again, wearing new clothes. Harrow had designed it. But she had drawn the map.

There was a third way. There was always a third way; you just had to survey for it.

"The cut," she breathed. "Marta—if they went down the cut, the haul road rejoins the main line at the trestle—" She wheeled Bors off the summit trail onto a goat path only she had charted, a thin thread contouring the mountainside above the closed branch, dropping toward the junction where the old haul road met the trestle over Whitecap Creek. If Harrow's men pursued the documents down the cut, they would come out at the trestle. And if she got there first—

She drove Bors down the path with the storm screaming around her, branches raking her shoulders, trusting the mule's footing more than she'd ever trusted anything except her own memory. Below and to her left, half-glimpsed through the white, she saw them: three riders pushing along the haul road toward the trestle, having guessed—or been sent—to intercept the lower party. Between them and the trestle lay a quarter mile of road. Between her and the trestle's far end lay a tenth of that, straight down the goat path.

She arrived as the first rider did, from opposite ends of the bridge, and the look on his face when he saw a lone woman on a mule materialize out of the blizzard on *his* side of the crossing was something she would carry to her grave as private treasure.

"Trestle's mined," she lied, with the calm of a woman reading a timetable. "Company ordnance. Turn back."

They believed her, because the truth is rarely delivered with such confidence. While they argued, she heard it below: voices, mule bells, the scrape of boots on the cut's rocky throat—Marta and the twins, coming up the haul road toward her, documents intact, faces white with cold and triumph.

Then, over the ridge behind her, a single figure on foot, limping, leaning on a rifle like a staff. Gray with powder smoke and blood at her temple, but upright. Upright as a fence post.

"Odile!"

"Switchback's closed," the foreman rasped, grinning through the blood. "Permanently. Told you I'd survive. Where's my hot food?"

Jonah laughed—a wild, cracked sound swallowed instantly by the storm—and pulled the old woman's arm across her shoulders. Around them the snow thickened toward evening, erasing tracks, erasing the road, erasing everything except the six of them and the chest and the miles still between here and the border.

"One day," Jonah said, counting automatically, helplessly. "One day, maybe fourteen hours, if the storm holds off."

"Stop counting," Odile muttered into her shoulder. "Start walking."

But at the edge of the trestle, Jonah paused and looked back the way they'd come—at the white chaos where Harrow's men milled, cheated, and where somewhere in it, she knew, Elias Marchetti stood watching, choosing, his choice still unread. The storm gave nothing away. Neither did the future.

Midnight tomorrow. The permits expired then, and so did every version of herself she'd ever hidden behind.

She tightened her arm around Odile and led them into the white.
