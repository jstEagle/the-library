## 9. Testimony at the Border

The tribunal hall had been a customs house once, back when Vessany still existed to collect the duties. Its stone walls still bore the ghost of that purpose: a faded double-headed crest above the magistrate's bench, chiseled off by some zealous mason but never quite erased, the outline of it showing through whitewash like a scar through skin. Jonah stared at it while the clerk read out the procedural formalities, and thought: *my father stood in rooms like this.* Not this room—rooms older, grander, in a capital that no longer answered its own name—but rooms with the same seal on the wall and the same hush before the papers were read.

She had imagined this moment for six weeks, and in every imagining she was running. Running up the escarpment road with the ledgers strapped to her back, running through the pass ahead of Harrow's men, running across the border line into whatever country would take her testimony and let her vanish after. That had been the plan she'd built in the dark, the way she built all her plans: get there, deliver it, disappear. Miles instead of years.

But Odile was in the infirmary two streets away with a shattered collarbone and a splinted leg, and every mile Jonah had ever measured had been measured away from something. She sat in the witness box with her hands flat on the rail and made herself be still.

The three magistrates sat behind a scarred oak bench. The middle one, a woman named Corvane with iron-gray hair cropped short, had been reading the documents since dawn. They lay before her now in ordered stacks—the forged concession permits with their counterfeit Vessany seals, the paymaster ledgers in their careful columns, the correspondence that tied them together. Beside the stacks, Harrow's counter-evidence: affidavits, sworn statements, a leather folio thick as a Bible.

"The permits expire at midnight," Corvane said. Her voice carried without effort; the room was built for carrying voices. "Under the succession statutes, any concession granted under the former government lapses with its instruments unless renewed or validated by this tribunal. The railway company claims valid original grants. The claimant—" a nod toward Jonah— "asserts those grants are forged."

"I do," Jonah said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. Three years of giving measurements to foremen and timetables to strangers had taught her how to say true things plainly.

"Then you will tell us how you know."

* * *

She told them about the river first, because it was true and because it bought her time to find her footing. The spring flood, the bridge inspection, the dredge lines coming up heavy. The chest caked in gray silt, and the seal emerging from under the mud like a face surfacing from deep water. She described the wax impression inside the lid—Vessany's double-headed eagle, the one head crowned, the other bare—and watched Corvane's pen stop moving when she mentioned the bare head.

"You recognize the distinction," Corvane said.

"My father taught me heraldry the way other fathers teach fishing." Jonah kept her eyes on the magistrate. If she looked at Harrow now, standing at the railway's table with his gray coat and his patient hands, she would lose the thread. "The crowned head for the sovereign line. The bare head for the chancery—the office that issued concessions, permits, charters. A permit sealed with the crowned eagle alone is wrong. A permit sealed correctly but struck from the wrong die—" she nodded at the documents on the bench— "is worse than wrong. It's evidence."

"Of what?"

"That whoever made these had access to the chancery's records but not its dies. Someone who knew exactly what a real permit looked like. Down to the watermark." She paused. "That narrows the list of possible forgers considerably. And I'm going to give you the names on it, including one you won't want."

Corvane leaned forward slightly. "Proceed."

Jonah had rehearsed this part on the switchbacks, in the dark, with Odile's blood on her sleeve and the storm coming down the mountain. It hadn't made it easier. Nothing could make it easier. But rehearsal had taught her where the words lived, and she went to them now the way she went to landmarks on a mapped line.

"My father was Aldous Iversen. He was second secretary in the Vessany chancery's concessions bureau." She heard the murmur move through the gallery—railway men mostly, summoned by Harrow, and a scattering of locals who had come to see whether their valley's future would be decided honestly for once. "In the last years of the government, when everything was being sold and everyone knew the state was dying, the bureau began issuing instruments for land it had no authority to grant. Concessions, water rights, mineral claims. My father drew the documents. He copied the seals from archival impressions. He was good at it. I know because he taught me, though I didn't understand then what I was learning."

She took a breath. This was the part she had not rehearsed enough, because no amount of rehearsal could carry it.

"He is dead nine years. I can't produce him. What I can produce is his hand." She indicated the ledgers. "Page forty-one of the paymaster ledger. The notation for the escarpment survey, dated four months before the collapse. The numeral seven has a broken tail—he broke it on a drafting table in 1889 and never fixed his grip. Every seven he wrote afterward looks like that. I grew up watching him write sevens. There are eleven in that entry. All of them broken the same way."

Corvane turned pages. The hall was quiet except for paper.

"And yourself," Corvane said, not looking up. "You've given us your father. What do we call you?"

Here it was. The moment she had been fleeing for three years, arriving at last not because she ran toward it but because she had finally stopped running anywhere at all.

"Jonah Iversen," she said. "Daughter of Aldous Iversen. Heir to whatever debt this is. I didn't forge anything—but I benefited from the silence around it, and I hid inside that silence for three years, working a line my family helped steal the ground out from under. I'm not here as an innocent. I'm here because the debt doesn't expire at midnight even if the permits do, and someone has to sign for it."

* * *

Harrow rose to cross-examine, and Jonah finally let herself look at him.

He was exactly as composed as always—gray coat brushed, hands folded, the face of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because he had never once lost an argument. But she had spent six weeks learning to read him the way she read weather, and she saw the small things. The way his thumb pressed the edge of the folio. The glance he gave the door—not nervous, just confirming, the way a man confirms the exits of a room he intends to control.

"Miss Iversen." His voice was mild. "You found these documents in a river."

"In a chest in a river."

"A chest of unknown origin, salvaged during routine work, which you then kept secret from your employer for weeks while arranging to remove company property across an international border." He let that sit. "You'll forgive me if I observe that your custody of these materials rather undermines their evidentiary value. A chain of custody requires, at minimum, a chain."

"The chain is the water," Jonah said. "The chest went down with the ferry *Meridian* in the autumn of the collapse. The ferry manifest lists diplomatic freight bound for the border garrison. Your own company's records will confirm the sinking—they filed an insurance claim on recovered cargo rights the following year. You knew what was down there, Mr. Harrow. That's why you were asking questions in the valley within a day of it coming up."

Harrow did not blink. "I asked questions because a salvage crew pulled up a chest bearing a foreign seal. Any competent administrator would have done the same."

"Then you won't mind the tribunal asking why your questions included the crew's families' names."

She felt the gallery shift behind her. Corvane's pen scratched.

Harrow opened his folio with unhurried care. "I have here sworn statements from the railway's chief engineer attesting to the authenticity of the original grants. I have a certified copy of the concession register from the successor archive at Brasten. And I have testimony regarding the claimant's conduct—her concealment of the documents, her flight across company land, her association with persons who assaulted company agents on the escarpment road." He closed the folio gently, as if closing a coffin. "The tribunal may weigh a drowned chest against all this however it likes. But I'd ask it to consider what kind of witness opens a sealed chest belonging to another party and rewrites history with its contents."

"The kind whose name is in it," said a voice from the back of the hall.

Jonah turned so fast her shoulder hit the box rail.

Elias Marchetti stood in the doorway, snowmelt still dark on his boots, his coat torn at the seam where Harrow's men had held him at the pass. Two bailiffs flanked him—not escorting him, she realized, but having decided he wasn't worth restraining. He walked the length of the aisle like a man crossing a frozen river, testing each step, and stopped at the bar.

"Chairman," he said to Corvane. "I ask leave to testify. I am Elias Marchetti, courier in the service of the Vessany exile chancery-in-exile at Brasten. The chest was consigned to me. I was to bring it to this tribunal myself. The ferry sank under me instead, and I spent ten years assuming the water had settled the matter." He glanced at Jonah, and for one unguarded moment the whole tangle of it—his arrival, his lies, his stalling, his choice at the pass—passed between them without needing words. "It hadn't."

Harrow was already moving. "Chairman, this man is a self-declared agent of a defunct foreign power. His testimony—"

"Is corroborative, Mr. Harrow, not foundational," Corvane said. "And I will hear it." She looked at Elias. "You swear the chest's contents are genuine instruments of the former chancery?"

"I swear they are genuine *forgeries*—commissioned, paid for, and archived by the chancery itself in its final months, precisely so that someday someone could prove what the railway's founders bought." Elias set a folded paper on the bar. "The commission record. My principal's seal. And a list of payments, Mr. Harrow, that includes disbursements to your predecessor's office. Your company didn't inherit clean title and get cheated of it. Your company commissioned the forgery and then burned the workshop. The chest was the insurance policy. I was the courier who failed to deliver it until a surveyor fished it out of the river for me."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow load on a roof.

Harrow stood very still. Then, with the terrible composure of a man watching a structure fail beneath him, he opened his folio again—and Jonah saw him understand, in real time, that every affidavit in it was now worth less than the paper it was sworn on. The chief engineer's attestation. The certified register from Brasten. He had built his counter-case on archives, and archives were exactly what Elias had just turned against him.

"I withdraw the railway's exhibits," Harrow said quietly.

"Too late for withdrawal," Corvane said. "They're entered. They'll be weighed." She turned to the clock on the wall—a brass thing, older than the whitewash. "It is twenty minutes to midnight. The tribunal will adjourn briefly and return to rule before expiry. Miss Iversen—you will remain."

* * *

They remained. Everyone remained; nobody left; the gallery had become a single held breath.

At four minutes to midnight, Corvane read the ruling aloud in a voice built for carrying across borders: the concession permits declared forged, the railway's claim void as of expiry, the escarpment lands reverted to the public trust pending honest charter. Then she looked down at Jonah over the stacked papers, over the broken sevens and the drowned seals and the whole submerged decade coming up at last into daylight.

"The tribunal notes the claimant's testimony was given freely, against her interest, naming her own kin." A pause. "The debt you spoke of, Miss Iversen—it isn't discharged by truth. It's only made payable. There will be inquiries. There will be restitution proceedings. Your name is in the record now, permanently."

"I know," Jonah said. "That's why I gave it."

Outside, past midnight now, the first hard frost of the season glazed the customs-house steps, and Elias waited at the bottom of them with his ruined coat and his ten years of failure finally delivered, and Jonah understood that the hardest part—the part she had never once planned for, in all her maps of escape—was only beginning: staying.
