## 10. Zero Milestone

Spring came up the valley the way forgiveness does—slowly, and from the edges first.

Jonah noticed it on the morning she walked the river road without a coat. The alders had gone green at the tips overnight, or so it seemed; winter had held the escarpment in its fist for five months, and then one morning the fist simply opened. Snowmelt ran loud in every gully. The river, which had been a gray slab of silence since November, moved again with its old insolent confidence, carrying ice the size of doors down toward the lowlands.

She stopped at the bank where it had all begun—the stretch below the trestle where the dredge lines had hauled up the iron chest two springs ago. The water looked innocent now. Water always did. It kept what it was owed and gave back what it chose, and it had chosen to give her back to herself, though at the time it had felt like drowning.

"You're talking to a river," Odile said behind her.

"I'm inspecting it."

"At dawn. In April. With your hands in your pockets." Odile came up beside her, moving carefully still, her left leg stiff from the wound at the switchback, but moving, walking, standing in the cold morning air under her own power—which the surgeon in the border town had called improbable and Odile had called none of his business. "The new charter men arrive today. You know that."

"I know that."

"And you've been standing here since before light, which means you're either avoiding them or rehearsing."

Jonah didn't answer, which was an answer. Odile let it sit between them the way she let most things sit—without hurry, without judgment, as if the world were a load that would settle properly if you just gave it time on the flatcar.

Below the trestle, the dredge crew was already working. The same crew, mostly. Tamir at the winch, singing off-key. The Ferro brothers arguing about rope angles in their usual dialect of insults and devotion. Men who had carried forged papers over a mountain for a woman they'd known less than a year, because she asked, and because Odile vouched, and because some loyalties are chosen faster than they can be explained.

"The company's dead," Jonah said. "The concession's voided. Harrow's in custody or hiding, depending on which rumor you prefer. There's nothing left to be afraid of here."

"No," Odile agreed. "There never was anything to be afraid of *here*. That was rather the point of you, when you arrived." She turned, stiff-legged, and started back toward the camp. "They'll want signatures by noon. All of us. New charter, honest paper, names in ink where anyone can read them."

Jonah stayed at the rail a while longer.

Names in ink. Three years ago she had crossed into this valley under a name that wasn't hers, with a surveyor's kit and a talent for being unremarkable, and she had told herself it was temporary—a season, maybe two, until Vessany's ghosts stopped following her. She had measured her life in miles ever since. Miles to the next junction. Miles of track surveyed, staked, certified under a name that would vanish the moment she walked away from it. Miles were safe. Miles could be counted and left behind. Years accumulated; miles dispersed.

She had learned that arithmetic from her father, though he'd have hated to hear it. Aldous Iversen had been a man of years—of ledgers and seals and the long patient corruption of a nation's paperwork—and when Vessany dissolved, everything he had signed became either evidence or inheritance, depending on who was holding it. Jonah had spent three years refusing the inheritance. Then the river had delivered it in an iron chest, and she had spent six weeks learning that evidence and inheritance are the same thing: both demand that you stand still and claim them.

* * *

The charter men arrived on the noon train, two of them, dusty and earnest, with a folding table and a strongbox of documents and the particular cheerfulness of officials who believe paperwork can redeem geography. Perhaps it could. It had certainly done the opposite often enough.

They set up in the old paymaster's office, which still smelled faintly of the company's ledgers no matter how many times the windows were opened. One by one the crew filed through. Tamir signed with a flourish. The Ferro brothers signed each other's forms by accident and had to start over, laughing. Odile signed last of the crew, pressing her name into the page with the deliberation of a woman setting a stone in a wall she intended to lean on for years.

Then the younger official looked up. "Iversen? We'll need the chief surveyor's certification. That's you."

The room did not go quiet, exactly. But it gathered itself around her, the way a crew gathers around a dangerous cut in the line—not to watch her fail, but to be present if she needed catching.

Jonah looked at the form. *Name of certifying officer.* Beneath it, a blank line, white as fresh snow, waiting.

Three years of blank lines. Three years of signing *J. Merrick* or *J. Calloway* or whatever the junction required, initials dissolving into anonymity like salt into water. She had gotten so good at it that sometimes she'd catch herself hesitating even to write the false names too neatly, as if neatness were a fingerprint.

Elias stood near the window. He had stayed through the winter—through the tribunal, through the testimony, through the long gray months after, when there was no mission left to justify him and no exile duty to pull him home, and he had stayed anyway, repairing the trestle timbers with his coat off, learning the crew's card games, learning her silences well enough to tell the tired ones from the frightened ones. He caught her eye now and said nothing. He had learned that too: when not to speak.

"They'll ask about the old permits," she said to the official. "The certifications I signed under another name. The work itself was sound—I checked every chain length myself—but the name on those surveys isn't mine."

"We know," the official said. "The tribunal's ruling covers it. Amended record, corrected attribution. That's rather why we're here." He smiled apologetically. "We're putting the valley's books in order. All of them."

All of them. Her father's forgeries, struck from the record. Harrow's bribes, entered as evidence. And somewhere in the amended pages, a line item no larger than any other: *J. Iversen, chief surveyor, re-certified.*

It was such a small thing. A name on a line. She had crossed an escarpment in a blizzard, buried a friend's blood in the snow, testified against her own father's memory in front of a tribunal with the power to deport her—it had cost her, all of it, cost her the clean fiction of her own innocence and nearly cost her Odile—and now the whole of it came down to whether she could hold a pen steady over four inches of blank paper.

Her hand was steady. That surprised her most.

*Jonah Iversen.*

The letters came out plain, neither flourished nor cramped. A surveyor's hand: precise, unornamented, meant to be read by strangers decades from now. She wrote the date beneath it and set the pen down, and found that her eyes were burning, which was absurd, which she refused to acknowledge, and which everyone in the room politely pretended not to see.

"Well," Odile said, into the silence. "Now that the lady's finished writing her memoirs, we've a line to stake by dark. The spring flood took out forty feet of the lower grade, and I don't intend to explain to the charter men why their brand-new railway is missing a section."

* * *

They worked the afternoon the way good crews do—without wasted words, without wasted motion. Jonah ran the level along the washed-out grade while Elias held the rod and called readings, and the numbers came back true, and there was a pleasure in that she had almost forgotten: the world admitting, in figures, that it could be known. That it could be trusted to stay where you mapped it, more or less, if you respected its rivers.

At the lower bend, where the grade met the rebuilt culvert, the officials had planted a marker—a cast-iron post, painted white, stamped with the charter's seal and a single numeral: **0**. Zero milestone. Everything east of it would be measured from this point.

"It's tradition," the younger official had explained, slightly defensive, as if expecting mockery. "New charter, new origin. Every distance gets reckoned from here forward."

Jonah crouched beside it in the failing light. The iron was cold and smelled of paint. Somewhere behind her, the crew was loading the tools, and Elias was saying something to Tamir that made the whole winch gang laugh, and Odile was leaning on the culvert rail with her bad leg stretched out, watching the river with the expression of a woman who had lost everything once and been given back enough.

Miles instead of years. She had lived that way so long the habit sat in her like a second skeleton. But miles need an origin. Every distance is measured from somewhere—from a zero, a marker, a fixed and chosen point—and she understood, crouching there with her palm flat on the cold iron, that she had spent three years measuring from nowhere, which is another way of saying from fear.

From here forward, then. This valley. This crew. This name, in ink, where anyone could read it.

"Jonah." Elias had come up quietly, the way he did. He nodded at the marker. "First distance on the new line. Where does it run?"

She stood and looked east, up the valley, past the trestle, past the escarpment whose switchbacks she knew better than her own handwriting, toward the pass that had nearly killed them all and now stood open and ordinary in the evening light, wearing its snow like something seasonal rather than eternal.

"Upstream," she said. "There's a tributary cutting in from the north that nobody's ever surveyed properly. Flood data says it's rising. If we don't map it, it'll take out the new grade inside five years." She smiled. "Somebody should put it on the map."

"Somebody," Elias repeated, and his voice had the warmth in it that had undone her on a ferry deck two lifetimes ago. "You're volunteering?"

"I'm certifying." She picked up her kit. "Under my own name. First time for everything."

They walked back toward camp together as the light went amber and then blue, and behind them the zero milestone stood in the dusk, small and absolute, the point from which every mile ahead would be honestly counted.

That night, in the paymaster's office with the lamp turned low, Jonah spread the blank survey sheets across the table and wrote the header in full, unhurried letters: *Valley Line Survey, New Charter — Chief Surveyor, J. Iversen.*

Beneath it, in the margin, where no official would ever require it, she added one more line, small enough that only someone looking for it would find it:

*Measured from here.*

Outside, the river ran high and honest in the dark, keeping what it was owed—and giving back the rest.
