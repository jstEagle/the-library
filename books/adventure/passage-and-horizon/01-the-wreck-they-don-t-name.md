## 1. The Wreck They Don't Name

The sea gave up its dead slowly, and it never gave up its names.

Sebastian Villalobos knew this the way she knew the pull of her own lungs—intimately, without ceremony, learned in the cold classroom of water. She had been diving since she was nine years old, when her father first lowered her over the side of a boat not much bigger than the one she owned now, and the first thing he taught her was not how to swim or breathe or read a gauge. It was this: *the sea keeps what it wants to keep, and everything else is just borrowing.*

She was borrowing from the wreck again this morning.

Dawn came gray and thin over the harbor, the kind of light that made the water look like poured pewter, and Sebastian sat on the gunwale of the *Mariana's Grace* with her fins dangling above the swell, checking her gauges for the third time. Not because she doubted them. Because the ritual of it steadied her hands, and she needed steady hands today. The wreck lay four fathoms down off the point where the old charts went blank—a deliberate blankness, ink lifted from the paper by someone with authority and no imagination—and she had maybe forty minutes before the patrol boat made its morning sweep of the channel.

Forty minutes. She'd done it in thirty-one once, when the current was kind and her luck was better than it had any right to be.

"Come on," she murmured to the boat, to the day, to whatever indifferent power kept score over drowned things. "One more time."

She spat into her mask, rinsed it, pulled it down over her face. The world narrowed to rubber and glass and the sound of her own breath. Then she rolled backward off the gunwale and let the sea take her.

* * *

The descent was always the same, and it was never the same at all.

Above, the water was green-gold and busy with light, the early sun breaking into coins that scattered across the seafloor. At two fathoms the color drained out of the world like blood from a face. At three, the cold found her—not the sharp cold of surface swimming but a deep, patient cold, the cold of things that had been waiting a long time. And at four fathoms the wreck rose up out of the murk to meet her, black and vast and silent as a cathedral.

They didn't name it. That was the first strange thing, the thing that had hooked her years ago when she'd found the gap in the charts and followed it down on a dare to herself. Every other wreck in these waters had a name, a story, a date—fishermen's gossip and harbor legend stitched over them like scar tissue. This one had nothing. No name on any chart, no buoy marking it, no mention in the registry office in town where Sebastian had once spent an entire afternoon pretending to research salvage rights while an elderly clerk sweated through his shirt and lied to her with increasing desperation. The navy had come twice after she started diving it. Both times they'd told her, with great courtesy, that there was nothing there.

There was something there.

It was a ship—or had been, once. Now it was a broken spine of hull plating and collapsed superstructure lying on its starboard side across a quarter mile of seabed, its iron bones furred with coral and anemones, its interior rooms opening onto each other like the chambers of a drowned house. She'd mapped it over three years, room by room, corridor by corridor, in the waterproof notebook she kept zippered inside her wetsuit. She knew which hatches still swung and which had fused with rust. She knew where the deck plates had rotted through and where they would hold a diver's weight if she moved like a ghost, slow and even, distributing herself across the metal the way her father had taught her to distribute weight across ice.

And she knew where the good salvage was.

Not the brass—not anymore; she'd stripped the accessible fittings in her first season, back when she was young enough to think a windfall was something you could find instead of survive into. What she took now was smaller and stranger: instruments, mostly. Gauges and dials and switchgear from compartments the navy's surveys had somehow never catalogued, copper wire run in coils off broken conduit, the occasional piece of machined hardware that made her heart beat faster than the exertion could explain, because it was too fine, too precise, too new-looking for a wreck that officially did not exist. There were whole sections of the ship sealed off behind welded plates, and she had never cut through them. Partly because cutting torches underwater attracted attention. Mostly because some animal instinct older than sense told her that the people who welded those shut had reasons, and that reasons like that did not expire.

Today she wanted the instrument compartment aft of the number three bulkhead. She'd found it six weeks ago—a narrow space behind a sprung hatch, lined with racks of mounted equipment, much of it corroded past saving but some of it, improbably, intact. Brass fittings. A bank of pressure dials under glass. And last visit, half-buried in silt beneath a fallen shelf, a signal lamp with its lens unbroken, the kind of piece that Ferro's buyers in the capital paid real money for, the kind that meant the difference between a week of eating and a week of explaining to Ferro why she wasn't.

Her fins drove her forward through the gloom. The wreck swallowed her light and gave back only what it chose: a wall of coral, a drifting curtain of silver fish, the pale question mark of a ray stirring off the sand ahead of her and gliding away into the dark. Her bubbles rose in silver strings and unraveled somewhere far above, in a world that already felt like a rumor.

At the sprung hatch she paused, checked her air—two hundred bar, plenty—and slipped inside.

The compartment was exactly as she'd left it, which was itself a small miracle. Silt hung in the water like held breath. Her torch beam swept the racks, and there it all was, waiting for her with the dumb patience of machinery: the dials, the wiring runs, the lamp. She worked fast and gentle, the way her father had taught her—*salvage isn't taking, mija, it's negotiating. You ask the wreck politely. You don't grab.* His voice lived in her hands now more than her memory. She loosened the lamp's mounting bolts finger-tight, then tool-tight, then free, easing it out of its bracket and into the mesh bag clipped at her hip. The dials came next, wrapped in cloth. A coil of copper wire, heavy and bright under its patina. Each item went into the bag with a small, private satisfaction that she refused to call hope.

Hope was expensive. She'd stopped buying it around the time the debt crossed what Ferro called *the serious figure.*

* * *

She was backing out of the compartment, bag heavy against her thigh, when she heard it.

Not a sound. Underwater, sound traveled wrong, arrived from everywhere and nowhere. What she heard was a feeling—a deep, rhythmic pressure in the water, felt in the chest and teeth before the ears. Engines. Big ones, idling down.

Sebastian went still inside the wreck, her light cupped under her palm, and counted.

Every working boat in the harbor knew the patrol schedule, the way farmers know weather. The navy cutter *Vigilante*—and yes, everyone appreciated the joke—made its dawn run along the shipping channel at first light, then swung wide around the point around midmorning on days when the tide ran high. It was barely past first light now. Either the schedule had changed, or this wasn't the *Vigilante.*

The pressure grew. Held. Began to fade toward the north, toward open water—which meant the vessel had stopped, or slowed, somewhere near the point. Near the blank place on the chart.

Sebastian's heart was doing something complicated in her chest, part fear, part fury at the fear. She killed her torch and hung in the black, breathing slow and shallow, listening with her whole body. Above her, filtered through forty feet of water and the rusted skeleton of a ship nobody named, she could almost imagine she heard the slap of hull against swell, the cough of an engine dropping to idle. Almost. The sea was generous with almost.

She gave it five minutes. Five minutes is a long time to hang motionless in absolute darkness inside a dead ship, with your own heartbeat loud as a drum and your air gauge ticking down in your mind whether you look at it or not. Her father's voice kept her company: *panic is just interest on fear, mija. Don't borrow.* She breathed. She waited. The pressure in the water faded until she couldn't be sure it had ever been there.

Then she moved.

She came out of the wreck low and fast, skimming the sand rather than rising straight up, putting the bulk of the hull between herself and the north. At the edge of the reef she finally angled upward, kicking hard, watching the light above strengthen from bruise-gray to silver. She broke the surface fifty meters from the *Mariana's Grace*, keeping the boat between her eyes and the point, and came up the ladder quietly, shedding her tank and fins with hands that only shook a little.

From the deck, she looked north.

Nothing. Open water, the long swell running in from the horizon, a few terns working the tide line. Whatever she'd felt had either gone or never been. But as she stood dripping on her own deck, wringing out her hair, she caught it—the faintest smudge of diesel smoke hanging low over the water near the point, dissolving even as she watched, the way smoke does when it has nowhere it's allowed to be.

Somebody had been there. Somebody had stopped, and listened, and gone.

Sebastian stood very still, the wet cold working through her wetsuit, and thought about the blank space on the chart, and about the clerk in the registry office sweating through his shirt, and about the welded plates she had never cut.

Then she bent, stowed her gear, and started the engine. Fear was a luxury scheduled for later. Right now she had salvage to sell and a debt that didn't care what she'd heard underwater.

* * *

Ferro's man met her at the quay the way he always did, materializing out of the dockside crowd with the unhurried certainty of a man who has never once been late for someone else's misfortune. He was small and neat and wore a shirt too clean for the harbor, and he smiled at her the way a ledger smiles.

"Villalobos." He glanced at the mesh bags as she handed them up to the quay, weighing them by eye the way she weighed them by hand. "Good morning."

"It was," she said.

He crouched, opened the first bag, and went through her salvage with quick, expert fingers—turning the dials over, tapping the glass, holding the signal lamp up to the light and looking through its lens at her, which felt uncomfortably like being appraised by a lighthouse. He set everything back down with the care of a man handling evidence.

"The lamp's good," he said. "Dials are fair. Wire's wire." He stood, brushed off his knees, and named a figure.

It was a fair figure. It was also, she calculated instantly and without letting anything move in her face, almost exactly one week. Seven days of mooring fees, fuel, food, and the interest payment that Ferro's ledgers accrued on her father's loan like barnacles on a hull. One week of not losing the *Mariana's Grace*, which was not a boat so much as the last room in the house her life had become.

"I'll want the lamp price separately," she said. "It's mint."

"The lamp price is included."

"Ferro quoted me separately last month."

"Ferro quotes what the market says." The man smiled again, apologetic and immovable, a door that was also a wall. "You know how it is, Villalobos. Everyone's money is tight this season."

Everyone's money was tight every season. She took the figure anyway, because the alternative was watching him walk away with her week, and because the quay was full of eyes that reported to Ferro for pocket money, and because seven days bought her seven days. She counted the bills twice, folded them into the oilskin pouch inside her jacket, and shook his hand, which was soft and dry and closed around hers a half-second longer than a handshake needs.

"One more thing." He said it lightly, already turning away, the way you'd mention weather. "Ferro asks—are you still diving the point?"

The quay noise seemed to step back from the question, leaving a small clean silence around it.

"What point?" Sebastian said.

"Mm." He nodded as if she'd answered, touched two fingers to his brow in something adjacent to a salute, and melted back into the crowd, leaving her standing with her empty mesh bags and the smell of bilgewater and tar, doing arithmetic she didn't like.

He hadn't asked about the wreck. He'd asked about the point—the blank place, the unnamed thing. Which meant someone had talked, or someone had watched, or the diesel smoke she'd seen at dawn belonged to someone who reported upward through channels that eventually reached men who wore shirts too clean for harbors.

Seven days. She had seven days, a boat, a bag of salvage money, and a growing conviction that the sea she'd borrowed from all her life had finally started keeping accounts of its own.

That night, in the cabin of the *Mariana's Grace*, she took down the tin box from above her bunk—the one thing aboard she'd never pawn, never sell, never show anyone—and opened it in the lamplight. Inside, on a bed of flannel gone gray with age, lay her father's compass. Brass-cased, glass cracked in one corner, the needle still swinging true to a bearing that pointed at nothing on any chart she owned.

She hadn't opened the box in two years. She didn't know why she opened it tonight.

The needle trembled, settled, and pointed north-northwest—out past the point, out past the buoys, out toward the blank place where the water held its breath.

Sebastian closed the tin, and lay awake listening to the harbor slap against her hull, counting the hours of her seven days like a woman counting stairs in the dark.
