## 2. Compass Points

The Anchor & Chain smelled of diesel, brine, and the particular despair of men who drank at eleven in the morning because the sea had already taken everything else worth losing. Sebastian pushed through its door with her boots still crusted white from the dive, salt drying in crescents along the seams, and felt the room's attention shift toward her like ballast sliding across a deck.

She counted them without meaning to. Old habit. Six fishermen playing dominoes by the window, their tiles clicking like small bones. The bartender, Marisol, polishing a glass that had been clean for an hour. Two deckhands from the *Cormorant* arguing about fuel prices in low voices. And Ferro, sitting alone at the corner table with his back to the wall, a glass of something amber and untouched in front of him, watching her the way a watchman watches weather come in off the water.

He'd been waiting. Of course he'd been waiting.

Sebastian considered walking back out. The thought lasted exactly as long as it took her to remember that the bar was the only route to the harbor office, and the harbor office held her fuel ledger, and the fuel ledger was the paper version of the chain already fastened around her ankle. Running was for people who had somewhere to run to.

She went to the counter instead. "Marisol. Coffee. Black."

"Fresh pot." Marisol poured without looking up, then lowered her voice beneath the clatter of the dominoes. "He's been here since eight. Ordered twice. Touched neither."

"He can afford to waste liquor. I can't." Sebastian wrapped her hands around the cup. The heat was almost painful against skin still numb from forty minutes at depth, and she let it hurt, let it pull her back into the morning, into her body, into the simple arithmetic of a woman standing in a room where a man wanted something from her.

Ferro waited until she'd taken the first sip. He was patient that way — had always been patient, even back when patience was just another word for the way he circled her father's debts like a gull over a fish school. Then he rose, crossed the floor with the unhurried gait of a man who owned the ground he walked on because someone else held the mortgage, and settled into the stool beside her without asking.

"Villalobos."

"Ferro."

"You look tired."

"I look employed. You wouldn't know the difference."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the memory of a smile they'd once shared across this same counter when her father was alive and the debts were smaller and everyone pretended the arrangement was friendly. That ghost died quickly. Ferro set a folded paper on the bar between them and smoothed it flat with two fingers, the way a man smooths a chart before marking a position.

"Thirty days," he said.

She didn't look at the paper. Looking would acknowledge it, and acknowledgment was the first concession. "The terms were ninety."

"The terms were ninety when your father signed them. Your father is dead. Dead men renegotiate poorly." He tapped the paper once. "Interest doesn't drown, Sebastian. It doesn't take on water, doesn't rust, doesn't go down with the hull. It just grows. You've been paying the interest for three years. You've never once touched the principal. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means you're very good at arithmetic."

"It means," Ferro said, and his voice dropped into something quieter and worse than anger, "that you are not paying off a debt. You are renting your own boat. Every week you bring me salvage money, and every week I keep the boat, and the number you owe stays exactly where it is or climbs. You're not climbing out of anything. You're treading water in a rising tide."

Marisol refilled Sebastian's coffee without being asked. Small mercy. Sebastian stared into the black surface and saw her own face distorted there, wide-eyed and strange, and beyond it the reflected window, the harbor, the masts swaying — and among them, if she narrowed her eyes, the stubby mast of the *Mariana Q*, riding at anchor where she'd left it two hours ago, holding the week's salvage in her hold and nothing else of value in the world.

"How much," she said.

"Fourteen thousand, six hundred and twenty. By the thirtieth." He said the figure the way other men said prayers — smoothly, from long repetition. "Or the boat goes to auction. And before you say it: no buyer at auction pays what she's worth. You know that. Everyone knows that. She'll go for scrap price to a breaker who'll strip her copper in a week. So don't imagine there's a version of this where you lose the boat and keep your dignity. There isn't. There's only versions where you lose less."

Sebastian turned and looked at him fully for the first time. Ferro had aged since her father's funeral — not gracefully, the way driftwood ages, grayed and split and hardened all at once. His hands were still immaculate. A collector's hands. Hands that had never hauled a line or bled on a winch, yet somehow ended up holding everything the working hands earned.

"My father borrowed from you," she said, "because no bank would touch him. Because you were the only one who'd lend to a diver chasing a wreck nobody official admits exists. And now you sit here and act like the risk was charity."

"The risk was business. The charity was letting you keep making payments after he died, when by every right I could have taken her then." Ferro's eyes went briefly, unexpectedly, to the window — to the harbor, to some point past the masts. When they came back they were flat again. "I gave you three years, girl. Three years of grace nobody else in this port would have given. Don't rewrite me as the villain because the tide came in."

"The tide came in because you opened the sea-cock."

For a moment the dominoes seemed louder. One of the old fishermen laughed at something, a dry bark, and the sound passed through the room like wind through rigging. Ferro picked up his untouched glass, considered it, and set it down again without drinking. Nerves, maybe. Or theater. With Ferro it was impossible to tell where the performance ended and the man began, and she suspected he'd lost track himself years ago.

"There's a way through this," he said. "There usually is, if a person is honest about what she has."

"I have a boat full of brass fittings and portholes. Take them."

"They're worth four hundred. Maybe five." He waved this away. "I'm talking about the thing in your pocket."

The words landed in her chest like a hook finding flesh. Sebastian kept her face still — she'd learned stillness at depth, where panic meant death and every wasted motion burned air — but inside, everything contracted.

"I don't carry valuables to bars."

"No. You carry them to pawnshops, and then you buy them back, and then you carry them again. The broker on Calle Ancha tells me you've been in four times this year. Radio. Dive knife. Your mother's ring." He said the last item gently, which was somehow more brutal. "Each time a little longer before redemption. Each time a little closer to the day you don't come back for it. I know the pattern, Sebastian. I invented half of it."

"Then you know the answer."

"I know you're down to one item you won't bring in. And I know what it is, because I knew your father, and I know what he carried, and I know what he left you." Ferro finally lifted his glass and drank, a slow deliberate swallow, buying time or spending it. "The compass."

The name sat between them on the bar like a third person. Sebastian looked at the coffee, at the window, at the masts, anywhere but at Ferro's patient, terrible face.

It was a stupid thing, objectively. A brass hand-bearing compass, 1960s military surplus, the bezel worn to bare metal where a thumb had worked it ten thousand times. It didn't run true anymore — three degrees off east, her father had shown her, tapping the crystal with a fingernail, *remember that, mija, three degrees, always three, a compass is like a man, you have to learn its errors before you trust its promises.* It was worth perhaps sixty pesos to a stranger and nothing at all to a pawnbroker, who would see only the dents and the clouded crystal and the lanyard frayed to threads.

And it was the single object in the world she would not surrender, because it was the last thing her father had touched that still pointed anywhere at all.

"Sixty pesos," she said aloud. "That's what it's worth. You'd be a fool to want it."

"I'm not going to sell it. I'm going to hold it." Ferro leaned in, and she caught the smell of him — bay rum and paper, the scent of ledgers. "Collateral. Not sale. You redeem it with the balance. Until then it sits in my safe, and you sail knowing it's there, and I sleep knowing you have a reason to come back."

"That's not how collateral works. Collateral is worth what you lend against it. Sixty pesos of collateral buys me sixty pesos of time."

"Sentimental value is also value." His voice softened into something almost kind, which was the most frightening register he owned. "You think I don't understand? I watched your father open that compass case every night in this bar, right there, third stool, checking it against the clock on the wall like it was a religion. I watched him teach you to box the points when you were nine years old, sitting on that floor, drawing the rose in spilled beer. That compass is worth more than fourteen thousand to you. We both know it. That's precisely why it works."

*Because you love it,* he was saying. *Because you love it, I can use it.*

Sebastian finished her coffee. It had gone cold without her noticing; everything in this room did. She set the cup down with great care, aligning the handle with the bar's edge, because her hands wanted very much to do something else and she was not going to let them.

"No," she said.

"Sebastian—"

"No. You can have the boat. You can have the salvage, the engine, the anchor chain, my dive gear, the clothes I'm standing in. Auction it all. But the compass stays with me, and if you send anyone aboard to take it, I will sink the *Mariana Q* in the channel where she blocks your shipping lane, and I'll do it smiling, and we'll both start over from nothing. You know I mean it. My father taught me how to scuttle a hull so it goes down fast and lands soft. It's the first thing he ever taught me, actually. Before the compass points."

The silence stretched. At the window table, the dominoes had stopped. Old men pretending to play while they listened, because in a port this small, everyone's ruin was public entertainment.

Ferro studied her for a long moment. Whatever he saw seemed to satisfy some calculation, because he sighed — a genuine sigh, tired at the bottom of it — and gathered his paper off the bar. He folded it along its creases, precise as a man folding a flag.

"Thirty days," he repeated. "Not because you convinced me. Because I choose to believe you'll find the money, and because taking the boat from a woman in front of the whole harbor costs me more than waiting does. But hear me, Villalobos." He stood, buttoning his jacket, and his shadow fell across her coffee cup. "This is the last extension. There is no fourth year of grace. On the thirty-first, I file, and the law does the rest, and the law has no sentiment about compasses."

He put a card on the bar — heavy stock, embossed, absurdly formal for a man whose office was a folding table above the chandlery. Then he paused, halfway to the door, and spoke without turning around.

"Your father owed me too, you know. Not money. Something else. Something he promised me the last week of his life, out on the water, and died before he could deliver." Now he did turn, and his eyes were unreadable as deep water. "When you find whatever it was he was so careful about — and you will, because you're his daughter — remember that I was patient with you. Remember who gave you thirty days when the world wouldn't give you thirty minutes."

Then he was gone, the door swinging behind him, letting in a slab of noon light and the smell of the harbor before it swung shut.

Sebastian sat very still. Around her the dominoes resumed their clicking, Marisol resumed her endless polishing, and the Anchor & Chain folded itself back around the small violence that had just occurred, absorbing it the way the sea absorbs everything.

"What was that last part?" Marisol asked quietly, appearing with the pot to refill a cup Sebastian hadn't touched. "About your father?"

"I don't know." Which was true, and worse than any lie. Her father had been dead three years, drowned off the wreck they didn't name, and he had left her a boat full of debt, a compass that lied by three degrees, and apparently a promise to a man like Ferro — a promise she couldn't pay, couldn't read, couldn't even find the edges of.

She pressed her palm flat against her jacket pocket, where the compass rode against her hip, small and warm from her body, its needle trembling inside toward a north that wasn't quite north.

Three degrees off. Always three degrees. Learn its errors before you trust its promises.

Outside, the tide was turning, dragging the harbor's floating debris in a slow spiral toward the channel mouth — weed, driftwood, a torn scrap of orange fabric that might have been a marker buoy or might have been something else. Sebastian watched it go and thought about thirty days, and about what a man might promise a debt collector in the last week of his life, far out where the water turned dark.

She paid for the coffee, left a coin for Marisol, and walked out into the light to check the storm glass bolted beside the harbor office door. The liquid inside had begun, very slightly, to fall.

* * *

By evening the wind had come around to the northeast and the fishing fleet had started doubling its mooring lines, and Sebastian stood on the *Mariana Q*'s deck with the salvaged portholes stacked under canvas and the compass open in her palm, watching the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, three degrees off true, faithful in its error, while the first clouds of the season built up over the headland like smoke from a fire no one had reported yet.

She was so busy watching the needle that she almost missed the other thing — the glint on the tideline, past the breakwater, where the ebb was laying out its daily offering of weed and rope and unidentifiable wreckage along the sand.

Something orange. More than a scrap.

She told herself she'd check it in the morning.
