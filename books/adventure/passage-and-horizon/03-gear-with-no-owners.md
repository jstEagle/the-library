## 3. Gear With No Owners

The storm went out with the tide, the way storms do here—no apology, no aftermath worth the name, just a sky scrubbed pale and a sea lying flat as poured tin, as if it had never raised its voice at all. Sebastian walked the beach at first light because walking the beach after a storm was not superstition but arithmetic. The sea gave things back. That was the one law she had never seen broken.

She came down the track from the harbor road with her boots laced tight and a canvas sack folded under her arm, and the sand was still dark and packed from the surge, printed everywhere with the night's argument: driftwood stacked in windrows like bones sorted by size, mats of seagrass, a fishing float from some village two hundred miles down the coast, its paint scoured to bare glass. The air smelled of iodine and wet rope. Gulls worked the tideline with their heads down, industrious as clerks.

She had done this walk a thousand times. She knew what the storm would give: planks, netting, maybe a drum of diesel if she was lucky, the small currency of a life lived off other people's losses. What she did not expect was the smell of new plastic.

It reached her before she saw anything—a clean, chemical sweetness utterly wrong against the salt and rot, the smell of a shipping crate, of an office, of somewhere sealed and air-conditioned. She stopped where the wrack line bent inland around a low shelf of black rock, and there, tucked into the shadow of the rock as neatly as if a hand had placed it, sat equipment.

Not flotsam. Equipment.

Three hard-sided cases in olive drab, latched, dry, their stenciled markings barely scuffed. A coil of orange line still bound in factory plastic. A satellite phone in a rubberized cradle, its antenna folded, its screen dark. A chart tube, capped at both ends. Everything arranged with a tidiness that made her skin prickle, because the sea did not tidy things. The sea scattered. The sea broke and bruised and sandblasted everything it touched, and these cases looked like they had been set down five minutes ago by someone expecting to come right back.

Sebastian stood very still and let the gulls make their noise around her.

She had grown up reading beaches the way other people read faces. She read this one now, slowly, and what she read made the cold start at the base of her skull and work downward. There were no drag marks. No trail of disturbed sand leading up from the waterline to the cache, no furrow where heavy cases had been hauled out of the surf. And when she circled wide, careful not to step on anything that might matter, she found the same absence on the landward side: no footprints coming in, none going out. The sand around the rock was smooth as a made bed, rippled only by wind, undisturbed in every direction for twenty meters except by birds.

The storm had thrown half a forest onto this beach. It had not left room for a man to walk without writing his passage in the sand.

*Then how,* she thought, *did the gear get here?*

She told herself the obvious answers first, because she was a practical woman and practical women try the cheap explanations before the expensive ones. Washed ashore and buried, then exhumed by the surge—except the cases weren't sand-scoured, weren't even damp inside when she finally knelt and, after a long moment of arguing with herself, flipped the nearest latch. A wave could have planted them; nothing alive had touched them since. Buried by someone who then smoothed the sand behind them—possible, if the person was patient and the tide cooperated, but the tide had been running wrong all night for any such patience, and besides, who buries government equipment on a beach and leaves it?

Because the markings left no doubt whose it was. She knew the stencil style. She'd seen it on patrol boats, on the crates the naval base trucked through town at odd hours with their labels turned to the wall. Hydrographic Survey Unit. Serial numbers. A property stamp she recognized and wished she didn't—the same stamp that appeared, blurred and official, on the notices posted at the harbor office forbidding salvage within the marked coordinates three miles east. The wreck she dove. The wreck nobody named.

Her mouth had gone dry. She wiped her palms on her thighs and opened the second case.

Charts. Rolled and sleeved, and when she drew one halfway out into the grey morning light her breath caught and held. It was her coast. Her water. The bay, the headland, the shoals she could navigate blind—and over them, in surveyor's ink, a grid so dense it looked like lace. Soundings taken at intervals far tighter than any civilian chart carried. Annotations in a hand that wasn't hers, wasn't her father's, precise and unhurried: depth corrections, magnetic anomalies flagged in red, and along one contour line, repeated like a refrain, a notation she couldn't read—numbers and letters in a shorthand that meant something to whoever wrote it and nothing to her, yet.

The third case was smaller. Locked—not latched, locked, a hasp with a combination dial worn bright at the edges from use. Military grey, heavier than its size suggested, and when she lifted it something inside shifted with a dense, deliberate weight.

Every instinct she owned told her to put it down. Every instinct she owned also told her to take it, and the second voice was older, and it sounded like her father's, and it said: *the sea gives things back for a reason, mija. Find out why.*

She took it. She took all of it—the cases, the phone, the charts, the coiled line—because leaving government property on an open beach for anyone else to find was worse than carrying it, and because her hands had decided before her head finished arguing. The canvas sack swallowed the small items. The cases she carried one under each arm and one hugged to her chest, walking fast now, glancing up the beach every few steps at the empty curve of it, the pale sky, the flat sea. Nothing moved. Nothing ever moved, on mornings like this. That was the trouble with invisibility: you never got proof it was working until the day it didn't.

* * *

She stowed the cache beneath the floorboards of *Vela's Wake*, under the bilge boards aft of the engine, in the space her father had built for exactly this kind of cargo back when the kind of cargo a man hid changed from rum to radios depending on the decade. Then she sat in the cockpit with the locked case between her feet and the harbor waking up around her—gulls, generator hum, Ferro's men already drinking coffee on the seawall like crows waiting for a roadkill schedule—and tried to think like an innocent woman.

Innocent women didn't kneel on beaches finding things. Innocent women reported finds to the harbormaster, who reported to the naval liaison, who made everything disappear including, possibly, the finder. She had watched the machinery work before, at a distance, on other people. She had no intention of feeding it.

Which left the lock.

She almost laughed when she saw the dial up close. Four numbers. She tried her father's birthday, because grief makes you superstitious and superstition is sometimes just pattern recognition wearing a shawl. Nothing. The boat's hull number. Nothing. She sat with her thumbs on the dial and let her mind go quiet, the way she let it go quiet before a dive, and what rose up out of the quiet was a memory: her father at this same cockpit table, thirty years old in her memory, turning a compass over in his hands while her mother asked him when he'd be back and he said something she hadn't understood then—

*I'll know my bearing. I always carry my bearing.*

Numbers. He'd used numbers for bearings. He'd drilled it into her until it was reflex: bearings spoken as digits, never as points, because points could be overheard and digits sounded like nothing.

She dialed three-one-five. The hasp clicked open like a held breath released.

Inside, packed in foam cut with obsessive precision, lay instruments she mostly recognized—depth gauge, a dive light, a waterproof logbook with a broken seal—and, seated in the center of the foam in a molded recess, a compass.

A brass compass in a walnut case, its glass clouded with age, its needle sleeping under a locking lever. Hand-engraved on the inner lid, in script she would have known blindfolded, by touch, by weight alone: *E. Villalobos.*

Her father's name. Her father's compass—or its twin, its double, its mirror, down to the repair scratch across the bezel at two o'clock that she had watched him worry with a thumbnail his whole life. She owned one exactly like it. It sat in her cabin drawer under a shirt she never moved, and she had pawned everything else she owned twice over rather than touch it, and here was its identical brother inside a locked government case washed up on a beach with no footprints leading anywhere.

The beach tilted. Or she did. She gripped the gunwale and breathed, and the harbor went on being ordinary around her—Ferro's men laughing at something, a launch putting out past the mole with its diesel knock carrying flat across the water—and she understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only when the world stops pretending to be simple, that the missing survey team had been looking for something. Not surveying. Looking. And that whatever they were looking for, her father had known about it, had held a bearing to it in brass and glass, and had been dead eleven years while his secret waited under the sea for strangers to come dig it up.

She closed the case. Sat with her hands flat on the lid. Felt her own pulse come back in stages, like a diver ascending too fast learning to slow down.

Below her feet, under the floorboards, the rest of the cache slept in the dark. Somewhere out past the buoys, the navy's forbidden wreck held its silence. And in her cabin drawer, under an unmoved shirt, the original of this compass waited for her with questions she had spent eleven years not asking.

Sebastian looked north along the empty beach, then east, toward the water where the charts' red-flagged anomalies clustered like a rash, and felt dread and fascination settle into her chest side by side, twins, indistinguishable, inseparable.

She picked up the compass, and the needle, freed of its lock, swung once and settled—pointing not north, but east-northeast, steady as a verdict, at something out in all that flat grey water that wanted finding.

And behind her, on the harbor road, a car door closed.
