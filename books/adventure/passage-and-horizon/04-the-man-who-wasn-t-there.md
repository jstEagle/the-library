## 4. The Man Who Wasn't There

They came for her on the third morning after the storm, when the harbor was still wringing itself out and the light came down gray as dishwater.

Sebastian was elbow-deep in the *Mariana's* engine well, scraping salt crust off the fuel line with a broken spoon, when she heard boots on her dock. Not the shuffle of fishermen or the flat slap of Ferro's men in their cheap sandals—boots, leather-soled, deliberate, two pairs, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told to leave somewhere.

She didn't look up right away. That was the first rule of being invisible: never act like something has found you.

"Sebastian Villalobos." The voice was mild, educated, pitched to carry without effort. "I'd hoped to catch you at home."

*This is my home,* she thought, and kept scraping. The spoon bit into green corrosion. Her heart was going hard against her ribs, but her hands stayed steady, because hands were the one part of her that had learned obedience. "You've caught me," she said. "I'm working."

"So I see." A pause, and then he stepped into view at the edge of her hatch—a tall man in a navy working uniform without insignia of rank anywhere except the single thin bar at his collar, which somehow made it worse. Lieutenant. Junior enough to be hungry, senior enough to be dangerous. He was maybe forty, with a face like a man who'd been handsome once and had since decided that beauty was a tool other people needed. His eyes were brown and very still, the way water is still over something deep.

He held a folder. Folders meant paperwork, and paperwork meant someone above him wanted a trail, and trails meant this wasn't casual.

"You're blocking my light," Sebastian said.

"I apologize." He did not move. Instead he crouched, unhurried, and looked into the engine well with what appeared to be genuine interest. "Perkins diesel. Older than you are, I'd guess. You keep her running yourself?"

"I keep her running."

"That's rare, these days. Most divers your age lease time on someone else's hull. Own nothing, owe no one." He smiled faintly. "Independence. It must feel like safety."

The word landed exactly where he wanted it to land, and she hated him a little for his accuracy. She set down the spoon and stood, wiping her hands on a rag that was cleaner than her hands. Up close he smelled of starch and coffee and something underneath both, faintly chemical, like the inside of a new car. Government smell.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said. "You know my name."

"Lieutenant Reyes." He didn't offer a hand. Men like him never did; a handshake was a transaction, and he preferred to keep all transactions one-directional. "Coastal Survey Administration. We're conducting interviews regarding a research team that went missing last week. Six people, government charter, last logged position within thirty miles of this harbor." He opened the folder—not to read from it, she understood, but so she could see how thick it was. How many names were already in it. "Your name came up."

"How?"

"A fishing crew reported salvage lights off the point two nights ago. Before the storm." He tilted his head. "That would be you."

"Salvage lights off the point could be anyone. Half the harbor runs night sets when the patrol boats are lazy."

"The patrol boats aren't lazy," Reyes said pleasantly. "They're scheduled. And the lights in question were at a depth of forty meters, which rules out fishing." He let that sit. Forty meters ruled out everything legal. He didn't need to say the wreck's name, and she noticed—he noticed her noticing—that he didn't say it. The wreck they don't name. Even its silence had been bureaucratized.

"I dive where the work is," Sebastian said. "If you have a specific question, ask it. If you're here to fish, the season's closed."

Something flickered behind his stillness—appreciation, maybe, or amusement, the pleasure of a man finding an opponent worth the trouble. He closed the folder.

"May I?" He gestured at her cabin door without waiting, and stepped past her into the small space that was all she owned: the bunk, the chart table, the kerosene stove, the photograph of her father tacked above the navigation desk with its corners curling. She watched his eyes find the photograph and rest there a half-second too long, and cold moved up her spine like tidewater.

"Small quarters," he said. "Efficient. You live alone?"

"I live alone."

"No family?" He was looking at the photograph again. Her father, squinting into a sun twenty years dead, holding up a fish he hadn't caught for sport but for supper.

"My family's dead," Sebastian said. "It's in the file, I assume."

"It is." He turned. "Your father was a diver. Salvage, mostly. Some contract work in the sixties and seventies that isn't well documented." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Classified work, we'd call it now."

"He hauled scrap," Sebastian said. "Like everyone. Like me."

"Mm." Reyes picked up the brass compass from the chart table—her father's compass, the one thing she'd left out because it lived there, always, its needle swinging lazily toward a north that had never once pointed true—and turned it over in his long fingers. She wanted to take it back. She made herself stand still. "Beautiful old instrument. Liquid-damped. They don't make cards like this anymore." He read the maker's mark aloud, softly, like a man reading a psalm. "And here—" his thumbnail touched the hairline seam near the bezel, the one her father had soldered shut before Sebastian was born, "—someone repaired this. Recently, relatively speaking. Unusual work. Almost like it was built to hold something."

"It holds air," Sebastian said. "That's what compasses do."

"What compasses do," Reyes agreed, "is point north. Though this one doesn't, does it? Deviation of nearly nine degrees west. Any navigator worth her salt would have corrected that or thrown it away." He set it back down precisely where it had been, aligned even to its old angle, and that precision frightened her more than anything else he'd done. "Unless the deviation was the point."

Outside, a gull screamed and was answered by another, and the harbor water slapped the hull in its idiot rhythm, and Sebastian stood in her own cabin feeling like a stranger being shown through a museum of herself.

"I don't know what you're implying," she said.

"I'm not implying anything. Implication is for people with time." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "Here's what I know, Miss Villalobos, and please correct me where I'm wrong. Two nights ago you dove a wreck whose coordinates do not appear on any chart sold to civilians. You surfaced with salvage. The following day, a government survey team of six vanished from waters you know better than any living person. Their equipment has not been recovered. Their bodies have not been recovered. There are no tracks leading away from their last camp, and no boat departed the area except yours." He said it all gently, the way a doctor delivers a diagnosis, giving each fact room to breathe. "And yet here you are, repairing your fuel line, owing money to Ferro Ocampo, planning—as far as anyone can tell—to dive again tomorrow."

The cabin felt smaller than it had ever been. She was aware of the bulkheads pressing in, the low ceiling, the porthole with its salt-hazed glass, the photograph watching her with her father's squinting eyes.

"Last week," she said carefully, "did you say the survey team went missing last week?"

"Eight days ago."

"Then I couldn't have seen them. Eight days ago I was laid up with an ear infection. Ask the pharmacist in town. I couldn't dive if I wanted to."

Reyes considered this. She watched him consider it, watched him weigh the lie against whatever he actually knew, and understood with a lurch that the lie was good—it was even true, the infection, the pharmacist, the wasted week of debt compounding—but that truthfulness was not what would save her. What would save her, if anything did, was that he had no reason yet to spend the effort of proving her wrong.

"The ear infection," he said. "Convenient."

"Inconvenient, actually. It cost me a week's income."

"And yet you dove two nights ago."

"With a bad ear? No. That was my tender running lights for squid. My deckhand's cousin. You can ask him too." Every word was smooth and hollow, a shell with nothing inside, and she marveled distantly at her own fluency. Lying, she thought, was just diving: you controlled your breathing, you watched your depth, and you never, ever panicked near the surface where the witnesses were.

Reyes looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded slowly, as if concluding a pleasant conversation about weather.

"You lie well," he said.

"I don't know what you mean."

"No. Of course not." He took a card from his breast pocket—plain cream stock, a phone number, nothing else, not even a name—and laid it on the chart table beside the compass, squaring its edges to the wood. "If you remember anything. If anything washes ashore that shouldn't. If you happen to see equipment, documents, persons." He paused at the cabin door, silhouetted against the gray harbor light, and for just a moment the pleasantness drained out of his face entirely, leaving something older and colder, a face from deep water. "The navy pays for information, Miss Villalobos. But it collects debts too. Your father knew that better than most."

He stepped out. Boots on the dock, receding, unhurried. The second man—she'd almost forgotten him—fell in beside him, and their footsteps merged with the harbor sounds and were gone.

Sebastian stood in her cabin until her legs decided to work again. Then she sat at the chart table and looked at the card, and at the compass, and at her father's photograph.

*Your father knew that better than most.*

She picked up the compass. Nine degrees west of true, forever, the deviation her father had soldered into it—or sealed into it, or hidden inside it—like a secret kept so long it had become the instrument itself. She had grown up thinking the compass was simply broken, the way her father was simply quiet, the way their life was simply poor. Now a stranger had held it in his hands and told her, in the language of bureaucracy, that it was a lock.

Through the porthole she could see the harbor, the masts, the breakwater, the whole small world she had spent her life keeping invisible inside. And beyond it, past the buoys, the open gray water where a survey team had walked off the face of the earth without tracks or bodies, and where something waited that her father's generation had been paid to bury.

Her illusion of invisibility lay around her in pieces, and the strange thing—the thing she would think about later, in darker hours—was how much of her fear was not fear at all.

It was recognition.

Because Reyes had said *your father knew*, and he had said it like a threat, but Sebastian heard it like an inheritance coming due.

She turned the compass over. Along the base of the bezel, under her thumbnail, the soldered seam sat exactly where his had touched it. All her life it had been there, and she had never once asked what was under it.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow, when the harbor slept.

But the sea, being the sea, had other ideas about timing—and three nights later, walking the tideline after the storm, she would find gear with no owners, and inside it, a second compass identical to her father's, pointing nine degrees west of true.

* * *

That evening she did not light the stove. She sat in the dark with the card in front of her, reading the number until she knew it by heart, then burned it in the galley sink and washed the ash seaward through the drain. Some messages you answer by making sure they can never be sent.

Out past the breakwater, the water lay black and patient, keeping what it kept.

She slept in her clothes, with the compass in her fist, and dreamed of her father underwater, soldering a seam shut against the dark, looking up at her through forty meters of green silence as if to say: *not yet. But soon.*
