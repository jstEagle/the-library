## 5. Stranger on the Tide

Three days of silence after Reyes's visit, and Sebastian had almost convinced herself the world had gone back to its ordinary shape: debt and diesel and the slow arithmetic of salvage. Almost. She still checked the harbor mouth every morning before she checked the sky, and she still slept with the compass in her fist like a stone she could not put down.

The fourth morning she went out to the *Mariana's Ghost* at first light to run the bilge pump and found the hatch already unlatched.

She stopped on the dock with one foot raised over the gap between concrete and gunwale, and everything in her went very quiet, the way it did underwater when a shadow crossed the sun. The latch was a brass hook-and-eye she'd replaced herself two seasons ago; she always left it hooked, even empty, even at anchor, because a boat was a home and homes had doors. It hung open now by half a hand's width, swinging gently with the swell, and from inside came no sound at all—which was worse than sound. A man hiding made noise. A man waiting did not.

She considered going back for Ferro's crowbar, or the filleting knife she kept taped under the dock boards, or the harbormaster, or anyone. But whoever was aboard had been there long enough to know her habits, and if he'd wanted her dead he'd have had three nights of chances. So instead she did what her father had taught her to do in bad water: breathe slow, move deliberate, commit.

"Coming aboard," she said aloud, to let him know she knew. "Anything sharp in your hands, put it down now."

Nothing answered. She stepped across.

The cabin smelled wrong—of salt sweat and someone else's fear, faintly of antiseptic, and beneath it the ghost-smell of government-issue canvas that she'd learned only three days ago, opening the survey team's abandoned cases on the beach. Her skin recognized it before her mind did. She took the boathook off its clips by the companionway and held it low, angled, the way you hold things you intend to use but hope not to.

He was in the aft berth, wedged into the space behind the spare anchor line where the hull curved in, and he'd made himself small the way men learn to make themselves small in places they don't own. Mid-thirties maybe, though it was hard to tell—the face was hollowed, unshaved, gray around the mouth. He wore a fisherman's oilskin two sizes too big with the name *ELVIRA* stenciled on the chest, and his hands were open on his knees, palms up, an offering or a surrender. One wrist was wrapped in a strip of bedsheet gone brown at the center.

"I heard you coming up the dock," he said. His voice was soft, almost apologetic, the voice of a man who had spent his life being careful about volume. "I meant to be gone before you came. I couldn't walk yet."

Sebastian kept the boathook between them like a rail. "You've been here three nights."

"Two and a day." He said it plainly, without flinching from how bad it sounded. "I paid for the water I drank. There's coins in the dish by your stove."

There were. Four of them, stacked neat as communion wafers. Something in her chest loosened a degree—not trust, nothing like trust, but the recognition of a certain kind of man. She'd grown up around divers and smugglers and navy men, and she knew the difference between the ones who took and the ones who counted.

"You're the one who's been watching my boat."

"Yes."

"From where?"

"The seawall, mostly. The roof of the net shed when it rained." He shifted, and she saw him suppress a flinch, the injured wrist cradled against his ribs. "I wasn't following you. I was waiting to see if anyone else followed you. They haven't. Yet."

"And that makes it better?"

"No," he said. "It just makes it true."

* * *

She made him come out onto the deck because she would not conduct whatever this was in the closed box of her own cabin, and because the deck gave her the sea at her back and the dock within jumping distance. He moved slowly, favoring more than the wrist—a hitch in the left hip, a stiffness through the shoulder—and she catalogued it all the way her father had taught her to catalogue a wreck: what's broken, what's sound, what will hold weight.

"Name," she said.

"Callum."

"Just Callum?"

"For now."

"Occupation."

A pause. Then, quietly, with the flatness of a man setting down something heavy: "Security detachment, third survey group. Hydrographic Office contract. I walked away from it eleven days ago, which makes me a deserter, which makes the next part hard to believe, so I'll say all of it and you can believe as much as you can stomach."

The harbor lay bright and ordinary around them—gulls working the fish gutting tables, Ferro's boys hosing down the pier, the smell of diesel and brine and frying doughnuts from the kiosk. Somewhere a radio played tinny music. She listened to all of it while she listened to him, because Reyes had taught her that the ordinary world could be a costume.

"There were nine of us on the survey," Callum said. "Four scientists, four security, one team lead. We were charting the shelf east of the Serrano banks. Officially." He looked out at the water the way people look at a thing they can't stop seeing. "On the fifth night we got new orders. Not from the ship—from below. A priority channel none of us had clearance to hear. The scientists went down in the submersible and didn't come back up. Then the relief pair. Then our comms officer went to check the surface buoy and didn't come back either. No distress call. No debris. No bubbles."

"Bubbles," Sebastian repeated.

"A diver who dies down there makes bubbles. A diver who's taken doesn't." He said it without drama, which was somehow the worst way to say it. "Our commanding officer told us to log it as equipment loss. Then he told us to log ourselves as equipment loss. And then the ones of us who hesitated—" he lifted the bandaged wrist, turned it so she could see the bruise circling it like a shackle's ghost, "—were helped to hesitate less. I went over the side at slack water with a float bag and swam for the fishing lights. I've been moving along the coast since. Sleeping in boats. Listening."

"Listening for what?"

"For who else knows." His eyes came up to hers, and they were tired past the bottom of tiredness, but steady. "Your name is on the salvage registry for that stretch of coast. You dive the wreck nobody names. Three days ago a Lieutenant Reyes sat in a bar with you for forty minutes, and yesterday he requisitioned the harbor records, and this morning two of his men photographed your boat from the fuel dock. So either you're working for them—in which case I'm already caught—or you're not, in which case we're both standing on the same small island, and it's sinking."

* * *

She made tea because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. She would not have admitted to shaking. It was more that the morning had tilted, the way a deck tilts when a swell comes under you from a direction you weren't watching, and pouring water into a kettle gave the tilt somewhere to go.

Callum sat on the deck plates with his back against the mast step, cupping the mug she'd given him like it was warm-blooded. Up close, in daylight, he looked worse than he had in the dim cabin—there was a healing split in his eyebrow, and the shadows under his eyes had the purple depth of a week without real sleep. But his hands were steady, and he drank the tea without asking what was in it, and he waited for her questions instead of filling silence with salesmanship. That counted for something. In her experience the guilty talked too much and the dangerous talked just enough, and she hadn't decided which he was.

"Taken below," she said. "That's what you said. Taken *below*. Below what?"

"I don't know." He said it like a confession. "I know what I saw, which is a hatch in the seafloor that isn't on any chart, and lights under it that aren't running lights, and a survey team that went down a hole and never came up. I know the orders came through a channel that outranks the Hydrographic Office, which means it outranks everyone whose name appears on paper. And I know—" he paused, and chose the words with visible care, "—that when I deserted, I took something with me. Because I thought someone outside might need it."

"What thing?"

"A copy of the last transmission from the survey lead. Dr. Okafor. Before they cut our uplink, she got thirty seconds out on the emergency frequency. Most of it was coordinates and a word repeated. I memorized it, because paper gets searched and heads don't." He recited it the way sailors recite depths and bearings, flat and exact: a string of numbers, and then, twice, a word that landed in Sebastian's sternum like a dropped anchor.

*"Villalobos."*

The kettle ticked as it cooled. A gull screamed somewhere over the breakwater. Sebastian became aware that she was gripping the edge of the deck box hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go, one finger at a time.

"That's my name," she said. Stupidly, unnecessarily. Saying it anyway, because some things have to be said aloud to become real.

"I know," Callum said. "I've known since the second night, when I heard Reyes's men say it. I didn't come to you until I was sure no one was using you as bait." He set the mug down with exaggerated care, as if the deck might resent sudden movements. "Dr. Okafor knew a Villalobos. Or knew *of* one. Whatever your family did out there—it's why they sent the survey. Someone finally read the old charts and connected them to the old names, and they sent nine people to find out what your father's generation buried."

"They didn't bury anything," Sebastian said automatically, the family litany rising in her throat—*he was a diver, he sealed wrecks, he salvaged, he died in a squall*—and then heard how it sounded, how it had always sounded, a story with its seams soldered shut against the dark. She thought of the compass in the survey case, identical to her father's, twin instruments pointing at the same invisible thing. She thought of the annotations in the logbook she hadn't finished deciphering, the numbers that matched no lighthouse and no reef.

"You don't believe that," Callum said. Not accusing. Just observing, the way you observe weather.

"No," she admitted. "I don't."

* * *

They sat with it while the tide turned, the *Mariana's Ghost* swinging slowly on her mooring, the shadows of the rigging sweeping the deck like slow clock hands. Sebastian went below once, ostensibly to check the bilge, actually to stand with her hand flat on the chart drawer where the compasses lay wrapped in oilcloth, feeling their small iron hearts through the fabric. Two needles. Two norths. Her whole inheritance balanced on a point she couldn't see.

When she came back up, Callum was watching the harbor mouth, and there was something in his posture she recognized from her own mirror: the coiled readiness of a man calculating distances to cover.

"They'll come for me eventually," he said. "Deserters get processed. Loose ends get trimmed. I figure I have days, not weeks." He turned to look at her, and made himself hold it. "So here's what I'm asking. Take me out of this harbor. North, south, anywhere beyond Reyes's reach, any port where a man can disappear into a crew list. In exchange—everything I know. The orders, the timeline, the coordinates Okafor transmitted, the names on the priority channel. Everything. And my hands. I can fix engines. I can navigate. I can dive."

"I have a boat that barely floats and a debt that floats with it," Sebastian said. "Taking a deserter aboard turns my boat from a poor man's home into evidence."

"I know."

"If Reyes stops us, we're both done. Me worse—I'd be aiding, harboring, whatever word they invent for it."

"I know."

"And you might be exactly what you look like," she said, "or you might be the leash they tied to my ankle, walking me out to deep water where it's quiet." She crouched down to his level, forearms on knees, close enough to see the exhaustion trembling in him like a struck bell. "Give me one reason that isn't your face."

Callum was quiet for a long moment. Out in the harbor, Ferro's boys laughed at something; the radio changed songs; the world went on being ordinary, which was its most convincing disguise.

"When Okafor's transmission ended," he said finally, "there was one more thing on the emergency frequency. Thirty seconds later. A different voice. Older. Bad signal, half of it lost. It said—" he closed his eyes, retrieving it, and when he spoke again his voice had gone careful, gentle, the voice you use near a wound, "—it said, *'If anyone hears this, tell my daughter the bearing was never mine.'* Then static."

The harbor sounds fell away. The gulls, the radio, Ferro's laughter—all of it dropped out like a tide going out, and Sebastian was alone in the ringing space it left, with her father's voice arriving eleven days late across forty meters of green silence, saying the thing he'd been trying to say in her dreams.

*Not yet. But soon.*

"He was alive," she said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "Eleven days ago. Down there. Alive."

"I think he'd been down there a long time," Callum said softly. "And I think whatever he helped build, he spent his life trying to keep people away from it—including you. Including, at the end, them." He opened his eyes. "I can't give you your father back. I can't even promise I'm worth the risk. All I can tell you is that the same current is pulling at both of us now, and neither of us swims well alone."

Sebastian stood. She looked north, past the breakwater, past the buoys, to where the horizon drew its clean indifferent line—the horizon she'd claimed for years she wanted, sold to herself as freedom when it was really just the far wall of a smaller and smaller room.

"One condition," she said. "We leave tonight, on the dark of the tide, before the monsoon closes the harbor. And if I tell you to jump, you jump, and you don't ask what for."

Callum rose slowly, testing his hip, and nodded once. "Fair terms."

"It's not a partnership," she said. "It's passage. Remember the difference."

But as she turned to check the fuel lines and the water and the hundred small preparations of leaving, she caught herself already thinking of him as crew, and hated how fast the loneliness in her reached for company, the way a drowning man reaches for anything that floats—even another drowning man.

Out past the breakwater, the first cloud bank of the season was building on the northern horizon, tall and blue-black and patient as a verdict.

The monsoon was coming early this year.
