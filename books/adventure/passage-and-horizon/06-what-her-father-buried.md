## 6. What Her Father Buried

The two compasses lay side by side on the chart table, and Sebastian could not stop looking at the space between them.

Her father's was the older of the pair—a brass-cased Plastimo with a cracked bezel and a lanyard worn to the color of old rope. It had hung from his neck on every dive she could remember, swinging against his chest as he worked the hookah line, and it had come home without him the day the sea took him, floating in his abandoned gear bag like a note he'd forgotten to leave. She'd carried it eleven years. She knew its weight in her palm the way she knew her own heartbeat.

The other one was identical down to the serial number.

That was what made her hands unsteady—not the resemblance but the *sameness*. Same hairline crack in the bezel, same repair solder on the crown, same faint dent at seven o'clock where it had once struck a tank valve. She turned both over. On the reverse of hers, scratched into the brass in her father's cramped hand: *S.V. — don't trust the bearing.* On the reverse of the survey team's compass, in the same hand, the same words, the same depth of cut, as if the man who wrote one had written both on the same afternoon with the same graver and the same tired eyes.

But her father had been dead for eleven years. And this compass had been sitting in a government case on a beach three days ago, wrapped in foam beside a satellite phone that still held a charge.

She sat down slowly on the bench, because her legs had opinions about standing.

*Don't trust the bearing.*

Outside, the harbor ticked and groaned through the afternoon heat. A gull argued with something invisible on the breakwater. The ordinary sounds of a place that did not know what she was holding.

She picked up her father's compass again and looked at it properly for the first time in years—not as an heirloom, not as the last piece of him, but as an object that had been *made to do something*. The bezel turned too freely; she'd always assumed wear. Now she lifted it to the light and saw the fine scratch marks inside the bezel ring, index marks cut at intervals that didn't match any compass rose she knew. Not cardinal points. Something else. A code.

And beneath the card, when she pried the cracked crystal with a diver's knife tip—gently, the way you open something that might be alive—the compass card itself was wrong. The north needle was intact, magnetized, functional. But printed faintly under the degree markings, visible only at an angle, were numbers. Columns of them. Tiny. Deliberate.

Coordinates.

---

*Flashback came the way it always did now—uninvited, triggered by nothing she could name.*

She was nine years old, sitting on the fuel dock at dusk, watching four men she didn't know load crates onto her father's boat. The men wore no uniforms but moved like they did. Her father stood apart, arms crossed, and even from thirty meters she could read his posture: not agreement. Endurance.

One of the men handed him an envelope. Her father didn't take it right away. He looked at it the way you look at a snake on a path—assessing whether it will move or whether you must step around it. Then he took it, and put it in his jacket without counting, and said something low that made the tallest man laugh in a way that had no humor in it.

Later, in the galley, she asked him what was in the crates.

"Concrete," he said. "Rebar. Chain."

"What's it for?"

He was cleaning the brass compass with a cloth, slow circles, not looking at her. "For forgetting," he said. Then he caught himself, and smiled the smile he used when he wanted a question to be over, and told her to go see if the water tanks needed filling.

She went. She filled the tanks. She forgot.

Children are built for forgetting. It is how they survive their families.

---

Sebastian spread the survey charts on the table—the ones from the sealed case, salt-stained but legible—and weighted the corners with coffee mugs. They covered the shelf waters forty miles south, the deep ground beyond the fishing banks, the kind of featureless blue paper that governments print by the thousand because most of the ocean is supposed to be empty.

Except someone had marked these. Pencil, faint, erased and redrawn so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth. A grid of soundings off the true channel, clustered around a seamount that official charts recorded as a plain rise at sixty fathoms. The cluster was dense. Obsessive. Someone had spent a long time sounding one small patch of empty water.

She fetched her father's old dive logbook from the locker under the berth—the real one, not the sanitized copy he'd kept for port authorities. He'd taught her early that every working diver keeps two books: one for the harbormaster, one for the truth. She hadn't opened the true book since the funeral. Its pages smelled of mildew and diesel and, underneath, faintly, of him—cigarettes and neoprene and the particular salt of a man who lived in the water more than out of it.

The entries from the last year of his life were different from all the others. Shorter. Coded. Where earlier dives logged bottom time, visibility, salvage recovered, these logged only dates, a bearing, and a single repeated notation:

*Seal check — holding.*
*Seal check — holding.*
*Seal check — seal 3 weeping. Patched. Paid.*
*Paid.*

Paid. That word again. The envelope on the dock. Concrete, rebar, chain.

She laid the logbook open beside the survey charts and began matching dates to pencil marks. Her father's bearing—derived from the hidden numbers under the compass card, corrected by the index scratches in the bezel, which she now understood were not decoration but a cipher key—resolved to a point six miles southeast of the seamount's charted summit. The survey team's densest cluster of soundings sat on the same point like a fly on meat.

They hadn't been exploring. They'd been *looking for something specific*, and they'd known where to look, and the only way they could have known was if the knowledge had survived her father's generation despite everything those men had been paid to do.

Her hands were cold. The harbor was hot. Both things were true.

---

*Another fragment, this one with sound attached.*

Rain on the cabin roof, hard enough to sound like applause. She was fourteen. Through the bulkhead she heard her father arguing with someone in the cockpit—old Tomas Ferro's voice, younger then, still carrying the warmth he'd later trade away for ledgers.

"You signed," Ferro was saying. "We all signed. That's the whole point of signing."

"I signed to bury it. Not to babysit it forever."

"Then walk away. Give back the money."

A long silence. Rain. Then her father, quieter, in the voice of a man admitting something that shames him: "You know I can't. You know why."

"The girl."

"The girl."

Ferro again, softer: "Then it's not yours to dig up, amigo. It never was. We moved it, we closed it, we took the money so our kids would eat. That's the bargain. You don't get to feel clean about it twenty years late."

Her father didn't answer. She heard the hatch close, heard him stand in the rain a while, and when he came below his eyes were red and he told her school had a half-day tomorrow and would she like to go out past the buoys, just the two of them, just the horizon.

They went. It was the best day of her childhood, and she had never understood until this moment why he'd needed it so badly.

---

By lamplight she worked the cipher fully, and it gave itself up easier than she expected—as if it had been waiting, as if her father had designed it to be opened by exactly one person: someone who owned both compasses, someone who knew his habits, someone patient enough to sit with grief in one hand and brass in the other.

The bezel indexes keyed to letters. The letters keyed to the logbook's coded entries. The entries resolved, entry by entry, into a set of instructions written in the flat technical language of a man who had done a terrible job well:

*Vault access: lower shaft, original airlock, seaward face. Seals one through five poured 1974. Seal six is the door. Combination held by bearing offset — see card.*

And below that, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the next three pages, as though he'd needed the pen to hurt:

*If anyone comes asking — the station is not cargo. What's inside was put there to stay there. I helped put it there. God forgive us, we were proud of the work.*

Station. Not wreck. Not cache. Station.

Sebastian sat back and the lamp flame leaned in the draft from the companionway and for a moment she saw it whole: her father at twenty-five, strong and broke, taking government money to pour concrete over something in sixty fathoms of dark water, telling himself it was salvage work, telling himself the less he knew the better, knowing anyway. Knowing enough to keep checking the seals for twenty years. Knowing enough to build a compass that could only be read by his own blood. Knowing enough to die—or to be dead—before anyone came asking.

Because someone had come asking. Four someones, with government equipment and a security detail, and they had found the vault first, and then they had walked up a beach leaving no footprints and vanished between one tide and the next.

She thought of Callum's story—*they didn't leave, they were taken below*—and made herself hold it next to the evidence instead of flinching away. Taken below. Into her father's vault. Through her father's door. Under seals her father's own hands had poured.

Grief, she discovered, has a floor. You fall through loss and loss and loss and eventually you hit something solid, and the solid thing is complicity. Her inheritance wasn't a boat and a debt and a compass. It was a crime scene, and she was standing in it, and the blood on the concrete was partly paid for with money that had fed her, schooled her, kept her father's boat afloat through every lean season of her childhood.

She got up and went out into the cockpit because the cabin had become too small to contain what she was thinking. Night had come down over the harbor. Across the water, the naval base burned its usual arrogant lights, and somewhere among them a lieutenant named Reyes was almost certainly doing arithmetic that included her.

*Don't trust the bearing.*

She'd thought it meant navigation. Now she understood it meant everything. Don't trust the easy reading. Don't trust the surface. Don't trust the men who paid, the men who signed, the men who say the matter is closed. Her father had spent his last years carving warnings into brass for a daughter he hoped would never need them, and she needed them, and they were the only honest thing anyone had ever left her.

Below, on the chart table, the two compasses lay side by side, twin keys cut by the same hand, separated by eleven years and a vanished survey team. One had come home from the sea. The other had come from the people hunting whatever the sea kept.

Which meant the survey team hadn't just found the vault.

They'd found her father's compass first—or a copy of it, or the man who made it—and they'd followed it down to the door. And whatever had happened at that door had been quiet enough, thorough enough, important enough, that a navy officer was erasing harbors to keep it buried.

Sebastian looked at the lights across the water for a long time. Then she went below, wrapped both compasses in oilcloth, and stowed them in the one place on the boat no searcher had ever found—the false bilge her father had built, it turned out, for more than smuggling diesel.

She was zipping the locker when she heard the sound: an engine, throttling down outside the breakwater, running without lights, moving the way boats move when they don't want to be counted.

It slowed as it passed her anchorage.

It stopped.
