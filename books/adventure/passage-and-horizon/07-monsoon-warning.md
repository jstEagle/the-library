## 7. Monsoon Warning

The engine outside her anchorage held its breath for ninety seconds.

Sebastian stood in the dark of her own cabin with her hand still on the zipper of the false bilge and counted. She had learned counting from her father—not numbers, exactly, but durations. Ninety seconds was how long a curious man took to decide you were nobody. Two minutes was how long a careful one took. Anything past three minutes meant someone was reading your hull line by line, learning the shape of your life from the way you sat at anchor.

At two minutes and ten seconds, the engine woke again, dropped to a low grumble, and moved on north along the moorings, unhurried as a customs inspector with all night to spend.

She let out air she hadn't known she was holding and hated herself for it. Fear was a tax, her father used to say. You paid it every time you let yourself be surprised. The trick wasn't to stop being afraid—it was to stop being surprised.

But her hands weren't listening. They shook as she finished stowing the oilcloth bundle, and they shook when she climbed into the cockpit and looked north, where the engine sound had gone, and found nothing there but the first real darkness of the season pressing down on the water like a lid.

The sky over the headland had gone the color of a bruise going bad—green-black at the base, sulfur-yellow above—and the air had that thick, sweet, metallic taste that meant the monsoon had stopped flirting and made up its mind. Out past the point, the sea had changed its voice. It wasn't speaking anymore. It was clearing its throat.

Sebastian checked her anchor rode, doubled the snubber, and went below to listen to the radio.

* * *

By midnight the harbor master's channel had dissolved into a single repeated announcement, recorded by a man who sounded like he'd rather be anywhere else: *Harbor closed. Harbor closed effective zero-four-hundred. All vessels to designated storm moorings. All movement after closure requires naval authorization.* Then, appended at the end like a signature, the detail that turned her blood to cold bilgewater: *Coastal movement restricted by order of the Regional Maritime Authority. Random inspections will be conducted.*

Random. She'd grown up on that word. In her father's vocabulary, *random* meant *systematic but deniable*. Random inspections meant they had a list, and the list had been alphabetized, and hers was on it.

She sat in the cabin with the single lamp burning low and the rain starting against the deck overhead like gravel poured from a height, and she did what she always did when the walls came in: she made lists, because lists were a way of pretending the future was a thing you could hold.

Option one: stay. Blend into the storm moorings with forty other hulls, ride out the blow like an honest woman, let them come alongside if they came, show them a boat full of fishing gear and a woman with a debt and no story worth telling. Risk: the false bilge. Risk: Callum, asleep right now in the forepeak with his boots on, a deserter from a survey team the navy officially described as *relocated pending administrative review*, which was the kind of phrase that meant the opposite of itself. Risk: Lieutenant Reyes, who interviewed her with a smile like a scalpel laid flat on a tray, who knew about the wreck, who had left his card on her chart table face-up so she'd have to move it to use the table, and who would be running this inspection the way a surgeon runs a ward round.

Option two: run. Tonight, before the closure bit down at four hundred hours, before the cordon boats took their stations, out past the buoys on a hull she hadn't hauled since before the last spring tides, with a seam weeping somewhere aft that she'd been managing with a pump and a prayer and a bucket of compound she couldn't afford more of. Into the building sea. With a man aboard whose loyalties shifted like ballast.

She got up, pumped the bilge—forty strokes before it ran dry, which was six strokes worse than yesterday—and stood in the dripping cockpit looking at the harbor lights strung along the shore like beads on a wire, and understood that option two wasn't really an option either. It was just a different way of being caught: by the sea instead of the navy. At least the navy would leave a body.

The rain thickened. Somewhere up the moorings, a halyard began slapping a mast in the wind's first true gusts, keeping time like a metronome counting down.

* * *

Callum found her at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the storm—a gray half-light with the rain coming sideways now, the harbor gone the color of pewter, the headland erased. He came aft wrapped in the blanket she'd given him, moving with that economy of his, never a wasted motion, never quite a relaxed one either. He looked at the sky the way other men read instruments.

"It's early," he said. "The season. My grandmother would've said the sea's owed something."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have gotten along with mine."

"Probably." He lowered himself onto the cockpit bench, careful of the balance, and studied her. He had a way of studying people that should have been offensive and somehow wasn't—or wasn't yet. "You didn't sleep."

"You did."

"I slept because one of us should." He said it without apology, which was one of the things she was still deciding about him. "You heard the announcement."

"Everyone heard the announcement."

"They're not random." He said it quietly, watching the water instead of her, and she felt the floor of the morning tilt. "I worked the pattern for three years. Inspection rotations, cordon placements. There's no randomness in any of it—that's the first thing they teach you, and the first thing everyone forgets. When they say random, they mean *prioritized*. And when they seal a coast this fast—" he nodded at the harbor mouth, where through the rain she could just make out the running lights of a vessel holding station beyond the fairway buoy, big enough and patient enough to be what she feared it was, "—they already know whose boat they're looking for. The rest is theater."

Sebastian watched the lights beyond the buoy. A patrol cutter, riding the swell with its bow into the weather, planted at the harbor mouth like a nail driven through a door.

"How many boats does it take to seal this coast?" she asked.

"In this weather? Six, maybe seven, spaced on the headings. Plus the cutter here to catch anything desperate enough to try the front door." He glanced at her then, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not fear, not calculation, but a kind of tired recognition, the look of a man watching a machine he'd helped maintain begin to turn toward him. "They taught me the spacing. I could tell you the gaps."

"There are no gaps. Not in that."

"There are always gaps. That's the whole secret of the sea—everything has gaps. The question is whether you can survive the gap long enough to get through it." He pulled the blanket tighter. "Or whether you wait to be found."

"And which are you recommending?"

He was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed on the canopy. Beyond the breakwater the swell was starting to stand up, long and green and organized, the sea rehearsing.

"I'm recommending," he said slowly, "that you ask yourself what happens when they come alongside. Because they will. Maybe today, maybe tomorrow, but they'll work the moorings boat by boat, and yours is on whatever list Reyes is carrying in his breast pocket. And when they come, I'm aboard. A deserter off a vanished team, sleeping in your forepeak. You can't talk your way around me. I'm not salvage, Sebastian. I'm evidence."

She had known this, of course. She had known it since the night she'd caught him crouched among her spare lines with his hands up and his voice soft as an apology. But knowing a thing and hearing it said aloud in the rain with a cutter parked on your horizon were different economies entirely.

"So I put you ashore," she said. "Before they come."

"Put me ashore where? The harbor's closed. Every jetty will have a man on it by noon, checking faces against a list that has my description on it—height, build, a scar I'd rather not explain." He almost smiled. "You'd be delivering me. And then they'd ask you why, and where you found me, and what else you've found lately. And you'd lie, because you lie well—I've watched you—but Reyes doesn't conduct interviews. He conducts dissections. He'll take everything you say apart and lay it out on the table and make you look at it."

"You talk about him like you know him."

Something crossed Callum's face then, quick as a fish under a boat, gone before she could name it. "I know the type," he said. "The service is full of them."

It was the second time he'd lied to her. She was almost sure it was the second time. The trouble with a man whose loyalties kept shifting was that you could never get a clean bearing on him—you'd fix on something true-looking and then the current would take it and you'd realize you'd been navigating by a floating log.

* * *

The storm arrived at midday the way debts arrive—all at once, though you'd seen it coming for weeks.

The wind backed around to the northeast and built in the space of an hour from a nuisance to a fist. The harbor closed itself behind a curtain of rain so dense the far shore simply stopped existing. Boats strained at their storm moorings, and the whole anchorage began that groaning chorus of creaking hawsers and clanking hardware that Sebastian had slept inside her whole life and had never once gotten used to. The barometer on her bulkhead—the brass one, her father's, its needle trembling like a compass near iron—had fallen past the numbers the scale bothered to print.

And through it, on the harbor channel, the inspections began.

She listened on the VHF with the volume low while she pumped the bilge and re-lashed everything that could be lashed. The traffic was procedural, clipped, official: *Storm mooring fourteen, boarding party coming alongside, have your papers ready. Storm mooring twenty-two, prepare to receive inspectors.* Boat by boat. Methodical. Starting at the north end of the moorings and working south, which meant working toward her, which meant someone had drawn a grid and her grid square had a number.

Callum stood in the companionway behind her, listening too. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the radio wasn't already saying better.

At mooring thirty-one, a fisherman named Okonkwo argued with somebody about his papers, his voice rising through static until it cracked, and then there was a long silence, and then his voice again, flat and obedient, and Sebastian's stomach tightened around nothing. She knew Okonkwo. He fished honest and drank honest and had never in his life had anything on his boat worth arguing about. If they were pulling his boat apart, they were pulling everyone's apart.

"They're not searching," Callum murmured. "Listen. They board, they take twenty minutes, they clear. That's not a search, that's a census. They're counting who's here and who isn't." His jaw worked. "Which means the search comes later. After the storm. When they know exactly which hulls matter."

"Then I have until the storm ends."

"You have until the storm ends," he agreed. "Or until they reach your mooring tonight, whichever comes first. They won't stop for weather—they love weather. Weather is cover for them too."

Sebastian turned off the radio. In the sudden quiet the storm filled the boat, vast and indifferent, and she stood at the chart table with her palms flat on the wood and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the full weight of the corner she was in. Ten seconds. Her father's rule. Feel it fully, once, with a clock on it, and then get to work.

Then she opened the chart drawer.

* * *

The chart of the coast was old—her father's hand on it, his pencil annotations faded to ghost-gray—and she spread it under the lamp and weighted the corners with the two compasses still in their oilcloth, side by side, twins except for thirty years of wear. Outside, the wind screamed through the rigging in a register that made her back teeth ache.

North of the harbor, past the second headland, there was a river mouth. On the chart it showed as a broken blue line threading inland through mangrove country—shallow, shifting, unmarked by any channel buoy for fifteen years, because nothing bigger than a dugout had bothered with it since the logging died. Her father had penciled a single word beside it, decades ago, in the shorthand of a smuggler's son: *usable, 1.2m at high, locals friendly, don't trust the middle bar.*

A leaking boat could do 1.2 meters. A desperate one could do less.

If she ran seaward, out past the buoys, she ran the cordon gauntlet in open water with a cutter sitting on the front door and six more boats strung along the headings like beads on a wire. But if she went the other way—if she went *in*, up the river, into the mangroves, and tucked the boat into some creek where the charts themselves had given up—then the cordon became a fence with a hole in it, because no one seals a swamp. Navies sealed harbors. Swamps you had to walk into, and walking was slow, and slow was time, and time was the only currency she had left.

It was a bad plan. It was a plan built on a pencil note thirty years old and a river that rearranged itself every flood season and a hull that was taking water faster every day. It was exactly the kind of plan her father would have loved, and her father had drowned, and she tried not to draw conclusions from that.

But it was a plan, and it beat standing still to be counted.

"We don't go to sea," she said to Callum, without turning around. "We go up. The Marenko River, or whatever's left of its mouth. Mangrove country, forty miles of creeks no chart can find twice. We hide the boat and wait out the season's opening act."

She heard him come up behind her, heard him look at the chart over her shoulder. For a long moment he said nothing, and she could feel him doing the same arithmetic she'd done—depths, distances, the leak, the dark, the man-shaped risk sleeping eight feet away from her every night.

"The middle bar," he said finally. "Your father wrote don't trust the middle bar."

"My father wrote a lot of things. Half of them saved my life."

"And the other half?"

She looked down at the two compasses weighting the corners of the chart, twin needles trembling under the lamplight, both pointing the same way now, both pointing at the same nothing out in the storm-dark where a drowned station held its secrets and a lieutenant held his list.

"The other half killed him," she said. "So we'll trust the river and nothing else. We go tonight, on the top of the tide, before they finish their census and start their search. And Callum—" she turned, and made herself look at him straight, because this part mattered more than the river, more than the bar, more than the storm, "—if you're leading me into something, if there's a version of this where I sail us into a trap, I want you to know that I've already decided what I'll believe. And what I'll do."

The wind chose that moment to hit the boat broadside, and the whole hull leaned, and the lamp swung, and their shadows wheeled around the cabin like birds.

Callum held her gaze without flinching, which was either honesty or very good practice.

"Fair," he said. "Because I've been deciding the same thing about you."

Outside, through the rain, at the edge of hearing, a new sound joined the storm: the deep-throated bellow of the cutter's horn at the harbor mouth, three blasts, long and short and long. Not a warning. An announcement.

Boarding parties were crossing to the south moorings.

Hers was next.
