## 8. Out Past the Buoys

**Sebastian**

The harbor closed at dusk like a fist.

She'd watched it happen from the wheelhouse of the *Mariana's Ghost*, her father's boat, her father's name still flaking on the transom in paint gone the color of old bone. First the harbor master's launch, running its slow circuit with a loudhailer announcing curfew in two languages. Then the boom across the channel mouth, a chain of orange buoys and steel cable sagging under its own weight, lit at either end by patrol boats riding their anchors with their deck lights burning. Beyond them, out past the headland, the sea had gone the color of a bruise. The monsoon was coming. Everyone could feel it in the way the gulls flew low and silent, in the way the water slapped the hull without rhythm, like something impatient.

"They'll search every boat before they let anyone through," Callum said from the companionway. He'd been sitting there for an hour, knees drawn up, watching her work with that stillness he had—the stillness of a man who had learned to be furniture. "They did it at the last port. Every bilge, every locker. They're not looking for contraband."

"I know what they're looking for." She tightened the last hose clamp and wiped her hands on a rag already black with grease. The engine compartment smelled of diesel and salt rot, the smell she'd grown up inside, the smell that meant home more than any house ever had.

"You can't run," he said. Not accusing. Just laying it out, the way you'd lay out a chart. "Not tonight. They'll have thermal on the water. You'll be a candle in a dark room."

"Then I'll be a candle they never find." She climbed out of the engine space and stood, working feeling back into her knees. "You want to stay and be found, there's the dock. Reyes is probably ashore right now drinking coffee and being polite to someone."

He didn't answer that. He looked instead at the compass case lashed under the chart table, wrapped in oilcloth, and she saw him do the arithmetic she'd been doing for three days—what it weighed against what it cost.

"The tide turns at eleven-forty," she said. "The patrol boats change watch at midnight. There's a gap. Ten minutes, maybe twelve, when the eastern picket swings wide to clear the fishing fleet's moorings. My father used to run cargo through that gap. Before I was born."

"And if the gap closes early?"

"Then we learn to swim fast."

* * *

**Callum**

He had deserted once before, though no one had ever used that word for it. In his mind it had always been *leaving*, the way you leave a room where a conversation has gone wrong. But the navy had words for everything, and theirs was desertion, and the penalty was measured in years.

So he knew what it felt like to sit in the dark waiting for a moment that might not come. He sat in the cockpit while Sebastian killed every light aboard and let the *Mariana's Ghost* drift on the flood tide, engine off, just another shape among the moored fishing boats, indistinguishable unless someone came close enough to touch. Rain began—not the monsoon yet, only its herald, a fine cold needle-rain that beaded on his neck and made the whole world whisper.

Sebastian steered by hand, standing in the cockpit with one hand on the wheel and the other on the throttle housing, her face a pale smudge beneath the hood. She hadn't spoken in forty minutes. He'd watched her navigate since they left the mooring: three bearings taken by eye alone, off a church spire, a refinery flare, a radio mast blinking red on the ridge. Her father's daughter. He thought about the compasses again—the two of them lying side by side in that case, identical down to the worn brass of the bezel—and about what he had not told her, which was nearly everything.

The eastern picket boat appeared exactly as she'd said it would, a smear of white light swinging wide toward the fish moorings, its searchlight playing lazily across the water like a man looking for lost keys. Sebastian counted under her breath. At some number only she knew, she opened the throttle.

The engine caught on the second turn and the boat came alive beneath them, bow lifting, spray suddenly real and cold. They ran dark past the first buoy close enough that Callum could have reached out and touched the wet orange curve of it. Past the second. The channel opened ahead, black water and blacker sky, and for eleven seconds he allowed himself to believe.

Then the engine coughed.

It coughed again, longer, a wet mechanical choking, and the boat began to lose way, and behind them the picket boat's searchlight stopped moving.

"No," Sebastian said, very quietly, the way people say *no* to death and weather. "No, no—" She was already down the companionway ladder, flashlight clamped in her teeth, and Callum heard her open the engine compartment and heard the sound she made next, a short exhalation that was worse than screaming.

Water. He could hear it in the engine note even from above—or the absence of engine note. He went down after her.

The bilge was flooding. Not fast, but steadily, seawater welling up around the engine block, and the raw-water intake hose had split along a seam the salt had been eating for years. The pump was running but losing. Sebastian had the hose in both hands, trying to pinch the flow closed with her fingers, sea water spraying her face, and she was saying something under her breath in a language he didn't know, rhythmic, like prayer or cursing, possibly both.

"Spares?" he shouted over the wind coming down the companionway.

"Under the berth. Green box. If my father labeled anything right in his life, it's that box."

He found it, hauled it out, wrenched the lid. Inside: hoses, clamps, a hand pump, a roll of self-amalgamating tape gone hard at the edges. And beneath all of it, wrapped separately in plastic, a wooden-handled tool he recognized instantly, because he'd spent four months of his life watching men use them in places men weren't meant to go.

A pipe patch kit. Military issue. The kind carried by underwater engineering teams.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he took it up on deck into the rain, and he went to work.

* * *

**Sebastian**

She watched him do it and understood, with a cold settling feeling, that she was watching something she should have seen days ago.

He worked fast and without hesitation, the way her father had worked, the way only men who had done a thing ten thousand times could work. He drained the intake line, cut back the split section with the knife from his boot, sleeved it with rubber and wound the amalgamating tape under tension, spiraling it tight, each overlap exact. His hands never shook. The rain hammered down and the searchlight swept somewhere behind them and Callum knelt in the flooding bilge of a stranger's boat repairing a seawater system with the calm of a surgeon, and Sebastian Villalobos, who had survived on her ability to read water and weather and men, read him at last.

"You're not security detail," she said.

He didn't look up. "I was attached to the survey team. That part was true."

"That's not what this is." She nodded at his hands. "Security contractors carry guns. This—" she gestured at the patch, at the whole economy of his movements— "this is salvage diving. Commercial. Navy underwater engineering, maybe. You've done saturation work. Deep work." The word arrived in her mouth before she could stop it, and when it arrived it brought its shadow with it. "Deep work on *what*, Callum?"

The tape screamed as he tore the end free. He pressed the patched hose home, seated the clamp, and only then looked up at her, rain or bilgewater dripping from his chin.

"Start the engine," he said. "Please. We're dead in the water until you do."

She climbed up, turned the key. The engine turned over, coughed, caught—ran rough, then smoother, the patched line holding, water circulating where it should. The boat gathered way again and swung its bow toward the open sea, and the searchlight behind them swept past a hundred meters astern and kept going, and they were through, out past the buoys, out where the cordon was a rumor of lights on a distant shore.

Sebastian put the boat on heading and set the autopilot and came back down into the engine space, where Callum was coiling the tape roll with those same precise hands.

"Sit down," she said, and pointed at the bench across from her, and he sat.

Outside, the first true voice of the monsoon made itself known—a long rolling growl of thunder out over the water, and the rain thickened from needles to nails. The hull ticked and groaned around them. She left the flashlight off. It was better in the dark; faces were easier to lie to in the light, and she wanted nothing lied to tonight, least of all herself.

"My father dove on things the government paid him to forget," she said. "I've known that since I was nineteen. I thought it was wrecks. Cargo. Maybe war graves nobody wanted named." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "You patched that hose like a man who's been below on a station. A structure. Something built, not sunk. So tell me what my father sealed, Callum. All of it. Because I'm sailing my whole life into whatever it is, and I will not do it on credit."

For a long time he said nothing. The thunder rolled closer. Through the hull she could feel the sea changing, the long swell of the open ocean arriving beneath the harbor chop, the boat beginning to breathe the way boats breathe when they're finally where they belong.

"There were men living in it," he said finally. "When the survey team got inside. That's where I start, because everything else comes after that and none of it makes sense without it."

"Living in what?"

"The vault. Your father's coordinates. It's not a cache, Sebastian. It's a station. Built during the war—listening post, hydrophone arrays, records vault, the whole architecture of a country's paranoia poured into concrete and sunk past the reach of anyone who might come asking questions. Your father's generation welded the doors and took the money and kept quiet. That part's true too." He rubbed his hands together, warming them, or maybe trying to get the memory off them. "But the survey team found a way in. And inside, in the dry sections, there were men. Old men. Descendants, maybe, of whoever was stationed there—no one could prove it, but they had generators, they had stores, they had *children's drawings on the walls*. They'd been down there for decades, growing up in the dark beside all those records, and they didn't know what the records were. They thought the station was the whole world."

Sebastian sat very still. Somewhere above them the rain was doing its work on the wheelhouse glass, and the autopilot held the heading her father's hands had taught her, north by northeast, out past everything.

"What happened to them?" she asked, though some part of her already knew, the way you know a wave is going to break before it breaks.

"The team reported them. Wrote it up. Photographed everything." Callum's voice had gone flat, the flatness of a man reciting evidence he'd tried for months to stop hearing. "And Lieutenant Reyes's orders came down within the week. Not rescue. Not relocation. The report was classified, the access shaft was resealed, and the survey team was reassigned so thoroughly that six of us ended up scattered across three commands and one of us—" he stopped, started again— "one of us didn't end up anywhere at all. Officially, the team never existed. Unofficially, everyone who knows why is either bought or buried."

"And you ran."

"I ran." He met her eyes in the dark, and there was no shame in his face, only a tired, stubborn honesty that she found herself wanting to believe the way a drowning woman wants driftwood. "Because I helped seal that shaft, Sebastian. I stood on the deck and watched the welders go down and I told myself it wasn't my war, it wasn't my country, it wasn't my problem. And then I couldn't sleep anymore. That's the whole of my desertion. Conscience, at four in the morning."

The thunder passed overhead and away, and in its wake the rain eased to something steadier, almost gentle. Sebastian became aware that her hands were shaking, and folded them.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because you asked. Because you patched your father's boat with your father's hands and you deserve to know what he actually buried." He paused. "And because there's a piece I'm still holding, and I need you to trust me with it a little longer. One day. Maybe two. When we're clear of the coastal traffic, I'll give you the rest—all of it, including the part about your father's last dive."

Her breath stopped. "*His last dive.*"

"He was there, Sebastian. Three years ago. He tried to warn the survey team off, and Reyes knows it, and that's the real reason Reyes has been so patient with you. He doesn't think you have a compass." Callum's voice dropped. "He thinks you have your father's *notes*. And he's right."

The boat rose on a swell and settled, rose and settled, patient as breathing. Out past the buoys, out past the cordon, out past the edge of the life she'd been clinging to, Sebastian Villalobos sat in the dark of her father's engine room and made the only decision available to her.

"One day," she said. "Then everything. No more credit."

Callum nodded slowly. "One day. Then everything."

Above them, the monsoon arrived like a door closing, and the *Mariana's Ghost* ran north into the heart of it, carrying its two passengers and its drowned secrets toward the coordinates that waited at the bottom of the world.
