## 9. The Leaking Boat

The fishing station appeared out of the rain like something remembered rather than seen.

Callum had been at the wheel for six hours, hands numb on the spokes, when Sebastian's voice cut through the drum of the storm: "There. Starboard. The old cannery pier." He turned his head and there it was—a black shape against a slightly less black sea, the skeletal arm of a loading crane reaching over the water like a man drowning and grasping at nothing.

The *Mariana's Ghost* was dying by inches. Callum had known it since the second hour, when the bilge pump started its laboring cycle—running longer, resting shorter—and Sebastian had gone below with a flashlight clenched in her teeth and come up with her face gray. "We're making water faster than she's making way," she'd said, which he understood to be the nautical equivalent of a death sentence delivered politely.

Now the station rose off the port bow: three buildings on stilts above a shingle beach, their roofs peeled back to the ribs, a rusted winch house leaning seaward as if listening for something under the water. Behind them, hills folded into cloud. No lights. No smoke. Nothing living that Callum could see, though he had learned in the last two years that nothing living was not the same as nothing.

"Can you make the pier?" he asked.

Sebastian didn't answer right away. She stood braced against the wheelhouse frame, salt crusted white along her jaw, watching the water between them and the pilings with an expression Callum was beginning to recognize—the look of a woman doing arithmetic with her own survival.

"I can make the pier," she said finally. "Whether the pier can make us is another question."

* * *

It took four passes. The first two, the wind shoved them off like a hand on the chest; the third, a swell lifted the stern and swung them broadside to the pilings, and Sebastian swore in a language Callum didn't know and put the bow into the wind and tried again. On the fourth pass he went forward with a coiled line, waited until the crane's shadow swept over him, and threw. The line caught the bollard on the third try, and he hauled while Sebastian fed reverse in stuttering bursts, and somehow—by friction and fury and the sea's brief indifference—they were alongside, and then they were tied, and then it was over and his arms were shaking so badly he couldn't let go of the rail.

The hull had taken a beating somewhere in the cordon run. Below, in the lamplight, Sebastian found it with her palm before her eyes found it: a seam just aft of the keel where the caulking had worked loose, weeping a thin silver thread of sea. Not a gash. A wound that would heal if it were cleaned and bound.

"She'll hold," Sebastian said, and Callum heard what she didn't say, which was *because I will make her hold, because she is the last thing my father gave me and I have buried one parent already and I will not bury this too.*

They worked through the worst of the afternoon. Sebastian drove the boat onto the shingle at high water so the seam came clear at low, and while the tide fell away beneath them she scraped old oakum from the joint with a marlin spike, her movements quick and certain, a surgeon who had performed this operation a hundred times on herself. Callum held the light. It was, he thought, the most useful thing he had done in months, and the thought sat in him like a stone.

By dark the seam was packed and payed over with tarry compound, a sheet of canvas nailed across the planks inside, and the bilge pump ran for the first time in hours and found almost nothing to say. They ate cold rice and tinned fish in the wheelhouse, wrapped in blankets that smelled of diesel and mildew, and listened to the monsoon work itself into a frenzy offshore. It sounded like the whole ocean clearing its throat.

"You should sleep," Sebastian said.

"So should you."

"One of us has to keep watch."

"Then we'll both keep watch." He said it simply, without argument in it, and after a moment she looked at him—really looked, the way she rarely did, her eyes going over his face as if reading small print—and nodded once, and that was the closest thing to trust either of them had exchanged since the night he'd crawled out of her fish hold smelling of mackerel and desperation.

* * *

The station had been abandoned long enough that the sea had begun taking it back room by room. In the largest building—the old processing floor, where the stilts kept the boards dry above the tideline—there were rusted gutting tables in rows, hooks still hanging from chains overhead, and a wall covered in chalk tallies faded to ghosts. Someone had counted fish here for forty years, Callum thought. Ten thousand mornings of counting, and now the numbers meant nothing to anyone.

He found the stove in the corner office, a squat iron thing built for burning anything, and by the time Sebastian came looking for him he had a fire going with driftwood and a crate of ancient tarred rope, and the room was filling with heat and a smell like pitch and old smoke.

"This is theft," Sebastian said from the doorway, but she was already shrugging off her wet jacket, and there was no conviction in it.

"The owners are dead or rich," Callum said. "Either way they won't miss it."

She lowered herself onto an overturned crate near the fire, close enough to feel it, far enough to pretend she wasn't seeking it. Outside, the rain came down in ropes. The building groaned around them, timbers shifting, settling, alive in the way old wooden things are alive.

For a long time neither of them spoke. Callum watched the fire and felt the confession rising in him the way the tide rises—slowly, irresistibly, filling every low place. He had been carrying it since the survey boat. Longer, if he was honest. Since before the survey boat, back when he'd signed the security contract because the pay was triple what a farm boy from the northern islands could earn anywhere else, and told himself the secrecy clauses were standard government paperwork and not what they actually were: a lid.

"Ask me," he said.

Sebastian looked up. "Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not asking me since the harbor." He kept his eyes on the fire. "You want to know why a deserter runs to a stranger's boat instead of inland. You want to know what happened to the others. You've been polite about it, which is worse than asking. So ask."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a piece of the station's roof tore free and clattered down the shingle.

"What happened to them?" Sebastian said quietly. "The survey team. Reyes says they never existed. The navy says there was no team. But you were on it."

"Yes."

"And the others?"

Callum took a breath, and it hurt, the way the first breath always hurts when you've been holding it for a long time.

"There were eleven of us on the security detail," he said. "Six scientists, two technicians, a mission commander named Okafor, and me and ten other men whose job was to stand at doors and not ask questions. We went out on the *Corvus*—you'll find no record of the *Corvus*, don't bother—to coordinates that weren't on any chart I'd ever seen. Okafor had them written on a card in his breast pocket, and he checked that card maybe fifty times a day, like a man checking whether his heart was still beating.

"We anchored over open water. That's the first strange thing. No island, no shoal, nothing. Just a depth reading that made the sonar tech go quiet and stay quiet for the rest of the day. And then the divers went down, and they came back up, and the mission commander looked at what they brought up—photographs, mostly—and after that everything changed. After that we stopped being a survey and started being a quarantine."

Sebastian had gone very still. The firelight moved on her face. "Quarantine of what?"

"Not of what." Callum turned to face her fully now, because this part deserved a face. "Of who. There were men down there, Sebastian. Living men. Inside the structure."

The word *structure* landed between them, and he saw her understand it—not the vault, not yet, not the name for it—but the shape of it, the impossible fact of rooms under the sea with people in them.

"They'd been down there for years," he went on, and his voice was steadier than he felt, the way a rope is steady right up until it snaps. "Not prisoners exactly. Custodians. Their fathers had been custodians before them—it was inherited, the way your father's compass was inherited, except what they inherited was a job with no end. Keeping the systems running. Keeping the air scrubbers cycling. Keeping the records dry. They came up to the surface station once a season for supplies, and the supply runs were handled by civilians who didn't ask questions, and the whole arrangement was older than everyone involved. Some of those men had been born down there. One of them—his name was Ede, he was maybe sixty, with hands like tree roots—he shook mine and said, *You're the new tide, are you? We get a new tide every few years.* Like I was weather."

Sebastian's hand had risen unconsciously to her collarbone, where Callum knew the compass hung on its cord beneath her shirt. Her father's compass. The twin of the one in the sealed case.

"And the survey team found them," she said.

"The survey team was sent to find them. Or to find what they were keeping. Okafor knew more than he said—I think he'd known for years, from the inside, and this mission was his way of getting eyes on it officially, getting it documented, getting it *real*, so it couldn't be disappeared again. He was a good man. The kind of good that gets people killed." Callum's throat tightened. "The divers brought up photographs of the interior. Corridors. Racks of magnetic tape going back decades. Listening logs—hydrophone arrays, traffic analysis, intercepts. That station wasn't built to hide treasure. It was built to listen, and it never stopped listening, and everything it heard is still down there on tape. Every ship, every radio call, every conversation some government wanted private, for forty years. Names. Dates. Signatures. Enough to hang half the officials on this coast from their own words."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Neither of them fed it.

"And Reyes," Sebastian said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Reyes wasn't on the *Corvus*. Reyes was ashore, running the shore side—the cordon, the communications, the part of the operation that existed to make sure nothing reached land. When Okafor transmitted his preliminary findings, encrypted, official channels, by the book—" Callum stopped. Started again. "There was a reply. Not from the naval directorate. From Reyes, personally, relayed through a channel that shouldn't have existed. It ordered the mission suspended and the team retained aboard for debrief. *Retained.* Okafor read it twice and then he said, very calmly, *Gentlemen, we are no longer a government expedition. We are evidence.*"

"And then?"

"And then the *Corvus* stopped transmitting." Callum looked at his hands. They were steady. He'd trained them to be steady even when nothing else was. "I was on the night watch. I heard the boarding alarm—someone was coming out from the coast, fast, no broadcast, no lights. Okafor ordered the divers' records distributed, hidden, scattered—one copy to each of us, do you understand, he broke the evidence into pieces and handed it to enlisted men because he trusted us more than he trusted rank. Then he ordered anyone who wanted to go into the water could go. I went. Four of us went. I swam away from my own ship in the dark, and behind me I heard the hatches, and then I heard nothing, and I have been hearing nothing ever since, and the nothing is louder than any sound I've ever known."

The rain filled the silence. Down the beach, the surf worked the shingle with a sound like slow applause.

"The others," Sebastian said. "The ones who stayed."

"I don't know." He made himself say it plainly. "I tell myself some of them got away in the skiff. I tell myself the *Corvus* was scuttled and the crew dispersed with threats instead of bullets, because Reyes is careful, because bodies float and paperwork doesn't. But I watched that man's face once, in the harbor, when he thought no one was looking, and I'll tell you what I saw. I saw a man deciding something. And whatever he decided, he decided it about people."

Sebastian rose. She walked to the broken window at the front of the office, where the glass had long ago become a memory, and stood looking out at the black water, and Callum watched her shoulders and understood that she was not thinking about the survey team at all. She was thinking about her father. About a compass annotated in a dead man's hand. About what kind of man signs a contract to seal a door under the sea and then spends the rest of his life teaching his daughter to read water.

"My father dove on wrecks the navy doesn't name," she said, without turning around. "My whole childhood, he'd disappear for weeks and come back with money that didn't match the salvage. I thought he was smuggling. Everyone thought he was smuggling. Maybe he was. Or maybe he was being paid to forget, and the forgetting paid better than the smuggling, and he hated himself in a way he could never explain to a child." She turned. The firelight caught the wet in her eyes and she didn't wipe it away, didn't hide it, and Callum understood that this, too, was a kind of confession—that she was letting him see her grief with its skin off. "And now I'm sitting in a ruin holding his compass, and it opens the same door he helped close. What does that make me, Callum?"

"It makes you the tide," he said. "Ede's word. New tide, every few years. The sea sends someone eventually. It sent you."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be true." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the fire painted them both orange and shadow. "Here's the rest of it, since we're trading histories tonight. I didn't come to your boat by accident. I'd been in that harbor three weeks, watching, because the compass in that case—the one you pulled from the survey cache—is a key, and keys get reported missing, and I needed to know who'd find it. When I saw it was you, Villalobos, I nearly laughed. Half the old divers on this coast still whisper the name. Your father's generation built the lock. His daughter found the key. If that's accident, then the sea has a sense of humor, and I've always suspected it did."

Sebastian stared at him. Outside, the monsoon screamed against the tin that remained of the roof, and inside, the fire settled into embers, and the distance between them—which had been the width of a fish hold, the width of a lie, the width of two strangers sharing a wheelhouse—closed to the width of the truth.

"You could have told me at the harbor," she said.

"I could have. And you'd have put me ashore, or worse, and you'd have been right to. People who carry secrets alone survive longer." He shook his head. "But I'm tired of surviving, Sebastian. I want to be *right* about something before I'm finished. So now you know everything I know. The men below. The tapes. Reyes. Okafor's card in his pocket, checked fifty times a day. All of it. Do what you want with me."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she crossed the room, took the blanket from her own kit, and dropped it over his shoulders—the gesture brisk, almost rough, a sailor's tenderness, practical as a cleat.

"Sleep," she said. "Both watches can start tomorrow. Tonight nobody's coming through this weather, and if they are, we'll hear them drown first."

She lay down on the gutting table nearest the fire, boots on, coat rolled under her head, and within minutes her breathing slowed into sleep with the ease of someone who had learned to take rest wherever it was offered, because it rarely was.

Callum stayed awake. He watched the embers and listened to the rain and thought about Ede's root-hard handshake, and Okafor's card, and the four swimmers who had gone into the dark with him, of whom he was, as far as he knew, the only one still breathing. Confession, he discovered, did not feel like relief. It felt like setting down a weight and immediately feeling the absence of the weight as its own burden—his hands empty, his back too straight, the shape of his life suddenly visible to him from the outside.

Near dawn, the rain slackened. And in the gray half-light, Callum heard it: an engine. Far off, low, throttled down to a murmur—the sound of a vessel that did not want to be heard, moving slowly along the coast, passing the fishing station without pause.

He rose without waking Sebastian and went to the broken window.

Out on the steel-colored water, barely visible in the last of the dark, a gray hull slid past the point and held its course north. Toward the coordinates. Toward the vault. Painted on its bow, small and patient as a signature, was the number of a patrol boat Callum had last seen lying alongside the *Corvus* on the night the hatches closed.

Reyes wasn't hunting them anymore.

Reyes was racing them home.
