## 10. Ferro's Price

The fishing station had been dead longer than Sebastian had been alive. Its pilings leaned like drunk men, and the roof of the main shed had folded inward years ago, so that rain collected in the hollow of it and dripped through a hundred rust-holes onto a floor of split planking and gull droppings. But the seawall still stood, and the slip behind it was deep enough to take the *Marianela* at half tide, and that was all they needed. All either of them needed, now.

She spent the second morning elbow-deep in the bilge, scraping old caulking out of a seam with a hook knife, her hands raw from salt and the oakum's bite. Callum worked above her on the deck, feeding her tools and holding the lantern when the light went gray under the cloud ceiling. They had spoken little since the night he'd told her about the men living inside the vault—about Reyes ordering them silenced. Some knowledge sat in a boat like ballast. You didn't discuss it. You just felt it shifting under you in every swell.

The monsoon hadn't arrived in earnest yet, but it had sent scouts. Squalls came off the sea in long gray columns, walked across the water toward them, and broke apart at the last moment or drenched them for twenty minutes and moved on. The air tasted like a struck match. The barometer on the bulkhead had been falling since dawn, and Sebastian watched it the way she'd once watched her father's face for signs of weather he wouldn't speak aloud.

It was Callum who heard the engine.

She was under the deck plates when his boots stopped moving overhead. She knew the sound of his stillness now—knew it the way you know the silence of a house where something has just broken. She slid out of the bilge, wiping her hands on her thighs, and came up into a light gone the color of pewter.

"North," he said. "Coming slow. Staying outside the point."

She climbed to the seawall and looked. A boat, yes—low, dark, running without lights in the half-dark of the storm light. Not navy. Navy ran with lights on and didn't care who saw. This was the opposite: a boat that wanted to arrive before it was seen.

"Ferro," she said.

"You know his engine sound?"

"I know the way he comes." She turned back to the shed. "He doesn't sneak. He arrives early and lets you notice him late. It's the same thing, but he thinks it's different."

Callum came down off the wall and stood beside her, and she saw his hand had drifted, without his seeming to know it, toward the place under his jacket where he no longer carried anything. The survey team's security detail. He had been disarmed when he deserted, or he had left the weapon behind on purpose—she'd never asked which, and the not-knowing had become its own small kindness between them.

"He can't have tracked us," Callum said. "Not here."

"Everyone can be tracked. It's just a question of what you're worth to whoever's tracking you." She looked at the boat again, closing. "And I've been worth money to Ferro since the day I was born."

* * *

Ferro came up the slip alone, which was worse. Ferro with his two crewmen was business. Ferro alone was a message, and messages delivered in person by men like Ferro were never the ones you wanted to read.

He was smaller than she remembered him being as a child, when he'd sat in her father's kitchen drinking coffee and laughing too loud at nothing. Time had compacted him the way it compacts driftwood—harder, denser, all the soft grain burned out. He wore a canvas jacket dark with spray, and his eyes went over the *Marianela* with a professional's grief: the stripped deck, the opened seams, the tarpaulin lashed over the cabin like a bandage on a wound.

"She's in bad shape," he said.

"She's in repair. There's a difference."

"There is." He nodded slowly. "The difference is what it costs."

They stood in the shed, out of the drizzle that had begun to come down fine and constant, the kind of rain that didn't fall so much as hang. Callum had positioned himself by the door with a coil of rope in his hands, doing nothing with it, and Ferro's gaze touched him once and slid away with the particular blankness of a man deciding not to have seen something.

"You found me," Sebastian said. "I'm almost impressed."

"I didn't find you." Ferro took off his cap, shook the water from it, put it back on. It was an old man's gesture, and it made her chest hurt in a place she didn't want it hurting. "You were given to me."

The rain filled the pause. Out on the water, the *Marianela* knocked gently against the fenders, that patient wooden voice she'd heard her whole life.

"Given," Sebastian said.

"Reyes." Ferro said the name the way you'd say the name of a disease you'd only heard of until last month. "The lieutenant. He came to my office with your file. Your father's file. Your debt, the interest, the dates, the amounts. He knew numbers I'd have had to dig for." He looked at her, and for the first time she saw something in his face that frightened her more than menace would have: embarrassment. "He didn't threaten me. You understand that? He didn't have to. He'd already bought the paper. All of it. My claim on you is his claim now. I'm not your creditor anymore, Sebastian. I'm his hand."

The words went through her with a cold, clean horror, like a blade through water—no resistance until it was already deep. She had imagined Reyes hunting them with boats and boarding parties, with the cordon and the lockdown. She had not imagined this: the slow machinery of debt, the ordinary paper of ordinary greed, turned into an instrument. Reyes hadn't needed to find her. He'd only needed to find the man who was always going to find her anyway.

"So what does his hand want?" she asked.

"To know where you're going. To know what you have." Ferro's jaw worked. "To bring you in, if it comes to that. He gave me a number to call when I had you. He gave me a week."

"And if you don't call?"

Ferro was quiet for a long moment. Rain dripped through the ruined roof, ticking on the planks, ticking on the tarpaulin, ticking into the bilge of the world.

"I've been doing this work thirty years," he said. "Collections. You think I don't know what I am? I've been the thing people dread seeing walk into a room since before you could swim. And I told myself it was clean, because it was honest. A debt is a debt. Numbers. Paper." He turned and looked out at the *Marianela*, and his voice dropped to something almost private. "Your father borrowed from me twice. Both times I'd have forgiven it and never said. Both times he paid to the last peso and shook my hand like I'd done him a favor. That's the kind of man he was. So when I took over his paper after he died—when I started pressing his daughter—I told myself the same thing. Numbers. Paper."

"But?"

"But Reyes sat in my office and told me, very politely, that if I didn't deliver you, he would look into my other accounts. And I understood, sitting there, that I have spent thirty years being a leash, and I never once asked who was holding the other end." He looked at her then, fully, and his eyes were wet—not with sentiment, she thought, but with the particular fury of a man who has seen his own reflection at a bad angle. "He's not buying your debt, girl. He's buying me. And I'm letting him, because I'm a coward with a boat and two crewmen who eat because of me, and I don't know how to be anything else."

The rain came harder. Somewhere out past the point, thunder rolled along the water like a barrel being moved in a hold.

Sebastian looked at this man—her father's friend, her family's millstone, Reyes's new instrument—and felt the trap close around her with an almost gentle completeness. There was no fighting this. There was no running from it; Ferro knew her, knew the boat, knew the waters the way Reyes never would. There was only, as there had always been, the deal. The bargain. The trade of one weight for another.

And underneath the horror of it, she felt something else, something that shamed her even as it loosened her shoulders: relief. Because a deal, even a bad one, was a shape. It had edges. It was something she could hold.

* * *

They sat on upturned crates under the one corner of the roof that still shed water, and Callum stayed by the door, and the rain came down in sheets now, and Sebastian laid it out.

"Three days," she said.

Ferro's eyebrows rose. "Three days for what?"

"Three days, and then I'll give you something to take back to Reyes. Something real. Something that makes him call off the leash."

"There's nothing you have that man wants. He wants *you*."

"He wants what I know. And what I know is worth more than me." She leaned forward, and she watched her own voice come out of her mouth smooth and confident, and she hated how good she had gotten at this—how her father's daughter, the salvage diver, the woman who could read a wreck's bones, had also learned to read men's greed the way she read tide tables. "The survey team didn't vanish, Ferro. They found something. Something my father's generation sealed and were paid to forget. I have the coordinates. I have the way in."

Ferro had gone very still. Rain ran off the brim of his cap in a thin, unbroken line.

"You're talking about the station." His voice was flat. "The listening station. The one nobody mentions."

"You know about it."

"I know men who dove it in '71 came back wrong. I know your father stopped drinking with half the harbor after that year. I know there's a wreck out on the shelf the navy pretends isn't there, and I know better than to ask why." He shook his head slowly. "I don't want to know more than that, Sebastian. That's the whole trick of my life. I don't ask, and so far, nobody's drowned me for it."

"Then you don't have to know. You just have to carry a message. Three days, and then you tell Reyes that I'll trade. The location, the access—everything—for my debt, my boat, and my name cleared of whatever he's written on me. And you get a share."

Ferro stared at her. "A share. Of what? Of a hole in the sea full of dead men's secrets?"

"Of whatever it opens. Reyes wants it controlled, which means it's valuable, which means whoever helps deliver it gets paid. That's how men like him think. That's how men like him *have* to think." She heard the words coming out of her and heard, beneath them, the sound of a door closing on a version of herself she'd been trying to keep alive—the version that was going to sink it all, the version from the night dives, the version that had promised Callum. "Ten percent. Off the top. Before his cut."

"That's insane. He'll never—"

"He'll allow it. Because you're his leash, and a leash that gets a taste stays loyal. And because if you come back empty, he has no reason to keep you breathing either, and you know it, and he knows you know it." She let that sit. "Three days, Ferro. In three days you call him, or you take him to me, and you tell him I'm ready to deal. That buys me the time to finish the hull and get where I need to be."

"And where is that?"

"Where I need to be."

He looked at her for a long time. Outside, the storm was arriving in truth now, the squall columns no longer breaking apart but walking steadily in from the horizon like a slow army. The barometer in her mind—the one she'd inherited along with everything else—was falling.

"Three days," Ferro said at last. "And your share-talk, that's a promise made on your father's grave. You know I'll collect on that however I have to."

"Yes."

"And when this is done—whatever done looks like—you and I are finished. You hear me? No more paper between our families. Not forgiven. Finished."

"Yes."

He stood, heavily, the way men stand when the standing is the hard part. At the door of the shed he stopped, and without turning around he said: "Your father would have hated this. Not you. This." And then he was gone, down the slip through the rain, and the engine sound started, and faded, and the sea closed over it.

* * *

Callum came and sat across from her on the other crate. For a while neither of them spoke. The storm hit in full, and the shed groaned around them, and the rain on the roof was a single unbroken roar, as if the whole sky had become the sound of the sea.

"You promised him a share," Callum said finally.

"Yes."

"Of the thing you told me you were going to destroy."

She didn't answer. There was no answer that wasn't a lie, and she was too tired to lie to Callum, and too honest with herself, now, to pretend she hadn't heard exactly what she'd just done. She had bought three days with a coin she'd sworn never to spend. And the worst of it—the part that sat in her stomach like swallowed seawater—was how easy it had been. How the words had been there, waiting, polished, ready. How some part of her had been keeping that promise in reserve the whole time, the way a diver keeps a reserve of air she tells herself she'll never breathe.

"It buys us time," she said. "The hull needs three days. The weather needs—"

"I'm not asking if it was necessary." Callum's voice was quiet, and that was how she knew it was serious. He was a man who had deserted an army; he knew the difference between a lie and a necessary thing, and he knew how the two got tangled. "I'm asking if you meant it."

She looked out through the doorway at the *Marianela*, riding the rising chop, knocking against the fenders, patient as ever, asking nothing.

"I meant it enough to say it," she said. "That's what a promise is, isn't it? Something you mean enough to say, and then spend the rest of your life deciding about."

"That's what a debt is."

"Same thing, most of the time."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't. "Ferro will call Reyes tonight. Maybe from his boat, before the storm cuts the signal. Which means by tomorrow, Reyes knows you're ready to trade. And a man like that doesn't wait for the trade. He comes to collect the trader."

"I know."

"So three days might be two. Might be one."

"I know that too." She stood, and her body ached from the bilge and the cold and the weight of what she'd done, and she looked down at Callum sitting in the storm light with his hands empty in his lap. "Then we'd better be fast."

He nodded and rose, and they went out into the rain together, down to the boat, and began again the slow work of making a hull hold water. But all through the afternoon, as she drove the caulking iron and packed the oakum and felt the storm build around her like a fist slowly closing, Sebastian kept hearing her own voice in the shed—smooth, confident, her father's daughter, a woman who could sell anything, even a future she'd sworn to drown.

And she kept seeing Ferro's face as he'd said it: *I'm his hand now.*

Three days. She'd bought three days with a promise she meant to break, from a man who'd just learned what it cost to keep promises for other people.

Out past the point, the sea was already turning the color of iron, and the first true wind of the monsoon came down through the pass like something looking for them by name.
