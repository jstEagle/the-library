## 12. The Navy's Long Arm

The island announced itself by smell before it appeared at all: diesel and charcoal smoke and the sweet rot of fruit left too long in the sun, all of it carried out over the water on a wind that had turned warm and lazy after three days of gray. Sebastian stood at the wheel with her sleeves rolled and her hands cracked from salt, watching Pulau Keramat resolve itself out of the haze—a green thumbprint of land with a fuel dock, a church with a rusted tin roof, and a single street that ran from the pier to nowhere in particular.

"Small," Callum said beside her. He'd been quiet most of the morning, working a knot of frayed line between his fingers like it owed him an apology. "Small is good."

"Small is visible," she said. "Everyone on an island this size knows everyone else's business by lunchtime, and half of them sell it to whoever asks."

He considered that. "Still better than being at sea with a bilge pump we don't trust."

She couldn't argue. The *Magdalena* had been drinking seawater for two days, slow and steady, and the patch job they'd done off the fishing station was holding the way a held breath holds—not forever, not comfortably. She needed fuel, fresh water, and epoxy she could trust, and there was nowhere else within range. So: Pulau Keramat, population four hundred, one fuel dock, one church, one road to nowhere.

She brought the boat in slow, keeping the engine noise down out of habit more than caution, and tied off at the end of the dock where the pilings leaned like tired men. A boy of maybe ten watched them from atop a stack of jerry cans, chewing something, unblinking. Two fishermen mended nets under an awning and did not look up, which was its own kind of attention.

"Twenty minutes," Sebastian said. "Fuel, water, epoxy. You take the cans, I'll deal with the dock man. We don't split up past the end of the street."

Callum nodded, and then, because he was Callum, he said, "You could leave me here. If it'd be cleaner."

The offer landed wrong, the way his offers always did—half genuine, half test. She looked at him: the soft voice, the careful hands, the deserter's posture of a man permanently braced for a door to open. Three weeks ago he'd been a stranger folded into her engine compartment. Now he knew how she took her coffee and she knew he flinched at helicopter sounds, and neither of those facts made them safe. They just made them known.

"I'm not leaving you," she said. "I'm stuck with you. There's a difference, but it's not the one you think."

Something moved through his face—too quick to name—and he shouldered the empty jerry cans and went up the dock without another word.

* * *

The fuel man's name was Anselm, or he answered to it, which amounted to the same thing. He was a wide, sweat-shined man with reading glasses pushed up into gray curls and a radio playing tinny love songs behind him, and he pumped diesel with the unhurried dignity of a priest administering last rites. Sebastian haggled because haggling was expected, paid more than she wanted because the price of running was always higher than the price of staying, and was signing the chit when the church bell began to ring.

Not the hour. Not a service. Just ringing—fast, arrhythmic, the sound of someone hauling the rope without caring what it said.

Anselm took off his glasses. Down the street, doors opened. The net-menders under the awning finally looked up.

"What's that about?" Sebastian asked, already knowing she wouldn't like the answer.

"Patrol boat came in an hour ago," Anselm said mildly, replacing his glasses. "Navy. Big one, gray hull. Officer went to the church first—wanted the headman. Now they're doing a census, they say." He shrugged, a mountain range moving. "Census. On a Tuesday."

Sebastian's blood went cold and stayed cold, the way it did now instead of panicking; panic was a luxury the last month had taxed beyond her means. Gray hull. Census. Reyes didn't search islands—he *processed* them.

"Which way did they go?" she asked.

"Down toward the beach houses. Why?"

"No reason. I need epoxy—the good kind, marine grade. And I need it fast."

Anselm sold her the epoxy, and two liters of water, and a bag of limes she hadn't asked for, and if he understood anything about why her hands were shaking as she counted out bills, he had the good grace not to show it. Outside, the bell kept ringing. Somewhere down the street, a loudspeaker crackled to life in formal, bureaucratic Tagalog-tinged English: *all visitors currently on the island are requested to present themselves at the community hall for routine documentation.*

Routine. Reyes's favorite word. She'd learned that in the interview room, in the way he'd said *routine questions* while sliding her father's photograph across the table like a tarot card.

She found Callum at the boat, jerry cans filled, standing very still with his head cocked toward the loudspeaker the way a deer stands toward a sound it can't classify yet.

"They're here," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Doing a census." She started coiling the fuel line with hands that wanted to rush and forced herself not to. "We finish, we pay, we walk away casual, and we're gone before they reach the dock. This island has one road. They'll work it top to bottom. We're at the bottom."

"The engine—"

"—is full and the bilge is dry enough. Twenty minutes of running is twenty minutes of running." She cinched the line. "Get the stern line. We leave in five."

But the sea had other arithmetic. As Callum reached for the cleat, a navy launch rounded the breakwater—small, fast, riding low with six men aboard—and cut its engines fifty meters off the dock, drifting in on its own wake with the insolent ease of something that had never once been told no. A petty officer in the bow called out, cheerful and terrible:

"Ahoy! Fuel dock! We'll take thirty liters, and my officer wants a word with whoever runs this place!"

Sebastian's mind did the cornered-animal math it had gotten frighteningly good at: one exit, blocked; one road, soon crawling; one boat, fueled and ready and utterly unable to leave without answering questions she couldn't answer. Callum had gone the color of old paper. His hand wasn't on the cleat anymore. It was near the knife on his belt, and that—that would be the death of everything.

"Don't," she breathed, barely a sound. "Whatever happens. Don't touch it."

The launch bumped the tires hanging off the dock pilings. Six sailors came up the ladder, and behind them, unhurried, drying his hands on a white cloth as though he'd just finished lunch, came Lieutenant Commander Emilio Reyes.

* * *

He looked exactly as she remembered, which felt unfair. Some men you expect to be diminished by distance—smaller, sweatier, human. Reyes arrived in person precisely as he existed in memory: pressed khakis, silver at the temples, a face built for sympathy and used for accounting. His eyes found her immediately, and she watched him decide, in real time, whether to be surprised.

"Ms. Villalobos." A small, genuine-seeming smile. "What a coincidence."

"Lieutenant." She wiped her palms on her thighs and gave him the weary, irritated smile of a working diver interrupted. "You following me, or is this island just popular today?"

"Census." He spread his hands, the picture of administrative innocence. "Every vessel, every visitor, documented. Standard procedure during monsoon lockdown. You know how it is—the coast closes, and suddenly everyone wants to know who's where." His gaze drifted past her shoulder, casual as weather, and snagged on Callum. Held. Moved on. Came back.

And Sebastian saw it happen—the tiny recalibration behind his eyes, the flicker of recognition settling into certainty—and understood with total clarity that in the next ten seconds either she invented a story or she buried one.

Because Callum had served under this man. Had been on the survey team's security detail, had been at the briefing tables, had maybe shaken this exact hand. Reyes wasn't sure yet. But he was going to be.

"Your crew?" Reyes asked pleasantly.

"He's not crew." The lie came up out of her like water finding a hole—no thought, just pressure and release. She stepped sideways, deliberately, putting herself between them, and let her voice drop into the flat disgust of a woman describing an inconvenience. "He's cargo. Picked him up adrift off the fishing station during the storm—half drowned, babbling. Navy deserter, far as I can tell. No papers, no rank insignia, nothing but a knife and a story that changes every time I ask." She jerked her chin at Callum. "I was deciding whether to turn him in at the next port or put him ashore somewhere he can't steal my tools."

Silence. The petty officer shifted. Somewhere behind them the church bell had stopped, and the loudspeaker droned on, and the tide sucked softly at the pilings, and Sebastian stood inside her lie the way you stand inside a collapsing building—listening to which wall gives next.

Reyes studied Callum. Really studied him, the way he'd studied her father's compass in that interview room, turning it over in the light of his attention. Callum, to his eternal credit, played it perfectly: he slouched, he scowled, he let his eyes dart and settle on the middle distance like a man with nothing behind them worth searching. Whether it was acting or simply terror wearing its own mask, Sebastian couldn't tell, and didn't dare look long enough to find out.

"Deserter," Reyes repeated. "From which unit?"

"Wouldn't say. Said a name once that might've been a ship, might've been a bar." She shrugged. "You know how they get."

"I do know how they get." Something moved behind his pleasantness—she'd have sworn it was disappointment, briefly, before the accounting resumed. "You understand I'm obligated to take him into custody. Desertion during a classified deployment is—" he paused, savoring the word, "—complicated."

"Then take him." She crossed her arms and made her shoulders a shrug. "But you're taking my claim too. Salvage law's thin out here, Lieutenant, but adrift men wash up against your hull, they're yours; they wash up against mine, they're mine until I file. I found him, I fed him, I fixed him. I want that on record before you zip-tie him, because I've got debts riding on every hour that boat costs me, and I will file a grievance with whatever office handles grievances, and I will be the most tedious woman in your life for a year."

It was a gamble dressed as a tantrum, and she watched him weigh it. Petty officers glanced at their commander. The tide kept talking. And Reyes—Reyes smiled, slow and satisfied, like a man closing a ledger he'd been waiting to balance.

"There's no need for custody," he said. "Not if he's already claimed. Though—" and here he produced, from his breast pocket, a small leather folio, and opened it with the ceremony of a man dealing cards he already knew, "—I will need both of you documented. Names, ports, routes. Routine."

Of course. Of course. She could save Callum or she could stay invisible, and Reyes had arranged the board so she could only choose one. Her name going into his book meant she was no longer a diver he'd interviewed and released. It meant she was a suspect with a route, a heading, a pattern—something that could be tracked, predicted, cornered.

"Fine," she said, and spelled her name for the petty officer in letters like hammer blows. "Villalobos. Sebastian. Vessel *Magdalena*, out of Puerto Sombra. Route's my business, but since you're writing: north. Fishing grounds past the shoals. Alone, after I dump him wherever he gets off."

"And your destination tonight?"

"Wherever the anchor holds."

Reyes wrote that down too, unhurried, and she understood that he believed none of it and needed none of it to be true. The record wasn't evidence. The record was a leash. From this moment, every harbor she entered, every fuel dock, every chandlery, would have a copy of this page in it, and somewhere in a gray office a plotter would draw her wandering line across the sea and wait for it to bend toward coordinates she didn't officially know.

"There," he said, closing the folio. "All documented." He extended his hand, and she shook it because refusing would cost more than the touch. His grip was cool and dry. "Ms. Villalobos. One piece of unsolicited advice—from a man who spends his life around the water." His eyes were kind, and that was the worst thing about them. "The monsoon's coming in hard this year. Deeper water isn't always safer water. Sometimes it's just darker."

"I dive for a living, Lieutenant. Dark doesn't bother me."

"No," he agreed softly. "I don't imagine it does. That's rather what worries my superiors."

He walked back down the dock with his six sailors folding around him like a closing hand, and the launch pulled away, and the loudspeaker wound down mid-sentence, and the island exhaled into its ordinary noises—nets, gulls, the fuel pump, the boy on the jerry cans still chewing, still watching.

* * *

They didn't speak until the breakwater was behind them and the *Magdalena* had her nose pointed north into open water. Then Callum sat down hard on the fuel locker, like a puppet with half its strings cut.

"You lied to him," he said. "To his face. You called me worthless and he believed you."

"He believed what he wanted to believe. That's the only kind of lie that works." She kept her eyes on the compass—her father's compass, riding in its bracket on the binnacle, needle steady, pointing at nothing and everything. "Besides, it wasn't entirely a lie. You *are* worthless ballast who eats my food."

A breath escaped him that might have been a laugh in a kinder life. Then it died. "He recognized me, Sebastian. Before you stepped in—I saw it land. Which means he knows the deserter from the survey team is alive, and he knows whose boat he's on, and now he knows your name is in his book under a heading that says *north*."

"I know what it means."

"Do you?" He stood, and there was something raw under the softness now, something she'd only heard once before, at the fishing station, in the dark, talking about the men inside the vault. "It means there's no version of this where you go back to diving wrecks and haggling over diesel. You're not invisible anymore. You're *tracked*. Every port from here to the border has your name in it by tomorrow night. He doesn't need to catch us—he just needs to know where we're going, and stand there waiting."

Sebastian steered. The swells came in long and gray-green off the bow, and the bilge pump kicked on beneath her feet, faithful and doomed, and somewhere south behind them a gray hull was noting her heading in a book that would outlive them both.

No exit. She'd known it since the interview room, really—since the moment Reyes slid that photograph across the table—but knowing it and feeling it were different animals, and now the feeling had teeth. There was no path back to the woman who owed Ferro money and dove forbidden wrecks and pawned her pride one week at a time. That woman had been poor. This one was hunted. Poor, at least, had been her own.

Only deeper water, then. Only forward, toward the coordinates inked in her father's cramped hand, toward the station on the seabed where the truth sat drowning in the dark, waiting for someone reckless enough to go down and get it.

She opened the chart case, took out the two compasses—one her father's, brass worn to gold at the edges; one identical, pulled from a dead government's abandoned gear—and laid them side by side on the chart table. Their needles swung, settled, aligned. Pointing the same direction. North by northwest, out past the shoals, toward water so deep the charts went blank.

Callum looked at the compasses, then at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop talking.

"We make for the coordinates," she said. "Tonight. Before his book catches up with our feet."

The *Magdalena* ran north into the darkening water, and behind her the horizon closed like a door.
