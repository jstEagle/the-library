## 13. Two Compasses, One Bearing

The anchorage at Pulau Sembilan was a crescent of dead coral and green water, the kind of place charts marked with a single word—*anchorage*—as if that settled anything. Sebastian sat in the cockpit of the *Mariana's Ghost* with her knees drawn up and the two compasses open on the chart table below, watching the light die over the water. The monsoon had pulled back its fist for a day, maybe two, and the sea lay flat as poured glass, which somehow made everything worse. Flat seas meant patrol boats could run fast. Flat seas meant they were visible from a long way off.

Callum came up the companionway ladder with two mugs of the terrible instant coffee he'd bought at the fuel stop and handed her one without asking. He'd learned that much about her in three weeks: she didn't like being asked what she needed. She liked people to notice.

"You've been staring at those compasses since noon," he said.

"I've been staring at what's under them since noon." She took the mug anyway. "There's a difference."

He lowered himself onto the bench across from her, folding his long frame into the space the way he did everything—carefully, quietly, like a man who'd spent years being careful about how much room he took up. The last of the sun caught the scar along his jaw, the one he'd never explained and she'd never asked about. There was a whole geography of things between them they hadn't asked about yet.

"Then say it," he said. "Out loud. All of it."

So she said it. The vault beneath the drowned station. Her father's annotations, decoded down to their final line—the listening station, the records, the names of men who had signed orders in rooms with air conditioning while divers like her father sealed the doors behind them and were paid in silence. Half the region's officials, laid out in magnetic tape and paper logs forty meters down, preserved by cold salt water better than any archive on land.

"And Reyes's patrons are on that list," Callum said. "Which means Reyes doesn't want it found. He wants it *controlled*. Found by him, or not at all."

"Yes."

"Then we're holding something worth more than gold and worth less than nothing, depending entirely on who we hand it to." He turned his mug slowly on his knee. "I've been thinking about that. All day. Since the fuel dock."

Sebastian felt it coming before he said it—the way you feel weather change in your teeth. She looked out at the flat water and waited.

"There's a network," Callum said. "Foreign journalists. I met one of their fixers in Darwin, before I shipped out with the survey team. They run stories nobody else will touch—disappearances, black sites, the whole machinery. They have lawyers. They have reach. You give them the coordinates, the story goes out through six countries at once, and suddenly it's too public for anyone to make us disappear. Reyes can't erase us if we're already printed." He paused. "It's protection, Sebastian. It's the only kind on offer."

The words hung in the salt-smelling air between them. Below them, in the cabin, the two compasses lay side by side on the chart table, needle to needle, pointing the same way toward the same drowned door.

* * *

Callum watched her face do the thing it always did—not close, exactly, but *settle*, like sediment after a storm. He'd learned to read her in three weeks of shared watches and shared fear, and what he read now frightened him more than Reyes's boarding parties had.

"No," she said.

"You haven't even heard—"

"I heard. Journalists. Lawyers. Reach." She set her coffee down on the cockpit coaming with a care that was worse than slamming it. "Callum, I need to tell you something, and I need you to actually listen, because you keep talking about this like it's an idea instead of a history."

He said nothing. That was the right move; he knew it instinctively with her.

"My father wasn't the only diver who worked that seal job. There were four boats. Eleven men. They were young and broke and the navy money was good, and none of them—not one—knew what they were sealing *in*, only what they were sealing *out*." Her voice stayed level, which he understood now was effort, not ease. "Over the years, three of them tried to sell what they remembered. Not even the location, at first. Just rumors. Just enough to see if anyone would pay."

"What happened to them?"

"The first one sold a story to a Manila paper. Six months later he drowned in his own mooring field, in two meters of water, with no alcohol in his blood and his boots on. The second went to a foreign broadcaster—actual journalists, actual protection, the same thing you're describing. They moved him to another country. Gave him a new name." She smiled, and it was the coldest thing Callum had ever seen her do. "He came back eight years later to visit his mother's grave. His body washed up on the beach where he grew up. The third man never sold anything. He just talked too much in a bar in Cebu. They didn't even bother making it look like an accident."

The wind had died completely. The boat rocked on water so still he could hear fish moving under the hull.

"That was thirty years ago," Callum said quietly. "Different government. Different navy."

"You think the men who benefit from that vault being forgotten retired?" She shook her head. "Secrets like this don't age out. They *compound*. Every year the list gets longer, because every year there are more people who'd be ruined if it surfaced. You're not selling a story, Callum. You're selling a debt, and every person who ever owed it will come to collect."

"And the alternative?" He heard his own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "The alternative is we sit on this until Reyes finds us, or Ferro sells us, or the monsoon does his work for him. We hold it and we hold it and we hold it, and then one night a dark boat comes alongside and that's the end of the *Mariana's Ghost* and everyone aboard her. At least if it's public, we're worth more alive than dead. That's all protection is, Sebastian. It's not safety. It's just making yourself expensive to kill."

"It's making yourself *owned*," she shot back. "You don't hand a thing like this to an institution and walk away free. They'll own the story, and they'll own us—we'll be sources, assets, whatever word they use. And when the story stops being useful, when the editors change or the funding dries up or some bigger story eats ours, we'll be two people who know where the bodies are buried, standing alone, exactly like we are now. Except everyone will know we're the ones who talked."

"So your plan is what? Sail north and hope?"

"My plan is what my father's plan should have been." She stood, restless, gripping the shrouds, looking south toward water neither of them could see past. "Some things shouldn't be owned by anyone. Not navies, not networks, not us. Some things should go back to the sea."

And there it was, Callum thought. The thing underneath everything she'd said since the fuel dock, maybe since the day he'd stowed away on her boat. She wasn't arguing tactics. She was arguing theology.

* * *

They anchored for the night in the lee of the largest islet, and the argument followed them down into the cabin, quieter now, which made it worse. Lamplight swung with the swell. The compasses lay between them on the chart table like a third participant.

"You watched men die for trying to sell it," Callum said. "I watched men die for trying to *find* it. We're not having different arguments. We're having the same one from opposite ends."

"Then tell me what you saw." She said it almost gently, and that gentleness undid him more than shouting would have. "Tell me why you think handing it to strangers ends differently than every other time someone's tried."

Because he couldn't, was the truth. Because every plan he'd built in the last three weeks had been built on the assumption that somewhere, someone outside this nightmare could be trusted, and he had no evidence for that beyond wanting it to be true. He thought of the survey team—Okafor with her laugh, Brandt teaching card games, little Mendez who wrote letters home in a notebook he never mailed. Taken below, all of them, into the dark, and the world above had simply continued, unbothered, uninterested. No network had saved them. No story had been told.

But he also thought of himself, twenty-two years old, signing a security contract because it came with a salary and a sense of mattering, and discovering too late that mattering for them meant being disposable to them. If someone had told *him* the truth back then—if someone had published, exposed, named names—maybe Okafor would be alive. Maybe he wouldn't carry what he carried.

"Maybe I'm wrong," he admitted finally. The words cost him visibly. "Maybe there's no clean way out of this. But Sebastian—there might be no way out at all. Those aren't the same thing, and I'd rather bet on imperfect people than on the ocean keeping our secrets. The ocean doesn't keep secrets. It just waits."

She looked at him across the lamplight for a long moment, and he saw her doing the arithmetic he hated—that she loved him, that love was data, that data could be weighed against survival and found insufficient. He'd seen that look on officers' faces. Seeing it on hers was the worst thing the voyage had done to him.

"If we sell it," she said slowly, "we sell it together or not at all. I won't wake up one morning and find you've made the deal without me."

"Same rule applies to sinking it," he said. "I'm not going to help you drown the only leverage that keeps us breathing and then sail north like pilgrims."

"Then we're agreed on the process and nothing else."

"We're agreed on the process," he said, "and we'd better agree on the rest soon. Because Ferro has three days left on his clock, and Reyes has less than that on his."

* * *

Sebastian took the midnight watch alone. Callum slept badly in the V-berth, turning over and over, and she listened to him not-sleeping and felt the crack between them widening by millimeters, the way a hull takes water—not all at once, but steadily, in places you can't quite reach.

She held the compasses up in the moonlight, one in each hand. Identical casings. Identical needles. But her father's had a hairline scratch across the bezel from the day he'd dropped it on concrete, and when she ran her thumb over it she could feel thirty years of him—his hands, his silences, the long years when he'd come home smelling of diesel and said nothing about where he'd been. He had known. All those quiet years at the dinner table, he had known what lay under the water south of here, and he had chosen silence, and the silence had eaten him alive anyway. She'd found his notebooks after he died. Page after page of coordinates crossed out and rewritten, as if he'd been trying to talk himself out of knowing.

Was that what she was doing? Or was it what Callum was doing—dressing up desperation as principle?

The honest answer was that she didn't know, and that not-knowing was itself a kind of drowning. She only knew what she'd seen: that every hand that had reached for this secret had come back burned, and that the burning never stopped at the hand. It climbed. It took the arm, the chest, the people you loved.

Toward dawn, the radio crackled—a garbled fragment of a marine band broadcast, half-swallowed by static, but clear enough: a bulletin, repeated twice. *Vessel Mariana's Ghost, white hull, blue boot stripe, persons aboard considered dangerous, report sightings to naval authorities.*

Someone had described her boat precisely. Someone who'd seen it recently. Ferro, or the fuel dock, or both.

She killed the lamp and woke Callum with a hand on his shoulder, and in the grey pre-dawn light his eyes opened fully alert, no fog of sleep at all, and she understood that whatever happened between them—whatever cracked or held—they had perhaps hours, not days.

"They've put our name on the wire," she said. "We move now. North or south, Callum. Decide with me."

He looked at her, and then south, toward the coordinates, toward the drowned station waiting under its hundred meters of cold water.

"South," he said. "Before they seal it forever."

And far off across the flat grey water, faint as a scratch on glass, came the sound of engines running fast.
