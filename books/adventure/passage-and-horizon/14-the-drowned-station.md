## 14. The Drowned Station

The coordinates put them over open water that looked like every other stretch of the coast—gray-green, restless, indifferent. Sebastian had expected something. A marker buoy, a shoal, a change in the color of the sea. Instead there was nothing but the same monsoon-washed swell rolling in from the southeast, and the two compasses in her pocket, and the weight of her father's handwriting translated into a point on a chart.

"Here?" Callum asked. He stood at the wheel with the engine idled back to a murmur, and he kept looking over the side as if the answer might be written on the water.

"Here." She checked the bearing a third time, though she'd checked it six times since dawn. The needle swung and settled, swung and settled, patient as a heartbeat. Her father's compass and the survey team's compass lay side by side in her palm, twin brass hearts, and both of them pointed the same way: down.

She'd dived wrecks for eleven years. She knew the way a drowned thing announced itself—the dark shape resolving out of the blue, the sudden cold, the fish that scattered or gathered depending on what kind of death the structure had become. But when she rolled backward off the gunwale and dropped through the green, what she found below was not a wreck.

It was a door.

The station rose out of the seafloor like a drowned cathedral, a great concrete cylinder tilted maybe ten degrees, half-buried in silt, its upper works furred with a decade's growth of soft coral and drifting weed. A listening post, her father's notes had said. Built in the years when the world was afraid of missiles and men poured concrete into places no map admitted to. It had been decommissioned, sealed, and paid to be forgotten—and her father had been one of the men paid.

Sebastian hung in the water above it and felt her breath go loud in her own ears.

*You knew,* she thought, to the dead man, to the compass in the dive bag slung against her ribs. *You knew all along what you were leaving me.*

Callum descended beside her, his torch beam cutting a pale cone through the murk. He'd insisted on diving with her, and she'd let him, though trust was still a currency she spent carefully where he was concerned. He found the airlock hatch on the station's upper deck and worked the wheel with gloved hands, and it turned—turned easily, too easily—and Sebastian's stomach dropped before her body did.

"It's been opened," she said into the regulator, though he couldn't hear her. The words came out as a fog of bubbles. She tapped his shoulder and pointed, and he understood: the hatch had been opened recently. The survey team. And whoever had come after them.

* * *

Inside, the station was a throat.

The torch beams found a corridor running down into the dark, walls sweating with marine growth, the air long since replaced by water but the shape of the place still human—handrails, cable runs, a faded sign stenciled in a government font: LEVEL TWO AUTHORIZED. Silt lay over everything like gray snow, and their fins raised it in slow clouds that hung and drifted and refused to settle, so that they swam through their own disturbance, blinding themselves a little more with every meter.

Sebastian moved the way her father had taught her to move in a wreck: slow, left hand trailing the wall, right hand holding the light, always knowing where the way out was. But this was not a wreck. Wrecks were accidents. This had been made, deliberately, by people who wanted it to hold its secrets, and then sealed by people who wanted it to hold them deeper.

The first room they came to had been an operations space once—consoles like tombs, screens gone to milky blindness, chairs bolted to a floor that was now a ceiling of someone else's imagination. Callum's light swept across it and stopped.

"Sebastian." His voice came through the water muffled, almost tender. He was aiming his torch at a bulkhead.

At first she thought it was coral, the way growth patterns sometimes made letters, made faces, made things that weren't there. Then she swam closer and saw the scratches were deliberate—deep, desperate, cut into the paint and the steel beneath with something harder than a fingernail but not much sharper. A dive knife. A piece of broken glass. Whatever they'd had.

**WE WERE HERE. THEY CAME FROM BELOW.**

Below the first line, in a different hand, shakier:

**NOT DROWNING. THEY TOOK US DOWN. M. IS GONE.**

And below that, a third hand again, steadier, as if the writer had made peace with something:

**IF ANYONE READS THIS — DO NOT OPEN THE VAULT. IT ISN'T CARGO. IT'S WITNESS.**

Sebastian read it three times. The silt drifted. Her own bubbles rose past her face like small pale ghosts leaving without her.

*They were alive,* she thought. *They were alive down here and they wrote to whoever came next.* She put her glove against the scratched steel, gently, the way you'd touch a stranger's shoulder at a funeral. The steel was cold through the glove. Somewhere in the dark of the station, or in the dark of the sea that had taken them, the men and women who'd written these lines were still missing—no bodies, no tracks, exactly as Reyes had described it, exactly as he'd wanted it described.

Callum had gone very still beside her. When she looked at him, his face behind the mask was gray.

"I didn't know about this," he said. The words came out distorted, half-water. "I was topside security. They never let us below the second level. I told you that."

"I believe you." And she found, examining the feeling, that she did. It didn't make him innocent. It made him a man who had guarded a door without knowing what was behind it, which was most of the human race.

They went deeper.

* * *

The station's spine was a vertical shaft, and the shaft was where the water got colder, and the cold was where Sebastian's fear finally caught up with her and rode on her shoulders the rest of the way down. Her depth gauge ticked past numbers she didn't like. The light from above became a rumor. Their torches carved small rooms out of an ocean of black.

The vault door was at the bottom.

She'd imagined it a hundred ways on the passage out—bank-vault steel, rusted hatches, something dramatic. It was none of those. It was a pressure door, industrial and plain, set into the concrete like a tooth, and it stood open. Not forced. Opened with its own mechanism, its wheel spun back, its interior throat yawning into darkness that even the torches seemed reluctant to enter.

And beside the door, bolted to the wall at chest height, was a plaque. Brass, like her compasses. Sebastian wiped the growth from it with her glove and read the engraved lines, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the depth.

**IN MEMORY OF THE CREWS OF THE SURVEY VESSELS *MERIDIAN* AND *HALCYON*. THEIR WORK REMAINS CLASSIFIED. THEIR SACRIFICE REMAINS NECESSARY.**

The date beneath it was thirty-one years old.

Her father had been twenty-four when the *Meridian* went down. She knew this the way she knew her own birthday—because he'd drunk too much one night and told her, and then never spoken of it again, and she had spent her childhood thinking it was grief for drowned sailors. It was grief. But not the kind she'd imagined.

Because there was one more thing beside the plaque. Scratched into the concrete, low down, at the height of a kneeling man, in a hand she knew—she knew it the way you know a voice through a wall, the way you know footsteps on your own boat's deck—was a single line of letters, cut deep, cut by someone with time and a tool and a terrible need to be understood:

**S.V. — TELL MY DAUGHTER THE SEA KEEPS WHAT IT'S GIVEN. DO NOT DIG.**

*S.V.* Sebastian Villalobos. Her father had knelt at this door, thirty-one years ago or thirty-one days, and scratched her name into the wall of the thing he'd helped bury, and the message had waited down here in the dark for her the whole time, patient as the station itself, patient as the sea.

She had spent her whole life thinking he'd abandoned her to the water. That he'd loved the diving more than he loved her, loved the wrecks and the freedom and the horizon more than the small apartment and the girl who waited in it. She had built her whole stubborn, solitary, salt-scoured existence on that belief, and here it was, the truth, written in his own hand: he had died—or disappeared, or been made to disappear—trying to keep her away from this place. Trying to keep the sea from giving her what it had given him.

*Do not dig.*

She had dug.

Sebastian knelt in the silt before the wall, and her regulator hissed, and she let herself cry into her mask where no one could see, because the water was already there and one more salt contribution would never be noticed. She cried for the boy of twenty-four on the *Meridian*. She cried for the man who'd come back changed and silent and had sealed the vault a second time with his own hands and his own silence. She cried for the girl who'd inherited a compass instead of an explanation.

Callum hovered nearby. He didn't touch her. He was a man who understood, in his deserter's bones, when a person needed to be left alone with the dead. But he kept his torch trained on the scratched line so she could read it as long as she needed to, and that was its own kind of company.

* * *

They searched the vault's antechamber because Sebastian made herself do it—made herself stop grieving and start working, because grief was a luxury that got divers killed, and her father would have said so. The antechamber was a small concrete room off the main door, and in it they found the survey team's gear: a dive rack with tanks still clipped in, a tool case pried open, a slate with notes half-erased by the water. And on the slate, in careful block letters, a last entry:

**REYES KNEW THE COORDINATES BEFORE WE DID. ASK WHO GAVE THEM TO HIM.**

Sebastian stared at it until the letters swam. Reyes had sent them here. Or let them come. Either way, the lieutenant who had sat across from her in that harborside bar with his polite voice and his card and his patient menace had known this station existed before the survey team ever launched—and had sent six people down into it anyway, and then reported them vanished without tracks or bodies.

The vault door itself stood open onto darkness. Sebastian's torch reached maybe three meters inside before the black swallowed the beam whole. She could hear—she was almost sure she could hear, though sound down here was a liar—some vast slow respiration from the deep of it, water moving through drowned corridors, the station breathing in its sleep.

Everything her father had died to bury was in there. Everything Reyes would kill to control. Records, the scratched warning had said. *It's witness.* Thirty-one years of witness, sealed in concrete at the bottom of the sea, and the men it could indict were the men who ran the coast, ran the patrols, ran the quiet little war of paperwork and disappearances that people like Sebastian survived by never noticing.

She could open it. The door was open. She could swim in with a light and a slate and copy what was there, or rig a lift bag, or simply confirm it existed—and then spend the rest of her life being hunted by everyone who needed it to stay a rumor.

Or she could do what the dead man asked.

*Do not dig.*

Her father's compass hung in the dive bag against her ribs. Through the canvas she could feel it, or imagined she could—a small brass weight, a needle that had pointed her whole life toward this exact square of ocean.

"Sebastian." Callum's hand closed on her arm. Not gentle—urgent. His torch was aimed back up the shaft they'd descended, toward the faint gray suggestion of the surface, and his eyes behind the mask were wide.

She looked where he was looking and heard it a moment later: engines. Not the far-off thrum of a patrol boat on its lawful rounds. Close. Directly above. The sound of an engine slowing, stopping, holding position over them—and then, through the hull of the drowned station, through thirty meters of green water, the unmistakable metallic knock of a hull being lowered on davits.

A launch. Coming down.

Callum grabbed the slate and shoved it into her dive bag beside the compass. Sebastian took one last look at the scratched line on the wall—*do not dig*—and killed her torch, and pulled them both into the dark of the vault's antechamber as the first torch beam swept down the shaft like the eye of something that had been searching for them all along.
