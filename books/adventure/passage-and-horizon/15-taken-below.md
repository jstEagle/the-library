## 15. Taken Below

The sea gave them forty minutes of mercy, and then it took it back.

Sebastian felt the change before she saw it—the way the swell shifted from a long rolling breath to something shorter, choppier, as if the water itself had begun to hurry. She was on the surface line, tending the downline to the station two hundred feet below, her mask pushed up on her forehead, salt drying in white crescents at the corners of her mouth. Callum was still under. He'd gone down with the cutting torch and the last of their good air to free the record canisters from the rack where the survey team had chained them, and he'd been under long enough that she had started counting his bubbles instead of her own heartbeat.

That was when she saw the ship.

It came around the headland of the island to the north without running lights, without any sound she could name over the wind—just a shape darker than the darkening water, angular and wrong, like a piece of the night that hadn't finished falling. Her body knew it before her mind did. Her hands were already hauling the downline before she'd said the word *Reyes* out loud, and the word tasted like copper.

*Patricia M.*—her boat, her whole world bolted to a rusting keel—rode at anchor in the lee of the island, exactly where they'd hidden her, exactly where anyone who knew these waters would look first. Sebastian had told herself the monsoon's edge would keep patrols in port. She had told herself the storm front crawling down from the northwest would blind their radar. She had believed, the way you believe in a harbor bar at closing time, whatever made the night easier to sit inside.

The ship was half a mile out and closing, and it wasn't slowing.

She hauled the line hand over hand, the rope burning grooves into her palms, and shouted down into the water—a useless gesture, sound going nowhere but into the sea's indifferent ear. Then she did the thing she'd sworn she'd never do unless the boat were burning: she grabbed the spare tank, clipped it to the downline, and sent it sliding down into the green blackness after him. A message in the only language divers share. *Come up. Now. Something is wrong.*

The ship resolved. Gray hull, low superstructure, the blunt bow of a vessel built to push through weather rather than around it. No name visible. No flag. There didn't need to be. She knew the silhouette the way she knew the lines of her own father's face—from the day three years ago when one just like it had sat a quarter mile off the forbidden wreck while she hid behind a reef shelf with her lungs full of borrowed air, watching men in rigid inflatables lower instruments over the side and take readings on a grave the navy swore didn't exist.

A launch detached from the ship's flank. Two engines, six or seven shapes aboard, moving fast across the last light.

Sebastian's mind went very quiet and very fast, the way it did underwater, the way it had done since she was nineteen years old and her father's boat went down with him inside it. Options presented themselves and were dismissed with the cold efficiency of a dive computer cycling through algorithms. Run: no engine start would beat them to open water, and the storm front would find her alone in it. Fight: she had a knife, a flare gun, and a wrench. Hide: there was nowhere. The island was a spine of volcanic rock with a fishing hut that had lost its roof a decade ago, and the launch would be on top of her in four minutes.

Which left Callum, still below, still working by torchlight in the drowned throat of the station, unaware that the surface of his world had turned hostile.

She looked at the water. She looked at the launch. And she understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning in reverse, that she could not have both.

* * *

The launch came alongside in a wash of foam, and the men who stepped onto her deck were not the polite kind. Reyes had sent soldiers this time—or whatever the navy called them, men in gray fatigues with carbines slung across their chests and the particular blankness of people who had been told the person they were looking for was not a person. Their leader was a broad-shouldered lieutenant commander whose name tag read OKONKWO, and he looked at Sebastian standing on the deck of the *Patricia M.* with the expression of a man checking an item off a list.

"Ma'am. Step away from the rail."

"I live here," Sebastian said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "This is my boat. You're standing on my home."

"We have authorization to board and search." Okonkwo's eyes went past her—to the dive gear staged along the port rail, the tanks, the lift bags, the chart case lashed under the dodger. His expression didn't change, but something behind it did, a small mechanical click like a round being chambered. "Where's your second diver?"

"What second diver?"

"Sonar contact," he said mildly. "Two heat signatures on this vessel for the past six hours."

"The engine block," Sebastian said. "She runs hot. Ask any mechanic in Puerto Sombra."

He almost smiled. "I'll ask him next week."

They fanned out across her deck with the practiced economy of men who had done this many times, and Sebastian stood very still in the middle of it all and thought about the downline. It was still in the water. She'd dropped it when she'd seen the ship—dropped it rather than pulled it, because pulling forty meters of weighted rope takes minutes she hadn't had—and now it hung somewhere beneath the boat, a silver thread running down into the dark, straight to the station's broken hatch, straight to Callum.

If he'd found the spare tank. If he was ascending. If he surfaced anywhere within a hundred meters of this boat, he would come up into a ring of rifles, and the last thing he would see would be the gray belly of a navy launch and the first thing they would see would be him, a deserter, a loose end, a man the lieutenant commander's list very much wanted checked off.

She needed the line up. She needed it up *now*, and she couldn't move toward it without telling every man on deck exactly where to point their guns.

Okonkwo stopped beside the port rail. Looked down into the water. Sebastian watched his shadow fall across the place where the downline entered the sea, and her heart performed a slow, terrible maneuver, dropping through her chest like an anchor through thermocline.

"You've been diving," he said.

"Fishing," she said.

"At night. In rising weather. Off a derelict station." He turned and looked at her fully for the first time, and there was nothing cruel in his face, which was somehow worse. There was only procedure. "Lieutenant Reyes asked me to tell you something, if we found you. He said you'd understand it." Okonkwo recited it flatly, a message from one professional to another: *"Tell her the survey team's families received closed caskets. Tell her what was in them."*

The words went into Sebastian like cold water into a suit. She let them sit there. She did not shiver.

"I don't know what that means," she said.

"No," Okonkwo agreed. "You don't. Yet."

And that was when the water broke thirty feet off the starboard bow.

* * *

Callum came up fast—too fast, she could see it even at that distance, the uncontrolled buoyancy of a man who had heard the spare tank hit the bottom near him and understood its grammar perfectly. He surfaced gasping, mask around his neck, and for one suspended second the evening held all of them in it: the soldier at the rail with his hand drifting toward his rifle, the launch idling, the gulls crying somewhere inland, and Callum in the water, blinking salt and torchlight-afterimages out of his eyes, taking in the gray ship, the gray men, the gray arithmetic of his situation with an expression that was not surprise.

He had always known, Sebastian realized. Some part of him had been swimming toward this moment since the night he'd stowed away among her lobster traps. That was what desertion was—not an escape but a debt, carried forward, accruing interest, waiting for a collector.

"Don't," she said. She didn't know who she was saying it to. Everyone. The sea.

"Callum Reeve." Okonkwo read the name off a card, not a memory, and Sebastian understood that they had known it before they ever rounded the headland—that the sonar contact, the heat signatures, the whole theater of the boarding was choreography, and the ending had been written in an office she would never see. "Specialist, security detachment, Hydrographic Survey Unit Four. Absent without leave for fifty-one days." He raised his voice slightly, addressing the water. "Come to the ladder. Nobody needs to get wet."

Callum trod water. He looked at the ladder. He looked at Sebastian, and across twenty feet of blackening sea he did something she would carry for the rest of her life, however long the rest of her life turned out to be: he shook his head. Once. Small. *Don't do anything.*

Then he swam to the ladder, because the alternative was being dragged to it, and because he was the kind of man who paid what he owed, and Sebastian stood on her own deck with her hands open at her sides and let it happen, because the alternative was dying uselessly beside him, and someone had to finish what they'd started, and they both knew it, and knowing it made it worse, made everything worse, made the gulls' crying obscene.

They zip-tied his wrists behind him at the rail. They took the record canister he'd clipped to his harness—one of them, the small bronze cylinder, dripping, seawater running out of it in a bright thread across her deck—and passed it hand to hand like evidence, like communion, like a thing worth killing for, which it was.

Callum paused at the gunwale of the launch. The soldier behind him let him have three seconds, maybe out of contempt, maybe out of some soldier's courtesy for a man walking into a room he wouldn't walk out of.

"The east gallery," Callum said. Not loudly. Just loud enough. "It's not flooded. I lied about the east gallery."

Then they pulled him aboard and the launch swung away, and the gray ship took him the way the sea takes things—without malice, without ceremony, without giving anything back.

* * *

Sebastian did not cry. Crying was a luxury with a price she couldn't afford, and besides, the storm front was twenty minutes out and the barometer on her bulkhead had fallen so far it looked sick. She stood at the helm of the *Patricia M.* with her hands on the wheel and her teeth locked together and she did not cry, and what happened inside her instead was quieter and more permanent, like a weld setting in a seam.

They had taken Callum. They had taken the canister. They had taken the compass case—she discovered this when she went below and found the locker where they'd cached it hanging open, stripped clean, the government case and her father's brass compass and the twin compass from the survey cache all gone in one efficient sweep. Of course. Okonkwo's men had searched while she'd been standing at the rail performing grief for an audience. They had never wanted Callum at all; Callum was a bonus. What they'd wanted was metal and paper and the coordinates scratched in a dead man's shorthand, and they had it, and Lieutenant Reyes was somewhere on that gray ship right now turning the compass bezel in his fingers and smiling the smile of a man whose paperwork had finally come together.

Except.

Sebastian sat down on her bunk, in the dark, in the rising motion of the coming storm, and made herself think the way her father had taught her to think, thirty meters down, when the silt closes in and panic is the only creature hunting you: *What do I actually know?*

She knew the coordinates. All of them. Every annotation, every bearing, every depth correction in her father's cramped hand—they lived in her memory now, had lived there since the night she'd decoded them by lamplight with Callum asleep in the cockpit, and no search of any locker could take them out. Reyes had seized a compass. He had not seized her.

She knew the station. Its layout was printed on the back of her eyelids: the flooded main gallery, the collapsed access tunnel, the listening rooms with their racks of dead machines, and the east gallery—

*It's not flooded. I lied about the east gallery.*

Her breath caught. He'd said it to her, in front of them, in front of Reyes's men, and she had thought at first it was a farewell, a last gift, a way of telling her where the records really were. But Callum did not give gifts he couldn't afford. Callum bargained. Callum survived. And a man about to be taken below, into the hold of that ship, into whatever room Reyes used for conversations—what did such a man want to leave behind in the mind of the woman he was leaving above?

An answer: *I will trade. I will lead them to it. I am alive, and being alive means dealing.*

Or a warning: *Whatever they offer you, whatever they ask, remember that I told them the east gallery was dry—and remember that I lie.*

She couldn't know which. That was the wound of it, the part that hurt worse than the zip-ties would have hurt, worse than the loss of the compass: she could not know whether the last thing Callum had given her was a map or a mirror. Whether he'd handed her the truth or handed her the shape of a lie so that she'd recognize it later, worn by other mouths.

The rain arrived. It came off the northwest in a gray wall, hammering the cabin roof, erasing the island, erasing the horizon, erasing the wake of a gray ship that was already gone. Sebastian listened to it and felt the fury in her chest change state, the way seawater changes state at depth, compressed into something denser and colder and more useful than rage.

They had taken a compass. Fine. Compasses pointed north; hers had always pointed at her father, and her father was dead, and dead men kept their secrets better than governments did. They had taken the canister. Fine. Canisters could be opened by anyone with a wrench—but only she could read what was inside them, because the survey team had encoded their final inventory in her father's notation system, the same shorthand, the same private language between divers, and Reyes would stare at those pages for a month before he understood they were written in a tongue only one living person spoke fluently.

They thought they had taken the treasure. They had taken the box.

Sebastian rose, lit the gimbaled lamp, and pulled the waterproof chart tube from under the floorboards—the one cache they hadn't found because it wasn't a cache, it was ballast, it had been ballast for three years, and no searcher on earth looks twice at lead weight. Inside, rolled tight in oilcloth, was her father's original working chart of the coast, annotated edge to edge, and folded into it a single page in Callum's careful hand: the survey team's decoding key, copied the night before the storm, *just in case*, because Callum was a man who believed in just-in-case the way other men believed in God.

She spread it on the chart table and weighted the corners with brass fittings, and outside the rain tried to tear the roof off her boat and the lightning turned the world white once, twice, like a camera flash recording the scene.

*East gallery,* she wrote in the margin of her logbook, and then, underneath, because fury deserved precision: *Not flooded. Or not true. Find out which.*

The gray ship had gone north, toward the deep water, toward the station, toward the vault her father had helped seal and the records that could hang half a government. They had Callum, and the compass, and the canister, and the confidence of men who had never once been contradicted by the sea.

They did not have her father's daughter.

Sebastian banked the lamp low, ran the engine check by feel in the dark, and began, methodically, to plan a boarding of her own.
