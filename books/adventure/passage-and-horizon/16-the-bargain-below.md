## 16. The Bargain Below

They took his boots first.

Callum understood the logic of it even as they were unlacing them—a man without boots was a man who had stopped being a passenger and started being cargo. The deck of Reyes's ship was grey steel slicked with rain, and his bare feet on it felt like an admission. Behind him, the *Vela* sat low in the water where they'd left her, Sebastian's silhouette small in the wheelhouse, one hand raised—not waving, he decided, not waving—before two sailors walked him aft and the distance between them became a fact instead of a feeling.

The holding space was a storeroom that had been a storeroom once and was now something else: a bench bolted to the bulkhead, a hatch with no interior handle, a single caged bulb that buzzed like an insect trapped in glass. The air tasted of diesel and old rope and, beneath it, the cold mineral breath of the sea coming up through the hull, as if the ship were drinking through its keel and exhaling somewhere below.

He counted time by the engine's rhythm. Four hours, maybe five, before the door opened and Lieutenant Reyes came in carrying a metal chair, which he set down facing Callum with the unhurried care of a man arranging furniture in his own home.

"Sit," Reyes said, though Callum was already sitting.

Up close, the officer looked worse than he had at the fuel stop—or better, depending on how you measured it. The politeness was still there, laminated over everything like varnish, but beneath it Callum could see the grain now: a tiredness so deep it had gone geological. This was a man who had been awake inside his own decisions for a long time.

"You're wondering why you're alive," Reyes said. "Most people in your position would wonder that."

"I'm wondering about Wren."

Reyes tilted his head, acknowledging the name the way a man acknowledges weather. "Sergeant Wren Okafor. Yes." He folded himself into the chair across from Callum, knees high, hands loose between them. "You were her detail. You carried her pack when she sprained her ankle on the third day. You slept across the tent flap every night for six weeks. I've read your file, Callum. It's a boring file. That's a compliment. Boring men are reliable men, right up until they aren't."

"Where is she?"

"On this ship. Alive. Fed. Asleep, probably, because it's four in the morning and she has the good sense to sleep." Reyes said it flatly, without cruelty, and somehow that was worse than cruelty would have been. "You understand my situation. I have a deserter who knows what the survey team found. I have a sergeant whose testimony could end several careers, including mine, including people considerably above me. And I have a storm coming that will close every port from here to the capital for nine days." He spread his hands. "I don't actually want either of you dead. Dead men generate paperwork. Dead men generate *questions*. Questions are the one thing I cannot afford."

"So what do you want?"

"What I've always wanted." Reyes leaned forward. The caged bulb caught the wet shine of his eyes. "For this to be over. For everyone to go back to their lives with nothing changed, which is the most expensive thing in the world to buy."

* * *

They talked for a long time, or rather Reyes talked and Callum learned to listen the way he'd learned to listen on watch rotations—to what was said and, more carefully, to what was being spent to say it.

Reyes told him about the survey, or the shape of it. Six specialists and a security detail of four, sent out under a hydrographic charter so bland it might have been mapping sandbars. They'd found the station in eleven days. Eleven days, against divers who'd needed years and a sealed contract and a generation of forgetting. Because the survey team hadn't been looking for it. They'd been looking for *anomalies*—cold spots in the water column, magnetic ghosts, a sonar shadow shaped like architecture—and the station had lit up their instruments like a struck bell.

"They sent back one transmission before I cut the relay," Reyes said. "Do you know what was in it? Not coordinates. Not salvage manifests. A request for guidance. They'd found occupied structures. Occupied, Callum. Men living inside it, men who'd been living inside it since before either of us was born, maintaining equipment nobody had ordered maintained, for a country that officially never built the place." He shook his head slowly. "My orders were to contain. Containment, in my experience, always ends up meaning people. But orders don't ask what they mean. That's what makes them orders."

"And the men inside?"

"The sea keeps its own garrison." Something moved behind Reyes's face and was put away. "The point is this: the survey team saw too much, wrote too much, and asked for guidance from institutions that survive by never being asked anything. Wren is the last of them. The others—" he made a small gesture, seaward, inclusive of horizon, "—are accounted for."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." He stood, crossed to the hatch, then turned. "Here's what I'll offer, and I'll offer it once. Bring me to the vault. Walk me through whatever your diver friend intends to do down there. In exchange, Wren walks off this ship at the next neutral port with a discharge, clean papers, and enough money to disappear properly. Disappearing properly is a skill, Callum. I can teach it. I've had practice."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I keep you both until the storm passes, and after the storm there will be a tribunal, and tribunals are machines for converting inconvenient men into closed files." He said it without heat, almost gently. "I'm not threatening you. I'm describing Tuesday."

* * *

Left alone, Callum did what he always did in the dark hours: he took himself apart and looked at the pieces.

There was the piece that had signed on to the survey detail because it paid triple and sounded boring. There was the piece that had heard the first transmissions come up garbled from below and felt something turn over in his chest—not fear exactly, but the vertigo of realizing the map had edges nobody had told him about. There was the piece that had run, the night the team went quiet, not out of courage—he knew the difference now; courage would have stayed—but out of the animal arithmetic of a man who could see the shape of the ending and wanted no part of it.

And there was the newest piece, three weeks old, salt-cured: the memory of Sebastian Villalobos standing in her own wheelhouse with rain running off her jaw, telling him she didn't sell things, she salvaged them, and hearing in it the whole creed of a life. He had watched her give away food she couldn't spare. He had watched her read her father's annotations with her thumb pressed hard against the page, as if she could hold the man in place through the paper.

If he traded the vault, he traded her work. Her father's work. The one inheritance she'd found that wasn't a debt.

But Wren.

Wren, who had taught him to field-strip a radio in the dark. Wren, who had laughed at his terrible coffee for six weeks and drunk it anyway. Wren, alive, asleep four decks above him, breathing because someone had decided she should keep breathing, and that someone was currently sitting in a metal chair waiting for Callum's answer the way a fisherman waits—patiently, certain of the tide.

Moral compromise, his mother used to say, is just survival wearing a suit. She'd said it about his father, who had driven trucks for men he shouldn't have and come home every night and kissed her forehead and never once pretended the money was clean. Callum had sworn, at fifteen, to be cleaner than that. He'd made it to thirty-two. It was further than some got.

He thought about what Sebastian would do, and then he thought about what Sebastian would *say*, and those were different things. She would say sell it, burn it, drown it, anything but let it be used. But she had also looked at him across the galley table and said, *you don't leave people in the water*, and her voice had cracked on *water*, and he had understood that she was talking about her father, and that the crack was the truest thing she owned.

You don't leave people in the water.

Wren was in the water. Had been for weeks, treading it quietly, kept afloat by nothing but her usefulness to a man whose usefulness to *his* masters was itself a countdown.

When the hatch opened again near dawn, Reyes found Callum already standing, barefoot, steady.

"Yes," Callum said. "I'll take you down. On conditions. Wren goes free first—off this ship, papers in hand, before we make the final approach. Not after. Before. I know how 'after' works."

Something eased in Reyes's shoulders, barely visible, the way a ship eases when a load shifts and holds. "Agreed."

"And you tell me the truth about one thing. One."

"Ask."

"The vault—the records, the listening logs, whatever's down there that frightens your patrons." Callum held his gaze. "Why does it matter? Why not just flood it, seal it, forget it, the way the last generation did? My diver's father helped bury this thing once already. It can be buried again."

Reyes was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the hull. Somewhere above them, the ship's company moved through its morning rituals, indifferent as weather.

"Because it was never about the contents," Reyes said finally. "Do you think anyone upstairs cares about forty-year-old intercepts? Half of them were the ones being intercepted. No. What matters is *who knows it exists*. A secret isn't information, Callum. A secret is a debt ledger. Everyone who knows the station is there owes silence, and debts can be called. That's why it can't be buried—it can only be *held*. Buried, someone might dig it up and read it. Held, it sits in the dark making everyone who knows about it behave." He smiled, and it was the saddest expression Callum had ever seen on a uniformed man. "I'm not guarding treasure. I'm guarding obedience. And the moment the wrong person stands in that vault and chooses to walk away instead of kneel, thirty years of quiet arrangements start drowning."

"Then you understand," Callum said, "why someone might choose exactly that."

"I understand it better than you know." Reyes picked up the chair, tucked it under one arm like a man closing a shop. At the hatch he paused. "Your diver. Villalobos. She'll fight this. They always fight, the ones with blood in it. When she does—and she will—you'll have to decide which side of yourself signs the order." He looked back once. "Get some sleep. We make the approach tomorrow night, ahead of the storm's eye. You'll want your boots."

The hatch clanged shut. The bulb buzzed. Through the steel hull, faint and constant, Callum could feel the engines change pitch—turning, he realized, turning south, toward the coordinates, toward the drowned station where her father's handwriting waited in the dark.

He sat back down on the bench and put his face in his hands, and for the first time since the night he deserted, he prayed—not for forgiveness, which he suspected worked differently than advertised, but simply for the storm to arrive fast, and for everyone aboard to still be standing when it passed.

Above him, footsteps crossed the deck in quick, deliberate pairs. Below him, the sea went on keeping everything it had ever been given.

And in the brig two doors down, he was almost certain he could hear it: a woman's voice, low and unhurried, singing something with a work rhythm to it—an anchor song, a hauling song—the sound of Sergeant Wren Okafor, alive, marking time in the dark the way sailors always had.

He pressed his palm flat against the bulkhead, as if he could pass the warmth of his hand through forty years of steel.

Tomorrow night, he would lead Reyes straight into the heart of everything.

He just had to hope that Sebastian Villalobos was already planning how to take it all away from him.
