## 17. Debt Called Due

The rain had been falling for three days without conviction—too steady to be a squall, too thin to be the monsoon's true face—and it turned the whole harbor into a gray room with no walls. Sebastian stood under the corrugated awning of Ferro's chandlery and watched water sheet off the tin in a curtain that blurred the fishing fleet into ghosts. Somewhere behind her, Callum waited in the skiff with the engine ticking over, ready to run if she came out of this wrong.

She had told him she'd come alone because Ferro trusted no one who arrived in company. That was half of it. The other half was that what she was about to do might need to look like a betrayal from the outside, and it was easier to commit a thing like that when there was no one watching whose opinion you couldn't bear to lose.

Ferro's shop smelled the way it always had: diesel, rope tar, dried fish, and the sweet rot of old paper charts. She pushed through the door and the bell above it gave its broken half-ring, the same sound it had made since she was nine years old and her father used to send her in for shackles while he waited on the dock. Ferro sat behind his counter with a ledger open and a cup of coffee going cold beside it, and he did not look up right away, which was itself an answer. Ferro always looked up. Ferro had built a life on knowing who walked through his door before they cleared the threshold.

"You shouldn't have come here," he said finally, still reading numbers that had stopped meaning anything to him hours ago, by the look of him.

"Reyes bought my debt."

That got his eyes up. They were bloodshot, and there was something new in them she'd never seen before—something that looked, absurdly, like shame. "You've been talking to people."

"I've been listening. There's a difference." She crossed the room and set both hands flat on the counter, on the ledger, on the columns where her father's boat had been reduced to arithmetic. "Three days ago I promised you a share of whatever the compass opens. You took it. So either we're partners or you're Reyes's leash, and I came to find out which."

"Sebastian." He closed the ledger with a soft slap, as if shutting a coffin lid on something small. "Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." He poured coffee from the pot on the hotplate, hesitated, and poured a second cup anyway, pushing it toward her across the scarred wood. It was an old gesture, older than their enmity, and it cost him something to make it; she could see that in the way his hand lingered a half-second too long before withdrawing. "Your father drank his coffee standing too. Right where you're standing. He said sitting down meant you'd decided to stay, and he never liked deciding anything until the last possible minute."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make me love you before you tell me what you've done."

Ferro laughed—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man hearing his own indictment read aloud. He rubbed his face with both hands, and for the first time since she'd known him as an adult, since the years of threats and interest payments and polite menaces delivered over this same counter, he looked old. Not weathered. Old, the way wood goes old, hollowed out from inside.

"What I've done," he said, "is sign papers I didn't read closely enough. Or maybe I read them exactly closely enough and signed anyway, because a man in my position doesn't get to choose his creditors, only his posture toward them." He turned the ledger around so she could see it. The page wasn't debts anymore. It was a schedule. Dates, times, tide tables, patrol rotations written in a hand that wasn't Ferro's—neater, more patient, the handwriting of someone who expected to be obeyed rather than bargained with. "He wants your boat found by Thursday. He wants *you* found by Thursday. And he wants it done quiet, because apparently even now, after everything, there are people above him who prefer their disappearances tidy."

"And what do you want?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He stood, joints cracking, and went to the window. Through the rain-blurred glass the harbor lay like something drowned already. "I want to tell you I'm sorry, and I want you to believe it, and I know you won't, because why should you? I've spent eleven years squeezing you for money your father's death made impossible. I told myself it was business. Business is what cowards call cruelty when they want to sleep at night."

"Ferro—"

"No. Let me finish. I've been finishing nothing my whole life; let me finish one thing." His knuckles whitened on the windowsill. "Reyes came to me six weeks ago. Before the survey team vanished—I didn't know that then, I swear it on whatever's left of my soul. He bought the paper. All of it. Every debt in this harbor that touched your family, and there were more than mine, though mine was the biggest. He owns the Alvaros brothers' note. He owns the fuel dock. He owns half this coastline on paper nobody's ever seen, and every piece of it points at you like a gun barrel. I thought he was a collector. Collectors I understand. Collectors want money." He turned back to her, and his eyes were wet, whether from age or shame or the light she couldn't say. "This man doesn't want money. This man wants a world where certain things were never true, and he will burn every boat in this harbor to keep it that way. I understood that about four days too late, and by then I'd given him your heading twice."

The words landed like cold water down her collar. Twice. Her heading, twice. Every near-miss, every patrol boat that had appeared just over the horizon at the wrong moment—the ones she'd blamed on luck, on coincidence, on the navy's general appetite for harassment—they had been guided. By this man. Over this counter. With this bell half-ringing above the door.

"You knew where I was diving," she said quietly.

"I knew where you were diving."

"The wreck. The one nobody names."

"Yes."

"And you told him."

"And I told him." He didn't flinch from it, and she gave him that much credit—he had the decency not to flinch. "Because he asked me to, and because saying no to a man like that requires a kind of courage I told myself I'd outgrown the need for. I was comfortable, Sebastian. Comfort is the most expensive thing a poor man can buy, and I paid for mine with your father's daughter."

* * *

She made herself drink the coffee. It had gone bitter and lukewarm, and she drank it anyway, because her hands wanted something to do besides shake, and because her father had taught her that a diver's first job underwater is to control the breath, and the breath starts with the hands, and the hands start with small deliberate acts. Drink the coffee. Set down the cup. Look at the man who sold you and decide what he's worth.

Outside, the rain thickened. The first real gusts of the season came through the harbor mouth and set the masts swaying, a forest of them bending together, and somewhere a halyard began slapping against aluminum in a rhythm like slow applause.

"My father," she said, "died trying to warn those survey people off. Did you know that?"

Ferro shook his head slowly.

"He did. I've seen what he wrote on the bulkhead down there. His name, in his own hand, scratched into steel forty meters under the sea, next to a warning he finished too late to matter." She set the cup down. "So here's what I know about dying for things, Ferro. I know it doesn't buy anything unless somebody living picks up what the dead dropped. And I know that Reyes thinks he's holding all the levers in this harbor because he bought paper. But paper isn't the harbor. Paper isn't boats and crews and tides and the twenty years of favors you've traded with every fisherman from here to the point. Paper is what men like Reyes hold because they've never once been trusted with anything real."

Ferro stared at her. "What are you asking?"

"I'm not asking. I'm offering." She leaned forward. "Thursday night, the monsoon peaks. Reyes knows it—he's counting on it keeping honest boats in port, which means every hull that moves will be suspected, tracked, boarded. Fine. Let him track us. But he'll be tracking *one* boat, and a harbor full of fishermen knows how to be seen when they want to be seen and how to vanish when they don't, and nobody alive knows that better than the man who's brokered every favor on this coast for thirty years."

"You want my boat."

"I want your boat and your crew and your lights running a false course north past the buoy line, loud enough and stupid enough that every patrol eye in the region turns to follow it. I want Reyes looking at decoys while a diving tender nobody's ever bothered to learn the name of sits quiet over coordinates he can't read." She held his gaze. "And I want you to understand exactly what you'd be buying into, because I'm done doing business with men who haven't counted the cost. If this goes wrong, you're not a creditor anymore. You're a co-conspirator against an officer of the navy, and they don't repossess boats for that. They repossess lives."

The silence stretched. Rain hammered the awning. Down the street a shutter banged loose and clattered, and neither of them moved.

"Why?" Ferro said at last, and his voice had changed—stripped of the broker's smoothness, down to something rawer. "Why would you trust me? After the headings. After the paper. You should be putting a knife in me, not a plan."

"Because disgust looks different on a man than fear does." She nodded at the ledger, at Reyes's neat handwriting. "You've been carrying that page around for days instead of burning it. A scared man burns evidence. A disgusted man keeps it, because some part of him is waiting for someone to ask him to use it. I'm asking."

Ferro looked down at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. Then he laughed again, but differently this time—a cracked, disbelieving sound, almost young.

"Your father," he said, "once told me I was the only honest thief he knew. I threw him out of my shop for it." He pulled the ledger toward himself and tore the page free, folding it with careful, ceremonial slowness, and put it in her hand. "Patrol rotations. Boarding schedules. The cordon gaps he thinks he's sealed—there are two, and he's wrong about one of them because the chart he's using is three years old and the sandbar's shifted. Take it. And take this—" he reached beneath the counter and came up with a key on a loop of braided line, worn smooth by decades of other palms— "the *Marta Elena*. She's slow and she draws attention like a wound draws sharks, which is precisely what you want her to do. My crew's aboard already, loading nets. They think it's an early-season run. I'll tell them the truth tonight, and if any of them walk away, I won't blame them, and neither will you."

"They won't walk away."

"How do you know?"

"Because they're your crew, and you're about to become the man who finally finished something."

* * *

They stood together in the doorway before she left, watching the storm assemble itself over the water. The horizon had gone the color of bruised fruit, and the wind carried salt and ozone and the faint iron smell that always preceded the season's worst weather. Out past the breakwater, whitecaps were beginning to show their teeth.

"Sebastian." Ferro's voice was low, pitched under the wind. "There's a chance this ends with you at the bottom of the sea and me explaining myself to a tribunal. You understand that. You're spending your life on this."

She considered lying to him, telling him she'd survive, that she always survived, that survival was the one skill her upbringing had perfected. But he had handed her his whole rotten, redeemed self tonight, and he deserved the same currency back.

"I know," she said. "I've known since the day I found that compass on the beach that every road out of this led through deep water. Maybe I come back up. Maybe I don't. But if I go down, I go down having chosen it—not sold, not hunted, not squeezed dry over a counter by men with ledgers. Chosen." She tucked the folded page inside her jacket, against her ribs, where it lay warm as a second heartbeat. "My father spent his last dive trying to give the truth away and died holding it. I intend to spend mine making sure it lands somewhere. That's not a tragedy, Ferro. That's just a price. I've finally got something worth paying it for."

Ferro put out his hand. She took it. His grip was dry and strong and trembled only slightly, and neither of them mentioned it.

"For what it's worth," he said as she stepped out into the rain, "your father would have hated this plan. Too reckless. Too many variables." A pause, and then, with the ghost of the old broker's smile: "He'd have insisted on coming along."

She walked back down the quay through the strengthening rain, past shuttered shops and moored boats straining at their lines, and when she reached the skiff Callum read her face in a single glance and said nothing, only shifted over to make room and handed her the tiller. Behind them the harbor lights came on one by one against the darkening afternoon, and far out at the harbor mouth, barely visible through the rain, a navy patrol boat was turning toward the open sea—toward the north, toward the course the *Marta Elena* would soon be screaming down at full throttle with her decks lit like a carnival.

Two days, she thought. Two days to teach a decoy to dance, and then the monsoon would swallow everything, and the only question left would be which truths the sea decided to keep.

Callum watched the patrol boat shrink into the gray. "It's done, then," he said.

"It's done." She opened the throttle, and the skiff bit into the chop, and the rain came sideways, and ahead of them the whole coast was dissolving into storm. "Now we find out what we're worth."
