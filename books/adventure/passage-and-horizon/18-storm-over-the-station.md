## 18. Storm Over the Station

The monsoon did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a decision the sea had been making for days and finally committed to.

Sebastian stood at the rail of Ferro's boat—the *Marta Elena*, a name she'd never expected to be grateful for—and watched the horizon erase itself. There was no line between water and sky anymore, only a gray violence that swallowed both, and somewhere inside it, three kilometers east, the drowned station waited with its records and its dead men's messages scratched into bulkheads.

Ferro came up beside her, shouting over the wind without looking at her. "You understand what I'm about to do."

"You're going to run east with your lights on and your transponder screaming, and every patrol boat in the cordon is going to chase you like dogs after meat."

"And when they catch me—"

"They won't catch you. You'll lead them past the shoals at Punta Verde, lose them in the rain, and come back tomorrow with a story about engine trouble." She turned to look at him. His face was slick with spray, older than she remembered it being, and his eyes had the flat calm of a man who had already finished arguing with himself. "Why are you doing this?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Thirty years I've collected debts. You know what I learned? The ones people can't pay aren't money. They're things they did, or things that were done to them, and every year the interest compounds until there's nothing left of them but the owing." He spat over the side. "Reyes bought my debt because he thinks everything can be bought. I want to watch him learn otherwise. That's my price. Not a share. A seat."

"A seat for what?"

"For whatever happens down there." He nodded toward the gray. "I've earned the right to see how it ends."

She wanted to tell him he hadn't, that no one had earned any of this, that the whole inheritance was rot—but the wind took the words before she could shape them, and maybe that was mercy.

* * *

Callum's hands were steady.

That surprised him more than anything else. Three days locked in a compartment aboard Reyes's ship with a bucket for a latrine and a light that never fully went off, and now, moving through the flooding dark of the station's lower access tunnel with a borrowed dive knife and a guard's keycard still warm from the man's pocket, his hands were steady as a surgeon's.

It shouldn't have worked. He knew that. The transfer to the station had been Reyes's arrogance—bring the prisoner to the site, make him walk the last hundred meters on his own legs so he understood who owned the ground. Two guards instead of four, because the storm made extra bodies expensive and Callum was, in Reyes's arithmetic, a broken thing. Broken things don't run.

But Reyes had never watched a man spend three years pretending to be less than he was. Callum had been the survey team's security detail precisely because he could look harmless. He'd let them zip the cuffs too loose. He'd memorized the corridor layout during the transit. And when the guard named Ossler had leaned close to check his bonds in the entry chamber, Callum had taken the man's own knife and put him down gently—a chokehold, not a blade, because he was done adding bodies to this place—and taken the keycard off a body that would wake up angry in twenty minutes with a story nobody would believe fast enough.

Now the tunnel. Chest-deep water, rising. The station groaned around him like a ship in its death throes, and he understood with a diver's instinct that Sebastian was already here—that she'd rigged something, or was rigging it, because the flooding pattern was wrong, deliberate, valves opened in an order no accident would choose.

She was drowning the station from the inside. With herself possibly still inside it.

"Of course you are," he said to the dark, and waded faster.

* * *

Sebastian worked by feel and by the thin beam of her helmet lamp, both nearly useless against the silt the storm was pumping through the station's ruptured seams.

Compartment seven. Her father's annotations had called it the ballast throat—the old engineers' name for the junction where the station's flooding systems converged, built during the Cold War by men who had designed the place to be scuttled in an emergency and then, apparently, been paid to forget they'd ever designed it. Her father had been one of those men. She'd spent two weeks hating him for it. Now she was using his handwriting as a manual, and the hate had curdled into something she didn't have time to feel.

The valve wheel was crusted with thirty years of mineral scale. She put her weight into it, boots braced against the housing, and felt it give with a shriek that traveled through the water like a scream through a wall. Somewhere above, thunder detonated against the surface and came down to her as a dull pressure pulse, the sea's own heartbeat gone arrhythmic.

One compartment sealed. Six to go, and each one meant another wheel, another fight, another minute off a clock she couldn't see but could feel ticking in her chest like a second heart.

Her air gauge read fifty-one minutes. The swim back to the surface gap—the collapsed section of the access shaft where she'd slipped in through the storm-torn seam—was twelve minutes in good conditions. These were not good conditions. The current inside the flooded corridors had opinions, and all of them were bad.

She swam for compartment six.

* * *

Callum reached the central gallery and stopped, because the vault door was open.

Not forced—open, swung wide on its counterweighted hinges, the way it had been designed to open decades ago and almost certainly never had. Beyond it, the record room stretched back into darkness, row after row of sealed cabinets, the archive that half the region's officials would burn their own countries to keep buried. His lamp found the scratch-marks on the doorframe: the survey team's final tally, dates and initials, a message in Voss's cramped hand—*IT WAS NEVER CARGO. TELL THEM IT WAS NEVER CARGO.*

Voss. The team's oceanographer. The one whose testimony had surfaced anyway, weeks later, like a drowned body the sea finally gave back.

Callum stood in the doorway and felt the whole architecture of the last three years settle onto him at once. He had guarded this door. He had guarded men who were taken below it. He had told himself he hadn't known, and then he had known, and then he had run, and running had brought him here—to the exact spot where not-running would have mattered more.

The water at his knees shivered. Not current. Footfalls—or the memory of them, transmitted through the structure. Someone else in the gallery.

He killed his lamp and pressed into the shadow of the doorframe, knife low, and waited for whatever the storm had sent him.

* * *

Sebastian felt the station change before she heard it.

A new sound in the flooding symphony—a rhythmic knock, metal on metal, coming up through the deck plates from somewhere aft. Deliberate. Patterned. Three knocks, pause, three knocks.

Her father's signal. The one he'd used between divers working blind sections, the one she'd grown up hearing through the hull of his boat while she did homework above. *I'm here. Where are you?*

Nobody alive should know that signal. Nobody alive except—

She answered it. Wrenched a loose pipe free and struck the deck plate three times, pause, three times, and the reply came immediately, closer, and then a lamp beam cut through the silt and Callum was there—gaunt, unshaven, wearing a dead man's drysuit top over clothes that looked slept in for days, holding a dive knife like he actually knew what to do with it.

"You're supposed to be on Reyes's ship," she said, and hated how much her voice shook.

"I was. Now I'm here." He caught her arm, steadied her against the current. "Tell me what you're doing and how much time we don't have."

"Forty minutes of air. Five compartments left. When they're sealed, the lower decks flood completely and the silt load from the storm buries the record room under—" she gestured at the murk around them, the whole suffocating weight of the sea—"under that. Forever. No salvage crew touches it. No government dredge. It becomes geology."

"And us?"

"We're not here when it happens."

He looked at her for a long moment, water streaming between them, and she braced for the argument—for *there's another way*, for *the journalist network*, for all the fault lines they'd cracked open on the crossing north. Instead he said, "Show me which wheels turn which way," and pulled his mask down, and something in her chest that had been clenched since the day she'd found the compass in the survey case finally, partially, let go.

They worked in tandem after that, and it was the closest thing to grace she had ever known underwater.

He took compartments five and four while she took six and three, their lamps crossing in the silt like searchlights, their signals reduced to the language her father had taught her—knocks and tugs on the safety line they ran between them, a single umbilical of rope and trust. Compartment two fought her. The wheel was fused, and she had to hammer the housing with a spanner until the corrosion cracked, and by the time it surrendered her gauge read twenty-two minutes and her arms felt like someone else's arms, borrowed and badly fitted.

Callum appeared out of the gray at her shoulder, tapped his own gauge—eighteen minutes—and pointed aft. One compartment left. The deepest one, the keel chamber, where the final seal sat below the waterline of even this drowned cathedral.

They descended together.

* * *

The keel chamber was where the station kept its heart.

Callum felt it before he saw it—the hum, faint but unmistakable, of systems that had never fully died. Emergency power, trickle-charged by some thermal loop the original engineers had installed and then erased from every drawing. Thirty years of quiet endurance. The final valve sat in a nest of pipes at the chamber's center, and beside it, bolted to the bulkhead, a brass plate:

**SCUTTLE PROCEDURE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

And beneath it, in fresh scratches, in a hand Callum recognized because he'd catalogued the team's belongings after they vanished—Voss again:

*IF YOU ARE READING THIS, FINISH WHAT THE OLD MEN STARTED. WE COULDN'T. THEY CAME FOR US BEFORE WE COULD DECIDE.*

Sebastian's lamp found the scratches and stopped there. For a moment she hung motionless in the water, reading it twice, three times, and Callum saw her mouth form the word *couldn't*—saw her understand that the survey team had stood exactly here, at exactly this choice, and been murdered for the crime of hesitating.

She wasn't going to hesitate.

She set her hands on the wheel.

* * *

Above them, the storm reached its full voice.

Sebastian felt it through the hull as a continuous concussion, the sea hammering the station like a fist on a door, and she thought absurdly of Ferro out in it somewhere, running east with his lights blazing, spending his boat and his freedom to buy her these minutes. She thought of her father's compass in the case on her own boat, riding at anchor beyond the buoys, waiting to be sunk beside the thing it had pointed to for forty years.

The wheel turned. The seal released. Somewhere far above, a hatch she'd wedged open surrendered to the flood, and the station drank the storm in one long swallow.

"Done," she signaled. "Swim."

They rose together through corridors that were filling behind them, the water chasing them upward with the patience of something that had all the time in the world. The access shaft. The surface gap. The black mouth of the storm overhead, rain hitting the sea so hard it seemed to bounce.

Sebastian broke the surface first, dragged herself onto the rocks at the shaft's lip, and pulled Callum through after her. They lay on their backs in the rain, chests heaving, and beneath them—faint, then fading, then gone—they felt the station exhale its last pocket of air and begin its long descent into the silt.

"It's done," she said. Or maybe shouted; the wind ate the difference. "It's over."

Callum rolled his head toward her. Rain ran off his face, and he was smiling—not relief, exactly. Something rawer.

"No," he said. "It's not. Look."

He pointed north along the rocks, toward the station's old service pier, where the storm had torn away decades of camouflage.

Moored at the pier, lights burning against the gray, riding the swell like it owned it, was a navy patrol vessel. And standing at its rail, motionless in the rain, watching them with the patience of a man who had never once needed to hurry—

Lieutenant Reyes raised one hand. Not a wave. An invitation.
