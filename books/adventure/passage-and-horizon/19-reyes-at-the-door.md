## 19. Reyes at the Door

The vault door stood open between them, and through it Sebastian could hear the sea working at the station's bones—the slow groan of metal settling into silt, the tick and knock of pressure finding its way into every seam. Her headlamp beam wavered. Not from her hands. From her breath.

Reyes had come down alone.

That was the first thing she understood, standing in the flooded corridor with the rigging lines still warm in her grip and Callum somewhere behind her in the dark, freed from his cell but not yet free. Reyes had sent his boarding party up, had dismissed them with a word, had descended the ladder into the drowned station in his own gear—unmarked, civilian-black, no insignia—because what he meant to say here could not be said in front of witnesses.

He stopped six meters away, in the junction where the corridor widened into the old communications room, and he did not raise a weapon. He didn't need to. The water itself was his weapon, and the depth, and the distance to any air that belonged to her.

"Villalobos." His voice came through the comm clear and unhurried, a man discussing weather. "You've done more damage down here in four hours than the sea managed in forty years. I want you to know I respect that."

She said nothing. In the black water behind him, her lamp caught the pale bloom of a fish spooked by their lights, there and gone.

"I'm going to tell you something," Reyes went on, "and then I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to want to hear both before you do anything permanent. Because once those seals go, Villalobos—once you flood the lower compartments—they don't come back. Neither does what's inside them."

Her hand found the valve wheel at her hip. She kept it there, not turning it. Waiting.

* * *

He told her about her father first.

Not the version she knew—not the man who'd come home salt-crusted and silent, who'd taught her to read current off the surface of the water like handwriting, who'd gone out one November and never come back and left her a boat, a debt, and a compass. Reyes told her the other version. The official one, the buried one, the one that existed in a file cabinet three security classifications above anything she'd ever been permitted to see.

"Emiliano Villalobos was not lost at sea," Reyes said. "He was on the sealing crew. Nineteen eighty-one. One of six divers contracted to close this station after it was decommissioned. He was paid very well, and he was made to sign things, and he kept his mouth shut for eleven years. And then he didn't."

Sebastian's breath fogged her mask. She wiped it with a practiced roll of her wrist, an old motion, something her father had taught her, and hated herself for the tenderness of it.

"He started talking to journalists. Quietly. Feelers, nothing committed. We know because we were watching him—we watch everyone who's ever been inside. And in November of '92 he took his boat out alone toward these coordinates, and his engine was later found to have failed catastrophically, and no body was recovered, and the file was closed as accidental loss." A pause, measured out like medicine. "His engine did not fail catastrophically, Villalobos."

The station groaned around them. Somewhere below, water moved through a bulkhead she herself had opened, patient as erosion.

"Your father was going to sell it," Reyes said. "Same as you're about to. Same as the survey team tried to. There's a pattern, if you look. Everyone who finds this place decides they're the one who'll get away with telling. Your father decided it. Commander Osei's team decided it. You're deciding it right now, and you're doing it in the exact same water he died in, and I am asking you—before you finish—to consider that the pattern has never once broken in your favor."

"You killed him." Her voice sounded strange to her own ears through the comm, flattened by the gear, stripped of everything but the words.

"I wasn't in the service then. I was nineteen." No defensiveness. Just fact, laid flat. "But yes. The institution I serve did. And I have spent my career making sure it never happens again—not the killing, Villalobos. The telling. Do you understand the difference? I don't hurt people. I prevent history. It's uglier than it sounds, but it is not the same thing."

* * *

And then he made the offer.

He made it slowly, the way a man lays cards face-up when he wants you to see every one.

"Your father's name comes back clean. Not just clean—cleared. Official commendation, posthumous. Service to the Republic, sealed crew, distinguished conduct. The story becomes: Emiliano Villalobos died honoring his contract, and the record says so, and your mother—if she's still alive—gets the letter, and the pension attached to it, and the apology that goes with pensions." He let that sit. "You said once—I read your statement—that all you ever wanted was for someone to say his name without looking over their shoulder. This is me saying it. On paper. Permanently."

Her throat closed. Because he was right. That was what she wanted, had always wanted, the thing under every dive and every debt and every dawn patrol she'd raced with salvage bags full of other people's secrets. Not money. Not even truth, exactly. She wanted her father's name to be safe to say out loud.

"The debt too," Reyes continued. "Ferro's paper, whatever Reyes—" he paused, a dry almost-smile in his voice at his own name, "—whatever leverage I bought through Ferro, it dissolves. Clean title on your boat. You sail north with Callum, or alone, or wherever you like, and no one ever boards you again. No patrols. No interviews. You become genuinely invisible, which I understand is the only kind of safety you've ever believed in."

"And the records."

"Stay buried. That's all. They stay exactly where they are, and you help me make sure of it—you've already rigged the flooding; finish it. The lower compartments go, the archive section goes, forty years of intercepts and payments and names go under two hundred tons of collapsed structure, and no government, journalist, or historian ever gets near them. Including mine. Especially mine." He tilted his head, and the lamp light slid across the faceplate, revealing nothing. "I'm not asking you to protect my patrons, Villalobos. I'm asking you to bury everyone's. Mine included. That's the part nobody believes until I say it plainly: there are names in those files that would end my career faster than yours. I don't want them read either. We want the same thing. We've always wanted the same thing. You just haven't admitted it yet."

* * *

For one long moment, she wanted it.

She wanted it the way you want air—not as an idea but as a physical need, rising up through her chest, blurring the edges of the corridor. Her father's name spoken cleanly by strangers. The debt gone. The boat hers, truly hers, the horizon unguarded. Callum beside her, alive, unpursued. Every weight she had carried since she was nineteen years old lifted in a single breath, and all it would cost was silence—and hadn't silence been the family trade? Hadn't her father himself taken their money and sealed this place and held his tongue for eleven years? She wouldn't be betraying him. She'd be finishing his work. Completing the sentence he'd started with a welding torch and ended at the bottom of the sea.

That was the trap's genius, and she saw it even as she stepped toward it: it wasn't a lie. Everything Reyes offered was real. The papers would exist. The debt would die. The name would be cleared. Corruption at its sharpest doesn't ask you to do wrong—it asks you to do reasonable, and hands you a pen.

Her thumb rested on the valve wheel.

Behind her, faint through the water, she heard Callum moving—slow, careful, closing the distance down the corridor, and she thought of what he'd told her at the fishing station, his voice low across the stove: *They weren't cargo, Sebastian. There were men living in there. Men who'd been inside so long they'd forgotten which way the sky was.* And she thought of the bulkhead messages, scratched into paint with something harder than a fingernail: *TELL THEM WE WERE HERE. TELL THEM OUR NAMES.*

Names. Her father's name, cleared—but only if every other name stayed drowned. Osei's team, erased twice, once from the world and once from the record. The men who lived below. All of them traded, finally and forever, for one name: Villalobos. Hers.

The moment stretched. The station breathed around them, steel ticking as it settled, and somewhere in the dark below her fins the archive waited with its forty years of voices, and Reyes waited with his open hands, and her own heartbeat counted out the cost of both futures in the same currency.

"No," she said.

It came out small. Then she said it again, louder, into the comm, into the water, into the long corridor of her own wanting: "No. You don't get to buy his name. He spent the last of it trying to warn people off this place, and I'm not going to spend it keeping them out."

Reyes was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, the weatherman was gone from his voice.

"That's a pity," he said, and reached for the cutoff valve on the corridor's main line—the one that fed her air hose, the one he'd been standing beside the entire time. "Because I only needed your answer. I never needed your help."

Above them, muffled through forty meters of green water, came the sound of engines—many engines, converging—and the first flare of the monsoon's peak broke over the surface like a door slamming.
