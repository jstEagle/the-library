## 20. What the Sea Keeps

The offer hung in the water between them like a body that hadn't decided to sink.

Her father's name, cleared. Her debt, erased. A life handed back to her, whole and unmarked, in exchange for one small silence—one signature on the dark, one more seal welded over the truth. Reyes stood in the vault's flooded antechamber with his dive light angled so it didn't touch his face, and for one long moment—long enough for the current to push her twice against the doorframe, long enough to count her own heartbeats against the regulator's rhythm—Sebastian wanted it.

She wanted it the way a drowning woman wants air: without dignity, without thought, with every cell of her.

*Villalobos.* Her father's name spoken aloud by a man who had spent his career making names disappear, and spoken not as an accusation but as a gift. *He was a good man who made a bad choice. I can make the record say what it should have said all along.*

That was the poison in it. Not the money. The name. Because her father had died trying to warn the survey team off—she'd read his scratched message on the bulkhead an hour ago, letters gouged deep by someone working in the dark with a broken tool—and Reyes had stood there and offered to rewrite even that, to launder even her grief, to hand her back a father she'd never actually had in place of the complicated, complicit, brave man the sea had shown her.

She pulled her regulator free.

"You can't clear his name," she said, and her voice came out strange through the helium-thin air of the helmet comm, flattened by the gear, stripped of everything but the words themselves. "Because you'd be the one writing it. And nothing you write is true."

Reyes's light shifted. "Sebastian—"

"Everything my father did, he did because men like you paid him to forget. He spent thirty years trying to earn his way out of it. You want to sell me a clean version of him." She found the valve wheel at her hip—the station's emergency flood control, the thing Callum had described to her in the fishing station's lamplight, the thing she'd spent two days learning the shape of by touch. "I already know who he was. That's worth more than anything you're offering."

She turned the wheel.

* * *

It began as a sound—a groan through the hull, low and vast, the way ice sounds when it decides to move, the way a ship sounds when it has finally stopped arguing with the sea. Then the bulkhead lights, running on some stubborn reserve generator these forty years, flickered once and went out, and the darkness came in from below like a tide.

Sebastian swam.

Not away. *Through.* The plan had never been elegant; there had been no time for elegance. Open the lower seals, let the flooding compartments take the weight, and the silt shelf beneath the station—unstable since the monsoon had scoured the seabed—would do the rest. Tons of records. Decades of intercepted voices, ordered silences, payments logged in careful bureaucratic hands. Half the region's respectable men, filed away in waterproof cabinets in a drowned room off a drowned coast. All of it going down into the muck where no government could ever reach it, because no government dives in monsoon season, and no government admits this place exists.

The corridor ahead was filling. Water came through the opened seals in gray ropes, thick with stirred sediment, and her lights barely cut it. She kicked hard, following the line she'd rigged—Callum's rope, knotted every five meters, the knots counting down toward the archive room where he'd been taken.

Where *he* was.

The thought hit her mid-stroke like a fist. She'd done the arithmetic cold, back on the boat, the way her father had taught her to do all salvage arithmetic: what must be true for the job to work. The station floods bottom-up. The archive sits above the lowest compartments. There would be time. There would be enough time.

But arithmetic was a cold bedfellow, and now, swimming into the collapsing dark after the man she loved, she understood that she had built the whole plan on a number she couldn't verify, and the sea was under no obligation to honor it.

The knot-count reached eight. Nine. The rope went slack in her glove—cut. Someone had cut it.

Her blood went to ice water.

Then a hand closed around her ankle, and she screamed into her regulator, spinning, knife out, before her light found the face beneath her—Callum, half-floating in the flooded compartment's upper reach, wedged into a pipe gallery, his hands zip-tied behind him, his eyes wide and furious and alive above his beard.

He'd cut the rope himself. To climb. To get above the waterline. To wait.

She sawed through the ties while the station groaned around them, and when his hands came free the first thing he did was grab her helmet with both palms and press his forehead to it, hard, once, like a man checking that a wall was real.

Then he pointed up. *Go.*

* * *

They went.

The station was dying around them in stages, each stage announced. First the sound—that deep structural complaint, steel remembering it was supposed to be straight. Then the dust: forty years of silt shaken loose from every seam, turning their lights into two dim constellations of drifting stars. Then the water itself changed its mind about which way was up, currents reversing as compartments equalized, dragging them sideways down a corridor that had been dry ten minutes ago.

Sebastian's shoulder hit a doorframe. Something tore in her left arm—not the suit, something under it—and pain bloomed hot and specific, and she made herself keep kicking, because stopping was a decision and she refused to make any more decisions tonight except the one she'd already made.

Callum got his arm around her waist and took her weight without being asked. They moved as one animal, four legs instead of two, up through the tilting guts of the station, past the bulkhead where the survey team had scratched their last messages—she didn't look, she couldn't look, she'd already said goodbye to the dead—and out through the breach in the operations level where the sea had first gotten in, decades ago, patient as a creditor.

And then they were through, and up, and the black water became gray water became the hammered-silver surface of the monsoon sea, and they broke into the storm like two things born.

* * *

The boat was where she'd left it, which felt like a miracle until she remembered Ferro.

Ferro's trawler was out there somewhere in the rain, running lights blazing, playing the decoy with three crewmen who owed him money and one captain who had decided, somewhere between disgust and debt, that Reyes deserved whatever he got. She could see the patrol boats' searchlights swinging wild to the north, chasing the ghost Ferro had made of them, and she allowed herself four seconds—she counted them—to feel gratitude for a man she'd spent years hating.

Then she hauled Callum over the gunwale, and the four seconds were spent.

He lay on the deck coughing seawater, and she got his fins off and his tank off and checked him with hands that wouldn't stop shaking, and he caught one of her hands and held it flat against his chest so she could feel his heartbeat doing its work underneath everything.

"You turned the wheel," he said.

"I turned the wheel."

"The whole—" He started laughing, or crying, or both; in the rain and the dark it was impossible to tell, and maybe it always had been with him. "You sank your inheritance."

"It was never mine," she said. "That was the whole point. It was never anyone's. That's why it had to go."

Lightning walked the horizon, and in its light she saw the station's marker buoy—or where it should have been. Gone. Taken down sometime in the night, by whom she never learned. As if the sea had already begun tidying up, erasing the address, unmaking the crime scene the way Reyes would have erased it, only cleaner, only forever.

Below them, three hundred feet down, tons of steel and concrete were settling into the silt shelf, and the shelf was giving way, and the records of forty years of quiet crimes were being buried under a mountain of mud that no dredge would touch and no court would order raised and no lieutenant, however patient, however polite, would ever see again.

The truth went where power couldn't follow. Not revealed. Not destroyed, exactly. Just *kept*—held the way the sea holds everything, absolutely, without malice, without mercy, without ever needing to explain itself.

Her father had helped hide it. She had finished the job. Somewhere in the difference between those two acts, she thought, was the whole distance between obedience and choice.

* * *

They ran south through the storm's ragged edge with the engine at three-quarters, which was all the old hull would forgive, and near dawn the rain thinned to a gray gauze and the wind dropped from a shout to a mutter, and Sebastian stood at the wheel with her torn arm bound tight against her ribs and watched the sun come up the color of old brass.

Callum slept in the cockpit, curled against the leeward rail, one hand still loosely closed around the safety line as if even unconscious he expected the world to try to take something from him. His face in sleep looked younger than it had any right to. She let him sleep.

There would be a reckoning. Reyes had escaped the station—she'd seen the second set of bubbles, seen the fast-attack launch peeling away north through the rain with its running lights dark—but a man who surfaced from a collapsed site empty-handed, with a deserter loose and a diver's boat unaccounted for, would not report failure. He would report a threat. He would come again, or send someone quieter, or begin the slow bureaucratic murder of her name in harbors and registries across the coast.

Let him. She had watched the sun come up over water that kept its secrets, and she found that she was not afraid the way she used to be. Fear had been the architecture of her whole life—the fear of debt, of patrols, of being seen—and she had just burned down the largest room in that house. Whatever Reyes built next would have to be built smaller.

Her arm throbbed. Her lungs ached from the compressed air and the cold. The compass—her father's compass, the twin, the key—was gone to the sea with the salvage case, given back with interest, and she found she didn't mourn it. A compass points. That's all it does. It had pointed her here, and here was enough.

Ahead, the northern horizon cleared by degrees, showing a long smudge of coast she'd never bothered to visit, in waters where nobody knew her name and nobody was paid to forget it.

Callum stirred, opened his eyes, and looked up at her at the wheel, and something passed between them that needed no radio, no rope, no knots counted in the dark.

"North?" he asked, voice rough.

"North," she said, and brought the bow around, and the sea took them the way it takes everything—completely, and without asking what they intended to become.

Behind them, far below, the station settled another few meters into the dark, and kept what it kept.
