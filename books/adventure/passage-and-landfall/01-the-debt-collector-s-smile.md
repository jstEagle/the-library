## 1. The Debt Collector's Smile

The auctioneer in Vantrell had a voice like warm honey poured over a ledger, and Rosalind Sterling hated him for it.

"Lot forty-one," he sang out, tapping the placard propped against her plane. "One Stormwing Mk. III chase aircraft, twin engine, storm-hardened canopy, custom instrumentation. Previously owned by—" he squinted at his card, and here came the smile, the one that said he knew exactly who she was and was going to enjoy it, "—Miss Rosalind Sterling. Some of you may know the name."

A ripple went through the crowd on the customs quay. Not a kind ripple. The kind that travels through a room when people are deciding whether to laugh at you or with you, and have mostly decided.

Rosalind stood at the back with her arms crossed and her jaw set, wearing her second-best coat because her best coat had been collateral since March. She had told herself she wouldn't come. She had come anyway, because a person should be present when their heart is sold.

The Stormwing sat on the tarmac looking absurdly cheerful in the morning light, her red-and-white livery freshly washed by some dockhand who hadn't known whose plane it was. Eleven years Rosalind had flown her. Eleven years of monsoon edges and hail cores, of riding the black spine of a squall line while radio towers went quiet behind her. The plane had more hours in bad weather than most pilots saw in a career, and she'd been worth every coin of the debt she'd helped run up—right up until the season the storms stopped paying.

"Shall we begin the bidding?" the auctioneer beamed. "I'll open at four hundred marks."

Four hundred marks. The plane had cost eleven thousand new. Rosalind did the arithmetic anyway, the way you pick at a scab: even if the bidding ran high, none of it would come to her. It would go to the creditors' pool, into the great gray funnel of her debts, and what remained after would remain after for a long time.

"Four-fifty," called a salvage broker with rings on every finger.

"Five hundred," said someone else, and then a voice from near the front, smooth as oiled rope:

"Two thousand."

The crowd turned. So did Rosalind, though she already knew.

Mr. Quill did not look like a man who collected debts for a living. He looked like a man who arranged flowers, or perhaps taught piano to children of good families. He was small and neat and wore dove-gray, and his smile was the single most frightening thing on the Vantrell waterfront, which was saying something, because the Vantrell waterfront featured actual pirates.

"Two thousand," the auctioneer repeated, delighted. "Do I hear twenty-five?"

Nobody heard twenty-five. Nobody heard anything at all when Mr. Quill started spending money, because everyone on that quay understood what the money meant. It wasn't a bid. It was a message, wrapped in a bow.

* * *

He found her afterward at the pie stall on Harborside Row, where she was eating a meat pie she couldn't afford and pretending it was lunch and dinner both.

"Miss Sterling." He settled onto the stool beside her uninvited, arranging his coat tails. "You must forgive the theater this morning. I find public auctions establish such useful clarity."

"You bought my plane to make a point."

"I bought your plane because it was excellent value." He smiled at the pie vendor, who suddenly remembered an urgent delivery to the far end of the row. "The point was incidental. Though I confess I do enjoy it. Miss Sterling—the Meridian Optimists have purchased your debt. All of it. Every mark, from every lender, every chit, every IOU scrawled on bar napkins across three ports. You are now, entirely and exclusively, ours."

She put down the pie. "How much."

"Does it matter? Very well—you're the sort who needs numbers. Fourteen thousand, six hundred and twelve marks, plus accrued interest, plus our standard handling fee, which I won't bore you with." He produced a folded paper from inside his coat and laid it on the counter between them, smoothing it flat with two fingers like a man smoothing a tablecloth before dinner. "Repayment terms are generous. One season."

"One *season*." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Quill, I just watched my only asset sell for two thousand marks. Where exactly am I supposed to find fourteen thousand in one season?"

"That," said Mr. Quill warmly, "is the interesting question, isn't it? The Optimists believe in people, Miss Sterling. That's rather our whole philosophy. We invest in those whom conventional lenders have given up on. And when an investment matures—" he tapped the paper, once, gently, "—we expect it to mature. There are other arrangements, of course, for those who can't pay. Deck work. Salvage crews. Certain ships that go where honest sailors won't. But I think you'd find that life... diminishing. Whereas you, I suspect, are not a diminishing sort of woman."

He stood, brushed off his coat, and left the paper there between them like a bill for a meal she hadn't eaten.

"A season," he repeated pleasantly. "We'll be watching. We always watch our investments." And he tipped his hat to the pie vendor's empty stall, to nobody, and walked away whistling.

Rosalind looked at the paper for a long time. Then she finished the pie, because whatever else happened, wasting a pie would be unforgivable.

* * *

It was raining by late afternoon, a fine gray drizzle that made the harbor lamps bloom halos, and Rosalind was sitting on the seawall at the end of Pier Nine doing sums in her head and getting the same answer every time, which was zero, when the old man spoke.

"He bought your storm bird."

She turned. A prospector, by the look of him—canvas coat gone soft as cloth-of-gold with age, boots repaired so many times they were more patch than boot, a face weathered down to essentials. He was thin the way men get thin when something is eating them from inside, and there was a smell about him that Rosalind recognized from too many hospital waiting rooms: the sweetish, chemical ghost of medicines that aren't working.

"It wasn't a bird," she said. "It was a Stormwing."

"Mm. My wife wanted me to settle down. I bought a mule instead. Named her Stormwing." He lowered himself onto the seawall beside her with the care of a man negotiating with his own joints. "Worst mule in the archipelago. Bit the postmaster. Best friend I ever had."

Despite everything—a season, fourteen thousand marks, Mr. Quill's flower-arranger smile—Rosalind felt her mouth twitch. "What happened to her?"

"Outlived her, thank the sea. Couldn't have buried her." He looked out at the water, where the drizzle stitched silver threads across the black harbor. "You flew weather, didn't you? I've seen your name. Chased the Vantrell cyclone inland two seasons back, warned the fishing fleet off the Sable shoals. Saved maybe forty boats."

"Maybe thirty-eight. Two captains never listen."

"And now the Optimists own you." He said it without pity, which she appreciated; pity would have been unbearable. "They own half this port, one way or another. They call it investment. My father called it lending with fists. Same trade, better tailoring."

"You sound like a man speaking from experience."

"I sound," said the old man, "like a man running out of time, and I haven't got enough of it left for the long approach. So I'll come straight at it." He turned and looked at her properly for the first time, and his eyes were startlingly clear, pale gray-green, the eyes of a man who had spent seventy years reading horizons. "My name is Elias Wren. I'm dying—I give it a month, less if the coughing gets ambitious. I have spent forty years looking for a ship called the Corvane, and I know where she lies. And this morning I watched a debt collector buy a storm pilot, and I thought: there. That's who delivers my letters."

Rosalind stared at him. "Your letters?"

"In good time." He drew something from inside his canvas coat, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, and held it on his knee without offering it. "First, understand what I'm asking. This isn't charity, girl, and it isn't a gift. It's a bargain, and I mean to drive a hard one, because I'm told dying men get sentimental and I refuse."

"What bargain?"

"The map is yours. The Corvane's cargo is yours—or enough of it to make fourteen thousand six hundred and twelve marks look like pocket change, which I'm given to understand is a figure currently ruining your day." His mouth quirked. "In exchange, you carry my letters home. Six of them. Sealed, unopened, delivered by hand, in order. To people who will not thank you for them. That's the price. The gold buys your freedom; the letters cost you something no amount of gold covers. Both, or nothing."

The rain thickened. Out in the harbor, a bell buoy clanked its slow idiot rhythm, and somewhere behind them the taverns were lighting up, and Rosalind sat with the debt collector's paper still folded in her pocket like a stone, and thought about how a person could lose a plane and a reputation and a future in a single day and still, somehow, be offered one more thing than she could carry.

"Why letters?" she asked finally. "If you've got a treasure map, why does any of the rest of it matter?"

Elias Wren looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind the clear gray-green eyes—something old and tired and terribly patient, the way a reef is patient under pretty water.

"Because the map is easy," he said. "The letters are the hard part. And I've learned, too late, that the hard part is the part that counts." He held out the oilcloth bundle. "Well, storm pilot? Do we have a bargain?"

The buoy clanked. The rain came down. And Rosalind Sterling, who had spent her whole life calculating odds against weather that didn't care whether she lived, reached out and took the bundle—and felt, beneath the oilcloth, the shape of a promise being made that she did not yet know would cost her nearly everything, and give her back more.

"Yes," she said. "We have a bargain."

Elias Wren smiled, and began to cough.
