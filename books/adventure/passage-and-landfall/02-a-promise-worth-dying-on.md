## 2. A Promise Worth Dying On

Elias Wren died the way he'd lived, which was to say inconveniently and slightly ahead of schedule.

Rosalind had expected him to last until morning. He had promised her as much, in fact, with the particular stubbornness of a man who treated his own mortality as a scheduling matter to be negotiated. "I'll see dawn," he'd said, propped against the salt-stiff pillows of the charity ward in Vantrell's Seamen's Rest, "because I want to watch you read the map by daylight. Nightlight flatters nothing."

But at some hour past three, when the rain on the tin roof had settled into a rhythm like a patient drummer, his breathing changed, and Rosalind—who had slept in enough cockpits and waiting rooms to know the difference between resting and leaving—woke in the chair beside him with a jolt.

"Elias."

He opened his eyes. They were clear, which surprised her; the morphine fog had been thick for two days. He looked at her the way a man looks at weather he's decided not to fight.

"Ah," he said. "You're still here. Good. I half thought you'd take the map and run."

"I don't steal from dying men."

"No," he agreed. "Worse. You make promises to them." His hand found hers on the blanket—dry, light, bird-boned—and pressed something into it: a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with tarred twine, sealed with wax that had once been red and was now the color of old blood. The letters. And beneath them, rolled tight, the map.

"Sixth tide," he said.

"What?"

"The passage through the Sables. Everyone will tell you five tides is your window before landfall. Everyone is wrong. There are six. The sixth tide comes after the storm seems to have broken—when the sky goes kind and the water goes glassy and every fool alive thinks it's over." His grip tightened with surprising strength. "The sixth tide is the sea taking back its change. You wait out the fifth, you think you've won, and then—" He made a small gesture, fingers rising and falling like a gull dropping from the air.

"You're saying the charts are wrong."

"I'm saying the charts were written by men who never stayed long enough to be corrected." A smile flickered across his face, cracked but genuine. "Promise me you'll count six."

"I promise I'll count six."

"And the letters."

"They get delivered. Every one."

"To Juniper first. My granddaughter. She lives—" He stopped. Coughed. The cough went on too long, and when it finished, something in him had gone quieter, further away. "She lives where the letter says she lives. I wrote it down. I was afraid I'd forget, near the end, so I wrote everything down. That's what a map is, girl. Just a man writing things down before he forgets."

"Elias—"

"Deliver them in order," he said. "Not because the order matters to the postman. Because it mattered to me."

Then he closed his eyes, and though he breathed for another hour, shallow as tide-pool ripples, he did not speak again. Dawn came up gray and generous over the harbor, exactly the light he'd wanted, and Rosalind sat holding an oilcloth bundle and watching it arrive without him.

* * *

The Seamen's Rest charged nothing for the room of a dead man who'd paid in advance, which was fortunate, because Rosalind had eleven credits and a plane she no longer owned. She took the bundle to a tea house near the fish market—a place called the Drowned Gull, whose owner, a broad woman named Marta with forearms like dock rope, let Rosalind occupy a corner table for the price of one cup of tea stretched across four hours.

Marta had known Elias. Everyone in Vantrell's lower harbor had known Elias Wren; he'd been coming here for forty years, telling stories that got taller every season, buying rounds he couldn't afford on nights he could. When Rosalind unrolled the map on the scarred wood, Marta appeared at her elbow with a second cup Rosalind hadn't ordered and hadn't paid for.

"He always said he'd found it," Marta said. "We all assumed it was the rum talking."

"Was there rum in the stories?"

"There was rum in everything Elias said. That was the trick of him. He buried the truth under so much nonsense that when he finally told it straight, nobody believed him." Marta tapped the map with one blunt finger. "This is straight, isn't it."

It was. That was what unsettled Rosalind most. She'd expected a drunkard's scrawl, a fantasy of inlets and X marks. Instead she found a working chart, drawn in a surveyor's careful hand—soundings in fathoms along the channels, current notes in the margins, tidal corrections annotated in a script that grew shakier toward the edges, as if the map had been made over years and finished recently. The Sable Archipelago spread across the vellum like a scatter of broken teeth: nine major islands, dozens of islets, and threading between them a route marked in red ink.

*The Wren Passage*, someone had labeled it, and beneath, in smaller letters: *Nobody else's.*

"Six tides," Rosalind murmured, tracing the route. "He said count six."

"Mm." Marta refilled her cup without asking. "Monsoon's early this year. Harbor master's been saying landfall inside three weeks. Maybe less."

Three weeks. Rosalind looked at the route again and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. The Sable Archipelago sat eight hundred miles south-southeast of Vantrell, across open water, through straits that required a pilot even in fair conditions. In monsoon conditions they required a death wish. The passage closed with landfall—not officially, not with barricades and notices, but practically, absolutely, the way a door closes when the house falls on it. After landfall, no ship sailed those waters and lived to bill anyone for the attempt.

Which meant her window was the same window everyone else's was. Including—

Her stomach dropped. She set down the cup.

"The Meridian Optimists," she said. "Their salvage fleet. They run the southern lanes this time of year."

"Everyone knows it."

"Do they know about the Corvane?"

Marta shrugged, which was its own answer: everyone knew the story of the Corvane, the treasure ship that went down in the Sables sixty years ago with a hold full of Meridian silver and a passenger list full of people who should have known better. Everyone knew the story. Nobody had ever produced the wreck. Stories without wrecks were just weather talk.

But Elias had soundings. Elias had current notes. Elias had a red line drawn through water where no red line had ever been drawn before, and if the Optimists' fleet learned that a chart existed—if they learned that a woman with a motive and a debt had walked out of a charity ward carrying it—

Rosalind thought about Verrick, the debt collector with the pleasant face and the pleasant voice, explaining to her yesterday that the Meridian Optimists had purchased her obligations and considered her, in their phrase, *an investment awaiting return*. She thought about how pleasantly he'd smiled when he said it. She thought about the Optimists' fleet: seven hulls, armed, fast, crewed by men who called violence *asset recovery*.

They wouldn't need her map. They'd just need to follow her.

"Well," she said aloud, to the map, to the tea, to the ghost of a dying man's stubbornness. "That's a problem."

"That's several problems," Marta said comfortably. "More tea?"

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the Drowned Gull's corner doing what she did best, which was planning around impossible constraints. It was, she reflected, the same skill set as storm chasing: identify the wall you can't go through, find the gap, accept that the gap will be terrifying, go anyway.

Problem one: she needed a ship. Not a plane this time—the Sables were water country, reef country, tide country. A ketch, ideally. Something shallow-drafted and forgiving, which meant cheap, which meant broken, which meant she had eleven credits.

Problem two: she needed a crew. She counted on her fingers and ran out of fingers almost immediately. A pilot who knew the southern tides—she knew exactly one, and Sefa Ilori hated her, or at least had excellent reasons to. Hands. Rigger. Someone who could navigate by stars when the electronics drowned, because the electronics would drown.

Problem three: the fleet. Seven ships against whatever floating apology she managed to acquire. She couldn't outrun them. She couldn't outgun them. Which left outsmarting them, and the monsoon, and the sixth tide—Elias's gift, the extra beat hidden inside the storm's rhythm, the thing the charts didn't know. If the Optimists believed five tides was the window, they'd sail the window like everyone else. But if she waited for the sixth—

If she waited for the sixth, she'd be sailing water that killed everyone who trusted the official numbers, guided only by a dead man's marginalia and her own nerve.

Rosalind put her head briefly in her hands.

"You could sell it," Marta suggested, not unkindly, from behind the counter. "The map. The Optimists would pay well for it. Clear your debts, start over."

"I'd be selling a dead man's confession."

"You'd be selling a dead man's *map*. Confessions are free. He gave you plenty."

Rosalind looked up. "You're supposed to be the wise barkeep who gently steers me toward my better nature."

"I'm the realistic barkeep who's seen better natures drown." Marta wiped the counter in slow circles. "But I liked Elias. And he liked you, which worried me, because his taste in people was terrible right up until it wasn't." She nodded at the bundle. "What else is in there? The letters?"

Rosalind weighed the oilcloth in her hands. Heavy for paper. Dozens of envelopes, maybe more, bound in bundles by year. Forty years of writing home and never sending—or sending and keeping drafts; she wouldn't know until she broke the seal, and she wasn't going to break the seal. That wasn't hers to open. That was a granddaughter's, somewhere, waiting for a stranger to arrive with her grandfather's whole heart tied in twine.

"A promise," Rosalind said. "Sealed and addressed."

"And you keep those."

"I'm out of practice," Rosalind admitted. "But yes. I keep those."

Outside, the rain had thickened. The harbor master's flags had gone up at midday—storm advisory, southern lanes, all traffic advised to seek shelter. Three weeks, maybe less. Somewhere south, seven black-hulled ships were making ready for a salvage season, and somewhere among their ledgers was a page with her name on it and a number beside it that she owed.

Rosalind rolled the map carefully along its original creases, tied the bundle, and stood.

"Marta. If a girl comes looking for me—young, angry, probably shouting about her grandfather—"

"Wren's blood?" Marta raised an eyebrow. "I'll send her to you."

"Don't send her anywhere. Just tell me." Rosalind pulled her jacket on. "And Marta—do you know anyone selling a ketch?"

Marta's laugh followed her out into the rain, warm as a lamp in a window.

By nightfall Rosalind had walked the length of the lower harbor twice, gotten laughed at by three brokers, and found, wedged at the end of a rotting pier, a patched ketch named *Vespera* whose owner wanted an absurdly low price and an absurdly quick sale, and whose hull, when Rosalind knocked her knuckles against it, sounded honest. By midnight she'd spent ten of her eleven credits on a handshake agreement and a bill of sale written on the back of a tavern receipt.

Standing on the *Vespera*'s tilting deck in the dark, map under her arm, letters in her bag, one credit in her pocket, Rosalind laughed at herself—short, disbelieving, a little wild.

"Count six," she said to the rain. "All right, old man. I'm counting."

The monsoon would make landfall in three weeks. The Optimists' fleet would sail within days. And somewhere out there, a granddaughter named Juniper was about to learn that strangers kept showing up with her family's unfinished business—which meant Rosalind had perhaps a week, at best, before her quiet departure became a very loud confrontation.

She went below to sleep in a hammock that smelled of mildew and old adventures, and for the first time since Verrick's smile, she slept without dreaming of debt.
