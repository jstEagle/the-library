## 3. Crew of the Unwanted

Rosalind found Sefa Vane at the bottom of a bottle in a dockside tavern called the Drowned Gull, which was fitting, because the woman herself looked like something the sea had given up on.

"You're the tide-pilot," Rosalind said, sliding onto the stool beside her. "The one who threaded the Needle Straits blind during the Gray Gale."

"The one who *lost her license* threading the Needle Straits," Sefa corrected, without looking up. Her hands were remarkable even wrapped around a glass—long-fingered, scarred at the knuckles, steady as a compass rose. "There were two of us that day. Only one got blamed for it. Guess which."

"I read the inquiry transcript. The harbor master's son was at the wheel when she went aground."

Sefa finally turned. Her eyes were the pale gray-green of water over sand, and they took Rosalind's measure in about two seconds flat. "You did your homework. That's either flattering or alarming, and I haven't decided which. Who's asking?"

"Rosalind Sterling." She set Elias Wren's map on the bar between them, weighted with a coin so it wouldn't curl. "I need someone who can read water like scripture and isn't afraid of what the Optimists will do if they catch us reading it."

Sefa looked at the map for a long moment. Then she laughed—a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years.

"That's the Corvane route." She pushed the glass away as though it had personally offended her. "Nobody's run that passage in thirty years. Nobody's *survived* running it in twenty-five."

"Then we'll be the first in a while. I'm offering crew share, not wages. There's treasure involved."

"There's always treasure involved." But Sefa was already standing, already reaching for the oilskin coat slung over her chair, and Rosalind understood that the answer had been yes since the moment the map touched the bar. "One condition. I pick the rum ration. Last captain watered it and I nearly mutinied, and I say that with love."

"Noted. Welcome aboard the Vespera."

"She's a ketch? What condition is she in?"

"Patched."

"*Patched.*" Sefa said the word the way other people said *cursed*. "Wonderful. I've missed working with professionals."

* * *

Rosalind found Callum three hours later, and finding him was considerably less pleasant.

She'd gone to the Meridian docks to buy rope—good rope, honest rope, the kind that didn't fray when your life depended on it—and there he was, hauling crates up a gangplank with his sleeves rolled and his forearms corded, looking infuriatingly like their mother around the jaw. He saw her before she could decide whether to pretend she hadn't seen him.

"Well," Callum said, setting down his crate. "The storm chaser comes home."

"I don't come home. I'm buying rope."

"Eleven years, Roz. Eleven years, and you're buying rope forty feet from me."

"Twelve," she said, because she'd counted too, and she wasn't going to let him have the moral high ground of better arithmetic. "And you know why."

"I know why *you think*—" He stopped himself, exhaled through his nose, and glanced at the crate, the gangplank, the foreman already shouting his name. Something in his face shifted from anger to a tired kind of curiosity. "What are you actually doing here, Rosalind?"

She should have lied. A sensible person would have lied. Instead she heard herself say, "I bought a ship. I'm sailing into the Sable Archipelago ahead of the monsoon to find a wreck the Optimists want badly enough to kill for, and my tide-pilot says my rigging is an insult to the concept of rope."

Callum stared at her. Then, horribly, he started to laugh—that same laugh their mother had, helpless and warm even when it was aimed at disaster. "Of course you are. Of course. You always did treat suicide as a career opportunity."

"It's called adventure where I come from."

"Where you come from, people get buried at sea." He was quiet a moment, weighing something. Rosalind watched him weigh it and hated how well she still knew his face. "Crew share?" he asked finally.

"Crew share. No wages until we find it."

"Naturally." He picked his crate back up. "Give me till end of shift. And Roz—if this is your way of apologizing, it's the worst apology ever constructed."

"It's not an apology. It's a job offer."

"Right." He shouldered past her toward the hold, then paused. "It's a terrible job offer. I'll be there by six."

* * *

Juniper Wren had been tracking Rosalind Sterling for nine days, and she wanted it noted—for the record, for posterity, for whatever indifferent god ran the shipping lanes—that it had been *hard*.

The trail had started in Vantrell's charity ward, where a nurse remembered a storm chaser visiting a dying prospector. It had continued through three taverns, two pawnshops, and one extremely unhelpful harbormaster who'd tried to charge her for information Juniper then extracted for free by waiting until he was drunk. She was nineteen, she had eleven coins to her name, and she had spent most of them learning that grief makes excellent fuel and terrible navigation.

So when she finally spotted the tall woman with the wind-burned face coming down the gangplank of a patched-up ketch, Juniper didn't hesitate. She planted herself at the foot of the plank, arms crossed, and said:

"Give me my grandfather's letters."

Rosalind Sterling stopped. Looked at her. And Juniper watched recognition move across her face like weather—the flicker of *Elias Wren*, then the arithmetic of *granddaughter*, then something guarded settling into place.

"You're Juniper."

"I'm Juniper. You're the woman who promised a dying man something and then walked off with everything he owned. So." She held out her hand, palm up. "Letters. Now."

"They're on the ship."

"Then go get them."

For a moment Juniper thought the woman would refuse—or worse, patronize her, tell her to sit down somewhere safe, use that reasonable voice adults used when they'd decided you were a problem to be managed. Instead, Rosalind Sterling studied her the way she might study a storm front: carefully, respectfully, with an eye for what it might do next.

"When did you last eat?" Rosalind asked.

"What does that have to do with anything?"

"It has to do with the fact that you look ready to fight me, and I'd rather you did it on a full stomach. Come aboard. We'll talk."

"I'm not staying on your ship."

"No," Rosalind agreed, stepping aside to let her pass. "You're eating soup on my ship, and then you're deciding. There's a difference, and it matters."

Juniper went, mostly because her legs had been arguing for rest for two days and had just won the vote. The ketch below decks was cramped and smelled of tar and someone's attempt at cooking, and a gray-eyed woman at the table looked up and said, "Oh good, another one. Are we recruiting off the street now, or do they all track us down individually?"

"Individually," Rosalind said. "This is Juniper Wren. Elias's granddaughter."

The gray-eyed woman's eyebrows went up. "The mapmaker's granddaughter. Well. Sit down, girl, you've got sea-legs written all over your future whether you like it or not."

The soup was fishy and oversalted and Juniper ate every drop while Rosalind sat across from her and explained—plainly, without softening it—about the debt, the promise, the map, the monsoon, the Optimists. When she finished, she reached into a locker and brought out the bundle: letters tied with twine, sealed, addressed in a shaking hand to *J. Wren, Millbrook Lane.*

Juniper took them and did not open them. Not yet. Her hands wouldn't have been steady enough, and she refused to give this woman the sight of it.

"He wrote these for me?"

"He wrote them for you. He made me promise to deliver them. I intend to keep that promise."

"By sailing them through a monsoon instead of posting them."

"By delivering them myself, to you, alive, along with whatever else he left." Rosalind leaned forward. "Your grandfather gave me that map because he trusted me with it. I don't expect you to trust me yet. Trust is earned, and I've got nothing in the bank. But I'll tell you what I told him: I'll see it done right or die trying, and I'd rather it was the first one."

Juniper turned the bundle over in her hands. Somewhere under the twine and paper was her grandfather's handwriting, his voice, the man who'd vanished from her mother's life before Juniper was born and shown up again only to die in a charity ward smelling of salt and failure. She had questions that would fill a book. She suspected the letters would answer none of them honestly.

"You're going into the Archipelago," she said.

"We leave with tomorrow's tide."

"The Optimists have a fleet."

"Yes."

"And you have a patched ketch, a disgraced pilot, and—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the deck, toward the docks, "—whoever else you've scraped together."

"That's about the shape of it, yes."

Juniper looked down at the letters. She thought of her mother, who had spent thirty years not talking about Elias Wren and had taught her daughter to do the same, and how that silence had curdled into its own kind of inheritance. She thought of eleven coins. She thought of the nurse in the charity ward saying, *he talked about you at the end, dear. Talked about you constant.*

"I want to read these first," Juniper said. "Alone. Before I decide anything."

"Forward cabin's empty. Door doesn't lock, but nobody on this ship knocks anyway; it's a whole culture problem we're working on."

Juniper stood, bundle clutched to her chest, and paused at the cabin door. "If I read these and they say you cheated him—lied to him, stole from him—I'll take the map and burn it."

"Fair," Rosalind said. "I'd expect nothing less from his blood."

That, unexpectedly, almost made Juniper smile.

* * *

She read until the lantern burned low. Some of the letters were decades old, unsent, yellowed soft as cloth. Some were recent, the handwriting disintegrating line by line as the illness took him. All of them were addressed to her, or to her mother, or to a version of the family he'd walked out on—and all of them circled the same confession without quite landing on it: *the Corvane, the Corvane, I have to see it once more, and then I swear I'll come home.*

He never came home. He died forty miles from where he'd started.

When Juniper came back up on deck, dawn was graying the harbor and Rosalind was already awake, coiling rope with a man who had Callum's jawline and a stranger's wariness. They both stopped when she appeared.

"I'm coming," Juniper said. "Not for you. For him. Somebody in this family ought to finish what he started, and I'm the only one left."

Rosalind set down the rope. Something moved behind her eyes—not triumph, exactly. More like relief wearing triumph's coat.

"Welcome aboard the Vespera," she said. "We sail on the morning tide."

"And after that?" Juniper asked.

Rosalind nodded toward the harbor mouth, where, far out beyond the breakwater, a black-hulled ship with clean lines and too many guns was sliding slowly across the horizon, flying no colors at all.

"After that," she said quietly, "we race."
