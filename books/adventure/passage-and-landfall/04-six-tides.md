## 4. Six Tides

Sefa Tideborn had been reading water since before she could read words, and the first thing the water told her about the *Vespera* was that she was a liar.

Not a bad ship. A patched one—new canvas on old bones, a hull scarred with a dozen repairs, her name repainted so recently you could still smell the turpentine under the salt. She told the world she was a humble coastal trader, and she was lying, because no coastal trader in history had ever carried a jury-rigged windlass like the one bolted to her foredeck, or a hold reinforced for heavy cargo, or a captain who checked her lines three times before dawn like a woman saying goodbye to something.

Sefa stood at the helm with her bare feet on wet planks and let the ship lie to her. Lying was fine. Sailing was about knowing which lies would hold.

"Port tack," she said. "We'll shave the point."

Rosalind Sterling looked up from the chart spread across the companionway hatch, pencil between her teeth. "The point has rocks."

"The point has *some* rocks." Sefa tapped the rail. "The tide's turning. In an hour it'll be a different argument. We take it now or we lose two hours tacking wide."

Rosalind studied her for a long moment—the way she'd been studying everyone since Vantrell, cataloguing them like items in an inventory she couldn't afford to get wrong. Then she nodded once and went back to the chart.

That was the thing about Rosalind Sterling, Sefa was learning. She didn't argue with the sea's employees. She just wanted to see the ledger.

* * *

By noon they had cleared the outer islands of the Sable Archipelago's northern fringe, and Sefa called the first crew meeting of the voyage, mostly because she needed to say out loud what she'd been calculating alone since dawn, and numbers spoken aloud became real in a way numbers kept in the skull never did.

She chalked it on the deckhouse door, because the *Vespera* had no slate and Callum had suggested the table and Juniper had said, flatly, "I'm not eating off your arithmetic."

"Six tides," Sefa said, drawing a jagged line across the door. "That's what we have."

Callum leaned against the rail, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who had spent his whole life being compared to his sister and had decided to enjoy losing. "Six tides to what, exactly? Ruin?"

"To the wreck lagoon. Before landfall." Sefa drew six marks. "Everyone knows the monsoon closes the passage. What nobody says out loud is *when*. It doesn't close all at once—it closes from the south, island by island, like a hand closing finger by finger. The wreck sits past the fifth channel. After landfall, the sixth channel—" she tapped the last mark hard enough to snap the chalk, "—becomes a killing ground. Forty-foot seas funneled into a half-mile gap. No pilot alive takes a ship through that. I wouldn't. I *am* a pilot, and I wouldn't."

"So we have until the sixth tide," Juniper said.

"We have until the sixth tide *begins*. If we're not anchored in the lagoon when it starts, we don't dive the Corvane. We go home. Or we sink trying, which is also going home, in a sense."

A silence followed, the particular silence of four people doing the same math and arriving at the same ugly number.

"And the Optimists' fleet?" Rosalind asked quietly.

"Faster ships. Deeper keels—they can't follow us through the shallows at Third Reef, but they don't need to. They know where we're going; they've known since Vantrell. They don't have to beat us. They only have to arrive while we're still hauling treasure up from eighty feet down." Sefa wiped chalk dust on her trousers. "Which means our race isn't against them. It's against the sky. Them, we can outthink. The monsoon, we can only outrun."

Callum whistled low. "My sister always did pick ambitious ways to die."

"She's not dying," Juniper said, with a fierceness that surprised everyone including, by her face, herself. "My grandfather mapped this route. He sailed it twice. There are shortcuts in his notes that aren't on any chart."

"Shortcuts," Callum repeated. "In the Sable Archipelago. During monsoon season."

"He called them 'opportunities.'"

"He sounds like he was a delight at dinner parties."

"He died alone on a beach," Juniper said, "so probably not many dinner parties toward the end."

The silence that followed was different from the first one—heavier, more ashamed—and Sefa watched Rosalind step into it the way she stepped into everything: directly, without checking the depth first.

"Elias Wren gave me this chance," Rosalind said. "He knew exactly what it cost. We're not going to waste it arguing about who grieves correctly. Juniper—your grandfather's notes. Callum—you're on the foredeck, and if you make one more joke about dying I'm putting you on bilge duty. Sefa—"

"I know," Sefa said. "I sail."

"You sail."

* * *

They made the point with twenty minutes to spare, the *Vespera* sliding over rocks that were already beginning to breathe beneath the rising tide, close enough that Callum, leaning over the rail, claimed afterward he could see a fish looking back at him with what he chose to interpret as respect.

It was the first time the crew laughed together, and Sefa noted it the way she noted wind shifts: a small change, possibly nothing, possibly everything.

By the second evening, the changes were accumulating. Callum turned out to be good with rope in a way that suggested he'd learned it somewhere he didn't talk about, and he'd stopped making jokes about dying, mostly, and had started making jokes about Sefa's cooking instead, which was safer territory because her cooking was genuinely terrible and everyone agreed.

"You call this rice?" he said, poking his bowl.

"I call it fuel," Sefa said. "You want flavor, swim ashore and negotiate with an island."

"He's right, though," Juniper said, not looking up from Elias's notes, which she read every evening by lamplight, copying passages into her own hand as though afraid the originals might evaporate. "It's terrible rice."

"It's *efficient* rice."

"It's rice that has given up," Callum said. "Even the rice knows we're doomed."

And Rosalind, sitting apart with the map, laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge that hadn't moved in years—and Sefa saw Juniper glance up at the sound and then quickly away, filing it somewhere.

Found family, Sefa thought. Nobody plans it. You just look up one evening and realize you'd sink for these people, and you have no idea when that happened.

* * *

It was near the end of the third day, with the light going long and gold across a sea that was behaving suspiciously well, that Sefa felt it first—not saw, *felt*, the way you feel someone watching the back of your neck.

She climbed the shrouds without being asked to, wedged herself against the crosstrees, and looked astern.

There. Far back, at the edge of sight where sea becomes rumor: a sail. White, clean, running the same course they were running, holding the same distance it had held, Sefa would have bet her license, for the last six hours.

She dropped to the deck and said nothing dramatic. That wasn't her way. She simply took the wheel from Callum, adjusted their heading two points to the north, and watched.

Ten minutes later, the sail adjusted too.

"Sterling," she called. "Company."

Rosalind came up the companionway ladder fast. Juniper appeared from the notes. Callum shaded his eyes and squinted aft like a man hoping the horizon would blink first.

"Optimist scout," Rosalind said. Not a question.

"Been shadowing us since midday, I'd guess. Keeping just past visual range. Testing whether we change course." Sefa held the wheel steady. "Which we just did. So now they know we know."

"Can we lose them?"

"Not in this ship, not in this wind. She's built for shoals, not speed. And even if we could—" Sefa nodded south, where the horizon had begun, very faintly, very early, to organize itself into something darker than weather should be, "—we don't want to lose them. We want them to follow us exactly where we're going. Right up until the moment following stops being survivable."

Juniper looked at her. "Third Reef."

"Third Reef," Sefa agreed. "Their keels draw nine feet. The reef passage draws seven and a half, and it doesn't draw seven and a half everywhere—it draws seven and a half in a channel as wide as this ship is long, and it moves with the tide. Their charts show it as impassable. My charts show it as impassable." She allowed herself a thin smile. "Elias Wren's charts, apparently, show it as an opportunity."

"My grandfather," Juniper said slowly, "was either a genius or a murderer."

"Often the same person," Callum said. "Family trait, I hear."

Rosalind was watching the scout sail, her face unreadable, doing whatever arithmetic she did behind those eyes. "How long before they signal the fleet?"

"If they haven't already? A day. Maybe less." Sefa calculated, then recalculated, hating both answers. "Which means the race just changed shape. It's not six tides anymore. It's five, maybe four and a half, because once the main fleet gets our position they'll stop following politely and start arriving."

"Then we don't sleep much," Rosalind said.

"We don't sleep at all," Sefa corrected. "I need everyone who can hold a line to hold a line. Callum—foredeck. Juniper—I want your grandfather's reef notes memorized by morning, every sounding, every marker. If I ask you 'what did Elias write about the third channel' in the dark, in rain, with the ship falling off a wave, you answer me in one breath or we die."

"That's a lot of pressure to put on a dead man's handwriting," Juniper said, but she was already turning toward the lamp.

"It's a lot of pressure to put on all of us," Sefa said. "That's what a race is."

Rosalind lingered at the wheel beside her as the others moved off, and for a moment neither woman spoke. Astern, the white sail held its patient distance. Ahead, the southern sky continued its slow, deliberate bruising.

"You've done this before," Rosalind said. "Run from something bigger than you."

"Once." Sefa kept her eyes on the compass. "I lost the ship and my license and eleven years of my life. But I saved the people. So I count it a win, and the licensing board counts it otherwise, and that's the difference between me and them."

"What happened?"

"Bad weather. Worse orders. I was told to run a passage I knew was closed, because the cargo was worth more than the crew." She shrugged. "I ran a different passage. Saved the crew. Lost the cargo. The Meridian Optimists owned the cargo."

Rosalind went very still. "The Optimists."

"They ruin everybody eventually, Sterling. You, me, that poor man Elias. It's almost a kind of equality." Sefa nodded at the white sail. "Which is why I took this job despite the rice, the pay, and the company. Because some debts you pay in coin, and some you pay in weather."

Rosalind looked at her for a long moment. "Thank you," she said finally, as though the words cost something, as though she hadn't said them to anyone in years.

"Don't thank me yet," Sefa said. "Thank me when we're through Third Reef. Thank me when we're anchored in the lagoon. Thank me on the sixth tide, if we live to see it."

She put the wheel over two points, and the *Vespera* leaned into the freshening wind like a tired animal that had decided, against all evidence, to trust its rider.

Behind them, the scout's white sail caught the last of the light and burned like a signal fire.

Ahead, the first outrider clouds of the monsoon stood up along the horizon, black and enormous, waiting to be counted.

Four and a half tides, Sefa thought. Give or take everything.
