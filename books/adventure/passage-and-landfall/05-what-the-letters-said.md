## 5. What the Letters Said

Juniper Wren had been avoiding the bundle for four days, which was impressive, considering she slept with it under her head.

It sat there every night like a warm stone, breathing against her ear. She could feel the letters through the oilcloth—thirty-odd envelopes gone soft at the corners, tied in twine that had been knotted and reknotted so many times it looked like a sailor's fist. Her grandfather's handwriting on each one. *For Maren.* Her mother's name, over and over, in ink faded from brown to the color of old tea.

She hadn't opened them. She told herself it was because there hadn't been time—the Vespera was a small ship running a hard schedule, and everyone aboard had a job. Juniper's job, as assigned by Rosalind Sterling on her second day aboard, was "cook, lookout, and general conscience," which Juniper suspected was a polite way of saying *passenger*. But the truth was simpler and uglier: as long as the letters stayed sealed, her grandfather was still a man who had walked out of their kitchen one gray morning to "check his traps" and simply never come back. As long as they stayed sealed, he might still have an explanation.

On the fifth morning, the sea went strange.

Juniper came up on deck with a pot of coffee and found Sefa standing at the bow with her head tilted, listening to the water the way other people listened to music.

"Barometer's falling faster than I like," Sefa said without turning around. "There's weather behind that headland. Big, slow, stupid weather."

"Stupid?"

"Weather that doesn't know where it's going." Sefa accepted coffee with both hands, which was how you knew she was worried; she usually trusted no one else's grip. "Rosalind wants to scout ahead of it. We'll run up the channel while it decides."

The Vespera was patched and stubborn and smelled permanently of tar and Callum's terrible pipe tobacco, and Juniper had begun, against all her intentions, to love her. Which made what happened next worse.

Because below decks, in the narrow cabin she shared with crates of dried fish, Juniper finally sat down, untied the twine, and opened the first letter.

*My dear Maren,* it began. *I have found it.*

---

The letters were dated across eleven years. Juniper read them in order because disorder felt like disrespect, and by the third one her hands were shaking.

He had found the Corvane. Not rumors, not a chart with a hopeful circle drawn on it—he had *found* it, thirty years ago, diving off a borrowed boat with a rope and a lantern, and he had seen the masts standing in clear green water like a drowned forest. He described the gold in language that embarrassed her to read, the way a grown man describes a woman he's too proud of. And then, in the fourth letter, written in a hand gone suddenly small:

*I am coming home. I mean it this time. The tide will hold another season, and a man can't spend his whole life holding his breath. Tell your mother I'm sorry for the winter I missed. Tell her I'll be at the spring planting.*

He wasn't. The fifth letter explained why: a storm season, a debt, a partner who'd stolen his charts. The sixth letter apologized. The seventh apologized again, longer, with more detail about tides. By the ninth letter he was apologizing for the apologies, and by the eleventh year the letters stopped being addressed to Maren at all—they were addressed to himself, justifications wearing her name like a coat.

*I'll go back when I've secured our future. What kind of grandfather would I be if I brought you nothing?*

Juniper read that line six times. She thought of her mother, Maren, who had waited three winters before she stopped setting a place at the table, and who had never once said a word against Elias Wren in front of his granddaughter, which was its own kind of wound. She thought of herself at nine years old, learning to gut fish because someone had to, learning not to ask.

And then she read the last letter, the newest one, the one with the map folded inside it, and understood that the map was not a treasure chart at all.

It was a confession. Every reef marked, every safe anchorage, every warning about the sixth tide—it was Elias saying *here is everything I chose over you, laid out plainly, so you can weigh it yourself.* He had even written it in the margin, in that cramped late hand: *If you're reading this, Junie, I traded you for this. I don't expect forgiveness. I expect arithmetic.*

"Well," Juniper said aloud, to the empty cabin, in a voice she didn't recognize. "Arithmetic."

She came up the companionway ladder with the letters clutched to her chest like something she meant to throw overboard, and found Rosalind Sterling at the wheel, scouting the weather line with a spyglass, looking infuriatingly calm.

"You knew," Juniper said.

Rosalind lowered the glass. "Knew what?"

"He found it. Thirty years ago. He stood on the deck of some rotten borrowed boat and looked at it, and he turned around and wrote my mother a letter promising to come home, and then he—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "He did the arithmetic. You were there when he died. Did he tell you? Did he do his little confession, hand you the map, and let you carry it away like it was clean?"

"Juniper—"

"Don't." She advanced down the deck, letters fanned out like a losing hand of cards. "You want to know why I came after you? It wasn't the gold. My mother is dead, Rosalind. She died last spring still waiting for him, still defending him, and I wanted—I needed—one honest thing out of that man. One letter that said *I chose wrong.* And instead he wrote a *chart.*"

The wind chose that moment to shift, and Sefa's voice came down from the bow, sharp as a gaff hook: "Both of you! Reef board! Shoal water off the starboard bow!"

But Juniper was twenty-two years old and full of eleven years of letters, and she kept walking. "You didn't even know him. You met him *dying.* You got the best version, the version that finally wanted to give something back, and you think that makes you his heir—"

"I think it makes me his postman!" Rosalind shouted back—and then her face changed, because she'd looked past Juniper's shoulder at the water, and the water was wrong.

It was wrong in the way Sefa had been dreading all morning: a long pale seam of broken color stretching across the channel where no chart showed anything but deep blue-green, and the Vespera, driven by a gust that arrived like a slammed door, was sliding straight toward it.

"Hard to port!" Sefa screamed. "CALLUM!"

Callum came up the hatch already moving, and the two of them threw themselves at the wheel together, and the ship heeled so hard that Juniper went to her knees on the deck, letters skidding from her arms. One page tore free and went cartwheeling aft, and Rosalind—wheel pinned under her hands, knuckles white—caught it against her boot with a dancer's reflexes and held it there, trapped, until the moment passed.

They cleared the shoal by what Sefa later estimated was "a boat length and a prayer, and I only vouch for the boat length." The uncharted reef slid past beneath the hull, close enough that Juniper could see pale coral through the water, beautiful and indifferent, like everything else in her family.

Silence, afterward. The bad kind, and then the working kind, sails trimmed, course steadied, everyone breathing.

Juniper sat on the deck with her back against the mast. Rosalind came and sat down beside her—not too close, an arm's length of truce—and held out the rescued page. Juniper took it. It was the last letter, the confession, the arithmetic.

"I didn't know him either," Rosalind said quietly. "You're right. I met a dying man who was kind to me because I was the first person in years who looked at him like he was worth something. Maybe I mistook that for wisdom." She watched the horizon, where the storm was building its slow gray cathedral. "But here's what I know. He gave me those letters and made me promise to deliver them before he'd even show me the map. The map was the *afterthought*, Juniper. The letters were the point. He wasn't asking forgiveness. He was asking delivery. There's a difference, and I think he knew it, and I think it scared him."

Juniper stared at the page in her hands. *I expect arithmetic.* But underneath it, smudged, almost erased, a second line she'd missed in the cabin's bad light: *Tell her I kept every one of her replies. Even the angry ones. Especially the angry ones.*

Her mother had written back. All those years, Maren had written back, and Juniper had never known.

"Oh," she said, very small.

"Yeah," said Rosalind. Gentle, for once. No smile, no strategy, just a tired woman on a tired ship.

Sefa appeared at the rail above them, and her face was doing something Juniper had learned to dread. "That reef isn't on any chart I've ever seen," she said. "Which means either my charts are wrong—and they're not—or somebody's been keeping this passage quiet on purpose." She paused. "Also, we have company. Sail astern, hull-down, running the same heading. Been there since midmorning."

Callum came to stand beside his sister, and for once he didn't joke. "Optimist colors?"

"Too far to tell." Sefa's jaw tightened. "Too fast to be a fishing boat."

Juniper folded the letters along their old creases, carefully, and tucked them inside her shirt, against her heart, where they should have been all along. The storm was coming, and whatever was behind them was coming faster, and somewhere beneath all that gray water lay the thing her grandfather had loved more than his family—or perhaps, she was beginning to suspect, the thing he'd believed he had to choose between.

"The sixth tide," she said. "His notes say the passage closes at the sixth tide. Whatever we're going to do, we'd better do it fast."
