## 6. The Optimists' Bargain

Callum Sterling had spent eleven years learning to be invisible on docks, and he was good at it. He could stack crates like a man thinking about nothing. He could mend netting with the vacant serenity of a monk. He had once stood three feet from a harbormaster who was shouting his name and been waved through the gate as "some other fella."

So when the black-hulled ship came up over the horizon astern of the *Vespera*—sleek as a knife, sails trimmed with the obscene confidence of money—his first instinct was to become furniture.

His second instinct, arriving about four seconds later, was to notice that the ship was gaining on them, and that no amount of crate-stacking was going to help.

"They're not even trying," Sefa said at the wheel, squinting aft. She said it the way a craftsman describes bad joinery: personally offended. "Look at their wake. They've got half a knot in reserve. They're *strolling*."

"Maybe they're friendly strollers," Callum offered.

Nobody laughed. Juniper looked up from Elias's notes long enough to say, "Optimist hull. I've seen the shape in Grandfather's sketches. Meridian fleet." Then she went back to reading, which Callum had learned was her version of screaming into a pillow.

Rosalind stood at the stern rail for a long moment, watching the flagship grow. Callum watched his sister watch it, and saw the arithmetic happening behind her eyes—the same arithmetic she'd done at fourteen, dividing their mother's last coins into "food" and "escape." It always came out short.

"We can't outrun them," she said finally. "Sefa?"

"Not in this ketch, not in this wind, not in this lifetime."

"Then we don't try. We let them catch us, we smile, and we lie beautifully." She turned, and her gaze snagged on Callum a beat too long. "Callum. You've dealt with Optimists before."

It wasn't a question. Heat crawled up the back of his neck. Everyone aboard knew—he'd told them himself, back in Vantrell, the whole humiliating ledger of it: two seasons of salvage work gone sideways, a debt he'd signed too fast, interest that grew like barnacles. The Optimists didn't send collectors for small debts. They sent *opportunities*.

"A bit," he said.

"Good," Rosalind said, with the bright false cheer she used when things were worst. "You're our ambassador."

---

The flagship's launch came alongside an hour before dusk—a longboat crewed by six rowers with matching oiled jackets, and one passenger sitting in the stern like cargo that had opinions.

She introduced herself as Captain Vess Ondine of the *Meridian Grace*, and she climbed aboard the *Vespera* with the ease of someone who had never once been told no by a boat. She was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with a laugh line architecture suggesting she found most things funny and none of them amusing.

"Rosalind Sterling," Ondine said, shaking her hand. "We've met. Vantrell, the auction where they took your plane. You made a joke about the engine. It was a good joke."

"It was a true joke."

"All the best ones are." Her gaze swept the deck—the patched sails, Sefa's white-knuckled elegance at the wheel, Juniper pretending to be furniture now, which ran in the family apparently—and settled on Callum with a small, satisfied click, like a lock finding its key. "And you'd be the brother."

"Callum."

"I know. We hold your paper." She smiled warmly. "Four hundred and sixty marks, plus carrying costs. It grows while we talk. Isn't that marvelous? Debt is the only honest crop. You plant nothing and harvest everything."

"You should write almanacs," Callum said.

"I have written almanacs. Nobody buys them." She turned back to Rosalind. "Here's my situation, Sterling. My fleet was scattered by that squall two nights ago—one ship lost contact entirely, and I've spent a day being furious about it. Meanwhile, here you are, a woman with a reputation for finding weather nobody else can find, sailing a straight line through the Sable Archipelago during monsoon season, which no sane person does." She spread her hands. "I'm not a suspicious woman. I'm a curious one. Curiosity is just greed wearing spectacles."

"We're visiting family," Rosalind said.

"In the archipelago?"

"The family is a reef."

Ondine laughed—a real one, full-bodied, and Callum felt the trap close another notch, because she was *likable*, and that was the Optimists' whole genius. Not cruelty. Charm. Violence with excellent manners.

"Well," Ondine said, "I'll leave you to your reef. But before I go—" and there it was, the pivot, smooth as a tide turn—"I understand your brother has been meaning to settle his account. Four hundred sixty marks is a heavy thing to carry across open water. Storms sink men under less."

Every person on that deck stopped breathing politely.

"I'm offering him a job," Ondine continued, addressing the whole company now, magnanimous as a festival. "Aboard the *Grace*. Fair wages, fair terms, debt cleared on signing. A fresh start. Some men aren't cut out for family voyages. No shame in it. Blood is a terrible reason to trust anyone—I've read that somewhere, possibly in my own almanac."

She let it sit there. Callum felt everyone looking at him and kept his face doing what it did best, which was nothing.

"I'll think about it," he said.

"Think quickly," Ondine said pleasantly. "We'll be alongside again tomorrow evening. After that, well—" she gestured at the darkening sky, the building swell, the whole enormous indifferent machinery of the monsoon—"the sea decides things after that. And the sea has never once honored my contracts."

She went down the ladder, waved like a departing aunt, and was rowed away into the purple.

---

They didn't sleep much. Rosalind paced. Juniper sat with her grandfather's notes and radiated the specific fury of someone who has been offered proof of a suspicion. Sefa steered through the night and said only one thing, around midnight, unprompted:

"She'll make the offer to him alone. That's how they do it. Alone, so shame can work on him."

Callum lay in his bunk listening to the hull talk to the water and thought: *she's right, and I already know it, because they did it to me twice before.*

---

Captain Ondine sent for him the next afternoon, and Rosalind, jaw tight, let him go—because refusing would tell the Optimists everything, and because, Callum suspected, some part of his sister needed to know. Needed him to come back or not come back, so the waiting could end either way.

The *Meridian Grace*'s cabin smelled of cedar and ink. Charts everywhere, real ones, beautiful ones, brass weights holding down corners. Ondine poured him tea without asking and pushed a document across the desk.

"Sign, sail with me, debt's ash. Or—" she tapped the paper, then slid a second sheet beside it, blank except for a heading—"you stay with your sister, and you simply tell me where she's going. Coordinates. A heading. One number set, and your ledger closes all the same. You don't even have to leave the *Vespera*. I'm not buying a sailor. I'm buying a sentence."

"And if I give you nothing?"

"Then your debt keeps growing, and eventually we collect it the way we collect everything." She sipped her tea. "I want to be clear that I like you, Callum. You have a dockworker's stillness and a debtor's survival instincts. Those are the two best qualities a person can have in my line of work. Which is why I know you're already thinking about saying yes."

He was. That was the filthy truth of it. He was looking at that blank chart and feeling the weight lift already—four hundred sixty marks, gone, like a crate set down after a decade of carrying. And it wouldn't even hurt anyone, he heard the voice say, the reasonable voice, the Optimist voice that lived rent-free in his head now. *They'll find the wreck anyway. You'd just be saving everyone time. Rosalind would understand. Rosalind left you first, if you want to get technical about it.*

That voice had material. Eleven years of material.

"May I see your charts?" he asked instead.

Ondine raised an eyebrow, then gestured graciously. "Be my guest."

He leaned over the table and studied the Sable Archipelago charts—good ones, better than theirs, updated soundings, the works. And while he studied them, his hands, dockworker's hands, hands that had copied manifests and forged signatures and once reproduced a customs stamp from memory, quietly did what they knew how to do. He traced the channels with one finger. He asked questions. *Is this passage dredged? What's the draft limit here?* And Ondine, proud of her charts, answered every one.

He wasn't copying their chart. He was letting her chart copy *him*—letting her show him exactly what the Optimists already knew, which was nearly everything, which meant they already had a heading, which meant—

Which meant the decision had already been made for him, hadn't it? They knew. They'd always known. His answer was worth nothing.

Except. Except Ondine wanted the coordinates confirmed. Wanted them from *inside*. Because a fleet doesn't chase a guess into monsoon waters; a fleet chases certainty, and certainty was the one thing Callum could still withhold.

"Here's my counteroffer," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "You tear up my debt. Today. Now. And I tell you whether my sister's heading is north of the Third Reef or south of it."

Ondine studied him for a long moment, and something in her expression shifted—not anger; respect, which was worse. "North or south," she repeated. "One bit of information. That's cheap, Sterling."

"It's the most expensive thing I own."

She laughed again, delighted, and pulled his ledger page from a drawer, and held a pen over it, and waited.

Callum looked at the pen. At the page. At four hundred sixty marks standing on the edge of oblivion, arms out, ready to fall.

"South," he said.

Ondine wrote something. Tore the page. Handed him the pieces. "Pleasure doing business."

---

He rowed back to the *Vespera* in the gold light of late afternoon with torn paper in his pocket and a sickness in his stomach, because he had lied to Ondine—Elias's route ran *north* of the Third Reef, he was almost sure, mostly sure, reasonably sure—but he had also handed the *Meridian Grace* a heading into the archipelago, and fleets are big, and guesses cover a lot of water, and he had felt, for one entire heartbeat, ready to say yes to everything.

Rosalind met him at the rail. She didn't ask. She just looked at him, and Callum discovered that eleven years of perfect dockside blankness were worth absolutely nothing against his sister's eyes.

"I tore up my debt," he said. "Gave her a heading. A wrong one." He swallowed. "Probably wrong."

"*Probably.*"

"There's a chance I just sent the Optimist fleet confidently toward a reef," he said. "There's also a chance I sent them toward you."

Rosalind was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Get below and fetch the chart case. If we're going to be chased by an armada, I want Sefa choosing our reefs personally."

As he turned to go, Juniper looked up from Elias's notes, and her voice cracked across the deck like a whip:

"The sixth tide. Grandfather wrote—the Optimists won't be what kills us. Something else knows we're coming."
