## 7. Mutiny at the Third Reef

Rosalind knew something was wrong before she knew what it was. It was the way Denny Halloran stood his watch—too still, too attentive, like a man listening to a conversation through a wall.

She'd hired him in Vantrell for three reasons: he could splice a line in the dark, he didn't ask questions about cargo, and he'd smiled at Juniper's jokes without seeming to calculate them first. That last one had mattered more than she'd admitted. A crew of four was barely a crew at all, and every hand counted twice as much when two of them were family you hadn't chosen.

Now, on the morning of the third tide, with the Third Reef rising out of the sea ahead of them like the spine of some drowned animal, Rosalind watched Denny coil a rope that was already coiled, and felt the cold slide down her back.

"Sefa," she said quietly, at the rail. "How long has he been standing there?"

The tide-pilot didn't look up from her charts. Her hands were stained with ink to the second knuckle; she'd been recalculating since dawn, muttering numbers like prayers. "Since the moon set. Which is odd, because I relieved him an hour ago and he told me he'd take the next one."

"He's had no sleep."

"He's had no *need* of sleep." Sefa finally glanced up, and her eyes were flat and bright as coins. "Rosalind. He asked me yesterday how deep the channel runs at slack water. Not where the wreck is. Not what we're carrying. The *channel depth*."

"Maybe he's curious."

"Nobody's curious about channel depth. Channel depth is a question you ask when you're planning to put a hole in a hull."

The reef ahead of them was a broken crescent of black coral, five miles of teeth with a single gap in the middle—the Third Passage, which Elias Wren's map called "the throat" and every pilot in the archipelago called, when they called it anything, *the widow-maker*. The tide was turning. They had perhaps ninety minutes of usable water, and after that the passage would be a churning white lie that killed anyone who believed it.

Rosalind looked back at Denny. He'd stopped pretending to work. He was watching her watch him, and neither of them blinked.

"Well," she said. "That settles that."

* * *

She did not confront him. That was the first lesson Vantrell had ever taught her: never corner a man who's already decided. Instead she gave the crew breakfast—hard biscuit, salt pork, coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in—and watched who ate and who only pretended to.

Denny ate everything. That almost made her doubt herself. Then Callum, sprawled against the mast with his bowl balanced on his knee, said cheerfully, "Denny, you've got ink on your sleeve."

Denny looked down. There was no ink on his sleeve. But for half a heartbeat—a heartbeat exactly long enough—his eyes went to the chart locker under the helm.

Sefa kept her charts there. All of them. Including the copy of Elias's route that they'd all agreed, without ever saying it aloud, was worth more than the ship.

Rosalind stood up, stretched, and said, "Juniper, come help me with the jib. Callum, take the helm. Sefa—" and here she let her voice go light as gull-feathers, "—come look at this cloud formation with me."

They were mid-deck, ostensibly squinting at weather, when Rosalind said, very low: "He's going for the charts during the passage. When everyone's hands are full."

"I know," Sefa said. "I've been waiting for him to make his move so I could break his fingers."

"We don't have time for your hobbies. We're going through the throat in an hour with a skeleton crew and a traitor aboard."

"So throw him overboard now."

"And if he's already signaled the fleet? If there's an Optimist ship waiting on the far side because he told them our window?" Rosalind rubbed her face. "No. We let him think he's clever. We tie the real charts to my body, we give him a decoy locker, and we get through the reef. Then we deal with him on open water, where throwing him overboard is a *solution* instead of a *question*."

Sefa considered this. "You've become devious."

"It's the company I keep."

From the helm, Callum called out, "Whatever you two are whispering about, the tide won't wait for it!"

"No," Rosalind murmured, watching Denny Halloran smile at nothing. "It certainly won't."

* * *

The throat took them at noon.

It was worse than the charts promised, because charts are drawn by survivors, and survivors are liars by definition. The passage was a quarter mile wide and the water in it moved three directions at once, green glass over brown chaos, and the coral heads came up out of nowhere like fists. Sefa stood at the helm with her whole body, steering with her hips and her knees and her teeth, calling depths off a lead line while Callum hauled sail and Rosalind read the water color ahead—brown, brown, *green*, hard left—

Which is why nobody was watching Denny.

She heard it rather than saw it: the soft scrape of the decoy locker, then the harder sound of the real one, and turned in time to see Denny Halloran straighten up with Elias Wren's original map in his fist and a knife in his other hand.

"Nobody move," Denny said. His voice had changed. All the dockside warmth had drained out of it, leaving something flat and professional. "I'm sorry, Captain. Truly. But the Optimists pay better than promises."

"The Optimists bought your debt too?" Rosalind kept her voice level. Behind her, Callum had gone very still by the main sheet. Ahead, the reef went on being the reef, indifferent to human drama.

"They didn't have to buy mine. They *made* it." He laughed, short and ugly. "You think a man splices line in Vantrell docks because he loves rope? I owed them two hundred crowns. Now I owe them nothing, and there's a cutter waiting past the north head with my name on the manifest."

"You're doing this in the middle of the *throat*," Sefa said, and her voice cracked on it—not fear, fury. "You're doing this *now*?"

"Now's when I was told." Denny backed toward the stern, knife up, map crammed inside his shirt. "Ship stays on heading. Nobody follows me to the boat. In ten minutes I'm gone and you can tell yourselves whatever story helps you sleep."

And that was when the sea decided to vote.

The current shifted—early, wrong, the way Sefa had been dreading all morning—and the Vespera's bow swung six degrees off the channel in the space of a breath. A coral head rose dead ahead, black and barnacled and patient.

"Hard to port!" Rosalind screamed, and threw herself at the helm beside Sefa, and the world became noise.

The Vespera struck glancing—a shriek of timber, a lurch that threw Denny against the rail—and then they were through, somehow, into the wider water beyond the worst of the teeth, but the ship was groaning in a new register, low and structural, and water was coming in somewhere aft. And Denny Halloran was scrambling for the tender, knife still in his teeth, map still against his heart—

Callum hit him like a falling yardarm.

Rosalind had spent years believing her half-brother was a man made entirely of charm and bad decisions. She revised this opinion permanently as she watched him take the knife slash across his forearm without appearing to notice it, pin Denny against the caprail, and say, in a voice she'd never heard him use, "You brought a blade onto my sister's ship."

"It's not—it's a *ketch*, it's hardly—"

"*Her ship.*"

What happened next happened fast. Denny twisted free, made the rail, and would have made the tender too, except that Juniper Wren—nineteen years old, five foot nothing, furious—stepped into his path holding the boat's boarding axe and looking, Rosalind thought later, exactly like her grandfather must have looked the day he chose her over a fortune.

"Sit down," Juniper said.

Denny looked at the axe. He looked at Callum's bleeding arm. He looked at the sea, which offered no assistance whatsoever. And then, remarkably, he sat down on the deck and started to cry.

* * *

They dealt with him the way sailors deal with most things: practically, and slightly too late for anyone's comfort.

The tender went over the side with Denny in it, oars, water, and half their remaining biscuit—because Rosalind Sterling had been hungry enough times to know that even betrayal deserved a meal. The north head was two miles distant; the Optimist cutter beyond it was, with any luck, waiting for a man who would arrive empty-handed and soaked.

"He'll tell them everything anyway," Callum said, wrapping his arm while Juniper stitched—she'd learned field medicine from Elias's notes, of all things, and her hands were steadier than her face.

"He'll tell them we're headed for the Corvane," Rosalind agreed. "They already knew that. What he doesn't know—" she patted the oilskin bundle strapped beneath her coat, where the true chart rode against her ribs, "—is the route. Six tides, remember? He knows the destination. He doesn't know the door."

But the victory tasted thin, because the Vespera was hurt. The glancing strike had opened a seam aft, and Sefa—who had gone below with a lantern and come up gray-faced—delivered the verdict in her usual fashion, which was to say like a woman reading a death sentence in a language she resented.

"She'll hold. Barely. But the seam needs careening and we've no time to careen. Which means we take the fourth-tide passage through the Narrows instead of the safe channel around." She sat down heavily on a crate. "Except the Narrows aren't in any chart I trust anymore. My tables are three years old and this coast eats pilots."

Silence fell over the deck. The wind was building out of the southeast, the monsoon's advance guard, and the light had gone the color of pewter.

Then Juniper spoke.

"There's a note," she said. Everyone turned. She had Elias's journal open on her knees, the one she'd carried since Vantrell and guarded like a relic. "Grandfather wrote about the Narrows. 4th of the Rains, thirty-one years ago—he came through here in a boat smaller than this one. He marked the safe line." Her finger traced the page. "*'Steer for the split rock until it swallows the far peak, then fall away south and pray.'*" She looked up, and her chin was trembling, but her voice wasn't. "He drew it. He drew the whole line. He just—he never told anyone, because by then he'd already decided to turn back."

Rosalind crossed the deck and put her hand over the old ink. Elias's handwriting, careful and cramped, a dead man's finger pointing the way home.

"Sefa," she said. "Can you steer a dead man's line?"

Sefa looked at the page a long moment. Then she stood up, wincing from the bruise blooming along her ribs where the helm had thrown her, and took the wheel.

"Aye," she said. "If his granddaughter reads it out."

They went through the Narrows at dusk, Juniper's voice steady at Rosalind's shoulder—*split rock swallowing the far peak, now, fall away south*—and the Vespera threaded the needle with a torn seam singing in her hull and four people aboard who, forty-eight hours earlier, had been strangers and liabilities and were now, unmistakably, a crew.

On the far side, the sea opened out dark and enormous, and away to the southeast the clouds were stacked up like mountains on fire. The monsoon, arriving early. Landfall coming whether they were ready or not.

Rosalind stood at the bow and felt the first fat drops hit her face.

"Fifth tide," Sefa said quietly behind her. "We'll be cutting it finer than fine."

"Then we'd best not sleep," Rosalind answered—and far behind them, at the edge of sight, a sail appeared on the horizon, running before the storm.
