## 8. Landfall

**Rosalind**

The sky went the color of a bruise at noon, which was wrong by half a day and wronger by a lifetime.

Rosalind stood at the Vespera's wheel and watched the horizon fold itself into something vertical. She had chased storms for eleven years. She had flown into the teeth of cyclones with instruments strapped to her chest and called it a living. But she had never seen a monsoon make landfall early, and she had never seen one do it while she was inside its front pocket.

"Sefa!" she shouted over the rising wind. "Tell me I'm imagining this."

Sefa appeared at her elbow, squinting through salt-crusted lashes, and said nothing for a long moment. That was answer enough. Sefa was a woman who narrated everything—the tide tables, the barometer, the state of the ship's biscuits—and her silence was louder than any alarm.

"Landfall's early," Rosalind said.

"Landfall is early," Sefa agreed. "Which means the fifth tide comes tonight instead of tomorrow night. Which means we have—" she checked the sun, found no sun, and made an educated guess— "perhaps six hours to reach the lagoon before that wall of water arrives and decides what lives and what doesn't."

Behind them, Callum was already hauling canvas with the grim efficiency of a man who had once been thrown off a ship exactly like this one and had never quite forgiven the ocean for it. Juniper clung to the rail, gray-faced, feeding the sea her breakfast with a kind of apologetic dignity.

"We could run north," Callum called up. "Sit it out in the lee of Marrow Cay."

"And let the Optimists take the wreck while we're hiding from weather?" Rosalind shook her head. "No. Elias timed this route for six tides. We're on five. The lagoon is sheltered—it has to be, or the Corvane wouldn't have lasted a century down there. We make for the lagoon."

"It has to be," Sefa repeated dryly. "Wonderful. Our entire survival plan rests on the hydrology of a dead man's optimism."

"He wasn't optimistic," Juniper said quietly, from the rail. "He was stubborn. There's a difference. Optimists hope. Stubborn men check twice."

Rosalind caught her eye, and despite everything—the storm, the fleet behind them, the reef scars along the Vespera's hull from the Third Reef—she smiled. "Then let's be stubborn. All hands. We're going in ahead of the weather or not at all."

* * *

**Juniper**

Juniper had decided, somewhere around the fourth hour of vomiting, that she hated the sea personally, as an individual, the way you might hate a specific rude neighbor.

Her grandfather had loved it. That fact sat strangely in her chest now, next to everything the letters had taught her—that he had chosen the water over her mother, that he had carried his guilt across forty years of charts like ballast he refused to jettison. She had come aboard demanding answers and had received them all at once, like a wave she hadn't braced for.

Now the wave was literal, and enormous, and coming directly at them.

"Grandfather's notes say the eastern channel," she told Rosalind, shouting over the wind while gripping the chart table with both hands. "Two fathoms over the sandbar, then hard to starboard past the split rock. He wrote that the rock looks like a sleeping dog. He drew it. He actually drew a little dog."

"Of course he did." Rosalind leaned over the chart, hair whipping loose from its knot. "Is there anything else? Currents? Hazards?"

Juniper flipped pages, her fingers trembling—from fear, she told herself, though some of it was grief doing its slow work. "There's a note here. 'The lagoon breathes with the tide. When the tide runs out fast, don't fight it. Let it carry you in.' " She looked up. "What does that mean?"

"It means your grandfather knew about a tidal surge channel," Sefa said, appearing between them with the uncanny timing of a cat who wants dinner. "It means when the fifth tide starts draining the outer shoals, there'll be a river of displaced water running straight into the lagoon, and if we're clever, we ride it like a hand pushing us home." Her grin was wild and white in the darkening air. "If we're not clever, it pushes us onto the coral and grinds us into chum."

"I vote clever," Callum said from the doorway, dripping.

"Noted," said Sefa. "Democracy prevails."

Juniper almost laughed. Almost. Instead she pressed her grandfather's notebook flat against her chest and felt, absurdly, that he was aboard with them—that somewhere in the roar of the wind was the same stubbornness that had drawn a little dog on a rock so his granddaughter might someday find his way home.

"All right," she said. "Let's go find his dog."

* * *

**Callum**

Callum had spent three years working the Vantrell docks telling himself he didn't miss the open water. It had taken him approximately three days aboard the Vespera to admit this was a lie, and another two to stop being angry about it.

Now, with the world ending around him in sheets of rain, he found he wasn't angry at all. He was terrified, yes—his hands shook on the halyards—but underneath the terror was something worse: he was enjoying himself.

"You're smiling!" he yelled at Rosalind across the deck, because if he was going to be damned, he wanted company.

"I'm always smiling!"

"You're smiling like someone who's about to do something stupid!"

"That's just my face!"

The first gust hit like a fist. The Vespera heeled until the rail kissed the sea, and Callum went down the tilting deck on his boots, catching the mainsheet before it could flog itself to ribbons. Rain came sideways, warm and thick as soup. Somewhere aft, Juniper was reading depth soundings aloud in a voice that cracked but did not stop, and Sefa was at the bow calling out the water's color changes—green, green, brown, *brown*—like a woman describing wine.

"There!" Sefa bellowed suddenly, pointing. "Split rock! Tell me that's a dog!"

Callum squinted through the deluge. A black shape rose from the foam ahead, jagged and unmistakable.

"It's a dog," he confirmed. "An ugly one."

"Elias never claimed he was handsome!" Juniper shouted back, and even in the screaming wind, Callum heard the laugh under it.

They came around the rock with the tide beginning to turn beneath them, and Callum felt it happen—the sea gathering itself behind the shoals like a held breath, then releasing, a brown-green river surging toward the lagoon's mouth. The Vespera lifted. Accelerated. For thirty glorious, horrifying seconds they were not sailing but *thrown*, hurled through the channel on the shoulder of the fifth tide, spray sheeting over the bow, everyone yelling things that were probably prayers.

And then—quiet. Relative quiet. The lagoon opened around them like a cupped palm, ringed by low green islands, the storm still raging beyond the reef but broken into noise instead of violence. Rain fell straight down. The ketch drifted to a stop in water the color of hammered tin.

Callum sat down where he stood, which was in a puddle, and laughed until his ribs hurt.

* * *

**Sefa**

Sefa did not sit down. Sefa walked the deck with a lantern, counting damage, because celebration was for people whose ships weren't held together with prayer and borrowed caulking.

The tally: mainmast sound, mercifully. Jib shredded, replaceable. The patch over the Third Reef wound holding, barely, seeping a slow confession of water that the pump handled without complaint. One dinghy lost. One crew seasick, one laughing hysterically in a puddle, one captain grinning like a lunatic at the wheel.

One Sefa, with a bruised shoulder she'd gotten at the reef and hadn't mentioned to anyone, because mentioning it would require discussing feelings, and Sefa had a strict policy against that before sundown.

She paused at the rail and looked down.

The rain was thinning. Through a tear in the clouds, late light fell slantwise across the lagoon, and the water—which had been opaque all day—began, slowly, impossibly, to clear. Storm-churned sediment settled in long veils. And below them, perhaps four fathoms down, shapes emerged from the gloom.

Masts. Two of them, tilted, furred with a century of growth, but unmistakably masts, their spars glinting pale gold where the light touched them.

"Well," Sefa said softly. "Hello, Corvane."

She didn't call the others. Not yet. She stood alone at the rail for one full minute, watching a dead ship sleep beneath her feet, and thought about tides—how they always came back, how nothing the sea took was ever truly gone, only waiting for the right water to rise.

Then she cupped her hands and bellowed, "Oi! You lot! You'll want to see this!"

They came running—Rosalind first, skidding on wet planks; Juniper clutching her grandfather's notebook; Callum still damp and disbelieving. They crowded the rail and looked down together, four faces lit gold from below, and nobody spoke for a long time.

"She's real," Juniper whispered. "He wasn't mad. He was never mad."

"No," Rosalind said. Her voice was strange—triumphant and tired and shadowed all at once. "But someone else knows we're here."

Sefa followed her gaze south, past the ring of the reef, to where the storm was tearing itself apart against the horizon. Through a gap in the black, brief as a struck match, she saw it: sails. More than one. Scattered, battered, driven wide by the monsoon—but closing, all the same, like fingers curling into a fist.

"How long?" Callum asked.

Sefa calculated. Tide, wind, distance, the Optimists' hulls and their hunger. "Hours," she said. "We dive at first light. Whatever we take, we take fast."

Below them, the Corvane's masts gleamed patiently in the clearing water, keeping a secret a hundred years old—and above them, out of the storm's wreckage, the Meridian Optimists were coming to collect.
