## 9. The Weight of Gold

The water in the lagoon was the color of bottle glass, and Rosalind had never trusted it less.

After six tides of black water and screaming wind, this calm felt like a held breath. She floated at the surface with her mask on, breathing through the snorkel, looking down at a sight she had chased across half an ocean: the masts of the *Corvane*, rising from the seabed forty feet below like the fingers of a drowned giant, furred white with coral, glinting where the morning sun found them.

"Bottom's at nine fathoms," Sefa called from the Vespera's rail, her injured arm strapped tight against her chest, her voice still rough from the reef fight two days past. "You'll have maybe eight minutes of good work before your head starts arguing with you. Listen to your head."

"My head has terrible judgment," Rosalind said. "It's how I got here."

"It's how we all got here," Callum said, coiling rope beside her. "Some of us with more enthusiasm than others." He nodded down at Juniper, who was already in the water, fins flashing, descending toward the wreck with the impatience of someone who had spent three weeks on a small boat with four people she'd only just learned to tolerate.

Rosalind spat into her mask, rinsed it, settled it against her face. "If I'm not up in ten minutes—"

"We come get you," Sefa said. "That's the whole speech. We've done it twice already."

She went down.

The world narrowed to blue-green silence and the rhythm of her own breath. She followed the masts down, hand over hand along the mainmast's coral-crusted shrouds, until the hull rose beneath her—a great dark whale of a ship, broken-backed across the sand, its stern castle collapsed into a cave of timber and stone. The Corvane had been a treasure ship once, a merchantman of the old Meridian lines, and even now, even rotted, she looked rich. Gold does that. It refuses to rot.

The hold gaped open where the deck had caved. Rosalind swam over the edge and dropped inside.

And there it was.

Not the romantic scatter of coins she'd imagined in Elias Wren's smoke-filled room back in Vantrell. This was cargo, deliberate and dense: iron-bound chests fused together by centuries of concretion, their shapes unmistakable even under the crust. Silver bars stacked like firewood. A chest that had split open, spilling coins across the sand in a golden drift, as if the sea itself had decided to count them and given up halfway.

Eight minutes, Sefa had said. Rosalind had planned to spend them hauling up the first chest, marking positions, being efficient and professional about it.

Instead she saw the logbook.

It lay wedged between two chests, half-buried in sand—and it couldn't be ship's papers, because no ship's papers survived sixty years underwater unless they were sealed, and this one wasn't sealed. It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, tucked deliberately into the gap like something hidden rather than lost. And stamped into the crusted leather, faint but legible:

*E. WREN.*

Her heart did something complicated in her chest. She pulled the book free, checked her air without checking it, and kicked for the surface.

* * *

They gathered around it in the cockpit while the lagoon went gold with late afternoon, Juniper holding the log with hands that shook only a little, because she'd insisted on opening it herself and nobody had been fool enough to argue.

The pages were warped but readable. Elias Wren's handwriting, younger and sharper than the shaky scrawl Rosalind remembered from Vantrell, marched across the pages in ink that had somehow held.

*"Third day searching the lagoon,"* Juniper read aloud. *"Found her. Gods above, found her. The hold is intact. I could take enough to buy a fleet. I could take enough to never work again."*

She paused. The water slapped gently against the hull. Somewhere on shore, the islanders who had helped them beach the longboat were singing something low and unhurried.

*"But Maren's letter came with the last supply boat. Juniper has the fever. The doctor says if I'm not back by the spring tides—"*

Juniper stopped reading.

"You don't have to," Rosalind said quietly.

"No." Juniper wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at them. "No, I do. He made me promise to deliver his letters. Seems fair I deliver his last page too." She cleared her throat. "*I sat on the reef all night doing sums. The gold will keep. It's kept sixty years; it will keep sixty more. She won't. I'm turning back. If anyone finds this—she was worth more than the hold. Tell me I was right.*"

Silence, except for the water.

"He turned back," Callum said at last, and his voice was strange. "He found all of it, and he turned around and rowed home to a sick little girl."

"That's why the map was a confession," Rosalind said. "That's what he meant when he said the sixth tide takes what greed brings. He wasn't warning us off the weather." She looked at the log, at the young man's handwriting asking the sea to tell him he'd chosen right. "He was warning us off himself. Off who you become when you can see this much and want it too much."

Juniper closed the log and held it against her chest. "Tell him yourself," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "When you see him. Because you're going to live, and you're going to find some way to tell him, because I am not carrying that message alone."

"Deal," Rosalind said, and meant it as a joke, and discovered halfway through saying it that it wasn't.

* * *

They worked the rest of the day, and it went better than any of them dared hope. Two chests raised by sundown, hauled up on lines rigged from the mainmast, cracked open on deck in the last light: silver bars, coin by the hundredweight, a jeweled compass rose that made Sefa laugh out loud for the first time since the reef. Enough. More than enough. Enough to pay Meridian Optimists in full with interest and insult left over, enough to fix the Vespera properly, enough to send Juniper home wealthy and Rosalind—

Rosalind stood at the rail that night, watching the moon come up over the reef, and realized she didn't know what she was anymore, if not a woman chasing wrecks to outrun debt. The thought should have frightened her. Mostly it just felt like standing on new ground.

Callum brought her tea without being asked, which was so unlike him that she took it with suspicion.

"You're brooding," he said.

"I'm planning."

"You're brooding with charts. I know the difference. You used to do it in the treehouse when Father sold the boat." He leaned on the rail beside her. "What is it?"

"The fleet," she said. "Two ships scattered in the monsoon. One of them is going to have limped here same as us. Maybe not today. But soon."

"And when they come?"

Rosalind watched the moonlight silver the lagoon, the masts below it, the gold sleeping in their hold. "Then I decide what this is worth," she said. "All of it. Not the coin. The whole thing—the map, the letters, the promise, this crew. What it's actually worth, and what I'll trade to keep it."

Callum was quiet a moment. "You've gotten dramatic since the treehouse."

"You've gotten honest since the docks. We've both changed. It's disgusting."

He laughed, and for a moment it was almost easy, almost the two of them as children again, and then the lookout—Juniper, up in the rigging because she hadn't slept properly in days and refused to lie down—went stiff against the stars.

"Sail," she called down. "West passage. Running dark, no lights." A pause, and then, flat and cold: "Gun ports."

The Optimists' surviving ship came out of the west passage like a knife out of a sleeve, hull down and closing, and every soul aboard the Vespera stopped moving at once.

Sefa appeared at Rosalind's shoulder, arm still strapped, face grim in the moonlight. "That's the *Meridian Grace*. Flagship's sister. Forty guns if she's carrying full fit, twelve if she's battered—which after that storm, she's battered." Her eyes flicked to Rosalind. "We can't run. We can't fight. So."

"So," Rosalind said.

Below them, in the dark water, the Corvane's gold waited. In the hold behind them, two chests of it sat under canvas. In her pocket, Elias Wren's log pressed against her hip like a hand on her conscience.

The *Grace* ran up no colors. She simply came on, patient as debt, and Rosalind understood with perfect clarity that whatever she chose in the next hour would be the truest answer to a question she'd been asking her whole life: what kind of Sterling was she going to be?

"Get Juniper down from the rigging," she said. "Wake the islanders. And somebody hide those chests—we're going to be poor for one more night."

* * *

They buried the silver above the tideline in the hour before dawn, working by feel and whispered argument, Juniper directing operations with the fierce competence of a woman who had organized her grandfather's chaos her whole childhood. The islanders helped without being asked, moving crates into the mangroves with the ease of people who had hidden things from ships before, and when Rosalind tried to thank their headman, he waved her off.

"Elias Wren carried our children through the fever year," the old man said. "Whatever is yours is safe here. Whatever is his, also."

By first light the Vespera sat alone and innocent in the lagoon, decks scrubbed, holds empty-looking, a patched ketch with nothing aboard but four tired sailors and a dead man's letters. Rosalind stood at the rail in her least ruined coat and watched the *Meridian Grace* come to anchor a cable's length away, her guns run out, her decks crowded with men who moved like they were owed something.

A launch dropped from her side. In the bow stood a broad man in a fine wet-weather coat, and even at distance Rosalind knew the type, because she'd seen it across desks in Vantrell: the smile that preceded the arithmetic.

"Captain Ravensworth," the man called up cheerfully, as the launch bumped against their hull. "Or may I say *Miss Sterling*? The Meridian Optimists have been so worried about you. We've come to discuss your account."

Rosalind smiled back, and found—to her own surprise—that it wasn't even hard.

"Of course," she said. "Come aboard. Let's talk about what things are worth."
