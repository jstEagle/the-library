## 10. What the Sea Keeps

The monsoon came for us the way my grandfather used to come for supper—loud, inevitable, and smelling faintly of salt and old arguments.

Juniper Wren, standing at the rail of the *Vespera* with rain hammering her shoulders, thought that if Elias could see what she was about to do with his map's coordinates, he'd either weep with pride or rise from the grave purely to complain about the paperwork.

"Say it again," Rosalind called from the wheel. "Slower. I want to enjoy it."

"I said," Juniper shouted back over the wind, "that we're going to sink our own ship on purpose, in the middle of a storm, and hope everyone involved is a bad enough swimmer to make it convincing."

"That's the spirit."

Sefa, lashed to the mast with a splinted arm and an expression of profound theological objection, raised her good hand. "For the record, I have piloted through eleven monsoons and I have never once recommended scuttling as a navigational strategy."

"For the record," Callum said, hauling the last of the oil drums toward the hold, "you also once recommended we outrun a squall by sailing *through* it, so your record is not unblemished."

"My record got us here, didn't it?"

"It did," Rosalind said quietly, and something in her voice made even Sefa stop arguing. "It got us here first."

* * *

The plan had been Rosalind's, though Juniper liked to think she'd contributed the crucial detail—the part that made it work rather than merely survive. They'd spent two days in the wreck lagoon, diving between storms, hauling up what the Corvane had kept safe for sixty years beneath its green-silted decks: coin chests wrapped in canvas, a strongbox of pearls gone grey with age but heavy as sin, gold bar stamped with a mint that hadn't existed since before any of them were born. Enough to pay a debt that had been strangling Rosalind since Vantrell. Enough, Elias's log suggested, to buy a dozen small mercies besides.

But the Optimists' surviving ship—the *Charity*, because of course they'd named it that—was still out there, limping and armed and angry, and Captain Vess did not strike Juniper as a woman who accepted disappointment gracefully.

So they would give her a shipwreck.

"There," Sefa said, pointing with her chin at the northern channel where the reef teeth showed white even through the rain. "The shallows off Kettering Cay. Twelve feet over sand, then coral. A hull driven hard onto that shelf will break her back but leave her visible—masts above water, cargo hatch flooded but intact-looking." She paused. "She'll look like a prize. She'll just be an empty one."

"And the islanders?" Callum asked.

Juniper answered that one. "Kettering owes my grandfather." She'd learned it from the letters, from the last one especially, the one she'd read four times now and kept in her coat pocket like a stone. In '61, Elias Wren had carried a fevered Kettering fisherman forty miles through open water to reach a doctor, and had refused payment, and had said—according to his own embarrassed account—"something foolish about how debts to the living matter more than debts to the dead." The whole island had remembered. Islands always did.

"They'll hide the cargo in the copra sheds," Juniper said. "Under the drying racks. Old Man Tavu says nobody's searched those sheds since the last war, mostly out of respect for the smell."

Rosalind laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, and Juniper thought again how much her brother's face changed when he heard it. Callum had been watching Rosalind like a man watching weather he didn't trust yet. Maybe that was fair. Family was just weather you'd agreed to stand in.

* * *

They ran the *Vespera* onto the reef at dusk, in the worst of it, which was the only honest way to do it.

Sefa took the helm herself, splinted arm strapped down, because she said no one else would hit the shelf at precisely the right angle and she'd sooner die than watch an amateur ruin her craftsmanship. Rosalind stood beside her. Callum and Juniper crouched in the lee of the cabin with the dinghy painter in their hands, counting waves.

"Now," Sefa said, and put them aground.

The impact was enormous and strangely gentle—sand first, a long grinding shudder, then the crack as the keel found coral and gave up its argument with the sea. Water came through the seams they'd opened below, cold and businesslike. By the time the four of them pulled away in the dinghy, the *Vespera* lay heeled against the reef with her masts leaning into the rain like tired dancers, looking exactly like what she was pretending to be: a treasure ship that had lost her race.

Juniper looked back until the dark took her. She'd known the ketch for barely three weeks. It felt like leaving a funeral early.

"She served," Rosalind said beside her, reading her thoughts the way she increasingly did. "That's more than most of us get."

* * *

Kettering Cay received them the way islands receive smugglers, castaways, and nephews of old favors: without questions and with too much food.

Old Man Tavu turned out to be ninety if he was a day, blind in one eye, and sharp enough in the other to make Juniper feel thoroughly audited. He looked at her a long moment when she introduced herself.

"Wren," he said. "You have his chin. Stubborn bone." Then, to Rosalind: "And you'll be the one who brought him home?"

"He brought himself," Rosalind said. "We just carried the luggage."

Tavu laughed until he coughed, and then it was settled, the way things settle on small islands—by consensus, cassava, and the application of forty years of gratitude. By midnight the Corvane's cargo sat under the copra racks, under tarpaulins, under the collective silence of an island that had decided these strangers were theirs now. Tavu's granddaughter, a girl of maybe fourteen with Sefa's exact expression of skeptical competence, appointed herself chief of security and began drilling the fishing boys in lookout signals.

"They'll keep it safer than a bank," Sefa murmured, watching the girl organize. "Banks can be bought. Granddaughters cannot."

* * *

Two days later, huddled in Tavu's boathouse with a spyglass and a pot of terrible coffee, they watched the *Charity* find the wreck.

Captain Vess brought her ship in slow, guns run out, clearly expecting resistance. When none came, her boarding parties swarmed the broken *Vespera*, and Juniper imagined them below decks—finding the flooded hold, the scattered ballast, the one crate they'd left behind containing nothing but ballast stones and, as Callum's final joke, a single coconut with *COMPLIMENTS OF THE MERIDIAN OPTIMISTS* carved into it in his terrible handwriting.

Through the glass, Juniper saw a figure on the *Vespera*'s deck go very still, then very animated, gesturing at the sea. Reporting the loss. Reporting that the Sterling woman and her crew of nobodies had drowned with their ship, or fled empty-handed, or both—and that the great salvage investment of the Meridian Optimists had returned exactly nothing, in the middle of a monsoon, at ruinous expense.

Vess would spin it somehow. That was what syndicates did; they called their losses investments and their violence due diligence. But she'd spin it alone, and broke, and beaten north by weather, and Juniper found she couldn't even hate her. There was no room left for it. The anger had all been spent somewhere around the third reef, and what remained was something quieter and harder to name.

"She's turning away," Callum reported.

"Let her," Rosalind said. "The sea keeps what the sea keeps. Let her explain the rest to her investors."

* * *

The letters went out with the last calm morning.

There were nine of them, and eight had addresses—harbors up and down the coast where people Elias Wren had loved and failed and loved anyway had waited decades without knowing they were waiting. Juniper delivered the first one herself, to a widow on Kettering who read it standing in her doorway and then pressed it to her chest and said only, "He spelled my name right. He never could spell anything else." The rest they'd carry north, port by port, because some promises are meant to be walked the whole length of.

The ninth letter had no address. It said only *For the girl, when she stops being angry.* Juniper had read it so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth. It wasn't an apology, exactly. It was a map—not of reefs this time, but of a man's whole cowardice and whole love, drawn honestly for the first time in his life. *I chose you,* it ended. *I just chose you badly, from far away, for too long. If someone is reading this to you, I hope they're better at arriving than I was.*

"You're not angry anymore," Callum observed one evening, finding her at the rail.

"I'm furious," Juniper said comfortably. "It's just not pointed at him. Turns out I've got plenty to spare for syndicates, monsoons, and brothers who copy charts behind people's backs."

Callum winced. "You're going to carry that forever, aren't you."

"Family heirloom," she said. "I'm having it engraved."

* * *

They bought a new boat with the first share of the recovered gold—or rather, Tavu's cousin sold them one, a fat-bellied trading schooner called the *Kindly Tide* that had spent twenty years hauling copra and had opinions about nothing. Sefa inspected her hull for an entire afternoon, pronounced her "slow but honest," and claimed the helm as hers by right of seniority, injury, and general superiority.

Rosalind paid the Meridian Optimists in full—delivered to their Vantrell office in a locked chest, with a receipt demanded, witnessed, and framed. Juniper had insisted on watching the clerk's face when he counted it. She described it afterward, twice, with sound effects.

And on a bright, rinsed-clean morning with the monsoon finally spent and the trade winds back in their proper quarters, the *Kindly Tide* stood out of Kettering Cay with four people aboard who had started as a debtor, a disgrace, a stranger, and a stowaway of sorts, and had become, without anyone voting on it, a crew.

"North," Rosalind said at the wheel, and it wasn't a question, but Sefa answered it anyway.

"North," she agreed. "Eight harbors. Eight letters. And after that?"

Rosalind looked at Juniper, and Callum, and the wide open water ahead, and Juniper watched her half-sister—the woman who'd lost a plane and gained everything—arrive at some private decision and smile.

"After that," Rosalind said, "there's a chart in Elias's log with three wrecks marked that nobody's ever salvaged. He drew them himself. And there's a note in the margin."

"What kind of note?" Callum asked.

Juniper already knew. She'd read it a hundred times. She said it with him, or nearly—she said it aloud, and let the wind take it north:

"*For the girl, when she's ready to go looking.*"

"Well," she added, grinning as the *Kindly Tide* leaned into the swell, "good thing somebody finally taught me how to arrive."
