## 1. The Debt Collector Smiles

The storm came in off the water like something with a grudge, and Elodie Joubert drove straight into its teeth.

Rain hammered the windshield of the chase truck in sheets so thick the wipers surrendered every third stroke, and beyond the glass the coast road unspooled gray and glistening, hemmed on one side by cliffs the color of wet slate and on the other by a sea that had turned the green of old bottle glass. Lightning walked along the horizon in long, silent strides. The monsoon cell sat over the water two kilometers out, a bruise-colored cathedral of cloud, rotating slow and patient as a thing deciding where to strike.

"Pressure's dropping like my will to live," Bex said from the engine well behind her, voice half-swallowed by the roar. There was a clank, a curse, then: "Starboard sensor's dead again. I've got it reading us at four thousand meters altitude, which, last I checked—"

"We're not flying, Bex."

"Don't tempt me. I'd leave you all here."

Elodie grinned without meaning to. It was easier than saying what she actually felt, which was that she hadn't slept more than three hours in any night for eleven days, and that her hands had developed a fine, permanent tremor she hid by gripping the wheel harder, and that somewhere under the exhaustion lived a small cold animal of dread that woke every time she thought about the number. Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve. That was the number. She knew it the way other people knew their own birthday.

One month left.

"Tomas," she said into the comm, "tell me the drone's up."

"Telling you the drone's up." Tomas's voice came through calm and slightly amused, the way it always did, as though he were narrating someone else's adventure from a comfortable chair. "She's riding the inflow band now. Cell's organizing tighter than anything I've seen this season. If it makes landfall in the next hour, we're looking at a full salvage window—wreckage, cargo, maybe hull plating off the *Coriander* if she finally lets go of that reef."

The *Coriander*. A freighter that had been dying on the rocks outside Halfway Sound for six years, picked clean season after season, and still giving. Salvage rights during declared storm events belonged to whoever could document recovery, and documentation required proximity, and proximity required chasing storms in a truck held together by Bex's stubbornness and prayer. The Optimists took forty percent. They always took forty percent, smiling about it.

"There," Junie said suddenly, from the jump seat where she was absolutely not supposed to be, because Elodie had told her twice this week that storm runs were no place for a sixteen-year-old, and Junie had pointed out—with the flawless logic of the young—that the alternative was sitting alone in the harbor town where nobody wanted her either, so really Elodie could pick which neglect she preferred.

"There what?" Elodie asked.

Junie leaned forward between the seats, pointing one chapped finger at the water. "Waterfall."

Elodie looked. For a moment there was nothing but rain-lashed sea. Then lightning flashed, and she saw it—a column of spray rising off the surface a kilometer out, impossibly tall, hanging in the air like a rope of white smoke while everything around it bent horizontal in the wind. Water being pulled *up*.

"That's not a waterspout," Tomas said quietly over the comm. "Spouts don't pull against the wind gradient. That's—" A pause. Static. "That's suction. Something down there is pulling."

"Something down there is going to make us rich if it wrecks a ship," Bex said. "Eyes on the prize, people."

But Elodie was already reaching, without quite deciding to, for the chart case wedged beside her seat—the one thing she owned that had belonged to her mother. She flipped it open one-handed, steadying it against the wheel, and found the coastal chart of this stretch, the one covered in her mother's handwriting, annotations in three colors of ink, most of them blue for tides, black for hazards.

And red for the things her mother had never explained.

There was a red mark here. On this exact stretch of coast, ten kilometers south of Halfway Sound, a small red circle drawn around nothing at all—open water—and beside it, in her mother's tight, slanted hand: *When the sea gives back what it took, go in before it changes its mind.*

Elodie stared at it until the truck hit a pothole and the chart case slid off her knee.

"You okay?" Junie asked.

"Fine." She put the case away. Her heart was doing something complicated. "Watch the road, that's your job."

"I'm sixteen, I don't have a job."

"Then watch it anyway."

* * *

Marlowe arrived the way he always arrived: too soon, and smiling.

They had made landfall salvage by dusk—the cell had come ashore north of their position after all, but the surge it dragged behind it tore the *Coriander*'s aft section loose from the reef and delivered it, half-submerged and groaning, into the shallows of a cove they happened to be sitting in. By full dark they had documented and winched out two tons of copper fittings and a sealed crate of instrument glass worth more than the truck. Enough for a good week. Not enough for a good life.

Marlowe came down the beach at low tide wearing a pale linen suit that should have been ruined by the salt spray and somehow never was, his hands clasped behind his back, his smile fixed at the precise angle of a man who has never once had bad news he enjoyed delivering. Two Optimist porters carried the collection ledger between them like pallbearers.

"Elodie Joubert." He said her name like a toast. "You look wonderful. Storms agree with you."

"They don't."

"Well, they pay you, which in my experience is better." He gestured, and the porters set the ledger on an overturned crate, opening it with ceremony. "Let's see. Copper, glass, documentation fees, less equipment depreciation, less the interest accrual—" His finger traveled down the column. "Forty-one thousand, five hundred and ninety. You're down twenty-two from last month. Marvelous progress."

Twenty-two. Out of forty-one thousand. Elodie felt the small cold animal turn over in her chest and go back to sleep.

"One month," Marlowe said, and his smile widened by exactly one tooth. "Then the terms change. You know the terms."

"I know the terms."

"The Optimists are flexible people, Elodie. We want to help. But flexibility has a floor, and below the floor there's the indenture schedule, and below *that*—" he spread his hands, delighted, "—well. Nobody's ever needed to find out. Isn't that lovely? Nobody ever finds out."

Bex made a sound behind her like a kettle beginning to boil. Tomas put a hand on her shoulder.

"Run," Bex said flatly, when Marlowe had gone, his porters' lanterns shrinking up the beach. "That's what I keep saying. We take the truck, we take the boat, we go east past the survey line where their contracts don't reach. People do it."

"People who do it end up on the indenture schedule," Elodie said. "Running costs more than staying. You know that."

"Staying costs *everything*. Look at you. You're gray, Elodie. You sleep in the driver's seat. When's the last time you unpacked a bag?"

Elodie didn't answer. Her bags stayed packed. They had stayed packed for nine years, since the funeral, since the harbor town that stopped feeling like hers the day her mother's charts went into a case instead of a drawer. You didn't unpack when you might have to leave. Unpacking was a promise, and she had learned not to make promises to places.

"She's right, though," Junie said quietly. It was the first thing she'd said since Marlowe arrived. She was hugging her knees on the driftwood log, chin down, watching the tide come back in over the sand. "About the bags. I don't even have bags. Maybe that's why I'm not tired."

Tomas laughed softly. "That's not why."

"It's one month," Elodie said. "We run the season, we clear the *Coriander*, we keep the fees coming in, and I walk away clean. Then—" She stopped. She had never finished that sentence before. *Then what.* Then she would unpack? Where?

"Then what?" Bex asked, merciless.

"Then I'll figure it out."

The wind had died with the storm's passing, and the sea lay flat and black under a sky rinsed clean of cloud, stars doubled in the water. And it was then, in that enormous quiet, that Tomas stood up very slowly from where he'd been checking the mooring lines, and said:

"Elodie. Come look at this."

His voice was wrong. Not afraid, exactly. Reverent, maybe, and afraid underneath it, the way people sound in churches.

She followed him to the waterline, Bex and Junie behind her, and looked out at the cove, and understood.

The tide was going out. It had come in an hour ago—it was a rising tide, she had watched it rise, she had *documented* it rise—and now it was falling, fast, the water peeling backward off the flats in long silver sheets, exposing sand and stone and ancient black rock that no living person on this coast had ever seen exposed. The seabed breathed out a smell of iodine and deep cold things. And far out, where the water had withdrawn furthest, a dark shape showed against the starlit mud: an opening. A mouth of stone, tall as a church door, breathing fog.

Elodie's mother's voice rose in her memory, ink-red and slanted: *when the sea gives back what it took, go in before it changes its mind.*

"That's not possible," Bex whispered. "Tides don't do that."

"It's doing it," Junie said.

Elodie stood at the edge of the retreating water with her bags packed and her debt unpaid and one month left on the calendar, and felt—for the first time in nine years—the strange, terrifying sensation of a door opening exactly where she was standing.

"Get the lights," she heard herself say. "We're going in."
