## 2. What the Tide Unearths

Tomas had been diving these waters for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once seen the sea flinch.

But it flinched now. He stood on the shingle at the base of the cliffs with his boots in the foam and watched the water pull back like a held breath—further than any tide he'd charted, further than any storm surge had ever shoved it. The seabed emerged in long glistening ribbons: kelp forests lying flat as combed hair, a wreck's ribs he'd only ever known by sonar shadow, pools of stranded fish flashing silver in the bruised light. The monsoon cell they'd chased for three days hung offshore, fat and purple-black, and beneath it the ocean was simply... leaving.

"Bex," he called over his shoulder, not taking his eyes off it. "Bex, you need to see this."

"I'm seeing it." She had climbed down from the *Kestrel's* engine housing, grease to her elbows, and she stood beside him with her head cocked the way she did when a machine made a sound it shouldn't. "That's not a tide. Tides have schedules."

Junie came skidding down the shingle, barefoot, hair full of salt. "The charts are wrong," the kid announced breathlessly. "The whole coast is wrong. I checked the almanac against the barometer and the moon tables and none of them agree with what's happening right now, which means either every instrument we own is lying or—"

"Or something underneath is pulling," Tomas finished quietly.

He felt Elodie before he heard her—the particular weight of her footsteps when she was trying not to run. She came down the rocks with her mother's satchel already open across her hip, and Tomas watched her stop dead at the tideline, watching the water withdraw, and watched her face do the thing it always did when the past reached up out of nowhere and grabbed her by the throat.

"No," she said.

"You haven't even heard—" Junie started.

"No. I know that look on the water. I've seen it in her maps." Elodie crouched, pulled a folded chart from the satchel, and spread it against her knee with hands that were almost steady. Tomas leaned in. The coastline was inked in fine brown lines, annotated in her mother's cramped hand—but here, where the cliffs bent inward toward a cove no commercial chart bothered naming, a mark burned in red ink. A circle. And inside it, in letters pressed so hard the paper had thinned: *WHEN THE SEA WITHDRAWS, DO NOT FOLLOW IT.*

"Well," Bex said into the silence. "That's about the most inviting thing I've ever read."

"She wrote it like a warning," Elodie said. Her thumb moved over the red circle, back and forth, back and forth. "Everything else in these maps is an invitation. Depths, currents, salvage marks, safe harbors. This is the only place she told me to stay away from." She looked up, and Tomas saw the exhaustion under the bravado, the thing she carried like ballast. "Which means it's the only place that mattered."

The sea kept retreating. And there, revealed at the base of the cliff where no entrance should be, yawned a cave mouth forty feet wide—a black arch dripping weed and ancient light, its floor ribbed with stone, descending into the dark like a throat.

Tomas had spent half his life underwater. He knew caves. He knew that everything about this one was wrong: the air moving *out* of it, steady and cool, smelling of rain and green things and something mineral-sweet, like the inside of a geode. Caves breathed shallowly if at all. This one exhaled like a sleeping animal.

"We have maybe two hours before that cell makes landfall," Bex said, checking the sky. "And the tide comes back eventually. Whatever pulled it out has to let it go."

"Then we go now, look fast, and leave," Junie said, already hopping from foot to foot. "Elodie. *Elodie.* Your mom drew a treasure map and told you not to read it. That's basically a dare."

"It's basically a warning," Elodie corrected, but she was already re-folding the chart, and Tomas recognized the motion of someone losing an argument with themselves. "Ropes. Lights. We stay within sight of each other. If the water starts coming back, we drop everything and run. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said everyone, in the tone of people who had no intention of running from anything.

* * *

Inside, the cave was a cathedral that had never been meant for congregations.

Tomas went first, because he always did—his lamp beam sweeping across walls furred with pale blind shrimp and stone that glittered where the water had polished it for ten thousand years. The floor descended in smooth terraces, each one wetter than the last, and the exhalation grew stronger, sweeter, until Junie whispered, "It smells like the first day of summer," and nobody laughed at them for saying it, because it was true.

They came to the third terrace and stopped.

A river ran through it. That alone would have been strange enough—a river, underground, in a sea cave—but the river was flowing *up*. Water poured steadily from the lower darkness behind them toward the higher darkness ahead, climbing terraces that no honest water would climb, silent except for a sound like slow breathing, like a pulse heard through a pillow. Tomas crouched and trailed his fingers in it. Warm. Warmer than blood-warm. It felt less like touching water and more like being acknowledged.

"That's impossible," Bex said, but she said it gently, the way she talked to engines that defied her and then worked anyway.

"Mom's map," Elodie breathed. She'd unrolled it again, holding her lamp low, and her voice changed—thinned out, went young. "There's more. Under the red circle. I never... the ink bled through, I thought it was just staining." She turned the chart over. On the reverse, faint as breath on glass, a second drawing: the river, drawn exactly as it flowed before them, upward, and at its source a small symbol like a spring or an eye, ringed in red. Beneath it, her mother's hand: *IT KNOWS WHO YOU ARE.*

Nobody spoke. Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the first thunder of the approaching cell rolled through like a warning shot.

Then Junie, who had wandered ten feet ahead the way kids do, said, "Um. Guys? Why is there rope?"

There was rope. A modern line, synthetic, bright orange, anchored to a stone spur with a camming device still gleaming with factory grease—and coiled neatly beside it, a gear bag bearing a logo Tomas knew too well. The rising sunburst. The cheerful yellow stitching. Optimist-brand expedition kit, less than a season old.

The wonder in the cavern curdled so fast it was almost audible.

"They've been here," Elodie said. Her voice had gone flat and cold, the voice she used with Marlowe, the voice that cost her something every time she put it on. "Recently. Days, maybe." She picked up the coil and let it run through her fingers. "How. How did they find it before us? It took my mother's *maps*—"

"Maybe it didn't take their maps," Bex said quietly. "Maybe it took their money. Somebody sold somebody something."

"The debt," Tomas said, and hated himself for saying it. "Marlowe said you had one month. What if this is why? What if they already knew what was down here, and your debt was never about money at all—it was about keeping you close. Keeping you *chasing*."

Elodie stared at him, and he watched the thought land, watched it rearrange everything behind her eyes—the storms she'd chased, the salvage fees that never quite dented the principal, Marlowe's smile that never quite reached anything. Her mother's chart trembled very slightly in her hands.

*It knows who you are.*

Upstream, the river pulsed once, strongly, warm air rolling over them like a greeting—or a summons. And somewhere far above, the thunder arrived properly, and with it, distantly, a sound Tomas would remember for the rest of his life: the sea, coming home all at once.

The water hit the cave mouth behind them like a door slamming.
