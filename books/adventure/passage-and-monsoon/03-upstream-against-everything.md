## 3. Upstream Against Everything

Junie had never been anywhere that moved the wrong way before.

The river ran uphill. That was the fact of it, plain as arithmetic, and Junie kept saying it in her head like a prayer against panic: *the river runs uphill, the river runs uphill, and you are climbing beside it, and it is carrying you toward something.* The water came at them from below, out of the black throat of the cave, sliding past their boots with a sound like a crowd whispering, and every step they took upstream was a step the water had already taken. It felt less like walking a riverbank than swimming through air.

"Keep your hand on the wall," Tomas said behind her. His voice was steady the way a rope is steady—something you could put your weight on without thinking about it. "Left side. Always left."

Junie kept her hand on the wall. The stone was slick and warm, warmer than it should have been, warm like skin, and she tried not to think about that either.

Above them—or ahead of them; direction had gone soft in here—the passage narrowed into a throat of collapsed rock, and beyond it the darkness was not empty. It had texture. It had *attention*. Somewhere back down the river, in the flooded galleries they'd waded through an hour or a lifetime ago, someone was following them. Someone with Optimist-brand lamps and fresh-coiled rope and a walk that didn't echo right.

Vess.

Junie had seen her once, from the deck of the *Salt Lark*, standing on Marlowe's barge with her hands folded and her face arranged into nothing at all. Marlowe smiled when he collected debts. Vess did not smile. Vess was what happened after the smiling stopped working.

"She's gaining," Bex called from the rear, where she'd wedged herself with a spanner and half a flare canister, rigging the last of their noise-makers to the ceiling. "Not fast. But she's not stopping, either, and we're carrying—" she patted her own ribs, where the bruising from the surge had already gone purple-black under her shirt, "—baggage."

"We're not baggage," Junie said.

"You're cargo I chose," Bex said, and grinned at her in the lamplight, and Junie's chest did the thing it always did when Bex grinned at her, which was to fill up too fast, like a bilge pump running backward.

Elodie stood at the head of the line, her mother's chart held open against the rock, red ink glowing like a wound in the lamplight. She'd been quiet since the surge took them. Quiet in the way a storm is quiet in its center—all the noise pushed outward, all the violence coiled somewhere unseen.

"There's a gallery ahead," Elodie said. "The map marks it. My mother marked it." Her thumb pressed flat against the red circle. "She drew this forty years ago, and she drew *this room*, and she never once told me it existed."

"Maybe she was saving it," Tomas offered.

"Maybe she was hiding from it," Elodie said, and folded the chart away, and climbed.

* * *

The gallery opened around them like the inside of a bell.

Stalactites hung in ranks from a ceiling so high their lamp beams couldn't find its end, and the reversed river widened here into a slow, glassy channel that ran between pale stone banks, and everything—the walls, the water, the air itself—hummed. Not a sound exactly. More like the memory of a sound. Like standing inside a struck bell after the ringing has passed through you and gone on ahead.

Junie stopped walking. She couldn't help it.

"It's singing," she whispered.

"It's breathing," Bex said, coming up beside her. For once there was no grin. Bex looked at the water the way sailors look at weather they don't have a name for yet. "Things that breathe can be killed. Things that sing can be silenced. Remember that if anyone offers us a deal in here."

The deal came eleven minutes later.

They heard her before they saw her—not footsteps but the *absence* of them, a pocket of silence moving up the humming dark—and then Vess stepped out of the far archway with her lamp held low and her hands visible and empty, which frightened Junie more than any weapon would have.

"Elodie Joubert," Vess said. Her voice was soft and even, a voice for reading terms aloud. "You've made good time. So has the spring. Can you feel it? It knows you're close."

"Stay where you are," Bex said, and hefted the flare canister.

"I'm not here to fight you." Vess's eyes moved across each of them in turn, unhurried, cataloguing. "I'm here to tell you what Marlowe will never say plainly, because Marlowe believes hope is a better leash than fear. This spring grants belonging. That's what the old accounts call it. Whoever reaches it first is *claimed by it*—held, housed, kept. And whatever is claimed can be bottled. And whatever is bottled can be sold." She paused. "Sold to erase debts. All of them. Yours included, Joubert. One month becomes zero. You'd be free."

"And owned," Elodie said quietly.

"Bottled things are cared for," Vess said. "Bottled things are *kept safe forever*. Ask yourself how long anything you love has stayed unbroken."

It was Bex who answered—not with words. She fired the flare canister straight up into the unseen ceiling, and the whole gallery went white and screaming, and in that screaming light Junie saw Vess move for the first time: fast, economical, terrible, closing on them through the blaze like a shadow learning to run.

"UPSTREAM!" Elodie roared. "GO GO GO—"

They ran along the singing bank, and the passage ahead began to come down.

* * *

Later, Junie would understand that Bex had done the math faster than any of them—that she'd seen the collapse starting at the archway behind them and calculated, in the half-second her engineer's mind worked, that someone had to hold the jammed rock long enough for three people to pass under it instead of two.

She didn't say so at the time. At the time Bex just shouted *"Through! Through! Don't look back!"* and wedged herself under the failing lintel with both arms and one shoulder, holding up four tons of mountain with muscle and stubbornness and a spanner braced like a prop, and Junie went through, and Tomas went through, and Elodie went through, and then the world came down.

The boom swallowed everything. Dust turned the lamplight solid. When it thinned, there was a wall of rubble where Bex had been, and a gap at its base no wider than a coffin, and from inside the gap, muffled and furious and unmistakably alive:

"Well. That could've gone better."

Junie was at the gap before she decided to move. She got her fingers into the crack and pulled until her shoulders screamed, and Tomas was beside her, and Elodie, and none of them said *move aside, let me, you'll hurt yourself*—they just dug, four sets of hands on one impossible problem, and it occurred to Junie, distantly, absurdly, in the middle of the dust and the terror, that she had never in her life been part of a *we* this size.

She'd been a stowaway. A stray. A kid the *Salt Lark* hadn't managed to throw off at any of nine ports because nobody could quite bring themselves to do it. Unclaimed. That was the word the harbor office used on her intake form. *Unclaimed minor.*

Tomas's hands closed on Bex's collar and hauled, and Bex came out coughing, one arm hanging wrong, dust caked in the lines of her face like mortar in brickwork.

"My shoulder's dislocated," she announced, in the tone she used for reporting engine trouble. "Also I think I'm fantastic."

"You're an idiot," Elodie said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle.

"You're welcome," Bex said.

They set the shoulder against a boulder while the river sang to them from the dark, patient as tide. Tomas did the pulling, Elodie held her steady, and Junie—because there was nothing else her hands could do—held Bex's other hand, the good one, and squeezed it through the worst of it, and Bex squeezed back hard enough to grind bone, and neither of them let go until it was over.

Afterward they should have run. Every instinct, every plan, the whole desperate arithmetic of pursuit said *run*. But Bex couldn't run, not really, not fast, and so for the first time since the surge had swept them into the dark, the crew slowed down. They bound the shoulder with Tomas's spare shirt. They shared water. They ate the last of the ration bars sitting in a row on the warm stone like children on a dock, legs dangling over the singing channel, and Vess's silence somewhere behind the rubble wall became just another thing the night contained.

"You know what I figured out?" Junie said. Her voice came out small. She made it bigger. "Back home—I mean, back at the boat—" she corrected, and Elodie's head turned, slow, like a compass finding north. "There isn't a back home. There's just wherever the *Lark* is. Wherever you three are. That's—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it, because Bex had held up a mountain for her today, and the least she owed the world was one true sentence. "That's home. You're my home. I'm claiming it. Out loud. So nobody can take it back."

Nobody laughed. Nobody teased. Tomas put his arm around her shoulders, heavy and warm, and Bex leaned her head briefly against Junie's hair, and Elodie looked at her for a long moment with an expression Junie had no name for yet—grief and gratitude tangled together like rope in a locker.

"Claimed," Elodie said finally. "Witnessed by all present."

"Unbreakable contract," Bex added. "No refunds."

And then the river changed.

The hum deepened. The water quickened beneath them, sliding faster uphill, urgent now, eager, and the darkness ahead filled with a light that didn't come from any lamp—a pale, living glow, rising and falling like breath, like a heartbeat the size of a cathedral.

Elodie stood. She unfolded her mother's chart with shaking hands, and Junie saw the red ink circle, and saw, for the first time, the tiny words her mother had written inside it, so small they'd needed this exact lamplight to read:

*It will offer you everything you lost. Do not go alone.*

The glow brightened. And from beyond the rubble wall, faint but approaching, came the sound of unhurried footsteps, and a voice reading terms aloud.

"She's found another way through," Tomas breathed.

Elodie folded the map. "Then we reach it first."
