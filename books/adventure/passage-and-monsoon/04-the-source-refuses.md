## 4. The Source Refuses

The river stopped fighting them at the last bend.

Elodie felt it in the water first—a loosening, like a hand unclenching—and then the passage opened all at once into a chamber so vast her headlamp beam died before it found the far wall. The reversed river poured uphill here in slow silver threads, braiding and rebraiding, all of them feeding a pool at the center of the cavern floor. The pool glowed. Not with reflected light but with its own, a pale warm gold that moved beneath the surface like breath.

The spring.

Her mother's maps had marked it in red ink, and red ink was what you used for warnings, and Elodie had spent thirty years telling herself that was all it meant.

"Elodie." Junie's voice cracked on the second syllable. The girl stood at the water's edge with Bex's arm over her shoulders, Bex's leg bound in Tomas's shirt, and behind them all the dark they'd climbed through. "It's—look at it. It's *looking* back."

It was. Elodie felt the attention of the thing like sunlight on her face, patient and enormous, and she understood with a lurch that this was not water. This was a creature that had learned to be wanted, or perhaps had always been wanted and simply waited for someone to arrive who could survive the wanting.

*Come,* said the spring, without sound, without words, in the way a fire says come to a freezing man.

Tomas went first. He knelt at the edge and the gold rose to meet him, and his face changed—softened, broke open. "My brother," he whispered. "He's here. He's—" His hand dipped toward the surface and Elodie saw the water shape itself around his fingers, form a suggestion of a broad-shouldered boy lost off the Punta reefs twenty years ago, smiling the way he'd smiled before the sea took him.

"Tomas." She caught his wrist. "It isn't him."

"I know." He didn't pull away from her, but he didn't pull away from the water either. "I know what it is. I just—" His voice failed. "Let me have the minute."

She let him have the minute. That was the cruelty of the thing, she realized: everything it showed you would be true. Not a lie. A truth, held just out of reach, forever.

Junie saw whatever Junie saw—a door with her name on it, maybe; parents who hadn't given her up so much as misplaced her—and wept standing up, fists clenched, refusing to kneel. Bex laughed once, low and wondering, staring at hands that the light made whole again. And Elodie—

Elodie looked into the pool and her mother looked back.

Not a memory. Not the flat photograph from the chart table. Her mother stood waist-deep in the gold, gray-streaked hair loose, wearing the oilskin coat Elodie had buried her in, and she was young the way the dead stay young, and she was *waiting*, arms slightly open, the way she used to wait at the end of every dock in every port Elodie had ever run away from.

"You knew," Elodie said aloud, and her voice came out small in the vastness. "You mapped this place. You found it. And you never told me why."

Her mother's mouth moved. No sound crossed the surface. But Elodie heard it anyway, the way you hear your own name across a crowded harbor: *Because I couldn't leave, little bird. Once you drink, you can't leave.*

And there it was. The whole architecture of the trap laid bare—not malice, never malice, the spring was too kind for malice—but belonging as a debt. Stay, and be loved, and never move again, never want again, never ache again. It was the Optimists' contract written in water. It was Marlowe's smile rendered in gold.

It was everything she'd been running from, offered as a place to stop running.

Behind them, boots rang on wet stone.

Vess came out of the upstream dark without hurry, which was somehow worse than if she'd been running. She was drenched to the shoulder, rope coiled at her hip, Optimist-brand carabiners glinting cheerful yellow in the spring's glow, and she carried two things: a rifle slung across her back, and a dozen empty bottles in a net bag, each one stamped with the sunburst logo and the word BELONGING in friendly round letters.

"There it is," Vess said softly, and for one unguarded moment her voice wasn't an enforcer's at all. It was hungry. It was a woman looking at the end of every debt she'd ever carried, including the ones she'd collected for other people. "Do you know how many of us there are? How many people signed a smile onto a contract because the alternative was starving?" She knelt at the pool's edge, reverent, and the gold rose toward her and she flinched back from it like a sinner from communion. "No. Not yet. Bottles first. Rules are rules."

"Vess," Elodie said. "Listen to me. You drink that, you're done. You'll stand right there until you rot, happy. That's the price. My mother mapped it. She *knew*, and she stayed away her whole life, and she still died owing people money—because staying away doesn't clear anything. It's not salvation. It's a nicer cage."

"Everything is a cage," Vess said. "At least this one has good lighting." She pulled the first bottle free.

Bex shifted against Junie's shoulder. "El," Bex murmured. "Whatever you're going to do. Do it soon."

Elodie stepped between Vess and the spring.

The gold surged up behind her, warm against her back through soaked cloth, and her mother's arms opened wider, and every tired cell in Elodie's body screamed *yes, yes, kneel down, you've earned it, you've been so tired for so long.* She thought about the bags she'd never fully unpacked in eleven years of rooms. She thought about the month Marlowe had given her. She thought about three people bleeding and limping and crying behind her who had followed her into the dark without being paid to.

"I offer a trade," she said, loud enough for the whole chamber. Spring and enforcer both. "That's how debts work, isn't it? Something for something. So: take my life. All of it. Every year I've got left. In exchange, the debt dies with me—the Optimists' claim, the interest, all of it—and these three walk out free, and the spring stays whole and yours and untouched by bottles."

The pool went very still.

*Yes,* it said, gentle as tide. *Yes, that is acceptable. Come in, little bird. Come home.*

"No!" Junie's voice, raw. "Elodie, no—"

"It's a lie," Elodie said, not turning around, watching the water watch her. "Isn't it. There's no version where I get in and they walk free. Because you don't trade. You keep. That's what you do—you keep things. You kept my mother's ghost in here for thirty years waiting for me."

The gold dimmed, just slightly. Guiltily.

And that was answer enough.

Vess had the bottle in the water now, filling, glowing, and Elodie did the arithmetic of her whole life in half a second and chose, finally, to stop counting.

She drove her shoulder into Vess, and the enforcer—surprised, off-balance, human—went down hard on the stone. The bottle spun away, shattering. Then Elodie turned to the spring, to her mother's open arms, to the beautiful patient trap, and picked up the largest shard of glass on the rocks, and drove it down into the source.

The sound it made was not breaking glass. It was a whale-song note, huge and grieving, a sound like a continent exhaling, and then the spring *broke*—the gold splitting along fault lines of white, the figure of her mother dissolving even as her expression shifted from welcome to something Elodie would spend the rest of her life deciding whether it was fury or relief.

Then the water came for them.

All of it. The reversed river reversed its reversal, the whole mountain's worth of stolen sea remembering its way home, and the chamber became a throat and they were swallowed. Elodie was tumbling, blind, slammed against stone and then cushioned by some last act of the dying spring's current—pushed, she was almost sure later, *pushed* outward, toward the light, toward the sea, the way a mother pushes a child out of a burning house.

She grabbed Junie's hand. Someone grabbed hers. They were a chain, the four of them, torn and battered and holding on, and the last thing Elodie saw before the white water took everything was Vess clinging to a ledge, empty-handed, screaming something that might have been rage and might have been thanks.

* * *

They washed ashore at dawn, four miles south, in a tangle on a grey sand beach, coughing salt and laughing in the ugly gasping way of people who cannot believe it.

Bex's leg was broken. Tomas had cracked two ribs. Junie had not let go of Elodie's hand since underwater and showed no immediate signs of intending to. And when Elodie fumbled for the folded contract she always carried in her inner pocket—the smile-stamped paper, the sunburst, the number that had ruled her life—it came out soaked and blank. Every line gone. Every figure dissolved. Just clean paper, drying in the wind.

Marlowe found them there at noon, walking down the beach with his umbrella furled and his smile flickering like a bad bulb. He looked at the blank paper Elodie held up, and something behind his eyes recalculated, and for the first time since she'd known him, the smile fell all the way off his face.

"Well," he said. "That's that, then."

"That's that," Elodie agreed.

* * *

The town was called Sable-Anse, and it had a chandlery with a room above it, cheap because the roof leaked in exactly one spot, over the corner where nobody needed to sleep.

Elodie unpacked.

It took an hour, because eleven years of living out of bags means eleven years of things you forgot you owned—a tin of her mother's chart pencils, a dress for occasions that never came, a photograph of a woman in an oilskin coat squinting into weather. She put the photograph on the sill. She hung the dress behind the door. When Bex knocked, crutching up the stairs with Tomas carrying paint for the leaky patch and Junie already arguing about whose bed went where, Elodie looked at the open bags, empty at last, and did not pack a single one of them again.

Outside, the monsoon season was ending. Inside, somebody put the kettle on.

For the first time in her life, Elodie Joubert was nowhere else she needed to be.
