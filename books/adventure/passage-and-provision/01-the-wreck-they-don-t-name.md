## 1. The Wreck They Don't Name

The river told Coral Okonkwo everything she needed to know before her boots touched the water.

It was running brown that morning, heavy with silt from the high country, which meant visibility would be a meter, maybe less. It was running fast, which meant the current at depth would try to peel her off the wreck like old paint. And it smelled wrong — a faint chemical sweetness under the mud and rot, the way it had for three years now, though no one official had ever said so out loud.

She stood on the gunwale of the navy launch and let the harness take her weight, watching the brown water slide past six feet below. Around her, the two sailors assigned as surface support fidgeted with their lines and pretended not to stare. They always stared. A woman doing salvage work for the navy was still enough of an oddity in this delta to earn looks, even after four years of it. She'd learned to make herself boring to look at: plain black wetsuit, no insignia, face blank as a slate. Invisibility wasn't a talent you were born with. It was a discipline, like breath control, and Coral had practiced both until they were indistinguishable from who she was.

"Okonkwo." The launch's coxswain, a broad man named Ferro with a voice like gravel in a drum, checked his watch. "Tide's turning in forty minutes. You want the window or not?"

"I want the window."

"Then get wet."

She spat into the river — an old diver's habit, a small transaction with whatever lived down there — and stepped off backward into the brown.

* * *

The water closed over her like a hand.

Cold first, then the silt, then nothing. At the surface the delta was all noise — engines, gulls, the slap of water against hulls — but three meters down the world went quiet in a way that most people found terrifying and Coral found honest. Down here there were no debts, no ledgers, no officers with clipboards. There was only what was actually in front of your face, and usually that was very little.

Today it was less than little. She descended by feel, left hand trailing the shot line, right hand extended into the murk where the sonar contact waited. The navy called the target *Object Delta-Nine*. Not a ship. Not a wreck. An object. As if language itself could keep the thing from having been real.

Her glove brushed steel.

She followed the curve of it — a hull plate, riveted, furred with a season's growth of algae that felt like wet velvet under her fingers. The vessel lay heeled over on its port side in nine meters of water, half-buried in the delta's soft bottom, and it was bigger than the survey boat the file claimed. Much bigger. Coral had read the file eleven times. *Coastal survey vessel, twenty-two meters, lost in a storm event.* But her hands, moving along the plating, counted out a beam closer to thirty meters, and her fingers found the clean, machined edges of naval-grade fittings, the kind of hardware that didn't go on civilian survey boats, the kind that cost money and required signatures.

Someone had signed for this. Someone had signed for all of it, and then signed again to make it disappear, and now they were paying her — paying her out of a debt she hadn't incurred — to bring up the one piece of it they wanted back.

She found the breach on the starboard side. That was the thing that had kept her awake, insofar as anything kept her awake anymore. Storms tore hulls open in ragged spirals, plates peeled outward, metal screaming away from metal. This opening was inward. Clean-edged. A scuttle charge set low and aft, exactly where a demolition man would put it to flood a machinery space fast and keep the ship from drifting before it went down. Coral had seen scuttled hulls before, in her father's day, when divers still talked about such things in dockside bars with their voices lowered. You didn't forget what one looked like. The river remembered it too; the wound in the steel was already healing over with silt, the delta quietly agreeing to keep the secret.

Inside, the wreck was a throat. She swam the corridor with her light pulled close to her chest so the beam wouldn't bloom against the suspended sediment, past doorways standing open, past a galley where cups had floated free and settled in drifts, past a compartment whose racks of bunks told her the crew had been larger than any survey team. Dozens of bunks. Survey boats carried eight men, ten at most.

Her orders were precise, and precision was the one form of obedience she'd made peace with. *Proceed to the communications compartment. Recover the emergency transmitter assembly. Do not examine, test, or open the unit. Return it sealed.*

Do not open. In four years of navy salvage work, nobody had ever needed to tell Coral Okonkwo not to open something. You didn't tell a person not to breathe. The instruction existed because someone above her believed she might want to — and because they were right.

The communications compartment was aft, behind a watertight door someone had dogged shut from the outside. That detail she noted and filed away without letting herself feel anything about it. Dogged from the outside meant someone had left this compartment knowing there were people inside it, or equipment inside it, that they did not want getting out. The wheel spun stiffly under her hands, protesting four years underwater, and then gave with a groan she felt through her bones rather than heard.

The transmitter sat in its rack like a patient thing. Military housing, olive drab, heavier than the civilian units she'd recovered from fishing boats — heavier because it was built to survive exactly what had happened to this ship, whatever that had been. Its beacon light was dark. Of course it was dark. Someone had killed it before the water came in, or shortly after, and the fact that the navy knew precisely which unit to send her for, in a compartment behind a dogged door, in a wreck they refused to name, told her more than the file ever had.

She worked the mounting bolts loose with slow, even pressure. This was the part of diving she loved, if love was the word — the small mechanical conversation between her hands and a task, the way the rest of the world fell away and left only torque and resistance and breath. Her regulator hissed. Her gauge showed plenty of air. Above her, forty minutes of tide turned toward slack, and somewhere overhead Ferro was watching his watch and thinking about his lunch.

Coral thought about her father.

He had been a diver too — the best the delta had ever produced, people still said, though they said it quietly now, the way you mention a man who died badly. Emeka Okonkwo had taught her the water before he taught her to ride a bicycle. He'd taught her that the river gives nothing freely, that every salvage is a negotiation, that a diver's job is to be calm when everything else is drowning. He'd taught her all of it, and then the navy had taken him apart in a courtroom in a single afternoon, and the same navy had come to her a year later with a ledger full of numbers and a proposition: work it off, or watch them sell the house out from under her mother.

They called it restitution. Four years of hazardous salvage duty against fines and penalties assessed for crimes she'd been twelve years old when they were allegedly committed. Her father had been convicted of falsifying cargo manifests and selling navy property — scrap, mostly, brass fittings and copper wire — through a fence in the port district. The evidence had been thorough. The witnesses had been credible. Coral had been a child, and children believe courts, and it took her until her second year of indentured diving to start asking the questions children don't know to ask. Why would the best diver on the delta risk everything for scrap? Why did the arresting officer get promoted within the year? Why did the case file have pages missing?

She didn't ask those questions out loud. Asking questions out loud was how you got reassigned to fuel barge duty, or worse. Instead she did what she did best: she stayed invisible, she worked, she paid down a debt that never seemed to shrink, and she kept the resentment banked down deep where it couldn't be seen — a slow fire in a flooded compartment, burning anyway.

The last bolt came free. The transmitter released from its rack with a soft click that sounded, in the silence, enormous.

That was when she saw the second unit.

It was tucked behind the main rack, smaller, cobbled together from parts — a field-modified emergency beacon, wired straight into the ship's battery bus with someone's hurried, brilliant soldering. Not navy issue. Navy transmitters looked like this one in her hands, standardized and stamped. This thing looked handmade, desperate, the kind of rig a person builds when the official channels are the danger. Its casing was scarred. Its antenna had been run up through a cable gland some engineer had drilled himself, and beside it, taped to the bulkhead, was a waterproof pouch with papers inside, swollen with four years of river.

Coral hung there in the dead water of the drowned corridor, her own bubbles rising past her face like a curtain, and understood that she had perhaps ten seconds to decide what kind of person she was going to be.

The orders said recover the transmitter. Singular. The orders said do not open, do not examine. The orders assumed she was a tool, and tools see only what they're pointed at.

Her light moved to the pouch. Through the plastic, blurred by water and time, she could make out printed circuit diagrams, a page of dense handwriting, and — her chest went tight around the mouthpiece — what looked like survey charts of the upper river, beyond the Kessel Range, territory no navy chart in her experience bothered to draw.

"Okonkwo, status." Ferro's voice crackled through her earpiece, tinny and far away. "Tide's on the turn."

"Recovering now," she said. Her voice came out level. It always came out level. "One unit secured."

She looked at the second transmitter for three more seconds. Then she took the pouch.

Not the beacon — she left the beacon wired and dark in its hiding place, because taking it would raise questions and Coral Okonkwo did not raise questions. But the pouch was paper, and paper was evidence, and evidence was the one currency her family had never been allowed to hold. She rolled it tight, stuffed it inside her suit against her ribs, and began the long ascent with the navy's transmitter cradled in her arms like a sleeping child she'd been ordered not to wake.

* * *

The surface took her back the way it always did — too bright, too loud, too full of people who thought the river was empty. Ferro hauled the transmitter aboard with a boathook and winch, grunting at the weight, and the two sailors wrapped it in canvas without once looking at it closely, because looking closely at things was not what sailors on classified jobs were paid to do.

Coral stripped her gear in silence, the pouch flat and cold against her ribs beneath the wetsuit. Nobody searched her. Nobody searched her because nobody imagined the invisible woman needed searching. In four years she had learned that being overlooked was not a weakness. It was a position, like a crevice in a wall, and you could hold it for a long time if your arms were strong.

Ferro handed her a thermos of tea without ceremony. "Clean dive?"

"Clean dive," she said.

"Ship give you any trouble?"

"It's just an object," Coral said. "Delta-Nine."

Ferro laughed, short and hard, and turned back to his wheel. The launch swung upstream toward the navy pier, and the delta unspooled around them — brown water, grey sky, the distant smudge of the Kessel Range holding its line against the horizon like a wall built by something with no interest in human traffic. Somewhere up there, past the mountains, was whatever country the charts stopped drawing. Somewhere upriver, the water picked up that chemical sweetness. Somewhere in a courtroom file with missing pages was her father's name, and underneath it, written in a handwriting she was beginning to recognize everywhere she dove, was the word *closed*.

Coral drank her tea and watched the range grow slowly larger and said nothing, because saying nothing was the skill that had kept her alive.

But against her ribs, drying slowly against her skin, the stolen pages pressed their small, patient weight — and for the first time in four years, Coral Okonkwo was carrying something of her own.
