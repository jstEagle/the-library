## 2. Salt in the Ledger

The summons came at low tide, which was the navy's idea of a joke, or perhaps just its idea of efficiency: call the diver when the water is lowest, when the delta lies most nakedly exposed, all its mudflats and rotted pilings and sunken secrets bared to the sky. Coral read the message twice, folded it along its crease, and put it in the pocket where she kept her knife. Then she ate her breakfast — rice, dried fish, tea gone bitter from oversteeping — and gave herself the forty minutes it would take to walk to the naval station without arriving either early or late.

Punctuality was a language. Arrive early and you looked eager, and eager people got noticed. Arrive late and you looked careless, and careless people got remembered. Arrive exactly on time, every time, and you became a fixture, a thing the eye slid over the way it slid over a bulkhead or a mooring post. Coral Okonkwo had spent four years learning to be a fixture. She was very good at it now.

The station at Port Meridian had been a colonial customs house once, and it still wore its history the way old buildings wear their paint: in layers, peeling. The naval wing sat behind it in a compound of prefab offices and drying racks, and Lieutenant Vance's office sat at the end of the corridor, the only one with a door that closed. Everyone in the salvage unit knew what that meant. Everyone knew, and no one said, because the door was Vance's and the silence was theirs.

The clerk at the desk outside waved her through without looking up. Coral knocked once, heard the flat *enter*, and went in.

Vance kept his office cold. That was the first thing anyone noticed — the air conditioning running at a temperature that made civilian visitors shiver and pull their jackets closed, while Vance sat in his rolled sleeves with a cup of black coffee, comfortable as a man in his own skin. Coral had decided years ago that the cold was deliberate. It kept visitors slightly uncomfortable, slightly hurried, slightly eager to be somewhere warmer. It was a negotiation tactic disguised as a thermostat setting. She had learned to dress for it, and she had learned to let the cold do nothing to her at all.

"Lieutenant." She stood at the mark on the floor — there was a mark, a strip of tape, and everyone understood it as a line not to cross without invitation.

"Okonkwo." Vance did not look up from the file on his desk. He let her stand. He always let her stand, for a count of ten, sometimes fifteen, a small tax collected at the start of every meeting, a reminder of whose time was whose. Coral counted silently and found the silence lasted eleven seconds today. A short tax. That meant he was pleased with her, or wanted something.

"Sit."

She sat. The chair across from the desk was metal and had no cushion, and she had long ago stopped wondering whether that was also deliberate.

"You recovered the unit from the *Kestrel Survey*." It wasn't a question. Vance read as he spoke, turning a page. "Black-box transmitter, hull-intact condition, logged at oh-eight-forty yesterday. Clean work. The report was clean too. Concise. No embellishment." He looked up then, and his eyes were the color of shallow water over sand, pale and unreadable. "You're good at that. The conciseness."

"Thank you, sir."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment." He closed the file. "Though it wasn't entirely not one, either. Sit — you're sitting. Good." He took a sip of coffee. "Do you know what your father's debt stands at, Okonkwo? The current figure?"

"I could estimate, sir."

"Four hundred and twelve thousand." He said it the way a man reads a tide table. "Fines, court costs, restitution to the Crown Maritime Authority, interest compounding quarterly at the standard rate for criminal restitution. Four hundred twelve thousand, three hundred and six, as of this morning's ledger. You earn, after your subsistence allotment, eleven thousand a year against that. Do the arithmetic."

She had done the arithmetic a hundred times, in a hundred cold offices of her mind. Thirty-seven years, at current rates. She would be sixty-one. "Yes, sir."

"Of course you have." Vance set down the cup and laced his fingers on the desk, and Coral understood that the preamble was over. "Here's what you may not have done the arithmetic on. The Authority is restructuring its salvage commitments. Budget review. Some debts are being reclassified. Some are being — renegotiated." He let the word sit in the cold air. "There's a category of service that converts a debt like yours into something more manageable. A single engagement. High difficulty, high value. Completion converts the remainder to a nominal figure — payable, realistically, within two or three years of ordinary wages. Possibly less, with a commendation."

Coral kept her face still. Inside, something turned over, slow and heavy, like a hull shifting on a seabed. *Three years.* She had never once, in four years, let herself think the word *freedom* as a thing with a date attached. Now it sat in the room with her, wearing a number.

"What engagement, sir?"

"Escort duty." Vance opened a drawer and produced a folder, slid it halfway across the desk, and did not let go of it. "A civilian survey team is going upriver. Past the delta, into the foothills of the Kessel Range. Hydrographic work, mineral assessment — the usual. The Authority has contracted to provide a salvage specialist for equipment recovery and underwater work at the survey sites. You'd be attached to the convoy for six to eight weeks. You'd dive where they need diving, keep their instruments working, and keep your mouth shut about whatever you see. Standard terms."

"Standard terms," Coral repeated. The phrase was doing a lot of work, and they both knew it.

"You'll sign the usual acknowledgments. Non-disclosure. Operational silence. The survey is civilian in composition but the contract is naval, which means everything they find belongs to the Authority, and anything you see or hear is covered by your existing obligations." Vance's pale eyes held hers. "It's honest work, Okonkwo. Diving. Instruments. Rivers. The things you're actually good at, instead of the things we've had you doing." A thin pause. "And at the end of it, the ledger changes."

The folder sat between them, half in her reach, half in his grip. Coral looked at it and did not reach for it, because reaching too fast was a tell, and Vance read tells the way she read water — for what moved beneath the surface.

"Why me, sir? There are divers with cleaner records."

"There are divers with cleaner records and worse hands." Vance released the folder. "I need someone who can work unsupervised, keep quiet, and not embarrass the contract. Your record is exemplary. Your discretion is documented. And frankly, your father's name buys me a certain leverage I'd be a fool not to use." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Vance never wasted cruelty. Cruelty was inefficient; leverage was not. "You have until eighteen hundred to decide. Though I'll note that decisions like this one are rarely improved by sleeping on them."

Coral took the folder. It was lighter than it should have been — a contract that thin was a contract with things left out of it — and she made herself stand, and nod, and say "Thank you, sir," and walk out of the cold office into the corridor's stale warmth with her pulse beating hard and even, like a diver's, like a woman descending.

* * *

She did not sign it at eighteen hundred. She signed it at seventeen-forty, in the clerk's office, with the cheap pen that skipped, and the clerk stamped it and filed it and never looked at her face, and Coral walked out of the station into the last of the daylight with the transaction complete and something in her chest sitting wrong, like a weight belt buckled over a bruise.

*One last job.* That was what they always said. Her father had said it too, she'd been told — one last contract, one last run, and then the boat would be theirs and the ledger closed and the family free. Instead there had been a tribunal, and a fine no boat could ever pay, and a name that went into the record like a body into deep water. *One last job* was the salt in the ledger. It got into everything. It never washed out.

She went back to her bunk in the divers' annex, a room she shared with two other debt-divers who were out on night work, and she sat on her cot, and she took out the thing she had taken from the *Kestrel Survey*.

It was not in her report. In her report, the black-box transmitter had been recovered intact, sealed, logged, and delivered to the station's evidence locker, which was where Vance believed it was. What was in the evidence locker was a transmitter. Whether it was the transmitter, Coral had decided in the ninety seconds she'd spent alone with the unit on the deck of the recovery launch, was a question of definition. The *Kestrel Survey*'s black box was a dual-unit assembly — primary logger and auxiliary beacon, bolted to a common frame, designed so that if one unit failed in a wreck the other could still be found. The recovery protocol said *unit*. Singular. She had logged a unit. She had kept a unit.

She had told herself it was insurance. That was the word divers used for the things they did that they couldn't explain. Insurance against what, she hadn't let herself ask.

Now, in the failing light, she set the auxiliary beacon on her cot and looked at it. It was the size of a shoebox, scuffed grey composite, its casing scarred where the river had ground it against the wreck for however many months the *Kestrel Survey* had lain on the bottom of that channel before anyone was sent to name it. The navy had sent her to recover it from a wreck in a channel the navy denied operating in, from a survey vessel the navy's charts did not list, and the recovery order had come down classified at a level that made her, a debt-diver, technically not cleared to know what she was carrying. She had learned, over four years, not to ask about such things. Asking was how people disappeared from the duty rosters.

But she had opened the casing anyway, that first night, with the small screwdriver she used for her regulator, because the casing had been opened before — the screws were scored, the seals re-seated wrong, someone had been inside it since it went into the water — and a diver who finds a sealed thing that has been unsealed by other hands feels a professional obligation she cannot name and cannot ignore.

Inside, the logger board had been stripped. The memory chips were gone, cut out, the solder pads bright and fresh. Someone had taken what the *Kestrel Survey* knew. But the auxiliary beacon was a separate circuit, a separate power cell, and it had not been stripped, because no one thought a beacon mattered. A beacon only did one thing. It listened, and it repeated.

Coral had powered it up on the second night, earphone pressed to her ear in the dark of the annex, expecting the standard loop — a vessel identifier, a timestamp, the flat automated litany of a dead ship. And she had gotten it. *Kestrel Survey, hull registry, position, date of loss.* The dead ship's litany, repeating on its thirty-second cycle.

And underneath it, at the very floor of the signal, so faint she'd had to press the earphone until it hurt, another transmission. Not the beacon's voice. A relayed signal, weak, bouncing off some repeater far upriver, riding the beacon's carrier the way a remora rides a shark. A distress pattern. Not automated — the intervals were wrong for automation, uneven, human, someone keying it by hand with a dying battery. And a coordinate prefix she recognized, because every delta diver knew the coordinate grids the way farmers knew weather: it was from beyond the Kessel Range. From the other side of the mountains. From a place no survey vessel had any business reaching, and no survey vessel had any business being lost in.

She had sat with it for a long time. The signal cycled, and cycled. *Someone is alive up there. Someone is keying a distress call by hand.* And the navy had sent her to pull the box from the water and had ordered her, in writing, to open nothing, and had stripped the memory from the logger before the box ever reached the riverbed, and the arithmetic of all of it came to a sum she did not want to say out loud even inside her own skull.

Now she held the beacon in her hands and did the arithmetic again, because the contract was signed, and the contract said she was going upriver. Past the delta. Into the foothills of the Kessel Range. Toward the signal's origin — or near enough that nearness mattered.

She could turn it in. That was the clean path, the safe path, the path the woman she was supposed to be would take. Walk it to Vance's cold office, set it on his desk, say *sir, the auxiliary unit was still active, sir, there's a signal, sir*, and watch what his face did. His face would do nothing, of course. That was the thing about Vance. But the ledger would change. A discovery like that, delivered by the daughter of a disgraced diver — commendation, reclassification, the debt collapsing like a rotted piling. Three years. Maybe two. Maybe one.

Maybe her father's name, cleaned.

The beacon cycled in her hands, its little indicator LED pulsing green in the dark of the bunkroom, patient as a heartbeat. *Kestrel Survey. Position. Date of loss.* And beneath it, faint as a voice through a hull, the human rhythm of someone still alive, still keying, still asking a question no one had been sent to answer.

Coral thought about her father. She had been eleven when the tribunal happened and what she remembered was not the courtroom but the boat after — the way he'd sat in the cockpit of their impounded salvage skiff and looked at the water as if it owed him an explanation, and the way he'd finally said, *they'll say what they want about me, Coral, but the river knows what happened, and the river doesn't lie.* She had built her whole life since on the opposite principle: the river doesn't testify either. The river keeps everything, and gives back nothing, and the only safety is to be a thing the river doesn't notice.

The LED pulsed. Green, green, green.

She thought about the ledger. Four hundred twelve thousand. Thirty-seven years. She thought about a number that could become a smaller number, a name that could become a clean name, and how the price of it was a beacon in an evidence locker that should have been, and a signal dying somewhere beyond the mountains that no one was supposed to hear.

*One last job,* Vance had said. The salt in the ledger. It got into everything.

Coral wrapped the beacon in her spare shirt, and the shirt in the waterproof dive bag she was entitled to keep under her bunk, and she stowed the bag at the bottom of her kit where the inspectors' hands had learned to stop reaching, because Coral Okonkwo's kit was always exactly what the manifest said it was. Then she lay down in the dark and listened to the annex settle around her, the creak of the pilings, the slap of the tide against the seawall, and she told herself the story she would need to believe: *I'm keeping it because it's evidence. I'll decide what to do with it when I know more. This isn't betrayal. This is insurance.*

The story didn't quite hold its weight. She knew that the way a diver knows when a seal is failing — not by seeing it, but by the faint wrongness in the pressure. She had taken a thing that belonged to the navy, hidden it from the navy, and signed a contract to carry it, and herself, upriver toward the voice inside it. Whatever word you used for that, *insurance* was not the whole of it.

But the tide kept slapping the seawall, and the annex kept creaking, and somewhere beyond the Kessel Range a hand was keying a distress call into a battery that would not last forever, and Coral Okonkwo, debt-diver, fixture, invisible woman, closed her eyes and let herself want the thing she had signed for, and did not let herself name what else she was carrying.

The convoy departed in four days. She spent them the way she spent everything: quietly, punctually, exactly as expected, a woman the eye slid over. She checked her dive gear twice. She read the survey team's manifest and memorized the names. And on the last night, before the early call, she took the beacon out one more time and listened to the full cycle, all of it, the dead ship's litany and the living signal beneath, and she heard something she had missed before — a change in the rhythm, the human keying gone slower, weaker, the intervals stretching like a swimmer's stroke lengthening toward the end of their strength.

Whoever it was had less time than she did.

Coral packed the beacon away, and lay back, and watched the ceiling of the annex go grey with the coming dawn, and understood, with the flat clarity of cold water, that she had already made her choice — that the signature on the contract was not the betrayal, and the hidden beacon was not the betrayal, but that somewhere upriver, in the foothills or beyond them, a moment was waiting for her in which it would become one, and she would have to decide, finally, whose ledger she was keeping.

The call came at oh-five-hundred. Coral Okonkwo rose, shouldered her kit, and walked down to the docks in the dark to meet the convoy — and the first face she saw on the survey team's charter boat, a lean grey-haired man coiling lines with a guide's economy, looked up at her and read her, top to bottom, in a single glance, the way she read water.

She would learn his name within the hour. She would learn that he was trouble. But that was later. For now there was only the river, wide and brown and keeping its secrets, and the boat, and the mountains standing up pale and enormous at the far edge of the world.
