## 3. Upstream, Downwind

The convoy smelled of diesel and rotting fruit before Tomas Reyes ever saw it.

He stood on the mud landing at Puerto Ceniza with his duffel over one shoulder and a letter of introduction he didn't believe in folded inside his jacket, watching three flat-bottomed barges idle against the current. The river here was wide and brown and patient, the color of old tea, carrying silt down from the Kessel Range in quantities that said the mountains were still eating themselves alive upstream. Eight years since he'd last worked this water. The river hadn't changed. He had — enough that the boatmen who remembered him looked away when he passed, which was its own kind of greeting.

They'd cashiered him for losing an expedition. That was the official language, stamped into his file like a brand: *lost*. As if the mountain had been a set of keys. As if four people could be misplaced the way you misplace a glove, and the fault must belong to whoever was holding them at the time.

Tomas had stopped arguing about it around year three of the disgrace. Now he just took work nobody else wanted, and this — escorting a navy survey team upriver into a delta the charts barely acknowledged — was exactly that kind of work.

A woman in civilian clothes was arguing with the convoy master at the second barge's gangplank.

Tomas noticed her the way he'd been trained to notice things, back when noticing kept people alive: not her face first but her weight distribution. She stood with her feet planted slightly wider than a civilian would, knees soft, as if the deck might pitch under her at any moment. Her canvas bag sat at her heel instead of between her feet. And when the convoy master gestured broadly at the river, she tracked the gesture with her eyes only, her head staying level, economical.

Diver's shoulders, too. He saw it when she shifted the bag — the way the muscle wrapped over the joint, built by hauling gear and fighting current rather than by any gym.

Navy. Or navy-adjacent, which on this river amounted to the same thing.

He drifted closer without meaning to, the way water drifts toward a hole in a hull.

"—told me two days," she was saying. Her voice was low and even, the kind of voice that didn't repeat itself. "Your manifest says the survey equipment loads at Puerto Ceniza. I'm the escort for that equipment."

"And I'm telling you, señorita, the forward hold is full of fuel drums because *somebody* at the depot—"

"Then we stack it on deck."

"On deck? In this weather?"

She glanced up. The sky was the color of wet concrete, but it wasn't raining yet, and they both knew it. "It'll rain when it rains. Your tarps are on board already. I checked."

*She checked.* Tomas felt something shift in his chest, half admiration, half alarm. Civilians didn't check other people's tarps.

The convoy master, a heavy man named Bracero whose barge Tomas had once poled through flood season, threw up his hands in surrender and began shouting orders in the abbreviated dialect of men who shout for a living. The woman turned to supervise the loading, and that was when she saw Tomas standing there, close enough now to be conspicuous.

Her eyes did a thing he recognized. A quick inventory — hands, waistline, eyes, exits — performed so fast most people never caught it. Then it was gone, replaced by a face of pleasant nothing.

"You're the guide?" she asked.

"I'm a guide." He let the distinction sit there. "Tomas Reyes."

Something flickered behind the pleasant nothing. She knew the name. Everyone on this river knew the name, and what came attached to it. He watched her decide whether to say so, watched her decide against it, and filed that away too. People who declined easy cruelty were either kind or calculating, and he hadn't lived long enough among either to stop being suspicious of both.

"Coral Okonkwo," she said.

Okonkwo. Not a name from these parts. He offered his hand; hers was calloused across the palm in the pattern of rope work, and her grip was brief and dry and exactly as strong as it needed to be and no stronger.

"You've done river work," he said.

"Some."

"Salt work, though. Originally." He nodded at her forearms, where the tan line ended in a hard edge below the elbow — the mark of a wetsuit worn past the wrist. "Delta diving is different. Freshwater eats your buoyancy assumptions. The thermoclines will lie to you."

"I'll manage."

"I'm sure you will." He shouldered his duffel. "I'm just telling you what killed the last person who managed badly."

It was a cruel thing to say and he knew it, and he watched it land — watched her jaw tighten by a millimeter, watched the pleasant nothing crack just enough to show the resentment underneath, banked and old and patient as coals. Good, some ugly part of him thought. Better she shows me now than on the water later.

"The last person," she said quietly, "wasn't managing. Was he."

It wasn't quite a question. It also wasn't the question everyone else asked, which was *what happened up there*, asked with that particular hunger people have for other people's disasters. She'd skipped the hunger entirely and gone straight to the structural failure. As if she'd read the accident report. As if she'd written one.

"No," Tomas said. "He wasn't."

She held his eyes for three seconds longer than politeness required, then turned back to the loading. Conversation over. Point scored by nobody.

Tomas walked the length of the barge and told himself the tightness in his chest was just the old shame doing its rounds. It didn't feel entirely like shame.

* * *

They cast off at dawn the next morning, three barges lashed in tandem, pushing against a current that wanted them gone. The survey team — six men and a woman under a Dr. Ferro who spoke to subordinates the way weather speaks to landscapes — occupied the lead barge with their instruments crated in foam. The middle barge carried fuel, food, and the escort, Coral Okonkwo, who spent the first day doing nothing visible and somehow knowing everything that happened on all three boats.

Tomas ran the stern sweep of the middle barge and watched her do it.

By noon he'd catalogued: she'd memorized the load plan within an hour. She'd identified which crewman drank before breakfast and which one skimmed the rations, and had arranged, through means he couldn't see, for both problems to quietly stop. When a crate of survey instruments shifted in the swell, she was bracing it before anyone called out. And twice — twice — he'd caught her standing at the rail with a small metal case open in her hands, looking at whatever was inside with an expression he could only call grief, except that grief usually had softer edges.

The second time, he came up beside her deliberately.

"That's government issue," he said, nodding at the case. "I know the stamp."

She closed it. Fast, but not panicked — controlled, the way you close a valve. "It's communications equipment."

"It's a black-box transmitter off a wreck. The casing's been underwater — see the corrosion bloom at the seam? Saltwater corrosion, in a freshwater delta, which means it came off a salt hull or it was stored wrong, and the navy doesn't store anything wrong." He kept his voice conversational, pitched under the engine noise. "So either you're salvaging, or you're investigating, and escorts don't investigate."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere ahead a bird screamed and got no answer.

"You talk a lot for a guide," she said.

"You ask very few questions for an escort. One of us is doing this wrong."

That almost — *almost* — made her mouth move toward a smile. It died before it arrived, but he'd seen the muscle fire, and he stored it away with everything else. Under the banked resentment and the professional calm was someone who remembered how to be amused and resented herself for it.

"Reyes." She said his name like she was testing its weight. "You lost four people in the Kessels. The inquiry blamed route-finding error. Your license was revoked, your outfit dissolved, and every outfitter on this side of the range blacklisted you inside a year. That's not gossip. That's the record."

"Records are written by people who weren't there."

"Were you there?"

"Yes."

"What happened?"

And there it was — the hunger after all, except hers had no relish in it. It was the hunger of someone assembling a file. He thought about lying, thought about the stock answer he gave barkeeps and border officials, and then thought about the metal case she'd closed, and the grief-expression, and the way she'd said *he wasn't managing* like a woman reading a coroner's report she disagreed with.

"A storm cell dropped on us above the tree line," he said. "Whiteout for thirty-one hours. My radio had a cracked antenna mount I should have caught at the last resupply. We got separated on the descent. Two made it down. Four didn't." He heard his own voice go flat and procedural and hated it. "The inquiry decided a cracked antenna was negligence, and negligence was mine, and that was tidier than asking why a survey expedition was above the tree line in calving season in the first place. Which I asked. Repeatedly. Loudly."

"Who commissioned the expedition?"

He looked at her sharply. "Why?"

"Because you're angry at the wrong layer," Coral Okonkwo said. "Or maybe the right one. I don't know yet." She turned back to the brown water sliding past. "But in my experience, when an inquiry closes too fast, it's because somebody needed it closed. And those somebodies tend to be the same somebodies every time."

Tomas stood at the sweep and let the current argue with the blade and said nothing for a long while. Eight years he'd carried the loss like a stone sewn into his coat, and everyone — the inquiry, the outfitters, the boatmen who wouldn't meet his eye — had insisted the stone was his own fault, his own weight, his own to carry. And here was a stranger with diver's shoulders and navy calluses saying, in effect: *check the stitching.*

He didn't trust her. Trust was a currency he'd stopped minting. But he recognized the thing underneath her words, the same thing that lived under his own: the specific anger of a survivor who has been handed the blame along with the survival, and told to be grateful for the second part.

Fellow survivor. The phrase arrived uninvited and unwelcome and true.

"Okonkwo," he said. "That's not a delta name."

"No."

"But you're from a delta somewhere. You stand like moving water is home."

For the first time she looked genuinely off-balance — a half-second hitch, quickly recovered, but he'd spent eight years learning to read water and people read about the same. "My father dove," she said finally. "Commercial salvage. He taught me."

"Is he still in the trade?"

"He's in prison." She said it the way you report a depth gauge reading. "My debt paid for his crimes. That's why I'm on this boat wearing a navy escort's job like a costume. Any other questions, guide, or can we get through this convoy pretending to be strangers?"

"We're not going to manage that," Tomas said.

"No," she agreed, and there it was again, the ghost of the smile, dead on arrival. "Probably not."

* * *

That night they tied up against a drowned forest where the river had taken a stand of trees decades ago, their black skeletons leaning out of the shallows like the masts of a fleet that had sunk in place. The survey team drank in the lead barge. The crew gambled. Coral ate alone at the bow, and Tomas, against every instinct that had kept him alive and alone and unforgiven for eight years, brought his tin cup of coffee and sat down on the cargo netting across from her without asking.

She looked at the cup. Looked at him. Said nothing, which from her was practically an invitation.

"Tomorrow we pass the Narrows," he said. "After that, the banks empty out. No villages, no traffic, nothing on the charts until the foothills."

"I read the same briefing."

"There's one thing not in your briefing." He blew on the coffee. "There's a village on the south bank, forty kilometers past the Narrows. Fishing people. Been there longer than the charts have existed. It's not on any map I've ever seen, and I've seen maps that aren't supposed to exist."

Coral went very still. It was a diver's stillness — total, deliberate, the conservation of motion he'd seen in men hanging at fifteen meters waiting for a decompression window. "Why isn't it on the charts?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He sipped. "I guided a scientific party up here nine years ago, before my name turned to poison. We stopped at that village. They fed us fish and wouldn't let us photograph the children. The old woman who fed us — sharp eyes, sharper tongue — asked me who I worked for, and when I told her, she spat in the water and said a word I didn't understand then." He met Coral's eyes across the cup's steam. "I understand it now. It was the word for *the ones who take and don't give back.*"

"You think the navy erased them from the record."

"I think rivers remember what charts forget. And I think a woman who checks other people's tarps is going to want to see that village." He stood, brushing netting fibers from his trousers. "Fair warning, Okonkwo. Whatever you're carrying in that transmitter case — whatever the navy thinks is at the end of this river — the people in that village are going to be at the end of it too. They always are."

He was three steps away when she spoke, quiet enough that only the river and he could hear it.

"Reyes. The antenna mount. Did you really miss it, or did someone hand you a cracked radio and count on you missing it?"

Tomas stopped. The drowned trees leaned over the black water. Eight years of a stone in his coat, and this stranger had put her hand flat against it in two days and asked the one question the inquiry had refused to ask.

"I've wondered that every night for eight years," he said.

"Well," said Coral Okonkwo, turning back to the rail and the darkening water, "keep wondering. Because I'm starting to think we're on the same river twice."

Behind them, far downstream, a sound rolled up the valley — deep, percussive, wrong for thunder. Both of them turned. In the last light, the Kessel Range stood white and distant against the bruised sky, and somewhere up in that whiteness, ice was letting go of itself.

Calving season was coming early.

Tomas looked at the mountains, then at the woman who was not an escort, and understood that whatever this convoy was carrying upstream, the river had already decided it would not come back down the same way.
