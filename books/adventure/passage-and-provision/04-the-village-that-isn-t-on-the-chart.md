## 4. The Village That Isn't on the Chart

The village appeared on the third morning, and it did not exist.

Coral saw it first from the bow of the *Meridian Star*, where she'd taken to standing at dawn because the survey team's technicians slept late and the river belonged to her alone for an hour. Mist lay on the water in long gray ribbons, and through them came the shapes: stilt houses on the left bank, a dozen of them, maybe fifteen, their thatched roofs dark with dew, a line of drying nets strung between two posts like laundry hung out by giants. Smoke rose from a cooking fire. A dugout canoe knocked gently against its mooring post.

She checked the chart case strapped under her arm. She'd been studying it every morning since the convoy left the delta staging point, partly from habit, partly because knowing the river was the only thing that made this job feel like hers instead of theirs. The chart showed the left bank as unbroken green — floodplain forest, seasonal inundation zone, no settlements marked for forty kilometers in either direction.

"Reyes," she said.

Tomas Reyes was already awake, of course. He sat amidships with his boots off, rubbing salve into the fingers of his right hand, which he claimed was an old injury and which he treated with the tenderness other men reserved for lovers or rifles. He looked up without surprise. He'd been looking at the bank too.

"I see it," he said.

"It's not on the chart."

"No." He pulled his boots back on, unhurried. "It wouldn't be."

"What does that mean?"

He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and came forward to lean on the rail beside her. Up close he smelled of diesel and camphor and something older, like the inside of a chest that had been closed a long time. "Means somebody decided it wasn't worth mapping," he said. "Or decided it was better unmapped. Both happen. Usually the second one's more interesting." He squinted at the shore. "That's not a new village, either. Look at the pilings. Those posts have been replaced three, four times. That village has been there longer than the chart."

The convoy was slowing. Ahead, Lieutenant Vance stood at the rail of the lead boat, binoculars up, and even at this distance Coral could read the set of his shoulders — the particular stillness of a man recalculating. He lowered the glasses, spoke to the pilot, and the convoy began to swing wide, hugging the far channel, putting maximum water between itself and the left bank.

"They're avoiding it," Coral said.

"Survey teams avoid what isn't on their maps," Reyes said. "Navy avoids what is on no map at all. Different reasons. Same result." He glanced at her sideways. "You know this river better than your file says you do. Delta girl?"

"Born on it."

"Then you'll know names I don't. Ask me later if any of them come back to you." He said it lightly, but there was weight under it, the way there always was with Reyes — everything he said seemed to cost him something to lift.

* * *

The trouble started at the fueling stop.

There wasn't supposed to be a fueling stop. The plan had been to run straight through to the range foothills on the drums lashed to the deck, but the *Meridian Star*'s intake filter had been chewing sediment since the second day, and the chief engineer — a hollow-eyed man named Okoye who spoke to engines more fluently than to people — declared they'd ground to a stop within the hour unless he back-flushed the lines. Vance authorized a halt at a marked fuel cache sixty kilometers upstream, and the convoy nosed into a cut in the bank just before noon.

The cache was military. Coral recognized the construction: concrete pad, chain-link cage, fuel bladder under camouflage netting, all of it stamped with a supply code she knew from her father's old requisition forms. What she didn't recognize was the village.

Because the village was here too.

Not the same one — this was smaller, six houses, set back from the bank among breadfruit trees — but built the same way, stilted against the floods, nets drying, children running down to the water's edge to watch the boats. And it was not on the chart either. The chart showed the fuel cache standing alone in empty forest, which was exactly how the navy liked its assets: present on paper, neighbors erased.

A woman came down to the bank with a woven basket, selling fruit to the sailors. Small transaction, ordinary, the kind of thing that happened at every stop on every river Coral had ever known. But when the woman reached the *Meridian Star*'s gunwale and looked up at the faces along the rail, she stopped. Her eyes went across the sailors one by one, quick and practiced, the way a market woman counts change — and then they found Coral and stopped dead.

Coral felt it like a hand closing on the back of her neck.

The woman was old, seventy at least, small and upright, with a face seamed like dried river clay and eyes that had gone pale as shallow water. She stared at Coral for a long moment. Then she said something in a language Coral didn't speak — low, urgent, addressed half to herself — and switched to trade pidgin, loud enough to carry across the rail:

"Okonkwo. You are Okonkwo's daughter."

The name went through Coral like cold water through a wetsuit.

Every head on the deck turned. She was aware of them all at once — the sailors, curious; Okoye frozen halfway into the engine hatch; Reyes at the stern, very still, watching her with an expression she couldn't afford to read. And at the rail below, the old woman holding her basket of mangoes as if it were evidence.

"My father's name is not a word I use," Coral said carefully. "You've mistaken me."

"Mistaken." The old woman laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. It was a dry sound, like reeds breaking. "I carried fish to your father's station when you were a baby on his hip. He gave me a diving knife once, for my son, who drowned in the channel. He said the river should give something back." She lifted her chin. "I do not mistake faces. Faces are the one thing this government never taught us to forget."

Coral's mouth had gone dry. Behind her she heard boots on the deck plating — Vance coming aft, unhurried, the way he moved when he wanted everyone to know he had all the time in the world.

"Ladies," he said pleasantly. "Trouble?"

"No trouble, Lieutenant," Coral said. "A case of mistaken identity."

"Is it?" Vance looked down at the old woman, and his smile stayed exactly where it was while his eyes did the work. "Ma'am, this is a restricted naval operation. I'll have to ask you to step back from the vessel."

The old woman did not step back. She looked at Vance the way villagers everywhere look at uniforms — a long, level look that measured him and found him lighter than he pretended. Then she reached into her basket, took out a mango, and held it up to Coral.

"For the daughter of Okonkwo," she said. "Whether she admits it or not."

And she set it on the gunwale, turned, and walked away up the bank, upright as a mast, not hurrying, not looking back, while the children scattered around her like minnows.

Vance watched her go. Then he picked up the mango, turned it over once in his gloved hand, and dropped it over the side into the brown water. It made hardly any sound at all.

"Interesting country," he said, and walked forward, and that was all.

* * *

They got underway again at fourteen hundred. Coral spent the afternoon coiling line and checking lashings and doing every small mechanical task she could find, because her hands needed work the way other people's mouths needed talking, and because if she stood still she would have to think about what had just happened, and she was not ready to think about it yet.

But thinking came anyway. It came the way the river came — steadily, from behind, whether you paddled or not.

*Okonkwo.* Her father's name spoken on this river, by a woman who had known him, who had carried fish to *his station*. His station. Her father had been a salvage diver, a good one, decorated twice before the fall — and salvage divers worked wherever the navy sent them, on rivers and harbors and coastlines all over the territory. Coral had grown up assuming his postings had been random, the ordinary shuffling of a career. She had never once asked herself why a delta man kept getting assigned back to the same watershed.

And then the court-martial. The falsified manifests. The story the navy told: that Chief Diver Emeka Okonkwo had been skimming recovered cargo, that the evidence was found in his own gear locker, that his disgrace was his own doing. Coral had been eleven. She had spent fifteen years carrying that story the way you carry a stone in your boot — painful, familiar, finally part of how you walk.

Now an old woman on an unmapped riverbank had looked at her face and said *you are Okonkwo's daughter* like it meant something. Like the name belonged here. Like the river remembered him.

She thought about the charts again. Two villages, both real, both old, both absent from official surveys. A fuel cache stamped with navy codes standing in forest the navy insisted was empty. A survey vessel scuttled in the delta with orders sealed so tight that Coral, the diver who raised its transmitter, had been forbidden to open the casing. And now this: a surname with roots in soil that officially did not exist.

Her father had dived these waters. Her father had been posted to a river whose people knew his face. And her father had been destroyed by evidence that appeared, conveniently, inside his own equipment — the way evidence always appears when someone needs it to.

*Don't*, she told herself. *You're building a cathedral out of a coincidence.* Old women misremembered. Names traveled. Rivers were long.

But the stone in her boot had shifted, and she couldn't stop feeling it.

* * *

Near dusk she found Reyes at the stern, mending a net with the crew — he'd attached himself to the boat's working life within a day of boarding, and the sailors had already stopped treating him as a passenger. She sat down on the coil of rope beside him and watched his damaged hand move through the knots, slower than his left but sure.

"You heard her," Coral said. It wasn't a question.

"I hear most things." He tied off a mesh and looked at her. "You want to tell me it was a mistake?"

"It doesn't matter what I want to tell you."

"That's true," he agreed comfortably. "But you're going to tell me anyway, because you've been carrying it around since noon and it's heavy, and heavy things get heavier after dark. I know. I've got a few myself."

Coral watched the water slide past. The banks were closing in as the river narrowed toward the foothills, the forest taller now, older, the light failing earlier between the trunks. Somewhere ahead, past all this green, the Kessel Range stood up out of the earth like a wall, and beyond the wall, whatever the lost expedition had found or failed to find.

"My father dove this river," she said at last. "Before I was born, maybe. Before his arrest, certainly. That woman knew him. Knew him well enough to grieve him." She paused. "Nobody ever told me he was stationed here."

"People don't tell us the geography of our own wounds," Reyes said. "They just hand us the scar and let us guess at the map." He folded the netted section down. "Your father took the fall for something. Everyone in the service knows the shape of that story, even if they don't know yours. Question is what he fell for."

"The manifests said stolen cargo."

"And you believed them?"

"I believed I had no choice but to believe them. There's a difference." She heard the bitterness in her own voice and didn't try to sand it off. Fifteen years of professional calm, and one old woman with a basket had cracked it in ninety seconds. "Why do you care? You don't know me. You've spent three days deciding I'm navy surveillance in a dive suit."

"I have," Reyes said. "And you've spent three days deciding I'm a drunk who got people killed. We're both probably right." He smiled briefly, without much humor. "But here's the thing, Okonkwo. I ran expeditions on this river for nine years. Nine years, and I never once put in at a village that wasn't on the chart — because I never once saw one. Villages don't hide from cartographers. They get hidden." He nodded upstream, toward the gathering dark and the mountains beyond it. "Somebody emptied this valley off the record. And now a navy survey team is running up it with sealed orders, and a navy lieutenant won't let his boats touch the bank, and the one name from the old days that anybody ashore remembers is yours." He met her eyes. "I care because I've seen this pattern before, and the last time I saw it, I was standing in the middle of it when it closed."

The lanterns came on along the deck. Forward, Vance stood at the rail with his back to them, watching the eastern sky where the first peaks would be, though nothing was visible yet but blackness.

"Get some sleep," Reyes said quietly. "Tomorrow we make the foothills. And whatever your father stepped in on this river—" he gathered up the net, "—I'd bet my last credential it's still down there. Rivers keep everything. That's the first thing divers learn, isn't it?"

Coral didn't answer. She sat on the rope coil until full dark, listening to the engine's heartbeat and the water's silence, and the mango she hadn't eaten — fished from the wake-slop by a grinning sailor who'd seen the whole exchange and said nothing — sat in her jacket pocket like a small round grenade.

That night she dreamed of her father's hands, scarred and steady, tying off a line in brown water, and woke with the taste of the river in her mouth and one thought fixed and cold as ballast:

The transmitter casing in the hold had her father's unit stamp on the inside of the seal. She'd seen it during the recovery and filed it away as routine. It was not routine. Nothing on this river was routine.

In the morning, the radio traffic from the survey team ahead would go strange — and Coral would have to decide, faster than she wanted, which side of the water she was standing on.
