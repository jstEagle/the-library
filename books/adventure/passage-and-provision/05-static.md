## 5. Static

The radio died on the fourth morning above the last fuel depot, and it did not die dramatically. That was the first thing Coral noticed, standing in the shallows of the convoy's wake with her hands in her pockets, watching the survey team's lead boat sit dead in the current a hundred meters upstream. No explosion, no smoke, no shouting. Just a voice that had been talking every hour on the hour — Engineer Diallo's clipped status reports, weather notes, depth soundings — going quiet between one transmission and the next.

Silence on a river is never empty. It has weight, like water over a wreck.

She'd been listening to those reports for nine days without meaning to. Habit, maybe. Or the same instinct that made her count the strokes of every dive, track her air by feel, keep a running ledger of everything that could kill her. The survey team checked in at 0600, 1200, and 1800 sharp, and Lieutenant Vance had stood at the rail of the command barge each time with his hands clasped behind him, receiving the reports like tribute. At noon that day, Diallo had reported clear water and good progress through the foothill narrows. By evening check-in: nothing.

By the second silent check-in, Vance had stopped pretending to be calm.

"Jamming," he said, in the convoy's evening council, held under an awning strung with lamps on the barge's flat deck. Moths battered the canvas overhead. "Or equipment failure. Either way, we're not chasing ghosts into the narrows with the calving season six weeks out."

"The narrows are charted," said the convoy master, a thick-necked woman named Brakke who'd run this river since before Coral was born. "Diallo's team had satellite relay until yesterday. If they went dark in the narrows, they went dark for a reason."

"They went dark because rivers eat radios, Master Brakke. Moisture, interference, terrain shadow." Vance's voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man explaining arithmetic to children. "We are a salvage-and-escort operation, not a rescue expedition. Our contract ends at the range foothills. It ends *tomorrow*."

Coral stood at the edge of the lamplight where she always stood — close enough to be counted present, far enough to be forgotten. She'd gotten very good at that position over eleven years of navy service. There was an art to being invisible in plain sight: keep your hands still, your eyes down, your breathing even. Never stand directly behind anyone; stand beside their blind spot. Her father had taught her none of it. The navy had. Eleven years of debt service would teach anyone how to disappear inside an institution that owned them.

But she watched Vance's face while he talked, and she saw what he thought no one could see.

He wasn't worried about the survey team. He wasn't even surprised.

* * *

She turned it over all night in her bunk aboard the escort skiff, listening to the river slap the hull. Equipment failure happened. Rivers ate radios — she'd heard Brakke say so herself, and Brakke knew this water better than the charts did. But Diallo's team carried redundant sets, primary and backup, and the backup ran on its own power cell. Two radios didn't fail at once unless someone made them.

And there was the other thing. The thing she'd been carrying in her chest since Salt Harbor like a swallowed stone.

The transmitter.

She'd opened it. Of course she'd opened it — three nights before the convoy departed, alone in the evidence locker with a torque driver and eleven years of curiosity compressed to a single point. The order had been explicit: recover, seal, transport, do not open. But orders were just words, and words had never once in her life protected her father from what people decided he was. She'd cracked the casing expecting circuitry, serial numbers, maybe a coordinates log she could memorize and forget.

What she'd found instead was a second signal buried under the first, faint as a heartbeat under a drumbeat. A repeating burst on a frequency that shouldn't have existed in a navy transmitter — an old expedition band, pre-war, the kind Tomas Reyes's generation still swore by. And threaded through the static, when she'd pressed her earpiece hard enough to hurt: a pattern. Not noise. A distress cadence. Three short, three long, three short, cycling every ninety seconds, tagged with coordinates that put the source somewhere past the Kessel Range, deep in country no navy chart acknowledged.

She'd copied the coordinates onto the inside of her left forearm in grease pencil, then scrubbed it off and copied them again into memory, the way divers memorize wreck layouts — by swimming through them until the mind holds the shape without the map.

She hadn't told anyone. Not because she feared punishment. Because some part of her, the part that had stayed invisible for eleven years, understood instinctively that this signal was not meant to be found — and that whoever had hidden it inside a scuttled wreck wanted it to stay hidden.

Now the survey team had gone silent in the foothills below that same range, and Vance wanted to turn around.

* * *

At dawn the convoy began its reversal, boats swinging in the brown current, deckhands coiling lines with the brisk cheer of men going home. Coral worked her assigned skiff without speaking, as always, and watched Vance across the water.

He stood on the barge rail with binoculars, looking north at the Kessel Range, which rose out of the morning haze like the spine of something drowned. He looked at it the way she'd seen men look at graves they'd dug themselves. Then he lowered the binoculars, spoke briefly to his signals officer, and went below.

That afternoon, Coral swabbed the skiff's deck within earshot of the signals tent. She hadn't planned it. Her body simply arranged itself there, sponge in hand, invisible, while inside the tent Vance's voice moved low and fast.

"—no recovery attempt. I want that on record as my operational judgment. The terrain is unsuitable, the season is closing, and the survey's findings were preliminary at best." A pause. "Because preliminary findings are *all* they'll ever be, Sergeant. That's not cynicism. That's logistics."

Another pause, longer. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing, and Coral had to stop breathing to catch it.

"If anyone asks about the second frequency, you never heard it referenced. The transmitter comes back sealed or it doesn't come back at all. Am I understood?"

Coral wrung the sponge out slowly and moved on, her heart tapping against her ribs like a knuckle on a door.

*Second frequency.* He knew. He knew about the signal she'd found, which meant he knew what it was, which meant the wreck in the delta — the wreck she'd been sent to strip — hadn't been an accident at all. Vessels didn't sink themselves. Transmitters didn't hide distress calls inside themselves. Someone had built that silence deliberately, layer by layer, and Vance had been sent to make sure it held.

And now a survey team full of engineers and scientists had gone quiet in the mountains above the thing he was burying.

Obedience, Coral thought, watching the foothills darken, is a debt like any other. You pay it long enough and you forget you ever had anything of your own.

She was done paying.

* * *

She waited two days, because invisibility was also patience, and because a deserter who bolted on the first night got caught by the first patrol. She inventoried the skiff's stores with a salvager's eye: ration tins, a spirit stove, a coil of climbing line, a flare kit she palmed without breaking its stowage seal. She noted that the convoy's route home bent east around the delta shoals, leaving the western bank — the bank that ran toward the range — patrolled only at dusk and dawn. She learned the watch rotation by heart without appearing to learn anything at all.

On the second night, rain came up the valley, soft and steady, the kind of rain that erased footprints and flattened sound. Perfect weather for a drowning; perfect weather for a disappearance.

She went over the side of the skiff at 0300 with her pack wrapped in oilcloth, swam the thirty meters to the western bank underwater the way she'd been diving since she was nine years old, and came up in the reeds with the river's cold still clinging to her like a second skin. Behind her the convoy lay at anchor, lamps dimmed, sentries hunched under tarps. Ahead of her the land climbed away into blackness, toward foothills she couldn't see and a signal she could hear in her head even now — three short, three long, three short — patient as a pulse.

She'd taken nothing that could be traced and left nothing but wet footprints the rain was already erasing. Eleven years of being nobody had trained her for exactly one moment, and this was it.

She was a mile into the trees when she heard the footsteps behind her.

She stopped. Drew the knife from her boot without turning. The rain ticked through the canopy.

"Don't bother with the blade," said a voice she knew — rough, unhurried, faintly amused. "If I wanted you caught, I'd have shouted already."

Tomas Reyes stepped out of the dark with a pack on his back and his collar up, gray-bearded, weathered, moving with the economy of a man who'd spent forty years walking away from things. The disgraced guide. The man who'd lost an expedition beyond the Kessel Range and never forgiven himself, if the convoy gossip was true — and Coral suspected it was, because guilt clung to some men the way silt clung to a hull.

"You followed me from camp," she said.

"I watched you inventory that skiff for two days like you were pricing it for auction." He tilted his head at the slope ahead. "You're heading for the narrows. Upstream. Toward the signal."

Her blood went cold and slow. "What signal."

"The one you've been hearing in your sleep since Salt Harbor." He shrugged. "I've carried a lost expedition inside me for nineteen years, girl. I know what it looks like from the outside. You've got the same walk."

Coral said nothing. The rain filled the silence.

"I know these mountains," Reyes said. "Every pass, every shelter stone, every lie the maps tell. And I know the calving season opens the high route for perhaps ten days, six weeks from now, and then seals it until spring. If your people are alive up there — and if that transmitter of yours says what I think it says — you cannot cross alone. You'll die in the first whiteout, and they'll die waiting."

"And you?" she said. "Why follow a deserter into the mountains?"

Something moved behind his eyes, old and heavy. "Because nineteen years ago I turned back when I should have gone forward, and I've been walking toward that mistake ever since." He shouldered his pack and nodded uphill, into the rain, toward the range. "Well, diver? Are we going, or are you going to arrest me?"

Coral looked back once, downstream, at the blurred lights of the convoy — at the life she'd been lent and never owned. Then she sheathed the knife and turned north.

The obedience died quietly, right there in the rain, and she felt its absence the way you feel a weight coming off a wound: not relief exactly, but room. Room to move. Room, at last, to be someone.

Behind them both, far downriver, a lantern flared on the barge deck — and a voice began calling names through the storm.
