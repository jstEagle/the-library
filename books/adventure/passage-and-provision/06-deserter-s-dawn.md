## 6. Deserter's Dawn

Tomas Reyes had spent twenty years learning to sleep like a man with nothing left to lose, which was to say: badly, and with one ear open.

The camp at the range foothills was a sprawl of navy canvas and survey crates, generators humming their flat note into the dark. He'd pitched his own tent apart from the others, the way he always did now, the way a man sleeps after he's buried an expedition. And so he heard it — the small sounds, past midnight, that didn't belong. A zipper drawn slow instead of fast. Boots carried rather than worn. The particular silence of someone moving through a camp who has rehearsed it.

He lay in his bag and listened to Coral Okonkwo steal herself away.

He'd known for two days. Maybe three. It was in the way she stood at the waterline each evening, not looking at the river but through it, upstream, toward the white teeth of the Kessel Range catching the last light. It was in how she'd asked him about the pass — casually, over the cook fire, the way you ask a question you've already answered yourself. *When does the calving season close it? How many days of window? What's the ice bridge rated for?* Questions dressed as small talk. He'd given her honest answers because lying to her felt like a debt he couldn't afford, and because some traitor part of him wanted to hear what she'd do with them.

Now she was doing it.

Tomas unzipped his bag without a sound. Twenty years ago he'd have lain there and let her go, told himself it wasn't his business, that a cashiered guide doesn't chase navy divers into the mountains. But twenty years ago he hadn't yet learned what it cost to be the man who stayed behind when everyone else went up.

He dressed in the dark. Took his pack, already half-packed — it had been half-packed since the foothills, though he'd have denied it under oath. Left the tent.

* * *

She was good. He'd give her that. She moved along the generator's noise-shadow, kept to the lee side of the supply crates, timed her crossings between the sentry's turns. Navy-trained, obviously, but more than that — she had the instinct. Some people are loud even when they're quiet; they broadcast themselves in every gesture. Coral Okonkwo was built out of not-being-noticed, and Tomas recognized the architecture because he'd lived inside something similar since the inquiry. You learn to make yourself small enough to disappear, and then one day you realize you can't remember how to take up space.

She reached the tree line at the edge of camp and stopped.

Tomas stepped out of the shadows ten meters ahead of her, deliberately, where the moon off the glacier face would catch him.

She didn't gasp. He watched her weigh it — fight, run, talk — in about a second and a half, her hand drifting toward the knife at her hip and then settling. Professional calm. He was starting to know its texture, and starting to suspect what it cost her to keep it polished.

"You're going up," he said. Not a question.

"Reyes." Her voice was low, level. "Walk back to your tent."

"The pass closes in nine days. Maybe eight, if the warm front off the coast keeps pushing moisture into the range." He shouldered his pack so she could see it. "You've got a copied set of coordinates on a transmitter you were ordered never to open, no route knowledge above the tree line, no rope team, and a crevasse field on the north shoulder that eats two climbers a decade whether they're guided or not."

"I'll manage."

"You'll die. Slowly, at first, and then all at once. That's how the mountain does it."

She looked at him for a long moment. The moonlight did her no favors and she didn't seem to care; she stood in it plainly, a woman deciding something. "Why are you here, Reyes? Did Vance put you up to this?"

"Vance doesn't know I'm awake."

"Then why?"

And there it was — the question he'd been walking toward since he'd heard her zipper. He could lie. He was good at lying; the inquiry had taught him that too, taught him how a man learns to say *conditions were unforeseeable* until the words wear grooves in his mouth.

"Because eleven years ago I led six people onto the Kessel icefield in a weather window I misread," he said. "I brought back two. The mountain took the other four, and the board took my certification, and I have spent every season since telling myself the pass is closed to me. It isn't closed. I just never went back." He breathed. "You're going up that mountain tomorrow morning with or without me. With me, you live long enough to reach whatever it is you think is up there. Without me—" He shrugged. "The crevasse field. Two a decade."

Silence. Somewhere down the slope, the sentry turned at the wire, boots crunching frost.

"Why do you care what happens to me?" she said finally. "You've known me nine days. You think I'm navy. You think I'm a liability. You said so."

"I said you read like navy. There's a difference." He took a step closer, careful, the way you approach a spooked animal or unstable snow. "Nine days is long enough to watch a woman copy distress coordinates out of a transmitter she's forbidden to touch, and hide it, and say nothing, and then lie awake deciding. That's not a liability, Okonkwo. That's the only kind of person worth following up a mountain."

"You don't know what I'm deciding."

"No. But I know what it looks like from outside. It looks like a woman choosing between the life she's been sold and the thing she actually believes." He held out his hand, palm open, empty — an old guide's gesture, *nothing in it, nothing up my sleeve*. "I'm not asking you to trust me. Trust is for people with time. I'm asking you to take the guide."

* * *

They walked out of the foothills in the blue hour before dawn, when the cold comes up out of the ground and everything smells of stone and iron. Coral set a hard pace and Tomas matched it without comment, reading her as he read any client: stride length, breathing, where her eyes went on the broken ground. She picked lines like a diver, he noticed — always testing footing before weight, committing fully once committed. Water habits. They'd serve on ice, mostly.

An hour out, as the sky behind them went from black to bruise, she spoke without turning around.

"How bad is it really? The pass."

"Bad enough that we move like this every day. The calving season opens the route for maybe a week — the glacier pulls back off the moraine, opens the ice bridge across the Kessel throat, and then it starts shedding and the whole upper valley seals itself until spring. Eight months, if you miss the window." He glanced at the range, white against the paling east. "We won't miss it. But we'll arrive tired, and tired people make the mistakes I made eleven years ago."

"What happened?" she asked. Flat, direct. Not cruelty — curiosity, the surgical kind. "Your expedition."

Tomas walked thirty paces before he answered. "Weather window. My call. I read the pressure gradient wrong — or I read it right and talked myself out of what I knew, which is worse. We were on the icefield when the front came through early. Whiteout for sixty hours. When it lifted, four of them were gone. Two walked out with me." His voice stayed level; it had practice. "The board called it negligence. My clients' families called it worse. Both were right."

"And you've been guiding river convoys ever since. Down here. Where nothing can fall on you."

"Where nothing falls on anyone," he corrected. "That was the deal I made with myself."

She was quiet for a stretch. The path bent along a stream course, and she moved through the willow scrub ahead of him, small and certain, a shape the landscape seemed to agree to carry.

"My father dove salvage," she said, not turning. "Delta work, same water I dive now. They convicted him for falsifying cargo manifests — losing navy property, covering losses. Eleven years in the stockade. He comes out next year an old man." A pause. "I signed my indenture the week after the verdict. Eight years of service against his debt. I've got fourteen months left."

"That's why you're going up there."

"That's why I was going to stay," she said. "What I'm doing now, I haven't decided yet."

But Tomas had watched her copy those coordinates, and he had watched her face while she did it, and he knew a decision when it was already walking uphill at dawn.

* * *

They made the tree line by full light, the last stunted pines giving way to rock and lichen and the first dirty patches of old snow. Below them, the camp was a scatter of gray geometry beside the brown river, smoke standing straight up in the windless morning. No alarm yet. By noon there would be.

"They'll come after you," Tomas said. "Vance. He strikes me as a man who balances books personally."

"He'll come after us both. A deserter and a guide with a record." She said it without self-pity, inventorying it, the way she must inventory everything. "You understand that. Coming with me isn't redemption, Reyes. It's just a different way of being ruined."

He laughed — short, surprised out of him. "Lady, I've been ruined at this altitude before. At least up here the view's better."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The beginning of the muscle memory of one.

They climbed. The morning hardened around them, sun striking off the snowfields, and the Kessel Range rose ahead in its terrible patience, white shoulders, blue shadowed couloirs, the glacier throat hidden somewhere beyond the first ridge like a held breath. Tomas checked the sky out of habit — pressure steady, wind light from the northeast, a window. For now.

At the midday halt, sharing water, she looked at him across the rock.

"Why now?" she said. "Eleven years, and you walk back up for a stranger?"

Tomas screwed the cap back on the canteen and considered the question honestly, because she'd earned honesty and because he'd been asking himself the same thing since the tree line.

"Because you're the first person in eleven years who's headed toward the thing that ruined them instead of away from it," he said. "I've been circling this mountain for a decade, telling myself I stay away out of humility. Humility." He shook his head. "It was fear. And you — whatever's up there, whatever Vance buried, whatever your father's name is worth — you're walking straight at it. I figured some of that might be contagious."

She held his look. Then she stood, shouldered her pack, and offered him the thing he'd offered her at the tree line: an open hand, palm empty.

"Then we go together," Coral Okonkwo said. "Two runaways. Nobody's ledger."

Tomas took it. Her grip was a diver's grip — strong, unhurried, built for holding on in current.

Behind them, far below, a sound came up the valley thin with distance: a whistle, repeated, urgent. Camp alarm. Someone had counted tents.

Coral didn't turn. "How long before they organize a pursuit party?"

"Twelve hours if Vance is good. Six if he's angry." Tomas squinted up at the ridge line, measuring it the way he used to measure it, before. "So we stop being two people on a mountain and start being weather. Move."

They moved.
