## 7. Vance's Ledger

The message came in at 0412, and Lieutenant Marcus Vance was awake to receive it because he had not slept properly in eleven days.

He sat in the operations trailer at the delta staging post with a cup of coffee gone cold beside him and the transmitter's maintenance log open on his screen. The log told him three things. First, that the unit had been powered on for forty-one minutes on the night of Coral Okonkwo's return from the wreck — forty-one minutes during which she had been alone in the equipment bay, ostensibly cleaning her dive gear. Second, that someone had accessed the auxiliary channel buffer, the one that stored everything the primary receiver discarded. Third, that at 0237 this morning, a diver's knife from the supply locker had been used to pry open the case of the backup beacon — the cheap one, the decoy they'd planted in the rack precisely because it looked important enough to steal.

Vance read the entries twice, then closed the file and sat very still, listening to the river move past the pilings below the trailer.

So. The Okonkwo girl had opened it after all.

He'd wondered. He'd watched her sign out the transmitter at the dock with that careful blankness divers wore like neoprene, and he'd thought: either this woman is exactly as obedient as her file says, or she is the best liar the navy ever produced. Her file said obedient. Her father's file said traitor. Vance had learned long ago that files were written by people, and people were written by their appetites.

He keyed the intercom. "Sergeant Okafor. Wake the recovery team. Full kit, boats in the water by oh-six-hundred."

A pause. "Sir, the survey escort contract—"

"The survey team is dead or gone, Sergeant. That's why we're going." He cut the channel before the man could ask the next question, which would have been *going where*, and Vance did not yet have an answer he wanted to say aloud.

* * *

The problem, as he laid it out for himself in the grey hour before dawn, was a problem of arithmetic.

Item: the survey vessel *Meridian* had been scuttled on navy orders eight months ago, its crew paid off under seal, its findings classified under a designation so deep that most of the officers who signed the paperwork didn't know what they were signing. Vance knew. He'd been the one who drafted the scuttling order, and he'd done it because the alternative was letting Amara Diallo's water samples reach a civilian lab.

Item: Diallo's samples showed hexavalent chromium and processing solvents in concentrations that would kill a delta village inside two years. The mining operation upriver — Kessel Consolidated, navy-licensed, navy-protected — had been dumping tailings into the watershed since before the village of Nsukka Bend appeared on anyone's chart, which was convenient, because officially it never had.

Item: Coral Okonkwo's father had been convicted of salvage fraud for "diverting" a cargo of industrial filtration equipment that had, in fact, been bound for Nsukka Bend. The equipment had never existed as cargo. It had existed as evidence. The trial had made sure it arrived at the bottom of the delta instead of in front of a magistrate.

And item, the newest one: somewhere between the wreck site and this trailer, a debt-bound diver had copied a distress signal from an expedition that had gone missing beyond the Kessel Range fourteen months ago — an expedition whose last recorded transmission mentioned, in language the censors had flagged but not fully scrubbed, *contamination consistent with upstream extraction*.

Vance turned his cold coffee in a slow circle and considered the shape of his career.

It was not, he told himself, corruption. Corruption was taking money. Corruption was selling your country's secrets to a rival power. What Vance was doing was triage. The delta was poisoned; that fact existed whether or not anyone wrote it down. If it stayed unwritten, the remediation could be handled quietly, over years, through channels that wouldn't destroy the licensing regime that kept half the province's economy upright. If it got out now — if a disgraced diver's daughter stood up in an inquiry with a transmitter log and a dead expedition's coordinates — there would be no quiet years. There would be hearings, and resignations, and Kessel Consolidated's lawyers descending like gulls, and every officer who'd touched the file would be fed into the machine to protect the ones above them. Vance had seen how those machines worked. He intended not to be ground meal in one.

The lost expedition was the hinge. Fourteen months ago, a civilian survey team led by a guide named Tomas Reyes had crossed the range and stopped transmitting. Officially: weather, terrain, misadventure. Unofficially: they had found the tailings ponds, photographed them, sampled them, and started home with the intention of publishing. The navy's contribution to their journey home had been a delayed rescue authorization — seventy-two hours of it, while a storm closed the pass — and by the time anyone went looking, there was nothing to find but an empty high camp and a story too convenient to question.

Reyes had taken the blame. Cashiered, disgraced, unemployable. And now Reyes was missing from his billet in the river town, and Okonkwo was missing from hers, and the two absences together formed a shape Vance did not like at all.

* * *

At 0530 he stood in the boat bay watching the recovery team load. Six men, all of them his own picks from the garrison security detachment — men whose service records he held the way a gambler holds marked cards. Not one of them clean enough to testify against him without testifying against themselves.

"Rules of engagement," he said, and six faces came around. "The targets are a deserter and a civilian guide. They are not to be harmed unless they present a lethal threat, and I will be the judge of what presents a lethal threat. We bring back the data drives intact and we bring back the people who can tell us what they've already transmitted." He let that sit. "Anyone have a problem with any part of that?"

No one did. He hadn't expected anyone to. That was the thing about men with debts: they understood other men's debts better than their own consciences did.

Okafor lingered as the others cast off. "Sir, the report to Command. How do you want to frame the objective?"

Vance had spent two hours on this. "Search and rescue," he said. "Two navy-affiliated personnel overdue in deteriorating conditions ahead of calving season. We are conducting a humanitarian recovery operation with secondary responsibility for sensitive government property." He met the sergeant's eyes. "That is also true."

"Yes, sir." Okafor hesitated. "Is it, sir?"

"It's true the way a chart is true, Sergeant. It shows you the water and leaves out the rocks." He picked up his pack. "Get the second boat ready. I'm coming along."

* * *

They found the first sign at the foothills camp the convoy had abandoned two days earlier: a dive knife wedged under the wheel chock of the lead truck, placed where it couldn't be missed. A message, or a taunt. Vance turned it over in his gloved hand and felt something he refused to name.

She wanted him to know she'd gone. Which meant she thought knowing would stop him. Which meant she believed he still operated inside some boundary of rules — that a lieutenant would hesitate to pursue a deserter into the high country, would weigh regulations against a woman's life and come down on the side of procedure.

She wasn't wrong about the weighing. She was wrong about the outcome.

"Trackers say two sets of prints heading north-northeast," Okafor reported. "One heavy-soled boot, one lighter. Moving fast for civilians."

"They're not moving fast," Vance said. "They're moving scared. There's a difference, and it means they'll make mistakes." He looked up at the range, white and indifferent above the tree line, and did the arithmetic again: four days to the pass at military pace, six at theirs, and the glacier reports said calving season would open the ice bridge sometime in the next week and close it for good shortly after. Eight months of closure. Whatever happened on the far side of that ice would happen without him — unless he was standing on it when it opened.

"Command wants an update by eighteen hundred," Okafor said.

"Then at eighteen hundred they'll get search and rescue." Vance sealed the knife in an evidence bag, though there would be no evidence chain, no court, no moment when this bag was ever opened again. He kept it anyway. He kept everything. A man built his ledger line by line, and the lines were all he'd have left when the totals came due.

In the trailer that evening he composed the report himself, three sentences long, each one technically accurate and collectively a lie. *Recovery operation initiated. Subjects believed alive and mobile toward Kessel Pass. Sensitive equipment recovery proceeding in parallel.*

He sent it, then sat looking at the screen for a long time. Somewhere in the back of the trailer, the radio hissed through its channels — routine traffic, weather, the empty litany of a garrison that didn't know what it was guarding. On one of those channels, if she'd found a relay, if she'd gotten far enough, there might be a voice right now reading contamination figures into the open air. He turned the volume down until the hiss became silence, and told himself, as he told himself every night, that silence was a kind of mercy. That the village was already gone. That the truth would only burn people who were still standing.

The lie fit well. It had fit for fourteen months. He buttoned his coat, killed the light, and stepped out into the dark where the mountain waited, and behind him on the desk the transmitter log glowed faintly on the standby screen — forty-one minutes, one auxiliary access, one stolen beacon — the first entry in a ledger he was beginning to suspect he would not be able to balance.
