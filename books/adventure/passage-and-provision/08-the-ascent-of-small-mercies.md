## 8. The Ascent of Small Mercies

The foothills began where the river gave up pretending to be gentle. Coral felt the change in her calves before she saw it in the terrain — the soft alluvial mud hardening into scree, the water braiding itself into cold white threads that argued their way downhill. Behind them, the delta flattened into a green haze, and somewhere in that haze was a convoy, a handler, and a version of herself she had decided, sometime in the dark hours before dawn, to stop being.

Tomas walked ahead. He walked the way he did everything — without wasted motion, without looking back, as if the mountain had already agreed to let him pass and he was simply honoring the appointment. His pack rode high and tight on his shoulders. Hers sat lower, heavier with the transmitter wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, a hard rectangular heartbeat between her shoulder blades.

She had not told him what it was. She had not told him much of anything.

They climbed through the morning in a silence that was not companionable but was at least honest — two people conserving breath for altitude, each privately auditing the other. Coral catalogued him the way she catalogued everything: guide's boots resoled twice, jacket too good for a man who claimed to be broke, hands that checked terrain with a walking stick he clearly didn't need. A man performing poverty the way she performed obedience.

He, no doubt, was doing the same arithmetic on her. Navy posture. Diver's shoulders. The way she'd taken the lead on the river crossing without asking permission.

By midday they had gained the first shelf of the foothills, a terrace of grey stone stitched with moss, and Tomas finally called a halt beside a stream that ran thin and glass-clear over slate. He dropped his pack, worked his way out of its straps with a small grunt he tried to hide, and sat down heavily on a boulder.

"Water first," he said. "Talk after."

"I didn't agree to talk."

"You didn't agree to anything. That's rather the point of you." He unscrewed his canteen, filled it, drank, filled it again, and held it out to her without ceremony. "You're dehydrating. I can see it from here. Pride is heavy, but it doesn't weigh enough to justify dying of thirst on day one."

She took the canteen because refusing would have cost more than accepting. The water tasted of stone and snowmelt. It was the best thing she'd had in weeks, and she hated that she noticed.

* * *

Tomas watched her drink and thought: she opens things.

It wasn't the transmitter — though he'd have bet his last credential she'd cracked it; nobody stole a sealed navy unit out of an evidence locker by accident. It was everything else. The way she'd handled his maps last night, turning them over like they might be lying to her. The way she'd looked at the delta when they left it, not with relief but with something closer to an unpaid bill.

He knew that look. He'd worn it for six years.

"Your father," he said, because the silence had gone on long enough and because he wanted to watch her face when he said it. "Okonkwo. The diver."

Her head came up slowly. "You know the name."

"Everyone who works this river knows the name." He turned his own canteen in his hands. "Emmanuel Okonkwo. Convicted of falsifying salvage manifests. Of taking payment to lose cargo that was never lost." He paused. "Of drowning a village's water supply, if you believe the sentencing statement."

"Do you believe it?"

"No," Tomas said. "That's why I'm telling you I know the name."

Something moved behind her eyes — not gratitude, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a trap being sprung or a door being opened; from where he sat they looked identical.

"My mother," he said, before she could decide which it was, "was a hydrologist. Did you know that? Nobody ever asks about the parents of disgraced guides. They ask about the expedition. Eleven people, one glacier, zero survivors except me." He laughed, short and airless. "My mother taught me to read water. Every map I've ever drawn started with something she showed me. And every inquiry since has used her name to explain how her son could fail so completely. Bad blood. You've heard the phrase."

Coral set the canteen down. "I've heard it."

"They said my father's temper made me reckless. They said it in the hearing, in print, like genetics were evidence." He looked up at the ridgeline, where the Kessel Range had begun to show itself — white shoulders above brown foothills, patient as a creditor. "Six years I've been climbing with that sentence in my pocket. So. Your turn, Okonkwo. Why does a navy diver with fourteen months left on a clean, quiet debt steal a beacon and run uphill with a man she met three days ago?"

* * *

Because the wreck was scuttled, Coral almost said. Because the hull plates were blown outward, not in. Because there was a second signal under the distress call, repeating on a frequency no survey vessel has any business carrying, and when I opened the case — which I was ordered never to do — I found a beacon log that someone had tried to burn out with an electromagnetic pulse and failed.

Because I think my father took the fall for whatever killed that ship.

She said none of it. What she said, staring into the middle distance where the range stood waiting, was: "I opened it."

Tomas went very still. Guides learned stillness the way divers learned breath control; she recognized the discipline even as she registered the effect.

"The transmitter," she said. "Ordered sealed. Ordered returned unopened, logged, buried. Fourteen months I've done exactly what they asked, exactly how they asked, invisible, punctual, grateful. And I opened the case on the deck of a navy tender with a screwdriver and my heart going like a hammer drill." She heard her own voice flatten into the professional register she used underwater, and let it. "There's a signal underneath the distress call. Old. Repeating. Someone tried to kill it and couldn't. And the coordinates it's broadcasting aren't survey coordinates. They're past the Kessel Range, into country your charts don't cover."

"And instead of reporting it, you copied it and came to find me."

"You were the only guide within two hundred kilometers who owed the navy nothing and the mountains everything."

"That's a compliment," Tomas said, "or an indictment. With you it's hard to tell." But he was already pulling out his maps, spreading them on the stone, weighting the corners with rocks, and she understood that the decision had been made somewhere below language, in the part of both of them that had been waiting six years and fourteen months respectively for permission.

"Here." His finger traced the river valley up into the foothills. "If we make the high shelf by tomorrow night, we can be at the calving face in four days. After that—" he tapped the glacier's snout on the map, a blue-white wedge like a broken tooth "—the pass seals. Eight months, maybe nine. Whatever's broadcasting past that ice will either be found in the next four days or not at all."

"Not at all," Coral said, "suits some people fine."

"Yes," Tomas said quietly. "It does. Which is why we move now."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and the mountain began its slow interrogation.

It asked questions in the grammar of gradient and footing. It asked whether Coral could keep pace with a man who read slope the way she read current. It asked whether Tomas could trust his life to a woman whose institution had taken eleven people off his glacier and called it weather. Neither of them answered out loud. The answers accumulated in the small transactions of the trail — her hand finding his forearm at a slick crossing without either of them remarking on it; his stopping, unasked, when her breathing went ragged at four thousand meters, and pretending to study the skyline while she recovered.

Near dusk they reached a col between two shoulders of the foothills, and the wind found them, and Tomas called the camp in the lee of a rock fin with the economy of a man who had done it ten thousand times. While he rigged the shelter, Coral melted snow and rewrapped the transmitter, checking its casing for cracks with a diver's fingers, blind-fast, thorough.

"You check it like it's alive," Tomas observed.

"It is, sort of. It's listening." She sealed the oilcloth. "Everything up here is listening. That's the problem with mountains. No privacy."

"Then we should probably stop having secrets." He fed the stove a tablet of fuel and watched the blue flame catch. "Yours cost you a career's worth of obedience. Mine cost eleven lives. Between us we could fill a library."

The flame hissed. Above the rock fin, the first stars were coming out over the range, hard and white and indifferent.

"When I was nineteen," Tomas said, to the fire more than to her, "I led a scientific party onto the Kessel icefield. My call, my route, my timetable. We had a window of five days and I spent two of them waiting on weather that never properly arrived, because the client's funding depended on the summit samples and I wanted the contract renewed." His voice stayed level, recitation-flat, the voice of a man who had given testimony so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones. "On day four the temperature dropped and the cornices loaded. I pushed anyway. One hour later the slope above camp let go."

Coral said nothing. There was nothing to say that the mountain hadn't already said better.

"Eleven people," Tomas said. "I was roped to the meteorologist, a woman named Ferro, and when the slide hit, the rope held, and that's the only reason there was anyone to pull out of the debris. The inquiry asked me one question I've never stopped answering: *did you consider turning back?* And the truth is yes. I considered it at breakfast. I considered it at the col. I considered it standing on the slope with the snow ticking down around us like static. And every time, I calculated the cost of turning back and found it higher than the cost of going on." He looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were steady and terrible. "That's the whole crime, Okonkwo. Not cowardice. Arithmetic."

Coral fed another fuel tablet to the stove. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and understood it as a kind of inheritance — her father's hands, steady on the salvage deck while the marshals read the verdict.

"My father signed manifests for cargo that was 'lost,'" she said. "That's what they convicted him of. Taking bribes to misfile wrecks so certain shipments could vanish. Fourteen years. He'll serve eleven more." She paused. "I was twelve when they arrested him. I remember the cuffs. I remember thinking they were the wrong size for his wrists — too big. Everything about the trial was the wrong size. The charges were too large for the man. The evidence was too neat. The other divers who testified for him changed their stories mid-hearing and nobody asked why." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "I joined the navy salvage corps at seventeen because they offered to apply my wages against his restitution. Fourteen months ago they told me one more job and the ledger closes. Then the job became a wreck nobody names, in a delta the navy denies operating in, carrying a box I'm ordered never to open."

"And you opened it."

"And I opened it." She looked at him across the small blue flame. "You want to hear the worst part? When I cracked the case and saw the second signal — old, burned-at, surviving — my first feeling wasn't fear. It was *relief*. Like a knot I'd been carrying since I was twelve finally showing me its shape. Because if that ship was scuttled, if that signal was hidden, then maybe the manifests weren't lies. Maybe they were corrections. Maybe my father filed those wrecks honestly and someone needed them filed dishonestly, and the difference between those two words is the whole architecture of my life."

The wind moved around the rock fin, testing the shelter, finding it adequate.

"So we're the same," Tomas said.

"We're nothing alike."

"We're exactly alike." He said it without heat, the way he stated elevations. "We both did the arithmetic. You kept your head down for fourteen months because the cost of looking up was your father's freedom. I pushed onto a corniced slope because the cost of turning back was my contract. And now we've both run the numbers again and come up with a different answer, and the answer is this mountain, in this season, with these odds." He gestured upward, at the range going blue-black against the stars. "The only question left is whether we've gotten better at arithmetic, or just more desperate."

"Both," Coral said. "Definitely both."

And then — she surprised herself — she laughed. It was a small sound, quickly strangled by the altitude and the wind, but it was real, and Tomas caught it and held it a moment like something rescued from a fire.

* * *

They slept in shifts, as climbers do, and in the dead hours Coral took the watch and let the mountain talk to her.

It talked in wind and settling stone. It talked in the far-off groan of ice, a sound she felt in her sternum before she heard it with her ears — the range clearing its throat, the glaciers shifting in their sleep. Somewhere beyond those white shoulders, a signal older than her debt was repeating itself into the dark, patient as her father in his cell, and she had four days to reach it.

Behind her, Tomas breathed in the rhythm of exhausted sleep. She found herself memorizing it — the small human metronome of it — and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she had crossed some line in the last two days that maps did not mark. Not trust. Not yet. But the place where trust becomes possible: the shared arithmetic, the matched guilt, the discovery that another person's disgrace fits against yours like two halves of a torn document.

At first light they struck camp. Tomas flexed his right hand inside his glove — she saw him do it, saw him hide the wince, saw him fail to hide it from her — and said nothing, and she said nothing, and they climbed.

By noon the foothills fell away behind them and the true range stood up in front of them, and Coral stopped on the last shelf of brown earth and looked at the white wall of the Kessels rising into a sky that had begun, very quietly, to change.

Clouds were building in the west. High ones. Thin ones. The kind that meant nothing today and everything in three days.

Tomas followed her gaze. "Weather window," he said. "We'll want to be across the ice before that closes."

"How long do we have?"

"If it behaves? Five days." He rolled his shoulder under the pack straps. "If it doesn't behave—"

"Then we find out what kind of arithmetic we are," Coral said, and stepped off the shelf, onto the snow, toward the sound of the groaning ice.
