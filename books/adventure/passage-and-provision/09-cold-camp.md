## 9. Cold Camp

The storm came down on them like a verdict.

One hour they were picking their way through a saddle of grey scree, the Kessel Range shouldering up ahead in walls of black rock and dirty ice; the next, the sky closed and the wind arrived with a sound Coral had only ever heard underwater — that deep, freighted roar of pressure moving through a space too small for it. Tomas shouted something she couldn't parse over the noise, pointed left, and then they were both stumbling toward a dark seam in the cliff face as the first snow hit sideways, hard as gravel.

The seam widened into a mouth, the mouth into a chamber. Not comfortable. Not dry at the back, where meltwater had carved a channel through the stone. But out of the wind, which by then was the only category of comfort that existed.

Coral got the tent fly stretched across the entrance while Tomas hammered the last pegs with his good hand, and then the world outside simply ceased. No range, no sky, no horizon. Just white noise and the drumming of her own pulse, and the two of them breathing fog into a space the size of a navy bunkroom.

"Fuel check," she said, because someone had to say it first.

Tomas held up his canister and shook it. The slosh inside was thin, apologetic. "Half. Maybe less."

"Then we cook once a day. Melt snow in shifts, drink warm, eat cold."

"You've done this before."

"I've done worse." She said it without drama, the way you'd report a depth reading. "Three days trapped in a flooded ballast tank off the *Corvus*, waiting for a cutting crew. You learn to ration your panic before you ration your air."

He looked at her across the dimness — really looked, the way he'd been circling around doing since the delta — and then nodded and started unpacking the stove without another word. That was one of the things she was beginning to trust about him: he didn't waste breath on sympathy. Sympathy was a luxury fuel, and they were both running low.

* * *

By the second day the storm had settled in like a tenant who intended to stay through winter.

Coral kept time by tasks. Snow melted and decanted into the water bottles. Boots dried, rotated, dried again. The rope coiled and hung from a stone nub so it wouldn't freeze in a heap. She inventoried their food twice a day and wrote the numbers on the inside of her wrist in grease pencil, a habit from diving when her slate might be lost — the ledger of what remained, kept where she couldn't lie to herself about it.

Tomas's right hand was the number that worried her.

It had gone bad somewhere above the tree line, before the storm — three fingers stiff and bloodless, the color of old candle wax. He'd been hiding it, tucking it into his armpit whenever he thought she wasn't looking, flexing it in little secret rituals that fooled no one. Now, in the cave's permanent dusk, there was nowhere to hide it.

"Take the glove off," she said that morning. Not a question.

"It's fine."

"Tomas."

He exhaled through his nose, the way a man does when he's decided a fight isn't worth the calories, and peeled the glove back. The fingers had gone from wax to mottled purple at the tips. When she touched them, they didn't feel like parts of a person. They felt like cargo.

"We need to warm them properly," she said. "Slow. Not against skin — you'll trade frostbite for burns. Warm water if we can spare the fuel. Otherwise body heat through cloth, and movement, every hour, whether it hurts or not."

"Especially when it hurts."

"Especially then." She met his eyes. "I've watched men lose hands to cold because they stopped arguing with it. The pain is the negotiation. As long as it hurts, you're still talking."

Something moved behind his face — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "You always talk like that? Like you're reading off a checklist?"

"Checklists keep people alive." She was already unwinding her spare sock, wrapping his hand with practiced, impersonal efficiency. "My father taught me that. Everything is a procedure until it isn't, and then it's too late to wish you'd had one."

"Your father." He let her work. "The diver they convicted."

"The diver they convicted." She kept her voice level, kept winding. "Smuggling. Moving contraband on salvage runs out of the delta. That's the official story. I was eleven when they took him, fourteen when the debt transferred to me. Navy arithmetic: his sentence became my service."

"And you believed the story?"

She paused, one loop short of finished. Outside, the wind found some new register, a shriek that seemed to come from inside the stone itself.

"I believed that believing it was safer," she said finally. "There's a difference. I'm only now learning how wide it is."

* * *

That night the temperature dropped hard enough that the condensation on the cave walls froze into a crust that glittered when the candle flame moved. They lay in their bags side by side, close enough that their shoulders touched through the nylon, because shared warmth was arithmetic now, not intimacy.

Except that it was becoming intimacy anyway, in the way that deprivation distills things.

"Tell me about the expedition," Coral said into the dark. "The one you lost."

A long silence. Long enough that she thought he'd refuse. Then, quietly: "Fourteen people went up the Vireo headwall with me as lead guide. A client-funded survey, same as this one, different mountains. Weather window looked clean. It wasn't. I called it wrong. We turned back late, and the mountain took five of them on the descent." His voice stayed flat, but she could hear the cost of keeping it that way, the effort under the words like current under ice. "Board of inquiry stripped my certification. Said I'd pushed past the margin. Maybe I did. Maybe the margin was thinner than anyone knew. I've spent nine years not knowing which."

"Do you still dream about it?"

"Every night. Same dream. I'm counting heads and the count keeps coming up nine instead of fourteen, and I wake up counting." A breath. "You?"

"I dream about the tank. The *Corvus*. Except in the dream the cutting crew never comes, and I have to decide how long to keep breathing slow." She stared at the frozen glitter overhead. "I always wake up before I decide. I think that's what the navy liked about me. I'll hold my breath indefinitely as long as nobody makes me choose."

"And now?"

"Now I deserted my handler in a river camp and I'm crossing a mountain range with a disgraced guide to find people the navy says don't exist." She felt rather than saw him turn toward her. "So apparently somebody made me choose."

His laugh was small and real, a single note in all that roaring dark. Then his hand — the bad one, clumsy even through the wrappings — found hers and settled there, not gripping, just resting. An anchor light, not an anchor chain.

"Coral Okonkwo," he said. "When we get down off this thing—"

"Don't." Gentle. Firm. "Don't make promises the mountain can hear. It takes those personally."

But she didn't move her hand either.

* * *

On the third day the fuel ran below the line she'd penciled on her wrist, and Coral made the call she'd been rehearsing since the first night: one hot meal every other day, snow-melt cold, bodies huddled to conserve everything.

Tomas's hand worsened anyway.

The purple crept past the knuckles. The pain he'd been negotiating with began to fade in two of the fingers, and that frightened her more than any scream would have, because she knew what silence meant in a body the way she knew what silence meant in a radio. Dead air wasn't peace. Dead air was a system failing quietly.

"We get below the tree line," she told him, wrapping the hand again, working warmth into it through the cloth with both of hers, "and we find fuel, and we get you to a doctor, and you keep the hand. That's the plan. Say it back."

"That's the plan," he echoed, and his eyes held hers a moment longer than the words required, and she understood that he was saying something else entirely, and that she was answering it without speaking, and that neither of them could afford to say any of it aloud yet.

Survival tasks filled the hours. She learned the geography of his silences — which ones meant pain, which ones meant memory, which ones meant he was watching her work and finding something in it worth watching. He learned hers. Once, melting snow over the last blue flame of the day, he said, "You hum when you're calculating. Did you know that?" She hadn't. She filed it away next to the grease-pencil numbers, part of the new inventory of herself she seemed to be taking whether she'd planned to or not.

Near midnight, the storm changed its mind.

Not ended — Coral had lived through enough weather to know a lull from a mercy. But the roar dropped to a mutter, and through the gap in the fly came a sight that stopped her breath: stars. Hard, high, indifferent stars over a world remade in white, the Kessel Range suddenly visible as a wall of moonlit glass, and above it, far up the glacier line, a faint green shimmer that might have been aurora or might have been her own eyes failing.

Tomas struggled upright beside her, following her gaze. His face, frost-nipped and exhausted, lit anyway.

"That's the pass," he said hoarsely. "The col above the icefall. If the sky holds—"

"If the sky holds, we move at first light. Before it changes its mind again." She was already reaching for the grease pencil, updating the ledger on her wrist: fuel, food, hours of dark remaining. "Sleep. Both of us. Real sleep."

He caught her wrist gently before she could settle back down — the good hand, careful of the bad one — and turned it over, studying the columns of numbers written there like a fortune-teller reading lines.

"What does it say?" he asked. "All that arithmetic. What's the bottom line?"

Coral looked at the numbers, then at him, then out at the impossible stars.

"It says we're going to make it," she said. "Barely. Which is the only way anything worth making has ever been made."

And far to the south, though neither of them could know it yet, lanterns were moving through the foothills — a recovery team climbing steadily toward the pass, led by a man with a ledger of his own, whose bottom line had their names written in it.
