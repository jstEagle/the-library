## 10. The Pass Opens

The mountain told them first.

Tomas heard it before dawn, a sound like a door slamming somewhere deep in the earth, and he was out of the sleeping bag with his boots half-laced before Coral even stirred. He stood at the mouth of the ledge camp they'd cut into the windward slope and watched the glacier across the valley do something no guide wants to see and every guide prays for: it was calving. Early. Weeks early.

Seracs the size of chapel towers sheared off the icefall and went down in slow, thunderous ruin, throwing up a plume of spindrift that caught the first grey light like smoke from a burning city. The sound rolled up the valley and came back changed, doubled, hungry.

"Coral." His voice cracked on the second word. "Coral, wake up. It's opening."

She was beside him in seconds, hair frozen stiff at the temples, eyes going straight to the ice the way his never could — reading it like water, like depth, like something you had to survive by understanding rather than outrunning. He'd learned in nine days on this mountain that Coral Okonkwo did not panic. She assessed. It was the most frightening thing about her.

"That's not an opening," she said. "That's a collapse."

"It's both." He pointed with his good hand — the left one stayed wrapped now, three fingers gone numb past saving, a debt the cold had already begun collecting. "See the shelf below the serac line? When the face calves that hard, it unloads the pressure on the lower tongue. The bridge over the crevasse field — the one that's been holding all season — it'll flex. Open. Maybe stay open for days before the next movement seals it or drops it into the dark."

"Maybe."

"Maybe," he agreed. "That's mountaineering. Everything worth crossing is a maybe."

She looked at him then, and in the flat pre-dawn light he saw what he'd been seeing more of each day: not the navy diver, not the debtor, not the invisible woman who moved through checkpoints like smoke. Just Coral, tired and scared and ferociously alive, deciding.

"How long to reach it?"

"Six hours if the weather holds. Eight if it doesn't. And after that—" he nodded at the glacier, still groaning, still shedding itself into the valley, "—after that we take whatever the ice gives us and we don't ask twice."

She started breaking camp without another word. That, too, he'd learned: she didn't waste breath on decisions already made.

* * *

They climbed through the morning in a world stripped to three colors — white ice, blue shadow, the black of exposed rock where the wind had scoured everything else away. Tomas led when the route needed reading; Coral carried most of the weight because his hand couldn't be trusted with a rope under load, and neither of them said this out loud because saying it would make it true in a way that slowed them down.

By midday the wind rose. By early afternoon it was a living thing, shoving them sideways across the nevé, and the temperature dropped fast enough that the sweat from the morning climb turned to frost inside his shell jacket. He called a halt behind a serac the size of a cathedral pillar and checked the sky with the flat, professional dread of a man who has buried weather judgment before.

"We go now or we don't go for two days," he shouted over it. "And in two days the bridge could be gone."

"Then we go now." Coral was already re-racking the rope. "How bad is the crossing?"

He told her the truth, because lying to her had stopped being possible somewhere around the fourth day, somewhere between her story of her father's trial and his own confession about the expedition he'd lost — six names, one winter, a decision he'd made at a whiteout pass not so different from this one. "Bad. Two hundred meters of ice bridge over a crevasse field that's been flexing since last night. If it holds, twenty minutes. If it doesn't—"

"I know how bridges fail," she said. "I've dived wrecks. Structures tell you before they go. You just have to listen."

"Then listen with me."

They roped together at twelve meters — shorter than he'd have liked, longer than safety strictly allowed with two people and this much trust. She took point without being asked, which surprised him until he understood: she wanted to feel the ice give its warnings first. A diver's instinct. Read the structure. Trust the tremor before the crack.

The bridge announced itself as a pale seam across the crevasse field, a snow-covered spine sagging gently at its center like the back of an old animal. Beyond it, the far wall of the range dropped away toward the eastern valleys — green-black forest, a thread of river, the whole descent that meant survival. Behind them, the way they'd come was already erasing itself in cloud.

Coral stepped onto it.

Tomas felt the bridge answer through his boots — a deep, elastic shudder, the whole span flexing like a held breath released. Snow dusted off its flanks in little ghosts. His stomach dropped.

"Keep moving," he said. "Slow steps. Even rhythm. Don't stop, don't jump, don't—"

The glacier spoke.

Not a groan this time. A report, sharp as artillery, somewhere beneath their feet, and the bridge dipped — actually dipped, a full handspan, snow sloughing off both edges in long ribbons — and Coral went down to one knee and kept moving, crawling now, distributing her weight, and Tomas understood she wasn't freezing. She was listening. Her palm flat against the ice, head tilted, reading the structure the way she'd read hull plates in black water.

"It's shifting under us," she called back. "But it's not letting go. Not yet. Move!"

He moved. Twenty meters of the worst walking of his life, the span swaying beneath them like a deck in swell, crevasses gaping on either side through the thinning snow cover — blue-black slots going down further than light cared to follow. His frostbitten hand screamed inside its wrapping. He let it scream. Pain meant blood still reaching it.

Halfway across, the far end of the bridge began to calve.

A section the size of a truck peeled off the anchor point and swung down into the crevasse with a sound like the world's largest door closing. The remaining span lurched, tilted, and for three full seconds Tomas was certain — certain the way he'd been certain once before, on another pass, in another lifetime — that the mountain was going to take its price from him again.

Then Coral was up, running the last thirty meters in a flat sprint no rational person attempts on flexing ice, and the rope hauled him after her, and they came off the bridge onto solid ground at the far anchor as the entire span behind them sighed, settled, and dropped ten centimeters — still standing, but dying now, visibly, the way a wreck dies on a reef.

Coral knelt at the edge, breathing hard, and looked back at it.

"Told you," she said. "Structures tell you before they go."

Tomas sat down hard in the snow and laughed — a raw, disbelieving sound he hadn't made in years, laughter torn straight out of the fear center. "You're insane. You're completely, clinically insane."

"You followed me."

"Somebody has to."

She pulled him up, and for a moment they stood there on the far side of the Kessel Range, alive, having crossed the thing that had killed better climbers than either of them, and the exhilaration hit him like altitude — dizzying, stupid, enormous. Below them the eastern valley opened in long green folds, the river catching afternoon light, the descent real and reachable. They'd beaten the calving season. They'd beaten the mountain.

That was when he saw them.

* * *

They were dots at first. Far below on the eastern approach — the long moraine trail that switchbacked up from the valley toward the pass they'd just crossed — a column of figures moving with the unhurried discipline of people who knew exactly where they were going and had equipment to spare.

Tomas counted eight. Maybe nine. Roped teams, sledges, the glint of optics catching sun.

Nobody climbed the Kessel Range's eastern approach in calving season. Nobody crossed it at all unless they had to, and nobody traveled in disciplined columns with sledges unless someone was paying them well and pointing them at something specific.

"Coral," he said quietly.

She was already looking. He watched her go very still in the way he'd learned meant her mind was working faster than her face would ever show.

"That's not a survey team," she said. "Survey teams argue. They stop. They complain about the food." A pause. "That's a recovery unit."

"You recognize them?"

"No. But I recognize the formation." Her voice had gone flat and careful. "Four-point diamond, sledge in the center, flankers high. That's navy mountain doctrine. That's how Vance moves."

The name landed in Tomas's chest like a dropped anchor. Vance. He'd heard it only once, whispered across a cold campfire — her handler, her leash, the man who owned her debt and wanted the transmitter buried along with whatever the lost expedition had found. Nine days he'd let himself believe they'd outrun the past, that the mountains were wide enough to lose a lieutenant in.

"They can't have crossed yet," he said. "The western approaches are worse than ours. They'd need—"

"They don't need to cross." Coral's voice was very quiet. "They were already here. Or they came around through the southern col while we were fighting the storm. Vance doesn't chase things, Tomas. He intercepts them. He knew where the pass opens. He's known the whole time."

Below them, one of the dots stopped. Raised something to its face — binoculars, a scope, glass catching the low sun in a single bright flash, unmistakable at this distance, deliberate as a wave.

"They've seen us," Tomas said.

"Yes."

"And the bridge is dying. We can't go back."

"No," Coral agreed. She shouldered her pack, and Tomas saw her check the strap on the waterproof case that rode against her spine — the copied data, the coordinates, the evidence that had cost them everything to carry this far. "We can only go down. Straight down, fast, into the tree line before dark."

"And if they're faster?"

She looked at him, and there was no fear in her face — only that diver's calm, the calm of a woman who had spent years in places where panic meant death and invisibility meant survival, and who had finally, finally decided to be seen.

"Then they'll learn what I learned in the delta," she said. "Water finds a way down. So do I. Come on — we've got maybe four hours of light, and I intend to spend all of them descending."

They started down into the green, two small figures on an enormous slope, and behind them the ice bridge groaned once more in the failing light — still standing, barely, a door left open just long enough for pursuit to walk through.
