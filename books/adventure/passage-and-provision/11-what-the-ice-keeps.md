## 11. What the Ice Keeps

Amara Diallo had learned to measure time in small units. Not days — days were too large, days invited despair. She measured in the burn of a single fuel tablet, in the forty minutes her body could hold the cold before shivering turned dangerous, in the slow crawl of the sun across the notch in the eastern ridge that she could see through the torn flap of the supply tent.

The notch had filled with sun eleven times since the others left.

She had counted. Counting was a discipline, and discipline was the last thing the mountain hadn't taken from her.

Now she crouched in the shadow of the overturned comms sled, a wrench in her right hand — not because she could fight anyone with it, but because her hands needed a task or they would shake, and shaking made noise, and noise, for eleven days, had meant death. Below the camp, on the glacier's lateral moraine, two figures were picking their way down through the rockfall. Not the navy team — she knew the navy team's silhouette by heart, the boxy packs, the deliberate spacing, the way they moved like men following a grid. These two moved like people who were lost and refusing to admit it.

A woman in the lead, moving with a strange, low economy, testing every foothold the way a swimmer tests ice. Behind her, a broad-shouldered man favoring his left hand, wrapped and clumsy.

Amara watched them for a full hour before she let herself hope.

Hope was a resource. She rationed it like fuel.

* * *

They came up the moraine as the light went amber. Amara had already made her decision — she'd made it three days ago, actually, the day she'd eaten the last of the survey rations and understood that waiting for rescue and waiting for the navy to come back and finish the job were the same act of patience — but her body resisted it anyway. Every instinct she'd built in eleven days of hiding said *stay down, stay small, stay nothing*.

She stood up into the open instead.

The woman saw her first. She didn't shout, didn't reach for a weapon — she went still, utterly still, the way water goes still, and Amara understood in one glance that this was a person who had spent a long time being unseen and had recently decided to stop.

"You're not navy," Amara said. Her voice came out cracked, a hinge unused. She tried again. "If you were navy, you'd have called out. They always call out. They want you to know they're coming."

The man lowered himself onto a boulder, breathing hard, his wrapped hand held against his chest like something borrowed and broken. "And who," he said, "are you, if you're not navy?"

"Amara Diallo. Structural engineer, Meridian Survey Expedition." She heard how it sounded — the recitation of a woman who had been rehearsing her own identity to keep it from dissolving. "Eleven days. Alone. The rest of my team went down the far valley with the navy escort nine days ago and I have seen nothing since except their smoke on the horizon, and I have been in this camp because leaving meant dying and staying meant—" She stopped. Made herself breathe. "You're not navy. So who are you?"

The woman came closer, and up close Amara saw the details the distance had hidden: salt-stiff hair, a diver's forearms, eyes the color of deep water under cloud. There was a transmitter case strapped to her pack — a navy case, olive drab, stenciled — and Amara's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the altitude.

She knew that case. She'd helped calibrate its sibling.

"That's a Vex-7 recovery unit," Amara said quietly. "That's a *black box*. Those don't leave navy custody. Those don't even officially exist."

The woman didn't flinch. "Coral Okonkwo," she said. "And you're right. It doesn't. I stole it."

* * *

They gave her water first, then a ration bar broken into thirds, and Amara ate the way she imagined animals ate — without dignity, without stopping, her body overriding every scrap of the engineer's discipline she'd clung to for eleven days. Tomas Reyes — the man with the hand, a guide, cashiered, a story Amara filed away to ask about later — kept watch on the moraine while she ate, and Coral Okonkwo sat across from her on an overturned crate and watched her with the patience of someone who understood starvation's etiquette: don't talk to the starving, just guard them.

When Amara could finally speak, she pointed at the comms sled. "You want to know why there's a survey camp full of looted equipment and no survey team. Ask."

"I was going to," Coral said.

"Then ask the right question. Not *what happened*. Ask *why the navy was here at all*. This is a hydrographic survey. River sources, glacier meltwater, sediment loads. There is nothing up here worth a naval escort and a military-grade recovery unit. I filed that objection in writing before we deployed. I was told the escort was 'standard force protection.'" She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "Standard. We had more armed personnel than scientists."

Coral had gone very still. "The wreck," she said. "In the delta. The survey vessel. *Meridian Sound*. I dove it. It was scuttled — seacocks opened, engine room flooded from the inside. I was told it sank in a storm."

"It didn't sink in a storm." Amara set down the ration wrapper and smoothed it flat with two fingers, an engineer's habit, giving her hands a problem. "I was aboard her when she went down. There was no storm. There was an order."

The wind came down off the icefield and moved through the camp, flipping the torn tent flap with a sound like a slow clap. Tomas looked back at them once, then resumed his watch.

"Tell me from the beginning," Coral said.

"The beginning is the river." Amara's voice steadied as it became technical; she could feel the engineer in her taking over, and she let it. "Our brief was sediment chemistry. Trace metals, pH, downstream gradient. Boring science. Except the numbers came back wrong from the first station. Arsenic at forty times background. Mercury. Compounds I couldn't identify — industrial solvents, something with a fluorine signature. And the gradient was wrong. Contamination *decreased* downstream, which is backwards. It meant the source wasn't upstream of the delta. It meant the source was *in* the delta, and being masked."

Coral's face didn't change, but something behind it did. Amara watched it happen — watched a woman receive information she had already, in some buried chamber of herself, been carrying for years.

"There's a mining operation in the delta," Coral said. Not a question.

"Upstream of the village. Unlicensed, unlisted, buried under three shell companies I traced before the navy confiscated my files." Amara's hands had stopped moving. "The village. You've seen it. It's not on the charts."

"I've seen it."

"The aquifer under that village is the river's floodplain reservoir. Everything the mine dumps goes into the ground, and the ground gives it to the wells, and the wells give it to the people. Our samples from the village intake —" Amara stopped, because the next part wasn't technical anymore, and she made herself say it anyway, plainly, the way you report a structural failure. "We tested the water the village drinks. I ran the panel three times because I didn't believe it. The children there are being poisoned. Slowly, invisibly, at concentrations that won't kill them this year. It will kill them over ten. And it will be recorded, if anyone ever records it, as bad luck. Weak constitutions. A village that was always sickly."

Coral said nothing. Her stillness had become total, the stillness of deep water, and Amara recognized it now for what it was: not calm. Pressure.

"The navy found out what we'd found," Amara continued, "because our data relayed through a naval comms node — that was part of the 'force protection' arrangement. Within a week we had new orders. Cease sampling. Return all cores and reagents to the escort. Prepare for extraction. And then, on the last night, the captain was summoned aboard the escort vessel, and when he came back he was gray, and he told us the survey was concluded, and that we'd be leaving on the navy's transport, and that our data would be 'processed through official channels.' I'd already copied everything. Every sample, every spectrum, every core log. Two drives. I kept them on my body."

"And the ship?"

"They told us to take the transport. The captain refused — he was a stubborn man, he said the *Meridian Sound* was his vessel and he'd sail her home himself. That night the navy insisted on putting an 'engineering assistance team' aboard to help with the river crossing. Six men. I watched them go into the engine room." Amara looked at her own hands. "I was in the chart room at 0300 when I heard the seacocks. You know the sound? No. Of course you don't. It's a sound like a ship deciding to die — a long groan of water coming in where water should never be. I got topside. The escort's boats were already in the water. They weren't there to rescue us. They were there to make sure no one else did. The captain went down with her. He went down *on purpose* — I saw him at the rail, and he looked at me, and he shook his head, once, and he stepped back inside. He knew what it meant. If the ship sank with all hands, it was a tragedy. If the ship sank with survivors, there would be questions. He chose the tragedy."

The wind again. Somewhere up the moraine, loose rock ticked down the ice.

"I went into the water," Amara said. "I had the drives in a dry bag against my chest. I swam for the bank. I've been walking toward this camp and away from the navy for eleven days, and every hour I expected to hear shots, and I never did, and I finally understood why. They don't need to shoot me. They just need the mountain to keep me. A dead engineer raises questions. A lost engineer is a line item written off." She looked up at Coral. "Except they sent a recovery unit for the transmitter. Didn't they. Because the transmitter recorded everything — the seacocks, the order, the captain's last transmissions. The one piece of evidence they couldn't let drift in the current."

Coral's hand had come to rest on the case at her pack, unconsciously, the way you touch a wound.

"I opened it," she said. "Three days ago. There's a distress signal on it. Live. Repeating. An expedition beyond the Kessel Range."

Amara stared at her. "That's not possible. The Vex-7's distress channel is hard-coded to naval frequencies. Unless—" She stopped, and Amara watched the engineer in her do what engineers do, which is follow a fault to its root. "Unless someone reconfigured it aboard the *Meridian Sound*. The captain. He was old navy, he knew the hardware better than the men they sent to kill him. If he had minutes in the chart room before the flooding reached the electronics — he could have re-routed the emergency beacon. He could have made the ship's death cry on a channel the navy wasn't listening to." Her voice went strange. "He sent out a distress call from a scuttled ship, on a hidden frequency, and it's still repeating. He's been calling for help from the bottom of the delta for weeks, and the only person who ever heard him is standing in front of me holding his voice in a box."

Coral stood up. She walked to the edge of the camp, to where the moraine dropped away toward the darkening valley, and she stood there with her back to them, and Amara let her stand, because there are moments when a person needs to be a landscape instead of a face.

* * *

Tomas came and crouched beside Amara at the cold fire ring, and for a while neither of them spoke. He fed a fuel tablet to the little stove, and the blue flame made their shadows lean together on the tent fabric.

"Your hand," Amara said.

"Is a problem for a warmer month." He flexed the wrapped fingers, winced. "Your captain. The one who went down with the ship. What was his name?"

"Okonkwo." Amara said it, and then heard it, and looked up sharply at the woman standing at the edge of the camp with her back turned. "Okonkwo. That's — the diver. The salvage diver they convicted. The one they said scuttled a navy vessel for insurance. That was her father."

"Yes," Tomas said quietly.

Amara looked at the drives in their dry bag, at the transmitter case on Coral's pack, at the woman standing against the last light with the whole poisoned river behind her and her father's name inside a metal box at the bottom of it. Eleven days alone on a glacier had stripped Amara Diallo down to her load-bearing members, and what was left understood load paths, understood how a structure fails, and where the weight has to go.

"She doesn't know yet," Amara said. "Does she. That the man they convicted — that the trial, the debt, all of it — that's the same operation. Her father was a salvage diver in that delta. He was in the water where our samples were. He probably *found* the contamination years before we did. And they needed a diver to blame for a scuttling, and there he was."

"He knows the river," Tomas said simply. "They didn't frame an innocent man. They framed the one man who could have testified."

At the camp's edge, Coral turned. The light was nearly gone, and her face was in shadow, but her voice carried clean across the cold air, the voice of a woman reading a depth gauge in dark water.

"Then we don't go down the far valley," she said. "We go back. Across the pass, down the river, to the village that isn't on the chart. And we put your data in the hands of the people it was taken from." She looked at the transmitter case. "And then we make sure the whole country hears their captain calling from the bottom of the delta."

Amara felt something in her chest that wasn't hope exactly — hope's harder, colder cousin, the thing that keeps you moving when hope is a luxury. She shouldered the dry bag with the drives, and high on the icefield behind them, faint but unmistakable in the still air, came a sound she'd been dreading for eleven days.

Voices. Many. Descending.

Navy voices, calling out to each other in the dark — because they always wanted you to know they were coming.
