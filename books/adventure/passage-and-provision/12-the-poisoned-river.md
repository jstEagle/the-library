## 12. The Poisoned River

Amara Diallo had spent eleven days rationing herself to four hundred calories and telling the mountain it would not beat her. Now, fed on Tomas's trail stew and wrapped in Coral's spare thermal layer, she sat in the lee of a fallen serac with three data drives fanned across her knees like tarot cards, and she understood that the mountain had never been the thing trying to kill her.

The navy had.

"You're sure about this?" Coral asked. She crouched at the edge of the firelight, hands wrapped around a tin mug she wasn't drinking from. "Once I see it, I can't unsee it."

Amara almost laughed. Almost. Her ribs still ached from the fall into the survey camp's supply trench, and laughter cost more than she wanted to spend. "That's the point," she said. "I've been the only one seeing it for five months. It was starting to feel like I'd invented it."

Tomas Reyes sat back on his heels, his bandaged right hand resting in his lap like something borrowed from another man. He said nothing. He was good at that — Amara had noticed it in the first hour of knowing him, the way he let silence do work that other men did with noise. A guide's patience. Or a guilty man's.

She plugged the first drive into the field reader they'd salvaged from the looted camp, and the screen bloomed green in the blue dusk.

"Start with the water," Amara said. "Everyone starts with the water, because everyone thinks the story is about the wreck." She pulled up a spectral analysis chart, its lines sharp as cuts. "This is from the delta. Sampled by our team six weeks before we went dark. Dissolved heavy metals — cadmium, mercury, arsenic species — at concentrations that don't occur naturally anywhere on this continent. Not in this geology. The Kessel Range is granite and old seabed. There is no vein up here that explains what was in that river."

Coral leaned closer. The firelight moved on her face, and Amara watched her read the numbers the way a diver reads gauges — quickly, completely, with the particular stillness of someone who has learned that bad news arrives without warning and must be absorbed before it kills you.

"These are delta samples," Coral said slowly. "Below the village."

"Above and below," Amara said. "That's the elegant part. That's the part that took me two weeks to confirm, because I kept thinking I'd mixed up my labels." She switched screens. Two columns of data, side by side. "Upstream of the village: catastrophic. Downstream: catastrophic but *diluted*. The village sits inside the plume. Every person in that settlement has been drinking mine tailings for years."

The wind came down off the icefield and pressed against their shelter. Somewhere above them, the glacier groaned — a long, tectonic complaint, the sound of something enormous deciding whether to hold or let go.

"What operation?" Tomas asked. His voice was flat. He already knew. Amara could hear it in the flatness — the tone of a man asking a question to which he has known the answer for a long time and has been waiting for someone else to say it out loud.

"The Meridian Consortium," Amara said. "Or that's the name on the paperwork. Shell company, registered through three jurisdictions, dissolved eighteen months ago on paper and very much alive in practice." She advanced to the next file. Survey photographs, timestamped, geo-tagged: an open pit carved into a hanging valley two ridges east of their current position, tailings ponds glowing an unnatural turquoise even in the grey aerial shots, haul roads switchbacking down toward the river's headwaters. "They were pulling rare earths out of a deposit our own geological survey flagged as marginal. Marginal deposits only make money if you don't pay for waste management. So they didn't. Everything went straight into the watershed."

"And the navy?" Coral asked.

Amara looked at her. This was the moment she had rehearsed in the dark of the supply trench, in the weeks of starvation, when keeping the data safe had become the only structure left in her life. She had imagined delivering this information to a naval officer and watching the officer's face close like a door. She had not imagined delivering it to this woman — this diver with frost-nipped cheeks and her father's surname worn like a sentence — and watching her face *open*, horribly, the way a wound opens.

"The navy didn't just know," Amara said. "The navy built the wall around it."

* * *

She walked them through it file by file, because the order mattered, because the architecture of the crime only became visible when you saw how carefully each piece had been laid over the last.

"Our vessel, the *Kestrel Survey*, was chartered under a joint civilian-military hydrographic program. Cover of legitimacy. Our actual tasking — the one that never appeared in any log — was baseline environmental monitoring of the delta and the river corridor. Someone in the Ministry wanted independent numbers on the water. I think now they suspected what Meridian was doing and wanted proof before they acted." She paused. "We got the proof. And then we became a problem."

The second drive held communications logs. Amara had decrypted maybe a third of them before the camp was hit; the rest were locked behind codes she hadn't cracked, but the third was enough. Requisition orders for the *Kestrel*'s scuttling charges, signed with a naval authorization code. A maintenance schedule showing the hull seacocks serviced and sabotaged in the same week. A signal intercept — fragmentary, but legible — ordering the destruction of "environmental materials" aboard and the recovery of "sensitive instrumentation" by dive teams.

"Dive teams," Coral repeated.

"Naval salvage divers," Amara said. "Contracted through the same debt-labor program that—" She stopped. She had read Coral's service file during the long months in the camp, piecing together who might come for them, and she knew exactly whose file she'd memorized.

"Say it," Coral said quietly.

Amara made herself say it. "Okonkwo. Ansel Okonkwo. Salvage diver, second class. Convicted of negligent destruction of naval property and falsified salvage reports in connection with the loss of a survey vessel in the Kessel delta." She met Coral's eyes. "That vessel was the *Kestrel*. Your father was one of the divers sent down to strip her before she sank — sent to remove the evidence. When he refused to sign off on the false manifest, when he reported that the ship had been scuttled from inside, they charged him with the scuttling itself. They needed a culprit who couldn't fight back. A debtor diver with a spotless record was perfect, because his record was the whole argument: men like that don't lie, so if he confessed—"

"He never confessed." Coral's voice was very steady, which was worse than shouting. "He maintained his innocence for nine years. He died maintaining it. They told me he confessed at the end, in the hospital. They showed me a document."

"They forged it," Amara said. "Or they showed you a confession extracted from another diver and put your father's name on it. We recovered fragments of the internal inquiry from the encrypted files. Three divers were approached. Two signed. One didn't." She let that sit. "Your father was the one who didn't."

The fire popped. Above them the glacier spoke again, longer this time, a sound like a door being dragged open somewhere under the world.

Tomas Reyes had gone very still. Amara watched him watch Coral, and saw in his face the same recognition she felt in her own chest — the vertigo of realizing that the person beside you has been living inside the crime you've been mapping, that the map and the territory have just become the same place.

"My debt," Coral said. She set the untouched mug down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something when your hands want to break it. "Nine years of indentured diving. They told me it was the price of his crimes. That I could work it off. That serving honorably would—" She stopped. Started again. "It was engineered. The whole thing. They ruined him, then they inherited *me*. They built a leash out of his disgrace and handed me the collar and called it provision."

"Yes," Amara said. Because there was no kind way to say it, and she had decided, starving in the dark, that if she ever got out she would be done with unkind kindnesses. "And it's better than that, or worse. The debt-labor program isn't incidental to the cover-up. It's load-bearing. Indentured divers can't refuse orders, can't talk to journalists, can't testify without permission. They built a workforce that legally cannot witness. Every diver who touched the *Kestrel* was either complicit or silenced. Your father was the anomaly. The one who thought his signature meant something."

* * *

The third drive was the smallest and the heaviest. Amara saved it for last, and when she slotted it in, she made both of them look at the header first: MEDICAL SURVEY — DELTA SETTLEMENTS — UNMAPPED COMMUNITIES.

"There's a village on the lower river," she said. "You've seen it. It's not on any chart. Officially it doesn't exist, which means officially no one poisoned it." The files opened: blood panels, hair samples, epidemiological tables compiled by the *Kestrel*'s medical officer, a woman named Osei whom Amara had last seen running toward the tree line while the hull charges went off. "Two hundred and forty residents. Elevated cadmium in ninety percent. Mercury neurotoxicity presenting in children under twelve at rates the medical officer described in her notes as, quote, 'consistent with a population drinking from a tailings pond.' Congenital anomalies. Renal failure clusters. And a mortality curve that bends upward starting eight years ago — which is when Meridian's pit went active."

"Eight years," Coral whispered.

"Eight years," Amara confirmed. "The same year your father was convicted. The inquiry into the *Kestrel* wasn't just a cover-up of the scuttling. It was the burial of everything the *Kestrel* found. Kill the messenger, burn the message, and the village stays unmapped and uncounted and conveniently dead."

Tomas spoke for the first time in twenty minutes. "The expedition I lost. Seven years ago. We were guiding a ministry inspection team over the range to the eastern valleys." His voice sounded scraped. "They said it was boundary survey work. I lost three clients and my license in the whiteout on the high col, and I never asked myself hard enough why a boundary survey needed environmental sampling kits." He looked at his bandaged hand. "They weren't checking borders. They were checking whether the contamination had crossed the divide."

Amara nodded slowly. "There's a memo in the third drive referencing a prior field assessment. Redacted author. Dated seven years ago." She didn't say the rest. She didn't have to. She watched the knowledge land on Tomas Reyes like snowload on a weak roof.

For a long time none of them spoke. The fire burned down to a red heart. The glacier above them ticked and settled, ticking and settling, like a clock counting toward something.

* * *

It was Coral who broke the silence, and she broke it by standing up and walking to the edge of the shelter, where the icefield fell away into blue darkness, and standing there with her back to them for a long moment.

Amara started to rise. Tomas caught her eye and shook his head minutely. *Let her.*

When Coral turned around, her face had changed. Amara had seen grief on faces for five months — the survivors' faces in the camp, her own face in the polished steel of a supply tin. This was not grief. Grief is soft. This had been through grief and come out the far side as something with edges.

"I spent nine years being invisible," Coral said. "It's a skill. You learn it young when your father's name is mud — you learn to walk through rooms like you're not in them, to answer questions with less than you know, to keep your head down and your ledger clean and hope the debt shrinks faster than the shame grows." She came back toward the fire, and the light climbed her face. "I thought the debt was weather. Something that happened to us. I built my whole life around enduring it — dove their wrecks, ran their rivers, kept my mouth shut, told myself that if I served well enough they'd eventually hand me back to myself."

She knelt and picked up the three drives, weighing them in her palm.

"But it wasn't weather. It was architecture. Somebody drew it. Somebody signed it. Somebody looked at my father — a man who wouldn't lie about a manifest — and decided he was cheaper broken than honest, and then looked at his daughter and saw free labor and a hostage in one package." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing. It got quieter and more precise, like a tool being ground to a finer edge. "They didn't take my life. They *commissioned* it. I've been working for the people who buried my father this entire time, and calling it penance."

"Coral," Tomas said gently.

"No." She shook her head once, decisively. "Don't soften it. I want it sharp. I'm going to need it sharp." She closed her fist around the drives. "These aren't evidence anymore. Evidence is something you submit. These are—" She searched for the word and Amara watched her find it. "These are the river. All of it, every dead child, every forged confession, every year of my father's silence — compressed onto silicon the size of a thumbnail. And I am going to carry it down off this mountain if I have to swallow it and swim."

"That's why they'll kill for it," Amara said. "Vance isn't chasing deserters. Deserters are an embarrassment. He's chasing the only copy of the thing that ends him."

"Then we give him a reason to hurry," Coral said. She turned to Tomas, and Amara saw the transformation complete itself — the invisible woman becoming the visible one, the diver becoming the leader, in the space of a single decision. "How far to the relay station?"

Tomas blinked. "Two days, if the weather holds. Three if the col ices up. But Coral — the station's transmitter works, which means it can be triangulated. The moment we key it, Vance knows exactly where we are."

"I know." Coral looked east, toward the pass, toward the closing weather, toward the man with the ledger coming up the mountain to collect. The glacier boomed above them, long and final, and far off in the dark a section of the ice shelf let go and fell, the sound rolling down the valley like the first word of a long sentence finally being spoken aloud.

"Good," Coral Okonkwo said. "Let him come. I want him to hear it too."
