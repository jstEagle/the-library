## 13. Three Against the Mountain

They left the survey camp at first light, and the mountain was already closing its hand.

Coral felt it the way she felt pressure changes underwater — not as weather but as weight, a slow compression of the air itself, the sky lowering like a lid. The clouds that had been sitting on the Kessel peaks all night had begun to spill down their flanks in grey tongues, and every hour they climbed, the tongues reached lower.

"Spindrift by noon," Tomas said, not looking back at her. He said it the way he'd been saying things for two days: flat, certain, leaving no room for argument. "Whiteout by dark if we're unlucky."

"And if we're lucky?"

"If we're lucky we're through the col before it starts." He flexed his right hand inside its mitten — she saw him do it, that small compulsive motion, testing fingers that no longer answered reliably — and kept walking. "Luck's a provision you carry, not one you find."

Amara Diallo laughed once, a dry bark with no humor in it, and adjusted the strap of her pack. She'd repacked her data drives into three separate containers, distributed across three bodies, and she'd made them all memorize which container held what. "The engineer carries the redundancy," she'd said. "That's the whole religion of engineering. Assume everything fails, including you."

Coral had assumed she would fail. That was the honest arithmetic of the last two days. Three people, one of them half-starved from six weeks in a looted camp, one of them a guide with a dying hand, and herself — a salvage diver four hundred kilometers from any water deeper than a melt pool, carrying evidence worth more than her life and less useful than a rope.

She checked the rope anyway. Third time that morning. It gave her something to do with her eyes that wasn't looking at Tomas's hand.

* * *

The trouble announced itself an hour before noon, on a slope of blue ice above a frozen waterfall, where the route narrowed to a ledge barely wide enough for one boot.

Tomas went first, as he always went first, cutting steps with the short ice axe held awkwardly in his left hand because his right had stopped cooperating sometime during the night. Coral watched him from below and saw the moment it happened.

He swung. The axe bit. He stepped up on his left foot and brought his right across to follow, and the right mitten caught on a nub of ice, and instead of gripping — instead of that small automatic clench every climber does without thinking — the hand simply lay there inside the wool like something packed away for storage. His balance went sideways. For half a second he was held on the mountain by nothing but the axe and habit.

Then Coral was moving. She didn't remember deciding to move. She was on the ledge, boots in his cut steps, her arm hooked under his pack harness, her shoulder taking his weight against the rock wall behind them, and the whole time her voice was doing something she didn't recognize — low, level, unhurried, the voice she used at forty meters down when a line fouled and the current pushed and panic was a luxury billed at rates nobody could pay.

"I've got you. Left foot up. Just the left. Find my step."

He found it. They stood pressed together on the ledge, breathing, the drop yawning away beneath them into white nothing, and Tomas Reyes looked at his own right hand as if it belonged to a stranger.

"It's been going since the cave," he said quietly. "You knew."

"I suspected."

"You should have said."

"You should have stopped," she said, and heard the edge in her own voice, and didn't take it back. "At the camp. At the moraine. Anywhere. You kept leading because leading is who you are, and your hand kept dying because the cold doesn't care who you are." She unclipped his pack straps one-handed, slid the load off his shoulders onto her own, and stood there with both packs and both their futures stacked along her spine. "So now I lead."

"Coral—"

"The col is northeast. Two ridgelines, then the ice bridge, then down. You told me this yesterday when you still trusted yourself to remember it. I remember it." She met his eyes. They were grey-rimmed, exhausted, furious mostly at himself, and underneath the fury something else — fear, real fear, the kind a man feels when the mountain takes a tool out of his hands. "I've spent nine years following orders I hated. I'm very good at it. Following orders is just navigation with someone else's destination. This time the destination is mine."

Amara, waiting below the ledge, called up: "For what it's worth, I'd follow her. She hasn't lost anyone yet."

"She hasn't led anyone yet," Tomas said.

"No," Coral agreed. "Time to find out which is worse."

* * *

She led the way Tomas would have led, which was to say she led the way he'd taught her without either of them naming it: reading the snow bridges before trusting them, probing with the axe shaft, keeping the rope taut but never dragging, choosing the ugly safe line over the beautiful dangerous one. By early afternoon she understood why he moved the way he moved — every choice was a scar from some previous expedition, some previous loss, each one paid for in full.

The weather came down the western ridge at two o'clock, ahead of every forecast the sky had offered.

It didn't arrive like a storm. It arrived like a decision. One minute there were three of them on a broad shoulder of wind-scoured snow with the col visible ahead, a notch of darker sky between two walls of ice; the next minute the world contracted to a circle ten meters across, then five, then the length of the rope, and the wind came off the glacier with grains of ice in its teeth that stripped the skin off anything exposed.

"Rope spacing!" Coral shouted, and couldn't hear herself, and shouted again, and saw Amara's glove flash twice — the signal they'd agreed on — and put her head down and navigated by counting.

Counting was a diver's skill too. On the wreck jobs, when the silt came up and turned the delta water to brown milk, you counted kicks. You counted seconds. You held the guideline and you trusted arithmetic more than your own eyes, because your eyes would lie to you about which way was up and arithmetic never did. So she counted paces. Four hundred paces on a bearing she'd taken from the last glimpse of the col. Then a traverse, two hundred paces, feeling for the rise of the ridge on her right side. The wind tried to rewrite the numbers, tried to make her doubt whether she'd started counting from the right place, and she thought — absurdly, in the middle of the white roar — of Lieutenant Vance's ledger, of a man who believed numbers only counted when they profited him. These numbers counted because she said they did. That was the whole difference between a ledger and a bearing.

The ridge rose under her right boot exactly on schedule.

She almost cried. She didn't. She anchored, hauled the rope signal twice, and waited for the other two shapes to condense out of the white.

* * *

They sheltered for ninety minutes in the lee of a serac, backs to the ice, sharing one fuel canister's worth of hot water passed between three mouths. Tomas's right hand wouldn't close around the cup. Coral held it for him, tipped it, watched him drink, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either cruelty or comfort and she hadn't decided which the truth was.

"We need to talk about the transmitter," Amara said.

Coral had been carrying the black-box unit since the delta — the navy transmitter she'd been ordered never to open, the one that had whispered a dead survey vessel's secret and then a live expedition's distress call. It rode against her spine now, wrapped in her spare socks, and she'd caught herself checking for it the way you check for a wound.

"Vance triangulated the survey team's emergency beacon to find their camp," Amara went on. Her voice was steadier than her hands. Six weeks starving and she still talked like a woman presenting findings. "He'll do the same to us. Every radio transmission is a flare. Which means the relay station —"

"— is a trap we have to walk into anyway," Coral finished. "I know."

"Do you?" Amara looked at her. "Because there's a version of this where we don't key it. Where we go down the far side quiet, reach the village, and tell the story mouth to mouth. Slower. Safer. Maybe."

"And a version where the story dies on the riverbank with the old woman who remembers my father's name," Coral said. "A version where Vance files his report, the mine reopens under a new company name, and the delta keeps poisoning children until the river forgets how to run clear." She looked east, into the white, toward the station none of them could see. "No. We broadcast. Open channel, full power, everything Amara has. And yes — Vance will hear it coming. Let him run. A man with a burned ledger runs faster downhill than we ever will."

Tomas made a sound that might have been a laugh. "You've changed your mind about letting him come."

"I've refined it." She passed him the cup again. "Before, I wanted him to hear the truth. Now I want him to hear it while he's being arrested."

* * *

The col, when they reached it near dusk, was worse than the crossing.

The ice bridge — the natural arch of compacted snow that spanned the crevasse field below the notch — had thinned since the season's calving began. Tomas, examining it with his good hand and thirty years of judgment, pronounced it passable at dawn, frozen hardest after the night's cold, and suicide by midday. Which meant they had to survive the night at five thousand meters in rising wind, on a notch of rock barely wider than the bridge itself, with fuel for one more boil.

Coral organized it the way she organized a dive: tasks, sequence, no discussion of outcomes. Tent pitched in the lee of the rock with Amara's frost-stiffened fingers guided through the pole sleeves. Boots swapped for dry socks, the wet ones stowed inside jackets to thaw against ribs. Tomas's hand unwrapped, inspected, and repacked in fresh snow-slush for ten minutes — the old field treatment, brutal and effective — while he stared at the darkening sky and pretended the treatment didn't hurt.

"How bad," she asked him, when Amara had crawled into the tent.

"Two fingers are gone. Maybe three." He said it like a man reporting river depth. "The question is whether the rest of the hand goes with them. Ask me tomorrow."

"Tomas."

"Coral." He turned his head and looked at her, and in the last grey light his face was older than it had been on the riverboat, older than it had been in the cave, aged by altitude and honesty. "When we get down — if we get down — I'm done guiding. You understand that. A guide with half a hand is a story, not a leader. Whatever happens at that station, whatever happens with Vance — after this, the outfit is yours if you want it. The routes, the clients, the name. I'll cook."

She wanted to argue. She opened her mouth to argue.

And then she understood that arguing would be a kindness to herself, not to him, and closed it.

"Sleep," she said. "I'll take first watch."

* * *

First watch became second watch became the night, because she didn't wake him. There was nothing to watch for except weather, and the weather needed no watching — it simply deepened, the way water darkens with depth, and Coral sat with her back to the rock and let the mountain make its noise.

Somewhere below and behind, on the far side of the range, Vance's team was moving. She was sure of it the way she was sure of currents — not by seeing but by knowing. He'd have the triangulation plots by now. He'd have mules or men or both, and a warrant with her name spelled correctly for once, and the particular patience of a man who believes the ledger always balances in his favor. Let him climb. Every meter he gained toward the col was a meter he spent from somewhere else, and somewhere else was where Amara's data was already traveling — duplicated, distributed, hidden in three containers across three bodies, and in one more place he'd never think to search: in Coral Okonkwo's memory, recited nightly like a prayer, coordinates and concentrations and dates, the whole poisoned history of the delta stored in the one vault no warrant could open.

Her father had taught her that without meaning to. Nine years ago, when the navy took his boats and his name and finally his freedom, he'd told her: *They can confiscate what you carry, little fish. Never what you know.* She'd hated him for the advice then, because advice was all he had left to give. Tonight, on a glacier, guarding two sleepers and a satchel of evidence, she forgave him.

Wind screamed over the notch. Below her, invisible, the ice bridge held its breath.

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and with it, a sound.

Not the glacier's usual booming — she knew that grammar now, the groans and reports of a living river of ice. This was different. This was mechanical, rhythmic, faint but getting less faint: the chop-chop-chop of rotors, carried up the valley on the wind's back.

Coral stood, stiff as a salvaged hull, and scanned the eastern sky through the tearing wind. Nothing visible yet. But the sound was coming from the direction of the relay station — from the low ground beyond the pass, where a man with a ledger would land his recovery team and start climbing with fresh legs and government fuel.

She woke them with a hand on each shoulder and no apology.

"They're here," she said. "Bridge holds or it doesn't. We cross now."

Tomas got up one-handed. Amara shouldered her pack. And Coral Okonkwo clipped herself to the front of the rope, faced the thin white arch spanning the dark, and stepped onto it first — because that, she understood at last, was what leading meant. Not certainty. Not talent. Just being the one whose foot falls first, so that everyone behind knows the ice can hold at least one person's weight.

Behind them, growing louder, the helicopters came on.
