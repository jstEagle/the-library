## 14. The Relay Station

Tomas smelled the station before he saw it — old creosote and rust riding the wind down from the saddle above them. He stopped on the moraine ridge, let his breath settle, and looked back the way they'd come.

Three days since the ice bridge. Three days of Coral leading while his right hand curled into itself like a dying fern. Three days of Amara Diallo walking on legs that had forgotten how, propped between them, counting her steps aloud in a whisper because she said the numbers kept her sane.

The Kessel Range fell away behind them in blue terraces. Somewhere on the far side of that white wall, Vance's team was moving. Tomas had no proof of it beyond a feeling in his teeth, but feelings had kept him alive in these mountains longer than any instrument had.

"There." Amara's voice was a rasp. She raised one arm, pointing past his shoulder to the saddle where the wind funneled through. "That's it. That's the shape I remember from the survey photos."

He turned. The relay station sat on a shelf of rock like something shipwrecked and left to dry. A low stone building with a corrugated roof half peeled back, a lattice tower leaning at fifteen degrees but still standing, guy wires singing faintly in the wind. Colonial work, sixty or seventy years old, built by men who expected the mountains to be grateful for their attention.

"Power?" Coral asked. She'd come up beside him without a sound. She always did that. Even wrecked, even frost-nipped, she moved like water finding its level.

"Hydro, probably," Amara said. "These stations were strung along the river systems. There'll be an intake up-slope, a penstock, a generator the size of a coffin. If the intake isn't silted solid, we might have light."

"And if it is?"

"Then we burn what's left of my survey kit and pray the batteries in the transmitter held a charge."

Tomas tested the wind with a wet finger, an old habit his father would have mocked. It came out of the south, steady, carrying the smell of thawing valley below. Good weather for a broadcast. Bad weather for hiding.

"Let's go see," he said.

* * *

The door had swollen shut decades ago and Coral opened it the way she did everything — patiently, without wasted force, working the frame until the wood gave with a groan that echoed off the rocks. Inside: dust, a collapsed bunk, a desk with its drawers pulled out and scattered, and against the far wall, under a tarp gone gray as cobweb, the transmitter.

It was a beautiful ugly thing. Brass fittings gone green, bakelite panels cracked, a dial face with the manufacturer's name worn to ghosts. Amara peeled the tarp back and stood very still.

"RT-9 field relay," she said. "They don't make them anymore. They can't kill them, either."

"You know this equipment?" Tomas asked.

"I serviced its grandchildren." She was already opening the access panel, blowing dust off the contacts, running her fingers along the wiring with a tenderness that made him look away. People loved machines like that sometimes. Safer than loving people.

Coral went outside to check the penstock. Tomas heard her boots on the scree, then the long silence of someone listening to water. When she came back her expression hadn't changed, which meant it was good news; bad news she wore openly.

"Intake's clear. There's flow. Generator might turn over."

Amara found the crank behind the fallen bunk. The first pull did nothing. The second produced a cough from somewhere deep in the building's bones. On the fourth, the generator caught with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and a single bulb overhead flickered, buzzed, and burned a weak amber that filled the room with shadows.

Amara laughed — a short, startled bark, as if the sound had escaped custody. Then she bent over the transmitter and began to work.

Tomas stepped outside.

Not because he didn't want to hear what came next. Because he needed to stand in the open air and look at the problem honestly, the way you look at a river before crossing it.

The problem was simple. The solution was a trap.

A working transmitter meant rescue for Amara, testimony for the dead village, evidence for whatever inquiry might someday crawl out of the navy's bureaucracy. It also meant a signal, and a signal could be triangulated. Vance had resources. Vance had direction-finding gear and helicopters and the particular patience of a man protecting something worse than a crime — a secret about himself. If they broadcast from this saddle, they might as well paint a target on the roof.

And there was a second problem, smaller and colder, sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone. His hand. He'd unwrapped it that morning at the stream, alone, before the others woke. The frostbite had gone past white into a color he didn't have a name for. Two fingers didn't answer when he told them to move. He'd been a guide long enough to know what came next: black, then the knife, then a life with one hand and a story he'd tell at parties until people stopped inviting him.

He flexed the fingers inside his mitten. Nothing. He put the hand back in his pocket and went inside, smiling, because Coral was watching him with those diver's eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"Well?" she asked.

"She needs an hour to align the set and charge the capacitors. Then we talk." He shrugged. "Then we run."

* * *

They used the hour badly and well. Amara worked. Coral slept, finally, curled against the warm generator housing with her chin on her knees, out in four seconds flat the way only the truly exhausted can manage. Tomas sat in the doorway with his back to the light and cleaned his rifle — the old lever action he'd carried since his army days, more ritual than weapon now — and thought about his father.

His father had been a river man too, though he'd have denied the kinship with Coral's line of work. A pilot, actually, flying mail and medicine into valleys like this one, back when the colonial government still pretended the map had edges. He'd taught Tomas two things. First: never cross a river at its widest point, because width means shallows and shallows mean you can read the bottom. Second: every solution is a door, and every door swings both ways.

Tomas had spent eleven years failing to understand the second lesson. Losing the Okonkwo-adjacent expedition — losing *people*, four of them, names he still said to himself on bad nights like a rosary — had taught him that doors swing. He'd led them through a pass he hadn't checked, in a season he'd misjudged, because a client's schedule mattered more to him than a weatherman's warning. The mountain had closed its hand. He'd walked out. They hadn't.

Now here he sat in another colonial doorway, watching another woman bet everything on another transmission, and the door was swinging again. He could feel the draft on his neck.

"Tomas." Coral was awake. He didn't know how long she'd been watching him. "You're doing the thing where your face goes somewhere else."

"My face goes lots of places. It's a well-traveled face."

"Cute." She crawled over and sat beside him, shoulder to shoulder, both of them looking out at the valley. Below them the river ran silver through brown tundra, already swollen with the thaw, already hurrying toward the delta where her father had drowned his name. "Talk."

"It's a trap," he said. "You know it's a trap."

"I know."

"We broadcast, Vance hears us within the hour and has our coordinates inside three. We broadcast, and we'd better be ready to move, and I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I'm not sure how fast I can move anymore."

She looked at his hand. Not the mitten — the hand beneath it, as if she could see through canvas and wool to the damage. Divers knew cold the way guides did. She'd known before he'd admitted it to himself.

"How bad?"

"Bad enough that I should say so out loud instead of letting you find out at the wrong moment." He unwound the mitten. In the amber light the fingers looked like something dredged up. Coral took his wrist gently, turned the hand, pressed a thumbnail to the dead skin and watched it stay white.

"We're two days from the valley," she said. "There'll be a clinic in the delta village. Maybe. Or Amara knows field surgery — she cut a cyst out of her own arm once, she told me, with a fishing knife and rum."

"That's not surgery, that's a bar story."

"It's what we have." She wrapped the hand back up, careful, her movements precise as knotwork. "So we broadcast fast, and we run slow, and we get low enough that the trees break up any thermal signature. And if Vance comes anyway—"

"He'll come anyway."

"—then we meet him on ground we choose." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, and somehow that was the most frightening part. This woman had decided something on the glacier and it had calcified into her spine. "Tomas. Look at me."

He looked.

"When I dove the wreck, I was invisible. That was the whole trick of my life — be small, be useful, be quiet, and maybe they forget you owe them. Down there in the dark I was nobody's daughter and nobody's debt. Just hands and lungs." Her jaw tightened. "I'm done being invisible. If broadcasting costs us, then it costs us. But the cost of silence is paid by other people, and I've seen who pays it. A whole village. My father. Four climbers whose guide couldn't admit a mistake."

The last sentence landed where she'd aimed it. He flinched, and she saw him flinch, and she didn't take it back.

"You're right," he said. "That's the worst part. You're right."

* * *

At dusk the transmitter warmed to life with a hum they felt in their fillings. Amara had rigged an antenna from salvaged wire strung to the leaning tower, and she tuned the set by ear, hunting through static the way Coral hunted through silt. Outside, the light went from gold to violet to nothing. The wind dropped. The mountains held their breath.

"Got it," Amara murmured. "Open channel. Emergency band. Anyone listening on a military net will hear this too — there's no way around that. Encryption's dead on this relic. Plain voice or nothing."

"Plain voice," Coral said. "That's the point. Everyone hears. Especially everyone."

Tomas stood at the window with the rifle across his forearm, scanning the dark slopes. The moon was three days from full and threw hard shadows off the moraines. He tried to imagine Vance's direction-finders, the vans and masts, the calm voice saying *bearing zero-one-five, signal strength rising*. Tried to imagine the helicopter lifting off in the pre-dawn.

"Whenever you're ready," he said.

Amara looked at Coral. Coral nodded once.

Amara keyed the transmit switch and began to speak — name, rank, expedition designation, date, position. Her voice steadied as she went, gathering authority like the generator gathering current. Then the data. Mining discharge volumes. Contaminant readings from the delta. Dates matching the navy's "survey" operations. The scuttling of the vessel *Meridian*, hull breached from inside, charges placed below the waterline by personnel unknown except that the detonation signature matched naval demolition stock.

And the village. She named it. She gave its coordinates, its population before and after, the readings from its wells. She spoke for six minutes without stopping, and Tomas realized halfway through that he was gripping the rifle hard enough to hurt his good hand.

When she finished she keyed off and the room went silent except for the generator's heartbeat.

Nothing answered. No acknowledgment, no jamming burst, no voice crackling out of the static demanding identification. Just the hiss of the universe being indifferent.

"Maybe nobody heard," Amara whispered.

"Somebody heard," Tomas said quietly. "Somebody always hears. That's the trap."

Coral crossed to the window beside him. For a moment none of them moved. Then, far to the north, across the range they'd crossed and the passes they'd bled for, a light blinked. Once. Twice. Deliberate. Not a star — stars didn't move sideways, and this one did, drifting along the contour of the distant ridge with the patient arrogance of a machine that knew exactly where it was going.

Or exactly where it was coming from.

"They've got a night aircraft up," Tomas said. "That's a running light. That's a helicopter running dark, following the ridgeline." He turned. "How long to pack?"

"Two minutes," Coral said. "We practiced."

"Good. Because we're leaving now, and we're leaving the antenna up, and we're going to make them think we're still transmitting." He was already pulling his pack together, the plan assembling itself in the old familiar place behind his eyes, the place that had failed him once and had been sharpening itself ever since. "Amara — can you rig the key to tap on a loop? Keep the carrier alive?"

She was already rewiring, fingers flying. "Give me ninety seconds. It'll send garbage, but it'll send."

"Ninety seconds it is." Tomas looked at Coral, and in the amber light he saw her clearly — not the invisible woman, not the debtor, not the deserter. The person she was becoming, the one who chose. "South slope, down into the tree line. We walk all night."

"And if they land here?"

"Then they land on an empty station with a transmitter chattering nonsense into the dark," he said, "and Lieutenant Vance gets to explain to his superiors why the mountains are full of ghosts."

Outside, the running light slid behind a spur of rock and did not reappear.

Which was worse.

Tomas shouldered his pack, took Coral's hand with his good one, and stepped out into the cold.
