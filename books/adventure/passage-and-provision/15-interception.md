## 15. Interception

Vance had learned long ago that the mountains did not care about rank.

The relay station sat on a saddle of black rock above the icefield, a colonial relic with a rusted mast and a generator shed that still smelled of diesel from some dead man's last refueling. His team came up the eastern approach in single file, six men roped against the crevasse fields, and by the time they reached the station walls the wind had stripped every one of them of anything resembling dignity. That was fine. Dignity was for people whose careers were not balanced on a knife's edge over a glacier.

"Perimeter," he said, and four of them fanned out along the rock while he and Sergeant Okoye took the main building.

The door was old teak, swollen in its frame. Vance put his shoulder to it and it gave with a crack that echoed off the ice below. Inside: dust, a collapsed bunk, a workbench under a tarp, and a transmitter that was warm.

He laid his bare palm on the housing. Still holding heat. Someone had been here within the hour — maybe two.

"They heard us coming," Okoye said.

"No." Vance crouched, examining the bench. A soldering iron, cold now but clean. A coil of fresh copper wire. A mug with tea dregs in the bottom, not yet dry. "They were working. They stopped mid-task." He looked at the window, its glass fogged from the inside until recently. "They're close."

The third member of the trio was not close at all. She was behind the generator shed, wedged into a gap between the fuel drums and the wall, and it was Private Hessler who found her when he went looking for firewood. Vance heard the shout and came around the corner fast, sidearm drawn, though he already knew what he'd find.

Amara Diallo stood with her hands half-raised, caught between surrender and defiance, and even starved down to sinew and frostbite she managed to look like she was doing him a favor by being captured. Her parka was patched with wire. Her lips were cracked and gray. Her eyes went over Vance's uniform, his team, his weapons, cataloguing all of it with the flat professional interest of an engineer assessing a load-bearing failure.

"Lieutenant Vance," she said. Not a question.

"You know me."

"I know your signature." She said it without heat, which was worse than if she'd spat. "I've spent three months reading documents you signed. The scuttling order for the *Meridian*. The convoy manifest that never listed us. The requisition that bought the mining company's silence." She tilted her head. "You have very tidy handwriting, Lieutenant. Tidy men always think no one will ever read their files."

Okoye shifted beside him. Vance raised two fingers — hold.

"Where are the others?"

"Gone."

"Where are the drives?"

"Gone." She smiled with ruined lips. "You're going to have to be more creative than this."

Vance holstered his weapon slowly. There was no point threatening her; he'd read her file on the flight up. Amara Diallo, thirty-one, structural engineer, commendations for courage under fire during the coastal floods. People like that didn't break under threat. They broke under arithmetic.

"Tie her hands," he said. "Front. And get her inside where it's warm." He met Diallo's eyes. "I'm not going to hurt you, Doctor. I'm going to feed you. There's a difference, and you'll understand it before morning."

* * *

They made camp in the transmitter room because it was the only space with a functioning stove. Diallo sat on a crate with her bound hands in her lap and ate the ration pack they gave her with the methodical discipline of someone who had learned, over three months on a mountain, exactly how fast to eat and no faster. Vance watched her and felt something adjacent to respect, which he filed away where it couldn't interfere.

His radio operator, Chen, had swept the surrounding ridgelines with thermal optics and found nothing. The storm front rolling in off the range would kill any trace by dark anyway. But Vance knew how people moved through country like this — he'd been born in country like this, before the academy scrubbed the delta mud out of his accent — and he knew they hadn't gone far. You didn't cross the calving zone at night. You holed up, you waited for the light, and you told yourself the weather would cover you.

It wouldn't. Weather covered nothing anymore. Every satellite the navy leased passed over this range twice a day, and Vance had spent the last week quietly reassigning their tasking. Somewhere out there in the white, three heat signatures were about to become four dots on his screen.

The only variable was time. The calving season would seal the southern pass within days, and after that the recovery operation became a siege, and sieges generated paperwork, and paperwork generated questions. Questions were the one thing Vance could not afford. Everything he had built — the quiet career, the clean record, the careful distance between himself and the decisions made in rooms he'd never entered — rested on the principle that no one ever asked.

Diallo finished eating. She watched him watch her.

"You want to know why I'm telling you the truth," she said.

"I assume it's a tactic."

"It's arithmetic." She flexed her bound fingers. "You can't let us broadcast. You can't let the drives reach the delta. Those are your constraints. I've read enough of your files to know you don't waste motion, so you won't kill us — bodies generate inquiries, and inquiries are the thing you fear most. Which means your only move is negotiation." She leaned forward slightly. "So negotiate. What are you offering?"

Vance studied her for a long moment. Then he said, "How much do you know about Coral Okonkwo?"

Something flickered across Diallo's face — surprise, quickly mastered. "Your diver. The one who stole my data and ran."

"The daughter of Emeka Okonkwo." He let the name sit in the cold air of the transmitter room. "Chief Salvage Diver, convicted eleven years ago of falsifying contamination surveys in the Kessel delta. The surveys that should have flagged the mining runoff. The conviction that ended the investigation before it started." He paused. "You've read the real reports, Doctor. You know the runoff was real. You know the village downstream lost forty-one people to it. And you know someone had to take the fall for the fact that the navy knew and buried it."

Diallo had gone very still. "Her father was framed."

"Her father was *necessary*," Vance said. "There's a difference, and it matters to exactly one person left alive who cares." He stood, crossed to the window, and looked out at the gathering cloud. "Coral Okonkwo has spent eleven years paying her father's debt in salvage hours the navy never intended to credit. She is invisible by trade and by temperament. She has no record, no advocate, no future beyond the next contract. And she is currently sitting somewhere on this mountain holding the only copies of your data drives."

He turned back. "Here is my offer. The drives, surrendered intact. In exchange: full pardon for Emeka Okonkwo, posthumous restoration of rank, back pay to the family estate. Full pardon for Coral's desertion, sealed file, honorable discharge at her option. And your survival, Doctor, guaranteed in writing — returned to the coast, debriefed as a distressed survivor of a maritime accident, free to resign your commission and say whatever you like to whoever will listen." He shrugged. "No one will listen. But you'll be alive to try."

Diallo stared at him. "And the village? The forty-one people? The river?"

"The river will be remediated," Vance said. "Quietly. Over years. It's already in the contingency planning." It was true; he'd written part of it himself, late at night, the way a man cleans a wound he refuses to show a doctor. "None of that requires a public inquiry. Public inquiries destroy more than they repair. Ask anyone from the delta."

"My whole team died on this mountain," Diallo said softly, "because of what your navy scuttled into that river. And you're offering me a quiet life as hush money."

"I'm offering you the only version of events in which anyone gets anything," Vance said. "That's not cynicism, Doctor. That's experience."

She held his gaze for a long moment. Then she said, "You'll need Coral to agree. I'm not the one holding the drives."

"I know." Vance checked his watch. "Which is why you're going to help me reach her. You'll tell her the offer yourself. She trusts you — or she will, more than she'll ever trust me." He moved to the door. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow we go and collect them."

"And if she says no?"

Vance paused with his hand on the latch. Outside, the wind had begun to rise, carrying the first fine grains of snow against the glass.

"Then tomorrow becomes complicated," he said, "and I have very little room left for complicated."

* * *

Coral heard the snowmobiles at dusk.

Two engines, high and thin across the ice, running without lights. She pressed herself flat against the boulder shelf where she and Tomas had made their cold camp — a hollow beneath a leaning slab, barely deep enough for two, with Amara's backup drive strapped inside her jacket against her sternum where her heartbeat could keep it warm. Tomas lay beside her, his bandaged hand cradled against his chest, breathing the shallow controlled breaths of a man conserving heat he didn't have.

Below, on the white plain, the engines cut. Voices carried in the still air — clipped, military, disciplined. A lantern flared once behind a distant ridge and went dark.

"They found the station," Tomas breathed.

"They found Amara." Coral's throat was tight. She'd left her at the relay with the transmitter warming up, with a plan: broadcast in short bursts, keep moving, meet at the moraine fork at dawn. Three hours later the snowmobiles had come up the eastern approach, and the plan had died somewhere between the second burst and the first engine note. "Vance doesn't leave witnesses on mountains. If he wanted her dead she'd be dead. He wants her talking."

"He wants *you* talking." Tomas shifted painfully onto his elbow. In the blue half-light his face was gaunt, the frostbite scars on his knuckles shining like wax. "Coral. Listen to me. Whatever he offers you — and he'll offer, it's the only tool he has left — it will be your father. It will be everything they took. He's done his homework. He knows exactly what your name costs and exactly what you'd pay to buy it back."

Coral said nothing. Below them, faintly, a voice called out across the ice — amplified, formal, absurdly courteous in the vastness. Calling her name. *Diver Okonkwo.* Offering safe passage, warm shelter, terms.

Terms. As if the last eleven years had been a negotiation she'd simply failed to attend.

She thought of her father's hands, scarred white from decades of cold water, teaching her to read current by feel. She thought of the ledger in the naval office, the debt column that never shortened no matter how many wrecks she dove. She thought of the old woman in the unmapped village saying *Okonkwo* like a curse and like a prayer, and the forty-one names Amara had recited from the contaminated well logs, and the way her own reflection had looked in the black water of the delta — invisible, competent, owned.

A pardon would give her back her father's name. It would not give her a name of her own. She understood that now, the way you understand a current shift — not with your head but with the pressure against your body, the whole water deciding to move.

But God, the offer was beautiful. That was the trap of it. Vance hadn't built a cage; he'd built a door, standing open, with everything she'd ever wanted visible through it. All she had to do was set down the weight she was carrying and walk through lighter.

"Coral." Tomas's voice, very low. "Whatever you're thinking —"

"I'm thinking he's right about one thing." She pulled the drive from her jacket and held it between them, small and dark and heavier than any salvage she'd ever lifted. "This ends tomorrow, one way or another. The pass closes in three days. We can't outrun them and we can't hide forever, and Amara is down there alone with a man who signs orders and calls it tidiness." She looked at Tomas, and in the dark her eyes were steady as ballast. "So we don't run. We go down there at first light. And we make him think I'm listening."

Tomas was silent for a moment. Then, despite everything — the hand, the cold, the engines in the dark — he smiled.

"There she is," he said. "The woman who opened the box."

Below them, the amplified voice called her name again, patient as tide. Coral slid the drive back against her heart, settled into the cold beside the man who had followed her up a mountain on a guess, and began, silently, to rehearse the lie that would buy them until dawn.
