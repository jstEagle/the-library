## 16. The Weight of a Name

They took Amara out first. Two soldiers, one on each arm, marching her down the relay station's central corridor with her wrists bound and her head held high in a way that made the binding look ceremonial. She didn't look back at Coral. That was the worst of it — or the best. Amara Diallo had spent eleven weeks starving on a glacier slope and she still refused to give Vance the satisfaction of a glance.

Coral counted boots. Six soldiers in the station, plus Vance. Tomas bleeding through a field dressing on his forearm where a rifle barrel had caught him across the old frostbite scar. The transmitter room at the end of the corridor, its colonial-era equipment humming on a generator that someone — the navy, decades ago, when this place still mattered — had built to outlast everything around it.

And her, standing in the map room with wet boots and a dead man's coat, watching Lieutenant Emeric Vance close the door behind the last soldier.

The latch clicked. The generator's hum filled the silence like water filling a hull.

"You look terrible," Vance said.

"You should see the other mountain."

He almost smiled. That was the thing about Vance that had taken Coral three years of servitude to understand: he wasn't cruel in the way the manuals warned you about. He was worse. He was *reasonable*. He treated atrocity like a scheduling conflict, and he did it with the calm of a man who had never once doubted his own arithmetic.

He crossed to the station's old stove, lit it with a practiced flick, and set a kettle on it. Actual tea. He'd carried tea up the Kessel Range.

"Sit down, Okonkwo."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." He pulled a chair out for himself, sat, and produced a flat grey case from his jacket — the kind of case that meant documents, which meant leverage, which meant the whole reason she was still breathing instead of face-down in the snow outside. He laid it on the table between them but didn't open it. "Before we discuss terms, there's something you've earned. A courtesy. You've been a good asset, and I don't like assets dying ignorant."

"I've been a prisoner," Coral said. "Prisoners don't earn courtesies."

"Three years of clean service. No disciplinary marks. You recovered the transmitter from the *Meridian* in conditions that would have broken most of my dive pool." He said it like a performance review. "And you crossed the Kessel Range in calving season with a disgraced guide and half a survey team. That's not prisoner behavior, Coral. That's the behavior of someone who wants something."

The kettle began its low whistle. Vance ignored it.

"What I want," she said, "is for you to open that case or put it away."

"Then let me tell you a story first." He leaned back. Outside, the wind worried the station's loose sheet metal, a long metallic moan that came and went like something breathing. "Nineteen years ago there was a dive contractor in the delta. Freelance. Good reputation, which was a problem, because the navy needed a bad reputation. Someone to take the fall for a salvage operation that went wrong — that was *made* to go wrong. A survey vessel scuttled in shallow water with cargo aboard that was never supposed to be found. Someone had to be holding the manifest when the inquiry came."

Coral's hands had gone still at her sides. She was aware of them the way she was aware of depth pressure — a fact of the environment, pressing in.

"The navy needed a diver with access and a reason to be in the wrong place," Vance went on. "They found one. He was arrested, charged, convicted. His daughter was eleven. She was taken into the navy's debt-education program — you know how that works, the state educates the children of debtors, and the debt compounds. Elegant system. Keeps families loyal for generations." He paused. "The order authorizing the operation — the scuttling, the framing, all of it — required a signature. A lieutenant commander's signature. Do you want to know whose?"

The kettle was screaming now. Vance reached over and moved it off the burner without breaking eye contact, and the sudden absence of the sound was worse than the sound.

"You," Coral said. Her voice came out flat. Diver's flat. The voice she used at forty meters when panic was a luxury and breath was a budget. "You signed it."

"I signed it." No flinch. No deflection. He said it the way he'd say *I signed for the supplies*. "I was younger. Ambitious. I was told the contractor was dirty anyway — and maybe he was, I've never checked, because checking would have been a courtesy to myself and I didn't need one. I needed the career. And it worked, Coral. It worked beautifully. The river stayed quiet, the mining consortium stayed happy, and a village that was always going to be poor got poorer, and no one important died except on paper." He spread his hands. "That's the whole confession. No dramatics. I'm not asking your forgiveness. I'm offering you something better."

"Which is?"

He opened the case.

Inside, on a foam bed, sat a data drive — one of Amara's, or a copy of one; Coral couldn't tell, and understood with a cold drop in her stomach that it didn't matter, because Vance only needed her to believe it was the real one. Beside it, a single sheet of paper. A pardon. Her father's name at the top — *Okonkwo, Eze* — and the word *exonerated* in the kind of language that only meant something when a court said it.

"Full pardon for your father. Posthumous exoneration, his record sealed clean, his name restored to the contractors' registry. Full discharge of your debt — you walk out of the navy a free woman, no ledger, no handler, no one's asset ever again." He tapped the paper once. "And no charges for desertion. You were never here. Reyes takes the fall for leading you astray — he's already ruined, it costs him nothing he hasn't lost — and Diallo's findings stay classified under national security exemption. The river stays quiet. The village stays unmapped. Everyone keeps what they had, and you get the only thing you've wanted since you were eleven years old."

The wind moaned through the sheet metal. Somewhere down the corridor, a soldier laughed at something, the sound thin and far away, from another world.

Coral looked at the pardon. She made herself look at it properly, the way she'd been trained to look at everything — without wanting, without flinching, just recording. Her father's name in official type. She had spent nineteen years rehearsing the sight of it. In the navy's education barracks she'd written it on the back of her hand so many times the ink had stained the skin. She had imagined the moment of his exoneration as a door opening, as air, as the first breath after surfacing.

And now it was on a table in a stolen relay station, being offered by the man who had closed the door in the first place, in exchange for a village full of dead and a river full of poison and an engineer starving in a cell down the hall.

"You've thought about this," she said.

"Since the foothills. I don't improvise, Coral. You know that about me."

"I know that about you." She looked up. "You signed the order. You've known for nineteen years what my name cost. And every time you looked at me — every dive briefing, every debt review, every time you said *your father's crimes* like a line item — you knew."

"Yes."

"You let me carry it."

"I let you carry what was useful." He said it without apology, and that was the moment the hatred in her stopped being a hot, formless thing and became something else. Something with edges. She could feel it happen — the way water at depth stops being cold and becomes simply a fact you work inside. The hatred clarified, condensed, took a shape she could hold.

Because here was the thing Vance had never understood about divers, about her, about the girl who'd learned to be invisible in navy barracks: the invisible survive by watching. By giving the surface what the surface expects. She had spent three years being exactly what Vance wanted — competent, quiet, obedient — and he had mistaken the performance for the person. He had just handed her the script for the final act, and he'd done it because he believed she wanted the pardon more than she wanted anything alive.

He was almost right. That was the terrible part. Nineteen years almost right.

"Amara Diallo," Coral said. "What happens to her?"

"Debriefing. Repatriation. Eventually a quiet posting somewhere far from rivers." A lie so smooth it was almost a kindness. "She'll be fine."

"And the village. The one that isn't on the chart. The old woman who knew my father's name before I said it."

"Unmapped territory has no legal standing. You know the doctrine."

"I know the doctrine." She let a silence stretch, and in it she let him see — carefully, in measured amounts, the way you vent pressure — the thing he wanted to see. The wanting. Her father's name. The pardon. She let her eyes go to the paper and stay there a half-second too long. She let her breath change. Nineteen years of rehearsal, and it still cost her something to spend the truth of her own longing as currency.

"You'd be free," Vance said softly. He'd seen it. Of course he'd seen it; he'd built his career on seeing what people wanted and pricing it. "No ledger. No handler. No one's asset. Your own name, clean, for the first time in your life. Think about what that's worth."

*Your own name.* She thought about it. She thought about the old woman on the riverbank saying *Okonkwo* like a wound, like a debt of her own. She thought about Amara's voice in the looted camp, steady through cracked lips: *they scuttled it, they scuttled their own ship.* She thought about Tomas's hand, wrapped and failing, and the way he'd said *I lost them* about an expedition nineteen years gone, still paying, still carrying it — and how she'd told him guilt was just weight, and weight could be carried or set down, and she'd meant it.

A name given by the man who ruined it wasn't a name. It was a leash with better stitching.

"I want the pardon in writing first," Coral said. "Signed. Before I touch the drive."

"Reasonable." Vance slid the paper across the table, produced a pen — of course he had a pen, he'd climbed a mountain with tea and a pen — and signed it in a quick, sure hand. E. Vance, Lieutenant. The same signature, nineteen years older, that had ended her childhood. She watched him write it and committed the motion to memory, the angle of it, the small flourish on the V, the way his wrist turned. Some things you keep.

"The drive," he said. "Where's the backup? Diallo would have made one — engineers always do. I want both."

"Buried on the glacier. Coordinates in my head. I'll give them up when my father's record is cleared in the system — not before. I've been your asset three years, Lieutenant. You taught me not to hand over cargo on promises."

Something flickered behind his eyes — irritation, or respect, or the two of them wearing each other's coats. "Tomorrow. The weather closes in forty hours and I'm not staying past it. You'll transmit the coordinates through the station relay, we verify, and then you walk out of here a free woman."

"Tomorrow," Coral agreed.

She picked up the pardon. Her father's name. *Exonerated.* The paper was warm from the stove and it shook very slightly in her hand and she let it shake, let him see that too, the last honest thing she'd give him.

"Get some sleep," Vance said, almost gently. "You've earned it."

* * *

They put her in a supply room with a cot and a locked door and a window too narrow to matter. She sat on the cot with the pardon in her lap and read it until the words stopped being words.

Then she folded it — once, twice, small — and worked it into the seam of her coat lining, where a diver learns to keep things that must not be lost. Not because she'd honor it. Because it was evidence. Because it was his signature, fresh, attached to her father's name, and paper like that had a way of becoming ammunition.

Through the wall, the generator hummed. Down the corridor, she could hear the transmitter room's door opening and closing as soldiers checked the equipment on Vance's orders — checking the very machine she'd need. Forty hours. A locked door, a narrow window, a station full of rifles, and a glacier outside that had already eaten the pass once and was hungry again.

She lay back on the cot and closed her eyes and began, methodically, to count — boots in the corridor, the interval of the guard's rounds, the pitch of the wind, the creak of the sheet metal — the way she counted at depth. Building the picture. Finding the current.

Tomorrow she would give Vance what he'd asked for. She'd smile. She'd hand over coordinates — real ones, a real glacier crevasse, an hour of his people's time wasted digging in blue ice. And while his men were in the transmitter room verifying a lie, she would be in it.

Vance had signed her father's ruin with that wrist, that angle, that flourish on the V. Tomorrow she'd make him sign something else — a transmission, an open channel, his own voice authorizing the relay — and she'd carry *that* signature down the mountain too.

The guard's boots passed her door. Paused. Moved on.

Coral opened her eyes in the dark and started planning.
