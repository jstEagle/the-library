## 17. The Calving

The relay station smelled of rust and old kerosene and, faintly, of the men who had held it for a century before the navy decided it was worth nothing. Coral sat on a bench beneath a window gone grey with salt-fog, her wrists bound in front of her with a zip tie that Vance's corporal had cinched with theatrical care, and she listened to the glacier.

It talked all night. That was the thing no one who hadn't lived beside ice could understand — it wasn't silent. It groaned like a ship's hull under load, cracked like rifle fire, sighed in long descending notes when the temperature dropped. Tomas had taught her its vocabulary on the ascent: *that one's settling, that one's just cold, that one you listen to.* She had thought she understood him then. Sitting in the station with her hands tied and Amara Diallo locked in the supply closet and Tomas somewhere out on the icefield where they'd dragged him after the crevasse field took his legs out from under him, she understood it completely.

She listened to the glacier the way she used to listen to hulls.

* * *

Vance came to her an hour after full dark. He'd changed out of his field kit into something cleaner, which was its own kind of message — he dressed for negotiations he intended to win. He set a tin mug of tea on the bench beside her, close enough to drink from if she bent her head like an animal at a trough, and sat down across from her with his hands loose between his knees.

"You should drink that," he said. "Altitude dehydrates you. Dehydration makes people stupid."

"I've been stupid plenty tonight," Coral said. "What's one more mistake."

He almost smiled. That was the worst thing about Vance — he was not a cartoon. He was tired, genuinely tired, and there were lines around his eyes that hadn't been there in the delta, and some traitorous part of her kept cataloguing these details because reading people was how she stayed alive, and reading Vance meant admitting he was a person.

"Your guide is alive," he said. "For now. My medic splinted what he could. The man walked half the Kessel Range on a broken ankle, which tells me something about him." He let that sit. "It also tells me something about you, because nobody carries a man like that down a glacier unless—"

"Don't," Coral said.

"—unless it matters," Vance finished anyway. He picked up the mug and held it out until she took it, because refusing would have cost more than accepting. The tea was terrible. She drank it anyway. "Here's where we are, Okonkwo. The station transmitter works. I've confirmed it with a test burst. In six hours I send a full report, my way, with my framing: survey team lost to weather, deserters recovered, evidence drives secured as classified material pending review. Review meaning a shredder. You know this. I know you know this."

"I know you signed the order," Coral said quietly. "Twelve years ago. My father's file. Your signature."

Something moved behind his face — not guilt. Recognition, maybe, that she'd gotten inside his ledger the way he'd gotten inside hers. "That order was policy," he said. "I executed policy. If you want someone to hate, hate the admiralty that wrote it."

"I do hate them," Coral said. "There's enough of me to go around."

He stood, straightened his jacket, and paused at the door. "Six hours, Okonkwo. Think about your father. Think about whether he'd want you to die on a glacier so a stranger's data could embarrass men who'll be retired by the time anyone reads it." He left. The bolt didn't go home — they needed her mobile for the descent, and besides, where would she run? Onto the ice? In the dark?

Coral set down the mug and looked at her wrists.

The zip tie was military grade. But she had spent eleven years working salvage in black water, where the difference between a tool and a weapon was only ever a question of patience, and patience was the one thing the navy had never managed to take from her. There was a strip of steel shelving bracket loose behind the bench — she'd felt it with her bound hands an hour ago, working it back and forth while Vance talked, the way she'd once worked a seized valve free during a hull inspection while a supervisor shouted at her to hurry. Friction. Heat. Time.

She began to saw.

* * *

The station was a single long room and two closets, built into the rock shoulder below the icefall, its walls lined with equipment racks stripped decades ago of anything portable. A diesel generator chugged in the lean-to out back, feeding the transmitter rack and three bare bulbs. Four of Vance's men: the corporal, a big quiet sergeant named Okafor who had carried Amara's drives without complaint, a radio tech called Mercer who never stopped chewing, and the medic, Reyes-no-relation, who checked Amara twice an hour through the closet door and pretended not to notice she was starving.

Amara was the problem. Not for them — for Coral. Every plan she turned over in her mind required leaving Amara locked in a closet on a glacier that was about to become a firing range of falling ice, and Coral found she could not make herself do it. She thought of the old woman in the unmapped village, saying *Okonkwo* like a wound being pressed. She thought of her father, who had gone to prison for crimes committed by men who filed paperwork. She thought: *I am done being the kind of person who survives by letting other people drown.*

So the plan grew a second stage, and the second stage made everything harder, and she did it anyway.

By the third hour she had sawed through the tie and re-looped it loosely enough to pass inspection. By the fourth, she had eased along the wall to the generator lean-to under cover of Mercer's snoring and found what she needed: the fuel line, a length of copper wire, and the transmitter's grounding strap, which ran through the wall in a conduit older than any of them. She couldn't destroy the transmitter — Vance would check it before his broadcast, and a dead radio would only move up his timeline. But a radio that *worked* and a radio that *worked correctly* were different animals, and Coral Okonkwo had spent her whole life in the gap between those two things.

She loosened the ground connection until it hung by a thread of contact. Under load, under moisture, under the vibration of a glacier shrugging off a hundred thousand tons of calved ice, it would arc. It would spike. It might burn out the final amplifier entirely, or it might simply fill every frequency within a hundred miles with screaming static — either way, whatever Vance transmitted in the next hour would go out garbled, or not at all, or accompanied by a noise so violent that any listening station would flag it for investigation rather than filing.

And if she was lucky — if the mountain cooperated — the interference wouldn't matter at all, because there would be nothing left to transmit *from*.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told Vance, hadn't told anyone, the thing Tomas had taught her with his frostbitten hand sketching shapes in the snow: the station was built into the rock shoulder *below the icefall*, and the icefall was rotten. They had heard it all evening — the deep structural groans, the cracks that didn't echo but *rolled*, the sound of a glacier preparing to shed weight. Calving season was early this year; the pass had opened early; the ice was already going. The station's builders had known it too — that was why the place was abandoned, why the navy had written it off, why the maps marked it with a small printed warning that Vance's team had evidently never read.

The glacier was going to calve. Tonight. She could feel it in the floor the way she'd once felt a torpedo wake through two hundred feet of black water — not hearing it, *knowing* it.

All she had to do was make sure everyone was outside when it happened.

* * *

She got back to the bench with forty minutes to spare and sat with her hands folded over the re-looped tie, doing the arithmetic of human beings. Four soldiers. One prisoner-guide, unconscious in the corner where they'd laid Tomas, his splinted ankle propped on a pack, his breathing shallow but steady. One engineer in a closet. Herself.

The medic woke first, as medics do. He went to check on Amara, and Coral said, quietly, "Corporal. The generator's running rough. I heard it when they brought me back from the latrine. If it dies, your transmitter dies, and I don't think the lieutenant wants to explain that."

The corporal frowned. He was twenty-three and wanted very much to do this correctly. "Stay there."

He went out to the lean-to. Coral counted to sixty, then said, louder, "Sergeant. The corporal's been gone a while. Fuel vapor, maybe. This station's older than his grandfather."

Okafor looked at her for a long moment — a look that said he knew exactly what she was doing and was deciding, consciously, to let it happen — and got up and followed. Later, in the inquiry, Okafor would testify that he'd smelled fuel. Coral would always believe he'd smelled opportunity.

Mercer woke on his own, annoyed, and shuffled out after them, still chewing. Which left the medic, who was kneeling by the closet door with a cup of broth, and Coral, and Tomas breathing in the corner.

"Medic," Coral said. "Your lieutenant wants the prisoner moved. Frostbite protocol — he can't stay on the cold floor, he needs to be somewhere the body heat circulates. Outside shelter, near the generator exhaust, it's warmest there."

"That's not—" the medic started.

"He also said," Coral lied, smooth as silt, "that if the man loses the foot, the lieutenant wants it documented that medical recommended it."

The medic got Tomas up under the arms, grunting, and hauled him toward the door. Coral rose, slipped the tie off her wrist, and stepped into the closet to unlock Amara, pressing a finger to her own lips. Amara's eyes were fever-bright and furious and understanding all at once. Together they came out into the night.

The cold hit like a wall. Above them the icefall stood against the stars, a cliff of blue-black seracs two hundred meters high, and even as Coral looked up, the glacier spoke — a crack like the world's largest tree splitting, rolling down the valley and bouncing off the far walls.

"Move," she said. "Away from the face. Everyone. *Now.*"

The soldiers froze — trained to obey officers, not prisoners. Then Vance came out of the station, buttoning his jacket, and the world chose that moment to answer her.

It did not begin with a bang. It began with a *slump*, a sound like a giant settling into a chair, and the whole face of the icefall leaned forward. Coral grabbed Amara's arm and ran, dragging her downslope, away from the station, away from the ice, and behind her she heard Vance shouting orders that dissolved into the roar.

The glacier calved.

Not a chunk — a *province*. A wall of ice the height of a church broke free along the whole front of the icefall and fell, and the sound was beyond sound, a pressure that punched through her chest and flattened the air itself. The impact wave hit the rock shoulder and the station simply ceased — racks, generator, roof, a hundred years of rust and kerosene, erased under ten thousand tons of falling ice that then, with a slowness that was somehow worse than speed, began to flow.

Coral pulled Amara behind a granite fin and held her there while the white cloud swallowed the world. Snow blast, ice dust, the smell of ancient air released from its prison. Somewhere in the roar she heard men screaming — not dying, she prayed, not dying, just *running* — and then the roar ate everything, minutes of it, the longest minutes of her life, and gradually, gradually, the mountain finished its sentence.

Silence. Real silence, the first in days. Snow drifting down like ash.

Coral lifted her head. Where the station had been there was a new moraine, a raw scar of churned ice and shattered rock steaming faintly in the starlight. The transmitter was under fifty feet of glacier. Vance's report, his framing, his shredder — buried. Her sabotage hadn't even been necessary. The mountain had done what mountains do, and she had merely made sure no one was standing in the way.

Liberation, she thought, tasted exactly like snowmelt and terror.

Then she counted heads. Amara, coughing, alive, gripping her sleeve. The medic, thirty meters down, waving. Okafor, hauling Mercer up out of a drift. The corporal, wide-eyed by the generator's corpse. And Vance, standing apart, staring at the burial mound of his career, turning slowly to find her in the dark.

"CORAL!" 

Not Vance. Smaller, raggeder, from upslope — from the high route along the icefield's edge, the direction they'd *not* come from, the direction of the crevasse field.

Tomas was not with the group. Tomas had not been hauled clear. In the chaos, in the blast, in the minute when everyone had run — he had been left where the medic dropped him, near the door, near the ice.

"TOMAS!" she screamed into the settling white.

Nothing answered but the glacier, groaning, already beginning its next long sentence.
