## 18. White Silence

The world ended at ten meters.

Coral knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat, because she had spent eleven years learning to work inside blindness. Underwater, past a certain depth, light gave up and left you alone with your hands and your gauges and the small animal voice in your skull that said *turn back*. Every diver learned that voice. The good ones learned to answer it: *not yet. One more meter. Check your line.*

She stood at the shattered edge of the relay station's foundation, and there was no line. There was no edge, either — not really. The calving had taken the station's eastern wall, the antenna mast, half the generator shed, and somewhere beneath the roar of it, Tomas. The glacier had shrugged and rewritten the map, and now the icefield beyond stretched out white and featureless in every direction, wrapped so thick in blowing snow that she couldn't see her own boots if she looked down too fast.

The drives were strapped across her chest under her parka, two bricks of data pressed against her sternum like a second heart. Amara's backup drive and the transmitter core, everything that proved what the navy had done to the river, to the village, to her father. Everything Vance wanted buried under a hundred meters of ice.

Everything that had just cost her the only person she'd chosen for herself.

*He's not dead,* she told herself. *You don't know that. You don't get to know that yet.*

Her breath came out and vanished sideways, torn away by wind that sounded like the sea. That was the thing about whiteouts nobody warned you about — they weren't quiet. They roared. The silence was inside them, the way the deep was silent inside its pressure. She pulled her goggles down, checked the rope coiled at her hip, clipped one end to her harness, and stepped off the foundation into nothing.

* * *

She worked it like a search pattern, because that was all she had. Grid squares, compass bearings shouted at herself over the wind, a marker wanded into the snow every twenty meters so she could find her way back to the station's bones. Her father had taught her that much before the disgrace, back when he was still a hero and she was still a girl who sat on the dock counting his dive flags: *the water doesn't care how scared you are, Coral. It cares whether you're methodical.* The ice didn't care either. The ice cared about nothing. That was almost a comfort.

Fifty meters out, she found the antenna mast, snapped clean, lying like a fallen tree. She marked it and moved on.

Eighty meters, a sheet of corrugated roofing, singing against the snow.

A hundred and ten meters, and her left boot punched through a snow bridge into air, and her whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. She froze, weight spread, breathing through her mouth. Below her — far below, or maybe not far at all, sound lied in wind like this — something echoed. A crevasse field. The calving had opened the glacier like a book, and somewhere in those pages was a man with a ruined hand who had crossed this range once already and lost everyone, and who had followed her into the dark twice without being asked.

*Tomas.*

She hauled herself back onto solid snow, lay flat, and crawled until the ground stopped trying to kill her. Then she got up and kept searching, because grief was a current and you could drown in it or swim across it, and she had never once in her life let a current decide for her.

* * *

The practical argument arrived around the second hour, as she'd known it would. It spoke in Vance's voice, because her mind was cruel that way.

*You have the drives. You have a window — maybe hours before his team regroups, days before the pass seals. Every minute you spend crawling around an icefield is a minute closer to him finding you. Drop the search. Come back for him after the storm. He'd tell you to go. He DID tell you to go — "if it goes wrong, you run, Coral, promise me."*

And the other voice, smaller, steadier, hers:

*He promised me first. Back on the river, night three, when I couldn't sleep and he was keeping watch. He said, "Whatever happens up there, I don't leave people in the ice. That's the whole reason they cashiered me, and I'd do it again." So no. I don't leave people in the ice either. Not him. Not ever him.*

Love against every practical argument, and love wasn't even the right word for it anymore, because love was what you felt and this was what you *did*: walking into a roaring white room to look for one warm body in a hundred square kilometers of killing cold, with frostbite whitening the tips of her fingers and the drives against her chest and no plan except the grid.

She found his glove at the two-hour mark.

Just the glove — the big insulated one he wore over the bandaged hand, torn open along the palm seam, frozen stiff, half-buried. Ten meters downslope of it, the snow bridge she'd punched through earlier gaped wider now, edges crumbling, and below it the blue dark went down further than her headlamp could reach.

She clipped her rope to a boulder of ice, tested it with her full weight twice, and went over the edge.

* * *

The crevasse swallowed the wind. That was the first mercy — sudden stillness, her own breath loud, snow sifting down through her lamp beam like static made visible. She descended hand over hand, boots against the wall, counting meters the way she counted fathoms. Fifteen. Twenty. At twenty-five the shaft narrowed and turned, and the rope swung her gently against ice sculpted smooth as blown glass, and she hung there in the blue listening.

"Hello," she said. Her voice cracked on it. She tried again, louder. "TOMAS."

Nothing. Wind above, faint groan of the glacier settling, her own pulse in her ears.

Then — so faint she thought she'd invented it — a scrape. Rhythmic. Deliberate. The sound of someone tapping metal on ice in a pattern, because he was too smart to shout himself hoarse and too stubborn to stop signaling.

Three taps. Pause. Three taps.

She followed it down another eight meters, around a bend in the blue, and her lamp found him.

He was wedged in a shelf of ice like cargo in a hold, one arm cradled against his chest, face gray, lips going the wrong color, snow packed in his collar. His good hand held a carabiner he'd been beating against the wall. When the light hit him he blinked, squinted, and then did the most Tomas thing possible: he looked at her hanging twenty meters up a crevasse in a whiteout, half-frozen, carrying stolen navy property, and he said, hoarse,

"You promised you'd run."

"I lied," Coral said. "Check your line."

His laugh turned into a cough, and the cough turned into something that might have been crying if either of them had time for it. She reached him, got her hands under his arms, and started the long, brutal arithmetic of hauling a grown man up a rope with frostbitten fingers and one working lung's worth of breath. He helped where he could, boots scrabbling, and between pulls he talked — because of course he talked, because talking was how he stayed alive, the same way grids were how she did.

"The station," he managed. "Vance—"

"Took Amara. Took the main drives. Doesn't know about the backup." Pull. Rest. Pull. "Doesn't know we're alive. Probably thinks the glacier solved his problem."

"He'll come back. He'll sweep the field."

"Then we won't be here when he does." She locked her legs, braced, hauled another meter. Above them, through the narrowing slot of the crevasse mouth, the whiteout churned — but lower down, in the blue, she could see it thinning. The wind was dropping. Storms in the Kessel burned fast; Amara had told them that. Hours, maybe less, and the sky would open, and open sky meant visibility, and visibility meant Vance's recovery team would be able to see two figures crossing an icefield from kilometers away.

Which meant they couldn't cross the field. Which meant—

"Tomas." She got her shoulder under him at last, rolled him onto the surface, knelt over him checking his pulse, his breathing, the blackening tips of his bandaged hand. "The crevasse shelter. Your old route. You said the survey team used them — snow caves, stocked caches. How far?"

He focused on her with effort, and she watched him do the math and hate the answer. "West ridge. Four kilometers. Through the field." His eyes went to the sky, to the thinning white. "But Coral — if the weather clears, we're targets. And if it doesn't clear, we're dead. Either way we move *now*, and either way—" he flexed the ruined hand, winced, "—I can't make four kilometers fast. Maybe not at all without help."

"Then I'll carry you," she said. "I've carried worse. I carried my father's name for eleven years."

Something moved through his exhausted face — grief for her, and pride, and the particular tenderness of a man watching a woman decide to be stronger than the situation deserves. "That's not romantic," he rasped.

"No," she agreed, hauling him upright, getting his arm across her shoulders, feeling the drives shift against her chest like a promise. "It's load-bearing."

They started west, into the thinning storm, toward the ridge, toward the caches, toward the long descent and the village that remembered and the broadcast that would end everything. Behind them the glacier groaned and settled over the wrecked station, keeping its secrets a little longer.

Ahead, far off through the shredding cloud, Coral saw it first because she saw everything first: a point of light on the western ridge. Steady. Artificial. Not a star.

Someone had reached the cache ahead of them.

Tomas followed her gaze and went very still against her side. "That's not ours," he said quietly. "Nobody camps on the ridge in a whiteout unless they're waiting for someone."

Coral shifted the drives higher under her parka, tightened her grip on the man she refused to lose, and kept walking toward the light.
