## 19. The Long Descent

Tomas woke to the sound of his own breathing and nothing else, which in the high ice was either mercy or a warning.

The crevasse shelter was a blue throat of light above him — the tarp he'd dragged across the gap at the last moment before the world went white, weighted with his pack and two blocks of sawn snow. He'd been awake when the calving came. He'd felt it through the soles of his boots first, a deep organ note rolling up from the glacier's belly, then the crack like the sky splitting along a seam, and then Coral's voice on the wind, screaming his name in a way he'd never heard anyone say it.

He'd answered. He was almost sure he'd answered. But sound lied on the icefield; it bent around seracs and died in the whiteout, and after that there had been only the storm and the slow, animal business of staying alive: digging into the crevasse's lip where the wind couldn't reach, rigging the tarp by feel, sealing himself into a pocket of still air no bigger than a coffin and twice as cold.

Now the light above had gone from black to grey to a pale, watery gold, and the storm had spent itself.

"Tomas." His own voice, testing. It worked. His throat worked. His hands —

He looked at them. The right one was bad. The frostbite that had started as an insult on the ascent had become, over the storm hours, a kind of argument, and his hand had lost it. The fingers were waxy, bloodless, curled like something long dead. He could feel them the way you feel a limb in a dream — present, insistent, useless.

He didn't let himself think about what that meant. There would be time for that lower down, where things grew. Up here, thinking about it was a luxury that cost warmth.

"Coral," he said again, louder, and the crevasse threw his voice back at him in a chorus of Tomases, all of them alone.

* * *

Getting out took forty minutes and cost him most of what he had. He climbed the rope he'd anchored before the storm — some part of him, the old guide part, the part Vance's navy had cashiered and the mountain had never managed to kill, had done that much — and hauled himself over the lip into a world scoured flat and blinding.

The sun sat low and hard in a sky rinsed clean. The icefield stretched south, broken now, transformed. Where yesterday there had been a route — his route, the line he'd read off the glacier's grain like a sentence — there was a field of wreckage. Seracs toppled into each other like drunks. A crevasse that had been a crack was now a canyon. And down-slope, maybe four hundred meters, a scar of shattered blue ice marked where the calving face had let go and taken the relay station's whole flank of the mountain with it.

The station was gone. Not damaged — *gone*, swallowed, its antenna a bent tooth sticking out of the debris cone like a fossil.

And somewhere in that wreckage or out on this white plain was Coral Okonkwo, who had sabotaged a lieutenant's radio and pulled a glacier down on both their heads, who had chosen strangers over her father's name, who had kissed him once in a cave full of storm with fuel running out, and who was now either dead or waiting for him to stop standing here admiring the view.

"OKONKWO!" He put everything into it. The word flew out over the ice and came back thin.

Nothing.

He started downslope, zigzagging, calling every thirty seconds, counting between calls because counting kept the panic in its cage. The right hand he tucked inside his jacket against his chest. The left he used for the axe. The debris field slowed him to a crawl — ice blocks the size of trucks, tumbled and refrozen into a maze, shadowed slots between them deep enough to swallow a person whole. He searched the shadows. He hated searching the shadows.

It was nearly an hour later, sun climbing, when he heard it.

Not a voice. A whistle. Two notes, low-high, repeated — the old survey signal, the one every guide in the range learned before they learned to tie a bowline. *Alive. Here. Alive.*

Tomas stood still in the middle of the ruin and let the sound walk through him like current.

Then he whistled back — low-high, low-high — and started toward it at the fastest speed the ice allowed, which was not fast enough, would never be fast enough, and did not care.

* * *

She was in a hollow between two collapsed seracs, sitting upright with her back against the ice, and for one lurching second Tomas thought she was dead, because she wasn't moving, wasn't shivering, and people on the edge of freezing stop doing both.

Then she turned her head and looked at him, and her eyes were clear and furious and the most beautiful thing he had ever seen on a glacier.

"You're alive," she said. Her voice was scraped raw from shouting. "You absolute — I dug through that collapse field for six hours. Six. I was about to start saying a eulogy."

"I was underground."

"I know where you were. I found your tarp." She held it up, balled in her fist, like evidence. "I thought — " She stopped. Started again, quieter, in the flat voice she used when the feeling underneath was too big to say straight. "I thought I'd carried those drives three hundred kilometers for nothing."

Tomas lowered himself down beside her, joints cracking, and leaned until his shoulder touched hers. For a while neither of them said anything. The sun moved. Somewhere far below, the glacier ticked and settled, doing its bookkeeping.

"The station?" she finally asked.

"Gone. Vance's team was on the ice side when it let go. Either they're buried or they're walking out the long way." He paused. "Amara?"

"With me when it hit. We got separated in the whiteout. She had the backup drive on a cord around her neck." Coral patted her own chest, and Tomas felt relief move through him like thaw water — Amara Diallo, half-starved and stubborn as a burr, was the whole case now, the living witness, the voice that could say *they scuttled it, I watched them do it* in front of an inquiry. "We agreed on a rally point if we split. Lower valley, first green water. She'll make it. She made it nine weeks on that slope eating half-rations and glacier moss."

"And the drives?"

Coral reached into her jacket and drew out the case — dented, scorched at one corner, but sealed. Amara's backup, plus the copies Coral had made in the relay station's dying minutes while the glacier groaned overhead like something waking up angry. Everything the navy had drowned a village for. Everything her father had been framed to bury.

"It's all here," she said. "Every reading. Every order. Vance's signature."

Tomas looked at the case, small and dirty in her frost-nipped hands, holding more weight than the glacier around them.

"Then we get it off the mountain," he said.

* * *

They descended.

There was no elegant way to describe it and Tomas, who had written route reports for fifteen years, declined to try. The calving had destroyed the upper icefall; the lower valley, though, had opened early — the same warm snap that had loosed the glacier had set the river running, and where there was running water there was a way down. They followed the melt. It began as a trickle underfoot, widened to a stream, became by afternoon a proper torrent running milky with rock flour, loud enough to talk over, which suited them both, because talking cost breath and breath cost heat and they were operating on margins now.

His hand got worse. He knew it without looking — the dead weight of it swinging at the end of his arm, the way the cold had stopped hurting, which was the worst sign of all. By midday he'd stopped pretending to use it. By late afternoon Coral noticed.

She didn't make a speech. She just dropped back beside him, took his left arm, slung it over her shoulders, and adjusted his pack straps with two efficient tugs so the weight rode better, and then they walked like that, hip to hip, down a valley that was coming apart into spring around them.

"When we're down," she said, after a kilometer of silence, "you're seeing a doctor. A real one. Not a navy one."

"If there's anything left to save."

"There will be." No doubt in it at all. "You kept me alive in that cave. You don't get to lose pieces of yourself to a little weather."

"A little weather," Tomas repeated. "That glacier tried to eat us."

"It failed." She glanced up at him, and there it was — the ghost of the crooked smile, the one that surfaced rarely and meant everything. "We're extremely annoying to eat."

They camped that night in the lee of a moraine, melting snow over a stove the size of a teacup, sharing one ration bar cut into precise halves because Coral measured everything, even generosity. Above them the peaks burned orange and then violet and then went out. Below them, far down, the valley floor showed a thread of darker green — trees, actual trees, the first since the foothills. Tomorrow they'd reach the treeline. The day after, the river broadened into the delta country, and the delta country held a village that wasn't on any chart, and a woman whose name Coral hadn't told him yet but whose recognition of her surname had changed something fundamental in the set of her shoulders.

"Tell me about her," Tomas said, nodding down-valley. "The old woman. You've been carrying her since the convoy passed. Time to put her down."

Coral was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "Her name is Ngozi. She looked at me on the riverbank like she was looking at a ghost, and she said my family name like it belonged to this river. Like Okonkwo was a thing that grew here." She turned the tin cup slowly in her good hand. "My father told me he grew up coastal. Fishing town. He told me a lot of things, Tomas. I'm starting to understand how many of them were assigned to him instead of true."

"And if Ngozi knew him? Before?"

"Then I was never his daughter in the way I thought. I was his *debt*. Someone else's crime, inherited, filed under my name before I could walk." She looked at him across the stove flame, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "But it also means someone here remembers him as a person. A boy on this river. And that's worth more than a pardon. That's worth knowing who the hell I actually am."

Tomas reached over with his left hand — the working one — and covered hers.

"Then we go meet her," he said.

* * *

The third day the valley opened its arms.

The treeline came up to meet them — stunted birch first, then pine, then a green so dense and ordinary after the ice that Tomas actually laughed out loud, a rusty sound that startled birds out of the branches. The river ran wide and brown-green and alive. His hand had begun, horribly, to hurt again, which Coral informed him was the best news she'd heard in a week, pain meaning blood, blood meaning fight. They wrapped it in the last clean bandage. He learned to hold a trekking pole with it anyway, out of spite.

They were following the river through a bend of tall grass when Coral stopped so suddenly he walked into her.

Smoke. Thin, domestic, woodsmoke — rising from behind the tree screen ahead. And beneath it, faint on the wind, another smell: fish drying, and woodsmoke, and something green growing.

Then figures. Three of them, emerging from the grass with spears held low but not friendly, and the tallest of them, an older man with a face like weathered rope, stared at the two of them — the frostbitten man leaning on a woman, both of them gaunt and ice-burned and staggering — and then his eyes went to Coral's face and the spear point dropped.

"Ngozi," he called back over his shoulder, in a language Tomas didn't know but understood completely. "Ngozi! Come! It's the river's daughter — she's come home!"

From somewhere in the village, a voice answered, old and strong.

And Coral Okonkwo, salvage diver, debtor, deserter, invisible woman, stood very still in the tall grass with a case of stolen truth pressed against her heart, and began, helplessly, to cry.
