## 20. The Village Remembers

Amara Diallo had spent eleven days learning to walk again before she understood that the village had been teaching her something else entirely.

Her legs were the least of it. Eleven days of broth and river fish, of Coral Okonkwo hauling her upright by the armpits like a salvage winch, of Tomas Reyes walking her along the bank in slow circuits while his bandaged hand hung at his side like something he'd stopped arguing with. Her body had come back. What hadn't come back — what she was beginning to suspect might never come back on its own — was her certainty about who would listen.

She had carried data for four hundred kilometers. Cores, water assays, chain-of-custody logs copied twice onto drives wrapped in waxed cloth against her sternum, the way you'd carry a child through a fire. She had rehearsed the sentence she would say when she reached help: *The navy scuttled the Meridian Survey vessel to conceal evidence of contamination originating from an unauthorized extraction site above the Kessel headwaters.* Clean language. Language that survived cross-examination.

But here, in a village that did not exist on any chart, the old woman named Ngozi had taken one look at the assay figures Amara spread on a woven mat and said, not *who do we report this to*, but: "That is why the fish left."

And then she had begun to count.

* * *

Ngozi counted on her fingers first, then on stones, laying them out in rows on the mat beside the printouts as though the two systems were translations of each other. One stone for the dry season the reeds browned from the roots up. One for the year the children's gums bled. One for the man who dove for salvage and came up coughing rust-colored water until he died in his sleep with his lungs full of what the river had put there.

"That one," Ngozi said, tapping a stone, "was called Joubert."

The mat went very quiet.

Amara looked up. Coral was standing at the edge of the shelter, half in shadow, the way she stood everywhere — a woman built out of the habit of not being seen. But her face had gone still in a way Amara recognized from the icefield, from the moment on the glacier when Coral had chosen the survivors over the proof. Not surprise. Recognition arriving late, with interest.

"My father," Coral said.

"He dove our dead out of the water when no one else would touch them," Ngozi said. "Then your navy took him." She said it without accusation, which made it worse. She said it the way you'd read a ledger entry. "They said he falsified his salvage records. They said the poison in the river was his doing — that he'd dumped drums. We knew it was a lie. The drums came down from the mountains, not up from the delta. But who listens to us? We are not on the map."

"Who listens now?" Amara asked, because someone had to ask it, and because she had learned on the mountain that the question you avoid is the question that owns you.

Ngozi gathered the stones into her palm and closed her fist around them.

"Now," she said, "you listen. And you write."

* * *

What happened over the next three days, Amara would later describe — in testimony, in the inquiry, in the single interview she ever gave — as the conversion of data into witness. It was not a process she had been trained for. Her training said evidence spoke for itself. Her training was wrong, and the village taught her how wrong in the space of an afternoon.

It began with names.

Every assay reading Amara had carried over the range corresponded to a season, and every season corresponded to people. The heavy-metal spike in the mid-water column samples: that was the year the fishing nets came up light and the traders stopped coming, because nobody buys fish from a river that smells wrong. The sediment core showing the extraction site's tailings signature: that was the year the well water turned sweet in a way that wasn't right, and three households moved inland and were fined by district officers for squatting on land they'd farmed for generations. The chain-of-custody log proving the survey team had flagged all of it and been ordered to stand down: that was the night, Ngozi said, that the navy boats came through with their lights off, and the next morning the channel markers had been moved, and a boy who swam out to check them never came home.

"You have numbers," Ngozi said, pressing her flat palm down on the printouts. "Numbers are ghosts. I will give them bodies."

She summoned them. That was the only word for it. An old woman with a bad hip sent word upriver and downriver through channels no military signal officer could have mapped, and people came. They came in dugouts and on foot, in ones and twos and then in groups of ten, and they sat in the long shelter where Amara had slept her first week, and they told what they knew while Amara wrote it down and Coral, unasked, began to organize it the way she organized everything — silently, precisely, without wasting a motion.

A woman named Bintu who had kept the village's birth records for thirty years, in a school exercise book, because the district office had never once sent anyone to register a birth here. A former deckhand off a supply barge who described, in the flat voice of a man reciting cargo manifests, the drums he'd helped load at the extraction site and the naval escort that met the barge below the gorge. Two men who had been paid to burn a stretch of riverbank after "an accident," and who had never spent the money because spending it felt like signing something.

Tomas sat through all of it with his ruined hand cradled in his lap, and Amara watched him do the thing he was actually good at, better than guiding, maybe: listening without flinching. When the deckhand's voice broke on the word *escort*, Tomas waited out the silence without filling it. When the two men who'd burned the bank couldn't look at anyone while they talked, Tomas looked at them steadily enough for everyone.

"You understand what this is," he said to Amara on the second evening, quietly, outside, under a sky going violet behind the range they'd crossed. "This isn't a village giving testimony. This is a village deciding to be on the record. There's a difference, and it cost them something to make it."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've watched you write their names down like coordinates. You're good at it. You're so good at it that I want to make sure you know the difference too."

Amara looked at him. His face was wind-scoured and half-healed and utterly without cruelty, which was its own kind of weapon.

"The difference being?"

"Coordinates don't get arrested," Tomas said. "Names do. Every person in that shelter just agreed to be findable. For years the navy's whole strategy was that these people didn't exist — no chart, no registry, no record. Now they exist. You're holding the paper that makes them exist. Don't you dare treat that like cargo."

She thought about the drives against her sternum. Four hundred kilometers of treating them like cargo. Of keeping them dry, warm, hidden. Of nearly dying — of watching others nearly die — for their safe delivery.

"You're right," she said.

"I usually am," Tomas said. "It's my worst quality."

* * *

On the third day, Coral brought out the transmitter.

Amara had known it was there — the black-box unit from the scuttled Meridian, recovered from the wreck, carried over the Kessel Range in a dive bag like the most dangerous piece of luggage in the world. She had assumed it was evidence. Chain of custody, provenance, the physical anchor of the whole case.

Coral set it on the mat between the stones and the exercise books and the assay printouts, and said to Ngozi: "This is what they sank a ship to hide. And underneath what it recorded, there's a second signal. A distress call. From the expedition your river leads to. From us."

"It still works?" Ngozi asked.

"It works," Coral said. "It has always worked. That was the problem."

Amara felt the shape of it before Coral said it, the way you feel weather change. The transmitter could broadcast. Not just record — transmit, on an open channel, anything they loaded into it. The findings. The testimony. The names. Not sealed into an inquiry file that could be buried by whatever remained of Vance's chain of command. Not handed to an authority that had already demonstrated, repeatedly, catastrophically, what it did with inconvenient truth. Broadcast. Public. Irretrievable.

"They'll hear it coming," Amara said. "Anyone with a receiver within range. Including whoever Vance reports to. Including Vance, if he's alive."

"He's alive," Coral said, with the certainty of a diver who knows what the water holds. "Men like Vance float."

"And when they hear it—"

"When they hear it, it will already be true in public," Coral said. "There's a difference between evidence and a fact. Evidence can be lost. Facts have to be survived." She looked down at the transmitter, and Amara saw her hand rest on it lightly, the way you'd touch a door you'd decided to open. "My father spent nine years being a lie the navy told about itself. I'm done handing things to people who bury them."

Ngozi picked up her stones, one by one, and pressed them into Amara's hand.

"Then we are ready," the old woman said. "But not tonight. Tonight we finish the record. If you are going to speak for a river, you speak for all of it."

* * *

They worked until well past dark. Amara transcribed; Bintu verified dates against her exercise book; Coral cross-referenced the testimony against the assay readings with a precision that made Amara revise her private estimate of the woman upward yet again. Somewhere past midnight, Coral paused over a page and said, without looking up:

"The deckhand. The one who loaded the drums. Ask him if he remembers the escort commander's name."

"Why?"

"Because I remember mine," Coral said. "Vance signed my father's transfer order. I've seen the number. If the same signature shows up on the escort manifest—" She stopped. Amara watched her decide how much to say, the old invisibility flickering and then, deliberately, being set aside. "If it's the same signature, then this was never a cover-up that got out of hand. It was policy. And policy has authors."

In the morning, the deckhand remembered. Lieutenant-Commander, he thought, or maybe Commander by then. Cold eyes, clean uniform, never gave a name but signed the manifest himself because he trusted no one else's handwriting.

Amara wrote the date down. Then she pulled the escort log fragment she'd salvaged from the Meridian's records — the one page she'd grabbed almost at random, in the dark, with the ship going down around her — and laid it beside the transcription.

Same signature block. Same initials. Same man.

"Oh," Amara said softly. "Oh, you arrogant bastard. You signed it."

* * *

They gathered at dawn on the bank, the whole village that could stand, and the ones who couldn't standing anyway, held up by their families. Ngozi stood at the front with her fist full of stones. Coral knelt at the transmitter with the drives connected and the antenna rigged on a salvaged mast, and Amara stood beside her holding the consolidated record — testimony, data, signatures, names — in a format that would survive being heard rather than read, because Coral had insisted they write it to be listened to. Short sentences. Plain words. Names spoken slowly.

"One pass," Coral said. "We get one broadcast before they triangulate us. So we make it count."

"And after?" Amara asked. "After they come?"

Coral looked out at the river — the wide, brown, patient river that had taken her father's name and given it back poisoned — and then at the line of villagers on the bank, and something in her face settled into place like ballast.

"After they come," she said, "they'll be coming for witnesses. All of them. In daylight. On the record." She keyed the transmitter. "Let them."

The power light went green. The carrier wave went out across the water, invisible, unstoppable, carrying the first name — Ngozi's — toward every receiver in the delta.

Amara closed her eyes and listened to a village become a fact.

* * *

Forty kilometers downstream, in a navy recovery skiff running dark with its radios live, Lieutenant Vance heard the first thirty seconds of the broadcast before his communications officer, hands shaking, cut the receive channel — and understood, with the cold clarity of a man watching his own ledger catch fire, that cutting the channel changed nothing at all.

He had already heard his own name.
