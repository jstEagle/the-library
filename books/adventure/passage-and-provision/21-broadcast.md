## 21. Broadcast

The wreck still sat where the river had put it, half-buried in silt at the delta's edge, her hull split along the survey deck like a fruit that had ripened too long underwater. Coral had been the one to find her four months ago. Coral had been the one sent down into her dark to pull out a transmitter and told never to ask what it said.

Now she stood on the survey deck itself, boots on plating that hadn't felt air since the navy's cranes dropped it here under cover of a fuel-spill story, and she was asking every question at once.

The transmitter came out of its housing easier than she expected. Amara Diallo held the light — a diver's torch, borrowed from Coral's own kit, because everything they carried now had come out of that kit or off Tomas's back or out of Amara's stubborn, half-starved hands. The drive sat beside the transmitter in a waterproof case scarred from the glacier, from the crevasse, from Vance's interrogation room where Amara had spent eleven hours saying nothing.

"You're sure about the power," Amara said. Not a question, really. She'd asked twice already.

"The wreck's auxiliary bank is intact. I checked it when I pulled the box." Coral's hands moved without hurry, seating connectors, testing grounds. Underwater you learned that hurry killed. Everything above water since had tried to teach her otherwise, and she had refused the lesson. "Four hours of charge, maybe five. Enough."

"Enough for what?" Tomas stood at the rail, watching the river. His bandaged hand hung at his side, two fingers gone to frostbite, three saved by Coral's refusal to let him sleep through the worst night of the descent. He didn't look at them while he talked. He'd taken up the habit of watching approaches — the river road, the tree line, the bend where a patrol boat would come from if one came. "Vance knows we're here. He knew before we left the relay station. That gives him—" he glanced at the sky, judging the light the way he judged weather, "—three hours. Maybe less if he pushed his people down the valley."

"Then we use three of them," Coral said.

She had imagined this moment for weeks, in fragments, usually at altitude with her hands too cold to feel the thought properly. In the imaginings it was always loud. A speech. A declaration. Her father's name spoken into the wind until the wind carried it to every desk in the naval administration.

What it actually was: a woman kneeling on a dead ship's deck, splicing a power lead with numb fingers, while an engineer read data aloud in a flat voice because flat voices were how Amara kept from screaming.

"Concentrate sample, third tributary, downstream of the Kessel mine works," Amara read. "Mercury at forty times safe threshold. Arsenic at sixty. Sediment cores show contamination beginning fourteen years ago." She paused. "That's the year your father was arrested."

"I know what year it was." Coral stripped the wire end with her teeth. "I've known what year it was my whole life."

* * *

The old woman's name was Nkem, and she had come down to the wreck at dawn with six others from the village that wasn't on any chart. They stood now at the rail's broken end, wrapped against the delta damp, watching Coral work with an attention that felt like a hand on the back of her neck.

Nkem had recognized the surname before Coral had finished saying it. *Okonkwo.* She'd said it twice, tasting it, and then she'd said something in the village tongue that made the others go quiet, and then she'd looked at Coral the way you look at a door you'd given up believing would open.

"My father never came here," Coral had told her. "Whatever he did, it wasn't here."

"He came here," Nkem said. "Before. When the sickness started. He was a diver then, not yet a criminal. He took samples from our water because no one else would come." She'd folded her hands. "He wrote down what he found. Then men came and took his notes, and took him, and the river stayed sick, and we stayed off the maps. You have his hands."

Coral had looked at her own hands, holding a soldering iron over a dead ship's guts, and understood for the first time that her talent for invisibility had been inherited — that her father had learned to be unseen the same way she had, in the same water, for the same reason. Because being seen, in this river, was what got you erased.

Now Nkem watched her splice the lead, and Coral worked like someone whose name was about to matter.

* * *

The transmitter woke with a hum Coral remembered from the dive — the same box, the same casing, the same faint vibration through her gloves. But down there it had been a thing to be extracted, sealed, delivered. Up here, fed by the wreck's dying batteries and pointed at a sky full of morning, it was something else entirely.

"Open channel," Amara said. "No encryption. If I encrypt it, it can be suppressed as classified material before anyone reads it. Open channel means it goes out raw, and every station in range hears it live, and once it's heard it can't be unheard."

"That's the point," Coral said.

"It's also the trap." Tomas turned from the rail at last. "The moment you key that mic, every receiver Vance has lights up. He'll know exactly where we are. He won't send a message this time. He'll come himself."

"Good," Coral said.

Tomas blinked. In all their weeks together — the foothills, the storm cave, the ice bridge, the whiteout where she'd crawled on her belly calling his name into wind that ate sound — she wasn't sure she'd ever seen him genuinely surprised.

"Good?"

"He's been hunting us across a mountain range to keep this quiet. Quiet is his whole strategy. So we stop being quiet." She tested the connection. The hum deepened. "He can't arrest a broadcast, Tomas. He can only arrest people. And there are witnesses here now." She nodded toward Nkem and the others. "And a survivor with documented evidence. And a signal going out on an open channel that his own superiors monitor. He buried this once by controlling who spoke. We're taking the speaking away from him."

Amara had the drives cabled to the transmitter's data port. She looked up at Coral. "Once I start the upload, it runs until the battery dies or the file finishes. No stopping it. No editing."

"No editing," Coral agreed. "All of it. The mine, the poison levels, the scuttling order, the village mortality records, the framing of the divers." She paused. "My father's case file. Whatever Amara recovered of it."

Amara's face did something complicated. "There are things in that file that will hurt to hear."

"There are things in that file I've been hearing my whole life. At least these ones will be true."

* * *

They broadcast at 0940, because Amara insisted on waiting for the ionospheric window and Coral insisted on letting her, because if you were going to burn your life down you might as well do it competently.

The first voice on the open channel was Amara's. She introduced herself by name, rank, expedition designation, and date of presumed loss — a list that made the word *presumed* sound like an accusation. Then she read. She read for forty minutes. Concentration tables. Core samples. Chain-of-custody logs from the mining operation, which had never been a mining operation at all but a navy extraction program run through three shell contractors. The scuttling order, with its authorization code, which Amara read digit by digit so no one could claim a transcription error. The medical records from the delta villages — not just the unmapped one; there were four, and all four had been struck from the survey charts in the same administrative week fourteen years ago.

Then Coral took the microphone.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that with a kind of distant curiosity, the way she noticed current strength at depth — information, nothing more.

"This is Coral Okonkwo. Salvage diver, Naval Service indenture number 7734-K, daughter of Emeka Okonkwo, convicted of falsifying water-quality reports on this river fourteen years ago." She let the number sit in the air. Numbers were how the navy knew you; she had learned to make numbers work both ways. "I recovered the transmitter you're hearing this on from the survey vessel *Kestrel's Wake*, which the official record lists as lost to grounding. It was scuttled. I have seen the order. I have dived her hull, and I can tell you where the charges were set, because I'm the one who found them."

Beside her, Nkem closed her eyes. One of the villagers translated in a low murmur for those who wanted it.

"My father reported mercury in this river. His reports were accurate. They were buried, and he was buried with them, and the debt they charged against me was built on the crime committed against him." She looked at the drive, still uploading, its indicator light pulsing like a slow heartbeat. "Everything I've said is verifiable. Everything Dr. Diallo has said is documented. We are broadcasting from the wreck of the *Kestrel's Wake* in the Kessel delta, and we will remain here, in the open, until this transmission is answered by someone with the authority to act on it."

She set the microphone down in its cradle and stepped back, and the transmitter went on humming, sending her voice's aftermath out into the morning.

* * *

It took fifty-one minutes for the answer to come, and it came from the wrong direction first.

Tomas saw the boat. A navy patrol hull, running fast up the delta channel, bow spray high — and standing in her well deck, unmistakable even at distance, Lieutenant Vance, field coat buttoned to the throat, doing arithmetic with his eyes. Counting witnesses. Counting exits. Counting, Coral thought, the seconds left in his career and looking for somewhere to spend them.

"He'll want the drives," Amara said quietly. "Or he'll want us dead and the drives 'recovered damaged.' Depends what kind of morning he's having."

"He's not having a good one." Coral was watching the boat's wake, and something about it bothered her. "Look at his speed. He's not approaching to board. He's approaching to talk. Fast, but he's showing us his gun crew with their weapons stowed. That's not a raid posture."

"Why would—"

The transmitter crackled.

Not static. A voice. Formal, clipped, carrying the particular flat authority of someone reading from a prepared statement because improvisation has been forbidden to them.

"All stations, all stations, this is Rear Admiral Osei, Office of the Judge Advocate, Naval Administration. This channel is now under inquiry jurisdiction. Lieutenant Darius Vance, if you are receiving, you are directed to hold position, disarm your detachment, and prepare to be taken into custody pending investigation. Lieutenant Vance. Acknowledge."

On the patrol boat, visible even at this distance, a man in a field coat stopped moving.

Coral picked up the microphone. Her heart was going hard now, finally, now that it was allowed to.

"Lieutenant Vance is receiving," she said. "I can see him. He's about nine hundred meters downstream, and he looks like a man who's just heard his own authorization code read back to him on an open channel."

A pause on the line. When Osei spoke again, there was something almost human under the formality. "Diver Okonkwo. Your transmission was received in full. All of it. By more listeners than Lieutenant Vance ever imagined existed." Another pause. "Your father's case has been reopened as of oh-six-hundred this morning. Stay where you are. Help is coming up the delta. And Okonkwo — the inquiry will want your testimony. All of it. In your own words."

"In my own words," Coral repeated, and found that after a lifetime of the navy putting words in her mouth — orders, debts, denials — she had plenty saved up.

Downstream, the patrol boat slowed. Through Amara's salvaged binoculars they watched it happen: Vance arguing with his own coxswain, then with a lieutenant junior grade who had apparently received the same broadcast everyone else had, then standing alone in his well deck as his own crew — his own carefully chosen, carefully compromised crew — began, one by one, to look anywhere but at him.

"He's calculating," Tomas said. "Even now. Watch. He's deciding whether to run for the border channels."

"He won't run," Coral said. "Running is a confession, and he believes to his bones that he committed no crime. He signed papers. He followed orders. He'll stand there and insist on procedure right up until they put the cuffs on." She lowered the binoculars. "That's the thing about men like Vance. They think the ledger protects them. They never understand that someday someone reads it aloud."

The patrol boat came about, slow and obedient, and held station nine hundred meters off, exactly as directed, its lieutenant standing very straight on his own deck like a man awaiting a verdict he had convinced himself would go his way.

The transmitter hummed. The drive's light pulsed. Out past the delta bar, the open sea waited with the patience of all large indifferent things, and somewhere beyond it, in offices Coral had never seen, people were listening to her father's name spoken without shame for the first time in fourteen years.

Nkem touched Coral's arm. Said something soft in the village tongue. The translator murmured: *"She says the river heard you. Now everyone has."*

Coral looked north, upriver, toward the mountains already fading behind the haze — toward a pass that would close within the week, a station that no longer existed, a crevasse she'd crawled into after a man everyone practical would have written off.

"Admiral Osei," she said into the microphone, "before your people arrive — there were survivors beyond the Kessel Range besides Dr. Diallo. Three more, sheltering at the lower valley camp. They'll need pickup before the weather closes. And there's a guide down there who lost two fingers getting your evidence off that mountain." She smiled, and it reached her voice. "His name is Tomas Reyes. Write it down. He's owed a pardon too — and I intend to collect."

The acknowledgment came back crisp and immediate, and Coral Okonkwo — invisible woman, debtor's daughter, deserter — stood on the wreck they didn't name and gave the orders instead.
