## 1. Ten Thousand Hours of Sky

There are two ways to measure a life. You can count the years, which is what gravestones do, or you can count the miles, which is what I do. Years lie to you. They pile up whether you've done anything or not, like snow on a roof you never asked for. Miles are honest. A mile is a mile is a mile, and you flew it or you didn't, and the fuel gauge doesn't care how old you are.

I have flown roughly one million four hundred thousand of them, give or take the ones I've tried not to think about. That works out to about fifty-six times around the equator, though I've never once crossed the equator, so the math is a lie of a different kind. It works out, more usefully, to ten thousand hours of sky, which is the number the aviation authorities care about, and about nine thousand nine hundred hours of sky where nothing went wrong, which is the number I care about.

The other hundred hours I keep in a separate ledger, the way you keep a scar in a separate place from your good memories. Not because I'm ashamed of them. Because they're the only hours that taught me anything worth knowing, and I like to know where the lessons live.

* * *

The morning I'm telling you about started the way most of my mornings start, which is to say with a fuel sample cup, a flashlight held in my teeth, and a quiet argument with a carburetor that had opinions of its own.

"Clear," I told it, after the third sample came back free of water. "You hear that? Clear. Let's keep it that way."

The carburetor, being a carburetor, said nothing. This is one of the things I like about engines. They don't talk back, they don't ask where you're from, and when they do have something to say, they say it early and loudly enough that you can land before it becomes a conversation with the ground.

My plane is a de Havilland Beaver, 1957, tail number Charlie-Foxtrot-Yankee, and her name is the *Marguerite*, after a woman I have never explained to anyone and never will. She has a fabric patch on the left wing the size of a dinner plate where a heron once expressed its opinion about my altitude, and she burns fuel the way a bishop burns candles—ceremonially, continuously, and with the vague suggestion that you should be grateful for the privilege.

I loaded her in the dark at the Rivergate strip, which is a generous word for a thousand feet of gravel with a windsock that's been flying at half-mast since the Trudeau administration. Medicine in the forward bay, because medicine rides up front where I can keep an eye on it: penicillin in waxed boxes, insulin in a cooler I'd frozen overnight, tetanus boosters for the school at Kettle Landing where a kid had found a nail with his foot the way kids find nails with their feet everywhere, always, since the invention of nails and feet. Mail in the middle. A crate of engine parts for Walt Bricker's sawmill, which Walt had described on the phone as "small" and which was, in fact, the size of a dishwasher.

"Walt," I'd said, "you said small."

"It's a small part of a big machine," Walt had said, with the serene confidence of a man who has never once carried his own freight.

I wedged it in behind the mail and told the *Marguerite* we'd both survive this, and by the time the sun came up over the ridge to the east—coming up the way it does up here, all at once, like a lamp switched on behind the trees—I was airborne and pointed north.

* * *

Here is the philosophy, since you've asked, or since I've decided you've asked, which in a cockpit alone at six in the morning amounts to the same thing.

Most people live their years in place. They wake in the same bed, drive the same road, sit in the same chair, and the years accumulate around them like dust. That's a fine life. I'm not knocking it. Some of the best people I know have never gone anywhere, and they know their own kitchens the way I know weather.

But I live my life in miles. I wake up in one place and I am, by lunch, a different person in a different place, and the person I was at breakfast is already dissolving behind me at a hundred and thirty miles an hour. There's a freedom in that that I'd die for, and I mean that with some precision, because there are men in this business who did die for it, and every one of them would tell you the same thing if the ground hadn't interrupted.

The miles add up to something the years never could. A year is just weather happening at you. A mile is a decision. You chose the heading. You chose the altitude. You read the sky and you committed, and the mile came true under your wheels because you made it come true. Multiply enough of those and you get a life that is, at minimum, *yours*—assembled by your own hands out of your own judgments, every hour of it accounted for.

That's the sales pitch, anyway. The one I give myself.

The fine print is this: a life measured in miles has no fixed address, and a man with no fixed address eventually has to ask himself what he's hiding from, and a man who asks that question honestly is in trouble, because the honest answer, in my case, has a name and a town and a family attached to it, none of which I have visited in nineteen years.

But that's fine print. Mornings like this one, the fine print stays fine.

* * *

Kettle Landing came up out of the mist at 7:40, one gravel bar among gravel bars, distinguished from its neighbors by a church, a schoolhouse, and Mrs. Adeyemi standing on the dock in a red coat, waving both arms, which is how everyone in the interior greets airplanes and how Mrs. Adeyemi in particular greets everything, including her own reflection.

I set down on the water in a long flat calm, taxied to the dock, and killed the engine, and the silence that follows a floatplane shutdown is one of the great products of this country. It's the silence the whole world would make if the world were well-run.

"Medicine's in the blue box," I said, hauling it up onto the dock. "Insulin's in the cooler, keep it cold. Tetanus is the small box, and tell the boy who found the nail that the needle's worse than the nail, so he should be smug about it."

"He's already smug," Mrs. Adeyemi said, taking the boxes. "He's told the whole school. He's calling it his battle wound." She peered past me into the plane. "You look thin, Gideon."

"I look aerodynamic."

"You look thin. When are you coming to supper? I've got a moose roast with your name on it, and I mean that literally, I write names on things, it's a habit."

"Next pass," I said, which is what I always say, and which we both understood the way you understand a hymn you've sung a thousand times—by feel, without believing any single word of it.

The mail went out, the freight came off, and a woman named Corliss handed me a parcel wrapped in butcher paper and asked, in the way people do, if I'd mind taking a letter to her sister down at Two Rivers, "since you're going that way anyway." I was not going that way anyway. I was going that way by ninety miles of detour. I said yes, because that's the other half of the philosophy, the part I don't put on the sales brochure: the miles are only honest if you spend some of them on other people.

* * *

By noon I was working the long leg south along the Rivergate itself, and this is where I'll ask you to pay attention, because the river is going to matter to this story more than I understood it was going to matter that morning.

The Rivergate runs out of the high country in the north—real high country, the kind that doesn't show up properly on satellite imagery because the satellites keep getting confused by the gorge—and it comes south through two hundred miles of country that has never entirely decided whether it belongs to people or to itself. Down where I was flying, it's a working river. Barges, fish camps, a hydro station, the town of Rivergate itself with its gravel strip and its diner that serves something called a Lumberjack's Regret, which is pancakes the diameter of a truck tire.

But upstream, past the last barge dock, past the last trapline cabin, the river goes into the gorge, and the gorge is a different animal. The charts I fly with—the good ones, the current ones—show the river narrowing, and then the contour lines bunching up like a crowd pressing against a door, and then, for eleven miles, nothing. Blank space. The cartographers gave up and printed a note: *RELIEF DATA UNAVAILABLE.*

Except that's not quite true. There is one mark in that blank space. Handwritten, in the margin, in ink that's gone brown with age, two initials and a date:

*E.Y. '03.*

I have never told a soul about that mark. I have flown over that blank space four times in my career—twice delivering for survey crews who turned back early, twice because weather pushed me off my line and the gorge was the only gap in the cloud—and all four times I looked down at that brown ink and then looked away, the way you look away from a photograph of someone at a funeral.

E.Y. is Elias Yarrow. My father. And 2003 is the year the interior stopped having a place in it where he could be alive.

* * *

But that morning I wasn't thinking about any of that, or rather, I was thinking about it exactly as much as I always think about it, which is to say I'd folded it up small and put it in the back of my mind behind the spare oil and the survival kit, where I keep the things that are essential and never opened.

That morning I was thinking about the weather, because that's what pilots think about, and the weather was thinking about me. There was a front sitting over the coast like a fat man on a park bench, and the forecast said it would sit there for two days before it decided to move, and when it moved it would move everything—cloud, wind, rain, the whole works—right across the interior like a broom.

I had two days of good flying. I intended to use them.

The afternoon went the way good afternoons go: fuel stop at Two Rivers, where the letter got delivered and Corliss's sister fed me beans and tried to set me up with her daughter, who was thirty years too young for me and forty years too sensible; a medevac that turned out not to be, a man at the fish camp who'd chest pains that resolved into heartburn and embarrassment; and a low pass over Walt Bricker's sawmill to confirm that the dishwasher-sized part had, in fact, fit, which it had, and Walt waved his hard hat at me from the ground like I'd personally saved the industrial economy of the north.

By six I was back at Rivergate, tying down the *Marguerite* in the last of the light, and the day's arithmetic came out the way I like it: six hundred and forty miles, four landings, zero incidents, one moose-roast rain check outstanding.

That's the life. That's the whole sales pitch, demonstrated. You get up, you read the sky, you make the miles come true, and at the end of the day the numbers balance and nobody has asked you a question you can't answer with a shrug.

* * *

I was walking up from the strip toward the diner—because a man can live on miles, but he can't live on miles *for supper*—when the radio at the strip shack crackled, and old Petey, who minds the strip the way a lighthouse minds a rock, stuck his head out and waved me over.

"Gideon. Radio. Fella's been asking for you by name for ten minutes. Been patient about it, but he's the kind of patient that's worse than impatient, if you take my meaning."

I took his meaning, because Petey's meanings are always load-rated for exactly what they carry. I went in and picked up the handset.

"Yarrow."

"Mr. Yarrow. Good evening. My name is Aldous Crane." The voice was smooth and city-made, the kind of voice that has been paid to be pleasant and considers it money well spent. "I understand you're the man to talk to about charter work up the Rivergate."

"Depends on the work."

"Supplies, mostly. A scientific expedition. And I'll be direct with you, Mr. Yarrow, because I'm told you're a direct man: I may also need someone willing to go upstream on short notice, should the need arise. Someone who knows that country."

I looked out the shack window at the *Marguerite* tied down in the dusk, and past her, north, at the place where the river went into the trees and the charts went blank except for two brown initials.

"Who told you I know that country?" I said.

A pause, small and expensive, like a man selecting the right tie. "Everyone says that about you, Mr. Yarrow. That's rather the point of you."

I stood there a moment with the handset against my ear and the front sitting fat on the coast two days out, and I had the distinct, unreasonable feeling—the kind of feeling a pilot learns to respect, the same feeling you get about an engine that's running *too* quietly—that the miles I'd been counting for nineteen years had just been counted by somebody else, and totaled, and filed.

"When do you need an answer?" I said.

"No rush at all," said Aldous Crane pleasantly. "Come by the lodge tomorrow. Bring your charts."

I hung up the handset and stood in the shack while Petey pretended to read a fishing magazine, and outside the windsock stirred once, from the north, and went still again.
