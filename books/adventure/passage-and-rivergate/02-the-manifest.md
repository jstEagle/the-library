## 2. The Manifest

The thing about a dirt strip is that it tells you everything about a town before the town can lie to you.

Rivergate's strip was four hundred meters of graded gravel with a windsock that had been patched so many times it looked like a quilted memorial to every storm that had ever come through. The grass along the edges grew in tufts, then gave up entirely where the tires chewed. At the south end, someone had painted RIVERGATE on a plank and nailed it to two posts, though the R had weathered into something closer to an ampersand, so the town now officially announced itself as &ivergate, which Gideon found honest in its way.

He taxied the Cessna 185 — call sign November-Charlie-Bravo, though he thought of her as the Mule, because she carried whatever was asked of her and complained only in a language of shudders and fuel numbers — up to the fuel bowser and shut her down.

Morning in Rivergate smelled like diesel, river water, and the fry bread somebody was making at the café three hundred meters away, because smell traveled farther than sound out here and appetite traveled farther than both.

"Gideon!" Dot Ferreira came across the gravel with a clipboard held like a shield and a coffee thermos like a sidearm. Dot ran the strip, the fuel, the mail contract, and — though this was not written anywhere official — roughly sixty percent of the town's opinions. She was seventy-one years old and had once told Gideon, apropos of nothing, that she'd buried two husbands and a floatplane and missed the floatplane more, on account of the husbands never once brought her anything but trouble and the plane brought her parts catalogs.

"Dot."

"You look terrible."

"Thank you. I slept well."

"That's what worries me." She handed him the thermos cap, poured without asking, which was the Rivergate way. "You sleep too well for a man your age. It's suspicious. Men your age should be up worrying about something. Pension. Hairline. A woman with strong opinions."

"I worry about weight and balance."

"That's not worrying, that's arithmetic. Worry has to keep you up." She flipped her clipboard around. "Manifest's on top. Halloway expedition resupply. Crane's office sent it down yesterday, all typed up proper, like they've got a secretary who doesn't smoke."

Gideon took the clipboard and read while he drank. He read manifests the way other men read letters from home: slowly, twice, looking for what wasn't said.

Rice, twenty kilos. Flour, ten. Fuel filters, six. Spark plugs, spare set. Batteries, D-cell, forty-eight. Rope, static line, fifty meters. Tents poles, replacement, two sets. And then:

Amoxicillin, 500mg capsules, quantity: 400.

He read it again. Four hundred capsules. The first resupply run, six weeks ago, had carried eighty. Eighty was generous for a team of five over a season; expedition med kits usually ran leaner, because sponsors counted pills the way misers counted coins. Four hundred was either a typo or a confession.

"Dot. This antibiotic count."

"What about it?"

"It's doubled. More than doubled."

She leaned over and looked, squinting through reading glasses that lived on a beaded chain around her neck like jewelry. "Huh," she said, in the tone of a woman filing something away in a drawer she didn't open in company. "Well. Fevers go around camps. Bad water, maybe. You want I should call Crane's office?"

"No," Gideon said, faster than he meant to. "No, it's fine. Load it."

Because here was the philosophy, the whole architecture of his life, laid down in ten thousand hours of sky: you fly the manifest. You don't fly the questions behind the manifest. Questions weigh nothing and cost everything. A man who started wondering why the antibiotics were doubled would next wonder why the rope order was doubled, and why the tent poles were doubled, and before long he'd be flying his own curiosity upriver instead of cargo, and curiosity had a way of not coming back.

His father had been a curious man. That was the family disease, as far as Gideon could tell. Curiosity, and then silence.

"Earth to Yarrow." Dot snapped her fingers. "You went somewhere."

"Checking the center of gravity in my head."

"Liar," she said fondly. "Come on, the freight's stacked by the shed. And there's one crate the Crane man called about special. Twice. He called twice about one crate, if you can believe it."

Gideon could believe it. Aldous Crane struck him as a man who called twice about everything — once to instruct and once to make sure obedience had taken root.

* * *

The freight was stacked under the open-sided shed beside the strip, on pallets that kept the worst of the damp off. Gideon walked the pile with his hands, because eyes lied about weight and hands didn't. Rice, dense and honest. Flour, lighter than the label claimed, which meant someone upstream had skimped and someone downstream — him — would carry the difference in trim. Fuel filters. Batteries, heavy as sin and twice as useful.

And at the end of the row, alone, sitting on its own pallet as if the others had been arranged to give it room: a wooden crate, maybe sixty centimeters square, banded in steel, stenciled on every face in black capitals:

FRAGILE: DO NOT OPEN

Underneath, smaller: PROPERTY OF CRANE HERITAGE SURVEYS. IF SEAL BROKEN, REFUSE SHIPMENT AND REPORT.

"Well," Gideon said to the crate.

The crate said nothing back, which he respected.

He circled it once. The wood was good — marine ply, the kind that laughed at rain — and the steel banding was new, and there were seals over the corners, little lead discs crimped flat, the kind of thing you saw on evidence bags and diplomatic pouches and nowhere near ordinary expedition freight. Somebody had built this box to know if it was opened. Which raised a question Gideon declined to ask: opened by whom? Him? The expedition? God?

He put his palm flat on the lid. It sat heavy for its size. Not drum-heavy, not tool-heavy. Something packed tight and dense, with padding. Instruments, maybe. A core sample in resin. Documents in a waterproof case — paper weighed almost nothing, though, and this had heft.

"Yarrow." Dot had come up behind him, arms crossed. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The standing-there-looking-at-a-thing-like-it-owes-you-money thing. Your father used to do it."

The words landed the way they always landed — not like a blow, more like a hand briefly on the back of the neck. Dot was the only person in Rivergate who said the name out loud, and she did it rarely, and never cruelly, which somehow made it worse. Everyone else in town had agreed, in the unspoken way of small towns, to let the past stay past. They knew him as Yarrow, single name, no history, a bush pilot who'd blown in seven years ago with a duffel bag and a commercial license and no stories. Small towns will grant you anonymity forever if you don't make them curious, and Gideon had made very sure never to be interesting.

"He did?" Gideon said. "I don't remember that."

"You were nine. You wouldn't. But I drove your father to this same strip once, summer of '03, when he chartered off me for that survey job up the gorge." She said it lightly, pouring coffee into her own cup now, eyes on the middle distance. "Stood right about where you're standing, looking at a crate just about like that one. Different stencil. Same attitude."

Gideon kept his voice level, which took practice, the way landing on a one-way bar takes practice. "What was in it?"

"Never asked." She shrugged. "Asking's how you end up knowing things. Knowing things is how you end up responsible for them." She pointed the thermos cup at him. "That's not cowardice, before you get that look. That's just good bookkeeping."

* * *

Loading took two hours, because Gideon loaded the way he did everything: deliberately, with the patience of a man who believed that most disasters were just hurry wearing a costume. Rice and flour low and forward, lashed to the cargo rings. Batteries amidships. The antibiotics went into the padded med case he kept behind the pilot seat — four hundred capsules, rattling like dice in a gambler's fist — and he caught himself counting them out of the box, twice, as if the number might change if he wasn't watching.

It didn't. Four hundred. Enough to treat a camp of five for pneumonia, twice over, with leftovers to trade with a village. Or enough for something else. Prophylaxis at scale. A sponsor hedging against a lawsuit. Or a sponsor who already knew something about the water up there, or the ground, or the dig itself, and wanted his people dosed before —

Stop. Fly the manifest.

The crate went in last, centered on the floor between the rings, padded with the old sleeping bags he kept for exactly this purpose. He ratcheted it down, checked the straps twice, and sat back on his heels in the cabin with the tailgate open to the morning.

From inside, the crate looked smaller. Boxes always did, once they stopped being a mystery and became cargo. Weight was weight. Center of gravity was center of gravity. Whatever Crane Heritage Surveys didn't want opened, it flew the same as rice.

Except.

Except that he'd flown freight for eleven years and he could count on one hand the crates that arrived with instructions about themselves. Freight didn't have rules. Freight had mass and dimensions. Rules were for people, and a crate with rules was a crate with a person folded up inside it somewhere — a promise, a threat, a liability, a secret wearing a shipping label.

He thought about the seals. Lead, crimped. If he broke one, he'd know, and Crane would know, and then Gideon Yarrow — quiet, uninteresting, forget-him-by-Tuesday Gideon Yarrow — would become a man with a story, and stories were debts.

He closed the tailgate.

* * *

Dot found him doing the preflight, walking the wings, draining fuel samples into a glass jar and holding them up to the light like a jeweler checking for water in whiskey. Clean. Good.

"She's heavy today," Dot observed, coming to stand under the wing.

"Halloway party's got five mouths and a sponsor with expensive taste in rope."

"Mm." Dot sipped her coffee. "You know the Voss woman is on that team. Mara Voss. The archaeologist. She came through in spring, chartering samples down to the university. Sharp as a fishhook, that one. You'd have liked her."

"I don't like people. People are weather."

"You liked me."

"You're climate."

Dot laughed, a real one, head back, and then sobered in the particular way she had, mid-breath, like a plane leveling off. "Gideon." First-name. She almost never used it. "Crane's office called again this morning. Third time. Wanted to confirm the crate made the load. Wanted to confirm *you* made the load. Asked after your experience upriver, your hours, whether you'd ever flown the gorge."

"And what did you tell them?"

"Told them you're the best stick on this river and that your hours are your own business." She looked at him over the rim of her cup. "They asked your full name, Gideon. For their insurance forms, the girl said."

The windsock stirred. Somewhere behind the café, the fry bread situation intensified. Down at the dock, the morning mail boat knocked against its tires, patient as everything else in Rivergate.

"And what did you tell them?" Gideon asked again.

Dot held his eyes for a moment longer than comfort allowed, and he understood that she understood — not the specifics, nobody alive knew the specifics, he'd made sure of that — but the shape of it. The outline. The way you can see a mountain under cloud cover without ever seeing the mountain.

"Told them you're Yarrow," she said. "Same as everybody. Same as the strip log, same as the mail contract, same as the church raffle we entered you in last Christmas against your will." She drained her cup and screwed it onto the thermos. "A man's entitled to be whoever his signature says he is. That's the law up here, far as I'm concerned." She patted the wing strut twice, the way you'd pat a horse. "But I'll say one thing, and then I'll mind my own business, which is my second-best skill after coffee."

"Go ahead."

"Crate like that, pills like that, a sponsor calling three times before dawn — that's not a supply run anymore, boy. That's a question with wings on it." She turned and started back toward the shed, then paused, not turning around. "And questions with wings? Those are the only ones that always come home to roost."

* * *

He finished the preflight. He filed the flight plan — Rivergate to Halloway camp, upper Rivergate drainage, two hours thirty, visual conditions, returning tomorrow weather permitting. The radio operator at the regional desk, a sleepy kid named Petey who did the job from a shack with a cat in it, read the details back and added, "Heavy load, NC Bravo. You want a heavier category?"

"Just a well-fed one, Petey."

"Roger that. Have a good one."

Gideon climbed in, ran the checklist, and sat for a moment with his hand on the throttle, the engine idling below him like a big animal deciding whether to cooperate. Through the windshield, the strip ran north into the trees, and beyond the trees, following the river the way a scar follows a wound, lay the country he did not fly. Everybody in the business had a line they didn't cross — a valley, a pass, a stretch of water that had taken somebody. Most pilots' lines were drawn by fear. His had been drawn by a blank space on a map, twenty years old, and initials in the corner in handwriting he'd know anywhere, because he'd spent his childhood trying to forge it.

E.Y.

The gorge charts ended there. So, officially, did he.

He flew the manifest north. That was all. Rice and flour and batteries and rope, four hundred pills, and one sealed box of somebody else's secrets, riding fat and quiet in the back like a passenger who knew where they were going and wasn't saying.

Halfway up the valley, with the river sliding silver beneath him and the weather still clean to the north, he caught himself doing it — the thing Dot had named, the standing-and-staring translated into cruise flight, his eyes going to the mirror where the cargo rode, his mind going to the seals, the lead discs, the weight of paper that shouldn't weigh anything.

Fly the manifest, he told himself.

But the manifest, he was beginning to suspect, was not the whole manifest. And nine days from now, when the Halloway expedition missed its first scheduled check-in, and then its second, and then its third, Gideon Yarrow would find out exactly what else had been on that list — the items typed in no office, signed by no hand, and waiting for him upriver where the maps went blank.
