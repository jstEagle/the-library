## 3. Radio Silence

The trouble with schedules, I've always thought, is that they make promises on your behalf. You agree to a check-in at 0900 on the ninth day, and suddenly there's a version of you out there in the world—a punctual Gideon Yarrow, a reliable one—and he's expected to show up whether or not the actual man is willing.

I'd made that promise to Aldous Crane nine days ago, standing on the Rivergate strip with my collar up against the wind and the manifest half-signed in my glove. "They'll call in every third day," Crane had said, tapping his fountain pen against the clipboard like it owed him money. "0900 sharp. If they miss one, don't panic. If they miss two—" He'd smiled then, a thin, expensive smile, the kind men like him keep folded in their breast pockets next to the pen. "Well. We'll discuss it."

They missed one.

That was four days ago, and I told myself what I tell everyone: rivers eat radios. Batteries die. Antennas fall down. A man named Hollis once went silent for eleven days because a wolverine had chewed through his cable, and when I finally landed he was furious—not about the wolverine, but about the rescue party drinking his good coffee. Silence upriver isn't an emergency. It's weather.

Then they missed two.

* * *

The radio room at Rivergate was really a closet with ambitions—a desk, a chair with three legs' worth of confidence, and a wall of equipment older than most of the town's dogs. I kept a bunk in the back for nights when the mail run ran late, which meant I heard the scheduled call come and go the way you hear a train pass through a town you're only sleeping in: present, then gone.

At 0917 on the second missed morning, someone knocked on the closet door. I knew who it would be before I opened it. Nobody else in Rivergate knocked like that—three precise raps, evenly spaced, as though the door were being invoiced.

"Gideon." Aldous Crane stood in the hall holding a paper cup of coffee he had no intention of drinking. He was one of those men whose clothes looked ironed even in rain, silver at the temples, gold at the cufflinks, and a way of looking at you that suggested he'd already read your file and found it adequate. "You heard?"

"Heard nothing," I said. "That's rather the point."

"Two missed check-ins." He said it the way other men say *two flat tires*. "I'd like to charter you to fly upriver and have a look."

I leaned against the doorframe. Behind me the radio hissed its patient static, the sound of nobody talking, which upriver is usually just nobody talking.

"When?"

"Now, ideally."

"It's raining sideways over the divide. I watched the front come down the valley this morning like a curtain falling on a bad play. I'm not flying into that."

Crane's smile made another appearance, unfolded carefully. "Triple rate."

"There's no rate triple enough for flying into a front like that. The plane comes back dented or I do."

"Then tomorrow, if the window opens. Triple rate, plus a bonus for speed." He set the untouched coffee on the desk among my charts, precisely, like a chess move. "Gideon. Those are six people. A doctor, three grad students, Voss, and the field lead. They have families. Well—" a small correction, done so smoothly you'd miss it if you weren't watching, "—most of them do."

"And if it's just a dead battery?"

"Then you'll have earned triple pay for a scenic flight, and I'll be the first to buy you dinner." He paused at the door, and for a moment the polish thinned and something underneath showed through—something that looked, surprisingly, like worry. Or a very good imitation of worry, built by a man who could afford the best. "Halloway was careless with everything except that expedition. If he's gone quiet, it isn't carelessness."

He left. The coffee sat there going cold, and I sat there going cold with it, listening to the static.

* * *

Here's the thing about being needed: it feels almost exactly like being useful, right up until you notice the difference.

Useful is when you choose the job. Needed is when the job chooses you, and by the time you've thought it through, you're already halfway to yes. I'd spent eleven years building a life out of useful—mail runs, medevacs, supply drops, all of it measured, all of it mine. Nobody depended on Gideon Yarrow showing up anywhere, because as far as the world knew, Gideon Yarrow was mostly a signature on a pilot's license and a name I answered to the way you answer to a nickname somebody gave you that never quite fit.

But a missing expedition—that has weight. Six people. Even Halloway, whom I'd met exactly once and found insufferable in the specific way of men who think enthusiasm is a qualification. Even him.

And under that, quieter, the thing I didn't let myself look at directly: the route. Up the Rivergate, past the last fuel cache, past where my charts went polite and vague, into the gorge country where the map simply stopped pretending and left blank space with two letters penciled in the corner in handwriting I'd know anywhere, because I'd spent a childhood copying it onto school exercises until a teacher called home about it.

E.Y.

I hadn't flown that stretch in eleven years. I'd flown around it the way water flows around a stone—thirty extra miles here, a dogleg there, a whole geography of avoidance drawn in fuel burn. Every pilot has a place he doesn't fly. Mine just happened to be shaped exactly like my father's last known coordinates.

* * *

That evening I did the thing I always do when I can't sleep, which is work on the plane. There's a honesty in maintenance that conversation lacks. You torque a bolt and either it holds or it doesn't; the bolt doesn't care what name you use.

I was halfway through checking the engine mounts by lamplight when boots crunched on the gravel behind me, and Del Marsh appeared out of the dark carrying two mugs of coffee and the expression of a man who'd been rehearsing a speech.

Del runs the strip—fuel, hangar, gossip, in roughly that order of priority. He's sixty-something, built like a fuel drum himself, and he handed me a mug without asking, which is his way of opening negotiations.

"Heard Crane's offering triple," he said.

"Heard fast, this town."

"Town's got nothing but time and ears." He settled against the wheel strut, which no other man alive would dare. "You taking it?"

"Window doesn't open till tomorrow, maybe."

"That's not what I asked."

I drank the coffee. It was terrible, which meant it was Del's, which meant it was free, which in my accounting system made it excellent. "I don't know yet."

"You know." He nodded north, toward where the mountains were eating the last of the light. "That's Halloway's lot up there. And that's the Yarrow route, if the old-timers are to be believed. Your father flew supply for a survey outfit out of here, back before you were born. Or—" he squinted, doing arithmetic on my face, "—no. Back when you were knee-high. You'd not remember."

"I don't remember," I said, which was true, and also the smoothest lie I'd told in years, because I remembered everything.

"Well." Del drained his mug and stood, joints reporting in one at a time. "Six people waiting on a yes from you. That's a heavy kind of cargo, son. Heavier than fuel drums." He patted the strut twice, gently, the way you'd pat a horse. "Plane'll be ready either way. She always is. It's the pilot I worry about."

He crunched off toward the lights of town, and I stood there in the cold with the lamplight and the open cowling and the static still hissing faintly from the closet radio across the yard, patient as tide.

* * *

I want to be honest about the arithmetic I did that night, because pilots live by arithmetic and lie by everything else.

Fuel: full tanks plus drums, if I stripped the cargo pod. Weight: me, survival gear, medical kit—the doubled antibiotics from that manifest came back to me unbidden, sitting in some tent upriver getting warm or getting used, and I couldn't decide which possibility I liked less. Weather: a front moving through tonight, clearing from the west by midmorning if the barometer told the truth, and barometers mostly do; it's people who lie. Time: forty-eight hours of decent air before the next system stacked up behind it, big and slow and mean.

And lives: six, possibly seven counting whoever might be walking out, possibly zero, possibly a number I didn't want written down.

Against all of it, one entry in the debit column, written small, in pencil, the way you write things you hope to erase: *you swore you'd never go back up that river.*

But here's what eleven years of measured miles teaches you. A promise made out of fear isn't a promise. It's a debt, and debts collect interest, and the interest on that particular one had been compounding since I was nineteen years old, paid out in detours and doglegs and thirty extra miles of sky.

The static hissed. Somewhere north of the divide, six people were either fine or not fine, and the difference between those two states was going to be decided partly by weather, partly by luck, and partly by whether a bush pilot with a fake name and a real fear could stand to point his nose at the blank space on the map.

I torqued the last bolt. It held.

* * *

Crane was on the strip at dawn, of course. Men like him treat dawn as a scheduling inconvenience others should accommodate. The front had gone through overnight exactly as promised, dragging the clouds east and leaving behind that washed, ringing clarity that makes the mountains look newly minted. Forty-eight hours, give or take. The clock was already running.

"Conditions?" he asked, before hello.

"Flying weather. Barely." I cinched the last strap on the fuel drums and turned to find him studying me with that ledger-keeper's attention, adding up figures I hadn't submitted. "I'll fly to the camp, land if there's water long enough, assess, and report. That's the charter. I'm not a rescue service, Mr. Crane. I'm a delivery man with opinions."

"Understood." He handed me an envelope—thick, cash-heavy, the advance already counted. Then, as I took it, he held on a half-second longer than necessary, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "If you find them alive, bring word fast. If you find otherwise—" the pause was surgical, "—bring back anything they were carrying. Papers especially. Field records. The sponsor retains rights to all expedition materials. It's in the contract."

"I never read the contract."

"I know," Crane said. "That's why I wrote it."

He walked back toward his truck, pressed and unhurried, and I stood there with the envelope in my fist feeling less like a hired pilot than like a key that had just been cut to fit a lock I hadn't seen yet.

Above the strip, the air was empty and enormous and clear all the way north—clear, I noticed, all the way to the gorge country, where the map ran out and the initials waited in the corner of the blank space like a signature on a document twenty years overdue.

I loaded the medical kit last, on top, where I could reach it.

Then I climbed in, ran the checks, and told myself—firmly, the way you tell a child there's nothing under the bed—that this was just another charter. Just miles. Just fuel and weather and a delivery.

The engine caught on the first try, which should have been my first warning. Nothing up that river was ever going to be that easy again.
