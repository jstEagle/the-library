## 4. What the River Keeps

The map room at Yarrow Air Services was also the kitchen, the office, and on one memorable occasion a birthing suite for a litter of fox kits that had gotten under the floorboards. It was a twelve-by-fifteen room in a hangar that leaned two degrees east like a man listening hard to a story he didn't believe, and it contained everything I owned that couldn't fly.

I spread the charts out on the table after dark, weighted at the corners with a coffee mug, a socket wrench, a tin of licorice pastilles, and the skull of a loon I'd found on the shore of Lake Vermet and never had a good explanation for. The single lamp swung slightly in the draft from the door seal, so the shadows moved over the paper like weather.

Here is what most people don't understand about maps, and what I'd spent ten thousand hours learning: a map is not the land. A map is an argument about the land, made by somebody who was there, or by somebody who talked to somebody who was there, or — and this is the category that kills pilots — by somebody who thought being there probably wasn't necessary.

I found the Rivergate sheet and smoothed it flat.

The river ran up the page like a blue vein, fat and confident through the low country where every bend had a name and a surveyed elevation and, in three places, a fuel cache I personally maintained. Then it climbed into the high country and began to narrow, the way rivers do when the land starts pushing back. The contour lines crowded together, brown crowding brown, until the cartographer gave up on elegance and just started hatching. The river went from a vein to a wire to a scratch.

And then it stopped.

Not the river. The map.

There was maybe four inches of blank paper where the gorge should have been — four inches that represented, at this scale, something like thirty miles of the deepest cut terrain in the interior — and in the upper right corner of that blankness, small and precise and twenty years old, two letters:

**E.Y.**

My father's initials. His handwriting, if you could call it that; Elias Yarrow wrote the way he flew, fast and certain, with the quiet arrogance of a man who believed the world would mostly cooperate if you approached it correctly. He'd drawn those letters on the master chart himself, back when I was eleven years old and thought my father knew the shape of every secret the country kept.

The survey office had never corrected it. I used to think that was sloppiness. Lately I'd begun to suspect it was something closer to superstition — nobody wanted to be the man who erased Elias Yarrow's name from the only place it still lived.

* * *

When I was nine, my father taught me to read contours at this same table, though the table was younger then and so was everything else.

"Lines close together, steep," he said, tapping the page with a pencil stub worn down to the size of a thumbnail. "Lines far apart, gentle. Lines like this—" he tapped a place where the hatching went mad, "—that's not flying country, that's apologizing country. You go there, you spend half your time saying sorry."

"Sorry to who?"

"To the ground, mostly." He grinned. He had a grin that made adults forgive him things they shouldn't have. "The ground always wins eventually, Gid. The whole trick is keeping the argument going as long as possible."

My mother, drying dishes at the sink behind us, said without turning around, "Elias. He's nine."

"He's a pilot's son."

"He's nine."

But she was smiling. That was the thing about my father I could never explain to people afterward, in the years when explaining him became my full-time occupation: he wasn't reckless. Reckless implies not knowing the odds. Elias Yarrow knew the odds better than any man alive and simply believed, with his whole chest, that he was the exception the statistics were waiting for.

He flew the Rivergate survey in the summer of 2003. I was fifteen. He came home twice between legs of it, smelling of river water and pipe smoke, full of talk about a gorge so deep the noon sun didn't reach the bottom, about rapids that sounded like freight trains, about a pre-war surveyor named Halloran or Hollis or some such who'd gone in with mules and a theodolite in 1931 and come out with maps nobody in any ministry would ever look at again.

"There's country up there, Gid, that no white man has named," he told me, and my mother said, "Elias, please," because she could hear where sentences like that led, the way you can hear a storm front coming by the way the birds go quiet.

He left for the last leg on the fourth of August. I remember because I'd wanted to come with him — I was fifteen and already had forty hours logged and a conviction of my own invincibility that I now recognize as entirely hereditary — and he'd said no, school first, sky later, and I'd sulked about it for exactly six days, which is how long the radio stayed silent before anyone official noticed.

Six days. They found the wreck site eleven days after that, or what they said was the wreck site, a smear of aluminum on a ridge forty miles short of where he'd filed he was going. No body recovered. The river had him, the report said, which was the coroner's polite way of saying we'll never know anything ever again, please stop asking.

I stopped asking. Out loud, anyway. Inside, I asked constantly, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

* * *

Now I sat in the lamplight with the Rivergate sheet and did what I'd done perhaps fifty times since I was nineteen and inherited this hangar along with everything else my father hadn't finished: I looked at the blank space.

Four inches of nothing. Thirty miles of the worst flying country in the territory, unmapped, unnamed except by whatever names the old surveyor gave it and whatever names my father gave it after, and none of either written down anywhere I could find. I'd written to the survey office twice. The first letter got a form reply. The second got a phone call from a tired-sounding man named Dombrowski who said, off the record, son, that section was pulled from circulation in '04, and when I asked why, he said, because the man who filed the original survey request was Aldous Crane, and Mr. Crane requested its withdrawal, and Mr. Crane donates to the survey office's budget the way other men donate to churches, and then he hung up before I could ask the question underneath the question.

Crane. The name had been circling me for weeks now like a bird deciding whether to land. He was the money behind the Halloway expedition currently sitting at the top of my father's blank space, waiting for me to bring them antibiotics and a crate I wasn't supposed to wonder about. Triple pay, he'd said, for a simple check-in run if they went quiet. And he'd said my name — my whole name — in a tone I didn't care for, the tone of a man handling a tool he'd been looking for.

I poured the last of the coffee and studied the blank space the way other men study faces in crowds, looking for someone they've lost.

Here's what I knew about the gorge, gathered over twenty years the way driftwood gathers against a fence: the river entered it through a slot canyon you could throw a stone across. The walls went up eight hundred feet in places, grey limestone banded with iron red. There was a set of rapids midway — my father had called them the Staircase, once, to a man in the Rivergate hotel bar, and the man had repeated it to me years later with the reverence people reserve for words they heard from the dead. Below the Staircase the river calmed for a stretch, and somewhere along that calm water, according to a trapper who claimed his grandfather had traded with the 1931 survey party, there was a side canyon with a cave mouth big enough to walk a horse into.

Whether any of that was true, I couldn't say. Whether my father had seen it, I couldn't say. Whether my father was in it — dead at the bottom of it, or alive at the bottom of it, or long since washed out of it and distributed across a hundred miles of delta — I could not say, and the not-saying had become, without my noticing, the load-bearing wall of my entire personality.

Ten thousand hours of sky, and I'd spent them all flying away from four inches of blank paper.

* * *

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, which meant rain tomorrow, which meant the strip would be soft, which meant if I was going to run the resupply charter I'd want to be loaded and gone by mid-morning.

I should have been thinking about weight and balance. Instead I put my finger on the blank space, on the exact center of it, and held it there like a man taking an oath or checking a fever.

Once, when I was sixteen — a year before, a lifetime before — my father had stood at this same table with this same chart, rolling it open with a snap, and said, "One day I'll take you up the Gate, Gid. Show you the real country. None of this milk-run business." And I'd said something teenage and dismissive, because that was my job description at the time, and he'd laughed and ruffled my hair, which I pretended to hate, and that was the last conversation we ever had about the gorge, because three weeks later the gorge had him.

Or didn't. Depending on which report you believed, and I had learned to believe all of them and none of them simultaneously, which is a skill that sounds like wisdom and feels like drowning.

Because here was the thing I had never said to another living soul, the thing that sat in me like a stone in a boot: the wreck site never made sense to me. Not the location — forty miles short, sure, headwinds happen, engines happen. But the photographs. I'd gotten copies when I turned eighteen, pulled strings, called in favors from a bush lawyer who owed my mother money. The wreckage in those photos was scattered wrong. Debris fields tell you a story if you know how to read them — impact point, heading, whether the engine was turning — and that field read to me like an aircraft that had come down slow and controlled and then been *taken apart*. Panels removed. The prop carried off. Things missing that a crash doesn't take and scavengers don't bother with.

An aircraft that landed, I'd thought, at nineteen, staring at those photos in this room, at this table. An aircraft that landed, and a pilot who walked away from it, into the blank space, carrying whatever he'd gone up there to find.

I'd never said it out loud because saying it out loud makes it a theory, and theories can be wrong, and I could survive my father being dead. I could not survive being wrong about him twice.

* * *

The lamp swung. The shadows moved. On the chart, the Rivergate ran up toward the blankness and stopped, obedient, at the edge of everything I refused to think about.

And I understood — standing there in the cold kitchen-light of midnight, with the rain starting early against the hangar roof — that the choice I'd been telling myself was simple was not simple at all. Fly the supplies up, keep my eyes on the strip, keep the blank space on my left where it had always been, and come home with triple pay and my history intact. Or look. Just once, deliberately, with adult eyes instead of a boy's, at the four inches of nothing that had raised me.

The radio on the shelf crackled — the night watchman at Rivergate reading weather, routine, half-asleep — and I let it wash over me and kept looking at the map.

Somewhere up in that blankness, right now, were seven strangers camped on my father's river, paid by a man who knew my name, guarding whatever it was my father had gone into the silence to find and never come out of. Somewhere up there was an answer with my family's name on it.

I took the pencil stub — his pencil stub, absurdly, still, after all these years, the same thumbnail-sized nub — and very lightly, in the margin beside the blank space, wrote the date.

Then I went to bed, and did not sleep, and in the morning there was a message waiting from Aldous Crane, and the message said: *We need to talk before you fly.*
