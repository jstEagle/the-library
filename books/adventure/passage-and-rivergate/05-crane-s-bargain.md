## 5. Crane's Bargain

Aldous Crane kept an office above the Rivergate trading post, which told you most of what you needed to know about him before he ever opened his mouth. The building was old Hudson's Bay timber, silvered and sagging at the eaves like a tired dog, but the man had installed a glass door with his name stenciled in gold leaf—*A. CRANE, CONSULTING & ACQUISITIONS*—and gold leaf on the Rivergate was about as subtle as a peacock at a funeral.

I climbed the outside stairs at seven in the morning because Crane's message had said *come early*, and men who say *come early* are men who want to catch you before your coffee does its work.

The office smelled of pipe smoke and old paper and, faintly, of the river below—the way everything in Rivergate smelled of the river, as if the water were patiently reminding the town whose territory this really was. Bookshelves lined one wall, filled with leather spines that I'd have bet my propeller had never been opened. A framed map of the interior hung behind the desk, and I made the mistake of glancing at it.

The gorge was there. Drawn in fine ink, labeled in a confident Victorian hand: *RIVERGATE GORGE — SURVEY INCOMPLETE.*

Incomplete. That was one word for it. My charts called it something blunter—a rectangle of white space with two letters penciled in the corner that I'd been staring at since I was old enough to read a legend: *E.Y.*

"Mr. Yarrow." Crane rose from behind the desk and extended a hand across it, as if we were meeting across a banker's table instead of above a store that sold ammunition and canned peaches. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. Coffee?"

"I've had some."

"You've had *pilot* coffee," he said, pouring from a silver pot anyway. "Which is to say something boiled past all recognition and drunk standing up out of grim necessity. This is actual coffee. Beans. A French press, if you can believe they let me keep it up here."

He was maybe sixty, built long and narrow like a folding rule, with a face that seemed assembled for benevolence—soft eyes, laugh lines, a smile that arrived easily and stayed late. His hands were the tell. They were very still. Men who are genuinely relaxed fidget; men who are performing relaxation hold themselves like Crane held himself, every finger accounted for.

I took the coffee because refusing would have been a conversation, and I sat because the chair was already there, and I told myself the same thing I'd been telling myself for nine days: this is a charter inquiry. This is business. You fly, they pay, nobody asks anybody anything interesting.

Nine days. Three missed check-ins. And then Crane's note slid under my door at the float shack, written in fountain pen on paper heavy enough to be furniture: *I'd like to discuss the Halloway expedition. Come early. Bring your discretion.*

Nobody writes *bring your discretion* unless they intend to test whether you packed it.

"The expedition," I said. "You want me to go look."

"I want to talk first." He settled back into his chair and laced his fingers over his vest. "Indulge an old man. Do you know what Halloway was looking for?"

"A pre-war surveyor's camp, Mara Voss said. Nineteen-thirties, government survey."

"That's correct. And do you know why anyone would care about a forty-year-old survey camp?"

"Fossils," I said. "Old maps. Archaeologists get excited about paper the rest of us use to start stove fires."

Crane laughed—a real laugh, warm, and for a second I almost liked him. "Paper," he said. "Yes. Paper is exactly the trouble. The government survey of 1936 mapped the lower gorge and stopped. Officially, the upper gorge was judged impassable and not worth the cost. Unofficially—" he tapped the framed map behind him without looking at it, the way a man taps a scar—"the surveyor did go up. He found something. He wrote a report. And the report never came down the river."

"What kind of report?"

"A mineral survey." Crane said it lightly, the way a man says *a small inheritance* when he means a fortune. "Copper, mostly. Possibly more. Enough that certain parties in the years since have spent a great deal of money trying to confirm it, and other parties have spent even more making sure no one else does. Mr. Halloway's expedition was funded by my company to locate that camp and recover whatever documentation survived. Photographs, field notes, samples. Historical material." He smiled. "Sentimental value, we call it in the trade."

"And now they're not answering their radio."

"And now they're not answering their radio," he agreed, and let the silence sit there between us like a third guest.

I drank the coffee. It was, annoyingly, excellent. "Mr. Crane, I'll be plain, because it saves us both time. You want me to fly upriver, find out what happened, and bring back whatever's in that crate marked DO NOT OPEN. Triple pay was mentioned."

"Triple, plus a bonus on delivery of the materials."

"And if the people up there need more than a look—if they need lifting out, or medicine ferried, or anything that isn't in the contract—"

"Then you'll be compensated for that too. Generously. I'm not a monster, Mr. Yarrow." He said it with such pleasant certainty that I believed he genuinely wasn't, in the way that a flood isn't malicious. It just rises.

"But?" I said, because there was a *but*. There's always a *but*, and with a man like Crane the *but* is the whole speech and everything before it is the wrapping.

"But," he said, and unfolded his hands, and reached into the desk drawer, "there is a small matter I'd rather settle now than later. For both our comforts."

He set a folder on the desk. Not a thick one. Thin folders are worse than thick ones; thick ones mean someone collected everything, thin ones mean someone collected *enough*. It was sealed with a strip of tape across the flap, and on the tape, in neat block capitals, someone had written a number and the word *CONFIDENTIAL*.

"This is yours, actually," Crane said. "Or rather, it concerns you. Which in law is nearly the same thing and in practice is considerably better."

I didn't touch it. "What is it?"

"It's a file, Mr. Yarrow. From a mining registry office in the south, where records go to sleep but don't die. It concerns a land claim filed in 1951 by Elias Yarrow—surveyor, prospector, and, I understand, pilot of some local renown—covering approximately eleven thousand acres along the upper Rivergate, including a good stretch of the gorge itself." He watched me the way a man watches a gauge. "The claim was never abandoned. Never lapsed. It simply... waited. And claims wait for heirs."

The room got very quiet. Outside, down on the strip, somebody was hand-propping a Cub, and the engine caught on the third swing with its usual asthmatic cough, and I remember being grateful for the sound because it gave the silence somewhere to go.

"My father's dead," I said.

"So the record suggests. A crash, I believe. Twenty years ago. Very sad. The claim, however, doesn't require him to be alive—it requires him to be *unresolved*. No death certificate was ever filed with the registry. No probate. No transfer. As far as the law is concerned, Elias Yarrow is a man of exemplary patience who has simply not come down off that mountain yet." Crane folded his hands again. "And you, Gideon, are his son."

"Gideon Marchetti," I said. "That's the name on my license."

"Is it." He said it flatly, not a question, and picked up the folder and turned it over once, slowly, like a card player showing the deck is honest. "Marchetti. Your mother's family, I'd guess. Italian, hardworking, respectable. But names are like paint, Mr. Yarrow—they cover the wood, they don't become it. I knew your father slightly. A better man than the gossip allowed, for what my opinion's worth. And when I heard a young pilot named Marchetti had appeared out of nowhere five years ago with a bush plane, a hundred hours too many for his age, and a way of reading a river chart like he'd drawn it—I confess I was curious. Curiosity is my profession. So I made inquiries. Modest ones. And I found a boy from a town down south who vanished at nineteen, right around the time a certain scandal was in the papers, and reappeared here under his mother's maiden name with calluses in all the right places." He spread his soft hands. "I'm not judging. God knows half the interior is built by people running from the other half. I'm merely observing that you and I have something in common: we both prefer the past to stay where we put it."

The coffee had gone cold in my hands, or I had gone cold around the coffee; it was hard to say which. Down on the strip the Cub droned away northward, small and free, and I hated it a little.

"What do you want, Crane?"

"To make you an offer, and to be honest about the terms, because I find honesty is cheaper in the long run." He leaned forward. "Fly upriver. Find my people. If they're alive, bring them home—that part I mean sincerely, and I'll pay for the fuel myself, contract or no contract. If the materials are recoverable, bring them down. In exchange: triple rate, the bonus, and this." He pushed the folder an inch toward me. "The file comes to you. Unopened by anyone else. The registry copy, the correspondence, everything I've gathered. It burns in your stove tonight, or it sits in your footlocker—your choice entirely. Either way, it stops being mine and becomes nobody's. Your name stays Marchetti. Your father stays dead on paper. Nobody in Rivergate ever has to learn that the quiet little freight dog with the good landing technique is the heir to eleven thousand acres of gorge he's never claimed."

"And if I say no?"

Crane sighed, and it was a good sigh, regretful, well-rehearsed by a man who had probably sighed at a hundred negotiations and meant it at least twice. "Then nothing terrible happens today. I hire someone else—there are pilots further south who ask fewer questions. The file goes back in my drawer, where files have a way of eventually getting read. Perhaps by a lawyer. Perhaps by a journalist, if the story ever gets large enough to be worth one. Perhaps by whoever ends up litigating that claim, which will happen someday regardless, because land like that doesn't stay forgotten forever. And you'll still be flying mail and medicine under a borrowed name, waiting for the day somebody else connects the same dots I connected over a bottle of wine and an afternoon of boredom." He shrugged, apologetic. "I'm not threatening you, Mr. Yarrow. I'm describing gravity."

There it was. Blackmail dressed in a dinner jacket, holding out its arm and calling itself opportunity. And the worst of it—the part that sat in my chest like a stone swallowed wrong—was that the offer was real. He *would* pay. He *did* want the survivors home; dead employees raise questions, and Crane struck me as a man who disliked questions aimed at himself. The kindness and the leverage came from the same hand, poured from the same pot, and there was no telling where one ended and the other began.

I looked at the folder. Thin. One number, one word. Somewhere inside it was my father's signature, probably—the same hand that had penciled *E.Y.* in the corner of every chart I'd ever flown by, the hand I hadn't seen since I was nineteen and the radio in our kitchen had gone on saying nothing, hour after hour, while my mother made tea she never drank.

Twenty years ago he'd flown up that river and the river had kept him. Now his son sat in a room above a trading post being offered the paperwork of his ghost, priced at three charters and a lie of silence.

"You knew him," I said. "My father."

"Slightly," Crane said again. "He beat me at cards once, in this very room, when it was someone else's room. Told me the gorge was the finest country God ever left unfinished." Something moved behind the soft eyes—quick, genuine, gone. "I've wondered for twenty years what he found up there. Professionally, I mean."

"Professionally," I repeated.

"Entirely." He smiled, and this time the smile didn't quite reach all the way home. "Well. You'll want to think it over. Everyone says they will, and everyone decides within a day, so I won't insult you by pretending the thinking matters much. But do check the barometer before you decide anything at all." He nodded toward the window, toward the south, where the sky over the far hills had gone a bruised yellow-green that any pilot reads like a headline. "That front's been building for two days. When it breaks, the rivergate closes—rain upstream means the gorge runs brown and nothing flies in or out for a week, maybe two. Whatever you choose, Mr. Yarrow, you'll want to choose it inside the next forty-eight hours."

I stood. My legs felt like borrowed equipment. At the door I stopped, hand on the frame, and asked the question I'd carried up the stairs without admitting it:

"If I go up there—and if I find something of his—what happens to it? Really. Not the contract version."

Crane considered me for a long moment, and for once the performance dropped, and what looked out at me was just an old man above a river, tired in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"Honestly? I don't know," he said. "I've spent twenty years wanting to know what happened in that gorge, and I've learned enough to suspect that knowing might be the most expensive thing a man can buy." He gathered the folder, held it out. "Take it. Read it or burn it. Consider it a down payment on whichever man you decide to be."

I took it. It weighed almost nothing.

Outside, the stairs creaked under me and the river ran loud and ordinary below, and the southern sky had begun, quietly, to stack itself into towers. Forty-eight hours, he'd said. I stood on the landing with my father's file under my arm and watched the weather build, and understood that Aldous Crane hadn't given me a choice at all.

He'd given me a countdown.

* * *

Back at the float shack, I put the folder on the table and didn't open it. I made coffee—pilot coffee, boiled past all recognition—and drank it standing up, out of grim necessity, and stared at the sealed tape and the block-capital number until the light through the window went strange and green.

Then I pulled my charts of the upper Rivergate out of the tube, unrolled them across the table over the top of the folder, and weighted the corners with fuel caps and a wrench.

The blank space was still there. It was always there. Two letters in the corner, patient as stone.

*E.Y.*

Forty-eight hours. I got out a pencil and started doing arithmetic.
