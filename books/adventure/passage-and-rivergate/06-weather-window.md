## 6. Weather Window

The barometer at Rivergate's airfield office had a hairline crack across its face, and old Tess Womack swore it read two points low in dry weather and three points high when her knees acted up. Gideon had learned to split the difference. On the morning after Crane's offer, the needle sat hunched down near STORM like a cat that knew something.

He stood in the doorway with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand and watched the sky do its arithmetic. The front was still a day out—way out, past the ridgeline to the northwest, stacked up in bruised purple tiers with that flat, hammered look that meant business. Behind it, if the charts on Tess's wall were honest, came forty-eight hours of wind and rain, and then a window. A clean one, maybe thirty hours wide, maybe less, before the next system shouldered in behind it like a late guest pushing through a door.

Thirty hours. Enough to fly upriver, land, look around, and fly back. Or enough to become a footnote in someone else's report.

"You're doing the thing," Tess said from behind him.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at weather like it owes you money." She came past him with an armload of chart tubes and thumped them into the cabinet by the door. Tess was seventy-one, ran the strip's fuel concession and half its gossip trade, and had once, according to everyone in town including herself, turned back a forest service superintendent for flying overweight. "You took the job, then."

"I said I'd think about it."

"You said you'd think about it while looking at me like a man reading his own obituary. That's a yes where I come from." She snapped the cabinet shut. "Triple rate. For a man who counts his money like a miser counts teeth, that's not thinking. That's arithmetic."

Gideon drank the cold coffee anyway. "It's a supply run," he said. "Medicine's already loaded. I'll add some fuel drums for their generator, take a look at the camp, radio back what I see. In and out before the second front."

"Uh-huh." Tess looked at him the way she looked at pilots who rounded their weight-and-balance numbers to the nearest friendly figure. "And the gorge?"

"I'm not going anywhere near the gorge."

The lie sat between them, plain as a dropped wrench. She let it lie there. That was one of the things he liked about Tess; she understood that some lies were load-bearing.

* * *

He spent the morning stripping the plane.

This was the part of the work nobody romanticized, and the part he loved most—the honest labor of subtraction. Every pound you didn't carry was a pound of margin, and margin was the only religion he'd ever kept. The Cessna 180 on floats was already a workingman's aircraft, no upholstery to speak of, but there was always more to take out. He pulled the spare seat cushions and left the frames. He took out the cargo net and replaced it with lighter tie-downs. He unbolted the second battery he carried for winter starts and set it on the shelf in the hangar, where it would sit like a dog told to stay.

Then he went over the engine himself, though Pete Aldous—no relation to Aldous Crane, a coincidence both men found funny for about four seconds—had signed off the annual two months ago. Compression check. Oil screen cut open and examined under the good light, every fleck of metal accounted for. Magneto drop, smooth. He re-torqued everything that could be torqued and greased everything that could be greased, and when he was done he wiped his hands on a rag that had been wiping hands since before he owned the plane, and stood back, and felt the particular emptiness that comes after preparation, which is just fear wearing coveralls.

"Just another charter," he said out loud, to the hangar, to nobody.

The hangar declined to comment.

By noon he was hauling fuel drums down to the dock on the hand cart, and this was where the day stopped being abstract. Forty-four gallons to a drum, and each drum weighed better than two hundred sixty pounds full, and the plane could only lift so much of anything, and every drum of fuel he carried north was a drum of something else he couldn't. He did the math three times on the back of a fuel slip, because math done once is a hope and math done three times is a plan.

Two drums lashed inside. One strapped to each float. It was ugly, it was legal-ish, and it would make the plane handle on the water like a barge with ambitions, but it meant he could top off at the gravel bar below the camp and still have enough to get home against a headwind. If there was a gravel bar. If there was a camp. If there was anyone left to need the fuel.

He caught himself doing that—stacking ifs like sandbags—and made himself stop. Thinking about outcomes was how you talked yourself out of airplanes. You thought about the next hour. The next leg. The next thing your hands needed to do. Everything else was weather, and weather was God's problem.

Tess found him at the dock, sweating through his shirt, wrestling the last drum onto the float strap.

"Crane called again," she said. "Twice. Second time he asked for you by a name I didn't recognize."

Gideon's hands went still on the ratchet. "What name?"

"He said, tell Mr. Yarrow—" she watched him carefully, enjoying it and not enjoying it at the same time, which was a skill— "that the window closes Thursday."

The ratchet clicked twice more under Gideon's fingers, tighter than it needed to be. So Crane knew. Of course Crane knew. A man who kept sealed files on people kept current ones too. Somewhere in a drawer in Rivergate or Vancouver or wherever men like Crane kept their drawers, there was a page with GIDEON YARROW printed on it, and somewhere behind that page was the nineteen-year-old who had walked away from a courthouse steps and a newspaper photographer and a mother who wouldn't stop crying, and changed his name to the sound of an arrow leaving a string.

Nobody here called him Yarrow. Nobody here called him much of anything except Gid, or that bush pilot, or—his favorite, earned honestly over nine years—*the man who landed on the Kettle River bar in a crosswind and walked away*. Names were things other people needed. He'd done fine without one.

"Tell him," Gideon said, "that I don't take scheduling advice from sponsors."

"I already told him something similar with more words in it." Tess squinted up at the northwest sky, where the front had begun, very slowly, to eat the first ridge. "That's a bad-looking customer coming."

"They all are, till you're through them."

"That's exactly the kind of sentence that gets carved on small stones," Tess said, and went back up the dock to yell at a fisherman moored crooked.

* * *

The afternoon went to weights and paperwork and the small ceremonies of departure.

He filed the flight plan himself, in the office, printing carefully: CHARTER, SUPPLIES AND INSPECTION, UPRIVER, ETA RETURN WITHIN WINDOW. He wrote *inspection* because it was true—he intended to inspect—and because it sounded like something a cautious man would do, and he was trying, very hard, to be a cautious man. Under PASSENGERS he wrote NONE, and the pen hesitated over the paper for half a second, because some animal part of him suspected that by Friday that line would be a lie, and he hated lying on forms. It was the one kind of lying he'd never practiced.

The medicine went aboard last, double-cased and padded, riding in the spot where a passenger's feet would go. Antibiotics, fever reducers, IV fluid, the whole quiet pharmacy of places where people get sick far from help. He'd doubled the order himself when he packed it in Rivergate, on instinct, the same instinct that made him carry two knives and memorize exits. Instinct wasn't evidence. But instinct had kept him alive through nine years of measured miles, and he'd stopped arguing with it around year three.

At five o'clock the front arrived early, the way fronts do when they want to ruin your confidence, and the first rain came down the lake in gray sheets, bending the water flat, hammering the hangar roof like applause from a crowd that wanted something from him.

He sat in the office with Tess and listened to it and drank tea instead of coffee, because coffee at five o'clock meant a night of staring at the ceiling, and he needed sleep the way a gambler needs chips.

"Radio says the window opens tomorrow evening," Tess said, marking the forecast sheet without looking up. "Sixteen hundred, maybe earlier. Closes when it closes."

"Then I go tomorrow evening."

"Alone."

"Alone."

She put the pencil down. Outside, the rain was turning the world into a rumor of itself. "Gid." She used the short name deliberately, he could tell, the way you'd tap someone on the shoulder. "I've known you nine years. You count everything. Miles, pounds, dollars, days. You count them so nothing can sneak up on you." She nodded at the window, at the black wall of weather sitting on the far shore. "But you've been counting toward this since the day you walked into my office asking for float time. Whatever's up that river—you've been flying toward it for years. You just didn't have a reason you'd admit to."

Gideon looked at his tea. "It's a supply run."

"Sure it is." She picked the pencil back up. "Bring my plane back."

"It's my plane."

"Then bring yourself back. The plane's replaceable."

He laughed despite himself—a short, real laugh, the first in days—and Tess allowed herself the smallest victory smile and went back to her forecasts, and the rain came down, and the barometer needle crawled lower along its cracked face like a man edging along a ledge.

* * *

He slept badly and woke at four, which was normal, and lay in the bunk above the hangar office listening to the tail end of the front thrash the lake, which was also normal, and then got up and made coffee and read the weather teletype twice, which was where normal ended.

The window was real. Sixteen hundred hours, winds backing to the southwest, ceilings lifting to three thousand along the river valley, a gap in the systems that the forecaster described—with the naked optimism of his profession—as "a favorable interval." Forty-eight hours from now, give or take, the next front would arrive, and after that, according to the same cheerful man, "unsettled and deteriorating," which in river country meant rain upstream, and rain upstream meant the Rivergate rising, and a rising river takes back its gravel bars the way a landlord takes back apartments.

So: go in the window, or don't go at all until the water dropped, and the water might not drop for weeks, and weeks was a word that had a different weight when there were sick people at the end of it.

He spent the morning doing final checks with the focus of a monk and the cheerfulness of a man at his own audit. Fuel total, computed and recomputed. Weight and balance penciled onto the kneeboard, every number honest, because the plane would know if they weren't. Survival kit refreshed. Flare gun. The rifle he almost left, then didn't, telling himself it was for bears, which was even true, in the way that most of his reasons were true—they just weren't the whole truth, the way a chart shows the shoreline but not what's swimming under it.

At noon he walked the docks one last time. The lake was still angry, whitecapped and slamming the floats of the moored boats, but the sky to the southwest had gone strangely clear and gold, that washed light that comes between weathers, and the air smelled like rain that had already happened.

Tess met him at the float with a thermos and an expression she'd clearly been practicing all morning into something casual.

"Tea," she said, handing it over. "For the road. And if you find anyone alive up there, you tell them Rivergate sent its regards."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you bring back the thermos and we'll argue about whose fault the tea was." Her voice held steady but her eyes didn't quite, and Gideon understood suddenly that underneath the bluster Tess had been afraid for him for nine years, quietly, the way you're afraid of a thing you've decided not to name, and that today she'd decided to name it by handing him tea instead.

"Thursday," he said. "I'll be drinking bad tea in your office by Thursday night."

"You'd better be." She stepped back off the float. "And Gid—fly the plane, not the plan. Plans are for people with someone to answer to."

He fired up the engine and let it warm, watching the gauges climb into their green arcs one by one, oil pressure, oil temp, the ammeter flickering as the alternator took the load. The drums rode heavy on the floats. The medicine rode quiet in its case. The plane sat lower in the water than it liked, patient, professional, ready to be asked to do something difficult.

Gideon ran up the engine, checked the mags one final time, and looked north up the lake toward the river mouth, invisible from here but fixed in his mind's eye as precisely as his own signature. Somewhere up there was a camp that had gone silent. Somewhere up there was a gorge his charts refused to finish drawing. And somewhere in the middle distance between those two facts was a man named Yarrow whom he had spent nine years burying, and who appeared, by all recent evidence, to be climbing out of the grave at a rate of roughly one mile per minute.

"Just another charter," he said again, and this time the engine noise swallowed it, which was convenient, because even he was starting to hear how it sounded.

He taxied out, lined up on the long gold lane of calm water the between-weathers light had laid down for him, and pushed the throttle forward.

The floats slapped, then smoothed, then flew, and the wheels of his ordinary life left the surface of the earth, and Gideon Yarrow—whoever that was—climbed north into the window.
