## 7. Upstream

The weather officer at Rivergate had a face like a man who'd been told bad news so often it had become a kind of hobby. He stood in the doorway of his shack with a mug of coffee he never drank and pointed north with his chin.

"Front's coming down out of the high country," he said. "You've got a window. Maybe."

"Maybe is my favorite word," Gideon said, cinching the last strap on the fuel drum.

"That's what worries me about you, Yarrow."

Gideon grinned and pulled on his headset. "Everybody needs a hobby."

The truth was that Gideon had spent ten years learning to read the sky the way other men read faces, and the sky that morning was lying to him. It wore a pleasant expression—high scattered cloud, good visibility, a breeze out of the southeast that smelled faintly of rain but nothing worse—but underneath it there was a tension, a kind of held breath, the way a room goes quiet just before an argument. He'd flown into enough arguments to know one when he saw it forming.

He went anyway. That was the arrangement. Crane's money was in his account, the drums were lashed in the back, and Mara Voss and whoever was left of her expedition were nine days past silence, which in the interior meant they were either dead, dying, or too busy staying alive to crank a radio. Gideon had made a career of not thinking about which. He told himself this was just another charter, the way a man tells himself the fourth drink is just another drink.

He ran the preflight twice. He always ran it twice, and the second time he found what he was looking for: nothing. Which should have been reassuring, and wasn't, because the plane was the only honest thing in his life and it had nothing to hide.

"Forty-eight hours," the weather officer called after him as Gideon taxied out. "After that, don't come crying to me."

"I never cry," Gideon said. "I bill."

The laugh he got back was thin, but it was a laugh, and he took it up into the air with him like a good-luck charm.

* * *

The first hour was the kind of flying that made the whole profession worth its bad coffee. The Rivergate valley unspooled beneath him in long green loops, the water running silver where the morning caught it, and Gideon settled into the hum of the engine the way a man settles into a familiar chair. He'd made this run in pieces a dozen times—mail to the fish camps, fuel to the placer outfits, a dentist once, God help everyone—but never this far, and never alone with this much time to think.

Time to think was a hazard. He'd built his life to avoid it. Miles were better than years; a man counting miles always had something to do with his hands. But the river below was doing something the river didn't usually do this early in the season. It was running high. Brown in the bends, white at the shallows, carrying more water than the calendar said it should.

"Snowmelt," he told himself. "Early thaw. Nothing personal."

The sky, apparently, took that as an invitation.

By the second hour the pleasant clouds had begun to organize. They came up off the ridgelines in the east like a crowd leaving a stadium, tall and grey-bottomed, and they were building in the wrong direction—building toward him, which weather is not supposed to do, weather being supposed to mind its own business. Gideon climbed to keep his options open, then climbed again, and the engine—faithful, honest, four hundred hours since overhaul—gave a small cough.

He went very still.

Every pilot has a relationship with their engine, and Gideon's was built on a single principle: never ask it a question you don't want answered. The cough came again, a hesitation in the rhythm, a skipped heartbeat. Then it smoothed out, ran clean for a full minute, and he let himself breathe.

"Say something," he murmured. "Come on. Talk to me."

The engine talked. Mostly it said what it had always said, but underneath the words there was a new note, faint and wrong, like a piano string a quarter-tone flat. Gideon had ten thousand hours and he'd learned to hear a quarter-tone. He also knew what it usually meant: water in the fuel, or a fouling plug, or something beginning to let go inside the cylinders that would, given enough time and enough sky, finish letting go.

He checked the charts. The camp was still better than an hour north, up a valley that narrowed toward the gorge, and the terrain was rising to meet him the whole way. Behind him, the weather he'd outrun was now a grey wall with his name on it.

The arithmetic was simple and it was ugly. Turn back and land long, in weather that was closing behind him, and lose the window—maybe the whole season. Press on and trust an engine that had just cleared its throat in a way engines shouldn't.

Gideon Yarrow had one rule above all the others: never make a decision you can't undo. He looked at the wall of weather, looked at the rising ground, looked at the fuel gauges, which were honest as always, and understood that he had already made the decision, somewhere back over the fish camps, when he'd loaded the drums instead of turning around.

"Fine," he told the engine. "But if you quit on me, I'm telling everyone you died of natural causes."

The engine, having no sense of humor, coughed again.

* * *

The valley narrowed. The river beneath him straightened and quickened, and Gideon saw the water doing something that made the hair on his arms stand up: it was running backward in the eddies, churning, confused, the way water does when there's a lot more of it coming. The rain upstream wasn't a rumor anymore. It was a fact, and the river was its messenger, and the message was *hurry*.

He dropped lower to get out of the building turbulence and the engine gave its worst performance yet—a hard stutter, three beats of silence that lasted a geological age, then a catch and a roar and a smell of something hot. Not smoke. Hot metal. The difference mattered, and the difference was bad.

Gideon did what he always did, which was to slow his breathing and start talking to himself in the tone he used for passengers.

"All right. Options." He scanned the terrain. The valley walls were forested right down to the water, black spruce standing shoulder to shoulder like a jury. Gravel bars dotted the river where the bends widened—thin, grey, wet-looking, some of them barely the length of his living room. "Options. Options."

There. Ahead and to the left, where a creek came in from the east, the bar widened into a long grey tongue of stone, maybe four hundred feet of it, maybe more, running with the wind. It was the best thing he'd seen in an hour, which was like saying it was the tallest midget in the room, but Gideon had landed on worse and walked away, and the engine had stopped asking permission.

He made his approach the way he did everything—measured, twice-checked, no heroics. Wheels on gravel at the upstream end, rolling, rolling, the plane shuddering over the cobbles, slowing, slowing, and stopping with forty feet of bar to spare and the tail swinging gently in the breeze like a dog deciding whether to be pleased.

Gideon sat for a moment with his hands on the yoke and listened.

The engine idled. It idled rough, with that flat note underneath, but it idled, and after a minute he shut it down before it could change its mind about anything.

The silence that followed was enormous. River noise, wind noise, the ticking of cooling metal, and underneath it all the sound of a man alone with his own decisions. Gideon got out and stood on the gravel and looked north, up the valley, toward where the camp was—two hours' walk if the going was good, longer if it wasn't, and the going never was.

Then he looked up.

The sky over the southern ridge had changed its expression entirely. The crowd was out of the stadium now, and it was coming this way, and the leading edge of it was the color of a bruise. Behind him, the bar he'd landed on ran down to the water, and the water was higher than it had been when he'd set the wheels down. Not much higher. An inch. Maybe two.

He'd landed on an island that was shrinking.

"Well," Gideon said to the river, in the reasonable tone he used for weather officers and engines and other parties who refused to negotiate. "That's not ideal."

He got the drums off the plane and rolled them up to the high end of the bar, above a line of bleached driftwood that marked, if the river had a sense of humor, the high-water mark of some previous argument between the water and the land. He lashed the plane to two of the sturdiest spruce trunks he could find, double-anchored, and dug out his pack: rope, tarp, first-aid kit, the .303 he carried for bears and had never once needed, a flare gun, two days of food he'd packed for a man who expected to be back the same day and had lied to himself about it thoroughly.

Then he stood at the downstream end of the bar and did the thing he was best at, the thing that had kept him alive for ten years: he counted.

Fuel: enough to get out, if the engine held, if the weather held, if the bar held. Weight: the drums, the gear, himself, and—somewhere north of here, in a camp that had gone quiet—however many survivors a fever left standing. Weather: a window that was closing at a rate he could almost hear, like a door on a bad hinge. Time: less of it than any of the numbers wanted.

And underneath all the counting, quiet as a man at the back of a church, the other thought. The one he'd been not-thinking since Crane had said the name *Yarrow* in that soft, careful voice, like a man laying a card face down on a table.

His father had flown up this same valley. His father's charts ended in blank space up ahead, past the gorge, and in that blank space there was nothing but initials and a twenty-year absence, and Gideon was about to walk into it because a rich man had paid him to and a river had left him no choice, and both of those reasons were true and neither of them was the real one.

The first drops of rain hit the gravel around him, fat and warm and scattered, like the sky clearing its throat.

Gideon pulled his collar up, checked the rifle, and started walking north along the shrinking edge of the world.

The camp, when he reached it—if he reached it—was going to have answers in it. He could feel them waiting the way you feel a toothache waiting: not yet, but soon, and worse than you're ready for.

He walked faster.
