## 8. Camp Hollow

The last half mile I walked the way my father taught me to walk country I didn't trust: heel first, slow, listening between my own footsteps.

Not that he'd taught me much else that stuck. But that one had burrowed in deep enough to survive twenty years of me trying to dig it out.

I'd left the Cessna on the gravel bar at 0740 with the engine cowl still ticking and a plan that fit on one hand: follow the cut line up through the spruce, find the camp, assess, walk back, decide. Simple. The kind of plan you make when you haven't yet seen what you're walking toward.

The forest had opinions about my plan. The cut line — the swath Crane's advance team had supposedly cleared for pack trains two seasons back — had already begun forgetting itself. Alder leaned across it in green elbows, and in places the only evidence a trail existed was the absence of anything taller than knee-height, a corridor of saplings and devil's club that grabbed at my sleeves like beggars who'd heard I had money. Twice I lost it entirely and had to climb a deadfall to get my bearings off the river's sound, which came up through the trees as a long gray rumor, the way water sounds when there's too much of it for comfort.

I talked to myself while I walked. Not because I'm unbalanced — though the jury remains out — but because talking keeps your breathing even and your mind off the arithmetic of distance. My father used to sing. Hymns, mostly, badly. I do inventory.

"Sixty feet of rope," I said to an alder. "Two days of food if I'm honest, three if I lie to myself. First-aid kit, the good one, the one I bought after the Ketchikan thing and never thanked God I had until now. Flare gun. One thermos of coffee going cold against my spine."

The alder declined to comment.

What struck me, as the morning wore on and the terrain began to flatten toward the river terrace where the coordinates said *camp*, was how quiet it wasn't. I'd expected silence — the dead-camp silence you read about in the kind of novels I pretend not to read. Instead the woods were riotously, insultingly alive. Ravens arguing over something in the canopy. A squirrel scolding me from a height of perfect safety. Somewhere close, water running where water hadn't been running when I studied the maps, which is the kind of detail a bush pilot notices and then wishes he hadn't, because new water means new rain upstream, and new rain upstream means the gravel bar under my airplane was living on borrowed time.

"Note it and move on," I told myself, and did, and mostly succeeded in not thinking about it, which is a skill I've honed to a professional standard.

Then I smelled woodsmoke. Faint, old, cold — smoke that had finished being a fire days ago and was now just a memory hanging in the spruce. And underneath it, another smell I knew from a bad year in Fort Nelson when a hunting party came out short two men: the sweetish, wrong smell of a body that has stopped being a person and started being a problem.

My stomach did what stomachs do. I let it, stood very still for ten seconds with my hand on a trunk, breathed four times, and then kept walking, because that's the job. That's always been the job. You feel the thing and then you put the thing down and pick up the next thing, and if you're lucky the things stay separate.

* * *

I saw the tents before I saw the camp, which tells you how wrong the camp was.

Tents — good ones, expedition-grade, the kind that cost more than my annual oil budget — don't shred themselves. These looked like they'd been opened by something patient and enormous. Canvas hung in ribbons. Poles lay snapped at their joints, laid parallel, almost tidy, the way a child might arrange sticks. Wind could tear a tent; wind doesn't fold the wreckage.

And the camp was arranged around a absence. Fire ring in the middle, black and rained-on. Supply crates stacked in a windbreak with real care. A tarp strung between two surviving poles, sagging full of last night's rain. Everything positioned as if expecting more people than were present. A camp built for six, holding — by the look of it — nobody.

"Hello the camp!" I called, because you call first, always, even when you're fairly sure of the answer. Especially then. "Gideon Yarrow! Bush charter out of Rivergate! I'm coming in!"

Nothing answered except a raven lifting off the supply crates with an indignant croak, having clearly been enjoying the accommodations.

I gave it a full minute anyway. Then I went in.

The radio was the first thing I knelt at. It sat on an overturned crate near the fire ring — a HF set, expedition quality, and somebody had killed it with a rock. Not smashed in panic; destroyed with attention. The case was stove-in at the transformer, the tuning dial cracked clean through, the handset separated from its cord and placed on top of the wreckage like a funeral offering. Panic breaks things everywhere. This was broken in exactly the right place, by someone who understood radios well enough to know that everything else was cosmetic.

Somebody in this camp had wanted no one talking to the outside world. Or wanted no one inside it talking to them.

I sat back on my heels and made myself look at the rest.

* * *

The dead man was in the second-largest tent, laid out with more care than the radio had received. That mattered, I decided later. That was the thread I held onto.

He was maybe fifty, heavyset, wearing expedition layers gone gray with sweat-stain. Someone had folded his hands on his chest and pulled a sleeping bag up to his chin and crossed his boots at the foot of the bag, neat as a display window. There was a paper tag tied to his wrist with dental floss — actual dental floss, which would have made me laugh in any other week — and on the tag, in small, disciplined handwriting:

**Dr. Emory Ladd. Fever, day nine. He was kind to everyone. Please take him home.**

No name signed. Just the request, sitting there in floss-tied patience, addressed to whoever came. Addressed, I supposed, to me.

I crouched by him a long moment. I'm not a praying man — prayer requires a belief in being heard, and I've spent most of my adult life cultivating the opposite skill — but I said something anyway, low, the way you talk to a sleeping stranger on a night flight. Told him he was going home. Told him someone had done right by him here, whatever else had happened.

Fever, the tag said. Day nine. Which meant it started before the missed check-ins, which meant the radio silence hadn't been an accident of weather or bad luck. Somebody had been sick, and somebody else had decided the world didn't need to know.

I checked him properly, because triage instinct doesn't consult your stomach. No injury. No sign of violence. Just a man emptied out by something his body couldn't fight, in a place too far from anywhere. His face in death was ordinary and tired, and I found that worse than horror, somehow. Horror you can act against. Ordinary tiredness just sits there.

* * *

The mess tent — or what remained of its frame — told the next part of the story, told it in the way camps tell stories, which is all in what's missing.

Six plates, by my count of the washing basin: six people had eaten here regularly. Six cups. But the supplies told a different arithmetic. The medical crate was open and nearly empty — and I mean *nearly empty* in a way that made me think of the doubled antibiotics on the manifest in Rivergate, the ones I'd noticed and filed under *sponsor's thoroughness*. They'd burned through a course of treatment meant for emergencies. Whatever this fever was, it hadn't responded politely.

The food stores were strange too. Rations for weeks, barely touched — but every chocolate item gone, every coffee brick gone, the sugar tin open and light as a promise. Sugar and caffeine. Fuel for people staying awake in shifts. Guarding something.

And there — on the map table, weighted down with a stone — a notebook, open, its last written page facing the ceiling. I picked it up. Field log, expedition stationery, the entries in several hands. Most of the recent pages had been torn out. Not ripped in haste: torn cleanly, along the perforation, one after another, and the stubs counted. Eleven pages removed.

You don't tear out eleven pages of a field log unless those pages are the reason for everything else. Unless those pages are cargo.

I stood in the wrecked mess of a camp built for six and holding one dead man, and I did the math I'd been avoiding since the tree line.

Six plates. One body. Two sets of boot prints leading away from camp on the north trail — I'd crossed them without understanding, fresh-ish prints, two walkers, unhurried, heading toward the gorge. That left two unaccounted for, and both possibilities were bad in opposite directions: either they were somewhere in this camp hiding from me, or they were somewhere worse.

I called out again, louder this time, and put everything I had into making it sound safe. "Hello the camp! I'm alone, I'm unarmed, I flew in from Rivergate looking for you! If you can hear me, I've got medicine and a plane on the bar!"

Silence. The raven came back and settled on the supply crates like a landlord.

But as I turned from the fire ring, I caught it — movement, or the ghost of movement, at the edge of the tree line on the far side of camp. Not a person. A shape of canvas: a small pup tent, pitched tight and low back among the spruces, deliberately out of sightline from the trail, deliberately apart. The kind of placement you choose when you want to see anyone coming and be seen by no one.

And in front of it, arranged on a flat stone with a deliberateness that raised every hair I own:

Three items. A water bottle, full. An energy bar. And a page torn from a notebook, weighted with a pebble, ink bleeding slightly from the damp.

I approached the way you approach a dog of unknown temper — sideways, hands visible, talking the whole time. "It's okay. I'm just going to read it. Nobody's going to touch anything else."

The note was in the same disciplined hand as the tag on Dr. Ladd's wrist. It said:

**If you have a plane: two of us are alive. We will come out when we decide to. Do not go to the gorge. Do not open the strongbox. Take Dr. Ladd and go.**

Below that, smaller, as if added later by a different and less certain pen:

**Please.**

I stood there holding the page, reading it a third time, while the ravens commented and the hidden water ran its new channel somewhere below the terrace and the whole silent camp watched me with its hundred snapped-pole eyes.

Two alive. Willing to negotiate terms with their own rescue. Afraid — not of fever, not of the river, not of me, or not only of me. Afraid of a box, and a gorge, and whatever eleven torn pages had said.

Any sensible pilot would have shouldered Dr. Ladd's dignity into a body bag, hiked back to the bar, and flown home to file the ugliest report of his career. Any sensible pilot would have followed instructions. Instructions were, after all, my entire philosophy of the sky: measured miles, known quantities, nothing volunteered.

I folded the note into my breast pocket, where it lay against my heartbeat like a second compass needle pointing somewhere I didn't want to go.

Because the gorge was north. And the two sets of boot prints led north. And twenty years ago, in a kitchen that smelled of coffee and hymns, a man with my same crooked smile had kissed my mother's cheek and said he'd call in from the Rivergate on Friday.

Nobody had ever told me where the calls stopped.

Now I was standing in the exact shape of the silence.

I looked north, where the trees thickened toward the rim of a country my own charts refused to draw, and I heard myself say — out loud, to the ravens, to the dead man in his careful tent, to nobody —

"Well," I said. "Damn it."
