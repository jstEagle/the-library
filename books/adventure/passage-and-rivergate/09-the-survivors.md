## 9. The Survivors

The rifle spoke before I did.

I'd heard it, actually—a single flat crack that rolled down the valley and came back off the gorge walls like a question. I dropped flat behind a fallen spruce with my heart doing something unscheduled in my chest, and for a long moment the only sounds were the river muttering to itself and my own breathing, loud as a bellows.

Then a voice, young and cracked: "That's a warning shot! I've got more!"

"Good," I called back, staying down. "Because I've got none."

Silence. The kind of silence where a person is thinking hard.

"I'm Gideon Yarrow," I said. "Charter pilot out of Rivergate. Crane sent me. There's a Beaver on the gravel bar two miles downstream, and if you're who I think you are, you're getting on it."

More silence. Then, grudgingly, from somewhere among the collapsed tents: "How do I know Crane sent you?"

"Because nobody else would," I said. "The man pays triple rates and asks questions afterward. That's his whole personality."

A pause, and then—there it was—the ghost of a laugh, quickly strangled. A young man rose up out of the wreckage of the mess tent like something growing out of compost. Tall, thin, maybe twenty-three, with a week of beard doing its best and failing, and eyes that had seen too much too fast and were still deciding what to do about it. He held the rifle at an angle that said he'd been taught how to hold it recently and under bad circumstances.

"You're really a pilot?"

"I'm really a pilot."

"The plane actually flies?"

"It has opinions," I admitted, standing slowly with my hands visible, "but yes. Mostly it flies when I ask nicely."

He looked me over the way you'd check a rope before trusting it—frayed spots, knots, load rating. Whatever he was looking for, he apparently found enough of it, because the rifle muzzle drifted toward the mud.

"Sam Okafor," he said. "Expedition tech. And—" He stopped. His jaw worked. Behind him, one of the tents sagged against a deadfall, and through its torn wall I could see a sleeping bag, occupied, motionless in a way that sleeping bags shouldn't be.

"And there's someone else," Sam said finally. "Mara. Dr. Voss. She's—she's sick. She's been sick since—" He stopped again. This boy was full of stopped sentences, each one a door he'd shut on himself. "Since before Halloway died."

"Halloway's the one in the bag?"

"He's the one in the bag." Sam said it flat, the way you say things you've had to say over and over to yourself until the words wear smooth. "Fever took him Tuesday. Or Wednesday. I've lost track of the days. Two others went downriver for help four days ago and haven't come back." His voice caught on that last part, and he covered it by coughing. "And Mara's in the cook tent. She won't stay down. She keeps trying to walk to the gorge."

* * *

The cook tent was less a tent than an argument between canvas and weather, and the weather was winning. Inside, the air was thick with the smell of woodsmoke, wet wool, and sickness—that particular sweetish wrongness that your nose learns before your head does. A camp stove ticked. A pot of melted snow sat cold on the burner.

Dr. Mara Voss lay in a nest of every blanket the expedition owned, and she was burning. Not figuratively—I could feel the heat coming off her from three feet away, radiating like a stone pulled from a fire. Her face was flushed dark, her lips cracked, her hair plastered to her forehead in ropes. She was muttering.

I knelt beside her and did the arithmetic I do at altitude when the engine hiccups: how bad, how fast, what can I carry, what must I leave. Fever, high. Dehydration, obvious. She'd been rationing water even while delirious, or someone had been rationing it for her—her canteen hung on the tent pole, nearly empty, deliberately placed out of her reach.

"Mara." I kept my voice low and level, the way you talk to a skittish animal or a nervous passenger. "Mara, I'm here to take you home."

Her eyes opened. They were extraordinary eyes, even now—dark, quick, and currently pointed at two different horizons at once. She looked straight through me at something behind my head.

"Don't," she whispered.

"Don't what? I'm not going to hurt you. I'm a pilot. I'm terrible at hurting people; I mostly just move them around."

"Not the box." Her hand crawled out from under the blankets, fingers grasping at the air, at nothing, at something only she could see. "Don't let him open the box. Don't let him take it down. It stays. It *stays.*"

"There's no box," I said gently. "There's just you and me and a plane."

She focused then. Actually focused, her gaze snapping onto my face with a precision that startled me—and for one strange second, half a heartbeat, something moved behind those fevered eyes that wasn't delirium at all. Something that looked almost like recognition.

Then it drowned again in the fever, and she turned her face away and began to count. Numbers, low and rhythmic, in what might have been French or might have been pure exhaustion. I checked her pulse—fast, thready but regular—and dug through the medical kit Sam brought me until I found the antibiotics, the doubled order from the manifest, the ones I'd noticed back at Rivergate and pretended not to notice. Someone had known. Someone had packed for exactly this.

"How many of those has she had?" I asked Sam, who stood in the tent doorway holding the rifle across his chest like a shield rather than a weapon.

"All of them. Almost. I left two doses." He swallowed. "They weren't working anyway. Halloway took the same course and it didn't—" He stopped. Door number four, shut.

"Water?"

"She won't drink unless I make her. She says the river water is listening."

"Well," I said, "she's not entirely wrong. Rivers do gossip."

It was the wrong joke. Sam flinched like I'd struck him, and I understood, a beat too late, that everything in this camp was the wrong joke. Death doesn't have a sense of humor, and neither, apparently, did whatever had happened here.

* * *

I spent the afternoon being refused.

Not dramatically—nobody raised their voice, nobody waved the rifle. Sam simply declined, with the tireless politeness of the very frightened, to tell me anything at all. What happened to the expedition. Where the other two had gone. Why the radio was a field of shattered ceramic and bent antenna lying in the mud like the skeleton of some small metal bird. Why the strongbox—the one Mara kept reaching for in her fever, the one I found wedged under the collapsed mess table, padlocked, dented, and marked with stenciled letters I recognized from a certain crate at Rivergate—sat within arm's reach of Sam at all times, including, I suspected, when he slept.

I tried the direct approach first, because I'm a direct man. It's cheaper than indirection and crashes less often.

"What happened here, Sam?"

"Fever," he said. "Accidents. Bad luck."

"That's a weather report, not an explanation."

"We got sick. People died. That's what happened." He wouldn't look at me. He was watching the tree line instead, in the constant, scanning way of a man who has learned that the world contains things worth watching for. "You're here for rescue. Rescue us. That's the job."

"It is the job," I agreed. "But I've got two seats' worth of weight to figure, a storm front coming down from the north, and a patient who keeps talking about a box. So the box and I are going to have a conversation eventually. You can introduce us, or we can meet awkwardly later."

Sam's knuckles went white on the rifle stock. "The box isn't yours."

"No," I said. "It appears to be Crane's. Which raises a question I'll ask now so it doesn't fester: why does a man who funds archaeology care this much about a sealed box on a river nobody maps?"

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Sure you don't."

"I *don't.*" Too fast. Too loud. The echo came back off the gorge and Sam winced at his own voice, as if the valley had betrayed him by repeating it.

I let it go. There's a time to push and a time to wait, and bush flying teaches you the difference. You can't force weather and you can't force people; you can only position yourself well and be ready when the window opens. So I positioned myself. I boiled snow for Mara in small doses, tipping it into her a spoonful at a time while she counted and muttered and once, terribly, called out a name—not mine, not Sam's, someone named *Elias*—in a voice stripped down to its bare wires.

I told myself I didn't react. I'm fairly sure I didn't react. But something in my chest went very quiet and very still, the way the air goes still before a storm front, and I filed the name away in the place where I keep things I'm not ready to look at.

* * *

Dusk came down the valley like water filling a bowl. I got a fire going—small, screened, the kind of fire that doesn't advertise—and heated broth over it, and fed Mara as much as she'd take, which wasn't much. Her fever hadn't broken. If anything it had climbed, and she'd begun to shiver even as she burned, which is the body's way of admitting the argument isn't going well.

Sam sat across the fire from me with the rifle across his knees, and for a while neither of us spoke. Young men and old men can share silence better than they can share conversation, sometimes. The river ran past in the dark, talking its endless talk.

"She was a good teacher," Sam said suddenly, to the fire. "Dr. Voss. Before all this. She used to say the ground remembers everything—you just have to ask it politely." He laughed, a small broken sound. "Nobody asked politely enough, I guess."

"What did you find out here, Sam?"

His head came up. In the firelight his face was all angles and shadow, and for a moment he looked older than me, which is a trick only fear can manage.

"Why does everyone keep asking that?" he said. "Halloway asked. Every day, near the end, he'd sit up and ask what we found, like he already knew, like he just needed to hear somebody say it. And now you." He shook his head hard. "We didn't find anything. It's a survey site. Old camp, old stakes, old paper. Nothing anybody would want."

"Then why is it locked?"

"Because rules are rules!" It burst out of him, and immediately he looked appalled at himself. He lowered his voice to a fierce whisper, glancing at the cook tent. "Because Crane said—because the contract said—it goes back sealed or not at all. Sealed inventory, signed chain of custody. If it opens outside authorized—" He ran out of contract language and just breathed for a moment. "If it opens, people get hurt. That's what Halloway said. That's the last thing he said, pretty much. Keep it sealed, keep it sealed, keep it—"

"Sam." I waited until he looked at me. "Who did Halloway think was going to hurt people?"

The fire popped. Somewhere upriver, a branch gave way and fell into the current with a sound like a sigh.

"I want to go home," Sam said, and his voice cracked clean through, and he was twenty-three years old again, and terrified, and alone except for a dying woman, a dead man, a locked box, and a stranger whose motives he had no reason to trust. "I just want to go home. Can you just—can you please just fly us out?"

"Yes," I said. "That's the plan. Both of you, first weather window. But I need you to hear me, son, because I'm only going to say this once." I leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough that the firelight reached both our faces. "I've been flying this country eleven years. I've hauled fish, fuel, mail, coffins, and one memorable shipment of goats. I have never once asked a client what was in the crates, because it's none of my business, and that rule has kept me alive and unbothered. But there are two things I need from you tonight. One: when I say we lift, we lift—no arguments, no going back for anything. Two:" I nodded toward the strongbox, sitting in the shadows between us like a third passenger. "Whatever's in there, whoever wants it, however scared you are of it—it does not outrank her life. Nothing in that box weighs more than she does. When the math gets tight, the box swims. Are we clear?"

Sam stared at me across the fire. And I watched something happen in his face—relief and terror fighting for the same ground, because a man can be desperate for permission and afraid of what the permission costs.

"Clear," he said at last. Barely a whisper.

"Good." I sat back. "Now get some sleep. I'll take first watch."

He hesitated. Then, with the exaggerated care of a man surrendering the last thing he owned, he laid the rifle down beside him—within reach, but no longer clutched—and crawled into the surviving dome tent. Within minutes his breathing went long and ragged with exhaustion, though his hand, even asleep, had found its way to the strap of the box.

I sat by the fire and listened to the river.

Behind me, from the cook tent, faint and steady as a transmission from far away, Mara Voss was counting again. And somewhere in the middle of the numbers, threaded through them like a signal through static, she said the name again—clearer this time, almost conversational, as if she were introducing me:

*"Elias. Elias, he's come back for you."*

The fire settled. The river ran. And I sat very still in the dark with my hands flat on my knees, telling myself the shiver that went through me was just the cold coming off the water.

Tomorrow I'd get her out. Tomorrow I'd get them both out, box or no box, answers or no answers.

Tonight, the name hung in the smoke above the fire, and I could not stop reading it.
