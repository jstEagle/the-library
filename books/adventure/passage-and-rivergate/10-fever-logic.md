## 10. Fever Logic

Gideon had a theory about fever that he'd never shared with anyone, mostly because it wasn't the kind of thing you could say at a dinner party without people quietly moving their chairs. His theory was this: fever didn't make people crazy. It made them honest. It stripped away all the polite furniture a person kept between themselves and the world—the small talk, the deflections, the careful arrangement of the face—and left them sitting in the bare room of themselves, saying exactly what they meant.

Which was why, when Mara Voss opened her eyes at two in the morning and looked directly at him and said, "You have his chin," Gideon's hands went still on the compress he'd been wringing out.

The camp's one intact lantern hung from the ridgepole of the supply tent, turned down to a coal-orange glow. Outside, rain worked steadily on the canvas. Sam was asleep sitting up against the strongbox again, rifle across his knees, head tipped back, mouth open—a boy-shaped question mark even in his sleep. Mara lay on the expedition's folding cot, which Gideon had dragged in from her shredded tent along with her sleeping bag, damp but serviceable. She'd been raving for most of six hours: about sediment layers, about a man named Dev who was dead, about water rising in a place she called the throat.

Now she was looking at him with eyes that were clear as tap water, and she said it again.

"You have his chin. Elias Yarrow's chin."

* * *

Mara surfaced from the dream the way she always did now—through mud, through brown water, through the feeling of being carried somewhere by hands that wouldn't tell her where they were going. The dreams were worse than the fever in some ways. At least the fever had a temperature. The dreams just had Dev, standing on the gravel bar in his red jacket, pointing downstream, saying *you have to tell them what we found*, and her trying to say *found what, Dev, found WHAT*, and him smiling that patient field-school smile and dissolving into current.

Then there was light. A lantern turned low. And a man crouched beside her cot wringing out a cloth, moving with the particular economy of someone who had done fieldwork in bad places—no wasted motion, everything within reach before he needed it.

She knew that economy. She'd spent four months reading a dead man's field notes, learning his habits from his handwriting, the way he crossed his sevens, the way he always noted wind direction first because the survey mattered more than the weather. And she'd spent three days watching this pilot move around the ruined camp, and every hour the resemblance got harder to file under coincidence.

He had the chin. He had the way of listening with his whole body while pretending not to listen at all. He had, when he thought no one was watching, the exact frown Elias Yarrow wore in the 2003 photograph—the one Crane had shown them in Rivergate, sliding it across the table like a man laying down a card he expected to win with.

"You have his chin," she said.

Her voice came out cracked and small. The man went still. Not startled-still. Still the way water goes still over something deep.

"Drink," he said, and held a canteen to her lips, and she drank because her body wanted it even though the rest of her wanted answers, and there was a brief undignified negotiation between those two factions that her body won.

"What's your name?" she asked when she could talk again.

"Gideon." He set the canteen down. "Gideon Marsh."

"Mm," said Mara Voss, who had spent fifteen years in a profession where you learned to hear the pause before a lie the way sailors hear the change in wind. "Marsh."

"That's right."

"Funny," she said. "Because I've been lying here for three days with a fever cooking my brain like an egg, and I've had a lot of time to think, and I keep coming back to one thing. Which is that Aldous Crane does not hire bush pilots named Marsh. Aldous Crane hires bush pilots who owe him money or shame, and he pays them enough to forget their own names, and—" she stopped, because a cough took her, deep and wet, and the man's hand was immediately at her back, steady, professional, and infuriatingly gentle.

"Breathe," he said. "Argue later."

"I'm not arguing," she managed. "I'm excavating."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The archaeological record of a smile.

"There it is," she said. "That's his mouth too."

* * *

Gideon sat back on his heels and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the moment he'd landed on the bar.

Three survivors. One dead. Two missing. A smashed radio. A strongbox guarded like a state secret. And now a delirious archaeologist who, in the space between fevers, had just dismantled his alias with the offhand precision of someone brushing crumbs off a table.

He considered denial. He ran the numbers on it. Denial required her to be stupider than she clearly was, and required him to keep lying to a woman with a 103-degree temperature, which even by his standards was poor sportsmanship.

"How long have you known?" he asked.

"Since you landed." She shifted against the cot, wincing. "You flared the plane twice before touching down. Over-cautious into a crosswind. Elias Yarrow wrote about doing that—his instructor beat it into him and he never lost the habit. I've read his notes so many times I could recite them. Wind first, then water, then ground. You checked the river before you even shut down the engine. I thought, well. Either this man studied Yarrow obsessively, or—"

"Or."

"Or." Her eyes were burning bright now, fever-bright and something else, something sharper underneath. "Say it. Say the name. I want to hear whether it costs you anything."

Outside, the rain thickened. In the corner, Sam stirred, muttered something about manifests, and slept on.

"Elias Yarrow was my father," Gideon said.

It came out level. That surprised him. Twenty years of not saying it, and when it finally came up the well it came up calm, like water that had been sitting so long it had gone flat.

Mara exhaled slowly. "Okay," she said. "Okay. God help me, I hoped I was wrong."

"Why would you hope that?"

"Because if you're his son, then you know what's in that box." Her hand crept toward the edge of the cot, toward the direction of the strongbox, and stopped, trembling. "And if you know what's in that box, then everything gets harder. For everyone. Including you."

"I don't know what's in the box."

"No," she said softly. "But you will. That's the problem. Everyone who finds out what's in that box wants to open it. Crane wants it opened and burned. We were paid to make sure it never got read at all. And you—" she looked at him with terrible clarity, "—you're the one person alive with more right to open it than anyone, and less reason to survive doing it."

* * *

Fever logic, her mother used to call it, back when Mara was a child burning through the flu in a house that smelled of eucalyptus and Vicks: the strange lucidity that arrived at three in the morning, when the body gave up defending itself and the mind walked out onto the ice and found, infuriatingly, that it held.

She was on the ice now. She knew it. Every sentence cost her something, and she spent them anyway, because the man by the lantern had his father's chin and his father's hands and probably his father's habit of walking into gorges, and someone had to say the true things to him before the fever took her under again.

"Don't go to the gorge," she said.

"I haven't said I was going anywhere."

"You've been looking at the maps since you got here. I watched you. You spread them out in Dev's tent like you already knew where you were going, like you were confirming rather than choosing." She pulled the blanket higher, fighting a shiver. "There's nothing there for you. Whatever you think you'll find—"

"I don't think I'll find anything. My charts end twenty miles short of it. Blank space. Somebody drew initials in the margin and gave up."

"Whose initials?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She watched him not answer, and understood that this man had been carrying that blank space on paper for twenty years the way she'd been carrying Dev's last request for three days: folded up small, kept next to the skin.

"The gorge took eleven people in 2003," she said. "Officially seven. Unofficially eleven—I've read the inquiry files, the ones that weren't sealed. The river there isn't a river, it's a verdict. And whatever your father went in there looking for, it killed him, and it nearly killed us, and it did kill Dev, and I am asking you—not as a patient, as the only person here currently capable of forming complete sentences—do not follow him in."

"Why did Crane send you?"

Her breath caught. Not from the coughing this time.

"That's not my story to tell," she said.

"Whose is it?"

"His." She nodded toward Sam, asleep against the box. "And Crane's. And maybe yours, eventually, God forgive us all. But mine—" her voice frayed, "—mine is only the part where I signed something I shouldn't have, and a good man died because of it, and I would burn my career to ash before I let it happen twice."

The lantern flickered. Somewhere outside, the river spoke in its sleep, low and constant, getting louder by the hour. Gideon heard it the way a farmer hears a change in the wind—without wanting to, without being able to stop.

"You should sleep," he said gently.

"I should warn you," she corrected. "Sleep is what I'll do regardless. I'm excellent at it lately. Award-winning." She caught his wrist as he reached to tuck the blanket—caught it with a grip far stronger than a woman her temperature had any right to. "Listen to me. Two things, and then I'll go under, and you can decide whether to believe me when I'm not here to defend myself. One: don't open the box. Two: don't go to the gorge."

"And if I do both anyway?"

"Then you're your father's son," Mara Voss said, "and God help us, we'll need you to be. But you'll wish you hadn't been." Her grip loosened. Her eyes were losing focus, the ice cracking under her. "Elias Yarrow went into that gorge to come home. Remember that. Whatever else is true—that part is true. I've read the last page. I'm the only living person who's read the last page, and—"

She was gone. Under. Breathing fast and shallow, murmuring coordinates to no one.

Gideon sat very still for a long time.

*Last page.* Of what? The box was sealed. Sam guarded it like it contained a live grenade. And this woman, half-cooked by fever, had just told him she'd read its final page—and that it said his father had gone into the gorge *to come home*.

Not running. Coming home.

He looked at the strongbox in the lantern light. Dented steel, expedition stenciling, a padlock that had survived things padlocks weren't rated for. Sam's rifle lay across his knees above it, and the boy's hands, even in sleep, rested near the bolt.

Gideon had flown nine hundred hours without opening other people's cargo. It was practically a religion: you carried what you were given, you delivered it whole, you asked nothing. Ten thousand hours of sky had taught him that every package was somebody's secret, and secrets flew fine as long as nobody shook them.

But his father's name was shaking inside this one. Had been shaking inside it for twenty years.

He stood, checked Mara's pulse—fast, thready, holding—draped the blanket properly, and stepped out into the rain to look at the river.

It had come up four inches since dark. He could see it on the gravel, a fresh dark seam where the water had licked higher than the last high-water mark. Upstream, past the black shoulder of the ridge, the gorge waited with its blank space and its initials, and somewhere in it—if a feverish woman could be believed, and the terrifying thing was how much he wanted to believe her—there was a last page with his name in it, written by a hand that had been dead for twenty years.

Sam appeared beside him in the tent flap, rifle slung, hair flattened by sleep, eyes instantly and completely awake. Seventeen kinds of wary.

"She talked," Sam said. It wasn't a question.

"She talked."

"About the box?"

"About everything except the box." Gideon turned to face him. Rain dripped off the brim of the tent. "Which means you're the one who's going to tell me about the box, Sam. Because the river's coming up, and I need to know what I'm carrying out of here—and whether it's worth dying for. Yours, hers, or mine."

Sam looked at the strongbox. Then at the river. Then, longest of all, at Gideon—at the chin, at the hands, at whatever he was seeing there that made him swallow hard and square his shoulders like a man stepping up to a scale.

"It's about your dad," Sam said.
