## 11. The Gorge Road

Mara Voss told me not to go. She told me twice while her fever burned at a hundred and three, and once more the next morning when it had come down to a hundred and one and she could hold a cup of water without wearing most of it.

"The gorge road," she said, propped against a supply crate I'd dragged into what remained of the mess tent, "is not a road. It's a rumor with ambitions."

"I've heard worse described as roads," I said. "There's a track outside Rivergate that's legally a highway and spiritually a creek bed."

"Gideon." She used my name deliberately, the way you'd set a heavy tool down on a table to make a point. "Don't go to the gorge."

I'd been hearing that instruction from women since I was nineteen years old, and I had never once followed it, which is probably why I was still alive and also why I was still alone. But Mara wasn't warning me off out of spite or fever-dream logic. There was something else under it. Something that looked almost like recognition.

I didn't ask her about that. Not yet. Instead I asked Sam Okafor where the trailhead was, and he looked at me with the particular expression of a young man who has been handed an ethical problem he did not study for.

"You're going anyway," he said.

"Yep."

"And if I don't tell you where the trailhead is?"

"Then I'll find it the way my father found everything—by walking in the wrong direction until it became the right one." I shouldered the pack I'd loaded the night before: water, rope, first aid kit, the flare gun, and a sandwich of questionable optimism. "Besides, you'll tell me. You're a bad liar, Sam. It's one of your better qualities."

He lasted eleven seconds. "Past the deadfall on the north side. Follow the cairns. And—" He stopped himself.

"And?"

"Nothing." He turned back to the radio he was trying to resurrect from its smashed corpse of parts. "Just come back before dark. The valley does things to light after dark."

* * *

The morning was the kind the interior specializes in—cool enough to feel like a gift, clear enough to feel like a setup. I crossed the deadfall, found the first cairn exactly where Sam said it would be, and started up.

The gorge road, for all Mara's slander, had been built by people who knew what they were doing. Switchbacks cut into the hillside, drainage channels still doing their jobs after twenty years of neglect, stones laid flat where the grade steepened. Surveyor work. My father's trade, before he traded it for whatever it was he traded it for.

I counted my steps because counting steps is what I do instead of thinking. Four hundred and twelve by the second cairn. Eight hundred and ninety by the third. The forest thinned as I climbed, the trees giving way to scrub and then to bare rock, and somewhere around step two thousand the gorge opened up below me like a sentence I'd been avoiding finishing.

It was beautiful. That's the thing nobody tells you about the places that ruin your life—they're usually gorgeous. The river ran silver-green through walls of granite streaked with white quartz veins, a thousand feet down and narrow enough that I could hear the water talking to itself even from the rim. The rapids Sam had described were visible as a seam of white stitching the river together, thread after thread of it, unbroken for as far as I could see.

No plane crosses that. No boat runs it willingly. The gorge was a door that only opened one way, and whatever my father had walked through it carrying, he hadn't carried it back.

I stood there longer than I meant to. Long enough for a hawk to circle me twice and lose interest. Long enough to notice that my hands were cold despite the sun.

Then I turned away from the drop and walked on, because the cairns continued along the rim toward a shelf of flat ground half a mile east, and because grief, in my experience, keeps better if you keep moving. Like milk. Like a lie.

* * *

The old camp announced itself before I saw it—a glint of rust in the green, then the corner of a canvas tarp gone the color of weak tea, flapping in a wind too small to justify it.

The surveyor's camp sat on the shelf like something shipwrecked but politely arranged. Two collapsed tents, their poles snapped but stacked with care beside them. A fire ring swept clean. A cache barrel sunk into the ground, its lid warped but latched. And everywhere, tools: a transit on a tripod, legs splayed, its brass body freckled orange with rust; a pickaxe and shovel crossed against a rock like sleeping soldiers; coils of wire rope; a wooden crate stenciled with a shipping company's name that had gone out of business before I was born.

Twenty years of weather, and someone had still tried to keep the place tidy. That detail hit me sideways, harder than anything else so far. Chaos I could have handled. Abandonment I could have handled. But this—this was a man who expected to come back, or who couldn't stop being careful even when careful no longer mattered.

I knew which one fit my father. Both of them fit my father.

The cairn stood at the camp's edge, larger than the trail markers, waist-high and deliberate. Not a navigation aid. A marker. Someone had built it stone by stone, and someone had wedged a flat slab across the top like a lid, and beneath the lid—

I lifted the slab. My heart was doing something unprofessional.

Inside the cairn's hollow: a tin box, army surplus, its paint blistered but its latch sound. And resting on top of the box, weighted down with a stone, a notebook bound in oilcloth and tied with waxed string.

I untied the string the way you'd defuse something. The oilcloth fell open. The notebook inside was a standard surveyor's field book, the kind sold in every hardware store between here and the coast, its cover softened by handling, its pages swollen and rippled from damp that had come and gone.

And on the first page, in handwriting I would have known anywhere—in handwriting I had spent nineteen years of my childhood watching form grocery lists and birthday cards and flight logs, handwriting I had caught myself imitating in my own notebooks until I made myself stop—

*Elias Yarrow. Expedition surveyor. Day 1.*

I sat down on a rock. I'm not sure sitting was a decision. It may simply have been gravity finally getting a fair hearing.

Here's what I knew, officially, about Elias Yarrow: he flew out of Rivergate in June 2003 with four men and a contract to survey the upper gorge for a mining concern, and he never came back. The search lasted nine days. They found nothing—not wreckage, not a camp, not a boot. Officially, my father died in the gorge, cause unknown, remains unrecovered. Unofficially, in our kitchen, my mother developed a theory a week and discarded each one like a cigarette. In my head, I developed just the one theory and kept it: he left. He walked away from us the way I later walked away from everyone else, and the gorge was just the place his story ended because every story needs a period.

I had built a whole life on that period. Ten thousand hours of sky, measured in miles, because miles don't ask questions.

And now here was his handwriting, saying *Day 1*, which meant there had been a Day 2, and presumably a Day 3, and the notebook weighed maybe a pound and a half and felt like it weighed the whole gorge.

I opened it to the second page.

*Day 2. Camp established on south bench above the narrows. Water good. Mosquitoes biblical. Ruiz swears they fly in formation now, which I told him was impossible, and he showed me. Fair enough.*

I laughed. It came out of me like a hiccup, sudden and ugly, and then it turned into something else halfway through, and I put my hand over my mouth like a kid in church and let the gorge have its privacy with me.

Because that was him. That was exactly him—the dry note, the fairness, the willingness to be shown wrong. I could hear his voice in it. Twenty years, and I had been so busy not-thinking about him that I'd forgotten I still knew his voice, and now some hardware-store notebook was handing it back to me on page two, between a joke about mosquitoes and a note about water quality.

Grief doesn't ambush you at funerals. Funerals are prepared positions; you've dug in, you've got rations. Grief ambushes you in the middle of an investigation, on a rock, holding evidence, when your professional mind says *catalogue this* and the rest of you says *that's my dad's handwriting and he was funny and he's been dead my whole adult life and I never once let myself miss him properly because missing him meant believing he was gone.*

I sat on that rock for a long time. The hawk came back and circled once, decided I was boring again, and left. Somewhere below, the river kept talking, patient as ever, keeping what it keeps.

When I could trust my hands again, I wiped my face with my sleeve, took out my own notebook, and started copying. Every entry, word for word, date for date. If the original got wet, burned, blown off this shelf, or lost to whatever had taken the rest of the expedition's peace of mind, there would be a duplicate. That was procedure. That was sanity. That was something to do with my fingers while the rest of me caught up.

The early entries were ordinary and therefore unbearable. Elevations. Bearings. Weather notes in a shorthand I recognized from my own charts. Rations tallied. Personalities sketched in two lines apiece—Ruiz the mosquito scholar, a geologist named Brandt who "argues with rocks and loses," a cook called Ferro whose coffee was "a controlled substance." Five men building a little orderly world on the edge of a hole in the earth, and my father recording it all with the neat, wry precision of a man who believed the future would want to know.

By entry thirty, the tone shifted. I noticed it the way you notice a change in engine pitch—before you can say why.

*Day 31. Brandt says the quartz veins don't follow any pattern he's seen. They converge. He won't say toward what. I've surveyed enough mountains to know when a man is excited and when he's scared, and he's both, which is the combination I like least.*

*Day 34. Found the overhang today. Photographs to follow. Ruiz wants to go in tomorrow. Told him we do this by the book or not at all. He agreed too quickly.*

*Day 36. Went in.*

That was where the copying stopped mattering and the reading started. I turned pages faster now, past elevations and bearings, past sketches of a cave mouth rendered in my father's clean drafting hand—and there, mid-book, the entries shortened, tightened, the jokes dropping away one by one like instruments shutting down in sequence.

*Day 38. We are not the first. There are markings. Old ones.*

*Day 40. Brandt has stopped arguing with rocks.*

*Day 41.* —and then a gap, several pages with nothing, and then a single line pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through:

*Do not let Crane—*

The line ended there. No name finished, no sentence completed. Just my father's pen driving into the paper and stopping, and the next page blank, and the next, and the next, until the last written page of the book, where the final entry began in a shakier version of his hand:

*If anyone finds this, I need you to know—*

And that was it. Mid-sentence. Mid-word, almost. The rest of the notebook empty, the last page soft with handling, as though he'd picked it up many times afterward and put it down again without writing more.

The wind moved the tarp behind me. The river talked. I read the broken line three times—*If anyone finds this, I need you to know*—and understood, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like temperature, that my father had spent his last days trying to finish that sentence to someone.

To whoever found the book.

Or—to a boy who used to sit by a radio waiting for a call sign that never came.

I closed the notebook. I put it inside my jacket, against my chest, which was not procedure and I didn't care. Then I looked at the tin box in the cairn, the one the notebook had been guarding, and my hand went to its latch and stopped.

Because down the trail, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of Sam Okafor shouting my name.

* * *

He came up the switchbacks at a dead run, which told me everything before he got close enough to say anything. Sam Okafor did not run uphill for pleasure.

"Gideon—" He bent over his knees, breathing like a bellows. "You have to—you have to come back. It's Mara. Her fever spiked again, and she's—she's talking. Really talking. And she keeps asking for you."

"Me?" I said. "She barely knows me."

Sam straightened up. His face in the afternoon light was young and scared and honest, and what he said next rearranged the furniture of my whole understanding of the last three days.

"She knows you," he said. "She's known since the strip at Rivergate. She saw you load the plane and she said—she said you walk like him." He swallowed. "She said you walk exactly like Elias Yarrow."

The notebook pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Behind us the gorge dropped away into its silver silence, keeping everything it had ever been given.

"What else does she know?" I asked.

"That depends," Sam said, "on whether you're going to open the box."

I looked back at the cairn. At the tin box with its blistered paint and its sound latch, waiting twenty years for someone with the right name to come along.

"Yeah," I said. "I think I am."
