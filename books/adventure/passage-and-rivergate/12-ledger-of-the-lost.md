## 12. Ledger of the Lost

The ledger was wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was wrapped in a man's stubbornness.

That was Gideon's first thought as he sat on the fallen spruce at the edge of the old surveyor's camp, the package across his knees, rain ticking against the plastic sheet he'd rigged overhead. Twenty years of weather had gotten at everything else up here—rusted the tools to orange lace, collapsed the tent frames, turned the cairn into a lopsided suggestion of a cairn—but somebody had wanted this book to survive, and had wrapped it the way you wrap a thing you intend to come back for.

Gideon knew that wrapping. He'd invented half his own versions of it. There was a way pilots bundled documents against rain that you could spot across a room: three folds, tuck, tuck, and the final flap folded *under*, so that water running down the package would bead off instead of wicking in at the seam. His father had taught him that fold on the tailgate of a truck in another lifetime, using a road map and a sandwich, because Elias Yarrow had never once in his life explained anything without a sandwich involved.

Gideon's hands were steady. He noticed that about them the way you notice a dog not barking.

He unwrapped it anyway.

* * *

The ledger was a school exercise book, the cheap kind with a marbled cover, the kind that cost less than the coffee you drank while writing in it. The cover had gone soft and gray, and the first dozen pages had fused together with damp, but the middle of the book—the middle was dry, protected, kept. He opened it with the care he'd use on a patient with a suspected fracture.

The handwriting hit him before the words did.

He'd been prepared for the words. He'd had the whole hike down from the rim to prepare for the words. What he hadn't prepared for was the handwriting—that forward-leaning, impatient cursive with the crossbar on every *t* slashed like a man in a hurry to get to the next thing. Gideon saw it and his body did something complicated and involuntary, a full-system recognition, the way you hear your own name across a noisy room.

*Day 1. Upstream past the falls at first light. River higher than the charts say, which the charts always do, which is why I married a woman smarter than the charts.*

Gideon laughed. It came out of him like a cough, one syllable, and then he had to sit there on the log with his hand over his mouth while the rain came down and the river spoke its one long sentence below.

*Married a woman smarter than the charts.* His mother. That was his mother in there, in a ledger written before Gideon could ride a bike without training wheels. His father had been dead—had been *missing*, had been *presumed*, had been every soft verb a family uses to avoid the hard one—for twenty years, and here he was, making a joke. Making a joke about his wife. To no one. In a notebook in the rain.

"Hello, Dad," Gideon said out loud. His voice sounded wrong in the gorge, too flat, swallowed immediately. He said it again anyway.

* * *

He read the way he flew a weather front: in committed stretches, with no going back, eyes moving faster than comfort.

The early entries were logistics—fuel counts, food weights, the endless arithmetic of an expedition, which Gideon recognized as the same arithmetic he did in his own head every time he loaded the Cub. *Rice down to 11 days if Voss keeps eating like a stevedore.* There was a Voss on the 2003 expedition too. Gideon made a note of that and decided, provisionally, not to think about what it might mean, because he was already holding as much as he could carry and his hands were full.

There was humor in it. That was the thing that undid him by degrees, the way a slow leak undoes a tire. He'd built his father out of the materials a nine-year-old has: the smell of avgas, the fold of the oilcloth, the sound of a man whistling while he preflighted, and then the long silence afterward, the empty chair, the word *missing* said in the kitchen in the tone people use for weather. He had not built in the jokes. He had not built in a man who wrote, on Day 9:

*Okafor's snoring has achieved a register previously recorded only near active runways. Have moved my tent twice. Considering moving it to the far bank.*

Gideon stopped. Read it again. *Okafor.*

Sam Okafor was twenty-four years old and could not have been on a 2003 expedition. But the name sat there in his father's handwriting, and Gideon felt the gorge tilt slightly, the way the world tilts when a chart turns out to be wrong. A relative, then. A brother. A father of his own, maybe, who'd told stories at a dinner table about a surveyor with slashed t-crossbars who complained about snoring, and a boy had grown up on those stories the way Gideon had grown up on the silence where the stories should have been.

Two families, fed on opposite halves of the same absence. Gideon sat with that a while. The rain helped. Rain is good for sitting with things.

* * *

By Day 14 the entries changed. The logistics fell away and something else came up through the page, something that started careful and got less so, the way a man writes when he's trying to be a professional and failing at it because his hands are shaking.

*Voss found the notch in the north wall. I told her it was a shadow. It is not a shadow.*

*Day 15. The notch is a slot. The slot is a passage. I am not going to write down what is in the passage, because a ledger can be lost, and some things should only travel in a head that knows their weight.*

Gideon read that three times. *The passage.* Two words, and around them a careful, deliberate absence, a hole in the record shaped exactly like the most important thing in the book. His father had found something in this gorge that he refused to name even to a notebook he'd wrapped in oilcloth against the end of the world.

Gideon looked up at the north wall. The cloud had come down to about four hundred feet, and the rock went up into it and kept going, gray into gray, and somewhere in all that stone was a slot that a man had looked into twenty years ago and come out of changed.

He felt the two oldest things in him pull in opposite directions, the way they always had, the way they'd been pulling since he was nine years old and a radio sat silent on a kitchen table. One of them said: *go look.* Climb the talus, find the slot, put your eyes where his eyes were, finish the sentence the ledger wouldn't. The other one said: *he didn't name it for a reason, and you are a man who lives by reasons.*

He was a man who lived by reasons. That was the whole architecture of his life—measured miles over lived years, fuel calculated to the liter, weather windows respected, rules never broken except once, recently, catastrophically, for three people he'd known for a day. He did not go look. He noted the coordinates his father had penciled in the margin—*N wall, 3rd gully, high water mark plus 40*—and he noted that his own heart was going like a tachometer redlining, and he turned the page.

* * *

The entries after Day 15 were shorter. He could feel the expedition tightening around itself, the way a crew goes quiet when the weather builds. Fuel counts. A note about a man named Reyes turning an ankle. A note, on Day 18, that made Gideon's chest do something unscheduled:

*Wish I could radio the boy. He'll be sitting by the set at 1900 sharp, he always is, bless his stubborn heart. Tell him, whoever finds this: the sky is not the only country worth mapping.*

Gideon put the ledger down on his knee.

He was thirty-one years old and he had spent twenty-two of those years proving, mile by measured mile, that he was nothing like this man—no family, no fixed address, no attachments that could go silent on a kitchen table. He had built a whole flying philosophy out of not being Elias Yarrow. And here was Elias, twenty years gone, guessing correctly that his nine-year-old would be sitting by the radio at 1900 sharp, and calling that stubbornness a blessing, and leaving a message for him across two decades like a man leaving a light on in a window.

*Whoever finds this.* His father had known, on Day 18, that a whoever might be necessary. That was the horror arriving on the page, patient as water. Gideon had come up the gorge braced for horror—the cairn, the rusted tools, the camp like a held breath—but he'd braced for horror the way you brace for turbulence: as a discrete event, something you fly through. This was worse. This was horror with handwriting. This was a man writing jokes about snoring on Monday and writing *whoever finds this* on Thursday, and Gideon could see the exact seam where the hope ran out, because the handwriting changed. The slashed t-crossbars got shorter. The forward lean went out of the cursive, page by page, like a man sitting down more and more heavily.

*Day 21. River's up two feet since yesterday. The passage floods from below; we can see it now, water coming out of the slot like breath. Voss says wait. I say we wait one day. Reyes says the plane can't lift in this anyway. Reyes is right, which is the worst thing a man can be.*

*Day 22. Waited. Voss was right, which is somehow worse.*

*Day 23. The bar we landed on is gone. All of it. Reyes is keeping count of the rice wrong on purpose, I think, so we won't do the arithmetic. I've done the arithmetic.*

Gideon's eyes did the arithmetic too, automatically, the pilot's reflex—three men, one flooded strip, a plane that couldn't lift in the weather, rice counted in single digits. He knew how this ledger ended. He had known since the cairn. Knowing it in advance did not help. Knowing it in advance was like knowing the stall speed: it didn't keep you from stalling, it only told you the exact moment you'd feel it.

*Day 24. Built a raft from the tent fly and the fuel drum frames. It is, conservatively, an insult to the concept of rafts. Voss has a cousin's boat on the lower river and says we can run the gorge if the water drops a foot. The water has not dropped a foot. The water has opinions.*

There it was again, the joke, arriving at the worst possible moment, and Gideon understood suddenly that the jokes weren't a temperament. They were a discipline. His father had been frightened—frightened in the way Gideon had only ever seen in passengers, the white-knuckle quiet—and he had answered the fear with humor the way you answer a stall with forward pressure: not because it fixes anything, but because it's the correct input, and the correct input is all you have.

Gideon had thought he was nothing like this man. He was reading the evidence, entry by entry, that he was exactly this man, that he'd inherited the whole cockpit—the arithmetic, the wrapping of important things in oilcloth, the jokes deployed at the moment of maximum fear—and had simply never known the inheritance was there, because no one had ever handed him the deed.

*Day 25. Water's down six inches. Voss says it's now or never. I agree, which frightens her more than the water did.*

*Day 26. We are going at first light. The raft will hold two and the survey case if the survey case behaves. I am leaving the ledger in the dry camp, wrapped, because if the raft is an insult, my handwriting is a crime, and one of us should survive to be read.*

Gideon turned the page. It was the last written page. The rest of the exercise book was fused blank.

The final entry began in the new, heavy hand, the one that sat down while it wrote:

*Day 26. First light. Reyes went first with the case. Voss second. I held the raft at the eddy to see them through the chute, and the chute took the raft sideways at the throat, and Voss went in, and Reyes went in after her, and I had the rope, and the rope was—*

That was all. *And the rope was—*

The sentence stopped mid-word, mid-thought, the ink trailing into a small dark blot where the pen had rested, or pressed, or been set down. Gideon stared at the blot for a long time. A blot is a moment of time made physical—a second, maybe two, twenty years ago, when a man in a gorge stopped writing because something was happening on the water that was more urgent than ink.

*The rope was—*

Was what? Was too short. Was too long. Was holding. Was not. Was in his hands, and his hands were on it, and then he'd put down the pen and taken up the rope and gone down to the water to do the last thing he ever did, which was, apparently, hold a line for other people.

Gideon sat on the fallen spruce with the ledger open on his knees and did not move for a long time. The rain came down. The river below kept saying its one long sentence. And the hope and the horror, which he'd been carrying separately since the rim, finally merged into the single thing they had always been: his father had survived the crash. His father had lived in this gorge for twenty-six days, doing arithmetic and making jokes and leaving messages in ledgers for a boy at a radio. His father had put down the pen and picked up a rope to save two people, and the ledger did not say whether he saved them, and the ledger did not say what happened after, because the ledger was in a dry camp and the man was on the water, and the water keeps what the water keeps.

He was not dead at the crash site. He had not simply vanished. He had lived three more weeks than the world ever knew, and the last thing he'd done with those weeks was reach for a rope.

"Okay," Gideon said to the gorge, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word, and he let it. "Okay. I read you."

He rewrapped the ledger in its oilcloth, three folds, tuck, tuck, flap under—the family fold, performed without thinking, performed as if his hands had been waiting twenty years for the instructions. He put it inside his jacket, against his chest, where a document travels when it is not cargo.

Then he stood, and the world did its small tilt again, because down at the mouth of the gorge, small with distance, a figure was coming along the waterline toward the old camp. Young, moving fast, favoring one leg, carrying a rifle slung across his back and something square and heavy in both arms like a man carrying an argument.

Sam Okafor. Guarding his box, walking straight toward the one person in the world who had just read the name *Okafor* in a dead man's handwriting.

Gideon stood very still at the edge of the trees and wondered, with the calm of a man whose fuel gauges have all just started disagreeing with each other, what Sam would do when he learned whose son was standing in his father's camp.
