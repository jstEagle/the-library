## 13. Sam's Rifle

The rifle was pointed at Gideon's chest, and the boy holding it was crying.

Not sobbing—Sam Okafor was too proud for sobbing, and too dehydrated for tears to come easily—but his face was doing that thing faces do when the body has decided to weep and the mind has not yet signed off on it. His jaw worked. His eyes shone. The rifle barrel trembled in small, honest circles, which was somehow worse than a steady aim, because it meant he hadn't decided anything yet, not even the direction of his fear.

"Okay," Gideon said. He kept his hands where the boy could see them, loose at his sides, fingers spread. He'd learned that trick from a man in Fort Nelson who'd talked down a drunk with a bear spray and a grievance, and it had never failed him, mostly because Gideon had never been in a situation where it was truly tested. "Okay, Sam. It's me. Gideon. I flew in this morning, remember? The plane on the bar. The one making that sad noise."

"I remember."

"Good. Great. Memory's intact. That's the first thing I check for in a patient, right after pulse and before 'do you want soup.'"

Sam made a sound that was almost a laugh and mostly a hiccup. "You're not a doctor."

"I'm not a doctor," Gideon agreed. "I'm a pilot who watched a lot of medical dramas and once helped a veterinarian pull a calf. Between the three of us—" he nodded toward the tent where Mara Voss lay muttering at the ceiling of her fever, "—we're about half a doctor, and half a doctor beats none."

The rifle dipped an inch. Gideon did not lunge for it. He'd learned that much about people in crisis: you didn't take things from them, even things that were hurting them. You waited for them to set the things down themselves.

"You came for the box," Sam said.

The word landed in the space between them like a stone in still water. Gideon watched the ripples move across the boy's face—fear, then suspicion, then something older and tireder than either.

"No," Gideon said, and it was mostly true, which in his experience was the most useful kind of true. "I came for three people. The sponsor stopped hearing from you nine days ago and opened his wallet. I stopped hearing from my common sense and opened the throttle." He let that sit. "The box can stay in the box. I've got no interest in it. I've hauled crates marked DO NOT OPEN before. You know what was in the last one? Taxidermy supplies. A whole moose head, disassembled, in formaldehyde. Some cargo you just deliver and don't ask about."

Sam's arms were shaking now, all of them, the whole architecture of him. He was maybe twenty-three, twenty-four, with the long limbs and unfinished face of someone who'd grown six inches in a year and hadn't caught up to himself. There was a gash above his eyebrow, scabbed and angry, and his lips were cracked from dehydration, and he was standing guard over a steel strongbox in a ruined camp like the last soldier of an army that no longer existed.

"Crane said—" Sam started, and stopped. Started again. "Crane said if anyone came, it would be his people."

"I'm nobody's people. I'm the mail plane." Gideon took one slow step forward, then another, the way you'd approach a dog on a highway. "Sam. Look at me. Not the tree line. Not the box. Me. I flew four hours through a storm front to get here because a voice on a radio told me three human beings were upriver and quiet. That's the whole story. That's the entire conspiracy. A pilot, a storm, and three people who need to not be here when the river comes up."

Something moved behind Sam's eyes at the words *the river comes up*. Gideon filed that away.

"The river," Sam repeated.

"It's been raining in the headwaters for two days. I flew through the front edge of it. That bar my plane is sitting on has maybe three days left as land." He said it plainly, without drama, because drama was a luxury and arithmetic was free. "So here's my offer. You put the rifle down, you help me get Dr. Voss stabilized, and nobody has to open anything. The box can stay shut for the rest of its natural life. I'll even sign a certificate. I know a notary in Fort Simpson."

The rifle came down in stages, like a drawbridge lowering—shoulders first, then the barrel, then the whole sad weight of it into the mud. Sam sat down next to it, cross-legged, and put his face in his hands, and for a while the only sound in the camp was the river and the wind in the torn fly of the mess tent and, faintly, Mara Voss in her fever, arguing with someone invisible about stratigraphy.

Gideon crouched down an arm's length away and waited. He was good at waiting. He'd had ten thousand hours of practice, and most of it had been waiting for weather, for radios, for a father who never called in.

"He drowned," Sam said at last, into his hands.

Gideon didn't move. "Who did?"

"Dr. Renner. Our expedition lead." He lifted his face. It was wrecked, but underneath the wreck Gideon could see the shape of a serious young man, the kind who alphabetized his gear and double-knotted his boots. "Six days ago. He took the box and the survey raft and went downriver at night. Alone. He didn't tell anyone."

* * *

They got Mara settled first—Gideon insisted, and Sam, emptied of his secret, obeyed with the gratitude of a man handed a task he could actually do. They got fluids into her, antibiotics from Gideon's emergency kit into her, and a wet cloth on her forehead, and she surfaced once, briefly, and looked at Gideon with eyes that were fever-bright and horribly clear, and said, "You have his chin," and then went back under.

Gideon decided, in the manner of a man stepping over something on the trail, to deal with that later.

Then they sat in the wrecked mess tent, on crates that had once held trowels and sample bags, and Sam talked. He talked the way people talk when they've been carrying a story too long and the carrying has become the whole of them—haltingly at first, then in a flood, with Gideon asking a question only every so often, the way you'd feed a fire one log at a time.

"We were nine," Sam said. "Nine people. Dr. Renner, Dr. Voss, two grad students, the Kowalczyk twins—surveyors, brothers, they finished each other's sentences—it was actually kind of beautiful—and four of us hired hands. Techs, cooks, muscle. I was tech. Radios, drones, GPS." He laughed, a small broken sound. "I was the guy who maintained the satellite phone. Did I mention that? The satellite phone that stopped working?"

"I noticed the absence of a phone."

"It didn't stop working. I stopped it working." He said it flat, watching Gideon for the flinch. "Renner's orders. Day four. He said the survey data was sensitive, that Crane's competitors had ears everywhere, and until we knew what we had, nobody transmitted. I thought it was paranoid. I did it anyway." He rubbed his face. "That's the thing nobody tells you about following orders. They feel exactly the same as decisions. Until later."

Gideon nodded slowly. He knew a thing or two about doing the paranoid thing because a confident man told you to.

"What were you looking for?" he asked. "Out here."

"A pre-war surveyor's camp. Nineteen-thirties, maybe older. There were rumors of a government survey team that went into the gorge and the records just—ended. Crane funded the whole expedition. Renner had worked with him before. Crane's money, Crane's map, Crane's list of coordinates." Sam's mouth twisted. "We found the camp in the first week. It was exactly where Crane said it would be. That should have been the first clue, right? You don't find a camp that's been lost for ninety years on day six unless somebody already knew where it was."

"What was in it?"

"Tools. A cairn. And a ledger." Sam said the word carefully, like it was fragile. "Field ledger. Survey notes. And a name signed all over it that made Dr. Voss go white as the tent canvas." He looked up. "You know whose name, don't you. You've known since you landed. You walk like a man who's reading a map he memorized as a kid."

Gideon said nothing. The wind moved through the torn fly, a soft ripping whisper.

"Yarrow," Sam said anyway. "Elias Yarrow. The pilot who crashed up here in 2003 and was never found. Crane's file said the expedition was about the surveyor's camp. But the ledger was the real cargo. Crane wanted it. Or wanted it gone. Renner wouldn't say which, and that was the second clue."

* * *

Sam talked for a long time after that, and Gideon let him, and the afternoon went gold and then grey around the broken camp while the story came out in pieces, the way wrecks come out of rivers—one piece at a time, each one telling you something about the whole.

The fever had come in the second week. It started with the Kowalczyk twins, who'd waded a side channel collecting water samples and come back shivering, and it moved through camp like the river moved through gravel—finding every low place. The grad students went down first, then one of the cooks. Gideon's doubled shipment of antibiotics, the one that had made him uneasy back at Rivergate, had been Renner's doing—Crane's money, Renner's insistence, a supply line that had looked, to Gideon at the time, like simple overcaution.

"It wasn't overcaution," Sam said. "Renner knew what the water was like. He'd read the old survey reports. He knew what had happened to the last people who drank from the side channels." He stared at his hands. "He knew, and he didn't say anything, because saying something would have meant explaining why we were really here, and he wasn't ready to do that. So people got sick, and he dosed them, and he kept digging."

"One of them died," Gideon said quietly. He'd seen the grave that morning, the cairn of stones with a trowel driven into the top like a flag. "The cook. I saw."

"Priya." Sam's voice cracked on the name and he let it. "Priya Anand. She made the best dal I've ever eaten, out here, on a two-burner, with powdered milk. She got sick on a Tuesday and was gone by Sunday. Renner dosed her with everything we had left and it wasn't enough." He was quiet a moment. "That's when he changed. Or maybe that's when I noticed he'd changed. He stopped sleeping. He started talking to Crane on the shortwave in code—Crane had given him a codebook, which, again, normal expeditions don't have codebooks—and then two of the others tried to walk out downriver for help, and that's when it all came apart."

"The two who are missing."

"The two who are missing." Sam nodded. "Dev and Lucas. They took the good raft and a week of food and said they'd be at Rivergate in four days. That was eleven days ago." He didn't say *the river took them*. He didn't have to. Gideon had flown this river long enough to know that the river took its tax, and that the tax was due whether you could pay or not.

"And then Renner took the box."

"And then Renner took the box." Sam laughed, and it was a terrible sound, the laugh of a man standing at the edge of something. "The night before he left, he came to my tent. He had the box under one arm and a pistol—Crane's pistol, another thing normal expeditions don't have—in his belt, and he said, 'Sam, if anything happens to me, the box goes back in the ground. Not to Crane. In the ground.' And I said, 'What's in it?' And he looked at me for a long time, and you know what he said?"

"What."

"He said, 'The truth about a man who deserved better than the story he got.'" Sam shook his head. "And then he was gone. Took the survey raft at two in the morning. Mara heard him go—she was already feverish, she thought she'd dreamed it. The gorge takes the river down through white water for about eight kilometers below us. Nobody runs that at night on a raft built for calm water. Nobody runs it at all, really. The Kowalczyks called it the Meat Grinder, as a joke, before the jokes stopped."

"He drowned in it."

"We found the raft three days later. Pinned against a strainer, downstream of the worst drop. And we found—" Sam's throat worked. "We found him. What the river gave back. About two kilometers down. Mara was lucid enough that morning to say the burial words. We buried him where we could dig, which wasn't deep, and marked it, and then Mara went under with the fever for real, and it was just me. Me, a sick woman, a strongbox, a rifle, and a radio I'd broken with my own hands." He looked up, and his eyes were dry now, wrung out. "So I sat here. For six days. Guarding a box I was afraid to open and more afraid to lose, waiting for either rescue or Crane's people, and not knowing which one would be worse."

Gideon sat with that. Around them the camp breathed its ruined breath—the shredded tents, the scattered instruments, the grave with its trowel flag. Somewhere a raven made a comment and received no reply.

"Crane's people," Gideon said. "You expected them."

"Renner expected them. He said Crane would never let the box leave the country in anyone else's hands. He said—" Sam hesitated. "He said Crane had already buried one man over this. That he wasn't going to be the second."

The words went into Gideon like cold water into a boot. He kept his face still. He was very good at keeping his face still; he'd had a lifetime of practice at being unreadable, mostly for his own benefit.

"Renner say who?" he asked, mild as milk.

"No. He never did. He'd start and then stop, like the name was a door he didn't want to open." Sam looked at the strongbox where it sat under the tarp, dented, rain-spotted, patient as a headstone. "It's documents. That much I know. Paper. Renner said paper was the whole point—that Crane didn't want gold or artifacts, he wanted a specific set of papers either recovered or destroyed, and he'd pay for either outcome, which tells you everything about a man, doesn't it? When someone will pay you the same to find a thing or burn it, the thing isn't the point. The point is that it stops existing."

Gideon looked at the box. He thought about the crate he'd loaded at Rivergate, FRAGILE: DO NOT OPEN, and about Aldous Crane's soft voice and softer smile, and about the sealed file the man had mentioned with the Yarrow name in it, the way you'd mention a loaded gun resting on the table between you.

"And you've had it six days," Gideon said, "and you haven't opened it."

"Renner asked me not to."

"He's dead, Sam."

"I know he's dead." The boy's chin came up, and for a second there was steel in him, real steel, the kind you can't buy or train. "He asked me not to, and I'm the last person he ever asked for anything. You don't get to be dead and have your last request expire. That's not how it works. That's not how *any* of it works." He looked away, out at the river, and his voice dropped. "Mara says the same thing you're thinking. She's said it twice, when the fever lets her. She says the truth doesn't belong to a dead man, it belongs to the living, and the living have a right to it. And maybe she's right. Maybe you're both right. But I made a promise to a man who died keeping a worse promise, and I've got exactly one promise left in me, and I'd like to keep it, because if I break this one too, then I'm just a guy who breaks promises, and I don't want to be that. I've been enough things I don't want to be this month."

Gideon was quiet for a long moment.

"Okay," he said finally.

Sam blinked. "Okay?"

"Okay. It stays shut. As far as I'm concerned, that box contains moose parts in formaldehyde." He stood, joints cracking, and looked downriver, where the water had come up another hand's width since morning and was chewing at the far edge of the gravel bar with the unhurried appetite of something that had all the time in the world. "But Sam. I need you to hear the arithmetic now, because I'm only going to say it once and I need you to actually hear it."

Sam stood too. "The river."

"The river. It's rained in the headwaters for two days straight, and it's going to rain for at least one more. This bar is going under. My plane is sitting on the last dry forty meters of it, and I can lift three people out of here with a full load and a tailwind, maybe, if the wind cooperates, which wind never does." He turned to face the boy. "Three people. You, me, Dr. Voss. The box stays, the rifle stays, the camp stays. Everything that isn't breathing stays. Can you live with that?"

Sam looked at the strongbox for a long time. Then at the grave with its trowel flag. Then at the tent where Mara muttered to the ceiling about sediment layers.

"Can *you*?" he asked.

It was a better question than the boy knew. Gideon thought of a ledger in a cairn up at the gorge rim, with a name in it that was half his own, and of an entry that trailed off mid-sentence, and of a father who had also, apparently, been very good at keeping promises to dead men.

"I've lived with worse cargo," Gideon said.

He was turning toward Mara's tent to start the packing when Sam caught his sleeve, and the boy's face had gone the color of the gravel.

"Gideon," he said. "The bar. Look at the bar."

Gideon looked. The river had come up again—not a hand's width this time but a visible surge, brown and fast, folding over the downstream tip of the gravel bar and taking it, right now, while they watched, an armload at a time.

The clock, which had been ticking quietly in Gideon's head since the storm front, began to ring.
