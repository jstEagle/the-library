## 14. Rising Water

The river told me before anyone else did.

I'd learned to read water the way some men read faces—half superstition, half arithmetic. And the Rivergate, when I woke on the third morning at Camp Hollow, was reading like a man about to lie to you. It had gone from that clean mountain green to the color of strong coffee, and it had stopped making conversation. A healthy river talks. This one had gone quiet, the way a river goes quiet when it's busy somewhere upstream, picking up things and carrying them toward you.

I stood at the edge of the gravel bar with my coffee cooling in my hand and did the thing I do instead of praying, which is counting.

The bar sat maybe four feet above the current. Four feet doesn't sound like much until you know that a river in flood doesn't rise four feet the way a bathtub rises four feet. It rises four feet the way a rumor rises through a small town—slowly at first, then all at once, and by the time it's everywhere it's too late to have an opinion about it.

The water was already licking at the willow scrub on the downstream point. Yesterday it hadn't been.

"Sam," I said, when I got back to camp. He was sitting on an overturned supply crate with the rifle across his knees, doing his guard impression. He'd been doing it for three days, and the rifle had never once pointed at anything except his own boots, which told me it was less a weapon than a worry he could hold.

He looked up. He'd gotten some color back since the fever passed through him, though he still had the hollowed-out look of a man who'd been awake too long in a place that didn't believe in mornings.

"The river's coming up," I said.

"I know. I heard it last night." He said it the way you'd report a stranger walking around outside your tent. "It sounded—bigger. Like it had more voices."

"That'll be the rain in the headwaters. There's a front parked over the divide." I'd heard it on the radio before the expedition's set got smashed, a low gray murmur of weather talk, and I'd filed it under Later. Later had arrived, wearing its boots. "Everything that falls up there ends up down here. Question is when."

"How long do we have?"

I crouched and picked up a stick and drew in the dirt, because some conversations go better with pictures. The bar, the channel, the cut bank behind camp where the willows grew, the plane sitting on its tundra tires at the upstream end like a patient dog that had been waiting a long time to be told what for.

"Depends how much rain. If it's a day of it, we've got three days, maybe four. If it's a week of it—" I dragged the stick through my drawing of the bar and left a brown scar where the gravel had been. "Then we don't have anything, because there won't be a bar."

Sam looked at the scar for a while. "And the plane?"

"The plane floats about as well as a piano."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be. Reassuring is for people with options." I stood and brushed off my knees. "Go wake Mara. Gently. If she's having a good morning, don't spend it all at once."

* * *

Mara's good mornings, I'd learned, came in two varieties. There was the kind where her eyes focused and she asked for water and complained about the taste of my rehydration mix, which was a fine kind, a normal kind. And there was the kind where her eyes focused and she looked at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere, an expression that said she knew something about me that I hadn't told her, which was impossible, because I hadn't told anyone anything in eleven years.

That morning it was the second kind.

"Gideon Yarrow," she said, before her eyes were even all the way open.

"Morning to you too."

"Sam told me. Your name. He said the pilot's name was Yarrow and I thought the fever was back, and then I saw you, and—" She stopped. Her hands had found the edge of the sleeping bag and were gripping it like the bag might make a break for the river. "You have his chin."

I'd been told that before, by people at funerals and people at bars, and it had never once been a comfort. "Lots of chins in the world."

"Not that one." She sat up, swaying, and I put a hand out on reflex and she ignored it with the particular pride of a woman who had recently been delirious and was determined to pretend it hadn't happened. "Elias Yarrow. Your father."

The name landed in my chest the way hail lands on a wing—small hard sounds, no damage you can see from the outside. "He's dead," I said. "Twenty years dead. It's in all the papers that matter, which is to say, none of them."

"That's what everyone thinks." She was watching me with those fever-bright eyes, and behind the fever there was something else, something that looked almost like pity, and I found I liked the pity even less than the fever. "Gideon, the river's rising. I can hear it. You need to get us out of here."

"That's the plan. That's been the plan. I'm doing the arithmetic on it right now, as a matter of fact."

"Do the arithmetic fast." She lay back down, suddenly spent, the sentence costing her more than it sounded like. But her hand caught my sleeve on the way, a light grip, a librarian's grip. "And Gideon. Whatever Sam told you about the box—whatever he told you, it's not the whole of it."

"Sam hasn't told me anything about the box."

"I know," she said. "That's how I know it's not the whole of it."

She let go of my sleeve and turned her face to the tent wall, and I went outside, where at least the problems were honest ones, made of water and weight.

* * *

I spent the middle of the day doing what I do best, which is standing in one place moving nothing, moving only numbers.

Here's the shape of it. The plane was a de Havilland I'd owned long enough that we'd stopped being two things and become one argument. Useful load, with the fuel she'd need to get off a gravel bar in warm afternoon air and climb out of this valley—call it nine hundred pounds of everything that wasn't the airplane itself. Me, two hundred pounds in boots. Mara, maybe a hundred and twenty after a week of fever, which the fever had been generous about. Sam, a hundred and seventy of long bones and apology. That left roughly four hundred pounds for fuel, gear, water, and whatever else the world insisted on.

Four hundred pounds of fuel is about sixty gallons, which is about two and a half hours of flying, which was enough to reach Rivergate with a margin I'd describe as thin and a lawyer would describe as negligent. It was doable. It was doable exactly once, with two people, and then the airplane would be here no more, because the bar would be a rumor and the plane would be downstream, doing its piano impression.

Which meant: two trips. And the second trip needed fuel waiting here, or at least reachable, and the only fuel within a hundred miles was the expedition's cached drums up at the old surveyor's camp, up the gorge road, four hours' walk through country that had already eaten one expedition leader whole.

I did the numbers again, because numbers are obedient in a way rivers are not, and they came out the same. They always come out the same. That's the thing nobody tells you about arithmetic—it's not a tool, it's a mirror. You look into it hoping for a different answer and it just shows you your own face, doing the math.

"Three days," I said out loud, to the river, because talking to rivers is a pilot habit and I'd stopped being embarrassed about it somewhere over the ten-thousand-hour mark. "Give me three days."

The river said nothing, which I chose to take as agreement.

* * *

Sam found me at the plane in the late afternoon. I'd pulled the cowl and was doing an inspection I'd already done twice, because there is a kind of worry that can only be treated with a rag and a flashlight and a lot of looking at things that were fine the last time you looked at them.

"Spark plugs look okay?" he asked.

"They look okay."

"But?"

"But they'll look worse the second we need them." I wiped my hands and sat back on the wheel fairing. "You want to ask me something. I can hear it from here. It's got a sound, a question like that. Like a screen door that hasn't decided."

He almost smiled. First one in three days; I counted it. "Mara says the river gives us three days. Is that true, or is that fever talking?"

"Fever talks a lot of sense, actually. It's the rest of the day I don't trust." I nodded at the water. "But yeah. Three days, maybe four. So here's how it has to go. Tomorrow, weather permitting, I take Mara out. She's the lightest and she's in the worst shape, and every day she spends at this altitude is a day her blood argues with her. Day after, weather and river permitting, I come back for you. That's the plan. It has exactly one plan's worth of margin, which means it isn't really a plan, it's a hope with a checklist."

"And you?"

"I'm on both trips. Somebody's got to fly the thing."

Sam looked at his boots for a while. Then he said, in a voice that had been sanded down to the grain, "When you come back, if the bar's gone—"

"Then I land somewhere else and we solve a different problem. I've been solving different problems for twenty years, Sam. I'm good at it. It's the same problem twice in a row I can't handle."

He laughed, short and surprised at himself, and then the laugh fell off his face like it had remembered where it was. "There's something I have to tell you. About the box. About—before you came. Before we lost the radio."

I put the rag down. Some part of me had been waiting for this conversation since I'd first seen him standing over that strongbox with the rifle, wearing the face of a man guarding a door he wished would stop being a door.

"Leader's name was Denholm," he said. "You never met him. He was—everyone liked him. That's the thing. That's the thing I keep coming back to. Everyone liked him." He rubbed his face. "Crane called in on the sat phone, near the end. Told Denholm the box had to come out, all of it, or none of us got paid, and Denholm—he took the box and went for the river. Said he'd raft it down to the confluence, meet the supply boat. Alone. Said it was safer that way."

"And it wasn't."

"The river took him at the first rapids. We heard it. We—" His voice did something and he stopped, and I let him stop, because I know the exact weight of a sentence you can't finish, having carried a few myself. When he started again it was quieter. "Mara wanted to send the box back with you and tell Crane to go to hell. I said we should at least get out alive first. And now the river's rising and I keep thinking Denholm thought that too. That there'd be time. There's always time, right up until the water's over your boots."

I looked at the strongbox sitting in the supply tent, dull green, patient, heavier than any of its pounds.

"Sam," I said. "Tomorrow I take Mara out. And the box stays here."

He stared at me. "You're not taking it?"

"I'm taking two hundred and eighty pounds of fevered archaeologist and sixty gallons of fuel and a prayer I haven't paid for yet. I'm not taking a box that makes men drown." I stood up and closed the cowl, and the sound of it—metal settling into metal, solid, decided—was the best sentence I'd said all day. "But I'll tell you what I am going to do tonight, while you two sleep. I'm going to open it."

Sam's mouth opened and nothing came out for a second. "Mara said—"

"Mara said a lot of things, most of them through a fever. And I've spent eleven years not opening doors, and I can tell you the exact interest rate on that." I picked up my flashlight. "Whatever's in that box has been running this camp from inside a lock for a month. It got a man drowned. It's got you standing guard with a rifle you don't know how to use, and it's got Mara talking in her sleep about a man with my chin. I don't fly cargo I haven't weighed, Sam. And I'm not leaving it here to float."

The river, downstream, said something in its new low voice. It sounded, I thought, like it had opinions about my plan, and like its opinion was the same as it had been about Denholm's.

I got out the flashlight and went to go find something to pry with.
