## 15. The Box

The camp slept badly, the way camps do when they've learned that sleep is a thing the river can take from you.

Gideon sat by the cold fire pit with the strongbox between his knees and his headlamp turned off, because there was still a sliver of light left in the sky—the long blue dusk of the interior, which never quite committed to darkness in June—and because some part of him, the part that had spent ten thousand hours alone above the tree line, believed that if he couldn't see what he was about to do, he might yet talk himself out of it.

That was the trouble with talking yourself out of things. It only worked until your hands got involved.

He'd told Sam he was going to check the tie-downs on the raft frame. He'd told Mara nothing, because she was finally sleeping like an actual human being instead of a woman being slowly unmade by fever, and he wasn't going to be the reason she stopped. And he'd told himself, for most of the evening, that the box could wait until morning, that there was no version of this where opening it at midnight made anything better.

But here's what Gideon Yarrow knew about himself, and had known since he was nineteen years old and walked out of his mother's kitchen without looking back: he did not have a curious bone in his body. Curiosity was for people with margin. What he had instead was a kind of structural dread, load-bearing, built into the frame. He spent his life making sure he never learned anything he couldn't survive knowing. He flew measured miles. He kept his tanks half-full and his past sealed like a crate marked FRAGILE: DO NOT OPEN.

And now here he was, on a gravel bar that would be underwater in three days, holding a crate that somebody had died trying to carry downriver, and the seal on his own self-discipline had cracked somewhere back at the surveyor's camp, in the ledger, at the sight of his father's handwriting slanting across a page after twenty years of silence.

*Elias.* The name sat in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed whole and never digested.

The padlock was cheap—expedition-grade, not vault-grade—and Sam's key ring had four keys on it, one of which fit. Gideon had borrowed the ring an hour ago while Sam dozed against a supply crate, and he'd stood there holding it for a full minute, feeling like a burglar in a house where everyone knew him. In the end Sam had opened one eye and said, "It's the small brass one," and rolled over, which was either trust or surrender, and Gideon hadn't asked which.

So: the small brass key. His hands were steady—he was a pilot, his hands were always steady, that was the whole religion of it—but steady isn't the same as willing. He worked the lock open and lifted the lid, and the hinges gave a small complaint, and the last of the daylight went out of the sky as if the world itself wanted to watch this part in the dark.

Inside, on top, was a photograph.

Gideon picked it up and angled it toward the fire he'd finally allowed himself to build, low and contained, more ember than flame. The photograph was old—not 2003 old, older. Black and white, corners soft with handling. A line of men standing in front of a canvas wall tent, squinting into whatever sun the photographer had promised them. Survey equipment. A dog, possibly, at the edge of the frame, mostly tail.

And third from the left, younger than Gideon was now, hat pushed back, grinning like a man who has just been told a secret he intends to keep forever—

His father.

Gideon didn't move for a long time. Somewhere down the bar, the river worked at its endless arithmetic, subtracting gravel, adding days to their sentence. A loon called out on the water and got no answer, which felt about right.

There was writing on the back. Faded pencil, careful hand:

*Halloway Survey Party, 1971. E.Y., R. Crane, T. Marsh, D. Okonkwo, foreman J. Pell.*

*R. Crane.*

Aldous Crane had been up this river fifty years ago, young and sunburned, standing next to Elias Yarrow close enough to touch shoulders. Close enough, apparently, to spend two decades afterward building a company on whatever those shoulders had carried out—or failed to carry out—of the gorge.

"Well," Gideon said aloud, to nobody, in the tone he used for weather reports. "That's a thing."

Under the photograph, wrapped in oilcloth, was a map.

He unfolded it carefully, the way you handle anything that has already survived more than you have. It was a survey map of the Rivergate gorge itself—the stretch his own charts rendered as blank space and two initials—and it was annotated in the same handwriting as the ledger, dense and confident, elevation lines and bearing marks and little penciled questions in the margins. *Verify fall line.* *Cairn unreliable—rebuild.* And along the lower gorge, boxed twice and underlined once, a feature labeled simply: **THE PASSAGE**.

No description. No coordinates beyond a rough grid reference. Just the name, repeated in the ledger, trailing off mid-sentence at the end of a man's last entry, like a door left open behind him.

Gideon traced the grid square with one finger. He'd flown over that country a hundred times—at altitude, at arm's length, at the polite remove of a man who looks down at geography the way you look down at an ex's street from a moving car. From up there it was just rock and water doing what rock and water do. From down here, with his father's pencil marks under his thumb, it was a room. A room someone had been in. A room someone had maybe died in, twenty feet from an exit, or a hundred miles from one; the map declined to say.

He folded it along its existing creases—his father's creases, worn soft as cloth—and set it aside, and told himself the next thing in the box would be something ordinary. Rope. Rations. A second, boring photograph.

It was a folder. Manila, water-stained at one corner, stamped along the top in red block letters that had faded to the color of old blood: **REGIONAL CORONER'S SERVICE — COPY — DO NOT DUPLICATE**.

Somebody had duplicated it.

Gideon held the folder for a moment without opening it, and in that moment he conducted a brief internal negotiation, the kind he was famous for with himself and no one else. The reasonable position: put it back, reseal the lock, return the keys, fly these people out, burn the box per plan, remain a ghost. The reasonable position had a lot going for it. The reasonable position had kept him alive and unexamined for nineteen years.

But the wound had already been reopened, back at the cairn, back at the ledger. You don't get to re-close a thing mid-bleed. Any bush doctor will tell you that.

He opened it.

The first page was a crash report. Aircraft: a de Havilland Beaver, registration he recognized the way you recognize a scar—*N-number ending in Yarrow's own middle initial*, because his father had named the plane after him, God help them both, a joke that had seemed funny at the time. Date: October 2003. Location: upper Rivergate gorge. Summary of findings: aircraft destroyed by impact and fire following loss of control in mountain wave turbulence. Pilot: Elias J. Yarrow, age 47. Remains recovered: partial. Identification: dental records, inconclusive due to fire damage. Presumption of death entered: [date].

Gideon read that word—inconclusive—three times.

Inconclusive is a word pilots use all the time. Inconclusive engine test. Inconclusive weather briefing. It means *we don't know*, and pilots are professionally comfortable with we-don't-know, because the alternative to we-don't-know is usually we-know-now, and by then it's someone else's report.

But coroners aren't supposed to traffic in inconclusive. Coroners are supposed to nail things down. That's the entire job. And so Gideon turned the page, slowly, the way you turn toward a sound in the dark, and found the second document, and understood why Sam Okafor had guarded this box with a rifle and why Mara Voss had looked at him across a fever and known exactly who he was before he'd said his name.

It was a supplemental memorandum. Undated, or dated recently enough that the ink was still sharp. Typed, unsigned, but with a letterhead he now recognized from the photograph's reverse side: **Crane Resource Holdings Ltd.**

The memo was short. It summarized, in the flat language of lawyers protecting themselves, the following: that a private investigation commissioned by the company had located a witness—a trapper, living up the Salmon tributary, since deceased—who reported encountering a man matching Elias Yarrow's description in the spring of 2004, approximately seven months after the presumed date of death. The man had been injured, walking, carrying survey equipment, and had asked after the fastest route downriver to Rivergate. The witness had directed him along the gorge trail. The man had continued upstream instead.

Upstream. Away from home. Toward the passage.

The memo concluded, in language so careful it practically curtsied: *In light of the above, the company elected not to pursue reopening of the estate matter, given the absence of remains and the potential complications attendant thereto.*

*Potential complications attendant thereto.*

Gideon read the phrase the way you'd read a threat written in a nice font. What it meant, stripped of its legal upholstery, was this: Aldous Crane had learned—had paid to learn—that Elias Yarrow probably walked out of that crash site alive. And Crane had done nothing. Had buried the finding deeper than the plane. Because a dead partner's claim lapses clean, and a living partner's claim comes back with interest, and there might be land involved, and there was certainly *the passage* involved, whatever the passage was, and Crane had spent twenty years and one archaeological expedition making sure the question stayed closed.

They hadn't sent Halloway's team up here to find proof of death.

They'd sent them to make sure of it. To confirm that the man in the trapper's story had never come downriver. To certify, with modern instruments and a signed field report, that Elias Yarrow was still in the gorge—conveniently, permanently, profitably in the gorge.

And the team had found the camp. Found the ledger. Found, God knew what else, in the cave mouth below the cairn. And then the fever came through, and the radio died, and the leader had tried to run the box downriver alone and drowned for it, and now the truth sat in a steel box on a drowning gravel bar, in the lap of the dead man's son, who had spent nineteen years perfecting the art of not knowing things.

Gideon became aware, distantly, that his face was wet.

He let it be wet. There was no one to see, and even if there were—Sam, Mara—they'd understand. This was the kind of rain that falls indoors.

Because here was the shape of it, and the shape was unbearable in its simplicity: his father hadn't abandoned them. His father had crashed, and burned, and crawled, and walked—on a broken body, through a winter, seven months, upstream, downstream, *somewhere*—trying to come home. And the world had filed him under *inconclusive* and moved on, and a man who could have said otherwise had chosen a signature over a search party, and Gideon had spent his entire adult life being angry at a ghost for a crime that had been committed against the ghost.

Every measured mile. Every half-full tank. Every year of silence with his mother, every charter taken under a false-sounding name, every rule invented specifically so it could be followed—all of it built on the assumption that Elias Yarrow had chosen the river over his family.

He hadn't chosen anything. He'd been erased. And the eraser was a wealthy man with a letterhead, currently waiting at the bottom of this river with a checkbook and a smile.

Gideon sat with that until the fire burned down to a red eye and the loon called again and got, this time, a faint answering call from far up the dark water, which he chose to take personally, as a good omen.

Then he did what he always did with unbearable information: he organized it.

Photograph: evidence of association, Crane and Yarrow, 1971. Map: evidence of the passage's location, and of his father's competence—this was good work, careful work, the work of a man planning to come back. Report: presumption of death, technically sound, honestly sloppy at the edges. Memo: the smoking gun, unsigned, deniable, damning anyway. And beneath all of it, when he lifted the last layer of oilcloth, one final item he hadn't expected.

A journal. Small, waterproof cover, military surplus, the kind sold in every hardware store in the north. Not the ledger from the cairn—this was different, newer, the pages less foxed. On the flyleaf, in that same slanting hand:

*If found, return to E. Yarrow, Rivergate. Or keep it. Either way, tell my son I was coming home.*

Gideon closed his eyes.

He did not open the journal. Not tonight. Tonight had taken everything he had, and a man can only bleed so much in one sitting, even alone, even in the dark, even with the river for a witness. He wrapped the journal back in its oilcloth with hands that were almost steady, laid everything back in the box in reverse order, clicked the cheap lock shut, and sat for a while longer with his forearms on his knees, watching the fire die, doing the arithmetic that pilots do when the numbers stop being about fuel.

Three survivors. One box. One drowned expedition leader whose family presumably believed he'd died of fever. One Aldous Crane, at the bottom of the river, expecting a delivery.

One father, upstream, in a cave, waiting twenty years for someone to come get him.

"Okay, Dad," Gideon said quietly, to the fire, to the river, to the dark shoulder of the gorge where the blank space on the chart began. "I'm listening."

Behind him, a floorboard of the supply tent creaked—the particular creak of a boot shifting weight, deliberately, so as not to startle. Gideon didn't turn around. He already knew it would be Sam, and he already knew, from the quality of the silence that followed, that Sam had heard at least some of it, and that tomorrow the boy was going to want to talk about what was in the box, and that the answer to that question was going to change what they loaded onto the raft, and the plane, and the rest of Gideon's life.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight, upstream, the rain had started again—soft at first, then steadier, the patient rain of a river gathering its argument—and Gideon Yarrow sat alone with a locked box and an unlocked heart, and listened to the water rise.
