## 16. Left Behind

The radio in the Yarrow kitchen had a voice like a tired schoolteacher. It crackled first, then cleared its throat, then delivered the world in fragments: weather advisories, floatplane traffic, a fisherman out of Talbot Lake reporting he'd taken on water and was fine now, thanks. Gideon knew all of its moods. He was fourteen years old and he had spent more hours with that radio than with any person except his mother, and he trusted it more than most.

It was the third of August, 2003, and his father was late.

Not badly late. Not yet. Elias Yarrow ran expeditions the way other men ran trains—on a schedule so precise it seemed like showing off. *Check-in at 1800 hours, son. If I'm five minutes late, start worrying. If I'm ten, call Aldous Crane and tell him my compass finally failed me.* That was the joke. His father's compass never failed him. His father's compass was famous in three provinces and one territory, a brass thing older than Elias himself, engraved on the back with initials that Gideon liked to trace with his thumbnail when nobody was looking: **E.Y.**

Gideon sat at the kitchen table with his homework untouched in front of him—a math worksheet about compound interest, which struck him even then as a subject invented by people who had never needed money urgently—and watched the clock over the stove do its slow, indifferent work.

"You'll wear a hole in that chair," his mother said from the doorway. She said it lightly, but Gideon had learned to hear the second sentence underneath the first, the one she didn't say: *he'll call.*

"He's only twenty minutes late," Gideon said.

"Mm." She came into the kitchen and put her hand briefly on his head, an operation performed with the efficiency of long practice, then went back to whatever she'd been doing in the other room that involved a lot of cabinet doors opening and closing. When his mother was worried, the house got noisy. Drawers. Cupboards. The washing machine running a load that didn't need running. Gideon had once timed her during a hailstorm warning: eleven minutes of continuous domestic percussion. Tonight the symphony had already started.

He turned back to the radio and gave the dial a small, respectful nudge, the way you'd clear your throat to remind someone you were there.

* * *

Here is what Gideon knew about the expedition, assembled from dinner-table briefings and the map spread across the living room floor:

His father had gone up the Rivergate with two men whose names Gideon had been made to memorize—*in case, Gideon. Always know the names of everyone in the field, because the bush doesn't return people, it returns names*—a geologist named Prakash and a surveyor called Tomlin, who reportedly snored like a landslide. They were going into the gorge country where the maps went shy. Gideon had seen those maps himself, had run his finger up the river's blue line until it thinned into a dotted suggestion and then nothing at all, just paper-colored blankness with a few penciled notes in his father's hand. At the edge of one blank space, small as a signature, sat the letters E.Y., marking the farthest point of the last trip, four years ago.

"What's out there?" Gideon had asked.

"Ask me again in August," his father had said, and grinned, and Gideon had spent the whole spring believing that August would answer.

That was the thing about Elias Yarrow that people either loved or couldn't stand: he treated the unknown like a reservation he'd made months in advance. Uncertainty wasn't a wall to him; it was a door he hadn't gotten around to opening. He talked about blank spaces on maps the way other fathers talked about retirement. And Gideon, fourteen and made entirely of admiration, had absorbed this wholesale, without ever noticing the cost line item at the bottom of the invoice—the mother counting drawers, the boy counting minutes.

At 1830 the radio offered him a weather advisory for the interior: building cloud, thunderstorms possible by evening, winds shifting northeast. Gideon wrote it down in the log book they kept beside the set, because his father had trained him to write everything down. *A pilot's memory is a rumor,* Elias liked to say. *Paper is testimony.* The log book was Gideon's favorite object in the house after the compass. It had entries going back years, in his father's blocky capitals, and lately in Gideon's own smaller hand, and there was something deeply comforting about the whole idea of it—that a life could be recorded, checked against itself, proven.

At 1900 his mother put a plate of stew in front of him without being asked and stood watching the window while he ate.

"Maybe the weather," Gideon said. "There's a front coming in from the northeast. Maybe they're grounded somewhere."

"Maybe," his mother said, in the tone of a woman performing agreement as a favor to the word.

At 1930 he raised the field frequency and called. "Rivergate base calling Rivergate party, do you copy?" The static answered him in its usual dialect, which contained no words but suggested many. He tried again at ten-minute intervals, and between attempts he did the math worksheet without absorbing a single number, and at some point his mother stopped opening cabinets and simply sat down across from him, and the two of them kept vigil together like a pair of lighthouse keepers waiting for a ship that was, presumably, fine, absolutely fine, just delayed, just weathered in, just—

At 2015 the radio spoke, and both of them came upright like marionettes.

"—all stations, all stations, this is Crane Expeditions, is anyone holding traffic for the Rivergate party—"

"That's Crane," Gideon's mother breathed. "Why is Crane on this frequency?"

Because Aldous Crane, sponsor and proprietor, was not a man who used radios himself if a telephone could be made to do the work, and telephones did not reach into the gorge country, which meant that if Crane was broadcasting, Crane wanted witnesses. Gideon keyed the mic before his mother could stop him.

"This is the Yarrow residence," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, pitched low in an imitation of his father that almost worked. "Go ahead, Crane."

A pause, long enough to have a shape. Then: "Ah. Young Gideon, is it?" Crane's voice was smooth and unhurried, a voice that had never once been surprised by anything it heard, including this. "Is your father with you?"

"No. He's due to check in. He's—" Gideon looked at the clock. "He's seventy-five minutes late."

Another pause. On the other end of the province, Gideon would think later, Aldous Crane was choosing his words with the care of a man laying stones across a creek. "Well," Crane said at last. "Elias always was punctual. I'm sure there's an explanation. Weather, most likely. These fronts come down off the divide faster than the forecasts say."

"Yes sir," Gideon said, because that was what you said.

"I'll want a full report when contact is reestablished," Crane continued. "Position, condition of equipment, condition of the—of the party. Everything. Can I rely on you for that?"

"Yes sir."

"Good lad." And there was something in the way he said it—not warmth exactly, but satisfaction, the sound of a man confirming that a tool was where he'd left it—and then Crane was gone, and the radio resumed its schoolteacher murmur, and Gideon sat there with his hand still on the key.

"Why does he want *you* to report?" his mother asked quietly.

"So there's a record," Gideon said. "Paper is testimony." He said it in his father's voice without meaning to, and his mother flinched as though he'd played a recording of it.

* * *

They took turns sleeping, or rather his mother slept in the chair by the radio and Gideon pretended to sleep in his bed while listening through the wall for the squelch click that meant a transmission. Nobody called that night. In the morning the weather advisory upgraded itself: heavy rain, rising water on the upper tributaries, small-craft warnings. Gideon copied it into the log in careful capitals and hated every letter.

By noon the next day the house had filled with other people, which was how grief worked in their town—it arrived as casseroles and men in canvas jackets saying *we've got a plane up, we've got two planes up*, and Gideon's mother moving among them with the terrible competence of a woman organizing a search for a man everyone else had begun describing in the past tense. Gideon refused the past tense. He sat at the radio and worked the schedule the way his father would have: hourly calls on the primary, half-hourly sweeps of the secondary, everything logged, times noted, signal strengths estimated. Paper is testimony. If his father came out of that gorge—if he walked down to some gravel bar with Prakash and the snoring Tomlin and flagged down a floatplane—there would be a record showing that somebody had been listening the entire time. That somebody had kept the light on.

On the third day a search pilot reported seeing debris in the lower gorge. On the fourth day they found the raft, or what the river had left of it: torn aluminum, a boot, a fuel tin crushed flat as a flower pressed in a book. No bodies. The river keeps what it keeps.

On the fifth day, Gideon's mother stopped opening cabinets and started sitting very still, and the men in canvas jackets began using phrases like *when you're ready* and *the company will want to send someone*, and Gideon understood, in the wordless way children understand large animals, that the adults had crossed a line he refused to cross. They had stopped waiting. Waiting, apparently, had a statute of limitations.

He kept calling anyway. Hourly on the primary. Half-hourly sweeps. He logged every call, every burst of static, every fisherman and freight hauler and lost tourist who drifted through the frequency, and once—on the ninth day, at 0400, in the dead gray hour when the radio's voice goes softest—he logged a signal he could not explain. A carrier wave, weak, steady, on the secondary frequency. Not voice. Just a hum, like someone holding a microphone near a machine, or like a machine holding a microphone near the world. It lasted ninety seconds. Then it was gone and never came back, and when he told the search coordinator about it the man patted Gideon's shoulder and said something gentle about how the atmosphere does strange things at night, and Gideon wrote in the log, in his smallest capitals: *UNIDENTIFIED CARRIER, 90 SEC, SIGNAL STRENGTH 2 OF 5. NOT WEATHER. CHECKED TWICE.*

Nobody ever checked twice. That was the lesson the summer taught him, the one he carried out of that kitchen and into the rest of his life: everybody stops listening eventually. The world's attention is a battery, and it drains. Casseroles stop coming. Planes get reassigned. Men in canvas jackets find other gorges. Even mothers, who love you past all reason, have to sleep sometime—and Gideon could not blame her, would spend decades not blaming her, for the morning she finally unplugged the radio herself, gently, the way you'd close a door on a room where someone had died, and said, "Come and eat, sweetheart. Come away from there."

But by then he had already counted. He had done the arithmetic that would organize his whole life: forty-one hours of scheduled check-ins missed, two hundred and sixteen calls transmitted, one unidentified carrier, zero answers. Numbers held still when everything else moved. Numbers didn't drift into the past tense. You could carry them, and they weighed exactly the same every day, and if you flew enough miles—if you measured the sky in careful, countable increments—you could outrun the fact that some questions don't get answered, they just get old.

* * *

Years later, people who flew with Gideon Yarrow—or with whoever Gideon Yarrow had decided to be that season—would notice his habits and mistake them for quirks. The way he logged every flight to the minute. The way he never let a passenger say *see you later* without answering *count on it*. The way he checked in early, always early, apologizing to dispatch with a grin: *didn't want anybody worrying.* They thought it was discipline. Some of them thought it was superstition.

It was neither. It was a promise made at a kitchen table in 2003 by a boy with his hand on a radio key: *no one waits for me the way I waited for you. No one counts minutes alone in a lit kitchen while the whole world decides, quietly, comfortably, to stop listening.*

And it might have stayed a private vow forever, folded up small and carried like the compass he'd inherited and rarely touched, if a charter manifest hadn't come through Rivergate one autumn with a crate marked FRAGILE: DO NOT OPEN, and a sponsor's name on the paperwork that Gideon recognized the way you recognize a voice on a frequency you thought was dead.

Aldous Crane. Still transmitting. Still wanting reports.

Gideon read the manifest twice, signed it, and told himself the tightness in his chest was just the altitude.
