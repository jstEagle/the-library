## 17. Mara's Testimony

The fever broke on the fourth morning, the way a fever does—suddenly, rudely, like a guest who has stayed too long and finally takes the hint. Mara Voss woke to birdsong, to the smell of woodsmoke, and to the distinct sensation of having been wrung out and hung to dry by someone with strong opinions about laundry.

She was lying in Gideon Yarrow's tent, which she recognized by the fact that it contained almost nothing. A sleeping bag. A dented kettle. A single tin cup, polished at the rim from years of the same mouth. A man who owned one cup was a man who had decided, at some point, that he would never need to set a place for anyone else.

She knew that arithmetic. She had done the same with her own life, once. Two chairs instead of four. One set of good plates.

"You're awake," said Sam, from the doorway, holding a bowl of something that claimed to be rice porridge. He looked terrible in the way that twenty-two-year-olds look terrible—rumpled, hollow-eyed, and fundamentally unbreakable. "How do you feel?"

"Like I've been read aloud from," Mara said. Her voice was a gravel road. "Badly. By someone who skips punctuation."

"That's the fever talking."

"That's the fever *leaving*. There's a difference." She took the bowl. Her hands shook, but they shook in the direction of her mouth, which was the important part. "Where is he?"

"Yarrow? Down at the river. He goes down there every morning and stands around looking at the water like it owes him money."

Mara ate half the porridge, which was an act of heroism, and then she sat up, and the tent tilted, and she waited for it to stop tilting, and then she thought: *I have to tell him.*

She had been thinking it, in fragments, through the worst of the fever. It had come to her in pieces, mixed in with hallucinations—her mother's kitchen, the smell of the dig at Kessel Flats, the sound of Crane's voice on the satellite phone saying *just confirm it, Dr. Voss, that's all I'm asking, just confirm it* —and now the fever was gone and the pieces had assembled themselves into a single, solid, terrible object sitting in the middle of her chest.

She had taken money to find a dead man. And the dead man had a son standing forty feet away, counting river water.

* * *

She found him at the gravel bar, as advertised. He was crouched at the waterline, doing something to a fuel drum with a wrench, and he didn't look up when her boots crunched the shale, but his shoulders changed—she'd noticed this about him, the way his whole body went quietly on alert when someone entered his space, like a man who had spent years being surprised by the world and had decided he wouldn't be again.

"You shouldn't be up," he said.

"I shouldn't be a lot of things. I'm a doctor of archaeology, not medicine, but I'm given to understand convalescence is best taken in small doses." She lowered herself onto a flat rock with the dignity of a queen taking a throne she suspected might be rigged. "I need to talk to you. Properly. While I'm lucid."

"You've been lucid."

"I've been *intermittently* lucid. There's a difference, and it matters for what I'm about to say." She watched him set down the wrench. Slowly. The way a man sets down a tool when he suspects the conversation is about to become the work. "You know who I am."

"You're Dr. Mara Voss. Field archaeologist. Your monograph on pre-war river trade settlements is the only thing on the shelf in my cabin that isn't a maintenance manual."

She blinked. "You've read my monograph."

"Twice. It's the only book up here I didn't already know the ending of." He still wasn't looking at her. "That's not what you're asking, though."

"No. That's not what I'm asking." She made herself say it plainly, because she had learned, in thirty years of fieldwork, that the only way through bad ground was straight across it. "I know who *you* are, Mr. Yarrow. Or I know who you were. Gideon Yarrow. Elias Yarrow's son."

The river made its noise. Somewhere upstream a bird said something rude to another bird.

"I figured," Gideon said at last.

"You *figured*."

"You said my father's name in your sleep. Night two. You said it twice, and then you told someone—I assume Crane—that you'd rather drown than file another report for him. Then you apologized to the tent pole." He turned and looked at her for the first time, and his face was doing something complicated and mostly succeeding at hiding it. "The apology was the part that convinced me. You don't apologize to tent poles unless your conscience is in bad shape."

Mara laughed, and it hurt her ribs, and she laughed anyway, because the alternative was crying and she had done her crying quota in 1998. "You have a terrible bedside manner, Mr. Yarrow."

"I'm not at your bedside. I'm at a fuel drum."

"Then your fuel-drum manner is terrible."

"Also true." He sat down on the gravel across from her, forearms on his knees, and waited. He was very good at waiting. She suspected it was his entire character, distilled: a man who had turned waiting into a profession and then into a personality.

So she told him.

* * *

"Two years ago," she began, "I got a letter. Not an email—a letter, on paper, with a letterhead. The Crane Foundation for Historical Preservation." She said the name the way you'd say the name of a disease you'd been diagnosed with. "It offered funding. Full funding. Equipment, satellite time, a helicopter insertion if I wanted it, for a survey of the upper Rivergate gorge. Pre-contact trade routes, they said. The usual."

"And you believed them."

"I *wanted* to believe them, which is worse and more common." She picked up a flat stone and turned it over in her fingers. "Do you know what funding looks like for a woman my age in my field, Mr. Yarrow? It looks like writing four hundred grant applications and getting polite letters back from committees that all contain the phrase 'at this time.' I had a reputation. I had publications. What I didn't have was a foundation, a chair, or a man's name attached to mine, and the arithmetic of that is very simple and very old. So when a rich man offered me the gorge of my dreams with no strings—" she turned the stone over again, "—I told myself there were no strings."

"And there were strings."

"There was a whole harp." She almost smiled. "The first year was legitimate. We surveyed, we mapped, we found genuinely wonderful things—trade beads, a ferry landing, a carved waymarker. Crane paid promptly and asked few questions. I thought, *here is a man who trusts scholars.* Then, fourteen months in, he visited the field office. First time. He brought a bottle of very good whisky and a folder, and over that whisky he asked me, very casually, whether we had come across any evidence of a survey expedition in the gorge. Nineteen ninety-eight to two thousand and four. A man named—"

She stopped. Not because she couldn't say it. Because he was looking at her, and she wanted to give him the courtesy of a warning before she said it.

"Say it," Gideon said quietly. "I've had two weeks to get used to the shape of it."

"Elias Yarrow."

The river kept on. Gideon's face did something small and controlled, like a man closing a door in a house he was trying to sell.

"He said the name like it was a debt," Mara went on. "Like the name itself had cost him money. And he asked—this is nearly verbatim, I've had a fever and a lot of time to replay it—*if the expedition could establish a date of death. Officially. With documentation.* He said there were 'outstanding legal questions' regarding property. And he said—" she made herself finish, "—he said the file had been closed in two thousand and four on the assumption of death, but an assumption wasn't a certificate, and a certificate wasn't a deed, and until there was proof, certain land claims remained, technically, in abeyance."

Gideon was silent for a long moment. "Whose land claim?"

"That's the part I didn't understand at first. I thought he meant *Yarrow* land—that the father had owned something and the son had walked away from it, and Crane wanted it." She set the stone down. "But it isn't the Yarrows' land he wants, Mr. Yarrow. It's the land *adjacent* to it. Sixty square kilometers of the upper gorge, mineral rights and water rights, that Crane Development has been trying to acquire for eleven years. And there's one parcel he can't touch. One claim, filed in 1999, held in trust, that doesn't lapse unless the claimant is legally confirmed dead."

"Whose claim?"

"Your father's. Elias Yarrow filed a claim on the gorge two years before he disappeared. Mining survey rights—he was a surveyor, that was his trade. The claim is still live. It's been sitting there for twenty years like a fishbone in Crane's throat, because a dead man's claim passes to heirs, and a missing man's claim just *sits*, and either way Crane can't get his machines into that gorge without a court fight he might lose." She watched him absorb it. "He didn't send us to find your father, Mr. Yarrow. He sent us to *bury* him. To find remains, a journal, a crash site, anything a coroner could sign off on. Proof of death. That's the deliverable. That's what the FRAGILE crate of antibiotics was really buying."

"And when you figured that out?"

"About six weeks ago." Her mouth twisted. "I confronted him by satellite phone. I told him I was a scholar, not a gravedigger for hire, and that if he wanted a death certificate he could apply for one like everyone else. He was very calm. He said, *Dr. Voss, everyone in that gorge is working for me, whether they know it or not.* And then he stopped answering our check-ins, and our resupply came with double rations and half the fuel we'd asked for, and I understood that we hadn't been abandoned." She looked out at the water. "We'd been *written off*. We were supposed to fail. A lost expedition, a tragedy, a sad footnote—and in the confusion of the inquiry, a court declares Elias Yarrow dead in absentia, the claim lapses to the territory, and Crane acquires it at auction. We were the paperwork. We were how he was going to get the gorge."

Gideon stood up. He walked three steps toward the water, stopped, walked back. She recognized the movement—it was what a man did when his body needed to be doing something and there was nothing to do.

"He knew," Gideon said. "Crane. He knew my father's claim was live. He knew the file, the dates, the—" His voice caught on something and he smoothed it flat. "He knew more about Elias Yarrow than I did. I was nineteen. I knew my father flew north one October and didn't come back, and I knew my mother waited by the radio for a year, and then we stopped waiting, and that was the whole of my inheritance. A silence. And this man has been carrying around a *deed*."

"He carries a lot of things," Mara said. "The folder he showed me had your family name on the spine. I saw it for maybe four seconds before he put it away. But I saw it."

Gideon turned. "What else was in it?"

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. But I know what he *said*, and I've had a fever with nothing to do but take notes." She counted on her fingers, an old field habit. "One: he said, *the son is not a factor.* His words. He said you'd changed your name and gone to ground and that men who bury themselves don't come up for inquests. He'd looked into you, Mr. Yarrow. He knows who you are, or he did. Two: he said the 2004 file contained a coroner's preliminary finding—*presumed deceased, remains unrecovered*—and that the final certificate had never been issued because the family, your mother, had refused to sign. Refused to accept it. She fought it for two years and then she stopped fighting, and Crane has been waiting ever since for someone to finish the sentence."

Gideon sat back down on the gravel. Slowly, the way old men sit down, though he couldn't have been more than forty. "She never told me any of that."

"Would you have wanted her to?"

"No," he said, almost immediately, and then, with something like wonder: "Yes. Both. She fought a corporation for two years by herself, in some town office, with lawyers she must have been paying for out of—out of whatever we had, which wasn't much—and she never said a word to me, because I was nineteen and already halfway out the door." He rubbed a hand over his jaw. "I thought she gave up. I thought she just... let him go. And she was in a room somewhere signing nothing, over and over, for two years."

Mara let the silence sit. She had learned that from the dig sites, too: the important layers are always under the loose soil, and you don't get to them by digging faster.

"There's a third thing," she said finally. "And it's the reason I needed to be lucid before I said any of this. The reason I needed to say it to your face."

"Go on."

"Sam has the strongbox. You know that. The leader of my expedition—Tom Brandt, a decent man, a better man than I've made him sound—died trying to take it downriver. What's in it is the expedition's full documentation. Surveys, photographs, the site records, the correspondence with Crane." She met his eyes. "Including a letter from Crane, on his own letterhead, instructing us—in writing—to prioritize the search for 'remains consistent with E. Yarrow' over all other objectives, and to report findings 'directly and exclusively' to him, bypassing the territorial registrar. That letter is a confession, Mr. Yarrow. It's Crane admitting, in his own hand, that he funded a fake expedition to manufacture a death certificate and seize a dead man's claim."

Gideon was very still. "You're saying we have him."

"I'm saying Sam is guarding a rifle and a box, and one of those things can end a man, and I'm saying that what we do with that letter is the biggest decision anyone on this river is going to make this year." She spread her hands. "I've thought about it through a week of fever, so believe me when I tell you I've considered every angle, including the cowardly ones. And I keep coming back to the fact that your mother fought that fight alone and lost it, and that there is a man alive right now who did that to her, and who then sent three people up this river to die for a signature."

The river ran. Gideon looked at it the way he'd been looking at it every morning, she thought—like a man taking measurements of something that couldn't be measured.

"First," he said, "we get everyone out alive. That's the whole list. That's the list and there's nothing under it." He stood, and offered her a hand up, and his grip was careful, as if she were something that had been broken and recently mended. "But I'm not going to pretend I didn't hear the rest. My mother signed nothing for two years. The least I can do is read everything in that box before I decide what the dead are allowed to say."

"That's the most sensible thing anyone's said to me since the fever," Mara said. Then, because her ribs hurt and her hands were still shaking and honesty was a fever of its own: "Mr. Yarrow. Gideon. There's one more thing, and then I'll let you get back to your fuel drum."

He waited.

"Crane didn't send us up here just to find a body." She held his eyes. "He sent us because the claim requires *annual survey activity* to stay live. Someone has been filing that paperwork. Every year, for twenty years. Someone has been keeping your father's claim alive with surveys and fees and filings—and Crane has never been able to prove who, because whoever it is knows the system better than he does." She watched the realization move through his face like weather. "Somebody out there has spent two decades guarding your father's ghost, Gideon. And I don't think it's a ghost that's been doing the filing."

Behind them, at the camp, Sam Okafor's voice called out—cracking, urgent, carrying across the shale:

"Gideon! The radio—it's picking something up. It's faint, but—there's someone on the old survey frequency. They're asking for Yarrow. They're asking for *Elias Yarrow*."
