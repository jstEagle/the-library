## 18. Fuel Math

The rain started an hour after I got back to the gravel bar, which was either terrible luck or the river's way of clearing its throat before it spoke.

I stood under the wing of the Cessna with my notebook open and let the first drops spot the paper, because for a while I forgot to move. That's the thing about arithmetic—it doesn't care about your feelings, but it does have a way of making you stand very still.

Three people. One airplane. A strip of gravel that would be underwater in—my best guess—four days, maybe five if the rain upstream was merciful, and nothing about this week had been merciful.

I clicked my pen and began.

* * *

Here is what a bush pilot knows that nobody puts on a written test: every airplane has two weights that matter. The one on the placard inside the door, and the one God actually allows you to lift off the earth on a given day, given wind, given temperature, given how much of a liar you're willing to be to yourself at the moment the wheels leave.

The placard said twelve hundred pounds of useful load. The day said less.

I wrote it out the way I'd write it for a student, clean columns, no sentiment:

Fuel for Rivergate against a headwind: ninety minutes' worth, plus reserve, because I don't fly on hope. Call it forty gallons. Two hundred and forty pounds.

Mara Voss: a hundred and thirty pounds of archaeologist, most of it fever, all of it trembling. She'd lost weight since the expedition went quiet—you could see it in her wrists—but sick people are heavy in ways scales don't measure. They lean. They can't brace. They take up more room than their mass suggests.

Sam Okafor: a hundred and seventy pounds, twenty-two years old, and carrying a rifle, a strongbox he wouldn't let out of arm's reach, and enough guilt to sink a barge. Say two-ten with everything.

Me: one-ninety, and I wasn't going anywhere except wherever the airplane went.

That left, by my first pass, something like six hundred pounds for gear, water, food, the survival kit we couldn't leave behind, and whatever the strongbox weighed—and the strongbox, when Sam finally let me put my hands on it, weighed a smug little sixty pounds, like it knew what it was doing to my numbers.

Six hundred pounds sounds generous until you start filling it. Sleeping bags, tarps, the medical kit, three days of food, water for the wait, the tools we'd need if the plane bogged in soft gravel. Four hundred gone before I blinked. Then the headwind came back around and ate another ten gallons of margin, and the column of *what fits* shrank like a wool sweater in hot water.

I did the math three times. It came out the same way all three times, the way true things do.

Two seats full. One person stays.

* * *

I found them in the remains of the mess tent, which Mara had insisted on re-pitching even though half its poles were splinters, because—as she'd informed me with the imperious clarity of a woman running a hundred-and-four-degree fever—"civilization is a decision you make daily." I liked her immediately and entirely too much for someone who might be dead in four days if I got the arithmetic wrong.

She was propped against a supply crate with a blanket around her shoulders, gray-faced but lucid today, watching Sam feed the camp stove with the concentration of a man defusing something. He'd gotten good at small tasks. It was the large ones that made his hands shake.

"Talk," Mara said, when she saw my face. "Pilots have two faces. The one that says everything's fine, and the one you're wearing now."

"The river's coming up." I crouched by the stove, held my palms over it even though I didn't need the warmth, because it gave me somewhere to look that wasn't her eyes. "Rain's been sitting on the headwaters since yesterday. This bar goes under. I've seen it happen on the Kettle in '19—took a whole outfit's gear and very nearly took the outfit."

"How long?"

"Four days. Five if we're lucky."

"And the plane takes how many?"

There it was. Fever or no fever, she'd been doing her own arithmetic since the moment I landed, and mine had only caught up to hers.

"Two," I said. "Comfortably. Three if I lie to myself about the wind, and I promised somebody once I'd stop doing that."

Sam set down the fuel can very carefully, the way you do when your body wants to throw it. "Then we draw lots," he said. "Or—no. No, Mara goes first. That's not a discussion."

"It's entirely a discussion," Mara said.

"You have a fever that won't break. You're the reason any of us are still—" He stopped himself, jaw working. Whatever he'd been about to say had teeth in it.

"I'm also the lightest thing in this camp besides the tent pegs," Mara said dryly. "Which I suspect is the actual engine of your chivalry, Mr. Okafor, and I respect it. But that leaves the two of you, and one seat, and a second run through weather that may not exist." She looked at me. "Will there be a second run?"

And there was the question I'd been circling since the first column of numbers, the one I'd been hoping the notebook would answer for me so I wouldn't have to say it out loud.

"If the bar holds and the weather breaks," I said, "yes. If either of those fails—" I shrugged, and hated the shrug, because shrugs are what cowards use instead of words. "If either fails, whoever's here walks out upriver or doesn't. There's no road. There's barely a gorge trail, and it's flooded past the narrows by now."

Silence, except the rain getting serious on the tent fabric, drumming like impatient fingers.

"So we're not choosing who goes," Sam said quietly. "We're choosing who gambles."

"That's one way to put it."

"What's yours?"

I fed a stick into the stove because my hands wanted employment. "Mine is that pilots are paid to be honest about weight and weather and nothing else, and I'm about to be dishonest about all three."

* * *

Here's what I didn't tell them, standing there in the drumming tent with the rain coming down like the sky had decided to settle accounts: I'd already done the other math. The private kind. The kind you do at two in the morning with your hat over your eyes.

One hundred and ninety pounds of pilot versus one hundred and seventy pounds of kid. If I stayed and Sam flew out with Mara—if I rode shotgun long enough to get them off the gravel and then handed over the controls, which he could just barely do, which I could teach him to do well enough in two days of ground instruction—the numbers worked. Barely. Ugly. But they worked.

And some part of me, the part I keep locked below decks where it can't steer, had heard that solution the moment I wrote *one-ninety* in the notebook, and had gone very quiet and very calm, the way it always does when it finds a door marked *sacrifice* and starts trying the handle.

Because that's the family business, isn't it. Yarrow men, going down with the manifest. My father flew into a gorge twenty years ago and became a blank space on a chart, and I had spent my whole adult life telling myself I was nothing like him—measured miles, no heroics, no flying on hope—and here I was at midnight doing sums that ended with me staying behind on a drowning gravel bar, and feeling *good* about it. Feeling clean. Feeling, God help me, like the arithmetic itself was a kind of inheritance, and I was finally collecting.

I caught myself smiling at the stove and wiped it off. Dangerous, that smile. I recognized it from photographs.

* * *

Morning came gray and low, the cloud deck sitting on the ridgelines like a lid. I walked the length of the bar twice, kicking at the gravel, reading the river the way you read a patient's chart. It had come up eight inches overnight. The willow clump at the downstream end was already standing in water, and the current had moved its attention to the upstream point, chewing at it in slow, thoughtful bites.

Four days. Maybe three. The rain hadn't stopped; it had just gotten organized.

Sam found me at the upstream point, where the water slid past close enough to spit in. He stood beside me with his hands jammed in his pockets, young and hollow-eyed, and for a while neither of us said anything, because the river was saying plenty.

"My dad fished," he said finally. "Back home. Every Sunday, this awful little pond, stocked trout, guaranteed catch. He used to say the fish didn't matter, it was the waiting that counted." He kicked a stone into the current. "First time I've thought about that in years. Funny what comes up when you think you're done."

"Funny," I agreed.

"Gideon." He turned to look at me, and I braced, because people only use your name like that when they're about to hand you something heavy. "Whatever you're planning—I can see you planning, you've got the same face Mara gets when she's redoing someone else's excavation notes—whatever it is, don't. We flip a coin, or we argue it out like adults, but you don't get to quietly decide to be the one who stays. I know that face. That's the face of a man writing himself out of the story."

"I don't have a face like that."

"Everybody has a face like that. Mine looks like filling out forms." He almost smiled. "Ask me sometime why I signed on with Crane's outfit. It's a real inspiring tale about a kid who wanted to disappear into work."

I looked at him—really looked, maybe for the first time since I'd pulled him off that rifle-guarded strongbox—and saw the thing I'd been too busy to see. Not a coward, not a zealot. Just a young man who'd learned early that being useful was safer than being known.

Well. I knew a little something about that.

"Nobody's writing anybody out yet," I said. "Today we prep the plane, we teach you the radio, and we watch the water. Tomorrow the math decides. Agreed?"

"Agreed," he said, and then, sly as anything: "You know Mara's going to win the argument, right? She's been practicing speeches since dawn. I heard her rehearsing. There were gestures."

"There were *gestures?*"

"Full theater. She's going to destroy us both."

Despite everything—the water rising, the clouds sitting fat and black on the headwaters, the notebook in my pocket with its three identical columns of bad news—I laughed. Actually laughed, out loud, on a gravel bar at the ass-end of nowhere with a flood coming down the valley. It felt so strange in my chest that I did it again, just to check.

* * *

We spent the day making ready, which is the oldest trick in the book for keeping despair busy. I stripped the plane to bones: out came the spare tire, the tool roll I could bear to lose, the camping kit trimmed to essentials. Sam inventoried the strongbox contents onto a single sheet and repacked it, and I noticed he'd added a sealed envelope addressed in block letters to a newspaper in the capital, and I noticed him noticing me notice, and we agreed without words to have that conversation later, or never.

Mara supervised from her blanket like a general on a folding chair, issuing orders in a voice that gained strength by the hour. By afternoon she was upright, arguing fuel policy with me on technical grounds she'd clearly absorbed overnight, and I let her win two points out of politeness and one out of genuine surprise.

"You fly like a man balancing a checkbook," she told me, apropos of my refusal to top off beyond calculated need. "Everything balanced, nothing wasted, no passion."

"Passion crashes airplanes."

"So does caution, eventually. It just crashes slower." She settled back against the crate, tired suddenly, the fever flickering behind her eyes like a lamp on a bad circuit. "My father was a banker. He used to say the books always balance in the end—they just don't always balance in your favor."

I thought of my notebook, three columns deep in unfavorable balances, and said nothing.

At dusk the rain thickened. I stood at the tent flap and watched the river come up another inch, then another, patient as a debt collector, and I took out the pencil and made the entry I'd been avoiding all day, small and square in the corner of the page, the way accountants mark the figures they can't make pretty:

*Day one. Water +14". Fuel sufficient for two souls, one crossing. Third soul: pending.*

Pending. That was the word for it. Pending, like a check that might clear, like a promise the weather hadn't co-signed.

Behind me, Sam was showing Mara how to work the radio's backup set, his voice patient, hers sharp with fever and focus both, and outside the rain kept up its arithmetic—one drop, another drop, ten thousand drops, all of it flowing downhill toward our little island of gravel, adding itself up.

Tomorrow, somebody would have to stop being pending.

I closed the notebook and went in to teach a sick woman and a scared kid how to survive me.
