## 19. Volunteer

The rain stopped an hour before dawn, and in the sudden quiet the river got louder, which is the thing about rivers nobody warns you about. Silence doesn't mean safety. It means the water has your full attention now.

I stood at the edge of the gravel bar with a cup of camp coffee — Sam's coffee, which was less coffee than a rumor of coffee, burnt twigs steeped in regret — and did arithmetic. I'd been doing it all night, in the tent, on my knees with the fuel drums and the scale I'd rigged from a pack board and a spring scale off Sam's wrecked equipment case. The numbers hadn't improved with the darkness. Arithmetic is honest that way. You can lie to a river, lie to a sponsor, lie to yourself with real fluency if you practice, but a weight-and-balance sheet just sits there looking at you.

Three souls. One drum of usable fuel after the raft run, minus what the climb-out would drink. Mara, who was walking again but walked like a woman carrying her own body as freight. Sam, who was nineteen and weighed about as much as my toolbox. Me, and the plane, and the margin.

The margin was a man.

I drank the coffee and let that thought sit where it landed.

* * *

Mara found me at the plane an hour later. She'd tied her hair back with a length of surveyor's flagging tape, orange, which should have looked ridiculous and instead looked like a woman who had decided to be practical about the rest of her life. Her fever had broken two days ago. What was left of her was thin and steady, the way a candle is steady after it's burned down to the stub.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"Neither did you."

"I slept." She looked at the drums, at the raft of lashed expedition debris pulled up on the bar, at the water marks on the stones that told the river's story better than any gauge. "You've been doing the math."

"I've been doing the math."

"And?"

I could have lied to her. I'd gotten good at lying — I'd lied to Aldous Crane's face on a dirt strip with my heart going like a prop at full throttle, and he'd bought it, and I'd discovered that lying is a skill like any other, which is a discovery I'd have paid good money not to make. But Mara Voss had spent nine days in a shredded camp guarding a box with a dying man and a boy, and she'd earned the truth the way you earn anything up here, which is by outlasting it.

"The plane takes two," I said. "Comfortably. Three if the wind cooperates and I'm honest about the fuel, and the wind hasn't cooperated since the Paleozoic."

"So one of us stays."

"One of us stays."

She was quiet. Downriver, the water had come up another hand's width overnight. You could see it in the changed shape of the bar — the downstream point had gone soft, drowned, like the river was eating the gravel one bite at a time and didn't intend to leave a tip.

"I'll stay," she said.

"No."

"Gideon."

"No," I said again, gentler. "You stay, and you're staying alone, because I'm not flying out and leaving you, and if I stay too, then we've got two people on a drowning bar and one seat going home empty, which is the stupidest arithmetic I've ever heard and I once watched a man in Fort Reliance try to trade a snowmobile for a canoe in January."

She almost smiled. It was a thin thing, but it was there. "And if you fly out with two of us and come back—"

"Come back through what?" I nodded upriver, where the sky over the gorge was doing that thing it does, stacking grey on grey like a man building a wall he doesn't plan to explain. "That front's going to sit on the gorge for three days, maybe four. The bar's got a day in it. Maybe less. There's no second run, Mara. There's one run, and then there's whatever the river decides about the rest."

"So you've known since last night."

"Since the raft."

She looked at me for a long time. There was something in her face I couldn't read at first, and then I could, and I wished I couldn't. It was recognition. Not of my name — she'd known my name since the fever broke, had said *Yarrow* once, quietly, like setting down something fragile — but of the thing underneath the name. The thing my father had, according to every story my mother ever told, and according to the ledger I'd read in a dead man's camp with my hands shaking.

The thing where a man does the math and puts himself on the wrong side of it.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're about to decide. Don't decide it quietly. That's how your father—" She stopped. The river filled the pause, patient, enormous.

"That's how my father what?" I asked, and my voice came out level, which surprised me.

Mara looked downriver. "I only know what the file said. What Crane's file said. That Elias Yarrow put himself last on the manifest too. That there was room in the raft for two and he counted the supplies instead." She turned back to me. "I'm telling you because you deserve to know it's a family habit. And habits can be broken."

* * *

Sam was waiting at the tent when we came back, and I could tell from the way he was standing — arms crossed, weight on his heels, chin up in that way teenagers have when they've rehearsed an argument in their heads all night and are determined to deliver it in one piece — that Mara had already talked to him, or he'd already talked to her, or the two of them had conducted one of those silent negotiations that people who've survived something together can do without a single word.

"I'm not going first," Sam said.

"Good morning to you too."

"I'm serious. Mara goes. She's the one Crane wants gone, she's the one who can testify, she's—" He ran out of argument and reached for the next one. "—she's the lightest. That's just physics. You said it's physics."

"It is physics," I agreed. "And you're the lightest of the two of you, so by your own logic—"

"That's not—" He caught himself, flushed, started over. "Look. I'm the one who can do the second run. If there is a second run. I can hold the camp, I've got the rifle, I know where the cache is, I can—" His voice cracked in the middle and he hated it, I could see him hating it, and I remembered being nineteen and hating the exact same thing. "I can survive out here. I've been surviving out here for two weeks."

"You've been surviving out here for two weeks with a rifle, a strongbox, and a river between you and the things in the gorge," I said. "And the river's leaving."

He looked at the water. We all looked at the water. It had a way of ending arguments.

"Sam." I waited until he looked at me. "There is no second run. I need you to hear that from me and not from the river. Whatever plan we make, we make it knowing the bar's got maybe a day, the weather's got the gorge locked for longer than that, and the fuel math gives us one trip. One. So the question isn't who's brave enough to stay. The question is who the plane can carry, and who it can't, and what we do about that without lying to each other."

"Then take Mara," he said, quieter now. "Please. Take her first. She's sick, she's — she's the whole reason any of this matters. I'll be fine. I've got the rifle and I've got the high ground and I'll rig a signal and—"

"You'll be fine," I said, "on a gravel bar that's going under, with no raft, no fuel, and no pilot."

He didn't have an answer to that. Nobody has an answer to that. That's the thing about the truth — it's a lousy debater but it wins every time, because it just stands there.

* * *

We spent the morning pretending to prepare, which is to say we spent the morning actually preparing while all three of us pretended the preparation wasn't a funeral arrangement. I stripped everything off the plane that wasn't airframe or soul: the spare tire, the survival kit down to its bones, the tool roll I'd carried for nine years and never once needed and would have carried nine more. I weighed it all on the spring scale and wrote the numbers on the back of my hand in pencil, and my hand looked like a gambler's cheat sheet, which was about right.

Sam helped without being asked. He was good at that, at reading what a job needed and doing it, and I found myself thinking — in the way you think things you don't say — that he'd have made a hell of a pilot. The kind who checks the tie-downs twice and never brags about the landing. The kind I'd spent twenty years pretending not to be, because being that kind meant being the other kind too, the kind who stays.

"You're doing it again," Sam said.

"Doing what?"

"That thing where you go quiet and your jaw does the thing." He cinched a strap. "Mara says your father did that."

"Your Mara," I said, "talks too much for a woman who was delirious three days ago."

"She says you're going to stay."

The wrench in my hand stopped moving. The bar was very quiet. Just the river, and the wind coming down the gorge ahead of the front, smelling of rain that hadn't started yet.

"Does she," I said.

"She told me not to let you. She said — she said that's what he did. Your dad. That he always put himself last on the manifest." Sam straightened up and looked at me, and his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and nineteen years old and absolutely certain he was right, which is the most dangerous combination in the world and also, I remembered, the most honest. "So don't. Whatever you're planning. Don't."

"Sam—"

"No, you listen." He'd never raised his voice at me before. His voice cracked again and he pushed through it. "You flew up here when nobody made you. You hiked into the gorge when Mara told you not to. You pulled me off that raft with a rope in white water, and you didn't even swear, and I swear constantly. You don't get to do all that and then drown in a river because some dead guy's habits are contagious."

I looked at him for a long moment.

Then I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came up out of me rough and surprised, the first real laugh since the strip at Rivergate, maybe the first real laugh in years, and Sam looked at me like I'd lost my mind, and maybe I had, a little, in the good way.

"Contagious," I said. "That's a new one."

"I'm serious."

"I know you are. That's why it's funny." I put the wrench down and sat on the pontoon strut, and after a moment he sat next to me, and we looked at the river together the way men do when the conversation is bigger than either of them. "Here's what I know, Sam. I know the plane carries two. I know the bar has a day in it, maybe. I know the fuel math, and the fuel math doesn't care about anybody's feelings. And I know that if I put myself on that plane, I'm spending weight that could be a human being, and I have spent my whole life being very careful about weight."

"That's not the same as—"

"It's exactly the same as," I said. "It's the whole thing. Everything else is decoration."

He was quiet. Then, in a smaller voice: "Mara said you'd say that."

"Mara," I said, "has spent too much time around my family's paperwork."

* * *

But here is what I didn't tell either of them, and what I'll tell you now, because a man ought to be honest somewhere, even if it's only on the page.

I had already decided.

I'd decided it in the night, somewhere between the third weighing and the fourth, when the numbers stopped being numbers and became a name. And the ugly, unspeakable truth — the one Mara had put her finger on, the one Sam had circled without landing on — is that part of the decision felt like relief. Not all of it. Most of it was simple, cold arithmetic, the kind I trust more than I trust people. But down at the bottom of it, in the silt, there was a small warm coal of something that wasn't arithmetic at all. Something that said: *this is the shape of it. This is what he did. This is the family trade, passed hand to hand — the man who stays, the man who counts the supplies, the man who puts himself last on the manifest so the manifest balances.*

My father had left a ledger in a dead man's camp with his handwriting in it. He'd left a journal in a cave with his last page asking a stranger to tell his son he was coming home. And I had burned the proof of his fate with my own hands rather than let Crane own it, and I had thought that was the end of the inheritance — that I'd carried it and set it down.

But inheritance doesn't work like cargo. You can't burn the part that's in your blood. It comes up the river whether you charter it or not.

So I would stay. I would put Mara on the plane because she was the mission, and Sam because he was nineteen and the world owed him more than it owed me, and I would stay on the bar with the rifle and the rising water and whatever the river decided, and I would not tell them, because telling them would make it a debate, and debates are how good plans die. I would tell them at the last possible minute, or not at all. I would do it quietly. That's how my father did it.

That's how my father did it, and look how that turned out. A ledger trailing off mid-sentence. A son counting miles for twenty years to keep from counting anything else.

Mara's voice came back to me, thin and steady as a candle: *habits can be broken.*

I stood at the edge of the bar and watched the water take another bite of the downstream point, and I made the decision again, and then — for the first time in my life, as far as I know — I made it out loud, to nobody, to the river, to whatever was listening.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. But not quietly."

* * *

We finished the loading by late afternoon. Mara sat on a crate and directed operations with the imperious calm of a woman who had organized digs on three continents and was not about to let a bush pilot and a teenager pack an airplane without supervision. She was right about nearly everything. I let her believe she was right about all of it.

The plane sat light and mean, stripped to its working bones, the drum lashed in the cargo bay, the seats configured for two and a prayer. I walked around her twice, checking the tie-downs, and Sam checked them a third time, because that's the kind he is, and I caught myself thinking again that he'd have made a hell of a pilot, and caught myself thinking something underneath that thought, something I wasn't ready to look at straight.

The sky over the gorge had gone the color of wet slate. The wind had come around and was pushing upriver, into our teeth, which was the last insult the arithmetic needed.

"First light," I said. "We go at first light. The air's smoothest then, and the front's still building — if we're through the notch before it closes, we're through."

"And you," Mara said. Not a question. She'd been watching me all day with those archaeologist's eyes, reading the strata of me, and she knew something had shifted even if she didn't know what.

"First light," I said, "we'll see."

Sam opened his mouth to argue, and I put a hand on his shoulder — the first time I'd touched him except to haul him out of a river — and whatever he saw in my face made him close it again.

We ate what there was to eat. Mara told a story about a dig in the Atacama where the cook had run off with the mule and they'd lived a week on canned peaches and spite, and Sam laughed, really laughed, and for about an hour the bar was just a place where three tired people sat near a fire instead of a stage where something terrible was being arranged. I let myself have the hour. You have to. That's another thing the interior teaches you: grief and laughter run in the same channel, and if you dam one, you lose both.

I turned in early, or lay down early, which is different. The tent flap glowed with the last of the fire. Outside, the river kept up its low enormous talking, and the wind tested the guy lines, and somewhere up in the black the front was assembling itself out of the Pacific's whole bad mood.

I lay there and did the math one more time, and this time I did it honestly, all the way down, and when I got to the bottom there was a man there, and I made myself look at him.

Then I got up, quietly, and took the pencil, and on the back of my hand, under the weight figures, under the fuel numbers, under all the honest arithmetic, I wrote one word.

I looked at it for a long time in the dark.

And that's when I heard the gravel shift outside the tent — a footstep, light, deliberate, the footstep of someone who had also been lying awake doing math — and the tent flap opened, and Sam stood there in the firelight holding my pencil in his fist, and he said:

"You wrote *Sam.*"
