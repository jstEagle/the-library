## 20. First Lift

Mara Voss weighed nothing.

That was the first thing Gideon understood as he lifted her into the Beaver's cabin and settled her onto the sleeping bag he'd spread across the cargo floor—she weighed nothing at all, or she weighed everything, depending on how you did the math. A hundred and ten pounds of fevered archaeologist, plus the blanket, plus the oxygen bottle he'd rigged from a fishing float and a length of hose because his first-aid kit had never been meant for this. Against four hundred pounds of fuel, against the tools and drums he'd already offloaded onto the gravel bar, against Sam Okafor standing in the shallows with water to his knees holding the strut like a man holding onto a decision.

"Say it again," Sam said. "The plan. All of it."

"The plan is there isn't one," Gideon said. "There's arithmetic. I fly low under the cloud, I follow the river, I don't fight anything the sky wants to do. Two hours if the wind behaves. Three if it doesn't."

"And then you come back."

"Then I come back."

Sam looked at him for a long moment. He was twenty-three years old and he'd spent nine days guarding a steel box with a rifle and no bullets left in it, and somehow his eyes were still steady. Gideon found himself thinking that the kid would make a better pilot than most pilots he knew, because the essential skill was exactly this: standing in cold water, holding on, asking the question twice.

"You'll come back," Sam repeated, not as a question this time. As an instruction.

"I'll come back," Gideon said, and climbed in before either of them could examine the promise too closely.

* * *

Mara surfaced from the fever twice on the run-up.

The first time was over the engine check, when Gideon ran the throttle up to full and held it there, watching the gauges the way other men watched horizons. The Beaver shuddered against its own brakes, and Mara's eyes opened—gray, unfocused, fixed on some point six inches behind the back of his head—and she said, clearly, with the terrible precision of the delirious:

"Don't open it."

Then her eyes closed again, and Gideon sat with his hands on the yoke and let the engine answer for him.

The second time was worse. He was thirty seconds from rotation, rolling down the gravel bar with the river sliding past on his left and the wall of the gorge rising black-green on his right, and Mara turned her head toward him and said, in a voice gone soft and almost kind: "You have his chin. Did you know that? You have Elias Yarrow's chin exactly."

Gideon pulled the stick back.

The Beaver came off the gravel heavy and graceless, wallowing a hundred feet, two hundred, and Gideon nursed it into a shallow climb along the river's line, and he did not look at Mara, and he did not think about chins, and he told himself—with the flat, practiced calm of a man who has been telling himself things for nineteen years—that what she'd said was fever talking. Fever reads faces the way water reads stone. It finds every crack and runs through all of them.

He set up his climb at sixty-five knots and pointed the nose south, and the storm's leading edge stood up ahead of him like a country deciding whether to exist.

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about weather, Gideon thought, watching it come: it doesn't arrive. It *negotiates*. The front wasn't a wall so much as a long argument between two airs—the warm river air going up, the cold mass shouldering down from the north—and he was proposing to thread himself through the exact seam where the argument was loudest.

The radio gave him fragments. Rivergate's weather officer, a patient woman named Odile Ferrand whom Gideon trusted more than most clergy, reading out winds and ceilings in her unhurried voice, as though reciting a recipe. "Ceiling lowering to eight hundred at the strip, Gideon. Squalls embedded along the ridge line. If you're coming, come now, and come low."

"Coming low," he said.

"And Gideon." A pause on the frequency, the particular pause of a woman choosing her words. "Your passenger. How is she?"

"Alive," he said. "Ask me again in two hours."

Behind him, Mara made a small sound—not words, just a sound, the sound of someone swimming up from very deep water and finding the surface farther away than advertised. Gideon reached back without looking, found her wrist, and squeezed it once. Her pulse hammered against his fingers like a bird in a box. Fast, but there. Present tense.

"You're doing fine," he said, to her, to himself, to the airplane. "Everybody's doing fine."

The lie settled over the cabin like a second passenger.

* * *

The first hour went the way good hours go: unnoticed.

He flew the river at four hundred feet, sometimes three, skimming above the brown water with the current running beneath him like a road he couldn't take. Rain began in patches—you'd fly through a curtain of it, ten seconds of drumming on the windshield, then out into clean gray air again, then another curtain. The wipers were a joke; he'd never replaced the blades because in nineteen years of flying mail and medicine he'd never needed them, and now he flew mostly by tilting his head to look through the clear triangle of glass at the bottom corner of the windshield, which gave him a view of the world about the size of a serving plate.

It was enough. It was always enough. That was the whole secret of bush flying, the one nobody wrote on the brochures: you didn't need to see everything. You needed to see the next thing. The bend. The bluff. The wire strung across a canyon by some trapper who never filed a flight plan. Ten thousand hours of sky, and every one of them had taught him the same lesson, which was that the sky rewards humility and punishes confidence, and forgives neither.

At the mouth of the Kettle tributary he checked his watch and did the fuel math for the fourth time. Full tanks minus ninety minutes burned equals enough to reach Rivergate with margin—if the wind stayed quartering instead of head-on. If the squalls didn't cost him detours. If the ridge—

The ridge.

He'd been avoiding the thought the way you avoid a tooth, and now the river bent east and delivered it to him whole: the Saddleback ridge, the last high ground between him and home, five miles of granite shoulder rising fifteen hundred feet above the valley, and the cloud sitting down on it like a lid on a pot.

Below the ridge, the river cut through a gap maybe six hundred feet wide. Above the ridge, the cloud base, by Odile's last report, was somewhere around eleven hundred feet and falling. Which meant a corridor of perhaps—perhaps—four hundred feet of flyable air over rock that ate airplanes for a hobby, and the wind funneling through the gap from the northwest at speeds the trees were already reporting in body language, bending and streaming and letting go of their leaves all at once.

"Gideon," Odile said in his headset, as if she could see him looking. "Squall line's moving faster than the model said. Over the Saddleback you've got maybe twenty minutes of ugly, then it gets honest."

"Honest how?"

"Honestly dangerous. Or honestly closed. Depends which side of it you're on when it arrives."

Gideon looked at his fuel gauge. Looked at the ridge. Did the arithmetic again, and got the same answer, which was the nature of arithmetic—it didn't care how many times you asked.

"Going over," he said.

* * *

The turbulence started a mile out, like a handshake from something much larger than him.

First it was a shudder, a shrug, the airplane muttering to itself. Then a downdraft took the Beaver by the collar and dropped it two hundred feet in four seconds, and Gideon's stomach arrived late, and behind him he heard the oxygen bottle roll and clank against the crate, and Mara cried out—a thin sound, more animal than human—and he said, "I've got you, I've got us," in the voice he used for frightened passengers, which was also the voice he used for himself at altitude in bad air, and which had never once worked on either.

He dropped to two hundred feet above the valley floor. The river became a brown blur beneath him, close enough to read the white seams of the current, close enough that a hard gust would introduce him personally to the water. But down here the air was layered and broken, and each layer fought the next, and the Beaver bucked through it like a boat crossing a wake field.

Then the gap in the ridge rose up ahead, and the wind came through it.

He felt it before he saw it—a pressure on the left wing, a shove, the airplane crabbing sideways as though the sky itself had opinions about his route. The rain hit all at once, a solid gray sheet, and the visibility went from poor to theoretical. Gideon tilted his head to the clear triangle of glass. Rock ahead, streaming wet. Cloud above, chewing at the ridge crest. And the corridor between them narrowing with every second as the squall line leaned down out of the north like a door closing.

"Come on," he whispered. "Come on, old girl. Just be an airplane for five more minutes."

He banked into the gap.

The wind hit him broadside halfway through, and this was where the day nearly ended. A gust from the north, hard and sudden, picked up the left wing as if to inspect it, and the right wing dropped, and the nose swung, and for one long second the Beaver was not flying so much as being handled—passed from the grip of one fist of air to another. The stall horn, that thin electric bleat he had heard perhaps fifty times in nineteen years and always briefly, always in practice, began to sing.

And did not stop.

Gideon's hands moved the way ten thousand hours move hands, without committee, without debate. Nose forward. Full power. Wings level—*level*, the horizon a rumor somewhere inside the rain. The stall horn kept singing, higher, angrier, and the sink came, that horrible lightness, the feeling of the sky withdrawing its support like a chair pulled back at dinner, and Gideon pushed the nose down into the sink instead of fighting it, gave the mountain the respect of gravity, let the airplane fall for a second and a half—an eternity, a heartbeat, a chapter ending—in order to make the wing bite again.

The wing bit.

The stall horn shut up mid-note, offended, and the Beaver surged forward out of the sink with forty feet to spare beneath the wheels and the wet granite of the ridge sliding past close enough to count the lichen, and Gideon discovered that he had been holding his breath since the gust, and let it out in a long shaking stream that fogged the windshield completely.

For a moment he simply flew. Straight and level, blind in the rain, alive.

From the cargo floor behind him, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of Mara Voss laughing.

Not fever-laughing. Something else. Something ragged and delighted and entirely lucid, the laugh of a woman who had surfaced from deep water at precisely the moment the ship nearly sank, and who found the timing hilarious.

"That," she said, "was *appalling*."

Gideon laughed too. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him rough and cracked from disuse, and it shook something loose in his chest that had been lodged there since the gravel bar, since the strongbox, since the ledger with his father's handwriting trailing off mid-sentence twenty years ago.

"Welcome back," he said.

"Where—" A pause. The rustle of her trying to sit up and discovering what turbulence does to such ambitions. "You're flying me out. You actually—you're taking me to Rivergate."

"Somebody has to file a police report," Gideon said, "and apparently it can't be me. I'm busy."

Another laugh, weaker, dissolving into a cough. Then, quieter, with the strange clarity that fever grants like a loan it will collect later: "Gideon. Whatever Crane offers you at that strip—and he'll be there, he's always there—remember that the box is not the only copy of what we know. We are the copies. Sam and I. Keep us alive and the rest is paperwork."

He gripped the yoke and said nothing, because the truth was he hadn't thought past the landing, and because she was right, and because the ridge fell away behind him and the valley opened out ahead, long and green and going home.

* * *

Rivergate materialized out of the rain the way it always did: suddenly, and slightly disappointing, and the most beautiful thing he'd ever seen.

The strip appeared as a darker stripe in the gray, the hangar roofs as smudges, the river beyond the town running high and chocolate-brown with the same upstream rain that was, at this moment, climbing the banks of a gravel bar fifty miles north where a young man stood guard over a steel box and a promise. Gideon came in over the trees with the wind quartering across the runway at a speed Odile would later describe as "spirited," sideslipped down through the last two hundred feet of gusts, and put the Beaver on the ground with a chirp of tires and a wobble and then, blessedly, stillness.

He let the airplane roll. He let it roll longer than he needed to. There is a specific silence that lives at the end of a landing like that, and he wanted all of it.

Half-fueled, he thought, shutting down. Half-fueled, one passenger lighter, one promise outstanding. The gauge said he could go back. The sky said ask again later.

Mara was asleep again before the prop stopped—true sleep this time, the heavy kind, her face slack and younger. Gideon climbed out into the rain, waved off the line boy, and carried her himself, fifty yards to the clinic truck that Odile had evidently summoned by foresight or witchcraft, and handed her over to people whose job was exactly this, and stood in the rain watching the truck pull away until he couldn't see the taillights.

"You look terrible," Odile said, appearing at his elbow with an umbrella she did not offer him.

"I feel terrific."

"Liar. Coffee's in my office. Also, there's a man waiting for you at the hangar."

Gideon went still, rain running off his cap brim. "Describe him."

"Silver-haired. Good coat. Been asking the line boys questions about your fuel load for the last hour." Odile looked at him with the level gaze of a woman who read weather for a living and people for a hobby. "He says his name is Crane. Says he's your sponsor."

Gideon looked north, where the storm was still eating the sky, where a gravel bar was getting smaller by the hour, where Sam Okafor was holding on.

"Yeah," he said. "He would be."

He walked toward the hangar through the rain, counting his steps, arranging his face into the blank pleasant mask of a man with nothing to hide—which was, as of today, a lie he intended to get very good at telling.
