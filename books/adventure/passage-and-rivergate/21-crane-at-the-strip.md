## 21. Crane at the Strip

The rain caught up with us twenty miles out of Rivergate, which was fitting. Everything in my life had a way of arriving just late enough to be inconvenient and just early enough to be unavoidable.

Mara Voss sat in the passenger seat with her forehead against the window, watching the country unroll beneath us—black spruce, muskeg, the Rivergate itself flashing silver through the trees like a coin dropped in grass. She'd slept most of the flight, which was what fever does to you: it borrows your body and doesn't leave a note. When she woke she looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

"You flew through that," she said, nodding at the wall of cloud ahead.

"I flew around most of it."

"And the rest?"

"The rest flew through me." I checked the fuel gauge, which read half, which meant it was somewhere between a third and a lie. "Hold on. The strip at Rivergate has a personality."

"What kind?"

"Passive-aggressive."

She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile I'd seen from her, and I found I wanted more of them, which was a dangerous thing to want from a woman who'd spent two weeks guarding a strongbox with a rifle.

The strip appeared the way it always did—one moment forest, the next a brown scar with windsocks drooping like wet laundry. I came in low over the river, sideslipped to kill altitude, and put her down on the first third because the last two-thirds belonged to the puddles. The tires found the gravel with that sound I love more than any music: the sound of a decision that turned out right.

A truck was waiting at the far end of the strip.

I knew whose it was before I could read the lettering. Aldous Crane did not send trucks; he sent statements of intent with wheels. It was one of those expensive machines that had never once been asked to haul anything, gleaming even in the rain, parked at an angle that suggested its driver wanted you to know he'd been waiting exactly long enough to be annoyed.

Mara saw it too. I felt her go still beside me, the way animals go still when they smell something on the wind.

"That's him," she said. Not a question.

"That's the money," I said. "Him is probably nearby."

"Gideon." She turned in the seat, and the fever was back in her eyes but underneath it was something harder. "Whatever he asks you—"

"He's going to ask me about a box."

"Yes."

"And I'm going to tell him I don't know anything about any box."

She studied my face, looking for the trick. I let her look. There wasn't one—or rather, there was only one, and it was mine.

"Can you do that?" she asked quietly. "Lie to a man like that?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than I usually give questions. Twenty years of flying mail and medicine into places without names had taught me a great many skills, but dissembling hadn't been among them. My whole method of survival had been the opposite: be so boring, so forgettable, so thoroughly *nobody* that nobody bothered to look twice. A ghost doesn't need to lie. A ghost just needs to be transparent.

But ghosts can learn new tricks. And somewhere between the gorge rim and the gravel bar, between a ledger in my father's handwriting and a coroner's report that said *survived*, I had stopped being merely invisible and started being something else. Something with a choice in it.

"Yeah," I said. "Turns out I can."

* * *

Crane met us at the tie-downs under an umbrella held by a man I took to be his driver, though the way the man stood—weight forward, hands loose, eyes doing laps of the airfield—said bodyguard, or worse. Crane himself looked exactly as he had in his office ten days ago: silver-haired, silver-tongued, dressed for weather he clearly considered beneath him. His coat alone cost more than my annual maintenance budget.

"Miss Voss." He stepped forward with his hand extended and his voice pitched at exactly the warmth of a man who has practiced concern in a mirror. "Thank God. Thank God. We feared the worst."

Mara shook his hand the way you'd shake hands with a fish. "Did we," she said.

"Your family's been notified. There's a doctor waiting in town—" He turned the full beam of his attention on me then, and I watched him do the arithmetic behind his eyes: pilot, plane, half-fueled, alone. Where are the others? "Mr. Yarrow. Remarkable work. Truly. Triple pay was, I see now, an insult. We'll discuss a figure that reflects reality."

"Reality's fine," I said, unlatching the cargo door. "I bill by the hour."

"Of course, of course." He drifted closer, casual as fog. "And the rest of the party? Mr. Okafor? Dr. Halloway's notes? The expedition materials?"

"Sam's alive. I'm going back for him tomorrow, weather permitting."

"Tomorrow." Crane's eyebrows performed a small tragedy. "The forecast—"

"Is my problem."

He smiled at that, and it was the first honest thing either of us had said. Then his gaze went past my shoulder to the plane itself, moving along the fuselage the way a customs officer moves along a suitcase. Looking for bulges. For weight where weight shouldn't be.

I had left the strongbox at camp, buried under Sam's watchful paranoia in the floor of a tent that had already survived one flood. But Crane didn't know that. Crane knew only that a box existed, that his hired archaeologists had gone silent guarding it, and that the man standing in front of him had flown in and out of their camp carrying—who knew what?

So I gave him something to find.

"There's gear in the back," I said, hauling out Mara's duffel and setting it down harder than necessary. "Mostly ruined. Fever does that to a camp. You'll want to burn the bedding."

"Fever." Crane tasted the word. "We understood there was illness, yes. And... difficulties. The radio went dark. Mr. Halloway attempted to descend the river alone, we're told."

"We're told," I agreed pleasantly.

"By whom?"

"By the survivors. That's generally how information works."

The bodyguard shifted his weight. Crane laughed—a good laugh, well-maintained—and clapped me on the shoulder with a hand that weighed nothing and meant everything. "You have your father's manner, you know."

The rain filled the pause. I let it. Rain is excellent at filling pauses; it works cheap and never complains.

"Do I?" I said finally.

"Oh, I never met Elias Yarrow myself. Before my time in this country. But men who flew with him—they talk. Stubborn. Careful with words. Landed where other men wouldn't." Crane's eyes were pale and patient, two coins at the bottom of a well. "You must have wondered, growing up. What happened to him."

"I wondered whether it would rain enough to float the gravel bar before Thursday," I said. "Everything else is history, and history's not load-rated for my plane."

* * *

He made his move in the truck, naturally. Men like Crane don't make offers standing in the rain; they make them seated, dry, with a flask or a folder within reach. He dismissed the driver with a flick of two fingers—the man melted away toward the hangar, though I noticed he positioned himself where he could watch the plane—and poured two fingers of something amber from a flask that had never known a rough day.

"Whisky?"

"I'm working."

"It's eleven in the morning and you just landed half-fueled through a storm front. By any reasonable definition, you've stopped working until tomorrow." He set the untouched second glass on the dashboard anyway, an offering to a god he assumed would eventually accept.

I took it. Not because I wanted the whisky—I couldn't have told you what it tasted like—but because refusing small things is how you tell a man like Crane that the big things frighten you. I'd learned that watching smugglers and prospectors negotiate at bush strips my whole career. Take the drink. Keep the distance.

"To Miss Voss," Crane said, touching his glass to mine. "To the ones who come back."

"To the ones who come back."

He drank. I drank. Outside, the rain worked the gravel, and the windsock pointed northeast, toward the gorge, toward Sam, toward everything I'd buried in a tent floor and planned to dig up at dawn.

"Now." Crane set his glass down with a click, precise as a latch. "Let's talk about the box."

"There's no box."

"Mm." He said it the way a man says *mm* when he's enjoying himself. "There's no box. And yet my expedition—which I funded, which I equipped, which I insured, incidentally, making certain items of equipment technically company property—went silent for nine days guarding something. Two men dead. One drowned trying to take it downriver. And when I ask the pilot who evacuated the sole surviving senior member, he tells me there's no box." He spread his hands, admiring. "It's a remarkable story. It's almost good enough to be true."

"Almost true is my best quality."

"I imagine it is." He leaned back. "Gideon. May I call you Gideon? Thank you. Let me tell you what I think happened, and you can correct me where I'm wrong, since you're so fond of accuracy. I think my people found the old surveyor's camp. I think they found papers. Papers are dangerous things—ask any government. And I think that whatever those papers said, it concerned a man named Elias Yarrow, who walked into that gorge in 2003 and, according to every record, every search, every court filing, died there." He paused delicately. "Which matters, because a dead man's claims lapse. Land reverts. Titles clear. Whereas a man who is merely *missing*—well. Missing men cast long shadows over property law. Do you follow?"

"I follow that you're talking a lot for a man who hasn't asked a question."

That landed. I watched it land—a flicker at the corner of his mouth, the recalibration of a man who had expected a frightened bush pilot and gotten, instead, someone who negotiated back. Good. Let him spend a little fear on me. Fear spends better than money in some markets.

"Very well." He reached inside his coat—not for the pistol his driver might carry, but for an envelope, thin and cream-colored, the kind of envelope that gets hand-delivered because it can't risk a mailbox. "Two offers. Consider them a matched set."

He laid the envelope on the seat between us like a card played face-up.

"First: fifty thousand dollars, cash equivalent, wired wherever you like, for the recovery of all documents recovered from the surveyor's camp. No questions about condition. No inventory required. You hand me an envelope, I hand you a number, everyone goes home happy."

"And second?"

"Second—and I confess this is the one I care about." Crane turned in his seat to face me fully, and for a moment the charm dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was older and colder and, in its way, more honest. "Erasure. You've spent nineteen years being no one, Mr. Yarrow. I've read the file. Gideon Yarrow, age nineteen, father missing presumed dead, mother remarried, boy changes his name by deed poll, vanishes north. No debts, no record, no trail. Frankly, it's the cleanest disappearance I've ever seen outside my own industry." He smiled thinly. "I can make it permanent. New registration on the aircraft, new tax identity, the Yarrow name sealed so deep in the archives that not even I could dig it out again. You fly mail forever. Nobody ever asks. Isn't that what you wanted? Wasn't that the whole point of the last two decades?"

The rain drummed on the roof. Somewhere across the field, the windsock snapped in a gust, pointing home.

And here is the thing I discovered about myself in that truck, sitting in leather worth more than my engine: he was right. That *was* what I'd wanted. Nineteen years of measured miles instead of lived years, of being the man who lands, unloads, and leaves before anyone learns your middle name. I had built the invisibility plank by plank, and here was a rich man offering to varnish it for free.

But he'd made one error, and it was the same error everyone makes about ghosts. He thought I wanted to disappear because I was hiding from something.

I wasn't hiding anymore. I was carrying something. And there's a world of difference between a man with nothing, whom anyone can move, and a man with cargo, who knows exactly where he's going.

"No box," I said.

Crane exhaled through his nose. "You understand I'll simply send my own people upriver. The bar will hold another week, perhaps. Boats can reach it."

"Boats can't run that water in flood season, and you know it. Your own leader proved that with his life." I said it flatly, and watched him flinch—just barely, just enough. So he'd cared about Halloway, in whatever way such men care: as an investment. "You need my plane. You need my hours. Which means right now, Mr. Crane, you need me more than I need fifty thousand dollars or a clean name."

"And if I raised the number?"

"You'd be raising a number attached to nothing. I told you—there's no box. There's a wrecked camp, a dead man's kit, some rusted tools, and a lot of mud. If your archaeologists found old paper, the river's welcome to it. Paper doesn't fly well anyway. Too light. Shifts the center of gravity." I shrugged. "Ask any pilot."

For a long moment he said nothing, and I gave him the silence whole, because silence was the one commodity I'd never sold and therefore the one he couldn't price. Through the windshield I could see Mara being helped into a jeep by the doctor's people, her face turned back toward the truck, toward me, one question written plainly across it: *can you do this?*

Yeah, Mara. Turns out I can.

"Here's what I believe," Crane said at last, softly, folding the envelope away. "I believe you're lying to me. I believe it with considerable pleasure, actually, because it means you've finally become interesting. But belief isn't evidence, and evidence is the only thing I buy." He opened the truck door, letting in a slap of cold air and noise. "Fly your second run, Mr. Yarrow. Bring back Mr. Okafor. And if, in the mud, among the rusted tools, you happen to notice any paper—remember that I pay well for paper, and better for silence."

"Silence I've got," I said. "Rates available on request."

He stopped, one foot on the running board, and looked back at me with something that was almost respect, which from Aldous Crane was more dangerous than hatred.

"Your father said something like that once," he said. "According to the men who flew with him. Right before he flew into that gorge and never came out." He tapped the roof of the truck twice, a little benediction, a little threat. "Do come back, Gideon. I'd hate to own the file twice."

Then he was gone through the rain, umbrella tilting, driver materializing at his elbow, the truck pulling away toward town with its expensive tires hissing on the wet gravel.

* * *

I stood by the plane for a while after, doing the walk-around I didn't need, because my hands wanted something honest to do. Fuel caps. Tires. Control surfaces. The pitot tube, plugged with a spider the night we arrived, freed with a twig and an apology.

Half fuel. Tomorrow's run needed full, plus drums cached at the bar, plus Sam, plus—no. Stop. Weight comes later. Tonight there was fuel to buy and a weather officer to argue with and a bunk at the hotel where I intended to sleep like a man with nothing on his conscience, which was nearly true, and getting truer.

The lie had gone down smooth. That was the part that surprised me. Nineteen years of scrupulous, tedious honesty—every logbook entry exact, every customs declaration complete, every fare quoted to the cent—and it turns out the talent was there all along, sleeping, waiting for something worth protecting. I'd told Crane there was no box, and I'd told it so smoothly that for a moment I'd almost believed it myself, and that, I understood suddenly, was the entire art: not deceiving him, but choosing, for the first time in my life, what got hidden and what got carried.

A ghost hides because it has nowhere to stand. A man hides things because he has somewhere to take them.

I buttoned the cowling, patted the propeller the way you pat a good horse, and looked upriver, where the clouds were already stacking themselves into tomorrow's argument.

Sam was still on that bar. The river was rising. And somewhere in a tent floor, under a paranoid young man with a rifle, lay the truth about my father—half of it, anyway. The other half was in a cave in the gorge, in a journal I hadn't told a living soul about.

Dawn was fourteen hours away. I intended to use every one of them.
