## 22. The Second Run

The weather officer at Rivergate was a small round man named Petch, who had the temperament of a man perpetually disappointed in clouds personally.

"Absolutely not," he said, when Gideon told him where he intended to go. "Absolutely not. Do you know what's sitting over the divide right now?"

"A lot of weather," Gideon said.

"A *lot* of weather. That is a technical term, Yarrow. It means I don't like the look of it and neither should you." Petch jabbed a stubby finger at the radar screen, which showed a great bruised smear of green and yellow crawling up from the southwest like something spilled. "This front isn't going to hold its breath for you. It doesn't care about your passengers."

"It cares about my fuel bill," Gideon said. "That's practically the same thing."

Petch looked at him over the top of his glasses with the weary patience of a man who had spent thirty years telling pilots not to do things and watching them do them anyway. "You just flew out of there half-dead yourself, if the rumors are true. You nearly stalled on the ridge coming out. Everybody in town heard about it. Denny saw you land and said you were white as the underside of a fish."

"Denny exaggerates."

"Denny said your gear was down crooked."

"My gear was fine."

"And now you want to go *back*. Into that." Petch gestured at the window, where the sky over the western hills had gone the color of an old pewter spoon. "There's a boy still sitting on a gravel bar in the middle of a rising river, and you want to fly back into a front to get him, knowing full well—" He stopped. He took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes. "Well. Knowing full well everything, I expect."

Gideon said nothing. There wasn't much to say. The arithmetic of it had been done already, done on the flight out with Mara Voss shivering in the seat behind him, done again last night on the back of a fuel slip: three hundred pounds of man, forty of gear, two drums lashed in the cabin because he didn't trust the external racks in that turbulence, and every pound of it fighting the headwind home. The plane could do it. The plane could do most things once. The question was whether it could do them twice, and whether the weather would let anyone be alive at the end of the asking.

"When's the front hit the gorge?" Gideon asked.

Petch sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to history. "If it holds its current speed—and fronts never hold their current speed—you've got maybe six hours before it swallows the high country. After that, nothing goes north for two days. Maybe three."

"The bar floods in two."

"Then you've got six hours and a prayer," Petch said. "And I'll tell you something else for free, since you're clearly determined to spend money today. The river's been climbing all morning. The logging camps upstream called it in. Whatever gravel that boy is standing on, it's less gravel than it was yesterday."

Gideon had known that too. He'd done the math on the river the way he did all his math—quietly, in the dark, where it couldn't argue back. But hearing it said aloud made it heavier somehow, the way a diagnosis gets heavier when the doctor writes it down.

"I need drums filled," he said. "All four. And I need them now."

* * *

Fueling by hand was slow, ugly work, and Gideon did it himself rather than let the line boys hurry it. He pumped and sweated and checked his strainers twice, and while the third drum gurgled full he stood under the wing and watched the sky eat the horizon.

There was a particular quality to light before a storm in the interior—a greenish, underwater tint, as though the whole world had been submerged and hadn't quite noticed yet. Birds went quiet in it. Dogs went strange. Old-timers claimed you could taste metal on your tongue. Gideon had flown through enough of them to have a private taxonomy: the front that would pass around you, the front that would pass over you, and the front that intended to pass *through* you, personally, like a debt collector. This one was the third kind. He could tell by the way the wind had begun to come in gusts instead of breaths, each one carrying a different smell—rain, then dust, then cold, then rain again—as though the sky couldn't make up its mind what it wanted to do to him first.

"You could wait," said a voice behind him, and he turned to find Mara Voss sitting on a crate of drilling mud with a blanket around her shoulders and a cup of coffee she was holding mostly for warmth.

"You're supposed to be in the clinic."

"The clinic discharged me. I signed myself out. Apparently I'm 'medically fascinating' but no longer 'medically urgent.'" She sipped the coffee and grimaced at it, which meant she was feeling better; the Mara of three days ago wouldn't have had the strength to grimace. "I heard you're going back."

"News travels fast in Rivergate."

"News travels fast anywhere people are scared." She looked at the sky with the flat professional assessment of someone who had spent seasons in places where weather was not an inconvenience but a colleague with opinions. "Sam's a good kid. Stubborn. He stayed so I could go. He'll stay again if you ask him to, and that's exactly why you shouldn't ask him to."

"I'm not planning to leave him."

"No," Mara said quietly. "I don't suppose you are." She studied him for a moment with those fever-bright eyes that were, even now, missing nothing. "You know, when we hired a pilot out of Rivergate, Crane's office sent us your file. Redacted. Everything blacked out except your certifications and your rates. I used to wonder what they'd cut out."

Gideon kept his face doing nothing at all, which after ten thousand hours of sky was a skill he had refined to an art. "Probably my rates. They're embarrassing."

"They're fair," she said. "That's what was embarrassing." She stood, folded the blanket over her arm with neat, deliberate movements, and looked at him straight on. "Bring him back, pilot. And whatever else you find up there—the box, the gorge, all of it—remember that some things are only heavy if you pick them up."

"That sounds like something you say when you've picked up a lot of things."

"It's something I say," Mara said, "because I did." She touched his sleeve, briefly, almost formally, and walked toward the clinic with her spine very straight, a woman determined to convalesce through sheer force of will.

Gideon finished the fourth drum, lashed everything down, and preflighted the plane by feel more than sight, because the light was going. When he climbed into the seat, the cabin smelled of avgas and canvas and, faintly, of the medicine he'd hauled out nine days ago and the fear he'd hauled back. He sat for a moment with his hands on the yoke, not starting the engine, listening to the wind push against the tail.

His father had flown in weather like this. That was the thought that came, uninvited, the way it always did now, perching somewhere in the back of his skull like a bird that had decided the perch was its property. Elias Yarrow had flown into country no chart covered, with a compass and confidence, and the country had kept him. Twenty years of keeping. And here was his son, strapping in to do the same thing, telling himself it was different—telling himself it was *rescue*, which was a word with clean hands.

It was different, Gideon told the empty cabin. It was different because he knew the risk to the ounce and the minute, because he'd counted everything, and counting was the whole difference between courage and catastrophe.

The engine caught on the second try. He taxied out past the hangar, past Petch in the tower window shaking his head with the solemnity of a man witnessing a funeral, and lined up on the strip.

"Rivergate traffic," he said into the radio, "Cessna two-zero-seven, departing north, VFR into deteriorating conditions, returning upriver for a passenger pickup. Should be back before dark or not at all."

He paused, then added, because it felt suddenly important, "This is Yarrow. Flying as Yarrow."

Nobody answered. The strip was empty. But saying it settled something in his chest, the way a latch settles, and he pushed the throttle forward.

* * *

The first hour was the good hour, the hour pilots live for and lie about afterward. The valley ran wide and brown-gold below him, the river unspooling like wire off a spool, and the front, for all Petch's warnings, held itself politely to the west, a towering gray wall with a soft gray skirt, grumbling but not yet swinging. Gideon climbed higher than he needed to, trading altitude for options, and let the old rhythms take him: scan, trim, sip, scan. The mountains came up to meet him the way they always did, gradually and then all at once, and he threaded the pass with room to spare and dropped down the far side into the Rivergate's upper country, where the trees pressed close to the water and the river narrowed and quickened and began, in earnest, to become the thing the maps drew with little angry marks.

He talked to himself on legs like this. He always had. Not aloud, exactly—more in the register just under speech, a running commentary of numbers and cautions that had kept him company for twenty years of solo flying. *Wind quartering from the southwest, gusting maybe twenty on the nose. Fuel at three-quarters. Bar should be coming up on the left in six minutes. Sam Okafor, twenty-three years old, weighs maybe one-sixty soaking wet, and thank God he is soaking wet because that means he's alive.*

The river told him before he saw it.

It was running high—that he'd expected—but it was running *wrong*, running with a brown muscular confidence, carrying whole trees sideways, and where the valley bent at the old slide scar the water had come up out of its banks and spread into the timber like something overflowing a cup. His stomach tightened. He keyed the mic out of pure habit, remembered there was nobody to talk to, and put the nose down.

The bar was smaller.

That was the first thing. The second thing was worse. The bar was not only smaller—it was *different*, reshaped overnight, the downstream point eaten away and rebuilt into a long cruel spit of wet gravel that gleamed like a fish's belly. The place where he'd landed four days ago, the smooth upper shelf where he'd set down so gently that Sam had cheered, was gone. Not flooded—*gone*, scraped away to bare cobbles, and beyond it the current ran hard against the cut bank, chewing.

Gideon flew the length of it twice, low and slow, flaps down, reading the surface the way other men read faces. There was a usable stretch left—maybe three hundred feet of it, upstream end, firm-looking, clear of driftwood. Three hundred feet was enough. Three hundred feet was enough on a calm day with a light load and a cooperative wind, and this was none of those things.

He circled once more and saw movement near the tree line at the bar's upstream tip: a figure, standing, waving both arms overhead with the huge exaggerated strokes of someone who has been waiting a long time and has rehearsed this moment. Even from eight hundred feet, Gideon felt the relief hit him like warm water. Alive. Standing. Waving.

And then the figure stopped waving, and pointed—not at the plane, not at the sky, but downstream, urgently, both arms swinging toward the lower end of the bar, and Gideon followed the gesture and understood.

The channel between the bar and the far bank, which four days ago had been a shallow braided ribbon a man could wade, was now a single deep torrent. And the route from the bar to the gorge wall—to the cache of fuel drums they'd staged in the rocks, the extra fuel that made the weight math work, the entire plan, the whole careful architecture of numbers he'd built his return on—ran along a bench of gravel that was, even as he watched, being nibbled at the edges by the brown water.

The river had taken part of the bar. It was working on the rest.

Gideon pulled up, climbed to five hundred feet, and made himself think, because thinking was the job now and panic was just thinking with the safety off. Land now, while the light held and the strip existed. Get Sam aboard. Fly out heavy, immediately, ahead of the front, with no second chances and no margin—or land, load, and gamble that the bar and the weather would both hold for the hours it took to raft upstream for the drums, because without those drums he couldn't carry Sam and the gear and climb out of this valley at all, and without the climb-out there was no rescue, only a slower kind of drowning with wings.

Six hours, Petch had said. Two days until the bar drowned. Both clocks running, and both lying, probably.

Ahead, the first outriders of the front had reached the far ridges, ragged veils of rain hanging off the peaks like curtains in an open window. Behind him, the valley he'd come through was still open—a corridor of dirty gold light that was closing at a rate he could estimate to within minutes and chose not to.

"Gambler's descent," he said aloud, to nobody, to the plane, to whichever of his father's ghosts was riding along in the back seat. "Watch this."

He swung wide over the cut bank, checked the wind sock—one tattered orange sock on a pole Sam must have rigged, streaming nearly straight out, quartering tailwind, wonderful, *wonderful*—and set up his approach to the three hundred feet of world that remained.

The bar came up fast. The water beside it came up faster.
