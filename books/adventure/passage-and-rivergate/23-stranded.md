## 23. Stranded

The river had eaten the west end of the bar.

I saw it from two miles out and my stomach did the thing it does—not a lurch exactly, more like a quiet professional recalibration, the way an accountant's face goes still when he spots the missing zero. The gravel spit where I'd landed nine days ago had been a fat comma of stone, maybe four hundred yards long, curled against the north bank like it had been there since the Pleistocene and intended to stay. Now it was a semicolon. The river had taken the tail of it in one clean bite, and where that tail had been there was nothing but brown water moving with the kind of confident muscle that makes a man feel very small and very dry-mouthed.

"Okay," I said to the plane, because I talk to the plane, and because saying *okay* out loud is cheaper than therapy and has better fuel economy. "Okay. We work with what we've got."

What we had was the head of the bar—maybe a hundred and fifty yards of gravel, wet at the edges, glistening like something freshly born. The wind was out of the southwest at fifteen gusting twenty-five, which meant I could land into it and shorten my ground roll, but it also meant the river behind me was being pushed upstream against the outflow, piling up, getting ideas.

I flew the bar twice. First pass high, just looking: checking for driftwood, new channels, anything soft. Second pass low, throttle back, feeling the air the way you'd test ice with your heel. The surface looked honest. Gravel doesn't lie the way muskeg lies, or snow, or men. Gravel is either under you or it isn't.

Third pass, I committed.

The wheels touched at the upstream end with a sound like applause from a polite audience—*shhhk shhhk shhhk*—and I held her nose up, riding the brakes light, using every yard the wind gave me. She rolled out with forty feet to spare before the wet shine of the new waterline. I sat there for a moment with my hands doing their post-landing tremble, which they always do no matter how routine the landing, as if to say: *we are aware of what you just did, and we would like to register a complaint.*

Then I saw Sam.

He was standing at the far end of the remaining gravel, near the tree line, and he was waving both arms over his head in big sweeping arcs—the universal signal for *everything is fine* or possibly *come here immediately*, depending on context. Given that the last time I'd seen him he'd been guarding a strongbox with a rifle and the expression of a man who had recently learned the world was not organized along kind lines, I taxied toward him at a speed best described as *cautiously optimistic*.

He looked worse than when I'd left him. Thinner. A week of expedition rations and river damp had done to him what a week of expedition rations and river damp does to everybody, which is reduce them to their essential components: eyes, worry, and stubbornness. But he was upright, and upright is ninety percent of alive.

"You're back," he said, when I shut the engine down. His voice cracked on *back*, and he pretended it hadn't, and I pretended I hadn't noticed. There's a whole etiquette to this business built out of things nobody mentions.

"I said I would."

"Yeah, well." He shoved his hands in his pockets. "People say things."

"They do." I climbed down onto the gravel, and my legs reported that they remembered how. "How's Mara?"

"On the mend. Eating. Yelling at me about boiling water properly, so—" He shrugged. "So she's Mara."

"And the fuel drums?"

And here his face did something complicated. It's a young face, Sam Okafor's, twenty-four maybe twenty-five, with the kind of openness that life keeps trying to invoice him for. Right now it went through surprise, guilt, and dread in about a second and a half, like a man flipping through a wallet looking for the right card and finding none of them work.

"That's," he said. "Yeah. That's the thing."

*

Here is what the river had done while I was away playing courier to Crane and lying to his face with a smoothness that surprised even me:

It had risen four feet. Not gradually—rivers this far north don't do gradual; they do *sudden*, like drunks and weather fronts. The rain that had been falling in the high country for six days had come down the gorge all at once, and the gorge, being a gorge, had concentrated it the way a funnel concentrates everything. The water had climbed the banks, taken the low ground, and turned our neat little logistics problem into a geography problem.

The extra fuel drums—six of them, cached at the mouth of the gorge where Elias Yarrow's ledger said a surveyor's camp once stood, three miles upstream along a trail that hugged the bank—were now on the wrong side of a channel that hadn't existed last week. The flood had cut a new slough through the low ground behind the bar, a quarter mile of fast brown water, thirty yards wide at the narrowest point I could see, moving at a pace that would sweep a swimming man downstream toward the rapids and then, shortly thereafter, toward the rest of his life.

"So we go around," I said. "Upstream past the slough head, cross where it narrows."

"It doesn't narrow." Sam crouched and drew in the gravel with a stick, quick competent lines—he'd clearly been thinking about this for days, probably lying awake thinking about it, which is the worst way to think about anything. "I hiked it yesterday morning. Before the second rise. It runs all the way back to the gorge walls. There's no crossing. Believe me, I looked. I looked like a man looks for his keys when he's late."

"How deep?"

"Chest-deep in the shallows. Mid-channel it's over your head and moving like a freight train." He stood up, brushed gravel off his knees. "Gideon, I did the math. You need five drums to get us all out with margin. Even if you fly out light and come back, without those drums you can take me *or* the fuel, and Mara's already gone, so that math ends with somebody standing on this bar watching the plane shrink, and I've done that once already this month and I didn't care for it."

I looked at the slough. It looked back, brown and busy, carrying a whole confetti parade of debris—branches, foam, one entire small spruce traveling root-first with the serene confidence of a ship that knew its destination.

"Well," I said. "Good news is, I'm excellent at improvising. Bad news is, most of my improvisations involve airplanes, and I notice we're short on airplanes that float."

Sam almost smiled. Almost. "You could build a boat."

"I could build a boat," I agreed. "Do we have a boat?"

"We have," Sam said, and paused with the particular relish of a man who has been saving this, "the wreckage of a scientific expedition."

*

We spent the afternoon taking inventory, and I want to tell you that there is a specific melancholy to walking through the bones of someone else's ambition. The Halloway expedition had come upriver with grant money and good intentions and laminated topographic maps, and the river and the fever between them had reduced it to a scatter of kit along the tree line: torn tent nylon drying on branches like strange pale fruit, a broken camp table, crates of soil samples that would never be analyzed, someone's spare boots sitting neatly side by side as if their owner intended to come back for them. I tried not to look at the boots too long.

But wreckage, I'll say this for wreckage, is honest material. And a bush pilot learns early that the difference between junk and equipment is mostly a matter of urgency.

"Tarps," I said, counting. "We've got four decent tarps."

"The green ones are shot. Sun-rotted."

"Three decent tarps. Rope?"

"Two coils of good line, one that's half kinked. The expedition had climbing rope but Halloway took—" Sam stopped. Took a breath. "Halloway took some of it with him. When he tried to run the box downriver."

I let that sit. There wasn't anything useful to say to it. A man had died trying to take a secret somewhere, and here we were salvaging his tarps. That's the river for you. It doesn't care what you were carrying; it just wants the weight.

"Barrels?" I said instead, because inventory is a kindness—it gives grief a chore list.

"Two empty fifty-fives by the mess tent. One's got a split seam. Plus the water cans, the aluminum frame packs, and—" he hesitated, then grinned, and it was the first grin I'd seen off him since the fever camp, crooked and tired and real, "—one inflatable kayak, single-person, rated for Class II water."

I stared at him.

"Halloway brought a *kayak*? On a scientific expedition?"

"He believed in work-life balance."

"Did he ever use it?"

"Once. In the oxbow lake. He capsized in eighteen inches of flat water."

"Wonderful. So it's structurally sound and its previous owner couldn't handle a puddle." I walked over and looked at it—a little yellow banana of a thing, patched once at the bow with what appeared to be duct tape applied with genuine devotion. "All right. Here's my thinking. We don't run the slough—we're not idiots, whatever else we are. We build a raft, big enough for cargo, stable enough that a drum of av-gas doesn't become a depth charge. We pole it across at the widest, slowest reach, where the current's lazy. Kayak goes alongside as a safety line and a ferry for the small stuff."

Sam was nodding along, and then he stopped nodding. "Widest, slowest reach," he repeated. "That's—" He pointed upstream, toward the gray wall of the gorge, visible between the trees as a suggestion of stone and old shadow. "That's toward the cache anyway. The slough bends. If we put in up there, we ride it down and across in one trip."

"Which means putting in inside the gorge mouth."

"Which means," Sam said gently, in the voice you use to hand someone bad news wrapped in a cloth, "you'd be floating on your father's river."

I didn't answer that right away. I picked up a length of driftwood and tested its weight, and found it good, and set it on the pile with the others, and kept my eyes on the work, because the work was easier to look at than the gorge.

"It's just water," I said finally. "Water doesn't remember who it drowned."

"No," Sam agreed. "But the guy standing next to it might."

*

Mara insisted on helping.

This was against medical sense, my sense, and her own stated policy of resting, which she announced and then abandoned within the hour, the way a general announces a strategy and then does whatever wins. She came down to the beach wrapped in two blankets and a personality, gray-faced but vertical, and planted herself beside the rope coil like a foreman.

"You look terrible," I told her.

"You look like a man who's been lying to a rich person," she said. "We all have our burdens. Hand me that line."

She couldn't haul, but she could splice, and it turned out Mara Voss could splice rope better than either of us—quick fingers, sailor's knots, no wasted motion. When I asked where an archaeologist learned rigging, she said, "Fieldwork in Patagonia. You learn or you walk home," and left it there, the way she left most things, in a place where you couldn't follow without permission.

We worked until the light went long and coppery, building the raft in the eddy below the bar: eight good logs, notched and lashed into a platform, cross-braced with the tent poles, decked with the least-rotted tarp. Two empty barrels lashed underneath at the corners for lift. It was, objectively, ugly. It was, subjectively, ours. By dusk it sat in the slack water looking like something a very determined castaway would build, which is exactly what it was.

Sam stood back and surveyed it with his hands on his hips. "It floats," he said, in the tone of a man who has decided to believe in things again.

"Everything floats," I said. "The question is whether it floats with us on it or without us under it."

"Gideon," Mara said quietly. She was looking past me, upriver, at the gorge. The evening had gone still, that pre-storm stillness where the air feels held rather than absent, and the water in the slough had smoothed out enough to carry reflections—dark trees, darker stone, a sky going to ash above the rim. "Tomorrow. When you pole up into the gorge mouth. You should know what you'll see."

"I've seen the maps."

"You've seen Crane's maps." She turned and looked at me straight on, and there was no fever in her eyes anymore, only the flat clear light of someone who has decided a debt is due. "There's a cairn at the cache site. Your father built it, or his people did. And there's a mark on the wall above the waterline, chiseled—E.Y., and a date. I saw it the day we found the cache. I didn't know whose initials they were then." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands, wrapped around the blanket at her throat, went white-knuckled. "I know now."

The river ran past us, brown and busy and indifferent, hauling its confetti of branches toward the sea.

"First light, then," I said, because someone had to say something, and the alternative was standing there letting a dead man's initials be louder than the water. "We load at first light. Sam, get some sleep—you're on the bow line. Mara, you're on shore watch with the radio, and if that level comes up another foot, you signal us and we abort, no arguments, no heroics. Nobody drowns for fuel drums. I've done the arithmetic on that too, and it never balances."

They went up to the tents. I stayed on the beach a while longer, the way you do, checking lashings that were already tight, straightening a coil that was already coiled, giving my hands something to do while the rest of me caught up.

Across the slough, upstream, the gorge waited with its mouth open—a black seam in the rock where the river went in and, twenty years ago, a man named Yarrow had also gone in, and the ledger said he'd been repairing a raft when the entries stopped.

A raft. My father had been building a raft, coming home the hard way, and the river had—

Well. The river keeps what it keeps.

I looked at our raft, ugly and lashed and honest, sitting in the slack water under the last of the light, and I thought: *we finish what gets started.* Then I thought: *that's exactly the kind of sentence that gets a man killed,* and I went and checked the fuel drums one more time, and slept badly, and dreamed of water the color of tea, rising.
