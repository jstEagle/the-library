## 24. Raft of Wreckage

**GIDEON**

The river had taken the west third of the gravel bar overnight, and it had done it politely, the way a landlord raises rent—no announcement, just a new line of wet stones where dry ones had been.

Gideon stood at the water's edge with his boots on and his socks in his pocket, because wet socks were the kind of small defeat that turned into a big one three days from now. The current ran brown and muscular, carrying sticks, a section of someone's tent fly, and what looked like half a folding chair spinning past with the dignity of a man late for an appointment.

"Okay," he said to the river, the way he sometimes talked to engines. "Let's see your paperwork."

The fuel cache was upstream—four drums cached at the old surveyor's camp before the expedition had gone quiet, because Aldous Crane's logistics planning had all the foresight of a man packing for a weekend when he was funding a season. Gideon had counted on those drums. Now the gorge road was cut, a hundred meters of churning water between the bar and the trailhead on the far side, and the plane sat behind him with enough fuel to reach Rivergate exactly once, carrying exactly one person, which was arithmetic that got people killed if you did it twice.

So: raft.

He turned back toward camp. Sam Okafor was sitting on an overturned supply crate, wrapping a strip of duct tape around his palm with the concentration of a man defusing something. The kid had a rope burn across it from yesterday's swim—not the dramatic near-drowning at the rapids, that was still ahead of them, though neither of them knew it yet—just a stupid slip crossing a creek, but Sam wore the bandage like a campaign medal.

"How's the hand?" Gideon asked.

"Functional." Sam flexed it, winced, flexed it again out of spite. "Mara says I complain too much for someone who's about to be rescued."

"Mara says a lot of things from inside a sleeping bag."

"She earned the sleeping bag." There was no argument in it. Sam had carried her down from camp hollow on his back through two kilometers of rain-soft ground, and he'd have fought anyone who suggested she didn't deserve every feather-filled inch of it.

Gideon crouched by the debris pile they'd started collecting. The expedition's wreckage was, he had to admit, impressive raw material. Two aluminum frames from collapsed tents. A stack of plastic drums that had held diesel and water and, according to the stenciling, something called SAMPLE MATRIX BUFFER, which sounded like a drink served at a very bad party. Rope, good rope, most of it dry. And the crown jewel: the pontoons off the expedition's swamped Zodiac, punctured in four places but fundamentally sound, lying in the mud like two beached whales who'd given up on evolution.

"We build a raft," Gideon said. "Frame out of the tent poles, lashed. Drums underneath, sealed. Pontoons on the sides for stability. Deck of crate boards on top."

Sam looked at the pile, then at the river, then back at the pile, doing the same math Gideon had done and arriving, apparently, at the same answer, which was: this is insane, and also it is the only answer.

"You've built rafts before," Sam said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've built one raft before." Gideon picked up a tent pole and sighted down it like a rifle. "It was on a lake. The lake was calm. I was eleven. My father supervised."

"What happened?"

"It floated for almost four minutes."

Sam laughed—a short, surprised bark, like the laugh had escaped custody—and then seemed embarrassed about it, and then decided not to be, which Gideon thought showed promise. For three days the kid had been a closed door with a rifle leaning against it. Now the door was open a crack, and behind it was just a twenty-four-year-old field tech with a burned hand and a dead expedition leader and more fear than he knew how to set down.

They worked. That was the mercy of work—it gave your hands a job so your head couldn't quit. Gideon lashed crossbeams with trucker's hitches and taught Sam the hitch without making a lesson of it, just doing it slow the first time, and Sam, who learned like most young people learn now, by watching once and pretending he hadn't, had it by the third tie.

"Drums go fat side up or—" Sam started.

"Fat side down, caps up, so we can check them for water. You tap each one first. Hollow means air, dull means the river's already living in there."

Sam tapped. Dull. Tapped another. Hollow. He worked down the row with his ear against the plastic like a doctor with a stethoscope, and Gideon watched him and thought, not for the first time, that competence was the most comforting thing in the world to look at. Not confidence—competence. Confidence was what Halloway, the expedition leader, must have had in abundance, right up until he loaded a strongbox into a boat and tried to run the river alone.

"Can I ask you something?" Sam said, without looking up.

"You can ask."

"The night you landed. On the bar. I had the rifle on you." He tapped another drum. Hollow. "You put your hands up and you said, 'I'm just the mailman.' Was that true?"

Gideon considered several answers. The honest one was complicated in ways he wasn't ready to pave.

"It was true enough," he said. "I brought mail. And antibiotics. And a bill for Crane's account, which I regret deeply, because it means you people owe money to a man I'm starting not to like."

That got another laugh, quieter. Sam sat back on his heels. "Halloway trusted Crane. Right up until the end. He kept saying, 'The sponsor wants documentation, the sponsor needs the site confirmed.' Like Crane was a person instead of a wallet." He paused. "Then the box became the only thing that mattered, and I watched a grown man stop being able to hear the word *no*."

Gideon tied off a lashing and tested it with his whole weight. It held. "People hear what they're paid to hear," he said. "It's a common condition. No cure known."

They worked until the light went long and coppery, and by dark the raft existed—not finished, but real, a skeletal thing of poles and drums and two pale pontoons, sitting above the tide line of wet gravel like a creature waiting for its nerves to grow in.

**SAM**

Sam couldn't sleep, which was fine, because somebody had to keep the fire fed and listen for the river, and the river had changed its voice again around midnight—deeper, more syllables in it, like it was rehearsing something.

He fed a branch to the flames and looked over at Yarrow, asleep sitting up against a crate, arms crossed, chin down, in the posture of a man who had trained himself decades ago to sleep anywhere, anytime, in any amount of noise. Even unconscious, the guy looked like he was keeping one eye on the fuel gauge.

Sam had shot at him. Well—had stood in a doorway with a rifle pointed at him and shaking so hard the muzzle drew little circles in the air, and the man had stopped ten meters out, put his hands up, and said, in the calmest voice Sam had ever heard, "I'm just the mailman, son, and I'd take it as a kindness if you didn't shoot the mail." And then, after a pause, "Also I brought coffee. Real coffee. Think it over."

The coffee had been the thing that broke him. Not the raised hands, not the steady voice—the coffee. Because it meant the man had loaded his plane thinking about what people at a silent camp would want, and that was a kind of thinking Sam hadn't encountered much in the adult world lately.

Now the same man was going to ride a garbage raft up a flooding river tomorrow to fetch fuel drums so that Sam and Mara could go home, and he'd planned it the way he seemed to plan everything: quietly, completely, and while pretending it was no big deal.

Sam poked the fire. Here was the thing he hadn't said out loud, the thing sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone: Halloway had been the competent one. Twenty years older, thirty published papers, a voice like a documentary narrator. Sam had followed him upriver without a single doubt. And Halloway had drowned eight meters from shore with a strongbox strapped to his chest, because the box mattered more than the math, and Sam had stood on the bank doing nothing, absolutely nothing, screaming his name.

Yarrow did math out loud. That was the difference, Sam decided. He counted everything—weight, fuel, minutes, meters of rope—and he counted it where everyone could hear, as if arithmetic were a life raft you could put other people in.

Around two, Yarrow stirred, checked the fire without fully waking, added wood, and settled again. Around three, Sam finally slept.

**GIDEON**

Dawn came gray and busy. The river had risen another hand's width in the night and had opinions about their schedule.

They launched the raft at first light in slack water behind the bar's upstream point—*launched* being generous; mostly they shoved it off a rock shelf and jumped aboard while it was still deciding whether to float. It floated. Gideon felt a private, eleven-year-old triumph about this that he kept strictly off his face.

"Rules," he said, poling them clear of the eddy. "One: nobody stands. Two: everything tied down, including yourselves—we loop the rope through the frame and around your waist, loose enough to bail, tight enough to find you. Three: if we hit something and I say *left*, you paddle left like the word has a price on it."

"And if you say right?"

"That'll mean I was wrong about left."

The first kilometer was almost pleasant. The river ran wide here, braided around gravel shoals, and the raft rode the channels with a low, satisfied gurgle, drums humming under the deck boards. Sam paddled in the bow with his bandaged hand wrapped in a plastic bag and his strokes getting longer and truer by the minute. The kid was stronger than he looked and coordinated in the specific way of people who played sports requiring balance—Gideon guessed climbing, maybe ultimate frisbee, something with rules you argued about.

"Second bend, stay left of the snag," Gideon called, and Sam read the water ahead of him without being told twice—reading the V of the current, the smooth tongue of the channel, the ugly boil where something waited under the surface. By the fourth bend Gideon realized he'd stopped giving warnings, because Sam was already adjusting.

Forced partnership, he'd have called it yesterday. This morning it was starting to feel like something with less forcing in it.

The surveyor's trailhead appeared on the right bank—an old cut in the willows, a rusted cable strung between two trees, and above the waterline, blessedly intact, the cache: four blue drums under a tarp weighted with stones, plus the surveyor's original winch post, iron and mossy, standing in the bank like a grave marker for some very practical man.

Getting to it was the problem. The current pushed hard along that bank, and the only landing was a slot of calm water behind a midstream boulder, maybe three meters wide, with the full weight of the Rivergate sliding past on both sides like traffic around a parked car.

"One pass," Gideon said. "We come in hot from upstream, ferry across, and I swing us into the slot. If we miss, we don't fight it—we ride it down to the bar and try again, and we do *not* grab the cable, because that cable is attached to a tree that is attached to a lever called your arm."

"Understood," Sam said, and then, "you've done this before, right? Ferrying?"

"I've done it in a plane." Gideon shipped the pole and took up a paddle. "Same physics. Worse consequences. Ready?"

Sam grinned—actually grinned, rain and rope burn and all. "Ready."

They came around the last bend and Gideon angled the raft into the main current and let the river take them, and for about forty seconds there was no talking and no thinking, only the water reading itself through his paddle and his arms answering, the raft crabbing sideways across the brown flood, the boulder growing, the slot opening up ahead like a door held by a stranger—

"Left!" he shouted, for form's sake.

Sam was already paddling left.

The raft slid into the slot with a crunch of drum on stone and went still, rocking, both of them breathing like they'd run a mile. Up close, the boulder was house-sized and streaked with old high-water marks, and the calm water around it was the color of strong tea and smelled like the whole watershed at once—pine, mud, glacier, fish.

"Not bad," Sam said, "for a lake raft."

"It floated for almost four minutes," Gideon said. "This one's going on six."

They got lines ashore, made fast to the winch post, and hauled the raft nose-first onto the gravel. The drums were where the expedition had left them, tarped and dry, and Gideon cracked the first cap and smelled aviation fuel, clean and sweet, and let out a breath he'd been holding since Rivergate.

Four drums. Enough for the second lift and margin besides—if they got them back down to the plane. Which meant loading two hundred kilos of fuel onto a homemade raft and running the flooded gorge downstream, through the same water that had eaten Halloway and half a gravel bar and, if the ledger in Gideon's jacket was telling the truth, nearly twenty years ago, a man named Elias Yarrow.

Sam was watching him read the water downstream, where the valley narrowed and the river dropped into the first white throat of the gorge.

"It gets worse down there," Sam said. "Doesn't it."

Gideon capped the drum and smiled the smile he used on nervous passengers, which was also, he was beginning to realize, the smile he used on himself.

"Worse than what?" he said. "We haven't agreed on a baseline."

But even as he said it, he was counting—meters per second, kilograms per drum, the distance to the first rapid and the width of the channel and the number of hours before the next rain band came over the ridge. The numbers were all saying the same thing, in the patient voice of arithmetic everywhere:

Go now. Go light. Go while the river is still deciding whether to be cruel.

"Load the drums," Gideon said. "We race the weather home."
