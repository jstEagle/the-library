## 25. White Water

The raft was, by any honest engineering standard, an insult to the concept of buoyancy.

We'd built it from the expedition's wreckage: two aluminum tent poles lashed into a frame, three fuel drums lashed beneath it as floats—two full, one half-empty and sulking—and a platform of torn groundsheet stretched over the top like a bandage on a broken leg. The paddles were a shovel, a tent pole with a groundsheet wrapped around one end, and my hands.

"She looks seaworthy," Sam said, in the tone of a man trying to convince himself that a dog was friendly while it showed him its teeth.

"She looks like she'll float," I said. "Seaworthy is a word for boats. This is a rumor of a boat."

It was morning, or what passed for morning under the gorge walls—a gray light that came down the rock in stages, like it was checking each ledge before committing to the next. The river had dropped maybe six inches overnight, which was the river's way of lying to us. Upstream, past the bend we couldn't see, the rain was still working. Every hour we waited, the gravel bar behind us got smaller and the water got more opinionated.

I checked the lashings a third time. Then a fourth, because the third time I'd been thinking about something else, and a man who checks knots while thinking about his father deserves whatever happens to his knots.

"Say it again," Sam said. He was standing at the edge of the bar with the rifle slung across his back, looking younger than his twenty-four years and older than mine, both at once.

"The cache is two kilometers up the gorge wall," I said. "There's a shelf above the third rapid where Crane's advance team stashed four drums and a case of parts last spring. Mara marked it on the map. We ride up along the inside of the bend, get out above the rapid, climb, load what we can carry down, and come back."

"And if we miss the eddy?"

"Then we go through the rapid, and we hope the raft is better at it than we are."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then the drums will be very surprised to find themselves flying."

Sam laughed—one short, startled bark, like the laugh had escaped before security could stop it. He climbed aboard. The raft took his weight with a groan of protest and settled two inches deeper, which was two inches it didn't have.

I pushed off.

* * *

The first kilometer was almost kind. The current took us and the gorge walls rose around us, close enough on either side that I could have thrown a stone at either one, and the water ran fast but smooth, a long green tongue sliding between walls of black granite streaked white with old flood lines. Fifty feet up, thirty feet up, twenty—the flood marks told the story without words. In spring, this whole passage became a single moving thing, a throat. Right now it was only clearing its voice.

Sam sat forward with the shovel-paddle and did honest work with it, keeping our nose pointed down-current. He'd stopped talking about the fever, mostly. His color had come back. There was a version of this kid, I thought, who in another life ran rivers for tourists and made terrible jokes about it, and I was meeting him sideways, through catastrophe, which was how you met most people's best versions.

"Left side," he said. "Rock."

"I see it."

"You keep saying that right before we nearly hit things."

"That's called narration," I said. "It's a service I provide."

The bend came up faster than the map had promised—maps always lie about bends; they draw them as curves when they are really decisions—and the river swung hard against the left wall and the water there went from green to brown to white in the space of a breath. The third rapid. Above it, maybe eighty feet up the rock face, a pale scar in the vegetation: the slide of scree below the shelf. Our cache.

The eddy was a slack tongue of water just below a granite fin that jutted from the right bank. Miss it and we'd be committed—sucked straight into the standing waves with two hundred pounds of fuel and no way back.

"Paddle," I said, and meant it the way surgeons mean things.

Sam dug in. I put my shoulder into the other pole and we both hauled against the current, and the raft swung, sluggish as a barge, the fin coming at us—

—and the eddy caught us like a hand.

We slid sideways into stillness so sudden that my stomach kept going downstream without us. Water chuckled against the rocks. Above us the rapid roared, throwing spray eight feet in the air, and the sound filled the gorge so completely that when Sam spoke I read his lips more than heard him.

"We made it?"

"We made it." I got a line around a knob of granite and snubbed us tight. My arms were shaking. I decided not to mention that.

* * *

The climb was worse than the rapid would have been, because the climb gave me time to think.

The scree slope ran at forty-five degrees, loose rock over solid, every step a small negotiation. I went first with the coil of rope off the raft, Sam following, and we climbed the way you climb when falling is not dramatic but merely inconvenient-then-fatal: slowly, testing everything, agreeing with gravity wherever possible.

Halfway up, Sam stopped.

"Gideon." His voice had gone flat. Not scared-flat. Something else.

I looked where he was looking. Below us, wedged in a crevice ten feet down the slope, was a rusted carabiner, green with age, and beside it a wooden stake driven into the rock with a length of rotten cord still tied to it. Old work. Twenty years old, if I had to guess, and I'd spent enough of my life guessing about that particular era.

"Somebody climbed here before," Sam said.

"Somebody climbs everywhere eventually," I said, and kept climbing, because if I stood still on that slope with that stake in front of me I was going to start doing arithmetic I wasn't ready to do. Twenty years ago. A survey crew. A man with my chin and my stubbornness, driving stakes into this same rock, looking down at this same water.

The shelf, when we reached it, was everything Mara's map had promised: a flat apron of gravel tucked under an overhang, dry even in the rain, and four drums of aviation fuel sitting in a row like patient soldiers, plus a crate of parts and—God's small mercies—a coil of good rope and a canvas tarp.

We loaded what we could carry. Two drums between us, slung on poles, plus the rope and tarp. It came to maybe three hundred pounds, and my knees filed a formal complaint somewhere around the fourth switchback down.

It was on the last stretch, the easy bit, that I heard the river change.

* * *

You learn to hear water the way farmers learn to read sky. The rapid below us had been roaring steady, a held note. Now there was a stutter in it, a deep throb underneath, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Rain upstream," I said. "It's rising."

We got down to the raft and the world had already changed its mind. The eddy was smaller. The fin that had sheltered us was taking water over its shoulder in little white tongues. And the rapid itself—that held note—had become a chord, lower and bigger, the kind of sound you feel in your sternum before your ears admit to it.

"Go," Sam said. "Now, now, now—"

We shoved off into the current and the current, for the first time since I'd known this river, declined to be gentle.

The first wave hit us broadside and the raft went sideways, then vertical, then airborne for a moment that lasted approximately one full prayer. I got my weight low and forward. Sam lost the shovel-paddle—it cartwheeled away, spinning, and was gone—and grabbed the lashing line with both hands.

"LOW!" I shouted. "GET LOW AND HOLD!"

The second wave broke over us entire. Cold hit like a slap. The raft surfaced, wallowing, one corner of the groundsheet platform peeled back and flapping, and we were through the first drop and into the trough and the walls of the gorge blurred past on either side, wet black streaks, and ahead—

Ahead was the hole.

Every rapid has one: the place where the water pours over a ledge and curls back on itself, a recirculating pit that takes whatever enters and keeps it, turning it, showing it to the river again and again until the river is bored. Raft guides call them keepers. The name is not comforting.

Ours was dead center, because of course it was, because the river had looked at our line and chosen spite.

"LEFT!" I had the spare pole, driving it against the water, feeling it skate off the surface like the river was made of glass. "PADDLE LEFT, SAM, ALL YOUR WEIGHT—"

He threw himself across the portside. The raft slewed. For one beautiful half-second we were going to miss it.

Then the drum came loose.

The aft lashing—I'd checked it four times, I'd checked it four times and it let go anyway, or maybe the wave had done in one pull what my knots couldn't imagine—snapped, and the half-empty drum, our sulker, our dead weight, tore free and skipped downstream like a coin flicked off a table. The raft lurched as the weight shifted, spun a quarter turn, and the hole reached up and took us.

* * *

Falling into a keeper is not like falling into water. It's like being grabbed.

The world went white, then green, then upside down. I was underwater and then I wasn't, and the raft was above me and then beside me, and the rope I'd snubbed earlier was still coiled on the platform, thank God, thank God, and my hand found it by pure animal luck.

Sam was not so lucky.

He came up on the far side of the pit, ten feet away and recirculating—around and back, around and back, the same water handing him to himself over and over. His head went under. Came up. Went under longer. When he surfaced the second time his eyes were wide and fixed on nothing, and his mouth opened and the river filled it before any word could get out.

I've said I don't count years, I count miles. Let me tell you what that actually means. It means that in nineteen years of flying I have developed a horror of exactly one scenario: watching someone fall away from you and having nothing in your hands but distance. It happened to my father, in reverse—he fell away from everyone, into blank space on a chart, and I spent my whole life making sure I was never the one in the blank space. Never the one who couldn't reach.

And now here it was. The blank space. Ten feet of churning green, and a boy going around in it like laundry.

The rope was in my hands.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the throw—flat, low, leading him the way you lead a duck, because you can't throw a rope at a drowning man, you have to throw it at where he's going to be. The coil unrolled over the pit, hung for a heartbeat against the spray, and dropped across his chest.

"HOLD IT! WRAP IT AROUND YOUR WRIST!"

His hands found it. Whether he understood or just grabbed, I'll never know, and it doesn't matter. I braced against the frame, wrapped the line twice around my forearm, and leaned back with everything my shoulders had ever earned.

The river pulled. God, it pulled—it was as if the whole gorge had taken a personal interest, as if twenty years of water had finally been offered something worth keeping. My boots skidded on the wet platform. My arm burned. Somewhere in the roar I heard myself making a sound I'd never made before, some wordless thing from below language, and I leaned further, and the rope came—

—and Sam came with it.

He came out of the pit like a cork, coughing, clawing, and I hauled him hand over hand across the gap and got a fist in his collar and dragged him onto the platform, where he lay on his back, streaming, gulping air in great ragged sobs.

For a while neither of us did anything but breathe. The raft, freed of the drum and riding lighter, had shot out the tail of the rapid and into the calm below, and the gorge closed around us again, quiet except for the drip of water off everything, including us.

"You," Sam said finally, wheezing, "checked those knots four times."

"Fifth time's the charm."

"You counted wrong."

"I counted exactly right," I said. "That's why you're here."

He laughed, and then he did something I hadn't expected: he rolled over and put his forehead against the wet groundsheet and stayed there, shaking, and I understood it wasn't fear anymore. It was the aftermath of fear, which is its own country, and I let him stay as long as he needed, holding the pole and steering us down the long green run while the gorge walls slid past, older than grief, indifferent as arithmetic.

One drum gone. One drum left, plus whatever we'd carried down from the shelf. Enough, barely, if the math held and the weather cooperated, and neither of those things had ever once happened in my life.

* * *

It was Sam who saw it first.

"Gideon. Look. On the left—up high—is that a—"

I followed his finger. High on the left wall, maybe sixty feet above the waterline, a dark seam split the granite—a horizontal crack, shadowed, with a scatter of pale stones below it that might have fallen there or might have been placed. A cairn. Small, old, weathered to gray.

A cairn meant a marker. A marker meant somebody had wanted this spot found—or wanted it remembered.

The ledger's word came back to me the way cold water comes back: *the passage.* Elias Yarrow's handwriting, trailing off mid-sentence, twenty years unfinished.

The current carried us toward it whether I chose it or not, and I found I wasn't fighting the choice. There was a shallow gravel beach below the seam—the first landing place the gorge had offered in two kilometers, as if the river itself had been herding us here all morning.

"Fuel first," Sam said quietly, reading my face. "But after."

"After," I agreed, and steered for the shore, and the dark seam in the rock grew larger, waiting, the way a door waits—for whoever was always going to come knocking, twenty years late.
