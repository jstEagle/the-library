## 26. The Cave Mouth

The rain came in off the gorge like something with an opinion.

It had been building all morning—a gray bruise of cloud stacking up over the western ridge while Sam and I hauled the last drum along the cutbank—and when it finally broke, it broke all at once, the way mountain weather does, without negotiation or apology. One minute we were squinting at the water level and arguing about whether the raft could take another crossing; the next, the sky simply opened and the world went white with noise.

"Shelter!" I shouted, which was stupid, because there wasn't any. The gravel bar offered nothing but drum casings and driftwood and the river's slow, patient appetite for both. Sam stood there getting soaked to the bone, holding the rope like it might anchor him to the idea of dryness.

"The wall," I said, pointing upstream to where the gorge pinched in. "There's overhangs along the north face. Move."

We moved. I'd noticed the overhangs on the flight up, months ago it seemed now—dark seams in the limestone where the river had been chewing at the rock since before there were rivers with names. What I hadn't noticed, because you can't notice from two hundred feet what the river has been hiding at eye level, was the gap between the second and third seam. A black slot in the cliff face, maybe four feet wide, breathing cool air out at us like something sleeping.

Sam saw it first. "Gideon." He said it quietly, the way you say a word you're not sure you want to be true. "Is that—"

"A cave."

"It's a cave."

"Well," I said, wringing out my cap with both hands, "the ledger did say *the passage*. I'd been assuming that was metaphorical. Poets do love a metaphor."

"You don't strike me as a poetry guy."

"I contain multitudes, Sam. Get inside before the river changes its mind about us."

* * *

The slot went back farther than it looked. Twenty feet in, the daylight gave up and we were feeling our way along smooth wet stone, and forty feet in, the passage opened into a chamber tall enough to stand in, dry enough to breathe in, and—this was the part that stopped us both mid-step—not empty.

Somebody had lived here.

I want to be precise about that moment, because everything afterward depends on it. There is a difference between finding evidence and finding a life, and the chamber held a life. A fire ring built from flat stones, stacked with the particular patience of a man who expected to need many fires. A bedroll gone to mold and shadow. Fish bones swept—swept, mind you—into a tidy corner. A rack of split alder poles near the entrance, seasoned by years of dry air, laid out in lengths that meant something to a man who understood boats.

And on the far wall, above the fire ring, a shelf. And on the shelf, a tin box with a rusted lid, and beside the box, propped upright against the stone like a man waiting to be introduced:

A skeleton.

Sam made a sound low in his throat and stepped back until his shoulders found the wall. I didn't step anywhere. My legs had made their own decision about the matter, which was to stop working, and so I stood in the dark with the rain roaring behind me and looked at my father.

Or at what twenty years of patience had left of him. The bones were settled, arranged by gravity more than anything else, seated against the wall with a kind of terrible dignity, as if he'd sat down to rest and the rest had simply continued. Tatters of cloth clung here and there—canvas, the olive-drab canvas of expedition gear I knew on sight because I'd grown up folding it. On the skull's curve, a dent I refused to look at directly.

"Gideon." Sam's voice, very small. "Gideon, is that—"

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

Because I knew. That's the answer nobody warns you about. You spend twenty years telling yourself a story—that he crashed, that it was quick, that the river took him the way the river takes everything—and then you walk into a room and your body knows the truth before your mind consents to it. It's in the set of the shoulders. It's in the way the boots are placed side by side, toes toward the fire, the way he always placed them. My mother used to nag him about it. *Elias, the boots.* He'd laugh and say a pilot keeps his boots ready because the ground is a rumor.

"Elias Yarrow," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Born 1954. Flew anything with wings and several things without. Terrible at goodbyes." I swallowed. "That's him."

* * *

Here is what I did not do, and I want it on the record because Sam watched me not do it and has never once mentioned it since: I did not fall apart. Not because I'm strong—I'm not, I've built an entire flying career out of the appearance of strength—but because grief, in my experience, is a lot like weather. You can't fight it and you can't outrun it. All you can do is keep your heading and wait for it to pass through you.

So I kept my heading. I got the fire going—there was plenty of dry wood in the rack, seasoned by the dead man himself, which is a sentence I still can't say aloud—and I got Sam eating, because he was shaking and shaking people forget to eat, and then I sat down across the fire ring from my father's bones and I opened the tin box.

Inside: a field journal. Waterproof paper, bound in oilcloth, the corners soft with handling. And beneath it, wrapped in a scrap of the same canvas, a photograph I already knew—the one from the strongbox, the one Crane wanted buried. My father at the mouth of this very gorge, younger than I am now, grinning like the whole world was a joke he'd just gotten.

There was also a pencil stub worn down to less than an inch. He'd been writing until the very end. Of course he had. Elias Yarrow never met a silence he didn't try to fill with words.

I turned to the first page.

*Day 1. Crash site secured. Both drums recovered, one punctured. Am alive, which I intend to treat as a professional advantage.*

I laughed. God help me, sitting in a cave with his skeleton six feet away, I laughed out loud, and Sam looked at me like I'd gone around the bend, and maybe I had, but if you'd known my father—if you'd spent nineteen years being lectured about airspeed and attitude and the moral inferiority of complaining—you'd have heard him in that line too. Alive, which I intend to treat as a professional advantage. That was Elias Yarrow entire. That was the voice that used to come crackling through our kitchen radio every Sunday evening, and then, one Sunday in 2003, didn't.

I read. The rain hammered the gorge outside and the fire popped and Sam dozed against the wall with the rifle across his knees, and I read my father's handwriting by firelight, page after page, and the dead man came slowly, stubbornly, back to life.

He'd survived the crash with a broken wrist and cracked ribs. He'd salvaged the drums, patched the puncture with pine pitch and canvas, and spent the first week simply staying alive, which the journal treats as a series of engineering problems rather than miracles. *Fish weir functional. Water boil times established. Wrist splinted with alder and cordage; grip strength returning, though handwriting suffers, for which the reader may consider themselves forewarned.*

The handwriting did suffer. It got worse by the week, a drunken downhill slalom of letters, and somehow that was worse than anything—watching a careful man's careful hand come apart one entry at a time and knowing that behind every wobble was pain he refused to mention.

Because he did refuse. Page after page, no complaint, no despair, no self-pity. Instead: plans. Endless plans. He surveyed the gorge the way he'd have surveyed it for pay, pacing distances, noting water levels, marking the rapids by their features. He built the fish weir, then improved it. He split the alder for the raft frame and lashed it with spruce roots and wrote, with what I can only describe as smug satisfaction, *Lashings hold under load. The spruce root is superior to commercial cordage and I intend to be insufferable about this if I ever get the chance.*

"You would have been," I told him softly. "You'd have been unbearable."

Sam stirred. "What?"

"Nothing. Sleep, Sam. We've got hours of rain yet."

But Sam didn't sleep, not really. He watched me read the way you watch someone handle something breakable, and after a while he said, quietly, "Twenty years. He lasted how long?"

I checked the entries against the dates. "Nine weeks."

"Nine weeks alone. With a broken wrist." Sam shook his head slowly. "My grandfather used to say some men are built out of stubborn. I never believed him till now."

"He wasn't stubborn," I said. Then, honestly: "No. That's a lie. He was the most stubborn man in three provinces. But that's not what kept him alive." I turned pages. "Hope did. Look—" I showed Sam the margins, where small tally marks marched in neat rows. "He counted days. But look at the columns. He wasn't counting down. He was counting *up*. Like he was keeping track of how long he'd been getting ready."

"Ready for what?"

I turned to the entries from week five onward, and there it was, in worsening handwriting, growing more detailed by the day: the raft. Drawn in plan view and elevation, dimensioned like a blueprint, annotated in his cramped engineer's hand. A raft built from alder and driftwood logs, buoyed by the empty fuel drums, rigged with a steering oar and a spray deck of sewn canvas. Designed to run the gorge.

Designed to go home.

*Day 38,* one entry began. *Raft frame complete. Launched her in the eddy below camp and she sits sweet as you please. If the drums hold and the river cooperates, I believe—cautiously, provisionally, in the manner of a man who has been wrong before—that I can run this thing. The middle rapid worries me. Everything worries me. Worry is free and I am a thrifty man.*

I had to put the journal down for a minute there. I got up and fed the fire and stood with my back to Sam so he wouldn't see my face, and I thought about a nine-year-old boy sitting cross-legged in front of a radio in a kitchen in Rivergate, waiting for a voice that didn't come, learning—being taught, by absence itself—that the sky takes people and doesn't give them back. Twenty years I'd carried that lesson like ballast. And here was the man himself, sitting in a hole in the rock, counting up the days, building a boat out of garbage and stubbornness, planning his way back to that same kitchen. Back to me.

He wasn't running away. He'd never been running away. He'd been trying, for nine weeks, with broken ribs and failing light and a river full of teeth, to come home.

"Gideon?" Sam said.

"Yeah."

"You okay?"

"No," I said. "But I will be. Hand me that stick. This fire wants feeding."

* * *

The rain eased near midnight, dropping from a roar to a steady whisper, and the fire burned down to coals, and Sam finally slept for real, curled up with his back to the warm stone. I should have slept too. Tomorrow we'd need everything—arms, nerve, luck, all of it—to get the drums across the river and the plane loaded before the bar drowned. I knew that. I sat there anyway, journal on my knees, reading by coal-light, because there were only a few pages left and I had waited twenty years for them without knowing it.

The last entries were short. The weakness was in them now, plain as a stall warning. Week eight. Week nine. The raft finished, dragged to the water, tied down. The final preparations listed and checked off, item by item, in a hand that barely resembled handwriting anymore.

And then the last page. Just a few lines, the letters large and loose, written by a man lying down, probably, using the last of something.

I read it once in silence. Then, because some words aren't meant to be read alone, I read it again—out loud this time, quietly, to the dark at the back of the cave, to the bones by the cold hearth, to the boy I'd been and the man I'd become, in a voice that only shook a little:

*"To whoever finds this. If it's a stranger, tell the story however you like, but tell it true: I did not die in the crash. I died building a boat. If it's Gideon—"*

My voice quit on me there. I sat with the page until the shaking stopped, and then I read the rest.

*"—if it's Gideon, then you found me, son, which means you flew farther than I ever did. Tell your mother the boots were by the fire. Tell her I was coming home. I was coming home the whole time."*

The coals ticked. The river ran on in the dark, high and hungry and ordinary, and somewhere above the rain clouds the stars were doing what stars do, indifferent and punctual.

Sam was awake. I don't know how long he'd been listening. He didn't say anything, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in twenty years.

I closed the journal and held it a moment, weighing it—not the ounces of it, though I weigh everything, it's a disease—but the other weight, the kind you can't tie down in a cargo bay. Because tomorrow morning, when the rain stopped and the light came, we'd load the drums and the raft timbers and ourselves onto a plane that had already told me, in the blunt arithmetic of weight and balance, exactly how much it was willing to carry.

And I had a feeling—one of those feelings a pilot learns to trust, the ones that arrive uninvited and turn out to be load calculations—that the journal and the drums and two living men were not all going to fit.

I looked at my father's boots, side by side by the fire, ready for a departure that took twenty years to board.

"Hold on, old man," I said. "I'm working on the weight and balance."
