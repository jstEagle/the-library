## 27. Last Entry

The rain kept its promise. It came down on the gorge the way it had been threatening to come down for two days—not in sheets but in a long, patient gray curtain, as if the sky had decided to wash this one place off the map and was taking its time about it.

Inside the passage, it didn't matter. The cave mouth faced south, and the overhang threw the weather back like an old man refusing a bill. Gideon had built a small fire near the entrance—small because fuel was fuel, and every stick he burned here was a stick Sam wouldn't have to haul—but small fires still gave light, and light was what he needed.

The journal lay open on his knees.

He'd been reading it since the fire caught. Not quickly. Some men would have torn through it in twenty minutes, hungry for the ending, and Gideon understood that hunger better than most—he'd been hungry for this ending since he was nineteen years old, sitting at a radio that never spoke. But you didn't rush a thing like this. You read it the way his father had apparently written it: slowly, in bad light, with cold hands, saying only what needed saying and not one word more.

Elias Yarrow's handwriting was exactly as Gideon remembered it from supply lists and birthday cards and the one postcard that had arrived from Fort Nelson two weeks after the plane went missing. Slanted right, impatient, the letters of a man who thought faster than his pen could travel. *Gid,* the postcard had said. *Rivers up here all run somewhere. Will explain when home.* That was the whole message. Eleven words, and Gideon had carried them for twenty years like a stone in his boot.

Now there were more words. Pages and pages of them, written in pencil that had faded to the color of old bone, and Gideon read them aloud—quietly at first, then not caring whether Sam heard, because some things were too heavy to carry silently.

*Day nine,* his father had written. *Walked out to the wreck again. She's done. No fixing her, no pretending otherwise. The tail's in the water and the water is in everything else. Salvaged the axe, the tarp, half the tool roll. A man could do worse than be stranded with half a tool roll.*

Gideon laughed. It came out of him sideways, surprising them both—a short, cracked sound that echoed off the cave walls and made Sam look up from where he sat sharpening the machete against a river stone.

"You okay?" Sam asked.

"No," Gideon said. "Keep going."

Sam went back to the machete. Gideon went back to the journal.

*Day eleven. Found the overhang. Dry floor, good sightline down the gorge, and a spring trickling out of the rock about two hundred yards east. Whoever named this place 'the passage' had a poet's heart and a surveyor's eyes. I'd have liked to buy him a drink.*

*Day fourteen. Leg is worse. Not the break—the break I can live with. It's the fever that comes and goes. When it goes, I think clearly enough to make plans. When it comes, I talk to your mother. Don't tell her I said that.*

Gideon stopped. His thumb rested on the edge of the page, and for a moment the rain was the only sound in the world.

"She know?" Sam said softly. "Your mother—that he talked to her?"

"I don't know," Gideon said. "I'm going to find out."

He read on.

*Day nineteen. Built the raft frame today. Driftwood and the aircraft's wing strut lashed with wire. It's ugly. It's beautiful. It floats—I tested it in the shallows and it floated like a drunk swan, but it floated. If the leg holds and the river drops, I'll run the lower chute and follow the water west. There are settlements on the lower Rivergate. There have to be.*

*Day twenty-two. Fever again. Bad one. Wrote letters I won't send because there's nobody to carry them. Here's the strange part, Gid—I wasn't afraid. Isn't that something? Twenty years of flying into places maps forgot, and the thing I was scared of was never the dying. It was the leaving wrong. It was you thinking I chose the sky over you.*

Gideon's voice failed on that line. He cleared his throat, which did nothing, and read it again anyway, because his father deserved to be heard twice.

*You thinking I chose the sky over you.*

"That's not—" Sam started, and stopped himself. He was twenty-four and wise enough, sometimes, to leave silence where silence belonged.

Gideon turned the page. The last entries were shorter. The pencil pressed lighter, as if the hand behind it was spending itself like a candle.

*Day thirty-one. River's dropping. Raft holds. Tomorrow, maybe. Or the day after. A man shouldn't rush a river any more than he should rush a goodbye.*

*Day thirty-three. Leg's infected through. I know what that means. I've known since day twenty. I've been writing this journal partly for the survey record and partly because paper listens better than rock walls do.*

Then, on the final page, the writing changed. It steadied. Whatever weakness had been creeping into the letters, his father had gathered it up and set it aside, the way he used to gather up his tools at the end of a job and wipe each one before putting it away. Gideon knew that discipline. He'd inherited it without knowing where it came from.

*To whoever finds this—and someone will, because I've left the cairn marked and surveyors are dogs for a marked cairn—*

*My name is Elias Yarrow. I flew out of Rivergate. I had a wife, Ruth, who deserved better than she got, and a son, Gideon, who was fourteen years old the last time I saw him and who I'd bet my life—which I suppose I have—grew up stubborn as me.*

*Tell him I wasn't running away.*

*Everyone will say I was. They said it before I left. A man takes a contract upriver after what happened with the land, and people talk. Let them talk. But tell him the truth if you can find it in yourself: I was coming home. Every mile of this was pointed home. I fixed the raft. I ran the numbers. I was going to come down that river and walk into Rivergate and stand in our kitchen and say the things a man should say, and the fever took the schedule out of my hands. That's all. That's the whole story. Not a man who fled. A man who was late.*

*Tell him the compass was always pointing home. He'll understand. He used to steal it off my dashboard when he was six and hold it up to the window and ask me why it always knew. I told him: because it doesn't decide where it wants to go, son. It just remembers.*

*I remember.*

*E.Y.*

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the river said what rivers say, which is everything and nothing.

Gideon sat with the journal open on his knees for a long time. Long enough that the pencil lines began to swim. Long enough that Sam quietly got up, added a stick to the fire without being asked, and sat back down facing the rain, giving a man the privacy of company.

"He stole the compass," Gideon finally said. His voice was rough, but it worked. "I did. I was six. I held it up to the window of the Cessna and asked him how it always knew which way was north." He shook his head slowly. "I'd forgotten that. Twenty years, and I'd forgotten it, and he wrote it down in a cave with a ruined leg so I wouldn't have to keep forgetting."

"Parents do that," Sam said. "Keep receipts."

Gideon huffed—half laugh, half something else. "Is that what you call it?"

"My mum keeps every letter I've ever sent her. In a shoebox. Labeled by year." Sam shrugged, embarrassed and unembarrassed at once. "I used to think it was sentimental. Then I spent three weeks watching Professor Halloway die of a fever in a tent, and I thought—no. That's not sentiment. That's a person making sure somebody knows they existed."

Gideon looked at the boy—at the young man—who had guarded a strongbox with a rifle and a fever of his own, who had watched his expedition leader drown, who had every reason to be hollowed out and instead was sitting here talking about shoeboxes. "She'd like you," Gideon said. "My mother. She collects people the way your mum collects letters."

"I'd like that," Sam said simply. Then, because he was Sam: "What happens now? With the journal, I mean. Do we—it's evidence, isn't it? Of what Crane did. Of what they were trying to bury."

"It's evidence," Gideon agreed.

"But?"

Gideon closed the journal gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping room. "But it's also the only conversation I'm ever going to have with my father," he said. "And I haven't finished having it."

Sam nodded slowly. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the overhang stone, and far below them the Rivergate climbed toward its flood with the patience of something that had drowned better men than either of them.

"We should check the water level," Sam said.

"In a minute."

"Yeah," Sam said. "In a minute."

They sat while the fire burned and the rain fell, and Gideon held the journal against his chest like cargo he hadn't yet decided how to load.

It was Sam who broke first, standing and peering out past the overhang at the gray wall of weather. "Gideon," he said, and there was something new in his voice—not fear exactly, but its cousin. "The river. Look at the river."

Gideon looked. Down in the gorge, where yesterday morning there had been wide gravel bars and a braided, shallow channel, the Rivergate now ran brown and single-minded, already licking at the base of the rocks they'd scrambled over to reach the cave. The flood wasn't coming.

It had started.
