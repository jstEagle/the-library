## 28. What to Carry

The rain had settled into a rhythm by the time we got back to the cave mouth—a steady, businesslike drumming on the overhang, like a landlord who'd stopped knocking and started letting himself in. Sam sat with his back against the rock, the journal open on his knees, reading it for what I figured was the third time. His lips moved when he read. I'd noticed that at camp. Nineteen years old, satellite uplink certified, drone-licensed, and he still read like a boy sounding out his first words.

"You could publish this," he said, without looking up.

I was counting fuel drums. It's what my hands wanted to do while my head did other work. Four intact, one dented but sealable, one crushed flat as a beer can under a slab of limestone that had come down sometime in the last twenty years—maybe while my father was alive in here, maybe after. I tried not to think about which.

"Sam."

"I'm serious." He looked up then, and there was something lit behind his eyes that I recognized from mirrors—the look of a man who has found an argument and is going to live inside it until someone drags him out. "Crane funded this whole thing to bury Elias Yarrow. To make sure nobody ever found proof he survived the crash, because if he did—if he lived weeks down here, building a raft, trying to get home—then Crane's claim on the land is built on sand. A coroner's report that says one thing and a field journal that says another? That's fraud. That's *fraud*, Gideon. We put it in front of a journalist and Crane doesn't just lose the land claim. He loses everything. The foundation, the board seats, probably his freedom."

"Probably," I agreed.

"So we take it out. All of it. Journal, photographs, the survey map—"

"Sam." I sat down across from him on the flat stone where my father had apparently eaten, slept, and written his way through the last weeks of his life. "How long have you been sitting here?"

"Since you went to check the drums. Twenty minutes, maybe."

"And how many times have you thought about giving it to a journalist?"

He had the grace to flush. "It's not about me. It's about justice."

"It's about a dead man who can't say no."

That landed. I watched it land—the little flinch, the way his thumb pressed flat against the page to hold it still, like the journal might get up and walk off without permission. Outside, the river spoke in its new voice, deeper than yesterday, the voice of a river that had started eating the gravel bar and wasn't planning to stop.

"Look," I said, gentler. "I've spent twenty years being a story somebody else told about me. The Yarrow boy whose father ran off. The kid at the radio every Sunday waiting for a call that never came. You know what I learned? That a story can be true and still be a cage. What your version does—it takes everything Elias wrote in here, every private sentence—" I tapped the journal, careful, the way you touch something that might be sleeping. "And it turns it into evidence. Exhibit A. Strangers reading his handwriting in a courtroom, journalists calling it 'the gorge diary,' some professor writing a paper about the psychological state of a man building a raft alone in the dark. He didn't write it for them. He wrote it for me. Or for whoever came. But not for *them*."

"Crane deserves—"

"Oh, Crane deserves plenty. And Mara and Sam Okafor's sworn testimony about what he paid your expedition leader to do—that'll do damage enough. Testimony is yours to give. You were wronged; tell the truth about it. But this—" I lifted the journal from his hands, and he let it go, which I took as a good sign. "This isn't ammunition. This is my father's voice. And I'm not handing his voice to people who'll use it as a club."

Sam was quiet a long moment. The rain filled the silence for him.

"He'd want justice, though," he said finally. "Wouldn't he? Your dad. A man left behind like that."

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved an honest answer and because I'd been turning the question over since dawn. My father, as far as I knew him—which turned out to be almost nothing—had been a man of ledgers and compasses, of entries made neat and final. A man who signed things. I tried to imagine him wanting his last weeks paraded through a courtroom so that Aldous Crane's stock portfolio would take a hit.

"No," I said. "I think he'd want to go home. That's all he wanted. Read the last entry again."

Sam did. I watched his face do what mine had done at three in the morning: the slow collapse of the argument, the arrival somewhere softer.

"*Tell my son I wasn't running away,*" he read quietly. "*I was coming home.*"

"Yeah. So we carry him home. Not the scandal. Him."

Sam nodded slowly, and then—because he was nineteen and honest in a way that hurt to watch—he said, "What about you? Don't you want Crane to pay?"

"Sure," I said. "But I've learned the difference between wanting a thing and needing to be the one who carries it. You want to know a secret about flying? Half the skill is knowing what weight to leave on the ground. Every pound you don't load is altitude you keep. Same rule applies." I stood, brushed grit off my knees. "Come on. We've got daylight math to do and the river isn't getting any politer."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon in the gray light, hauling drums down to the gravel bar two at a time on the improvised litter we'd lashed together from tent poles and pack straps. The bar had shrunk overnight—I paced it off and came up forty feet less than my landing run needed, which meant tomorrow's takeoff would be a negotiation rather than a certainty. I didn't say this out loud. There's a pilot's superstition that says the aircraft can hear you, and I figure the river can too.

The plane sat where I'd left her, nose into the wind, looking smaller than she had two days ago. Rain beaded on the fabric and ran off in threads. She'd taken water in the cowl during the storm and I'd spent an hour that morning drying the magneto with my shirttail and a prayer I didn't believe in, and she'd fired on the third blade, which I chose to interpret as forgiveness.

"Four drums," Sam said, arriving with the litter. "Plus us, plus gear. Is that—"

"It's close." I wiped rain off the strut and checked the tie-down, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "Drums are fifty-five pounds each when full. Two-twenty total. Me, you, survival gear, the raft frame if I can't bear to leave it—call it four-fifty all in. She'll fly. She won't fly *well*. Climb-out will be ugly and the ridge will feel closer than it is."

"And the journal? The photographs? The map?"

"In the map case, against my chest. Lightest thing aboard and the heaviest thing aboard, both at once." I smiled to show it was a joke, and Sam smiled back, and neither of us quite meant it.

He stood there a moment, working up to something. I let him. With young men it's better to let the sentence arrive on its own schedule; hurry it and it comes out wrong.

"Gideon," he said. "Back at camp. When I had the rifle on you. Why didn't you just... take it from me? You could have. You're twice my size and I was shaking so hard I couldn't have hit a barn."

"Didn't need the rifle," I said. "Needed you to trust me. Those are different problems."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I've got." I cinched the tie-down and looked at him straight. "You were guarding the box because guarding it was the only job left that felt like it mattered. Everybody else was sick or gone or drowned. Somebody had to stand between the box and the world. I wasn't going to take the one thing keeping you upright."

He swallowed. Nodded. Then, quieter: "Mara said you'd understand. She said, 'the pilot will understand, they always do.' I didn't know what she meant."

"She meant I've spent a lot of years standing guard over something that wasn't really mine to guard." I patted the wing. "Come on. Last trip before dark."

* * *

The last thing we carried up from the cave was my father.

Not all of him—we couldn't, and I'd decided in the night that we shouldn't try. The cave was a grave, and graves are not cargo. But the journal came out, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked against my chest where it rode warm as a second heartbeat. And the photographs. And the survey map with its penciled route downriver, annotated in that hand I'd spent twenty years forgetting and forty-eight hours relearning.

At the cairn, I stopped.

Sam had gone ahead with the last drum, and for a minute it was just me and the stones and the rain coming soft through the trees. The cairn was bigger than I remembered from the rim trail—somebody had been adding to it. My father, most likely, in his last weeks, stone by stone, the way a man talks to himself when there's no one to talk to.

I set my hand on the top stone. Cold, wet, ordinary. The kind of stone you'd kick aside on any trail in the interior and never think about again.

"I found you," I said. Out loud. Because there was nobody left to say it to, and because some sentences need air or they rot. "Twenty years late, but I found you. And I'm taking you home the only way I can."

The river answered in its deep new voice. I chose to take it as agreement.

Then I did the arithmetic one more time, standing there, because arithmetic is the prayer I actually believe in. Four drums. Two men. One journal. One compass—my father's brass compass, which I'd carried since the cave, turning it over and over like a worry stone, its needle swinging lazy circles whenever I held it still.

The needle pointed north-northwest. Toward Rivergate. Toward home, if you believed a piece of brass could want anything.

I weighed it in my palm. Three ounces. Nothing. Less than nothing—three ounces wouldn't change the climb-out, wouldn't matter to the ridge, wouldn't tip anything but me.

But that's the thing about weight. The scale doesn't measure the kind that matters.

I knelt and set the compass on the top stone of the cairn, nestling it into the gap where the rain had worn a little seat for it, needle pointing the way I'd be pointing soon. Let it stay here with him. Let it do its job the way he never got to—pointing at home from a place he couldn't leave. Some things you carry out. Some things you leave so the leaving means something.

"Keep it," I told him. "You always knew the way. That was never the problem."

I stood up and walked down to the bar without looking back, which cost more than the takeoff would.

* * *

Sam was waiting by the plane, soaked through, grinning the grin of a man who has loaded his last drum and discovered he's still alive. "Fuel, not vengeance," he said. "That's what you called it. I've been thinking about it. It's a good line."

"It's not a line. It's a loading plan."

"Same thing, out here." He hesitated, then: "For what it's worth—I'm glad it's you that came. Even with the rifle."

"For what it's worth, your grouping was terrible."

He laughed, and the laugh cracked something loose in both of us, and for a moment we were just two wet men laughing beside a small airplane while a river rose around us, and it was, absurdly, the best moment I'd had in twenty years.

Then I heard it—under the rain, under the river. A different sound. Thin, high, mechanical, wrong.

An engine. More than one.

Sam heard it too. His grin died. "Is that—"

"Aircraft." I tilted my head, tracking it. Coming from the south. From Rivergate. Flying weather no sane pilot would fly, in formation, deliberate, searching.

I knew, the way you know a voice on the radio before the call sign finishes, exactly whose money was keeping those engines in the air.

"Get in the plane," I said.
