## 29. Overload

The rain had settled into that patient, businesslike rhythm it kept up in the gorge—no drama to it, just a steady gray insistence, like a landlord who'd decided you were going to pay whether you liked it or not. It drummed on the cave mouth and ran in bright threads down the rock face, and somewhere below us the river climbed another inch.

I sat cross-legged on the cave floor with everything we intended to carry out of this place arranged in front of me like evidence at a trial. Which, in a way, it was.

One fuel drum, half full. The journal, wrapped in Sam's spare shirt inside a dry bag inside my flight jacket. Two men. One compass.

"Okay," I said. "Arithmetic time."

Sam looked up from where he was re-lashing the drum's cap with a strip of inner tube. He'd gotten good at that overnight—his knots had gone from enthusiastic to actually functional, which I told him was more than most pilots managed in a lifetime. He didn't laugh. He hadn't laughed much since the raft run, since the moment my rope had caught his wrist two feet above the white water and hauled him back into the world. I think part of him was still down there, looking up.

"Lay it out for me," he said.

I laid it out. I'd been doing the numbers in my head since dawn, but there's something about saying them out loud that makes them real, the same way writing a name on a manifest makes cargo exist.

"The Otter's useful load is about twenty-eight hundred pounds," I said. "Me, two hundred and ten soaking wet—and brother, I am soaking wet. You, call it one-sixty. Drum, eighty gallons of gas, call it five hundred pounds with the container. Fuel already in the tanks for the climb-out and the run home over the ridge: six hundred pounds. Survival gear, rope, the tools we're taking, forty pounds if we're honest, sixty if we're not."

Sam was nodding along, doing his own math, and I watched him arrive at the number before I said it.

"That leaves what—thirteen hundred pounds for everything else?"

"Thirteen hundred," I agreed. "And we've got the journal, which weighs nothing. And we've got the second drum."

The second drum was the problem. We'd found the cache upstream where the expedition had staged it, three drums lashed under a tarp against the rock, and the river had taken one of them sometime in the night—we'd found it wedged in the willows downstream, punctured like a tin can under a boot heel, its contents long gone into the Rivergate. That left two. One to burn getting off the gravel bar and home. One as margin, because flying the gorge in rising weather with zero margin is how you end up as a ledger entry yourself.

"We could carry both drums," Sam said. "Dump some gear. Leave the tent fly, leave the rope—"

"And when we need the rope on the climb-out because the bar's shorter than yesterday?" I shook my head. "No. Here's the truth of it, Sam. We can carry one drum and both of us, or we can carry both drums and one of us stays behind for a run that may never come. Those are the options. There isn't a third one hiding under a rock."

He was quiet for a while. The rain filled the quiet for him.

"You'd stay," he said finally. Not a question. "Yesterday, on the raft—you were going to stay. Before I said Mara goes first. You'd already decided it was you."

"I did the math," I said.

"No, you didn't." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "You did the math after. I watched you do it. First you just looked at me and Mara and made a face like a man reading his own obituary, and *then* you started talking about useful loads."

I wanted to argue with that. I searched around for an argument and found that he was right, which has a way of ending arguments before they start.

"It runs in the family," I said instead, and immediately wished I hadn't, because Sam knew exactly what ran in the family. He'd read enough of the journal by firelight last night. He knew about Elias Yarrow, who had spent his final weeks not escaping the gorge but repairing a raft to come *home* through it, and who had died within sight, probably, of the river that would have carried him back to a son waiting at a radio.

Sam didn't push it. That's one of the things I'd come to respect about him—he had a young man's courage and an old man's discretion, a combination I'd rarely seen and never managed myself.

"So we take one drum," he said. "Both of us go. Fine. Then what's left to cut? Because I've seen your face when you look at that takeoff run, and it's shorter every hour."

I looked at our little pile again. Drum, journal, gear, tools. And sitting apart from all of it, because I hadn't been able to make myself put it in the pile, the compass.

It was a brass thing, heavy for its size, with a lid that opened on a hinge worn smooth by thirty years of thumbs. My father's. I'd found it in the surveyor's camp up at the rim, two days and a lifetime ago, sitting in the rusted mess kit where a careful man keeps small valuables, and I had known whose it was before I turned it over and saw the initials scratched into the case. E.Y. He'd carried it through the war, according to my mother, who used to tell the story like a joke—that he trusted the compass more than the map, more than the radio, more than any person, and that she'd once asked him what he'd do if the needle ever lied to him, and he'd said, *then I'll know I'm lost, and knowing is most of the way to found.*

I'd carried it in my breast pocket since the rim. I don't know what I thought I was doing with it. Taking it home, maybe. Returning it. As if objects could be returned to the dead, as if there were some lost-and-found window in the ground where you could hand things back and say, *this was his, he'll want it.*

"Gideon." Sam's voice pulled me back. "The pile. What goes?"

* * *

We went through it item by item, the way you do, and it was grim comedy for about ten minutes. The tent fly went—sleeping wet builds character, I told Sam, and he informed me that I was the only person he'd ever met who could make deprivation sound like a sales pitch. The rope stayed, because I'd seen what the river does to men without rope. The tools stayed, minus the pick, which Sam mourned briefly and then admitted he had no idea why archaeologists needed a pick that heavy anyway. The first-aid kit stayed, lightened of everything but the essentials. Mara's field notes—the ones she'd entrusted to us, the legitimate record of the legitimate work—went in, weighing almost nothing and mattering more than anything else in the pile except the journal.

That still left us ninety pounds over. Maybe a hundred. On a hard surface, in calm air, I might have talked myself into ninety pounds. But the bar was soft gravel going to mud, the wind was coming down the gorge in gusts that arrived without appointment, and the trees at the end of the run had been standing there for two hundred years waiting patiently for someone overconfident.

"Ninety pounds," Sam said. "What weighs ninety pounds?"

"You," I said. "Roughly."

"Ha."

"The drum's fixed. The journal's fixed. Gear's already down to bones." I stared at the pile, and my eye went where it had been going all morning, sliding past it and then forcing itself back. "There's one thing that doesn't weigh ninety pounds. It weighs about fourteen ounces."

Sam followed my gaze to the compass. He was quiet a moment.

"That's yours," he said carefully. "I mean—it's your father's. That's not the same thing."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Here's what I'd figured out during the night, lying awake listening to the rain and the river having their long conversation. The compass wasn't cargo. Cargo is a thing you carry from one place to another because someone needs it there. The compass was a question I'd been carrying for twenty years, and questions don't get lighter with altitude—they get heavier, because up there you've got nothing to do but hold them.

My father had come back to this country to finish something, and the journal said so in his own cramped hand: *coming home.* He hadn't made it. But he'd also, in the end, stopped carrying things. The cave held what mattered—his words, his proof, his message to me—and the rest of it, the tools and the tins and the brass and the needle, he'd left for the mountain to keep. A man who knows where he's going travels light.

I picked up the compass. It was cold, the way brass gets in damp air, and the lid opened with that soft worn-hinge click I already knew by heart. The needle swung, wobbled, settled north, faithful as ever. Not lying. Pointing the way it had always pointed, through the war and the marriage and the long silence and the crash and the weeks alone in this gorge with a broken raft and a stubborn heart.

North. Toward home, if home is a direction. Toward the cairn, if home is a place.

"I'll be back in a minute," I said.

Sam started to get up. "I can—"

"Stay with the drum."

* * *

The cairn stood on the bench above the cave mouth, where the old surveyor's crew had raised it twenty years ago and where I'd added a stone myself the day I found the ledger, because that's what you do; it's the oldest courtesy there is. The rain had slackened to a fine mist, and the gorge below was running high and brown and loud, and beyond it the ridges went off into cloud like pages torn out of a book.

I stood at the cairn with the compass in my hand longer than I meant to. Long enough to get properly rained on. Long enough to have the conversation I'd been avoiding, the one where I explained to a dead man—or to myself, which amounts to the same thing at certain altitudes—what I was doing and why.

Because here was the thing. I'd spent my whole adult life counting miles instead of years, keeping the world at the length of a runway, being nobody from nowhere landing at strips where nobody knew my name. I'd called it a philosophy. Chapter one of the gospel of Gideon Yarrow, ghost. But standing there, I understood it was simpler and stupider than that. I'd been waiting. All those measured miles, all those careful landings—I'd been flying a holding pattern over my own life, waiting for word from a man who couldn't send any, keeping myself light and empty and ready in case he came back and needed a son.

He wasn't coming back. He'd never been coming anywhere except home, and the river had closed that account. What he'd left me wasn't a debt. It was a message, and messages are meant to be delivered, not carried forever.

You keep the cargo that someone's waiting for. You set down the rest.

I crouched and set the compass on the flat capstone of the cairn, needle pointing north, lid open so the morning—if the morning ever came—would find it working. Fourteen ounces. It felt like four hundred pounds leaving my chest.

"There," I said out loud, to the cairn, to the gorge, to whoever keeps the books on such transactions. "That's yours. I know where I'm going."

The needle didn't move. Of course it didn't. But I swear the mist thinned for about ten seconds, long enough to show me the far ridge, sharp and green and ordinary, before it closed again. I'm not going to build a religion out of ten seconds of weather. But I noticed it. I'm a pilot. Noticing is the job.

When I got back down to the cave, Sam had the drum rigged into a sling and the dry bag squared away, and he looked at my face and then at my empty breast pocket and didn't say anything, which was the correct amount of things to say.

"Heavier or lighter?" he asked instead.

"Lighter." I shrugged the pack on and checked its straps twice, because ritual matters even when the arithmetic is done. "Come on. Let's go see how much river we have left to hate."

* * *

The gravel bar had opinions about us.

We came down out of the rocks onto what remained of it—a brown tongue of stones maybe four hundred feet long now where it had been six hundred yesterday, the upstream end gone entirely, the water sliding over where the cache tarp had been with a smooth, indifferent muscle. The plane sat where I'd left her, tied down to the drum chocks and two deadfall trunks, looking small and patient and slightly offended, the way airplanes do when you leave them out in weather.

I walked her tires, checked the tie-downs, drained a spoonful of water from the fuel strainer and frowned at it professionally. Sam loaded the drum while I preflighted, and neither of us talked much, because there's a kind of work that goes better silent, and loading a floatplane on a shrinking island in a flooding river is that kind of work.

By the time everything was aboard—drum secured, bags weighed and placed, Sam belted into the seat Mara had ridden out in—I stood on the gravel and did the last calculation, the one that mattered, the one I'd been circling for two days.

Full tanks to the tabs. Half drum. Two men. Journal, notes, tools, rope. Wind quartering down the gorge at fifteen, gusting twenty-five, which meant a headwind component on the takeoff run, which meant—my mind offered helpfully—more runway needed, except there was less runway, and the water at the far end was moving fast enough to carry sticks.

"Talk to me," Sam said from inside. "Give me a number. How bad?"

"Bad in the way that's interesting," I said, swinging up into the left seat. "Interesting is fine. Boring would worry me."

"That's the most deranged sentence anyone's ever said to me."

"Welcome to aviation." I ran the checklist, touch by touch, every switch and gauge an old friend, and my hand passed over my breast pocket out of pure habit and found nothing there, and I let it find nothing, and the engine caught on the second blade and settled into its rough familiar hum like a big dog waking up.

I taxied to the upstream end of the bar, because every foot counted and I wanted all of them pointed the right way. Through the windshield the gorge narrowed ahead into its white throat, and the clouds sat on the ridgeline at about eight hundred feet, maybe lower, and the rain came slantwise across the water in silver sheets.

"One pass," I told Sam. "If she's not flying by the willows, I put her down in the shallows and we try again after lunch."

"After lunch," Sam repeated faintly. "Sure. Lunch."

I held the brakes, brought her to full power, felt the whole airframe strain against my boots like a hound at a leash, and let the numbers settle over me one last time—airspeed alive, engine temperatures green, the bar unspooling beneath us, the willows growing, the water at the end of the world coming up fast and brown and hungry—

And that's when I saw them. Three figures at the tree line on the far bank, motionless in the rain, watching us. Too far to see faces. Close enough to see that one of them raised something long and dark to his shoulder—not quite aiming, not quite not.

"Hold on," I said, and released the brakes.
