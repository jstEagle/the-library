## 30. Climb-Out

The rain came sideways off the gorge like it had somewhere better to be.

Gideon stood at the edge of what remained of the gravel bar and did the arithmetic one more time, because arithmetic was the only prayer he'd ever trusted. One drum of fuel, forty-four gallons. Two grown men. A field journal wrapped in oilcloth inside his jacket. The survival gear they hadn't eaten or burned. And the plane itself, which had never once in its life been asked to carry this much off a strip this short in weather this foul.

"Say the number again," Sam said beside him. He was soaked through, shivering, and grinning anyway, which Gideon had decided was either courage or a fever symptom.

"Two hundred and eighty-one feet," Gideon said. "Maybe. If I rotate at fifty-five and the wind doesn't lie to me."

"And if it lies?"

"Then we find out how much a Beaver floats."

Sam looked at the river, brown and swollen and moving fast enough to carry whole trees like matchsticks. "It doesn't float, does it."

"Not for long."

They worked in silence after that, because there is a particular silence that falls between two men who have spent a day building a raft through white water and have run out of things to prove to each other. They rolled the drum up the gravel on two tent poles as an axle, muscled it into the fuselage behind the cargo net, strapped it, swore at it, strapped it again. Gideon lashed the raft frame—what was left of it, the good poles—to the float struts, because he was not going to land short of Rivergate without something under him that wasn't aluminum. Sam packed their wet clothes into the spaces between crates, which was not weight-saving so much as grief management, but Gideon let him do it. A man needed to pack something.

The wind had come around out of the northwest an hour ago, which was bad, and then strengthened, which was worse, and then begun to gust in that irregular, thoughtful way that meant the storm behind it hadn't made up its mind yet. Downstream, the takeoff direction, the bar ran maybe three hundred feet before the river bent and the treeline stood up—a wall of black spruce, dripping, indifferent, close enough that Gideon could count individual branches.

He had taken off from worse strips. He told himself that. It was almost true. He had taken off from worse strips in better weather with less weight, which was a different sentence entirely, and his father's voice—the real one, from memory, not the journal's ghost—said in his head: *A pilot who talks himself into a takeoff has already crashed; he's just waiting for the ground to catch up.*

"Okay," Gideon said. "Listen. If she doesn't fly, you get out on the left, away from the prop. Don't think about the gear. Don't think about me. You swim for the right bank and you walk south until you hit the survey line, and you follow it."

"You're coming too."

"I'm giving you instructions. Humor me."

Sam's grin faded into something older and steadier. "Mara asked me to tell you something, before we launched the raft. She said: *Tell him his father would be proud, and also tell him his father would say check the mixture.*"

Gideon laughed despite himself—a short, surprised bark that the wind took away. "She's delirious."

"She's a professor. Same thing."

He walked the plane one last time, hands on the fabric and the struts, feeling for anything the rapids might have loosened. The floats were scarred but sound. The propeller had a new nick in it from a rock he didn't remember hitting, which was the river's way of signing its work. Fuel: full tanks plus the drum, plumbed in through a hand pump he could work in flight if it came to that, and it would come to that, because everything came to that.

He climbed in. Sam folded himself into the seat beside him, knees against the panel, journal-safe jacket zipped to his chin because Gideon had ordered it—if the plane went into the trees, the paper floated better than the pilot, and history had already cost enough men.

Gideon keyed the radio, mostly out of habit. Static. Then, faintly, through the wash of rain on the antenna, the Rivergate weather broadcast, thin as a voice through a wall: *...frontal passage expected by evening, winds gusting thirty-five along the ridges, ceiling lowering...*

"Ceiling lowering," Sam repeated. "That sounds bad."

"It means the sky is coming down to meet us halfway." Gideon primed the engine. "Considerate of it."

* * *

Starting a radial engine in the rain is a negotiation. Gideon pulled the prop through eight blades by hand first, standing on the float in the current's push, water sliding cold over his boots, and each swing felt like asking a question he wasn't sure he wanted answered. On the ninth, he called contact, scrambled up, and hit the starter.

The engine caught on the third blade, coughed, caught again, and settled into that ragged, magnificent roar that no turbine will ever imitate, the whole airframe shuddering like a dog shaking off water. Rain hammered the windshield. The river shoved at the floats. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled down the gorge like furniture being moved.

"Rivergate traffic, Beaver seven-four-something," Gideon transmitted, knowing nobody was listening, needing somebody to hear anyway. "Departing gravel bar, gorge reach, climbing through weather. Three souls aboard." He paused. Two souls. But he said three, because the third was riding in his jacket, and because some part of him wanted the record to show Elias Yarrow got out of this valley one way or another.

He taxied to the downstream end of the bar, turned into the wind—which was now a crosswind, because the wind had opinions—and held position while the engine came up to full temperature. The spruce wall waited. The river ran. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Twenty years of flying on a false name and his hands had finally learned what the rest of him was still learning: that fear and readiness feel identical in the fingers.

"Any last words?" Sam shouted over the roar.

"Yeah. Breathe out when I pull. It helps."

"It does not help."

"It helps *me*."

Gideon eased the throttle forward.

The engine bellowed. The floats bit the water, threw sheets of spray across the windshield, and the Beaver began to move—slowly, insultingly slowly, the overloaded hull dragging through the current like a man wading upstream. Fifty feet. A hundred. The airspeed needle crawled. The wind gusted from the left and the nose swung and Gideon corrected with rudder, gentle, gentle, every input small as a confession. A hundred and fifty feet. The bar's far end was visible now, and beyond it the bend, and beyond the bend the trees, tall and patient.

Two hundred feet. Forty knots. The needle was lying to him or the rain was distorting the pitot, and either way he had perhaps eighty feet of world remaining in which to make a decision that could not be unmade.

*Rotate,* said twenty years of instinct, and also, absurdly, his father's ledger, whose final unfinished sentence Gideon had read eleven times: *the lift comes if you commit all the way—*

He pulled.

The Beaver rose the way a tired old man rises from a deep chair—reluctantly, groaning, with visible effort. The floats left the water. The gravel bar slid beneath them and was gone, and the trees came up, huge, green-black, streaming.

Fifty feet above the water. Airspeed bleeding. The gust died at exactly the wrong moment and the left wing dropped and Gideon fed in opposite aileron and the wing hesitated, hung, obeyed. Thirty feet above the treetops now. Twenty. He could see individual cones on the spruce tips. He could see, absurdly, a bird's nest wedged in a fork, one brave idiot of a thrush peering out at the roaring metal monster passing overhead close enough to ruffle the nest.

Ten feet.

The stall warning sounded—a small electronic voice announcing, in effect, *you are asking too much of the world.* Gideon lowered the nose a half-inch, trading height for speed, the trees swelling toward them, and the engine, God bless its cast-iron heart, found another fifty RPM somewhere, and the airspeed ticked up to fifty-eight, and the Beaver stopped climbing and started *flying*, which are different things, and the difference was the width of a wingtip.

They cleared the last tree by what Gideon would later swear was six feet and Sam would swear was two, and both of them would be wrong, because neither of them was looking at anything except the space between the branches and the floats.

Then the valley fell away behind them and the river bent north and there was nothing under them but rain.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The cabin smelled of gasoline and wet wool and fear, which Gideon had long ago learned was the smell of being alive at altitude.

"We're up," Sam said finally, in the tone of a man reporting a miracle to a skeptical supervisor.

"We're up." Gideon's voice came out lower than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Climbing turn to the southeast. Stay in the valley until we're over the ridge, then we'll pick our way through the passes. No shortcuts today."

"No shortcuts," Sam agreed fervently. "I would like to file a formal complaint against shortcuts generally."

"Duly noted."

The rain thickened. Visibility dropped to what Gideon thought of as *honest*—a mile, maybe less, the world reduced to gray water, gray rock, gray sky, with the occasional black shoulder of cliff sliding past close enough to make Sam inhale through his teeth. Gideon flew instruments-and-instinct, the old bush pilot's compromise: eyes outside for the terrain, ears inside for the engine, hands doing the small constant work of keeping a heavy airplane honest in rising air.

The turbulence came off the ridgelines in fists. Once, a downdraft shoved them down four hundred feet in as many seconds, and Sam made a noise like a kettle, and Gideon heard himself say, conversationally, "That's just the mountain saying hello," and realized he was smiling. There was something wrong with him. Something had come loose in the gorge and reattached differently, and the result appeared to be a man who could smile in a downdraft.

The fuel drum shifted in its straps with a hollow knock.

"Was that—" Sam started.

"The drum. She's fine. Straps are fine." They were fine. He'd checked them twice. He checked them a third time with his eyes anyway, because trust is a renewable resource but verification is free.

They crossed the first ridge at ninety feet above the rocks, in rain so hard the windshield wiper—there was no windshield wiper—would have been pointless. The second ridge was worse, cloud sitting on it like a lid, and Gideon threaded the pass below the cloud base with the river as his guide, following the water home the way his father's journal said the old surveyors did, the way men had always done, the way the plane was only a faster version of a canoe and a canoe was only a faster version of walking and walking was only a faster version of wanting to get there.

Halfway down the far side, the engine stumbled.

Once. A single missed beat, like a heart skipping in the night.

Gideon's hand went to the mixture, enriched. The wobble valve on the auxiliary tank—he reached back, worked the hand pump, felt pressure build. The engine smoothed. Held.

"What was that?" Sam asked quietly.

"A conversation between me and the fuel system," Gideon said. "I won."

But he had done the math in the half-second of silence, and the math said the margin was thinner than he'd told Sam, thinner than he'd told himself. The headwind was stronger than forecast. The detour around the worst cell had cost miles. The drum was feeding slower than the engine drank.

He keyed the radio and raised Rivergate, and the voice that came back was Mara Voss's, weak and clear and absolutely level, reading him the wind and the altimeter setting like she'd been born in a tower, and underneath her words he could hear the thing she wasn't saying, which was *come home.*

"Fifteen minutes," he told her. "Tell the strip to leave the lights off and the coffee on."

"Coffee," she repeated, and there was the ghost of a laugh in it. "I'll see what survives in that town."

* * *

They broke out of the rain nine miles south of Rivergate, into a world washed silver and gold where the storm's trailing edge tore open and evening light poured through like something spilled. The river ran below them, high and brown and ordinary, doing what rivers do. Sam laughed—a cracked, exhausted, disbelieving sound—and Gideon found his own eyes stinging and blamed the wind, which had followed them the whole way and deserved the credit.

Then the radio crackled, and a voice came through that was not Rivergate's, not Mara's, not anyone's who belonged in this sky.

"Gideon Yarrow." The name landed on him like a hand on the shoulder. "This is Crane's aircraft, ten miles off your right wing. We've been listening. Turn north to the mining strip, Mr. Yarrow. We should talk about your cargo."

Gideon looked right. Far off, at the edge of the broken cloud, a shape hung in the gold light, watching.

His hands stayed steady on the wheel. He had cleared the trees by feet; he supposed he could clear this too.

"Sam," he said mildly, "how do you feel about a longer flight?"

"I hate it," Sam said. "I hate everything about it."

"Good," said Gideon, and banked south, toward Rivergate, and the other plane turned to follow.
