## 31. Intercepted

The rain found him eleven minutes after the trees.

Gideon had been counting the way he counted everything—by miles and minutes and the small honest arithmetic of fuel burn—and when the first fat drops hit the windshield he noted them without alarm, the way a man notes a bill arriving in the mail. He'd known it was coming. The weather officer at Rivergate had told him twice, with the weary patience of a man who has explained the same thing to pilots until his voice went smooth as river stone. *Front's moving faster than the models say, Yarrow. You want my opinion, you wait till morning.*

Gideon had thanked him politely and gone anyway, because Sam Okafor was sitting on a gravel bar that would be underwater by Thursday, and because morning was a luxury the river didn't sell.

He ran the numbers again out of habit. Full tanks minus the climb-out over the ridge, which had cost more than he'd liked—he'd felt the old Cessna's wings go heavy and sullen in the wet air, felt her ask him *are you sure* in the language of sluggish response and a manifold pressure reading half a needle-width shy of where it ought to be. He'd been sure. Mostly sure. Sure enough, which in bush flying is the only kind of sure there is.

Two hundred and ten miles to Rivergate. Headwind out of the southwest at twenty-five and building. That put him at just under two hours, burning just over what he had, with a margin thin enough to read a newspaper through. He'd flown thinner margins. He'd flown margins so thin they were practically theoretical. But those had been calm-air margins, honest margins, and this one came wrapped in weather that was going to get worse before it got better.

"Nothing for it," he said aloud, because the cabin was empty and a man alone at eight thousand feet is allowed to narrate.

The Cessna said nothing back, which he took as agreement.

Below him the Rivergate country unrolled in the gray-green way it always did, all ridgeline and drainage, the land folded like laundry nobody had bothered to sort. Somewhere down there was the gorge. Somewhere down there was the cairn where his father's compass now sat pointing at nothing, doing the one job compasses are never asked to do. Somewhere down there was the cave, and inside the cave the journal, wrapped in oilcloth in the map case behind Gideon's seat, riding home lighter than a drum of fuel and heavier than anything he'd ever carried.

He'd left the rest of the journal pages with Mara's notes and Sam's testimony, copies made by lantern light, distributed among three people so no single plane crash could end the story. Sam had called it redundancy. Gideon had called it not being stupid twice.

The rain thickened. He climbed a few hundred feet to stay comfortable above the terrain, watching the cell build ahead on the western horizon—a wall of bruise-colored cloud stacked up like dishes in a sink, lightning flickering inside it the way thoughts flicker behind a closed door. He could go around it north or south. North added forty miles. South added sixty. He went north, because north was shorter, and because the wind would be quartering rather than direct, and because by that point in his life Gideon Yarrow had learned that you don't beat weather, you negotiate with it, and every negotiation starts with giving up something you wanted.

He was twenty minutes into the detour, hand-logging his position out of habit though there was no one to log it for, when the radio crackled.

"—aircraft on two-six-two, aircraft heading one-four-zero at eight thousand five hundred, do you copy?"

Gideon's hand went to the yoke mic before his brain caught up. The voice was unfamiliar—not Rivergate traffic, not the weather officer, not any of the half-dozen charter frequencies he knew by their accents the way other men know faces. This voice was clipped and professional and had the particular polish of someone who talked on radios in climate-controlled rooms.

He keyed the mic. "Aircraft calling, this is Cessna seven-three-kilo, go ahead."

"Seven-three-kilo, this is Crane Resources aircraft niner-one-charlie, currently ten miles off your right wing." A pause, professionally measured. "We'd like a word."

Gideon looked right. Through the rain-smeared side window, past the strut, he saw it: a twin-engine turboprop, white with a blue stripe, hanging in the murk a mile out with the smug stability of an aircraft that cost more than the town it was named after. It had appeared out of the weather the way bad news appears out of a phone call—suddenly, and with the sense that it had been coming for a long time.

"Well," Gideon said, to the empty cabin again. "There he is."

He'd known Crane wouldn't sit still. A man who spends a fortune sending an expedition into the wilderness to confirm a death doesn't spend that fortune to have the answer fly away in a thirty-year-old Cessna. Gideon had allowed himself—foolishly, sentimentally—to hope the old man's reach ended at the Rivergate strip, that money bought proximity but not pursuit. Apparently money bought both. Money, it turned out, bought its own airplane and a pilot who could find a single Cessna in two thousand square miles of storm, which meant money had also bought radar data, flight plans, and probably a man in an office somewhere watching a little blip crawl across a screen with his coffee going cold.

"Seven-three-kilo," the voice said again, when Gideon hadn't answered fast enough. "Mr. Crane would like to speak with you. Please stand by."

Stand by. As if Gideon were going anywhere. As if this were a scheduled call and he had an appointment book.

Static, then a different voice—older, slower, with the unhurried confidence of a man who had never once in his life been kept waiting by weather, gravity, or God. Aldous Crane sounded exactly the way he looked: like a handshake that checked your ring finger for gold.

"Gideon Yarrow." The name landed strangely, hearing it spoken aloud by a stranger. Nobody used it. At Rivergate he was Yarrow, or more often "the pilot," or, from the old-timers, "that Marchetti boy's friend," a title he'd never earned and never corrected. Hearing his full name in Crane's mouth was like hearing someone read his mail. "Do you know how much a chartered search aircraft costs per hour? I'll tell you, since you're a businessman of sorts. More than your airplane. Considerably more than your airplane."

"Hello to you too, Mr. Crane."

"I'll be brief, since I understand you're low on fuel and the weather's turning." Of course Crane knew about the fuel. Of course he did. "You're carrying company property. Documents removed from a site under contract to Crane Resources. I'd like them back."

"They weren't yours when you sent people to die looking for them," Gideon said. "I'm not sure how they became yours afterward."

A pause on the radio, long enough that Gideon imagined Crane in the cabin of the turboprop, unhurried, perhaps smiling, the smile of a man listening to a debtor explain why the payment will be late.

"You misunderstand the situation," Crane said. "Which is understandable. You've spent—" a rustle of paper, theatrical, "—what is this, nineteen years? You've spent nineteen years being no one, Mr. Yarrow. No name, no history, no fixed address. I admire it, frankly. It takes discipline. Most men can't disappear; they keep leaving fingerprints. But you—you've done it beautifully. And here's what I'm offering: keep doing it. Fly home. Land wherever you land. Hand me the documents through whatever intermediary you prefer, and tomorrow morning you wake up exactly as anonymous as you were yesterday. No file. No record. No one in Rivergate ever learns what name you were born under or why you stopped using it."

The rain hammered the windshield. Gideon flew for a moment without speaking, hands light on the yoke, watching the wall of cloud slide past his left wingtip.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I make some calls." Crane's voice stayed pleasant, which somehow made it worse. "Aviation authorities take a dim view of unregistered charters operating out of unlicensed strips. And there's the matter of the expedition itself—two men dead, Mr. Yarrow, and a great deal of confusion about who authorized what. Confusion is such a flexible material. It can be shaped around almost anyone. Around a pilot, say, who flew into a restricted site against advice, in deteriorating weather, alone, and came back with documents that don't belong to him."

"You'd hang your own mess on me."

"I'd hang it on whoever it fits best," Crane said, with the honesty of a man who has never needed to lie because the truth bends for him. "That's not villainy, son. That's logistics."

Gideon laughed. He couldn't help it—a short, sharp sound, swallowed immediately by engine noise. There was something almost refreshing about it, hearing the thing said plainly at last. Twenty years of careful silence, nineteen years of being a man with no last name worth saying, and here was Aldous Crane, ten miles off his wing, explaining that his whole invisible life was simply inventory waiting to be repriced.

"You know what my father used to say?" Gideon said, keying the mic. "About flying?"

"I couldn't possibly imagine."

"He said the sky doesn't care whose son you are. It cares whether you respect it. Everything else—the name, the money, all of it—that's ground clutter." He watched the twin hold its position off his wing, steady as a thought he couldn't shake. "You're ground clutter, Mr. Crane."

"Poetry," Crane said dryly. "From a bush pilot. The interior does change people." Then, dropping the pleasantness one degree, the way a man drops his voice in a church: "Seven-three-kilo, let me put this in terms even a poet understands. We are going to shadow you to Rivergate. If you divert anywhere else, I will assume you intend to keep company property, and I will act accordingly. If you land at Rivergate and produce the documents, we conclude our business amicably and you return to your quiet life. Those are the options. There isn't a third one, despite what the novels say."

"There's always a third one," Gideon said. "That's what makes it flying instead of driving."

He switched the radio back to the common frequency and let the conversation die, and the strange thing—the thing he'd remember later, turning it over in the grounded weeks to come—was how calm he felt. Not brave. Calm. There's a difference, and the difference is fear. Fear was there, all right, sitting in the right-hand seat where Sam should have been, cold and attentive, running its own calculations. But beneath the fear was something older and simpler, something he'd built flight by flight across ten thousand hours of sky: the knowledge that the airplane would do what he asked it to do, and that he would ask it to do the right things, and that between those two facts a man could survive quite a lot, including rich men in nice airplanes.

The problem, of course, was the fuel.

He checked it again, because checking again is free and running out is not. The gauge said what it had said twenty minutes ago, minus twenty minutes. The headwind said what it had said, plus a knot or two. The math sat there in his lap like a stone. Two hours to Rivergate, give or take. Fuel for one hour fifty, give or take, and the give-or-take was all in the wrong direction, and the storm front ahead was going to add turbulence, and turbulence adds burn, and—

And Crane's twin was going to follow him the whole way, and if he set it down short—if he had to put it into a logging clearing or a road or a farmer's field, anywhere but Rivergate—he'd be alone on the ground with a dead-stick airplane, a box of papers, and a man with resources.

Unless he wasn't alone.

His eyes went to the panel, to the little satellite messenger mounted beside the GPS, the one Sam had insisted on wiring in before the second run, tapping it with a grease-stained finger and saying, *Boss, this thing texts from anywhere. Anywhere. Even places God gave up on.* Gideon had grunted and let him do it, the way you let a young man teach you things you already half-knew.

Sam was in Rivergate. So was Mara. And Sam, Gideon had learned on a gravel bar while building a raft out of wreckage, was the kind of young man who checked his messages the way other people check their pulse.

He didn't send anything yet. Not yet. First he'd see how the weather treated him. First he'd give the sky its chance to solve this honestly, man against arithmetic, the old clean way.

The sky declined the invitation.

Twenty minutes later, climbing to top a line of hills that shouldn't have been a problem, the Cessna met the downdraft. It hit like a hand pressing down on the tail—sudden, personal, rude—and the nose dropped and the ground rose and Gideon shoved throttle forward into air that gave back less than it promised. The engine, already hot and tired and drinking from tanks that were more memory than fuel, coughed. Once. Twice. A third time, longer, the propeller going briefly weightless in a way that no propeller should ever feel.

"No," Gideon told it. "Not now. Not yet. Come on, girl, we're nearly—"

She caught herself. The propeller bit air again, rough and ragged, and the climb-out numbers sagged, and Gideon eased the nose down and traded altitude for speed and speed for distance, nursing her along the way you nurse a sick animal, talking to her under his breath, promising things.

Behind him and to the right, the white twin with the blue stripe held its station, patient as a creditor.

Crane's voice came back on the frequency, soft and reasonable, a man offering an umbrella in a downpour. "Seven-three-kilo. You sound busy. I'll make this easy. There's a strip at Kettle Falls, forty miles south. Company-maintained. Dry, lit, fuel on the ground. Divert there, hand over the property, and I'll fill your tanks myself. You'll sleep indoors tonight. Everyone sleeps indoors tonight. Doesn't that sound better than the alternative?"

Gideon looked at the fuel gauge. The fuel gauge looked back, honest as a mirror.

Forty miles south was a trap with a roof over it. Two hours northeast was Rivergate, and between here and there lay forty minutes of fuel, maybe fifty if he flew her gentle and the wind forgave him, and the wind had shown no signs of forgiveness all day.

He keyed the mic one more time.

"Tell you what, Mr. Crane," he said, watching the storm wall slide past, watching the gray country below open up into a long valley with a thread of river in it, and along the river, faint through the rain, the pale scar of a logging road cut into the trees. "Ask me again in forty miles."

He clicked off, thumbed the satellite messenger open with his thumb, and typed four words to Sam Okafor:

*Logging road. Mile marker nine.*

Then he put the message down, put both hands on the yoke, and began, very quietly and with great attention, to glide.
