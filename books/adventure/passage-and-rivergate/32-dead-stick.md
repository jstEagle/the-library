## 32. Dead Stick

The engine quit at eleven thousand feet, which was the good news.

I want to be clear about that, because in the moment it did not feel like good news. It felt like the sky reaching into the cockpit and pulling the plug. One second the Lycoming was humming its one-note hymn against my spine, the next there was nothing but wind and the particular silence that every pilot carries around like a stone in the boot—the silence we spend our whole careers pretending we'll never hear.

But eleven thousand feet is a lot of feet. And a clearing is a thing you can find if you don't waste altitude on panic.

"Come on," I told the plane. "You've done this before. We've both done this before."

She hadn't, actually. Neither had I, not for real. Practiced dead-stick landings, yes—dozens, at every strip from Rivergate south, cutting power over the numbers and floating down like it was a game. But practice has a throttle you can push back up when you get it wrong. This afternoon the only throttle was gravity, and gravity does not negotiate.

I'd been fighting headwinds since the ridge, burning fuel I'd promised myself I wasn't burning. The math had been tight when I left the gravel bar. Somewhere over the burnt-over country east of the Kettle Range it stopped being tight and started being fiction, and I'd known it, the way you know a tooth is going bad, and I'd kept flying anyway because the alternative was landing on the river and watching the current eat my wheels. So now the debt came due, all at once, no interest, just principal: silence at eleven thousand feet, and me with my hand already moving to the mixture, the magnetos, the fuel selector—running the checklist not because it would help but because checklists are what you hold onto when your hands want to shake.

Fuel starvation. Not a mechanical failure. A *me* failure. I'd traded margin for speed, same as always, and the sky had finally sent an invoice.

"All right," I said. "All right."

Below me the land rolled out in shades of green and grey-green and the bruised purple of clear-cut slash, all of it running downhill toward the river I couldn't see anymore. Rain still fell ahead of me in silver curtains, but here, this pocket, this hour, the light came through low and gold and made the whole wrecked landscape look almost kind. Somewhere behind me were Sam and a drum of fuel and a raft tied to a tree. Somewhere ahead of me was Crane's man, or maybe he wasn't, or maybe he was exactly where Sam's satellite message said the police would find him.

I pushed the nose down and started trading sky for distance, hunting the clearings.

* * *

There were three candidates. A muskeg flat that would swallow the gear and probably the rest of the plane. A river oxbow shaped like a question mark. And a logging clearing, square-cut and pale, sitting on a bench above a creek about four miles off my nose—a stump field, honestly, studded with slash piles, but with a lane down the middle where the skidders had dragged logs out, packed dirt, smooth as a runway by bush standards.

Bush standards. That's the joke I made to myself, because if I didn't make jokes I'd have to do arithmetic instead, and the arithmetic said things like *glide ratio* and *wind component* and *you have one attempt.*

I picked the clearing. Of course I picked the clearing. Every pilot picks the clearing, right up until the clearing kills them.

Four miles out I had the field made and altitude to spare, and I let myself feel, for about six seconds, the warm bloom of a problem solved. Then I saw the truck.

It was parked at the near edge of the clearing, angled across the skid lane like a gate. Grey pickup, canopy on the back, mud to the windows. Even from two miles out and half a mile up, even through a rain-spattered windshield, I knew what it was. There are trucks that belong to logging operations and trucks that belong to the bush, and then there are trucks that belong to men who wait. This was the third kind. It sat too deliberately. Nobody parks across the only smooth strip in ten square miles by accident.

My first thought—and I'm not proud of this, but honesty is the whole point of telling it—was: *land long.* Put her down past the truck, roll out the other side, and take my chances with the stumps.

My second thought was that a stump at forty knots goes through a firewall like a spear through paper, and my third thought was that the man in the truck could simply drive faster than I could roll.

So I did what you do. I flew the airplane. I put the flaps where they belonged, trimmed her for seventy knots, aimed at the gap between the truck and the far treeline, and told myself the lie every bush pilot tells himself in the last thirty seconds of a forced approach: *this will be fine.*

The clearing rose up to meet me the way the ground always does at the end—all at once, after taking forever. Slash piles flicked past on either side. The skid lane came up smooth and brown and beautiful. I flared a little high, floated a little long, touched down with a chirp of tires and a lurch as the tail came down, and rolled out through the stumps with the plane shuddering and me standing on the brakes, and stopped forty yards short of the grey truck with the propeller still windmilling lazily and the whole world gone quiet except for my own heart banging around in there like a loose tool.

I sat for a moment. Hands on the yoke. Letting the adrenaline drain out through the seat of my pants, which is where it goes, I've found.

"Well," I said to the empty cockpit. "That'll leave a mark on the logbook."

Then I looked up, and the man got out of the truck.

* * *

He was younger than I expected, which somehow made it worse. I'd built a picture on the flight north—Crane's man, and I'd imagined grey at the temples, a hard-used face, somebody who'd been doing ugly errands so long the ugliness had settled into him like silt. What climbed out of the pickup instead was a big, neat, ordinary-looking fellow in his thirties, canvas jacket, clean boots, the kind of face you'd trust to tell you which platform your train leaves from. He carried a pistol the way you'd carry a thermos—low, casual, pointed generally at the gravel, like it was just something he happened to be holding while he waited for a friend.

"Gideon Yarrow," he called, before I'd even got the door open. Not a question.

And there it was. My name, spoken out loud in the middle of nowhere, by a stranger, in that flat confident tone people use when they've read your file. Twenty years I'd been nobody—G. Marchetti on the manifests, the quiet pilot, the man who flies medicine and mail and doesn't ask questions. I'd worn that namelessness so long it had grown into my skin. Hearing my real name come out of a stranger's mouth in a stump field felt like someone reading my mail aloud in church.

"You've got the advantage," I said, climbing down onto the skid lane. My legs were steadier than I deserved.

"Hollis." He didn't offer a hand. "Mr. Crane sent me."

"Mr. Crane sends his regards a lot. Radio call this morning, now you. He should get a hobby."

"He has hobbies." Hollis smiled a little, and it was a nice smile, and that was the most frightening thing about him. "This is one of them."

He stood about fifteen feet away, between me and the truck, with the pistol held easy at his side, and I stood beside my airplane with a dead engine and a fuel gauge reading zero and roughly four hundred miles of nothing in every direction, and I took inventory the way I take inventory of everything: what do I have, what does it weigh, where can it go.

What I had: a wrench in the door pocket, a survival kit under the baggage shelf, a strongbox's worth of nothing—because the journal and the photographs rode in a waterproof bag inside my jacket, against my chest, where I'd been carrying them since the cave. What he had: a gun, a truck, and, presumably, friends or at least a radio. What weighed the most, I realized, standing there in the gold light with the rain holding off like it was giving us privacy, was the paper against my ribs. Elias's handwriting. *Tell my son I wasn't running away—I was coming home.* Twenty years of weight, and I'd flown it straight into this man's parking lot.

"The plane's out of fuel," Hollis said conversationally. "Saw you come in dead stick. Nice landing, by the way. Really. Mr. Crane will want to know you fly like that."

"He could just ask Mara Voss. She'd tell him."

Something moved behind his eyes at her name—satisfaction, I thought, or relief. They didn't know yet whether she'd made it out. Or they hadn't, until this morning, when she'd landed at Rivergate and started talking to police. Maybe he still didn't know. I decided he shouldn't learn it from me.

"She's safe," I said, watching him. "If that matters to your employer."

"It complicates things," Hollis said, and shrugged, honest as weather. "Not much. Mr. Crane's very good at complicated."

"I've noticed. He's also very good at spending other people's money to avoid his own problems. How much is he paying you to stand in a field holding a gun at a man who just landed without an engine?"

"Enough." He said it without embarrassment, and I found I respected that more than anything else about him. "Look, Mr. Yarrow. Can I call you Gideon? Gideon. I'm not here to hurt anybody. I'm here because Mr. Crane believes you're carrying property that belongs to him. Expedition materials. Company-funded research. He's prepared to be generous about it."

"Generous how?"

Hollis reached into his jacket with his free hand—slowly, showing me it wasn't the gun side—and came out with a folded envelope. He held it up between two fingers like a magician showing the card.

"There's twenty-five thousand dollars in here. Cash. There's another twenty-five waiting in Rivergate. And there's this." He tapped his own chest, mirroring where my journal sat against mine, and I swear my heart stopped for a full beat before I understood he was only guessing. "A letter. Signed by Mr. Crane. It says that any record concerning Gideon Yarrow—any outstanding questions about the incident in your family's past—is closed. Permanently. No inquiries, no leaks, no file. You walk away clean. New name if you want it, old name if you want it, whatever you want. You become whoever you decide to be, and nobody ever gets to make you into anything else again."

The wind moved through the stumps. Somewhere down the slope the creek talked to itself. And I stood there and let the offer wash over me, and I'll tell you the truth, which is that for three or four seconds it was the most beautiful thing I'd ever heard.

Because that's the wound, isn't it? That's the whole wound. Nineteen years old, a scandal with my family's name splashed across papers I never read again, and I'd solved it the only way I knew how—I'd vanished. I'd spent two decades being nobody, counting miles instead of years, flying medicine to strangers who never needed to know what I'd run from. And here was a man in clean boots offering to sell me back the thing I'd lost, official and sealed and permanent. Anonymity, gift-wrapped. Erasure, certified.

Twenty-five thousand dollars wouldn't have tempted a dog. But the letter—

"That's quite an offer," I said, and my voice came out level, and I was proud of that later.

"It's a sincere one." He took a step closer. The pistol stayed down. "Here's the other part, Gideon, because I think you're a straight-shooter and I'll be straight with you. If you say no, I have to report that you refused. And then Mr. Crane stops being generous. He's an old man, and old men with reputations don't negotiate forever. There are people who can make that plane disappear, that cargo disappear—" his eyes flicked to my jacket, and this time I couldn't tell if he was guessing or knowing, and the not-telling was its own kind of violence, "—and, if it comes to it, a pilot disappear. Accidents happen up here. You know that better than anyone. Your father taught us all that."

The creek went on talking. The propeller had stopped turning. And something in me went very, very quiet—not calm, exactly, but the stillness that comes right before weather changes, the hush I'd learned to read in the sky over twenty years of measured miles.

"My father," I said, "died in that gorge. In 2003. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows what Mr. Crane paid to have everyone know," Hollis said gently. "Which is why the documents matter. Which is why you're going to give them to me."

And there it was, laid out plain as a runway: he knew. Not everything—maybe not even much—but enough. Enough to aim with.

I thought about Elias in the cave, weeks after the crash, repairing a raft by firelight, writing by whatever light he had, believing—right up until he couldn't believe anymore—that he was coming home. I thought about the compass I'd left on the cairn, because some things you carry and some things you set down. I thought about Sam on the gravel bar, and Mara in a hospital bed in Rivergate giving statements, and the river rising, and the strange new fact of my life, which was that three days ago I had lied smoothly to Aldous Crane's face and discovered that hiding was a tool, and tools can be picked up or put down.

I put my hand inside my jacket.

Hollis raised the pistol—not aiming, not yet, but no longer a thermos. "Careful."

"Relax." I brought it out slowly. A folded map. The survey chart of the gorge, water-stained, worthless now, a duplicate of what was already in police hands if Sam's message had landed—which I was betting it had, betting everything on it, the way you bet everything on a clearing in a stump field. "You want expedition materials? Here. Survey maps. Crane funded the dig; these are his. Tell him the rest burned with the camp."

Hollis didn't move. "You're lying."

"Probably," I agreed. "You'll notice I've gotten good at it. It runs in the family."

For a long moment neither of us breathed. He looked at the map, at me, at the map again, doing his own arithmetic—what he had, what it weighed, where it could go. And I watched him arrive, slowly, at the same number I had: that a man who lands dead-stick in a stump field rather than drown in a river is not a man who hands over his father's last words for twenty-five thousand dollars and a letter.

Down the valley, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I'd been listening for since I touched down without admitting I was listening for it.

An engine. More than one. And a siren, thin and rising, the way sirens sound when they're still miles out but coming.

Hollis heard it too. His head turned a quarter-inch toward the noise, and in that quarter-inch I saw the whole transaction reverse itself in his eyes—the buyer becoming the seller, the hunter doing quick math about roads and distances and who else knew where he was.

"Gideon," he said quietly, "we should talk fast."

"No," I said, and smiled, and found that it didn't hurt. "I think we're done talking. I think help is here."

The sirens came on down the logging road, and Hollis's pistol, for the first time, wavered.
