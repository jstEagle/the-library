## 33. The Price Named

The clearing was smaller than it had looked from eight hundred feet up—most things were—and the stumps stood around the edges like a jury that had already made up its mind. Gideon brought the Cessna down dead stick, flaring over the ferns, and felt the wheels kiss gravel with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

He sat there for a moment with his hands on the yoke, listening to the windmilling prop slow to a stop. The engine hadn't coughed so much as sighed, the way a tired man sighs when he sits down at last. Fuel starvation. His own arithmetic, his own fault—he'd traded fuel for altitude on the ridge crossing, betting the headwind would be kinder than the forecast said. The forecast had won.

Ahead, maybe forty meters, a man stepped out of the treeline.

Gideon had seen him before, once, across the Rivergate strip: gray jacket, gray face, the kind of man who looked like he'd been issued rather than born. He carried a pistol low against his thigh, pointed at the dirt, which was either courtesy or theater. Behind him, half-hidden in the alders, sat a mud-splattered pickup with a company logo on the door that someone had tried to peel off and given up on.

"Well," Gideon said to the empty cockpit. "That didn't take long."

He climbed out slowly, hands visible, because that was what you did in the bush—you showed people your hands. The morning was cool and smelled of cut cedar and rain coming somewhere behind the hills. Somewhere a woodpecker was going about its business with more conviction than anyone else in the clearing.

"Mr. Yarrow." The man said the name carefully, tasting it. "Or should I say Mr.—"

"Don't," Gideon said. "Whatever name comes next, don't. It's early."

The man almost smiled. Almost. "My name is Deverell. I work for Mr. Crane."

"I know who you work for. You have his posture."

Deverell ignored that, which took discipline. He came closer, boots crunching on the slash, and stopped a respectful distance away—the distance of a man who wanted to be heard clearly but not remembered too specifically. "You've had an eventful week."

"You could say that. I flew some people out of the gorge. I'm flying the rest out tomorrow. Then I'm going to sleep for approximately four days."

"The box," Deverell said. "And whatever was in it."

"There was no box."

"There was a strongbox, Mr. Yarrow. Serial number ending in seven-one-four. Purchased by Halloway Expeditions in March. We have the receipt."

"You people keep receipts for everything except human beings," Gideon said, and watched that land. It landed. Deverell's jaw did something small and controlled, like a man swallowing a stone.

"Mr. Crane is prepared to be generous."

"Is he." Gideon leaned against the strut of the wing, arms crossed, playing at being calmer than he was. His heart was going like a floatplane taking off in chop. "Generous how?"

"Cash. Enough to disappear properly this time. Not the half-measure you managed at nineteen—a real erasure. New registration on the aircraft, clean paper, no questions from Transport, no questions from anyone. And the file." Deverell paused, letting the word sit between them like a loaded crate. "The sealed file on Elias Yarrow. Gone. Burned. Your father's name stays exactly where the world put it twenty years ago, and yours stays wherever you want it."

The woodpecker kept hammering. Gideon found he could hear his own breathing.

There it was, then. The whole trap laid out flat in the morning light, and the terrible thing—the thing he'd understand later, sitting in Mara Voss's kitchen while she drank tea like a woman relearning how—was how good it sounded. Anonymity had been his religion for fifteen years. Measured miles over lived years. Nobody knowing your name meant nobody able to hurt you with it. He'd built a whole life on that principle, a life as tight and light as a stripped-down airframe, and here was a man offering to reinforce it, weatherproof it, certify it.

All it would cost was a dead man's voice.

Because that's what the journal was. Not evidence—not primarily, not in the way Sam meant it when Sam talked about ruining Crane publicly, with the bright vengeful energy of a young man who still believed exposure was justice. It was a voice. *Tell my son I wasn't running away. I was coming home.* Twenty years of silence, and then a few pages of pencil in a waterproof sleeve, saying the one thing Gideon had needed to hear since he was nineteen years old and counting miles instead of birthdays.

If he took the deal, the voice went back in the box. Permanently. And Crane went on buying gorges and burying men in them, one receipt at a time.

"No," Gideon said.

Deverell waited, as if the word might revise itself.

"It's not a negotiating position," Gideon said. "It's not 'no, unless.' It's just no. You can tell Crane the price is wrong. There isn't a number. Believe me, I've done the math—I do math better than anything else, it's practically all I am—and there is no figure where trading away what's in that journal comes out even."

"The file on your father—"

"Is mine to carry or burn, not his to sell me back. That's the whole point of it. That's why it doesn't work." He heard his own voice rise and brought it down. "You can't ransom a man with his own grief, Deverell. It's the one thing he'll always refuse to pay with."

For a moment the man in the gray jacket simply looked at him. And Gideon saw it happen—the hesitation, the beat he'd been promised by no one and had somehow known was coming. Deverell's eyes flicked toward the plane, toward the cargo pod, toward the duffel where the journal rode wrapped in a survival blanket like something newborn. His grip shifted on the pistol. Not raising it. Recalculating it.

Men like Deverell, Gideon thought, worked for money, but they stayed for the story they told themselves—that they were fixers, not criminals; that the paperwork of menace kept them on the right side of some line. A standoff in a logging clearing with a bush pilot who wouldn't bargain was not in the story. A gunshot was very much not in the story. Gideon watched him stand there weighing the story against the paycheck, and understood with a strange, floating clarity that the man was more frightened than he was.

Probably. Roughly. The odds were decent.

"You're making a mistake," Deverell said quietly.

"I make one or two a day. Keeps the average honest." Gideon pushed off the strut and stood square, feet planted, the way you stood on a gravel bar when the current was talking to you. "Here's what happens now. You get back in your truck. You drive back to Crane, and you tell him the Yarrow stubbornness is genetic, and it skipped a generation and landed on me twice as concentrated. Tell him the expedition's survivors are giving statements in Rivergate as we speak. Tell him Sam Okafor sent a satellite message three days ago that he thinks nobody noticed, and tell him—" Gideon smiled, and was surprised to find it wasn't a performance; it had grown from somewhere real, somewhere down near the grief and the relief, tangled together like roots. "Tell him the arithmetic finally caught up with him."

Deverell's radio crackled. He glanced down at it, and in that glance Gideon read the whole future arriving: the static, the clipped voice, the news traveling faster than any truck.

Because that was when the other plane came in.

* * *

It was the charter Beaver, the one Mara had hired at Rivergate with the last of her grant money and, she'd later admit, a truly creative interpretation of her expense policy. Gideon heard it before he saw it—the big radial drone rolling up the valley—and then it broke over the ridge to the south, fat and yellow and utterly unafraid, and dropped toward the clearing with the confidence of a machine that had never once doubted itself.

Behind it, faintly, came a second sound: sirens, far off, threading up the logging road. Regional police. Two vehicles' worth, by the time they arrived, which for the region was essentially a task force.

Deverell stood in the middle of the clearing with his pistol still low and his options evaporating around him like morning fog, and Gideon watched him perform the fastest moral inventory of his career. Run for the truck? The truck was boxed in by the landing Beaver's wingspan and a police Land Cruiser that materialized out of the treeline with the smugness of good timing. Raise the gun? Against two officers, a pilot, and whatever Mara Voss was about to become, which turned out to be a force of nature in a borrowed rain jacket.

He put the pistol on the ground. Slowly. Like setting down something fragile.

"That was well done," Gideon told him, meaning it. "Better than I expected."

"I want a lawyer," Deverell said, to no one in particular, to the clearing, to the sky.

"Everybody wants something today," Gideon said. "I wanted fuel."

The Beaver taxied to a stop and its door swung open before the prop had fully wound down, which was against every regulation ever written and entirely characteristic of its passenger. Mara Voss came out at a half-run, pale, thin, still moving like a woman whose body had recently been occupied by fever and only grudgingly returned—but her eyes were clear, and they found Gideon across the clearing with an expression he'd spend a long time failing to describe.

"You're alive," she said.

"Barely. Out of fuel, technically dead-stick, emotionally complicated. But yes."

"You absolute idiot," she said warmly, and hugged him, which in Mara's language was roughly equivalent to a medal for valor. Over his shoulder he saw Sam Okafor climb down from the Beaver's second seat, holding his satellite messenger aloft like a trophy, grinning through a face still scabbed from the raft run.

"Told you I hit send," Sam called. "Told everybody. Three days ago. Nobody listens to the tech guy until the tech guy saves everyone's lives, and then it's 'oh, Sam, how did you know,' and it's because I *read the manual*, Gerald—"

"He's been like this since Rivergate," Mara said into Gideon's shoulder. "We couldn't stop him."

The officers took Deverell's statement standing among the stumps, and he gave it in a flat, careful voice, asking twice whether he was being detained and once, oddly, whether anyone had gotten the tail number of the Beaver, as if that mattered. One of the constables—a young woman with rain-beaded eyebrows and the patient manner of someone who'd heard every version of every story the interior could produce—took Gideon's statement too, gently, and wrote down *fuel exhaustion, forced landing, no injuries*, and looked at the journal duffel and did not ask about it, and Gideon understood that she was leaving him a door and letting him decide whether to walk through it.

That decision, he suspected, was coming. But not today, and not here, and not with the adrenaline still singing in his blood like a wire in wind.

Mara stood beside him as the police loaded Deverell into the Cruiser. The Beaver idled. The woodpecker had finally quit, satisfied.

"You refused him," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"He offered me everything I've spent fifteen years building. Quiet, anonymity, a clean slate, my father's file burned." Gideon looked at the duffel in the plane's cargo pod. "Turns out I don't want the slate clean. I want it accurate."

Mara nodded slowly. "Halloway would have taken the deal," she said. "That's not an accusation. It's just—it's important that somebody took the other one. Somebody had to be the person who didn't." She touched his arm, briefly, and went to help Sam argue with the Beaver pilot about weight and balance, their voices rising in the old familiar music of people who had nearly died together and intended to keep each other alive out of spite.

Gideon stood alone a moment in the clearing, in the smell of cedar and coming rain, and let himself feel it—not triumph, nothing so clean, but something adjacent. The leverage hadn't just broken. It had reversed. Every threat Crane had owned yesterday—the file, the name, the past—was now a liability pointing the other way, because the man who'd been hiding from it had stopped hiding, and a man with nothing left to conceal is the hardest man on earth to threaten.

His father had come home the hard way, through a gorge and a cave and twenty years of silence. Gideon figured he could manage the last hundred miles by air.

He walked back to the Cessna, pulled the fuel-calculation card from the visor, and began, out of pure habit, working the numbers for the flight to Rivergate. Halfway through, he stopped, looked at the blank line where the pilot's signature went, and allowed himself a small private smile.

There'd be a name on that line soon enough. The right one.

But first, apparently, there were statements to give—and a town full of questions waiting at the end of the strip, and one question above all that he could already see forming behind Mara's clear gray eyes as she turned back toward him, journal-shaped, inevitable:

What was he going to do with the rest of the pages?
