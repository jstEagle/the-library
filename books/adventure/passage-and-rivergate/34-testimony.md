## 34. Testimony

The regional police had set up in the Rivergate community hall, which on weekdays was the library, on Sundays the church overflow, and on this particular Wednesday smelled of burnt coffee and the previous weekend's pie social. Mara Voss sat on a folding chair outside the little office they'd partitioned with two bookshelves and a curtain that had once been orange, waiting her turn, watching Sam Okafor go in looking like a man walking into an exam he hadn't studied for.

"You'll be fine," she told him.

"I've never given a statement before," Sam said. "What if I get something wrong? What if they think I'm lying?"

"Then they'll think you're a liar, and they'll be wrong, and in six months nobody will remember either way." She smiled at him. "Welcome to academia, Sam. That's peer review."

He gave her a strangled laugh and disappeared behind the curtain. Through the shelves she could hear the murmur of Constable Adeyemi's voice—patient, unhurried, the voice of a man who had learned that the fastest way through a story was to let it be slow. Sam talked for a long time. Once, his voice cracked on the words *he tried to take the box downriver alone*, and Mara looked at her hands and let him have his privacy, or the nearest thing to it a curtain could provide.

She had rehearsed her own statement on the walk over. Not the facts—those were solid, drilled into her by fever and then by three days of sleep—but the shape of them. Where to begin. How much to give. A career in archaeology taught you that a site tells you everything if you let it, but that you choose what to dig first, and how deep, and what you leave in the ground for another season, or for no one.

The trouble was that this site had a man sitting outside it in a canvas jacket, drinking bad coffee, pretending to read a fishing magazine from 2019.

* * *

Gideon Yarrow had been at the community hall when she arrived, and he had not gone in.

Mara noticed things. It was her profession. She noticed that he sat with his back to the wall, that he had chosen the chair with the clearest view of the door, that he read the same page of the fishing magazine four times without turning it. She noticed that when Constable Adeyemi had come out to say *Mr. Yarrow, whenever you're ready*, he had smiled that flat pilot's smile and said, "I'm just a charter pilot. Flew up, flew back. Everything else is their story," and tipped his head toward her and Sam.

And she noticed that when he said *their story*, something in his face closed like a hand around a match.

She understood, of course. She'd understood since the third night at the camp, when the fever had loosened something in her and she'd looked at him across the fire and said, "You have his hands," before she could stop herself. She'd watched the words land. She'd watched him decide—visibly, the way a man decides whether to check a suspicious noise—not to have heard them.

He knew who she was. She suspected he'd known from the moment he read the expedition manifest, or maybe from the moment Crane had hired him, which meant the whole flight upriver he'd been flying toward his father's grave without being told that's what it was. Or being told, in the way Crane told things, which was to say sideways, with a hook in it.

She was not going to be the one to drag it out of him. But she was also, she was discovering, not going to lie for Aldous Crane, and the line between those two positions was narrower than she'd like.

The curtain moved and Sam came out, pale but upright, like a man who'd handed in the exam and wasn't going to think about it until the grade arrived.

"Your turn," he said.

* * *

Constable Adeyemi turned out to be a woman in her fifties with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried competence of someone who had spent thirty years getting the truth out of people who'd rather give her anything else. She poured Mara coffee that was genuinely terrible and said, "Start wherever's natural. People always want to start at the beginning, and the beginning is usually the wrong place."

"The wrong place," Mara said, "is probably the funding application."

Adeyemi's eyebrows went up a millimeter. "Go on."

So Mara started there, because it was true, and because she had spent four days on a gravel bar doing the arithmetic of survival and had decided that the least she owed the dead was accuracy. She told Adeyemi about the Meridian Foundation. About the grant that had arrived with suspicious ease—no site visit, no revisions, a full funding cycle approved in six weeks when the normal wait was eight months. About Aldous Crane's representative who had briefed them in Rivergate with a folder of historical maps and one instruction delivered so casually she'd almost missed its weight: *the 2003 Yarrow expedition's route, if you happen to cross it, document it thoroughly.*

"We thought it was diligence," Mara said. "Crane's foundation had funded the original survey, back in the day. I assumed he wanted closure on an old liability. An insurance thing. I told myself a lot of stories." She wrapped her hands around the terrible coffee. "I'm an academic. Self-deception is practically a core competency."

"And when did you stop telling yourself stories?"

Mara thought about it. "The night Dr. Halloway opened the strongbox."

She told it plainly: the crate marked FRAGILE: DO NOT OPEN, which had traveled with them upriver and which Halloway, their leader, had finally pried open on the ninth night, feverish himself and convinced—Mara believed honestly convinced, though she'd never be able to prove the shape of another man's mind—that what was inside changed the terms of the expedition. Photographs. A survey map of the gorge in a hand Mara hadn't recognized at the time. And a coroner's interim report, unsigned, dated 2004, suggesting that the pilot lost in the 2003 crash had survived the impact.

"Elias Yarrow," Adeyemi said, not looking up from her notes.

"Yes."

"You knew, by then, that your charter pilot was his son."

Mara set the cup down. "I suspected. I confirmed it at the camp, when I was—" she gestured at herself, at the memory of the fever, "—not at my best. I said something I shouldn't have. He was kind about it, which was more than I deserved."

"Did Mr. Crane know?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Mara leaned forward. "Constable, I've been asking it since the gravel bar. Here's what I can give you. Crane hired us to find the 2003 site and document it. Crane hired Gideon Yarrow—under the name Gideon March, I checked the charter paperwork—to fly us in. Either Crane didn't know who he'd hired, which would make him the first careless man I've ever met, or he knew exactly, and he wanted the son to be the one who found the father." She let that sit. "And I'll tell you which one I believe. A man who funds an expedition to confirm a death so a land claim lapses—that man doesn't hire a bush pilot at random. He hires a bush pilot with a reason to stay quiet. A ghost has no standing to ask questions."

Adeyemi wrote for a while. The hall's clock ticked. Somewhere outside, the river ran high and brown and ordinary, and Mara found she could listen to it now without her chest tightening.

"There's a matter of the documents themselves," Adeyemi said at last. "The photographs, the report. The journal you mentioned in Mr. Okafor's presence."

"The journal is Mr. Yarrow's," Mara said. "By every law I know of and a few I don't. It was his father's. It was found by his father's son. If he chooses to give it to the inquiry, that's his choice, and if he doesn't, I'll testify that it exists and not one word about what's in it." She heard her own voice firm up and was glad of it. "I've spent my life telling people what dead people left behind means. I'm not going to do it to this one. He gets to decide what his father's last words are worth and to whom."

Adeyemi looked at her over the glasses for a long moment. "You're protecting him."

"I'm respecting him. Protecting is what you do to people who can't do it themselves." Mara smiled, tired. "That man flew a plane through a storm twice, ran rapids on a raft made of expedition garbage, and carried me out of hell in a seat he'd stripped of everything but the instruments. He can carry his own secrets. I just don't want anyone else's hands on them."

* * *

They gave the statements over two days, because that was how Rivergate did things—nothing in a hurry, everything thorough, the pie social's folding chairs repurposed with dignity. On the second afternoon, the foundation's paperwork arrived, subpoenaed, and Adeyemi spread it across the library tables like an excavation grid, and Mara and Sam stood over it together and watched a fraud come apart in layers, the way a midden does.

"Look at this," Sam said. He'd recovered his appetite for detail somewhere over the last week, and it suited him. "Two grant cycles, same project code. The 2003 survey was funded by Crane personally—before the foundation existed. Then the foundation funds the 2003 *recovery*, except the paperwork calls it an 'archaeological assessment.'" He tapped the page. "He never wrote down what he was actually buying."

"Because what he was buying wasn't legal," Mara said. "You don't put *confirm the death of a man whose land claim is blocking my development* on a grant application."

"And this—" Sam held up a bank transfer record, his voice going carefully neutral, "—this is a payment to a charter operator. Two payments. One dated before we went missing."

Mara looked at it. The amount was triple the going rate; she knew because she'd once priced the same route out of curiosity. The name on the invoice was G. March, Rivergate Air. The date was nine days before the first missed check-in.

"He hired the search before there was anything to search for," she said quietly. "He knew we'd go quiet. He knew the radio would fail or be made to fail. The whole thing was staged to look like an accident recovery, with a pilot who'd take the money and ask nothing." She straightened up. "Except he picked the one pilot in the interior who had every reason to ask everything."

The net, as it turned out, had been closing on Crane from several directions at once, most of them boring. The regional revenue office had been interested in the foundation for a year. A former project manager in the south had been writing letters. Mara and Sam's statements were the last stones in the wall, not the first. When Adeyemi told them, Mara felt something that wasn't quite satisfaction—more like the flat relief of a long hike's final kilometer, where the body keeps moving because stopping would be worse.

"They'll want you in the south, eventually," Adeyemi said. "For the hearing. Both of you. And Mr. Yarrow, if he'll come."

"He'll come," Mara said.

"You sound sure."

"He came upriver for strangers who'd lied to him. He'll come downriver for the truth. It's closer."

* * *

That evening the three of them ended up on the porch of the boarding house, because Rivergate had exactly one boarding house and nowhere else to be, and because after nearly dying together there was a gravity between people that no one remarked on and no one resisted. Sam had a cocoa. Mara had tea that tasted of the river it had been boiled from. Gideon had a chair at the end of the porch, angled toward the strip, where his plane sat under a tarp with a bent strut and a propeller that needed work.

"The constable says the hearing's in six weeks," Sam offered.

"Mm," said Gideon.

"You'll testify? About the flights, the payments, the—"

"I'll testify about the flights," Gideon said. "I'll testify that a man paid me triple rate to check on a silent camp, and that I found what I found. I'll testify about the raft and the rapids and the fuel drums until the lawyers' eyes glaze over." He turned his cup slowly. "I won't testify about my father."

The porch went quiet. Mara watched him the way she watched a section cut—reading the layers.

"It's not evidence," he went on, to the cup more than to them. "The journal. It's a man's last weeks. It's—" he stopped, and Mara saw him choose, deliberately, to say the true thing, "—it's the first time he's ever talked to me. Twenty years of silence and then a whole journal of it. I'm not handing that to a room full of lawyers so they can argue about land boundaries over it. Crane can hang on what he did. My father stays out of it. He was a surveyor, not a pawn. He doesn't get played one more move in somebody else's game."

"That's not how evidence works," Sam started, and Mara put a hand on his arm.

"It's exactly how it works," she said. "Chain of custody starts with a choice. He's the chain." She looked at Gideon. "The coroner's report, though. The photographs. Those came from Crane's box. Those are the inquiry's."

"Those are the inquiry's," Gideon agreed. "I burned the copies that were mine to burn. The rest can do their work. It's the difference between—" he searched, "—between a grave and a grave marker, maybe. One's for the law. One's for the family."

Mara nodded slowly. She thought of every site she'd ever closed, the tarp pinned down, the backfill tamped, the decision made about what the world got to see. Some things you published. Some things you left in the ground and told no one, and the telling-no-one was not a failure of scholarship. It was the scholarship. It was knowing the difference.

"You know," she said, "twenty years from now, some doctoral student is going to find the inquiry records and write a thesis about the 2003 expedition and the gaps in it. There will be a whole chapter about the missing journal. 'The Yarrow Gap.' They'll speculate."

"Good," Gideon said, and for the first time all week he smiled with something behind it. "Let them speculate. Speculation's free. The truth was his. He gave it to who he wanted."

* * *

Later, when Sam had gone up to bed and the porch was down to two chairs and one lamp, Mara said, quietly, "You know I'll have to tell the hearing about the fever. About what I said to you at the camp."

"I know."

"'You have his hands.' That'll be in a transcript. Some clerk will type it up."

Gideon was quiet for a while. Down at the strip, wind moved the tarp over the plane, a soft breathing sound.

"My mother's going to get a letter," he said finally. "With the last page in it. The part where he—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to; she'd read it, in the cave, by headlamp, while he read it aloud to the dark like a man returning a call twenty years late. *Tell my son I wasn't running away. I was coming home.*

"He was coming home," Mara said. "For what it's worth, I believe him. The raft was nearly done. He'd have made it."

"He'd have made it," Gideon said, "if the river had given him one more week." He looked out at the dark where the river was. "It gave me two. I keep doing the math on that and it doesn't come out to anything. It's just weather. It's just luck. But I keep doing it anyway."

"That's grief," Mara said. "It's very bad at arithmetic and it never stops showing its work."

That got a real laugh out of him, short and surprised, and she counted it a find—catalogued it, the way she catalogued everything, because some things you wanted to be able to locate again.

"Six weeks," she said, standing, gathering her cold tea and her tired bones. "The hearing. You'll come down with us?"

"Someone has to fly you," Gideon said. "You two are hopeless on a bush strip. Sam tried to help me refuel and nearly wore the avgas."

"He did mention that."

"He's a good kid. He'll make a fine archaeologist. He asks the right questions at the wrong times, which is the whole job." Gideon tipped his chair back. "Get some sleep, Dr. Voss. Constable said you're done tomorrow. Day after that, the foundation lawyers descend, and you'll want your feet under you."

Mara paused at the door. "Gideon. What you said on the porch. About testifying to the flights but not the journal." She weighed it, then said it anyway, because he'd earned it and because she was a scientist and the data wanted out. "It was the right call. For what it's worth. And I've spent thirty years being paid for my opinion of what's worth keeping."

He looked at her across the lamplight, this man who had spent twenty years being no one, and something in his face was less closed than it had been. Not open. Less closed. In her experience that was how people actually healed—not like a door, but like a river ice-out, a little at a time, from the edges in.

"Night, Mara," he said.

* * *

She was halfway up the stairs when she heard the screen door behind her and turned to find Sam on the landing, still in his boots, holding a piece of paper like it was ticking.

"You're up," she said.

"I couldn't sleep. I keep going over my statement. Did I say too much? About the box? About—" He stopped himself, and she watched him decide to say the real thing, the way everyone in this story kept learning to do. "Mara. The lawyers. Crane's lawyers. They're going to come after us, aren't they. Not Crane. Us. For talking."

"Probably," she said. "They'll be polite about it. They'll send letters with a lot of *without prejudice* in them."

"And we just—we ignore them?"

"We get a lawyer of our own. The inquiry will recommend one. And then we tell the truth again, slower, with better coffee." She took the paper from his hand—it was the boarding house's phone number for the regional legal aid office, copied out in his careful engineer's print—and gave it back. "Sam. You gave a statement that put a fraud in a courtroom and got three people off a river that was about to eat them. Whatever they send you, you've already survived worse. You survived the gravel bar. You survived my fever cooking. You survived Gideon's flying."

"Nobody survives Gideon's flying," Sam said. "They just fail to die during it."

Mara laughed, and the sound went down the stairwell and out the screen door into the dark, where the river ran high and ordinary and the tarp breathed over a broken plane, and somewhere south a man named Crane was learning what his paperwork weighed.

Below, she heard Gideon's chair scrape. He'd get up soon, she knew. He always did, last thing, and walk to the rail and look at the strip—not checking the plane, she'd realized. Checking that it was still there. That the life was still there. That the name, the new-old name he'd started using around town like a man testing ice, still held his weight.

Six weeks to the hearing. Six weeks of a grounded plane and a small town and a man learning to be somebody.

Mara went to bed thinking that the next chapter of this was going to be the hardest one yet for him: no river, no storm, no rapids to run. Just stillness, and a name, and all that time to hear it in.
