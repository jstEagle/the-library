## 35. Grounded

The mechanic's name was Dov Halprin, and he pronounced judgment on Cessna 180, registration number C-FGYX, with the gravity of a doctor delivering a terminal diagnosis.

"Cracked exhaust stack. Dented cowl. A propeller that's been through a war and lost. And whatever you did to the landing gear, don't do it again." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been gray since the previous administration. "Three weeks. Maybe four, if the parts don't come upriver on time."

"Three weeks," Gideon said.

"Four, if you keep standing there looking at her like that. Planes can feel it."

Gideon had been standing there like that since dawn, coffee going cold in his hand, looking at the little taildragger sitting in Halprin's hangar with her cowl off and her guts showing. She looked smaller than she had in the air. She looked, in fact, like what she was: an old machine that had been asked to do too much and had said yes anyway.

He had put her down on that gravel bar with the river chewing at one end of it. He had put her down overloaded in rain, and he had put her down in a logging clearing with the engine quitting like a man fainting mid-sentence. Three landings in two days, each one worse than the last, and the plane had forgiven him for all of them. Now she was grounded, and by extension, so was he.

"Three weeks," he said again, as if the words might improve with repetition.

"You keep saying that like it's a sentence. It's a vacation." Halprin patted the fuselage. "Go sleep in a bed. Eat something that didn't come out of a tin. Talk to a person who isn't me."

"I talked to plenty of people."

"Police don't count. Police are weather."

Gideon almost smiled. It was the first time his face had attempted anything in that direction since he'd landed, and the muscles complained about it, rusty hinges on a door nobody opened.

* * *

The trouble with being grounded, Gideon discovered over the following days, was not boredom. He had expected boredom. He had budgeted for boredom the way he budgeted fuel, with a margin for error. He had a stack of unread magazines, a borrowed novel from the Rivergate hotel lobby that someone had abandoned at the good part, and a standing invitation from Halprin to come watch him swear at a torque wrench.

The trouble was stillness.

In the air, or loading, or preflighting, or calculating, Gideon had never once had to sit with himself. There was always a next thing. Check the fuel. Check the weather. Check the tie-downs. Check the manifest. Twenty years of next things, stacked end to end like fence posts, and he had built his whole life along that fence without ever looking at what it enclosed.

Now the fence ended, and there was just him, in a rented room above the Rivergate general store, with a window that looked out on the strip and a silence that had teeth.

He tried the magazines. He tried the novel, which turned out to be about a sea captain's doomed love affair, and he put it down when he caught himself sympathizing more with the ship. He tried walking, which worked for about an hour at a time, right up until his feet carried him to the hangar, where Halprin would look up from under the wing and say, "Still here," in the tone other men said *still raining*.

"You could help," Halprin offered on the fourth day, in a moment of weakness or pity. "Hand me tools."

"I'll break something."

"Everything's already broken. That's the point of the building."

So Gideon handed him tools. It was not flying. It was not even close to flying. But it was a task with edges, and edges held a man together better than soft hours did. He learned which wrench was which. He learned that the exhaust stack had to come from a supplier in the south who answered letters, not telephones, and that the supplier's name was Mr. Prewitt and that Halprin had been feuding with Mr. Prewitt since 1961 over a disputed invoice for eleven dollars and forty cents.

"Eleven forty," Halprin said, tightening something with feeling. "He knows what he did."

"Maybe he forgot."

"Men like Prewitt don't forget. Men like Prewitt *file*."

Gideon held the light steady and thought, not for the first time, that there was a whole world of small wars going on everywhere, all the time, and that he had spent twenty years flying above them, and that from up there every war looked like weather.

* * *

The police came by on the fifth day, or rather, Constable Enid Marsh came by, who was the entire police presence in Rivergate and possibly in a hundred miles in any direction. She was a broad, unhurried woman with a notebook she consulted the way other people consulted scripture, and she had been patient with him during the statements, and she was patient with him now.

"Just checking in," she said, taking the chair in his rented room without being asked, which he respected. "Mara Voss is staying with the Hendersons. Sam Okafor is at the hotel, eating them out of bacon. You're in a room above a store."

"It's quiet."

"It's a store. Stores open at seven."

"It's quiet *after* seven."

She made a note of something, or pretended to. Gideon had learned in the past week that Constable Marsh's notebook was partly a tool and partly a prop, and that the real interviewing happened in the pauses between notes.

"Crane's lawyer came through yesterday," she said.

"Did he."

"Wanted to know if you'd be testifying."

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him testimony was like the weather. It comes or it doesn't, and nobody can make it." She looked at him over the notebook. "Voss and Okafor gave full statements. Crane's funding, the forged permits, the whole rotten scaffolding. It's coming down. There'll be an inquiry in the south, maybe charges. You're named in three places as the pilot who flew them out."

"I flew them out. That's all I did."

"That's what I told the lawyer." She closed the notebook. "You're not named as a Yarrow anywhere, as far as I can see. Your license says Yarrow, though. Funny thing about licenses. They follow a man around."

Gideon said nothing. Outside the window, the strip lay empty in the afternoon light, the grass along its edges going gold. Somewhere downriver the Rivergate ran high and brown with the season's rain, doing what it had always done, keeping what it kept.

"The inquiry will want a statement from you eventually," Marsh said, standing. "About the flight, not the family. You can give the flight."

"And the family?"

She shrugged, which from her was practically a speech. "Some things a man keeps. I've got a sister I haven't spoken to in nine years and I don't intend to explain her to a room full of strangers." At the door she paused. "Halprin says your plane's a write-off waiting on parts. Says you've been in his hangar every day like a dog outside a butcher shop."

"He invited me."

"He complains about you constantly. It's how he says he likes you." She put on her hat. "Get some rest, Mr. Yarrow. You look like a man who's about to make a decision, and those go better rested."

* * *

The name arrived at night, the way names do.

He had gone down to the river after supper, because walking was the only thing that still worked, and he stood on the bank where the water went by, high and fast and indifferent, carrying everything it carried. And he found himself saying it, under his breath, just to hear it in the air.

"Gideon."

Just the first part. That part had never been the problem. He had been Gideon to everyone for twenty years—Gideon the charter pilot, Gideon the quiet one, Gideon who didn't talk about where he came from. The first name was worn smooth, like a stone in a river. It was the second name that had teeth.

*Yarrow.*

He hadn't said it aloud, he realized, since he was nineteen. He had signed forms with an initial. He had let people assume. He had flown under a name that was technically his and practically nobody's, a name kept in a drawer like a suit from a funeral. And now the suit was out of the drawer, because a constable had read it off his license, and a lawyer had copied it into a file, and a mechanic shouted it across a hangar without knowing he was handling something with edges.

*Yarrow.* His father's name. His father, who had not run away. His father, who had spent his last weeks in a cave above the gorge, repairing a raft, writing a letter to a boy who was waiting by a radio two thousand miles south, a letter that ended with *tell him I was coming home*.

Gideon had read that last page so many times in the past week that he could recite it, and he had recited it, once, standing in the cave with the rain coming down outside, and Sam Okafor had pretended very hard to be checking a fuel drum. The boy had a gift for pretending. Gideon recognized the craft.

He sat down on the bank, on the cold grass, and did the thing he had been avoiding, which was nothing. Just nothing. The river went by. The light went down. A loon called somewhere upstream, that sound like a question asked in another language, and Gideon sat in the nothing until the nothing stopped feeling like drowning and started feeling like—what? Standing still. Just standing still. A man on a bank, at the end of a day, with no next thing.

It was terrible. He understood why he'd avoided it for twenty years.

* * *

On the eighth day, Halprin found him at the hangar before dawn, which was unusual, because Halprin's relationship with dawn was strictly professional and generally resentful.

"You're up early," Halprin said.

"Couldn't sleep."

"You've been up early all week. Either you can't sleep or you don't want to." He handed Gideon a mug of coffee that tasted like it had been boiled twice out of spite. "Prewitt wrote back."

"The exhaust stack?"

"The exhaust stack. And something else." Halprin dug in his coat and produced a letter, official-looking, with a crest on it. "Came addressed to the airfield. For you. Well—for *Yarrow*. First time I've ever seen mail come for that name here."

Gideon took it. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside was a single page from the regional aviation office in the south: a routine notice, the kind that went to every licensed pilot in the district twice a year. Renewal of certification. Medical examination. Proof of hours. And at the bottom, a form to be completed and returned, with a line that said, in small bureaucratic type:

*Full legal name of applicant:*

He looked at that line for a long time.

"It's nothing," Halprin said, reading over his shoulder with the total absence of boundaries that made him Halprin. "Renewal paperwork. Everybody gets it."

"I know."

"You've gone strange, you know that? You've been standing there so long the coffee's gone cold, and my coffee doesn't get cold, it gets *angrier*."

Gideon folded the letter and put it in his jacket, over the pocket where his heart was doing something it almost never did, which was announcing itself.

"The plane," he said. "How long, honestly?"

Halprin looked at the Cessna, stripped and patient under the hangar lights, and then back at him, and something passed over the mechanic's face—not a smile exactly, but the place where a smile would go on a man who'd had most of them worn off.

"Honestly? If Prewitt ships on time, two weeks. If you help, maybe less." He turned back toward the workbench. "Where you planning to fly her that's got you counting days?"

Gideon looked out the open hangar door at the strip, gold in the first light, and past it at the river, and past the river at the hills, and past the hills at the whole enormous country that had spent twenty years being his hiding place and was, he was beginning to suspect, also his home.

"Nowhere yet," he said. "But I've got a form to fill out."

* * *

That night, in the rented room above the store, with the shelves rattling faintly whenever a truck went by, Gideon laid the renewal form on the table under the lamp. He found a pen. He wrote his first name in a careful hand—*Gideon*—and then he sat with the second line for a long while, listening to the river he couldn't see, going by in the dark, keeping what it kept.

Then he wrote *Elias* after it, and his pen didn't hesitate at all, and that was how he knew.
