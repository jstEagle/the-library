## 36. Yarrow

The plane sat in the maintenance hangar at Rivergate like a patient in a hospital gown—panels off, cowling propped open, wires and hoses exposed in a way that made Gideon want to look away and check the pulse anyway.

"Still grounded," said Dot Ferreira, the mechanic, without looking up from where she stood waist-deep in the engine compartment. She was a small woman with forearms like dock rope and a habit of addressing airplanes as though they were wayward nephews. "And don't hover. Hovering makes him nervous."

"Him."

"Every aircraft's a him. They're all convinced they're immortal and they're all wrong." She wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime the previous year. "Three weeks. Maybe four. The crankshaft's fine, thank God, but you flew her through a monsoon with sand in the intake, and she's sulking about it."

Gideon had been coming by every morning for six days, ostensibly to check on progress, actually because an idle man with a grounded plane is a man with nothing between himself and his own thoughts. He'd discovered that the hangar was better than his rented room for this. At least here the silence had a job to do.

"Anything I can help with?"

"You can hold this flashlight and not offer opinions."

He held the flashlight. He offered no opinions, which cost him something. Outside, the morning was doing what Rivergate mornings did—burning off the river mist in slow sheets, the strip steaming, a heron standing in the shallows with the posture of a landlord inspecting property. Somewhere down the row of hangars a radio was playing a station that came in only half the time, so the songs arrived in fragments, as if the singer kept forgetting the words.

Dot worked in silence for a while, then said, conversationally, the way she said everything, "You know what your problem is?"

"I've got a list."

"Your problem is you don't know what to do with your hands when you're not flying. Look at you. You've reorganized my socket tray twice and I let you because it was pitiful."

He looked down. His hands were indeed holding a socket wrench he had no memory of picking up. He set it back in the tray, in the wrong slot, out of spite, and Dot made a small disapproving noise without turning around.

The truth was she'd diagnosed him correctly, and not just about the hands. Six days of stillness, and the stillness was beginning to do what stillness does: it let things surface. He'd spent nineteen years keeping himself moving precisely so nothing could. Miles over years. Keep the tachometer turning and the past stays in the mirror. Now the tachometer was at zero and the mirror was full.

He thought about his father's journal, which lived in a locked drawer in his room, wrapped in a shirt, like contraband. He thought about the last page, which he'd read so many times the creases had gone soft. *Tell my son I wasn't running away. I was coming home.* He thought about the letter he had not yet written to his mother, which sat in his head half-composed, the way unfinished letters do, taking up residence and paying no rent.

And he thought, with increasing frequency and a strange, low-grade dread, about his name.

* * *

It happened on the seventh morning, at the office of the Rivergate Aerodrome Authority, which was less an office than a desk in the corner of the general store, presided over by a man named Wilbur Trent who was simultaneously the aerodrome authority, the deputy registrar, and the only person in town licensed to witness documents, which he did with the gravity of a man notarizing treaties.

Gideon came in to sign the repair authorization. This was a formality. Dot had already done the work of estimating; his job was to put his signature on the line and let the money leave him, the way money does.

Wilbur slid the form across the counter. "Name there, and sign. And I'll need your pilot's certificate for the file, since the repair's over the threshold."

Gideon picked up the pen.

And stopped.

The line said NAME, in Wilbur's typewritten capitals, and beneath it a blank space about two inches long, and Gideon stood there looking at that blank space the way a man looks at a river he's about to swim. Because for nineteen years, the name he put on forms had been a smaller name. A serviceable name. G. Marchetti had been the name on his medical certificate, his insurance, his chart subscriptions, the registration of the plane he'd bought with money earned under that name. Marchetti had been his mother's maiden name, borrowed at nineteen like a coat from a rack, worn so long it had stopped feeling borrowed. It had fit. It had fit the way a disguise fits, which is to say well enough that you forget, until someone calls your real name across a room and you feel the seams.

He had signed a hundred forms as Marchetti. He had signed the flight plan out of Rivergate nine days ago as Marchetti. He had signed the fuel chit at the gravel bar, the raft manifest in his own head, the statement for the police—Marchetti, Marchetti, Marchetti, a signature so practiced it had become a kind of handwriting of its own, quick and closed, the letters leaning away from each other.

Wilbur waited. Wilbur was good at waiting; it was most of the deputy registrar's skill set.

"Pen's not out of ink," Wilbur offered. "It's one of the good ones. My wife sent it. She believes in stationery the way some people believe in saints."

"It's a fine pen."

"You're staring at it like it owes you money."

Gideon set the pen down. He looked out the store window, at the strip, at the hangar where Dot was rebuilding the heart of the only thing he'd ever been sure of. Then he looked at the blank space again.

Here was the thing he had not said aloud to anyone, not to Mara, not to Sam, not to the police sergeant who'd taken his statement with the flat patience of a man who has heard every possible version of every possible story: the name Marchetti had stopped fitting. Not that morning. Somewhere over the gorge, maybe, or in the cave with the journal open on his knees, or the moment he'd stood in the wreckage of the camp and understood that his father had spent his last weeks doing the same arithmetic Gideon did in his sleep—weight, fuel, distance, the price of getting home. Elias Yarrow had been coming home. And Gideon had spent nineteen years flying in circles around a word he wouldn't say.

*Yarrow.*

It wasn't even that heavy a name. Four letters after the G. A weed, technically. His mother had told him once, when he was small, that yarrows grew in the worst soil there was and bloomed anyway, white and stubborn, and that she'd chosen the name for him before he was born because she liked the sound of it and because she hoped he'd be hard to kill. She'd gotten her wish in ways she hadn't intended.

"Gideon," Wilbur said. Not impatiently. Wilbur had known him for years, in the way Rivergate knew people—shallowly, kindly, without questions. "You all right? You've got a look. My father used to get that look at the bank."

"I'm fine."

"You're holding the counter."

He was. He let go of it.

The thing was, he'd already decided. He'd decided somewhere over the ridge on the climb-out, overloaded and half-blind in the rain, when the only name he'd been able to think of—the name he'd said to himself, silently, like a prayer or a checklist item, the way pilots say *airspeed, wings level*—had been his own. He'd decided again when the sergeant asked him to state his full name for the record and he'd heard himself say "Gideon Marchetti" and felt the lie go in like a splinter, small and deep and certain to fester. He'd decided a third time last night, lying awake, when he'd understood that the letter to his mother could not be signed Marchetti, because his mother was the one person alive who would know exactly what it meant if it was.

He just hadn't decided *here*, at a counter, in front of a pen.

"You know," Wilbur said, in the tone of a man filling silence the way other men fill potholes, "I once knew a fellow who changed his name three times. Ran a hotel in the territories. Said a name was just a handle on a suitcase—you can swap it out as long as the contents stay the same." He paused. "I always thought that was nonsense, personally. I think the handle matters. You can't carry a suitcase without it."

"No," Gideon said. "You can't."

He picked up the pen.

He wrote, in careful block letters, the way you write something you want to be legible to whoever finds it in fifty years:

GIDEON ELIAS YARROW

The middle name was the part that undid him. He hadn't planned it. His hand had simply kept going after *Gideon*, because his full legal name had always been Gideon Elias Yarrow, because his mother had named him for a man she believed was dead, and because writing *Elias* felt less like signing a repair authorization and more like introducing two people who should have met years ago.

Wilbur looked at the form. Then at Gideon. Then at the form again, with the expression of a man doing long division in his head.

"Yarrow," Wilbur said.

"That's right."

"There was a surveyor up the Rivergate, years back. Yarrow. My father used to talk about him. Said he drew maps of country nobody else would walk into." Wilbur's eyes came up, and there was no accusation in them, only the mild, unhurried attention of a man who had spent decades watching people sign things and had learned to read the signing. "Any relation?"

Gideon thought about all the answers available to him. *No. Distant. I don't know.* He had spent nineteen years reaching for answers like those the way other men reached for light switches, automatically, in the dark. He found that he was tired of reaching.

"My father," he said.

The word came out steady. That surprised him. Inside, something was not steady at all—something was rearranging itself, the way furniture rearranges when you finally move a piece that's stood in one spot so long it's left a pale rectangle on the floor.

Wilbur nodded slowly. He took the form, examined it, and did something Gideon did not expect: he took out his own pen—the good one, the wife-sent one—and ruled a small, neat line through the word NAME at the top of the form and wrote above it, in careful capitals, LEGAL NAME, as though the form itself had been in error all these years.

"Well," Wilbur said. "Welcome to the file."

* * *

The rest of the bureaucracy took an hour, and Gideon found, to his astonishment, that he enjoyed it.

This was not a thing he had ever expected to enjoy. He had spent his adult life treating paperwork as weather—something to be endured, flown around, waited out. But there was a pleasure in it he hadn't anticipated, a dry, almost comic satisfaction, in changing his name one document at a time. The medical certificate renewal, which required a drive to the regional office and a nurse named Prudence who listened to his heart and said "well, whatever you've been doing, keep doing it." The insurance rider, which required a phone call to a man in a distant city who kept calling him "Mr. Yarrow" with the cautious respect of someone who suspected the name might be famous, and Gideon letting him think so. The registration transfer for the plane, which was the big one—the deed, the true one—and which he filled out at Dot's workbench while she watched over his shoulder with open, unembarrassed curiosity.

"You're doing it, then," she said. "The whole thing. Name and all."

"Whole thing."

"Going to be a pain. Every license, every logbook. You'll be correcting paperwork until Christmas."

"I know."

"And half the outfits you've flown for will have to be notified. The ones who knew you as Marchetti." She handed him a torque wrench, handle first, like a nurse handing over an instrument. "Some of them are going to ask questions."

"Some of them already have."

"What do you tell them?"

He considered the logbook open on the bench, the columns of hours he'd flown under the borrowed name—ten thousand of them, give or take, each one a mile stacked against a year. He thought about the version of the story where he told the truth in full: the scandal, the flight, the father, the gorge, the journal in the drawer. And he thought about the version where he told almost nothing, because almost nothing was, as it turned out, enough.

"I tell them I got my father's name back," he said. "That's all of it that's anybody's business."

Dot nodded, satisfied, and went back to the engine. But a minute later she said, without turning around, "Your old man would've liked the plane, I think. The way you fly her. Careful, but not timid. There's a difference."

Gideon looked at her back, at the small shoulders working under the coveralls, and decided that Dot Ferreira had just given him the nicest thing anyone had said to him in nineteen years, and that he would not tell her so, because she would put down her wrench and look at him and it would be unbearable.

"Thank you," he said anyway.

"Don't make it weird," said Dot.

* * *

That evening he sat in his rented room with the window open to the river smell and did the last of it: the flight plan.

It was a form he'd filled out hundreds of times. Route, aircraft, fuel, souls aboard, pilot's name. He filled in the route—Rivergate to the strip at Two Mile and back, a checkout flight, nothing, a loop around the valley like a man walking the fence line of his own life. Fuel: two hours. Souls: one.

At the line marked PILOT, he wrote *Gideon Elias Yarrow* and found that his hand no longer hesitated. The name went down smooth and whole, all three parts of it, and looking at it he felt something that was not quite happiness and not quite grief but lived in the neighborhood of both, the way a river lives between its banks.

His mother had signed him into the world with that name. A nurse had written it on a bracelet. His father had never seen it—had been gone before the birth certificate was filed, off drawing maps of country nobody else would walk into—and Gideon had spent half his life angry about that and the other half pretending the anger was fuel, burning it in the engine, getting miles out of it. Now the journal sat in the drawer with its soft-creased last page, and the anger had nowhere to burn, and what was left, when he let the fire go out, was just a son holding a name like a suitcase with a good strong handle.

There was one thing left to do with it. One person who needed to hear it.

He took a sheet of paper from the drawer—the hotel stationery, thin and cheap—and set it on the desk, and uncapped the pen, and wrote the date. Then he sat there for a long time while the heron outside stood in the shallows and the radio down the row played half a song and lost the rest.

*Dear Mom,* he wrote.

And stopped, because the next words were the ones he'd been circling for six days, and because he understood now that the letter was not really about the journal, or the gorge, or even his father. It was about the name. It was about telling the woman who gave it to him that he'd finally picked it up.

*Dear Mom. I've been meaning to write for nineteen years, and I have a lot to tell you, but I'm going to start with the easiest part, which is also the hardest:*

He looked at the line. He knew how it ended. He'd known for days.

*My name is Gideon Elias Yarrow, and I'm coming home.*

He set down the pen before his hand could shake, folded the unfinished letter once, and put it in the drawer on top of the journal, where it would wait for morning—because some cargo, he was learning, you carry only as far as you have to, and then you set it down.
