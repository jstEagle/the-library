## 37. The Letter

The general store in Rivergate sold three kinds of paper: a spiral notebook with a cartoon trout on the cover, a pad of yellow legal sheets that had gone brittle in the sun of the front window, and a box of cream-colored stationery that nobody in living memory had purchased. Gideon bought the stationery. He also bought a pen, because the one in his shirt pocket had been leaking altitude for weeks and now wrote only when it felt like it, which was a quality he admired in engines and could not abide in pens.

"That's four dollars," said Dot Fennick from behind the counter, ringing him up without looking at what he'd chosen. Then she looked. Her eyebrows performed a small, silent ascent.

"Writing a letter," Gideon said.

"To who?"

"My mother."

Dot was seventy if she was a day and had run the store since before the strip had gravel instead of mud, and she possessed the particular courage of small-town clerks everywhere, which is to say none whatsoever about her own opinions. "Well," she said. "That's nice." She slid the stationery into a paper bag as though it were something perishable. "You want an envelope? Those are separate."

"I figured."

"Two for a dollar. Or five for two dollars."

"That math doesn't—"

"It's the envelope math," Dot said firmly. "It's been that way since 1987 and I'm not going to be the one to fix it."

He took five.

* * *

Back at the boarding house — Mrs. Calloway's, where he'd been sleeping for eleven days while the plane sat in Halvorsen's hangar with its cowling off and its soul exposed — Gideon cleared a space on the little desk under the window. This required moving a lamp with a frayed cord, an ashtray he didn't use, and a copy of *Field & Stream* from March of some year that had already been read by every man in the building twice. The desk surface, once revealed, proved to have a map of Lake Superior burned into it by some previous tenant's carelessness with a cigarette, which felt appropriate. Everything worth doing happened over maps.

He sat down. He uncapped the pen. He wrote:

*Dear Mom,*

Then he stopped.

This was, he reflected, the entire problem in miniature. Nineteen years of silence did not fit on cream-colored stationery. There wasn't enough paper in Dot Fennick's store, or possibly in the territory, to bridge the distance between *Dear Mom* and everything that came after it. He looked at the two words for a long time. They sat there being true and insufficient at once, like most words.

He tried again on a fresh sheet.

*Dear Mom, I don't know if you remember me, I'm the one who ran away.*

No. Too much like an apology wearing a joke as a coat, and his mother had never once laughed at a joke that was really an apology. She'd look straight through it the way she used to look through his excuses about homework, with those gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave selectively.

Third sheet.

*Dear Mom, You probably have questions.*

Understatement, he decided, was not going to carry this letter either.

He got up and made coffee he didn't want, because that was what you did when a thing wouldn't start — you performed readiness around it until it got embarrassed and began. Downstairs, Mrs. Calloway was arguing on the telephone with her sister about a pie recipe, a dispute that had apparently been running since Thursday and showed no signs of resolution. Through the window he could see the strip, and past it the river, running high and brown and ordinary, carrying whatever it carried without comment.

His father had been carrying a letter too, at the end. That was the thing about the last entry that had lodged in Gideon like a splinter — not the raft, not the weeks of survival, but the sentence: *tell my son I wasn't running away, I was coming home.* Elias Yarrow had spent his final weeks writing to a boy of nineteen across twenty years and a gorge, and the letter had never been sent, and the boy had grown into a man who answered mail from no one.

Well. One of them could break the pattern.

* * *

Fourth sheet. He made himself write fast, before the internal censor woke up.

*Dear Mom,

I've started this letter four times, which you would know if you could see the wastebasket, and which you can't, so you'll have to take my word for it. I'm told honesty is the place to begin, so here is some: I am writing to you from Rivergate, in a boarding house that smells like woodsmoke and burnt toast, drinking coffee that would strip paint, and I have been here eleven days because my plane is in pieces in a hangar and for the first time in nineteen years I am not going anywhere, and it turns out that when a man stops moving long enough, all the things he outran catch up and sit down across the table from him like they've paid for the meal.

One of those things is you.

I know why I left. I told myself it was the scandal, the name in the papers, the lawyers, the way people at the funeral looked at us like we were a weather event instead of a family. All of that was real. But underneath it there was a simpler thing, which is that Dad went into the gorge and didn't come out, and I couldn't stand to be somewhere that reminded me of him, so I went somewhere that didn't remind me of anything at all. I flew medicine and mail and fuel drums for nineteen years and I counted miles instead of years because miles add up clean and years don't. I became very good at not being found. It's the one skill I have that I never wanted.

Here is the part I need you to hear, and I need you to hear it before anything else, because everything else depends on it: Dad didn't die in the crash. Not right away. He survived, and he lived for weeks after, and he built a raft to come home on, and he wrote in his journal that he wasn't running away — that he was coming home. To us. I have his handwriting in front of me as I write this and it hasn't changed since the grocery lists. Still slants left. Still presses so hard you can read the page underneath.

I'm enclosing a copy of the last page. I'm keeping the rest of the journal for now, and I'll tell you why, or I'll try: there are men who spent money trying to prove he died in that gorge, for reasons that have to do with land and lawyers and nothing that has to do with him, and the journal is evidence, and evidence is a heavy thing to carry, and I have carried it up a river and down a river and I intend to put it down in the right hands and nowhere else. But the last page isn't evidence of anything except that a man loved his family at the end of a hard road, and that page belongs to you.

I'm sorry it took me nineteen years and a flood and a wrecked airplane to say any of this. I'd tell you I was busy but you raised me and you'd know.

There's more. There's a lot more, and some of it is funny — you'd like the woman who runs the general store here, she sells envelopes at a loss if you buy four of them — and I promise I will tell it to you in person, because I am done saying important things at a distance. That's the other thing this letter is: notice. I'm coming to see you. Not yet — the plane needs wings and I need to be a man who shows up at your door with something better than nineteen years of silence in his pockets. But soon.

Tell me how you are. Tell me if the garden still floods every spring. Tell me anything. I'll answer.

Your son,
Gideon Elias Yarrow*

He read it through twice. His eyes snagged on *the scandal* and he almost softened it, almost explained, almost gave her the whole legal architecture of the Yarrow mess that had driven him north — and then he made himself leave it. She'd lived through it too. She didn't need it summarized back to her; she needed to know he knew she'd been standing in the same rain.

The signature was the part that scared him. Three names. He hadn't written the middle one since he was a boy signing school forms, and seeing it now in his own hand — Elias sitting there inside him like a passenger — did something to his chest that he chose not to examine closely, on the grounds that pilots who examined things too closely at the wrong altitude tended to stall.

* * *

Copying the journal's last page took longer than writing the letter. He did it by hand rather than photograph it — Mara had offered to transcribe it properly, with archival notes, and he'd said no, and she'd understood immediately in the way she understood most things, which was faster than he liked. A photocopy was a document. Handwriting was a voice. His father's hand pressed hard enough to read through the page; Gideon copied it pressing just as hard, tracing each word like a man walking a ridge line he'd only ever seen from above.

*I have the raft half done. Two more days of good weather and I think she'll float. If whoever finds this goes upstream along the east wall there is water that runs sweet and game trails that hold. I am not lost and I am not hiding. Tell my son I wasn't running away — I was coming home. Tell him the compass points home fine if you know which way home is. I always knew. I just ran out of days.*

Gideon set the pen down and sat with his hands flat on the desk for a while, the way you do after a hard landing, letting the machine finish shaking before you unbuckle.

The compass itself was on the cairn in the gorge, left there in chapter — no, he corrected himself, in fact, in fact and not in chapters — left there because a man can't carry everything, and some things belong to the place that made them. But the sentence about the compass he could carry. He suspected he'd be carrying it for the rest of his life, and that this was not a burden but ballast.

* * *

Mara found him at the post office counter the next morning, which was also the general store counter, because in Rivergate the post office was a wooden tray and a rubber stamp on the end of Dot's counter, between the penny nails and the fishing licenses.

"You're mailing something," she said, with the tone of a scientist observing a rare migration.

"A letter."

"To whom?" Sam asked, appearing from the direction of the hardware aisle with a coil of rope he had no conceivable use for. Sam had been haunting Gideon's vicinity for a week now, ostensibly helping with the plane, actually conducting a quiet apprenticeship in how to be alive after nearly not being. He'd developed the habit of materializing whenever anything interesting happened, holding equipment.

"My mother."

Both of them went quiet in the particular way of people who know exactly what that means and are deciding whether to say so. It was Mara who finally said it. "You're telling her. About Elias."

"I'm telling her the last page. The rest stays with the statements, the police, the lawyers, wherever it ends up. She gets the part that's hers."

Sam turned the rope over in his hands. "Is that enough? For her, I mean."

Gideon considered the question honestly, because the kid had earned honest answers at roughly the rate of one per near-drowning. "No," he said. "But it's a start, and starts are the only thing I've ever been good at delivering on time."

Dot stamped the envelope with more ceremony than the transaction strictly required, licked her thumb, and peered at the address. "That's a long way," she said. "She know you're coming?"

"Not yet."

"She'll know when she reads it." Dot handed the letter back to him. "You'll want to mail it yourself. Box is outside. And Gideon—" she lowered her voice to the register reserved for medical news and gossip of consequence, "—for what it's worth, my mother waited thirty-one years for a letter from my brother. Got it two weeks before she passed. She told me it was worth the wait, but I'll tell you what I told him: the waiting was the part that cost. Don't make her pay it twice."

"No, ma'am," Gideon said, and meant it down to the bone.

Outside, the morning was doing that high-country thing where the light comes down clean and sideways and makes even the mud look intentional. He dropped the letter in the box and stood there listening to it land — a small sound, paper on tin, nineteen years ending in less than a second.

"So," Sam said beside him, having followed him out with the rope, which Gideon now realized was simply Sam's version of a security blanket. "When the plane's certified. When you go see her. Could I—" He stopped, restarted, the way he did when the sentence mattered. "I've never been anywhere. Properly. I mean, I was somewhere, obviously, the gorge, but that was more of an involuntary experience."

"You're asking for a ride."

"I'm asking for a ride," Sam confirmed. "To meet your mother. Who I have never met. Which is insane. Forget I said it."

Gideon looked at the kid — twenty-three, sunburned, alive because of a rope and a raft and a stubbornness he hadn't known he owned until the river demanded it — and thought about cargo. Some you carry so you can set it down. Some you pick up on purpose.

"The plane seats three," Gideon said. "Ask me again when she's certified."

From the hangar across the field came the sound of Halvorsen's air wrench spinning down, and then Halvorsen himself, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since approximately the previous administration, calling across the grass: "Yarrow! Get over here. You're going to want to see what the inspection found — and then you're going to want to sit down."
