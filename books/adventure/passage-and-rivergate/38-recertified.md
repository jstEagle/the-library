## 38. Recertified

The inspector's name was Delia Fenn, and she arrived in Rivergate on a Tuesday with a clipboard, a thermos of coffee the color of creek water, and the expression of a woman who had personally witnessed every mistake a pilot could make and expected to witness them again before lunch.

"Yarrow," she said, reading off her paperwork at the strip. "Gideon Yarrow."

"That's me."

"Huh." She looked up. For a moment he thought she was going to say something about the name—everyone in Rivergate had said something about the name since the Crane business broke open, most of it kind, some of it merely curious—but she only tapped the clipboard against her thigh. "Cessna 185, tail number Charlie-Kilo-Foxtrot?"

"The same."

"She took a beating on that gravel bar, from what I hear."

"She took worse in the air," Gideon said. "The bar just finished the job."

Fenn grunted, which Gideon would learn over the next three days was her version of laughter, sympathy, approval, and doubt, all delivered with the same tonal flatness, like a man reading weather reports to a room full of insomniacs.

The inspection took two days because Fenn was thorough in the way that only people who genuinely love machines can be thorough—not suspicious, exactly, but unwilling to let anything pass unexamined. She pulled the cowling and ran her fingers along the firewall looking for cracks the repair shop might have missed. She drained fuel samples into a glass jar and held them up to the light like a jeweler checking for flaws. She sat in the pilot's seat without starting anything and worked the controls through their full travel, listening.

"You did your own preflight?" she asked at one point, head buried under the panel.

"Every morning for eleven years."

"And you checked the elevator cables how?"

"By feel, mostly. And a mirror."

"Mm." She emerged, wiping grease from her hands with a rag that was already more rag than it had been an hour ago. "Feel is good. Feel keeps you alive. But feel doesn't get written down." She handed him the logbook. "From now on, everything gets written down. You're flying passengers soon—I hear. People who trust you with their lives don't want to hear about your feelings about the cables. They want ink."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Don't ma'am me. I'm not anybody's ma'am." She went back under the panel. A pause. Then, muffled: "Good work on the fabric patch aft of the door, though. Whoever did that has patient hands."

"I did it. Took me four tries."

"Only shows if you know where to look." Another grunt. This one, Gideon decided, was approval.

* * *

On the third morning, Fenn signed the paper.

She did it without ceremony, which somehow made it heavier. She filled in the date, signed her name in handwriting that slanted hard to the right like something running downhill, stamped the inspection certificate, and slid the whole stack across the counter of the strip office to where Gideon stood.

"There," she said. "She's airworthy."

"Just like that?"

"Just like that." She capped her pen. "You sound disappointed."

"No. It's—" He picked up the certificate. The paper was ordinary. The weight of it wasn't. Eleven years of flying under a name that wasn't quite his, and now a government document said *airworthy* next to the name Gideon Elias Yarrow, and there was no asterisk, no footnote, no sealed file waiting in some sponsor's drawer. "It's been a strange season."

"So I heard." Fenn packed her clipboard away and screwed the lid onto her terrible coffee. At the door of the office she stopped, half-turned. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much, I've known a lot of pilots—a man who grounds himself when he should fly, or flies when he should ground himself, usually can't tell the difference between the two. But you can. I watched you check that plane six times this week when she was already signed off. That's not fear. That's respect." She shrugged. "Keep both. Lose neither."

Then she was gone, walking out to the truck that would take her back to the coast, leaving Gideon alone in the office with a certificate, a logbook, and a Cessna sitting on the ramp in the last of the dark, dew silvering the wings.

He didn't fly that day. He'd planned to—he'd told himself he would, wheels up within the hour, straight up the valley—but instead he found himself doing small unnecessary things. He wiped down the windshield twice. He reorganized the tie-down kit. He sat in the pilot's seat for twenty minutes with the doors open and his feet on the gravel, watching the sun come up over the ridge and thinking about nothing in particular, which was itself so unusual that it felt like a skill he'd have to practice.

Ruth Okonkwo found him there around mid-morning. She owned the diner at the end of the strip road and had adopted Gideon sometime during the second week after the flood, in the way that certain women adopt certain men—not through any declaration, but through the appearance of extra food, unsolicited opinions, and increasingly personal questions.

"Well," she said, hands on hips, surveying him and the plane. "You look like a man who got given permission to do something and then didn't do it."

"It's only been a few hours."

"A few hours is how long it takes a coward to talk himself out of it and a fool to kill himself doing it too fast." She handed him a paper bag that smelled like cinnamon buns. "You're neither, as far as I can tell, but why risk becoming either? Go tomorrow. Dawn. Fly it the way you want to fly it, not the way you're proving something."

"How do you know I'm proving something?"

"Gideon." She said his name—the whole name, first time anyone in town had used it casually, like it had always been there. "Everybody in this town knows exactly what you're proving and who you're proving it to, and honey, none of us are the audience."

She left him with the buns and the truth, which was, he reflected, a fair trade.

* * *

Dawn came up gold and cold, the way it did in late summer when the river ran high and the air above the water still held the night's chill. Gideon was at the strip before the birds finished tuning up. Preflight by flashlight, then by daylight, then once more by feel, just because Ruth had been wrong about one thing—it wasn't proving anything, it was habit, and habit was the only religion he'd ever kept faithfully.

Fuel: full. Weight: just him, no cargo, no drums, no strongbox, no ghosts in the baggage compartment except the ones he carried in his chest, and those didn't count against the useful load.

He wrote it all down. Ink, per Fenn. Time, fuel, destination. In the destination blank he paused, pen hovering, and then wrote: *Local — gorge and return.* There was nobody to hide it from anymore. There hadn't been for months. The habit of hiding was the last thing left to retire, and he figured retiring it ought to be done in writing, where hiding had always lived.

Engine start was clean. Oil pressure came up green. He taxied out past the windsock—it hung lazy and forgiving in the early calm—and lined up on the strip with the sun full in his eyes, gilding the dew on the grass on either side.

"Charlie-Kilo-Fox," he said to himself, out loud, the way he always did, the ritual call to an empty frequency. "Gideon Yarrow, departing Rivergate, climbing through the sunrise, heading upriver."

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

The takeoff was smooth, the old girl lifting off with the easy confidence of a machine that knew it had been loved back together. He raised the gear-less belly clear of the trees, turned north, and followed the river.

* * *

The Rivergate gorge announced itself long before you saw it, the way big country does. The valley walls began to lean in. The river below changed its voice—you couldn't hear it at altitude, but you could see it, the white stitching of rapids running through the green like a seam of lightning frozen mid-strike. Twenty years ago, a boy had sat at a radio in a farmhouse kitchen waiting for a voice from this country, and the voice never came, and the boy had grown into a man who measured his life in miles precisely so he would never have to measure it in waiting.

Gideon flew toward it at a steady cruise, hands light on the yoke, and let the country come to him.

There was a temptation, and he acknowledged it honestly, the way Fenn would have wanted: the temptation to descend. To drop down into the gorge and look. To find the cairn from the air, the cave mouth, the gravel shelf where a raft had been built by a dying man who was coming home. To look *down*, the way he'd looked down at that country his whole life—from above, briefly, at arm's length, the way you look at a photograph of someone you lost.

But Ruth had said fly it the way he wanted to fly it, and what he wanted, he discovered as the gorge mouth opened ahead of him like a gate, was not to look down.

He wanted to look *along*.

That was the difference, and it was everything. Looking down was grief. Looking along was direction. His father had died in that gorge trying to travel along it, downstream, toward home, toward a son at a radio. Every mile of the river between here and there was a mile Elias Yarrow had meant to cover and hadn't. So Gideon covered it. Not for him—that was done, that was settled, the journal read aloud once to the rain and then folded away—but because the miles were there, and somebody might as well fly them with their name attached.

The walls rose on either side. He gave the rock a respectful half-mile of clearance; the gorge had eaten enough Yarrows, and he was not interested in adding to the total. The morning light came down the canyon at a low angle, setting the rapids ablaze and leaving the deep pools black as ink. He flew the centerline of the current, the way the water went, the way a raft would have gone, the way home went.

Halfway through, somewhere above the stretch Sam had named White Water with the grim precision of a man who'd nearly drowned in it, Gideon caught himself talking. Out loud, to the plane, to the river, to whoever rides along in a cockpit at dawn.

"There's the bend," he said. "You'd have seen it about here. Right where the wall opens up on the east side. Good sight line. Bad water." A pause. The engine droned. "Sam made it through there on a raft made of tent poles and stubbornness. You'd have liked him. He argues with me, but he does the math first, so it's arguing worth having."

The river glittered below, indifferent and beautiful, running high and ordinary, the way rivers do after they've taken everything they're going to take.

"Mara says the university wants to put a marker at the camp. Something dignified. She's fighting the wording—they want 'lost,' she wants 'stopped here.'" Another pause. "I voted for 'stopped here.' Stopped implies they were going somewhere."

The gorge walls fell away behind him. Ahead, the valley opened wide and green and gentle, the river spreading out calm and brown-gold, running easy toward Rivergate, toward the strip, toward breakfast and paperwork and whatever came next. Gideon rolled out of the gentlest turn the Cessna had ever flown and set a heading downriver.

Not looking back. Looking along.

* * *

He landed at Rivergate forty minutes later, greased it on so soft the tires barely spoke, and taxied in with the sun fully up and the town stirring—smoke from Ruth's chimney, the freight dock waking up, a kid on a bicycle standing on the pedals to watch the plane come in, the way kids had watched planes come in here for fifty years and would for fifty more.

Ruth was waiting at the end of the strip road with two coffees and the expression of a woman collecting a debt.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"Don't you 'well what' me. How was it?"

Gideon climbed down, set his boots on the gravel of his own strip, in his own town, under his own name, and considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"It was a good flight," he said finally. "Short one. Nothing dramatic. Checked the engine, checked the river, came home."

"That's it?"

"That's it." He took the coffee. It was terrible, which meant it was hers, which meant it was perfect. "Turns out that's what closure looks like from the air. Mostly it looks like a normal flight."

Ruth studied him for a long moment, then nodded slowly, satisfied, and turned to go. She made it three steps before the radio in the strip office crackled to life behind them, loud enough to reach them both across the morning air.

*"Rivergate strip, Rivergate strip, this is Piper Six-Seven-Zero inbound from the coast, two souls aboard, requesting fuel and a word with a Mr. Yarrow—we were told you're the man to ask about charter work up the valley."*

Gideon and Ruth exchanged a look.

"You going to answer that?" she asked.

He looked at his plane, freshly certified, still ticking as it cooled. He looked at the river, running high and ordinary toward the sea. He thought about a boy at a radio, waiting for a voice that never came, and about a man at a radio, answering voices that did.

Gideon walked into the office, keyed the mic, and said yes.

*"Piper Six-Seven-Zero, Rivergate. Welcome in. Tell your souls we've got fuel, coffee, and room for two more."*
