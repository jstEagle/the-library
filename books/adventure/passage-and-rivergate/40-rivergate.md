## 40. Rivergate

The river came up in May the way it always did, without apology and without drama, the way a large animal turns over in its sleep. By the second week it had swallowed the willow flats below the town, turned the swimming hole at Miller's Bend into something brown and muscular, and climbed the pilings of the old freight dock until the top step of the ladder disappeared. The kids in Rivergate counted the steps like a countdown to summer. Six steps meant school was nearly over. Four meant the fish were running. This year it got to three before anyone stopped counting.

Gideon Yarrow noticed the river the way he noticed everything now—not as a threat, not as a clock, but as a thing that was simply going on. That was the strangest part of the whole season, if he was honest. The world had kept moving. The water kept rising and would keep falling. The mail still needed carrying, and the mail did not care what a man had burned in a gorge two hundred miles north, or what he had left on a cairn, or what name he had written on a flight plan in a hand that shook only a little.

He was carrying the mail this morning. Some things a man didn't hand off, even when he could.

The strip at Rivergate was dirt and gravel and, after the spring melt, a certain amount of mud that the town council had been arguing about paving since before Gideon was born. He taxied to the fuel bowser with the patience of long habit and shut the Cessna down in the smell of warm engine and cut grass. Marty Ellsworth was already there, as Marty was always already there, leaning against the bowser with a coffee thermos and the expression of a man who had been waiting not for Gideon specifically but for anyone at all.

"You look terrible," Marty said.

"Good morning to you too."

"No, I mean it. You look like a man who slept." Marty unscrewed the thermos cap and offered it. "It's unsettling. You used to look like a man who'd been awake for a week and liked it that way."

Gideon took the coffee. It was terrible, burnt and over-sweet, the way Marty had made it for eleven years, the way Gideon had drunk it for eleven years. "I've been sleeping," he admitted. "It's the strangest thing. You just lie down and then it's morning."

"Sounds like a scam."

"I keep waiting for the catch."

Marty topped off the tanks and they stood together in the sun, watching the river. A barge was working its way up from the junction, riding high and empty, its pilot leaning on the rail. Down at the dock, old Mrs. Calloway was selling smoked fish to tourists off the weekly packet boat, and her voice carried across the water in the particular flat carrying shout of a woman who had been out-shouting rivers her whole life.

"They're saying the gorge road's washed out again," Marty said. "Third year running. County says they'll fix it in the fall."

"They said that three years ago too."

"They did. And here we are, unfixed." Marty screwed the cap back on the tank and patted the wing. "Your girl's flying sweet, though. I saw you come in. That was a nice landing."

"It was an ordinary landing."

"From where I stood it looked like poetry."

"From where you stood," Gideon said, "you were looking at the wrong end of a coffee thermos."

But privately, in the place where he kept things he didn't say out loud, he agreed. The plane flew sweet. The overhaul had cost him nearly everything the Crane job had paid, and then some, and the inspector—a narrow, humorless man named Prewitt who read manuals the way other men read scripture—had signed off on it with only two notes in the whole logbook, both of them the kind of note that meant *I looked, and it was right*. Gideon had framed the inspection sticker in the office at the hangar, not because it was beautiful, but because it was the first document in twelve years with his full name on it that he hadn't flinched at signing.

*Gideon Elias Yarrow.* It still looked strange sometimes, like a jacket that fit but that he'd forgotten he owned.

* * *

The hangar office was a converted shipping container with a desk, a filing cabinet, a cot he almost never used anymore, and a wall where he'd pinned the sectional chart of the Rivergate country. The chart was old, one of the last print runs, and the blank space at the north end of the gorge—the space that had been blank when he was nineteen and flying his first solo, the space that had been blank when his father flew into it, the space that had been blank for reasons Gideon now understood had less to do with surveying than with money—had been filled in. Not by the government. By him, in pencil, over the winter, working from memory: the gravel bar, the bend below the gorge mouth, the cave, the cairn. He'd labeled the cairn in small letters. *E.Y. 2003–2003. Longer than it says.*

Mara Voss had seen it once, when she'd come through in March on her way to a conference in the south, and had stood in front of it for a long time without saying anything, and then had said, "You know they'll want to name something after him. When the university sorts out the claim. There's a whole process."

"There's a whole process for everything," Gideon had said.

"He'd have hated that. A process."

"He'd have hated the attention more."

"He was your father," Mara had said, not unkindly. "You're allowed to guess wrong about him."

She'd left him a postcard from the conference—a picture of a museum, of all things, with a note on the back that said only: *Sam says hi. Sam also says the raft was a death trap and you owe him a beer for life. He's right on both counts.* Gideon had pinned the postcard to the wall next to the chart, and it had been there ever since, curling slightly at the corners.

The radio on the desk hissed and crackled through the morning the way it always did—Marty's wife calling the store about a flour order, the packet boat calling the dock, a float plane out of the junction reporting traffic, all of it layered over the long patient static of a country that was mostly empty and liked it that way. Gideon answered what needed answering and let the rest run past him. He'd gotten better at that. Letting things run past.

At noon he flew the mail run south to the junction and back, an hour and a half each way, and the country unrolled beneath him in its spring colors—brown water, green banks, the black slash of burned timber from a fire two summers back already stitching itself over with new growth. He flew the way he'd always flown, which was to say he flew well, but the inside of it had changed. For years the flying had been a way of not being anywhere in particular, of putting distance between himself and a name. Now the miles still counted, but they counted toward something. He'd tried to explain it to Sam on the phone once and Sam had said, "So what you're saying is you used to be a ghost with a pilot's license," and Gideon had said, "Yes, exactly," and Sam had said, "Man, you cannot say that out loud to normal people," and they'd both laughed until it turned into the other thing, the quiet thing, the thing two men who had shared a gravel bar and a river and a near-drowning didn't need to put into words.

The fuel drums were gone from the gorge. The county survey crew had flown them out in June by helicopter, along with the expedition's remaining equipment, which the university had claimed and the police had released and Crane's lawyers had, through a long and losing series of letters, attempted to claim instead. The strongbox itself—the empty one, dented, salt-rusted, its lock punched through by a pry bar on a night in a tent while rain came down—sat on the shelf in Gideon's office behind the coffee tin. Mara had told him he should throw it away. He'd told her some cargo you carry so you can set it down, and you set it down somewhere you can see it.

She'd looked at him for a moment and said, "You know, that's the first thing you've ever said that sounded like him."

Gideon hadn't asked how she'd known what Elias Yarrow sounded like. She'd spent a season with the man's ledger and his maps and his handwriting, and some people you get to know through their marks on paper. He understood that better than most.

* * *

July came in hot and dry, which everyone said was wrong for the season and which everyone also said, in the next breath, was good for the flying. The strip dried to concrete hardness. The river dropped three steps, then four, then five, and Mrs. Calloway moved her fish table down the dock to follow it.

Gideon took on charters. That was the new thing, the thing that still surprised him a little every time the radio called for it. He'd spent twelve years carrying boxes and fuel and the occasional reluctant government surveyor, and now there were people who wanted to *go* places—fishermen up to the lakes, a geologist and her two students out to the limestone country, a wedding party of nine that had required two trips and a great deal of patience regarding a dress that would not be folded. He found he didn't mind the passengers. He found, in fact, that he liked pointing things out to them—the bend where the herons fished at dawn, the slide scar on the west ridge, the place where the river went quiet before the gorge—and that when they asked him questions about himself he had answers now that were true and small and cost him nothing.

"Been flying this route long?" the geologist had asked.

"Eleven years."

"You from here originally?"

"Up and down it, mostly," he'd said, which was true, and which was also, these days, the whole truth, because a man who had chosen his name could afford to be generous with the rest of it.

Sam came through in August with a duffel bag and a sunburn and a job offer he was pretending was casual. "There's a dig starting next season. Lower gorge, below the cave. Mara's running it. They need somebody who knows the river." He said it to the ceiling of the hangar, examining a rivet with great interest.

"You hate the river."

"I respect the river. It's different." Sam picked at the rivet. "Also I'd be lying if I said the pay was bad."

"You'd be lying about several things, then."

"Look, all I'm saying is, a man with a plane and a working knowledge of the gorge could do worse than a season of flying supplies for people who say please and thank you." Sam finally looked at him. "Mara thinks you'll say no."

"Mara's usually right."

"She said you'd say that too." Sam grinned, and it was the grin from the raft, from the bad arithmetic and the good rope work, from the night in the cave when Gideon had read his father's last page aloud and Sam had sat very still in the dark and not said a single wrong thing. "She also said, and I quote, 'tell him the cairn needs a new cairn stone before winter, and I'm not carrying it.'"

The stone was in the back of the hangar now, wrapped in a feed sack. Granite, fist-sized, flat on one side. Gideon had cut the letters himself with a cold chisel and a great deal of swearing, and he'd flown it up in September, on a clear cold day with the air smooth as glass, and set it on the cairn with his own hands. The new stone said what the old pencil label said, only shorter, only the part that mattered: *E.Y. Coming home.* He'd stood there a while in the wind. Then he'd flown home. It had been, he reflected on the way back, the shortest trip and the longest landing of his life.

* * *

October came, and the river ran high and ordinary, and on a Tuesday morning with frost on the grass and woodsmoke standing straight up from every chimney in town, the radio on the desk crackled at an hour when it usually had nothing to say.

"Rivergate base, Rivergate base, this is junction dispatch."

Gideon set down his coffee. "Go ahead, junction."

"Got a request for you. Doctor out of the junction, needs a run up to the Kettle Falls clinic this afternoon—medicine and a nurse, light load, back by dark. You available?"

Gideon looked out the hangar door. The strip was dry and gold in the low sun. The Cessna sat in it, fueled, inspected, sound. The river moved past the town in its long brown patience, going about its business, keeping what it kept and carrying the rest along. Somewhere north of all that, under a cairn on a ridge above the water, a man was resting who had spent his last weeks building a raft to come home to a son who had spent twelve years building a life out of getting away. Neither of them, Gideon thought, would have called it a tragedy anymore. It was just distance, finally closed.

"Rivergate base," he said, and picked up the pencil, and wrote his name at the top of the flight log—*Gideon Elias Yarrow*, in a hand that didn't shake at all—before he even answered.

"Affirmative, junction. Tell the doctor I'll take it."

He finished the coffee, folded the log closed, and walked out into the cold bright morning to go to work.
