## 1. The Last Contract

The rain in Puerto Ladeado had been falling for nine days, and Petra Reyes had learned to measure his life by rains the way other men measured it by years. This one was a lean rain, a hungry rain, the kind that filled the gutters with brown water and the pawnshops with wedding rings. He watched it from the doorway of the Cantina del Faro, where the ceiling leaked in three places and the fourth leak was a man named Ochoa, who arrived wherever money changed hands the way damp arrived through stone.

Ochoa took the table by the window without asking. He always did. He was a small man built like a knotted rope, with a face so ordinary that witnesses could never describe it, and he wore a linen jacket that had never once been wet because weather understood its place around men like him.

"Reyes." He said it the way you'd read a serial number off a dead man's boot.

"Ochoa." Petra didn't sit. Sitting meant negotiating, and negotiating meant wanting, and he had spent five years teaching himself to want nothing in front of anyone who might sell the information.

"You look terrible."

"I've been drinking the local water."

"That's not what I heard." Ochoa smiled with the bottom half of his face only. "I heard you've been drinking everything else too. Sit down, Reyes. I have work."

The word *work* moved through Petra's chest like a fishhook finding flesh. He sat down anyway, slowly, making it look like a concession rather than a surrender. The waitress came and Ochoa ordered two coffees without consulting him, which was another small violence, another way of saying: I already know what you'll accept.

"I don't do retrieval for consortium people," Petra said. "You know that."

"This isn't consortium."

"It's always consortium. You're their broom, Ochoa. When they want something swept under a rug, they call you."

"And yet here we are." Ochoa wrapped both hands around his cup as though it were warm, though it wasn't. "Six weeks ago, a scientific expedition went up the Río Sombra. Eight people. Glaciology, hydrology, some biology. Led by a Dr. Anders Halvorsen — Norwegian, respectable, published. They had a satellite schedule. Every Tuesday, a check-in ping. They missed four."

Petra turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. A habit. Aligning things when he couldn't align anything else.

"So the client wants them found."

"The client wants them *located*. Found is your problem after that. Coordinates, condition, a report. That's all."

"And if they're dead?"

"Then coordinates and photographs. The insurance requires proof."

The rain ticked against the window glass like a man tapping out a code no one wanted to translate. Petra looked at the street beyond it — the flooded gutter carrying a dead parrot downstream, the boys playing football barefoot in six inches of water, the mountains at the end of every avenue standing gray and indifferent behind the rain curtain. He had come down out of those mountains five years ago with two body bags and a reputation in flames, and he had never once gone back up, and every day since then he had told himself this was a choice.

It was getting harder to tell the difference between a choice and a cage.

"What's the pay?" he asked, and hated how quickly his voice went to it, how thin the pretense of reluctance had become.

Ochoa named a number.

Petra made himself wait before answering. Three seconds. Four. Long enough to seem like a man doing arithmetic instead of a man drowning who'd just been thrown a line. The number would clear his debts in Puerto Ladeado — all of them, even the one to the clinic, even the one he'd sworn he'd die before he let anyone know about. It would buy him a season of being nobody in particular, which was the only thing he'd wanted for five years, and it would buy it outright.

"It's illegal," Petra said. "No permit, no registry. If I go up that river on contract and someone finds out—"

"No one will find out. That's rather the point of me." Ochoa produced an envelope from inside the dry miracle of his jacket and set it on the table between them. Half now, the gesture said. The rest on delivery. "There's a condition attached."

"There's always a condition."

"If you find survivors, you make contact and you report back. You don't extract. Not until the client says so."

Petra frowned. "That's backwards. Retrieval means bringing them out."

"Location first. Extraction later, maybe. The client is — cautious about the optics." Ochoa sipped his coffee and grimaced, as if the coffee had personally disappointed him. "There are questions about this expedition, Reyes. About who's really on it, and why. The client prefers answers before commitments."

*Questions.* In Petra's experience, *questions* was what powerful people called the things they already suspected and didn't want written down.

He reached for the envelope. Stopped. Because there was one more thing, the thing that had been sitting in his chest since Ochoa first said the name, and he needed to say it aloud before his hand closed around the money and made him complicit in his own forgetting.

"Río Sombra," Petra said. "Why does the client care about the Río Sombra? There's nothing up there. No mines, no timber concessions worth the road. It's a dead-end drainage in the middle of nowhere."

"Is it?"

"You know it isn't. Nobody spends this kind of money on a dead-end drainage." He leaned forward. "So tell me. Who's the client?"

Ochoa looked at him for a long moment, and something almost like pity crossed the knotwork of his face — or perhaps it was only indigestion; with Ochoa it was impossible to say.

"You were in the Cordillera del Viento," Ochoa said. "Five years ago. The San Rafael traverse."

The room seemed to tilt a degree, the way a boat tilts when something large passes underneath it.

"Don't," Petra said quietly.

"Avalanche. Two clients dead. Your license revoked, your name on every blacklist from here to Denver. The consortium that ran the traverse operation washed their hands of you very publicly." Ochoa turned his cup another quarter-turn, mirroring him, mocking him. "And the same consortium has recently acquired, through eleven shell companies I will not name in public, the survey rights to the entire upper Sombra watershed. So yes, Reyes. You know the river. And the river knows you. That's not a bug in my plan. That's the whole design."

The rain came harder against the glass. Somewhere behind the bar a radio played a cumbia song about a woman who left, and the singer sounded like he understood exactly how Petra felt.

"They sent me," Petra said slowly, working it through, "because if this goes wrong — if I disappear up that river like the Norwegians did — nobody asks questions. A blacklisted guide dies in the cordillera, that's not news. That's *correction*."

"I prefer the term *deniability*."

"It's the same word in a nicer shirt."

Ochoa spread his small hands. "You can refuse. Genuinely. I'll walk out of here, and next month you'll still owe the clinic, and the year after that you'll still be guiding tourists up tourist hills for tourist wages, and the mountains you actually loved will still be closed to you. Or you can take the envelope, go up the Sombra, and come back with enough money to start over somewhere the name Reyes doesn't smell like smoke." He stood, buttoning the impossible jacket. "I'm not your enemy, Petra. I'm just the weather. You've been waiting five years for weather to change. Here it is."

He left coins on the table for the coffee, which was the most honest thing Petra had ever seen him do, and then he was gone into the rain, and the door swung shut behind him, and the envelope lay on the scarred wood between the salt shaker and the stain shaped like a country no one recognized.

Petra didn't touch it for a long time.

* * *

That night he laid out his gear on the floor of his rented room, which was less preparation than confession. Everything he owned that mattered fit on two wool blankets: rope, six years old but sound; a rack of hardware gone dull with humidity; the tent with the patched corner; the stove that lit on the third try if you spoke kindly to it; boots resoled twice, the leather cracked at the flex points like old paint. He checked each piece the way a priest checks his conscience, item by item, and found himself cataloguing not equipment but absences — the satellite phone he'd sold, the good parka he'd sold, the camera he'd sold last winter to cover a week when the fever went through the barrio and medicine cost more than food.

Five years. He measured them the way he measured everything now, in distances. Five years was roughly nine thousand kilometers of trail walked at tourist pace, four hundred clients walked safely up hills that couldn't hurt anyone, and zero meters above the snowline. Five years was the exact distance between the man who had stood on the San Rafael traverse with the mountain roaring off its shoulder and the man kneeling now on a cold floor counting carabiners.

They had never proved it was his fault. That was the wound under all the other wounds. The inquiry had been inconclusive — the weather records ambiguous, the route notes contradictory, the consortium's own safety officer suddenly transferred to a post in another hemisphere and unavailable to testify. Inconclusive was worse than guilty. Guilty gets a sentence. Inconclusive gets a rumor, and rumors don't have release dates.

Two clients dead. Elena Vasquez, thirty-one, a botanist who talked to her samples. Marcus Webb, forty-six, who had paid double for the summit and gotten exactly what he paid for, though not the way anyone intended. Petra had dug for both of them with his hands until the rescuers pulled him away, and the last thing he remembered of that mountain was the color of the debris field, which was the color of nothing, the color the world turns when it stops pretending to care.

After that, the blacklist. After that, the lowlands. After that, this.

He packed by lamplight while the rain kept on, and near the bottom of the pack, wrapped in a plastic bag inside a sock, he put the photograph. He hadn't looked at it in over a year. He didn't look at it now. It was enough to know it was there — Elena and Webb and Petra at the trailhead, squinting into sun, all three of them still believing the mountain was a place you went to come back from.

He zipped the bag. The number Ochoa had named sat in his head like a lantern, and he hated that it warmed him, hated that hunger was the truest thing left in him, more true than grief, more true than principle. A man can survive on grief. He'd done it for five years. But grief doesn't pay the clinic, and the clinic had stopped extending credit in the spring.

*Reluctant*, he told himself. *You're doing this reluctantly.*

The lie fit poorly, like boots two sizes wrong, but he'd walked farther in worse.

* * *

In the morning the rain had stopped and the river of Puerto Ladeado — the real river, the lazy brown one that gave the town its reason — had risen to lick the pilings of the market bridge. Petra stood on the bridge with the envelope in his inside pocket and the pack on his back and studied the boats moored along the near bank, looking for the one thing he trusted less than money: a man who knew the water.

He found Tomás on the third boat, or rather Tomás found him, calling up from beneath a tarp he was mending: "You're staring at my hull like it owes you money, friend. It doesn't. Only I owe it money."

Tomás was sixty-something and built like the river itself — wide, slow-seeming, and full of currents you didn't want to argue with. His boat was a long wooden craft with an outboard older than most governments and a hull patched in seven places, each patch a story he'd tell you for the price of a beer.

"I need to go upstream," Petra said. "Río Sombra. As far as the gorge."

Tomás stopped sewing. The needle hung in the tarp like a small silver exclamation point.

"Sombra," he repeated. "Nobody goes up the Sombra this time of year. Water's dropping fast. By the solstice you'll be portaging more than floating, and past the gorge there's no floating at all, only walking, and past the walking—" He shrugged elaborately. "Past the walking is the mission country. The people up there don't love visitors."

"I've heard."

"Heard from who? Nobody comes back from up there to tell anybody anything." Tomás squinted at him, taking inventory the way boatmen do — the boots, the rope coil, the way a man stands, weight balanced for altitude. "You're a mountain man. Mountain men don't hire boats. Boats are for people going *down*."

"I'm going up."

"To the gorge."

"Past it, eventually. But you can leave me at the gorge."

Tomás was quiet for a moment. Downstream a heron lifted off the shallows on prehistoric wings, and the sound of the market came across the water — fish thrown on ice, a woman arguing cheerfully about plantains, life conducting itself with total indifference to the fact that Petra Reyes was about to spend the last of his credibility on a river he should never have agreed to see again.

"My cousin's wife's brother," Tomás said at last, "worked a supply run up the Sombra, oh, eight years back. Before the trouble. He says there's a village up there that isn't on any map, and a mission house older than the republic, and people who watch the water the way bankers watch interest. He also says the river's named right. Sombra. Shadow. Things go up that river and the shadow keeps them." He resumed sewing, eyes down. "Triple rate. Half now. And I turn around at the gorge whether you're standing on the bank or swimming."

"Done."

They shook on it, and Tomás's grip was dry and strong and final, a contract in itself.

Petra walked back along the bridge toward the provisioner's, and the morning light broke open over the eastern ridges, and for one unguarded second he saw the high country shining up there beyond the haze — white shoulders, blue shadowed valleys, the whole vertical world he'd been exiled from — and his heart did the thing it always did, the lurching, traitorous, animal thing, reaching toward the heights like a plant toward light.

The Río Sombra came down out of that whiteness. Somewhere along its length, eight people had gone silent. Somewhere along its length, the consortium that had eaten his life was quietly buying water rights through eleven shells, and Ochoa's unnamed client wanted answers before commitments, and the only man expendable enough to go ask was the man whose name already smelled like smoke.

He should refuse. Some clear, cold voice in him, the voice that had once kept clients alive below seracs, said it plainly: *Walk away. Nothing up that river is yours. Not the money, not the truth, not the dead.*

But the clinic existed, and the debts existed, and the mountains existed, shining and forbidden, and a man can only stand on a bridge measuring his life in distances for so long before he needs to close at least one of them.

Petra bought rice, fuel, salt, and a new headlamp he couldn't afford, and by noon the envelope was lighter by half and his pack was heavier by twenty kilos, and the arithmetic of his life had narrowed, as it always did, to the next step.

Tomorrow they'd cast off at dawn. Upstream. Into the shadow.

That night he dreamed of snow, and in the dream it was falling upward, and Elena was laughing at it, and when he woke the rain had started again, patient as a debt, drumming on the tin roof like fingers counting down.
