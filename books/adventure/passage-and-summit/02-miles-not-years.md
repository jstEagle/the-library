## 2. Miles, Not Years

The scale in the back of Ferreira's outfitter shop had not been calibrated since before the war, which was fine by Petra Reyes. He didn't want precision. He wanted weight — the honest, dumb arithmetic of a pack on a hook, numbers that never lied about what they cost.

Forty-one kilos. He nodded at it as if it were an old acquaintance.

"Rope's wet," Ferreira said from behind the counter, not looking up from his ledger. "Everything here is wet. You want dry rope, you go to the capital and pay dry-rope prices."

"Wet is fine. It'll be wet where I'm going anyway."

Ferreira grunted, which in the dialect of Puerto Ladeado shopkeepers could mean *agreed*, or *you're a fool*, or *the rain will start again by four*. Petra had learned to read all three meanings in the same grunt over five years of coming through this town, and he still couldn't always tell them apart.

He laid his gear out on the scarred plywood table by the window while the afternoon light came down green through the banana leaves outside: tent, fly, groundsheet. Stove and two days of fuel, because he trusted rivers more than fires. Medical kit rebuilt from scratch — sutures, tourniquet, the good blister tape, a bottle of iodine that had gone the color of strong tea. Six days of food packed for nine, because nine was what these jobs always turned into. The map Ochoa's courier had left him, folded so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth, the Río Sombra drawn in surveyor's blue like a vein someone had cut.

And the rope. Forty meters of it, damp, coiled tight. He weighed it in his hands longer than he needed to.

Five years ago he would have checked every strand against the light, run the sheath through his fingers centimeter by centimeter, looking for the glaze of internal damage, the soft spots that meant a core broken under a pretty skin. Five years ago his clients' lives had hung off his inspections like fruit off a branch, and he had treated each knot as a small covenant with God.

Now he just coiled it and stowed it, and told himself the checking was a formality, and knew he was lying, because his hands went through the whole ritual anyway, slow and thorough, while the rest of him pretended to be somewhere else.

That was the thing nobody warned you about grief. It didn't take your competence away. It hid inside it.

* * *

Puerto Ladeado was a town built on the wrong side of waiting. Everything here faced the river — the cantina, the fuel depot, the church with its salt-eaten bell — as if the town itself were a passenger who'd been standing at the dock for forty years, sure the boat was coming. The boat never came. The boats went the other way, upstream, into the green wall of the cordillera, and they came back smaller and quieter than they'd left.

Petra walked the waterfront road with his debts arranged in his head the way he arranged gear: heavy items close to the spine.

Fourteen hundred to the clinic in Corrientes, carried now for three years, accruing interest at a rate the owner pretended wasn't usury out of some courtesy between ruined men. Two hundred to Ferreira himself, unspoken but real, folded into today's prices. Ninety to the woman who ran the rooming house, plus tonight. And beneath all of it, unpayable, unlisted, the debt that had no currency: two names, two families, five years of silence that no amount of money had ever been able to touch.

The fixer's envelope held eight hundred now and promised twelve hundred more on delivery. Enough to clear the clinic. Enough to eat through the winter. Not enough to justify anything, if you did the math honestly, which was why Petra had stopped doing the math honestly sometime around midnight two nights ago, when he'd signed for the advance with a pen that skipped.

*You know what this is,* Ochoa had said, or rather his courier had said, a boy of maybe nineteen with a courier's flat eyes and a gun he probably didn't know how to use. *Retrieval. Find them, confirm alive, mark the location, come back. No contact required. The client doesn't want drama.*

*And if they're dead?*

*Then you document it and come back. Same fee.*

Same fee. As if death were a line item. Petra had taken the envelope anyway, because hunger makes philosophers of no one; it only makes employees.

But it was the name that had done it. Not the money. Ochoa's man had said *Río Sombra* and something behind Petra's sternum had shifted like ballast sliding in a hull, and he'd heard himself say yes before the part of him that kept accounts could raise a hand.

He knew that river. He had spent one season of his life trying not to know it.

* * *

That night, in the rooming house under a fan that turned with the conviction of a dying animal, Petra slept the way he always slept in Puerto Ladeado: badly, and then not at all, and then in the deep place where the mountain waited.

It was raining in the dream. It was always raining.

Cerro Blanco, six years back. A legal commercial expedition, permits stamped, insurance current, everything correct on paper — that was the joke everyone told afterward, that everything had been correct on paper. Four clients, two assistant guides, and Petra at the front reading the mountain the way he'd read it ten thousand times: wind out of the west, loading the east faces, temperature hovering at the line where snow stops being snow and starts being cargo. They were two hours below the col, roped in two teams, moving through the white murk above the treeline, when the slope above them let go.

People said avalanche and thought of thunder, of drama. What Petra remembered was silence inside the roar — a pocket of quiet, half a second, where the whole world held its breath, and in that half second he saw Elena Vasquez turn her head toward the sound, unhurried, almost curious, a woman looking up to see what the weather was doing. Then thirty thousand tons of the mountain arrived, and curiosity was over.

They dug for eleven hours. They found Marcus Almeida first, or what the mountain had left of him, folded into the debris like a letter someone had given up writing. Elena they found at dawn, twenty meters downslope, her ice axe still in her hand, still planted, as if she'd tried to arrest the entire slide alone. She was the one who haunted him. Not because she died — guides die, clients die, the mountains keep their own books — but because of what came after, when the consortium's lawyers took the incident apart bolt by bolt and reassembled it into a story where the avalanche was Petra's fault.

*Route selection inconsistent with conditions.* *Failure to observe standard turnaround protocols.* *A pattern of risk tolerance incompatible with certification.*

None of it true. All of it documented. The consortium's lawyers wrote reports the way avalanches moved — total, impersonal, leaving nothing standing — and Petra learned too late that the report had been commissioned before the bodies were cold, because the route Cerro Blanco's east face guarded was the same drainage the consortium wanted for its access corridor, and a dead guide with a blackened name made the opposition to their road look like negligence instead of resistance.

Two clients dead. One guide destroyed. One valley opened for business.

He had stood at the inquiry with his hands at his sides and told the truth, the whole plain truth, and watched it land in the hearing room like coins down a well — sound, then nothing. The lawyers had the mountain. He had only his voice. Afterward his license was suspended pending review, and the review became a delay, and the delay became permanent, and the trade that had been his body and his craft and his name became a thing other people discussed in the third person while he carried loads illegally across borders for men like Ochoa.

Grief, he had learned, was mostly logistics. Grief was learning to sleep facing the door. Grief was weighing rope you no longer trusted yourself to weigh.

* * *

He woke before dawn to the fan clicking through its last rotations and lay listening to the rain — real rain, on the tin roof, the town's oldest music. In the gray light he did what he always did on the morning before a job: he counted.

Not money. Miles.

This was a habit from the years after, though it felt older than that, like something he'd been born with and only discovered later. His life had become a ledger of distances. Four hundred kilometers to the coast the winter his suspension was finalized. Sixty-two kilometers of jungle track to the village where he'd spent a month repairing a footbridge for food and a roof. Eleven hundred kilometers north, and eleven hundred back, chasing a rumor of work that turned out to be a rumor of work. Kilometer by kilometer, year by year, he had measured out the shape of his exile, and the shape was a circle, always returning to towns like this one, border towns, river towns, places where a man with no papers worth showing and no name worth saying could still find someone willing to pay cash for legs and lungs.

Five years. He tried to think of them as time and failed. Time was what happened to people who stayed put. For Petra the years had collapsed into distance — miles, not years — until he sometimes caught himself answering the question *how long has it been?* with a number of kilometers, and watching the asker's face go carefully blank.

He dressed in the dark. Boots softened by rain seasons, trousers with the knees gone thin, the wool shirt that smelled permanently of woodsmoke no matter how many rivers it crossed. Last, the map. He spread it on the floor by the window where the light was beginning, and looked at the Río Sombra running blue through the green blankness of the upper valley, and let himself feel, for exactly as long as it took the kettle to boil, the thing he refused to feel any longer than that.

Because here was the truth he'd been circling for two days: he wasn't going up that river for twelve hundred pesos. Twelve hundred pesos could be found anywhere. He was going because the Sombra was the far tributary of the same watershed Cerro Blanco stood over. Because the consortium's maps from those years had shown a dashed line running up this exact valley, a line labeled *Phase II — deferred*, and Petra had spent five years telling himself the deferral meant safety, and some animal part of him had never believed it. Because if the consortium was in the Sombra now — and Ochoa's client, whoever paid a fixer to send a deniable guide after missing surveyors, smelled like consortium the way wet rope smells like river — then the mountain he had fled had grown a new face and turned around to find him.

Or none of that. Or it was just a job, and the scientists had lost a radio to a landslide, and he'd walk up a beautiful river, collect a fee, and walk back down.

He poured the kettle water over the last of his coffee grounds and drank it standing, scalding, tasting mostly of tin.

Outside, the rain had thinned to mist. Down at the waterfront, the boats were waking — tarps coming off engines, the smell of two-stroke and wet wood, a boy slapping water out of a bilge with a length of tire. And tied third along the dock, riding low and patient, was a long wooden boat with a canopy frame and an outboard older than the boy bailing it, and beside it a broad man in a rubber apron smoking the day's first cigarette, watching the river with the expression of someone who has concluded a long negotiation with it and settled on wary respect.

Petra had seen the boat yesterday. Had seen the man, too, and asked Ferreira about him in the sideways way you ask such questions in a town like this.

*Tomás,* Ferreira had said. *Runs cargo as far as the gorge. Won't go past the gorge. Won't say why. Cheapest rate on the water and twice as reliable as the ones who'll talk to you.*

*Why won't they talk to me?*

Ferreira had shrugged his shopkeeper's shrug. *New face. Bad season. Take your pick.*

Petra finished the coffee, shouldered the forty-one kilos, and went down through the mud to the water, where the river was high and brown and moving with the patience of something that had already decided where everything was going.

Tomás looked at him, then at the pack, then at him again, and took the cigarette out of his mouth.

"How far?" Tomás asked.

"As far as you go."

"Everyone says that." Tomás flicked ash into the brown water. "Then we get to the gorge and they discover they are poets."

"I'm not going past the gorge," Petra said. "I need the water below it. Three days up, maybe four if the current argues."

Tomás studied him a moment longer — the boots, the rope coil, the map case worn shiny at the corners — and whatever arithmetic he performed behind his eyes, it came out acceptable. He jerked his chin at the boat.

"Load it yourself. Fuel's extra. And Reyes—" He paused, and something moved through his face, quick as a fish under the surface. "You're the one from the Blanco thing. The guide."

The mist drifted between them. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered in the cordillera like a man turning over in his sleep.

"I'm a guide," Petra said.

Tomás looked at him for a long moment, and then at the river, and threw the cigarette butt into the current, where it spun once and vanished.

"Get in," he said. "Before I change my mind."

Petra swung the pack down into the hull, took the painter, and pushed off, and the river took them both — up, into the green dark, toward the gorge and whatever the water had been keeping.
