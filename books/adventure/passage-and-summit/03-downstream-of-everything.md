## 3. Downstream of Everything

Tomás Ferreira smelled of diesel and river mud and, beneath both, of something older — the green rot of the flooded forest that the Río Sombra carried down from the high country every year like a debt being paid in installments. Petra found him at dawn on the third day in Puerto Ladeado, sitting on the gunwale of a flat-bottomed boat with a tin cup of coffee balanced on his knee, watching the water as if it owed him an explanation.

"You're the one asking about upriver," Tomás said without turning around.

"I'm the one asking about upriver," Petra agreed.

"Then you can buy me breakfast first. Nobody goes past the bend on an empty stomach. It's bad luck, and worse manners."

They ate at a place with no name, where the fish came fried whole and the coffee came boiled twice, and Tomás asked his questions sideways, the way river people asked everything — never *where are you going*, but *how many days of food*; never *who are you looking for*, but *how many will there be when you find them*. Petra answered what he could and lied about the rest, and by the way Tomás's eyes narrowed over the rim of his cup, he knew the boatman had counted the lies and filed them away for later.

"Six weeks ago a scientific expedition went up your river," Petra said finally. "Eight people. They stopped calling in."

"Rivers stop calls all the time." Tomás set down the cup. "Satellites don't work in the gorge. Radios drown in the rain. Six weeks is nothing. Six months is nothing. The Sombra keeps its own calendar."

"The people who hired me don't believe in calendars. They believe in invoices."

Something moved behind Tomás's face — not quite a smile, not quite a flinch. "And who are these people?"

"Bad ones."

"Bad enough that you won't say their name?"

"They didn't give me one."

That was true, and it was also the most honest thing he'd said all morning, and Tomás seemed to weigh it the way he'd weigh a stone before trusting it to hold an anchor. He looked out at the river for a long moment. The Río Sombra ran brown-green and muscular through the town, wide here as a lake's arm, carrying branches and silt and the smell of places Petra had spent five years trying to forget.

"Two hundred a day," Tomás said. "Half now. I go as far as the Gorge of Bells and no farther. Past the gorge the river belongs to other people, and I have a daughter who likes her father breathing."

Petra counted the money out from Ochoa's envelope, and Tomás counted it again, slower, and then shook his hand with a grip like wet rope.

"We leave in an hour," Tomás said. "Bring less than you packed. The river punishes optimists."

* * *

The first two days were almost kind.

The Sombra in its lower reaches was a working river, and they passed through a world of small commerce and smaller courtesies: dugout canoes loaded with plantains, a floating bar moored to a dead ceiba tree, children diving for coins off a rickety pier while their mothers washed clothes on the stones and shouted warnings nobody intended to obey. Tomás knew everyone. He traded cigarettes for news, news for fuel, fuel for a sack of oranges, and everywhere he went he introduced Petra with the same economical phrase: *"Va río arriba"* — going upriver — as if that explained everything, which among river people, it did.

Petra watched the banks change. The cattle pastures gave way to secondary forest, the forest to old growth, the old growth to walls of green so dense and vertical they seemed less like vegetation than architecture — buttressed trunks like cathedral pillars, lianas stitched between them like rigging, the canopy closing overhead until the daylight came down filtered and green as light through sea water. The air thickened. His shirt never dried. At night the insects sang in frequencies that felt less heard than felt, a vibration in the teeth, and once, close to midnight, something heavy moved through the undergrowth on the bank and neither he nor Tomás said anything about it, because some things were better left unclaimed.

He measured the trip the way he measured everything now, in distances. Forty kilometers the first day. Fifty-five the second, against a current that grew more opinionated by the hour. He had learned this habit in the years after the avalanche: never count time, count ground. Time was where grief lived. Ground was just ground.

But on the third morning, forty minutes past a bend Tomás called La Horqueta, the routine broke.

"There," Tomás said, and cut the engine.

The lower camp sat on a gravel shelf where a side stream braided into the main channel — a place any expedition would choose, high enough to flood-proof, flat enough for tents, with clean water and a clear line of sight up and down the river. Petra saw it from two hundred meters and understood why Halvorsen had picked it, and then he saw the rest, and understood why his employers had sent him instead of a lawyer.

The tents were still standing. That was the first wrong thing. Two dome tents and a larger mess shelter, all of them pitched properly, guyed against weather — and abandoned. Not collapsed, not torn, not dragged. Abandoned, with the flysheets still staked, as if the occupants had walked away from a functioning camp and never come back.

Tomás brought the boat in slow, nosing the shallows, and Petra stepped onto the gravel with the particular heaviness he always felt crossing from water onto evidence.

"Give me an hour," he said.

"I'll make coffee," Tomás said. "I've seen camps before, señor. I'll look at the river while you look at the ghosts."

The camp told its story in the order Petra chose to read it, and he made himself read slowly, the way he'd been trained — because the mountain taught patience or it killed you, and rivers, he suspected, kept the same syllabus.

Cooking gear: stacked and clean, pots nested, the stove fuel canister three-quarters full. Food stores: lashed under a tarp, intact, untouched by animals, which meant the camp hadn't been empty long enough for the forest to find it — or the forest had been discouraged. Boots by the tent doors. A pair of reading glasses folded on top of a field notebook, left exactly where someone had set them down after a last evening of reading.

Nobody abandons their glasses. Nobody walks out of a working camp leaving the stove fueled and the notebooks open. Either they left in a hurry — and nothing here showed hurry — or they left because someone told them to, and told them firmly enough that they took nothing.

Petra crouched at the edge of the camp, where a flat stone served as a table, and found the radios.

There were three of them. A satellite unit, a VHF base set, and a handheld backup — a professional expedition's full complement, the kind of redundancy that cost money and bought peace of mind. All three lay on the stone in a neat row, side by side, like exhibits.

All three had been smashed.

Not dropped. Not drowned. Smashed — deliberately, thoroughly, with a rock or a machete back or something with weight behind it. The satellite unit's casing was stove in, its antenna snapped at the root. The VHF set had been opened like a dissected animal, its circuit board cracked down the middle, the battery compartment crushed flat. The handheld had taken the least damage and even so its screen was spiderwebbed and its transmit key driven inward, jammed, dead.

Petra knelt there for a long time, the river talking behind him, the green light shifting overhead.

Weather didn't do this. Weather drowned radios, soaked them, buried them in mudslides, swept them downstream in floods. Weather was chaotic and indifferent, and its damage looked like chaos — scattered, random, half-finished. This was orderly. Three radios, arranged in a row, destroyed one after another by someone with time and intention. And then left in plain sight, not hidden, not buried. Displayed.

A message. Not to the expedition — the expedition was gone. To whoever came next.

*Don't try to call anyone.*

He sat back on his heels and let the dread arrive, because he'd learned that too: you couldn't outrun it, so you might as well let it land and take its measure. Six weeks of silence wasn't an accident. It wasn't a broken radio or a dead satellite pass over the gorge. Someone had made sure the Halvorsen expedition could not speak to the outside world, and then the expedition had vanished anyway, leaving behind clean pots and folded glasses and a row of murdered machines.

Rescue assumed accident. Retrieval assumed misadventure — lost, injured, stranded, dead. What Petra was looking at fit none of those shapes.

This was an investigation. He'd been hired to find out what happened, and more importantly — he turned the thought over, feeling its edges — to find out *quietly*. Why else send a blacklisted guide off the books? Why else pay in cash through a fixer? If the consortium wanted its people rescued, it would call the authorities, mount an official operation, notify families. Instead it had hired a man whose testimony no court would credit, a man with a ruined name and no institutional ties, and pointed him upriver like a probe into a wound.

If he found survivors, he was to bring them out. If he found bodies, he was to document them. And if he found something else — something the consortium didn't want documented — then Petra understood, with the clarity of cold water, exactly how expendable his contract made him. A blacklisted guide who died investigating a mystery on an illegal river run would generate no questions at all. He would be a footnote to a footnote. The perfect instrument: deniable, disposable, and already discredited in advance should anything he said ever surface.

"Señor." Tomás's voice, low, from the boat. "You want to see this."

Petra crossed the gravel. Tomás stood at the waterline pointing upstream along the near bank, where the side stream cut a shallow channel through the stones. Mud lay along its margins, soft and patient, the kind of surface that kept records.

Footprints. Many of them, overlapping, days old at least, softened by rain but legible to a man who had spent his life reading ground. Boot prints in expedition-grade rubber, several sizes. Bare feet, narrow, adult. And a pattern of tracks that made Tomás's jaw tighten: groups of barefoot prints moving together, evenly spaced, in formation — the gait of people accustomed to walking trails in silence, in company, with purpose.

"Not tourists," Tomás said quietly.

"No."

"River people. Upriver people." He glanced at Petra, and for the first time since Puerto Ladeado there was something in his face beyond professional caution. "There are communities up there, señor. Old ones. They came down during the troubles, years back, when the government tried to put meters on the water. They don't like surveys. They don't like strangers with instruments." He paused. "They especially don't like the people who flooded San Rafael."

The name landed in Petra's chest like a dropped anchor. San Rafael. He remembered it from the worst year of his life — a village two bends below the proposed reservoir site, condemned on paper, drowned ahead of schedule when the consortium's test gates failed. Eleven dead. He had read the report; he had been made to understand, in ways the report never admitted, how much of it had been buried with the village. His blacklist had been the burial's final shovelful.

So the valley remembers too, he thought. The valley has been keeping its own ledger.

He walked the perimeter once more, fast now, cataloguing. At the upstream edge of the camp, half-hidden in the grass, he found what he'd been half-expecting and fully dreading: a single survey stake, aluminum, stamped with a serial number and a logo worn but unmistakable — the stylized confluence mark of Hidroandina Consorcio. Driven into the ground and then, notably, *pulled back out*, leaning against a stone as if someone had started to remove it and changed their mind, or been interrupted.

Hidroandina. The same letters that had signed his termination letter five years ago. The same letters that had bought the avalanche report, buried the gate-failure findings, and explained to a room of lawyers why the mountain's failure was the guide's fault. The mountain he fled had followed him upriver, stake by stake, and it had been here first.

"Tomás," Petra said. "How far to the gorge?"

"A day, maybe less if the water cooperates. It won't." The boatman studied him. "You're still going."

It wasn't a question, but Petra answered it anyway. "I'm still going."

Tomás nodded slowly, and looked upriver, where the valley narrowed and the green walls drew closer and the river, somewhere beyond the next bend, began its long climb toward the bells. "My grandmother used to say the Sombra runs backward," he said. "Everything else carries things down to the sea. This one carries things up. Secrets. Bodies. Grudges." He stepped into the boat and steadied the motor. "Get in, señor. We've given the ghosts enough of our morning."

Petra took one last look at the camp — the neat tents, the clean pots, the three dead radios lying in their row like witnesses who'd been silenced in order — and got in.

As the current took them and the lower camp slid away behind the bend, he caught himself doing the arithmetic he'd sworn off. Eight scientists went up. Someone wanted them unreachable. Someone else — or the same someone — had taken them somewhere, and the footprints said the somewhere was upriver, among people with old reasons to hate the letters on that survey stake.

Ochoa had called it a retrieval job. Sitting in the bow with the spray cooling his face and the gorge waiting ahead like a held breath, Petra finally understood what he'd actually been hired for.

Not to rescue anyone.

To find out how much the river knew — and whether it could be made to forget.
