## 4. What the River Keeps

The river dropped another finger's width overnight.

Ines Calloway knew this the way she knew her own pulse — not by instrument but by habit, by the pale seam of stone above the waterline that grew a little wider each morning, by the sound of the current against the mission rocks, which had changed its voice in a week from a shout to a mutter. The Río Sombra was going quiet for the season, and everything that lived along it — the fish, the cane, the sixty-one souls of San Rafael de la Sombra — would have to learn to live on less.

She stood at the edge of the drying channel before dawn, barefoot on cold stone, and counted.

Not water. People.

Marisol's family, seven, with the new baby who had never yet seen the river high. Old Emeterio, one, too proud to admit his lungs were failing faster than the season. The Ortega brothers, two, who fished the shallows now because the deep pools had gone silent. The schoolchildren, eleven, who would need to walk farther each day for water fit to drink unless the cisterns held. Herself. Her brother Tomás was downstream somewhere with a boat and a stranger, but she did not count him; he had made his choice when he took the outsider's coin, and counting him felt like admitting how much she wanted him back.

Sixty-one minus one. Sixty.

The number sat in her chest like a stone she had swallowed years ago and never passed.

Behind her, the village woke in stages: the first smoke from Doña Rufina's cookfire, the cough of the generator they rationed to an hour a day, the low argument of men at the landing stage deciding which boats to haul above the flood line even though there would be no flood this year — only the opposite. The dry season had come early and hard, and everyone said so, and no one said what everyone was thinking: *the river has never been this low in living memory.*

Everyone except the people locked in the old mission house. They thought about the river differently. Ines was sure of it. She intended to prove it before the last pool evaporated.

* * *

The mission house had been built two hundred years ago by men who came upriver to save souls and stayed to take land, and Ines had always found a bitter symmetry in the fact that it was the only building in the valley solid enough to hold prisoners. Its walls were a meter thick, adobe over stone, cool as a cellar in any weather. Its windows were slits. Its single iron door had been forged in Puerto Ladeado and hauled up the gorge by mule train in her grandmother's time, and it shut with a sound like a verdict.

Inside were eight members of the Halvorsen expedition, as their papers called them. Surveyors, as Ines called them. Spies, as half the village called them. Guests, as no one called them anymore, though that was what they had been for the first three days, back when the community still believed in its own hospitality.

That had been six weeks ago. The word *guest* had died quietly, sometime between the discovery of the second cache of equipment and the night Marisol's husband, Damián, stood guard with a machete and his hands shaking, whispering to Ines that he could not do this, that he had never agreed to be a jailer, that his father had not drowned in the lower valley so his son could become the kind of man who locked doors.

Ines had told him: *You are not a jailer. You are a wall.* And Damián had looked at her with something close to pity and said nothing, which was worse than arguing, and gone back to his post.

Now she walked the corridor toward the cells with a lantern and a ledger under her arm, and the light swung shadows across walls that still bore, faintly, the whitewashed outlines of saints some well-meaning priest had painted and some practical ancestor had plastered over. A hand here. A halo there. The village children swore you could see them if the light was right. Ines had seen them all her life and had decided long ago that they watched her without judgment, which was more than she could say for anyone else.

The door at the end of the corridor was guarded by two young men — Chuy and his cousin Beto, seventeen and nineteen, armed with a machete and a salvaged shotgun with three shells. When Ines approached, they straightened the way soldiers do, and she hated that they had learned to do it. She had never taught them that. The situation had taught them. Six weeks of standing watch over frightened strangers had taught these boys to stand like garrison troops, and every morning she saw it happen a little more, and every morning she told herself it was necessary, and every morning the telling cost her something she did not get back.

"Anything?" she asked.

"They sang again last night," Chuy said. "The tall one. The woman doctor. Same song both times." He shrugged, embarrassed. "It wasn't a bad song."

"It's called *La Llorona*," Beto said. "My mother sings it. Why would they sing *that*?"

Ines didn't answer. She suspected she knew. People locked in the dark reach for whatever is nearest, and the nearest thing to eight foreign scientists in a Mexican mission house was the country around them, leaking through the walls in old songs and the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of the river dying outside. She chose not to find it touching. Touching was a luxury.

"I'm going in," she said. "Alone. Nobody opens this door for anyone until I come out. Not for them, not for us. If I call out, you count to fifty and then you open it. If I don't call out—" She let it hang. They nodded, solemn, and she saw that Chuy had rehearsed this in his head, that he had imagined the moment when he would have to decide whether the woman who led them was worth three shotgun shells, and she turned away quickly, before he could see what that knowledge did to her face.

* * *

Dr. Yolanda Ferreira sat on the floor against the far wall, as she always did, as far from the door as the room allowed, as if distance were a kind of dignity. She was fifty-some years old, gray-streaked, sunburned past saving, and she held herself with the particular rigidity of a woman who had decided not to beg. Beside her, asleep or pretending to be, lay the youngest of them, a graduate student named Pell who had spent the first week crying and the second week silent and had not spoken a full sentence since.

"Señora Calloway." Ferreira did not rise. "Come to count us again?"

"I count my people," Ines said. "I inventory you."

"A distinction without a difference."

"You'd be surprised."

There were chairs in the room — they had been furniture once, in the mission's schoolroom days — but Ines remained standing, as she always did. Standing kept the conversations short. Sitting invited hope, and hope in this room was a currency she could not afford to print.

"The river," Ines said. "How low does it have to go before your instruments tell you what we already know?"

Ferreira's mouth tightened. It was the closest thing to a confession the woman ever allowed, and Ines had learned to read her the way she read weather. The tightening meant: *you are right and I will not say so.* The flat stare meant: *I am still deciding whether you are a person or an obstacle.* Today she got both, which meant Ferreira was tired, and tired people leaked truth.

"We're hydrologists," Ferreira said. "Not surveyors. Not engineers. We measure. That's all we've ever done here."

"Measure for whom?"

Silence. The good, long silence of a woman choosing her words like footsteps across ice.

"For science," Ferreira said finally. "For the record. For whoever comes after us, so that when someone decides what happens to this valley, they have to look at real numbers while they do it."

"And when the numbers say the gorge is perfect for a dam — narrow walls, deep water, one road in — whose hands do those numbers end up in? Yours? Or theirs?" Ines lifted the lantern slightly, so the light moved across Ferreira's face. "You know the name. You've known it since before you came. Say it."

"I won't play this game."

"It isn't a game. Downstream of the gorge there was a village called Santa Ana. Four hundred people. My mother's people. There is a reservoir there now, and the church tower sticks out of the water like a finger pointing at God, asking Him why. The company that built it had surveys too. Beautiful numbers. Peer-reviewed. And every one of those numbers was true, and every one of them was used to drown a town." Ines set the lantern down on the floor between them, deliberately, so that the shadow of the bars fell across Ferreira's knees. "So when I find eight strangers measuring my river, I don't ask what they intend. I ask who paid for the trip. Because intentions are free. Money isn't."

Ferreira looked at her for a long moment. Then, softly, in a voice Ines had never heard her use — the voice of a woman talking to herself rather than to a captor — she said:

"Half of us didn't come for the science."

And then, as if hearing herself, she shut her mouth and turned her face to the wall, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

Ines stood very still. Half. *Half.* The word went through her colder than the river in flood season. Eight people. Half of four was four. Four of these eight were something other than what they claimed, and Ferreira had just admitted it aloud, perhaps accidentally, perhaps — and this was the thought that kept Ines awake after — perhaps deliberately, a coin tossed to the jailer, a thread left loose on purpose for her to pull.

She pulled nothing. Not tonight. Tonight she picked up the lantern and knocked twice on the door, and heard Chuy's careful count begin on the other side, and walked back down the corridor of ghost-saints with the word *half* burning a hole in her pocket like a stolen thing.

* * *

The council met that evening in the church, because the church had the only room big enough, and because Ines's grandmother had established the tradition that decisions about the whole community were made under the eyes of somebody, even a plaster saint with peeling paint. Twenty-three adults attended. The rest were on watch, or on the water, or had simply stopped coming, which was its own message and one Ines logged without comment.

She laid out the facts the way she laid out everything: plainly, in order, without decoration.

The river was dropping faster than any dry season in memory. The cisterns would last thirty days, maybe thirty-five, if they cut rations again. Thirty mouths were mouths they had fed for six weeks from stores meant for twenty-five. The stores would not last the season. And today, inside the mission house, Dr. Ferreira had said — had admitted, however briefly — that half her team was not what it seemed.

"So," Ines concluded. "We are feeding strangers who may be scouts for the people who drowned Santa Ana, with water we do not have, in a season that has already begun to kill the fish. And every day we keep them, we spend something we cannot replace. And every day we send them away, we send back to the consortium eight witnesses to everything we've done — the watches, the confiscations, the smashed radios at the lower camp. Witnesses who will tell the story their way, because we gave them six weeks to rehearse it."

The silence afterward had texture. Ines had sat in enough councils to know its varieties: the silence of agreement, dense and warm; the silence of fear, thin and high; and the worst kind, the silence of people who have realized their leader has run out of answers and are too loyal to say so. This was the third kind.

It was Damián who broke it, and she should have expected it, because Damián had been breaking things gently for six weeks.

"Ines." He stood, big and slow, a man built for carrying loads. "When we took them off the river, we told ourselves it was protection. Theirs and ours. I believed it. I still want to believe it." He looked down at his hands, which were scarred from rope and net and forty years of work. "But I stood guard last night, and I watched the tall doctor sing *La Llorona* to the young one through the wall, to help him sleep, and I thought: my father sang that song. And I thought: what has happened to us, that we hear our own songs coming out of a locked room and call it evidence?"

Nobody answered him. Nobody needed to. The question landed in the middle of the room like a dropped stone, and the ripples went outward through every person there, and Ines felt them reach her and keep going, out past the walls, down toward the darkening river.

Old Emeterio spoke next, wheezing, from his chair by the door. "My grandmother remembered Santa Ana," he said. "She remembered the water coming up the street, and the priest ringing the bell until the bell went under, and she said you could hear it for years after, on quiet nights, ringing underwater. She said the company men smiled the whole time. Smiling men with documents." He fixed his clouded eyes on Ines. "These ones don't smile. That's the only difference I can see. So decide fast, niña, but decide knowing that whichever way you choose, somebody's bell goes under."

* * *

Later, alone, Ines walked out to the point of rock above the gorge where her grandmother used to stand and listen for the drowned bell of Santa Ana, though the village was eighty kilometers downstream and no bell could carry that far. It was a listening done with the heart, not the ears. Ines had inherited the rock and the listening both.

The river below had shrunk to a silver thread in a canyon of wet black stone. Pools that had been swimming holes in her childhood were now shallow dishes, warm at the edges, crowded with stranded fish that the herons were taking one by one, patient as tax collectors. Upstream, invisible beyond the first bend, the water came down out of the high basin where no one from the village went — the source, the forbidden ground, the place the oldest stories said the river was born and the older stories said it was buried. Her grandmother had called it *la madre*. The mother. You did not visit your mother to take things. You visited to give thanks, and you left before she woke.

Ines looked upstream and then downstream, and understood that she was standing exactly at the center of the only question that had ever mattered: what the river keeps, and what it gives up, and who gets to decide.

The river kept everything, her grandmother said. Every secret thrown in it. Every body. Every promise. The consortium thought the river was a resource — so many cubic meters per second, so many megawatts, so much money per drowned church tower. The scientists thought it was data. The village knew it was neither. It was a ledger, and every account in it would be settled someday, and the only question was whether the settling would be done by the people who lived along it or by smiling men with documents.

She thought of Ferreira's voice saying *half*, and of Damián's question hanging in the church air, and of Chuy counting to fifty behind an iron door, and she felt the loneliness of command settle over her shoulders like a wet poncho. Everyone believed she was certain. She had worked very hard to make them believe it, because a community facing a dry season cannot afford a leader who hesitates. But certainty, she had learned, was mostly a performance given for other people's benefit, and the performer paid full price for every show.

What she actually was, standing on her grandmother's rock in the failing light, was a woman holding a river together with her hands while it drained through her fingers, guarding eight strangers she had come to think of — and this was the dangerous thought, the one she permitted herself only here, where no one could see her face — as people. Frightened, stubborn, half-corrupted, possibly guilty people. People who sang lullabies through walls.

Tomorrow she would interrogate Ferreira properly. Tomorrow she would pull the thread the woman had left dangling, and follow it wherever it led, even if it led to proof that she had been wrong to take them, or proof that she had not been wrong enough.

Tonight she stood on the rock a while longer, listening for a bell that had been underwater for forty years, and heard instead, faint and unmistakable, carried up the gorge on the evening air — engines. More than one. Far downstream, past the lower camp, past the place where Tomás had taken the stranger, something was moving up the Río Sombra against the current, and it was not a boat, because boats did not sound like that, and it was not the wind, because the wind had died with the light.

Ines turned toward the sound and felt the hair rise on her arms, and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling:

*They've sent someone. And whoever it is will be here within two days.*
