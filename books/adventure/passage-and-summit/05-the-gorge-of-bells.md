## 5. The Gorge of Bells

Tomás stopped the boat where the river narrowed to a throat of black stone, and would go no farther.

They had been on the water since before dawn, four days of it, the Río Sombra climbing beneath them in a long stutter of rapids and slack water, and Petra had learned the boatman's silences the way he learned all rivers: by what they refused to say. Tomás talked when the water was easy — about his daughters in Puerto Ladeado, about the price of diesel, about a cousin who had married badly — and went quiet an hour before every danger, as if the river spoke to him first and he needed time to decide how much to pass along.

Now he shipped the oars and let the boat drift, and the silence he fell into was the deepest yet.

"Here?" Petra said.

"Here the gorge begins." Tomás would not look at the walls rising on either side of them, which was itself a kind of answer. He looked instead at his hands, at the oar-locks, at the water sliding past the hull — anywhere but up. "They call it the Garganta de las Campanas. The gorge of bells. Before the dam people came, before your time and mine, there was a church in a village down there." He nodded at the water. "Flooded. But in the dry years, when the river drops low enough, the bell tower breaks the surface, and on windy nights the wind moves through the bells and they ring. Underwater. Half underwater. The old people say the drowned ring them themselves."

"And you believe that."

"I believe I have heard it." Tomás's jaw worked. "I believe what I have heard."

The walls rose sheer on both sides now, two hundred meters of granite veined with quartz, and the river ran through them fast and green and cold. Petra felt the change in the air — the gorge breathed its own weather, a cool exhalation from the deep water, and the sound of their passage came back to them doubled and tripled off the stone until it seemed a whole fleet was moving upriver instead of one leaking boat and two men who did not trust each other enough to call this a partnership.

"Sixty kilometers past this point," Tomás said, "there is a mission house. Old, old. The people who live there —" He stopped. Started again. "There are people who live there who do not want visitors. The river past the gorge belongs to them the way your name belongs to you. I take cargo to the mouth of the gorge. I have never gone past it. The men who went past it with me, twenty years ago —" He shook his head and did not finish, and Petra understood that the sentence had no ending Tomás was willing to build.

"You said Puerto Ladeado you'd take me up the Río Sombra."

"I said up the Río Sombra. This is up the Río Sombra. This is the top of what my boat and my life are worth." Tomás finally looked at him, and in the flat morning light Petra saw how old the man actually was, older than his voice, older than his hands. "You are a guide. You have legs. The trail is on the left wall, above the first rapid. It is a bad trail. It was cut by miners who are all dead. But it goes through, and it comes out above the mission, and from there, señor, you are on your own, which is where you have been since I met you, if you want the truth."

Petra looked upriver. The gorge bent away to the north, and around the bend there was nothing to see but more stone and the green seam of water, but he felt it anyway — that pull, that specific gravity, the sense that the thing he had come to find was not ahead of him but below him, drawing him down. He had felt it since the lower camp. Since the smashed radios.

"Wait for me here," he said. "Seven days. If I'm not back in seven days —"

"I will not be here in seven days," Tomás said. "I will be here in six. After six days I go downriver, and I tell the man who paid me that I delivered you to the gorge alive, and what happened after is between you and the river." He extended one weathered hand, and Petra took it, and the grip was brief and hard and final, a handshake that was also a goodbye. "You have water for three days. The river will give you more if you are smart, and take everything if you are not. The trail —" Tomás hesitated, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes that might have been, in a harder man, affection. "The trail has bells of its own. Loose stone. Listen before you step. The dead miners left you that much."

* * *

The trail was exactly as bad as promised.

It started above the first rapid as a shelf barely wider than Petra's boot soles, cut into the cliff face forty feet above the water, and it climbed from there in a series of switchbacks so steep he climbed them more with his hands than his feet. Below him the river ran white over drowned boulders, and the spray came up cold, and when he looked down — which he made himself do, once, because a guide who stops looking down dies faster than one who doesn't — the water was the color of bottle glass and moving with the patient violence of something that had all the time in the world.

He climbed for six hours. The gorge narrowed, then narrowed again, until the sky was a ribbon of blue overhead and the light reached the floor only in the middle hours of the day. Lichen painted the walls in continents of orange and grey. Somewhere above him, water fell from a height he could not see, and struck the stone below in a sound like slow applause, and the spray drifted across the trail in a fine cold veil that made every hold treacherous.

He thought about the radios.

He had turned them over a hundred times in his mind since the lower camp — six units, stacked in the expedition's supply tent, and every one of them not broken but *killed*. Antennas snapped off and driven into the circuit boards. Battery compartments flooded with river water, then dried and left to corrode. That was not weather. That was not accident. That was a message written in the only language that matters out here: *no one is coming, and no one is going.*

And the message had been left for the expedition, or left by the expedition. Those were the two possibilities, and they divided the world between them. If the team had destroyed their own radios, they had gone dark on purpose — which meant whatever they had found upriver was worth going silent for. And if someone else had done it —

Petra's boot skidded on wet stone and his whole body weight went onto his fingertips and for three seconds he hung on the cliff face above the green water with nothing between him and the rapid but air, and then his toe found a seam and he breathed again and kept climbing, and his heart went on hammering long after the danger had passed, the way hearts do.

He did not think about the avalanche. He was very good at not thinking about the avalanche. He had had five years of practice, and he had gotten so skilled at the not-thinking that the not-thinking had become its own kind of landscape, its own gorge, with its own trail through it, and he traveled it the way he traveled this one: one hold at a time, never looking down.

But the gorge would not let him not-think. Gorges were like that. They took your voice and gave it back to you doubled, and what came back was never quite your own.

*Two clients,* the walls said, in his own voice. *Two names on the manifest and two empty seats on the flight out, and you standing in the snow at the base of the slide with your transceiver in your hand, and later, much later, the consortium's lawyers explaining to a hearing room that the slope had been unstable, that the weather had been anomalous, that the guide —* his own voice, coming back off the stone — *the guide had made a route decision within the range of professional judgment.*

Within the range. As if the mountain were a dial and he had turned it too far.

He had never learned what he was supposed to have done differently. That was the poison of it, the thing that had eaten him alive for five years: not guilt, which at least has a shape, but the *absence* of guilt, the hollow where the mistake was supposed to be and wasn't. He had checked the slope. He had read the snow. He had made the right call with the information he had, and two people were dead anyway, and the consortium — Corvus Hydro, with its neat black bird stamped on every letterhead — had needed a name to feed the inquiry, and his was the one on the permit.

He climbed. The trail narrowed. The river roared below.

* * *

Near dusk the gorge opened.

It happened all at once, the way gorges end — one bend and the walls fell back and the sky poured down like water released from a cup. Petra came down off the cliff trail onto a bench of grey gravel and stood there in the last light with his legs shaking and his hands bleeding in small unimportant ways, and looked at the valley the gorge had been hiding.

It was not a large valley. The river widened and slowed, braiding itself around gravel bars, and on both sides the forest came down to the water in a dark unbroken stand — cedar, he thought, and something broader-leafed he didn't have a name for, growing in tiers so dense the ground beneath was black and secret. Mist stood in the hollows. The air smelled of wet stone and rot and green growing things, an smell so old and complete it seemed less like a place than a condition, something the world had been before people and would be again after.

And there, downstream, half a kilometer on, where the river bent against the far bank —

He saw it before he understood it. A shape in the water that did not belong to water. A vertical line, dark, breaking the current's skin, and around it the water moved wrong, parted and swirled in a pattern that meant stone underneath.

He walked down to the bank. The gravel gave under his boots with a sound like whispering.

It was a bell tower.

Only the top of it — the belfry's open arches and the peak of the roof, rising perhaps two meters out of the river, black with forty years of flood. The current combed around it and made a long V of white water downstream. And as Petra stood there, in the blue hour when the wind comes down off the heights to walk the valley floor, the wind found the tower, and moved through it, and something far down inside the flooded stone rang — one low note, more felt than heard, a vibration that came up through the gravel and the soles of his boots and into the bones of his legs.

The dead ringing their own bell. Or just wind and bronze and moving water, which is the same thing if you stay long enough.

Petra stood until full dark, listening, and did not let himself think about who had drowned, or how, or whose decision the water had been. He was saving that. He could feel himself saving it, the way you save the last of your water.

* * *

He made a cold camp in the treeline, no fire, and in the morning the valley gave him its second answer.

He found the stake by accident, or told himself he had. He was walking the near bank upstream, looking for the continuation of the trail, when he saw the flagging tape — a scrap of pink plastic, sun-bleached to the color of skin, tied to a branch. Then another, ten meters on. Then the stake itself, driven into the gravel bar at the river's edge: a length of galvanized steel with an aluminum cap, the kind he had driven a hundred of in another life, on other rivers, for other paymasters.

He knelt. He already knew what the cap would say. Some part of him had known since the smashed radios, since the flagging tape, since Tomás's voice saying *there are people who live there who do not want visitors* — some part of him had been walking toward this kneeling for five years, down a trail of not-thinking, one hold at a time.

The cap was stamped with a bird.

Black, stylized, wings folded like a knife — and beneath it, in letters small enough to require him to wipe the river mud away with his thumb: **CORVUS HYDRO — CUENCA DEL RÍO SOMBRA — ESTACIÓN 14**.

Station fourteen. Which meant stations one through thirteen were behind him, downstream, hidden in the country he had come through blind. Which meant the survey had been running for months, maybe years. Which meant the expedition — Halvorsen's expedition, six weeks silent, six scientists with smashed radios — had walked into the middle of something that had begun long before they arrived.

Petra sat back on his heels in the cold gravel and the vertigo took him.

Not the cliff kind. The other kind — the kind where the ground stays still and the past moves. He had fled this company across five years and half a continent and every river he could find work on, and he had come up this one because a fixer named Ochoa had paid him in cash he needed too badly to refuse, and the whole time, the entire time, he had been swimming *toward* it. The mountain he had run from had simply waited, patient as water, and let him descend into its gorge.

*They never let go,* he thought. *They just wait for the dry season.*

He looked upriver, toward the mission house he could not yet see, where six missing scientists were either dead or waiting, and where a community lived that had decided the answer to Corvus Hydro was to make its surveyors disappear. And downstream, faint, so faint he might have invented it, the drowned bell rang again in the morning wind.

Petra stood up. He took out his knife, and cut the flagging tape from the branch, and folded it into his pocket — evidence, small and stupid and real — and shouldered his pack.

Two days, Ines had said, in a valley he had never heard of, to an enemy he had never met. He had two days, and a bad trail, and a name he had spent five years trying to bury, and the bird on the stake had just dug it up again.

He started walking.
