## 6. Taken

The mission house announced itself before Petra saw it. A bell, cracked and toneless, tolled once from somewhere above the tree line — a single flat note that rolled down the valley and died in the water. Then silence. Then the bell again, the same note, the same weight, as if someone were striking it not to call people but to count them.

He had been walking since first light, following the river through a country that kept changing its mind about itself — jungle giving way to bamboo, bamboo to stands of aliso with pale peeling bark, the trail climbing away from the water and dropping back to it like a thing pacing a cage. His boots were wet through for the third day running. His shoulders had learned the pack so well he no longer felt it, only its absence when he set it down, which was never.

Tomás's words came back to him at the worst moments. *Past the gorge, you're not on any map I know.* And then, quieter, almost apologetic: *And whatever you find up there, hermano, don't tell them Tomás took you as far as he did.*

Petra had laughed then. He wasn't laughing now.

The bell struck again, closer, and this time he heard what followed it: a dog, low and continuous, not barking but announcing. Somewhere upslope, a machete rang against wood — once, twice, deliberate, the way a man chops when he wants to be heard chopping.

They knew he was coming. They had probably known since the gorge.

He considered his options the way he'd once taught clients to consider weather: calmly, completely, knowing all the while that the decision would be made by forces larger than himself. He could go to ground and circle wide, come at the settlement from above, spend a day he might not have learning terrain he didn't know. He could go back downriver, past the gorge, and ask Tomás to do the one thing Tomás had already refused to do.

Or he could walk in the front way, hands visible, voice level, and say the sentence he had been composing for two days: *I'm looking for six scientists. I was paid to find them. Nobody knows I'm here.*

It sounded worse every time he rehearsed it. Out loud, stripped of context, it was the confession of either a rescuer or a scout, and he had no way to prove which. He carried no papers — Ochoa's jobs came without paper, that was the point of Ochoa — no radio, no satellite phone, nothing but cash sewn into his jacket lining and Halvorsen's name written on a scrap of paper soft as cloth from handling.

*You're a guide,* he told himself. *Guides walk up to strangers in bad places for a living.*

But guides usually had clients behind them, and radios, and a company logo on their packs that said *if anything happens to me, someone will eventually ask why.* He had none of that. For five years he had been a man nobody would come looking for, and he had told himself it was freedom. Walking toward the bell now, through light that fell green and underwater-thick through the canopy, he understood it differently. It was also permission. Anyone could do anything to a man no one was missing.

The trail crested a low ridge of black rock, and there it was.

The mission house sat on a shelf of land above the river where the valley widened into something almost gentle — terraced gardens gone half wild, a chapel with a roof of corrugated tin gone orange with rust, and the main building itself, long and low, whitewashed once upon a time, now the color of old bone. Smoke rose from three cooking fires. Laundry hung between posts. Children — actual children — chased each other around a stone cistern, and their noise was so ordinary, so violently human, that Petra stopped dead on the ridge and felt something in his chest unclench that he hadn't known was clenched.

This was not a war camp. This was not a fortress. This was a village that happened to be holding prisoners, or thought it was, or needed to believe it was.

Then the dog appeared at the edge of the garden, ribs showing under yellow fur, and stood very still and looked at him, and the children's noise cut off all at once, the way water cuts off, and Petra understood that the ordinariness was the defense. Every person in that village had known exactly where a stranger should be spotted, and it wasn't by the sentries. It was by the children, who were the alarm no one ever thought to disarm.

A man rose from beside the cistern. Not hurrying. Wiping his hands on his trousers, picking up a machete from the stones with the unhurried ease of someone picking up a tool he'd used ten thousand times. Two more figures detached themselves from the shade of the chapel eave. A woman leaned out of a doorway and spoke a single word that Petra couldn't hear and didn't need to; the whole settlement turned toward him like a field of sunflowers finding the sun.

"Buenas," Petra called, and raised both hands, empty, palms out. The universal grammar. "Vengo en paz. Busco a unas personas."

The man with the machete started up the trail toward him. Behind him, others spread out along the ridge line with the casual competence of people who had done this before, cutting off the high ground, closing the low route along the river. Not soldiers. Farmers, fishermen, a woman with a fish trap still in her hand. But they moved like they'd drilled it, and that frightened Petra more than uniforms would have.

"I'm alone," he said, switching languages, then again in English, then again in the border Spanish of Puerto Ladeado. "Alone. Solo. Six people, scientists, they went missing six weeks ago—"

The man with the machete reached him and did not point the blade at him, which was somehow more intimidating than pointing it. He simply stood inside arm's reach and looked at Petra the way you look at a snake whose intentions are theoretically knowable but practically irrelevant.

"The bell rang four times," the man said.

"I heard it."

"You're the fourth stranger this year." He said it without heat, a fact laid on the scale. "The other three came from downstream too."

Downstream. Toward the consortium. Toward Vane's world, though Petra didn't yet have the shape of that word in his mouth. He felt the ground shift under the conversation, felt the sentence assembling itself in the man's head — *downstream means dam, dam means enemy* — and heard his own answer arrive too late and sound exactly wrong.

"I'm not from the consortium," he said. "I'm a mountain guide. I was hired to find the missing expedition—"

"Hired." The man tasted the word. Around them, the ring had tightened without anyone appearing to move. "By whom?"

And there it was, the hole in the middle of everything, the question he'd been dreading since the gorge because there was no honest answer that didn't sound like a lie. *By a fixer named Ochoa, paid by parties unknown, likely the consortium itself, possibly the families, definitely someone with money and reasons I can't see.* He watched the man read all of that in the pause, in the flicker of Petra's eyes toward the ridge, in the half-second where a truthful man hesitates and a liar calculates and no one watching can tell the difference.

"Take the pack," the man said finally, to someone behind Petra. Hands lifted it off his shoulders with practiced care — checking, even now, for weapons, for radios, for the hard rectangle of a satellite phone. They found the cash in his lining and the scrap of paper with Halvorsen's name, and the man read the name and something moved behind his face, some door opening onto a room Petra couldn't see.

"So you know that name."

"It's the job," Petra said. "Find Dr. Halvorsen. That's all I know."

"That's what they all say." But he folded the paper away into his shirt, carefully, and Petra understood that the name mattered here, that it was evidence of something, though of guilt or innocence he couldn't guess. "Walk," the man said, gesturing down the slope with the machete held loosely, civilly, at his side. "Slowly. If you run, the dogs are faster, and they've been fed on strangers before."

Petra looked once at the river — silver, indifferent, going about its long work below the shelf of land — and then walked down into the village with his hands still raised, feeling the eyes on his back like sun after cloud.

* * *

They put him in the granary.

Not a cell — the village had no cells, he'd give them that — but a raised storehouse on stilts, dry-goods smell of maize and chilies and dust, one window barred with a wooden lattice, a door that locked from outside with a beam of blackened hardwood thick as his forearm. Someone had swept it recently. Someone had left a clay jug of water and a woven mat, and these small courtesies, sitting inside the fact of his imprisonment, unsettled him more than cruelty would have. Cruelty he could have read. Kindness with a locked door was a language he didn't speak.

Through the lattice he could see a slice of the yard: the cistern, the laundry line, the corner of the chapel. People passed and did not look at him, which was its own kind of message. Twice, children dared each other close enough to peer in, shrieking away when he shifted. Once, an old woman stopped, looked at him for a long moment through the bars with an expression he couldn't parse — pity? curiosity? recognition? — and walked on.

The light moved across the floor. He measured it the way he measured everything now, in distances: the sun's travel from one crack in the boards to another, roughly forty minutes. He drank, he sat, he made himself still, because stillness was the one discipline five years of exile had perfected in him. Waiting was the work. Waiting was the whole profession, if you did it honestly — waiting out storms, waiting out rockfall, waiting out fear, your own and other people's.

Footsteps came in the late afternoon. More than one set. The beam scraped back, and the door opened, and the man with the machete stood there with two others behind him, and beside them — Petra's breath caught — a woman he recognized instantly, though he'd never seen her before.

She had the kind of face that photographs badly and lives vividly: strong jaw, deep-set eyes, hair going gray at the temples in a way she had clearly decided not to fight. She wore rubber boots and a canvas apron stained green with plant matter, and she carried herself with the specific gravity of someone whom everyone else in the room unconsciously oriented toward. He knew who she was before anyone said her name. You can't spend years reading mountains and not learn to read authority.

"Ines Calloway," she said. Not offering a hand. "Tomás sent word ahead. You paid him to bring you past the lower camps."

"He refused the gorge. I walked from there."

"He says you asked questions about the mission house. About us." She stepped inside — the room seemed to shrink to fit her, or perhaps just to hold still — and studied him with an attention that felt less like suspicion than like surveying. Taking elevations. "He says you claimed to be looking for the gringos."

"The scientists. Yes."

"They're not missing." She said it flatly, watching him. "They're here. They're fine. Fed, sheltered, alive. Which is more than I can say for the last village the people who sent them touched."

"I don't know who sent them."

"No," she agreed. "But somebody sent you. And somebody sent them. And I notice those somebodys keep being downstream." She crouched suddenly, bringing her eyes level with his, and this close he saw the exhaustion underneath the iron, the sleeplessness worn like a second skin. "So. One more time, guide, and think carefully, because the answer decides whether you eat tonight or don't. Who hired you?"

"A fixer," Petra said. "In Puerto Ladeado. Named Ochoa."

Something crossed her face at the name — recognition, distaste, the look of a woman confirming a diagnosis she'd already suspected. "Ochoa works for money."

"Everyone works for money."

"Everyone downstream works for money." She rose. "Upstream we work for water. It's a different economy." She turned to the man with the machete. "How much did they pay you?" she asked Petra, without turning back.

"Enough to make me stupid enough to come."

The ghost of something — respect, maybe, or just surprise — moved through the room. One of the men behind her shifted, and murmured something too low for Petra to catch, and Ines shook her head once, a small decisive motion.

"He stays," she said. "Separate from the others. Nobody talks to him. Nobody answers his questions." Her eyes found Petra's again, and held them, and what was in them was not hatred, which he could have fought, but certainty, which he couldn't. "You understand why. You come downstream, alone, paid, asking about my prisoners, the week our river starts dying. Either you're their scout, sent to confirm what they've taken. Or you're exactly what you say. And guide — I have met men who tell the truth, and I believed them, and it cost me thirty-one houses."

She paused at the door.

"The trouble with your story," she said, "is that it's the exact story their scouts would tell. So we'll wait. The river will tell us who you are. It always does."

The beam slid home. The footsteps receded. The light in the crack between the boards went from gold to copper to nothing, and Petra sat on the woven mat in the dark of the granary and did the arithmetic that had been waiting all day to be done.

If the scientists were here, alive, then his contract was half fulfilled — *locate* — and half impossible, because *retrieve* would require an army he didn't have and a trust he hadn't earned. If Ines believed he was a scout, then every hour he sat here was an hour the truth traveled nowhere. And if Ochoa's employers were who Petra was beginning to fear they were — if the money that bought his search came from the same accounts that had bought his ruin — then he hadn't been hired to rescue anyone at all.

He'd been hired to confirm a target.

The thought arrived with total clarity, the way avalanches arrive, and left him cold in the warm dark. Six weeks of silence. Smashed radios. A fixer with no paperwork and no questions. *Find them. Report back.* No one had ever said the word *rescue*. He had supplied that word himself, filled the silence of the contract with his own hope, the way a thirsty man sees water in every mirage.

Somewhere beyond the wall, the cracked bell tolled the evening count, and he heard, faintly, from the direction of the chapel — a second building, a separate silence — voices answering it. Six voices, maybe seven. Alive. Forty meters away and a world out of reach.

He lay back on the mat and stared into the dark and understood, with the patience of a man who has waited out storms before, that he had finally found his clients — and that he was their prisoner too.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow the river would tell them who he was.

He only hoped it remembered correctly.
