## 7. Halvorsen's Ledger

The mission house had been a church once. I could tell by the way the light came through the east wall — not through windows but through the empty sockets where windows had been, long rectangles of morning that moved across the floor like slow hands. Someone had plastered over the altar and hung a drying rack there, strings of river fish split and salted, and the smell of them had become the smell of the whole building: salt and smoke and old stone, the smell of my captivity.

Six weeks. Six weeks since the radios died, and I had counted every day the way a man counts the water level of a river he cannot see — by small, accumulating losses. The loss of my team, scattered into two buildings and a locked storeroom. The loss of my instruments, confiscated in canvas bags I could see through the barred window, stacked under the eaves like coffins. The loss, slowest of all, of the story I had told myself about what we were doing here.

I had been a scientist for thirty-one years. I want that on the record, wherever a record survives. I had believed that data was a kind of honesty, that a number could not lie, that if you measured a thing carefully enough the truth of it would stand up on its own and testify. I had built my whole life on that belief the way the villagers downstream had built their houses on the river's floodplain — trusting the water to stay where it had always been.

I was in the small room off the chapel, the one they used for storing rope and kerosene, because I had refused to be separated from the wall. Not the wall of the building — the wall between rooms. The mission's old confessional partition, half-rotten, still standing between the chapel and what had been the priest's cell, where they kept the American.

They had brought him in two days ago. I heard it happen the way you hear weather coming: the sentry's whistle, the dogs going off down by the landing, the particular quality of Ines Calloway's voice when it rises — not loud, but pitched, like wire pulled taut. Then footsteps, a scuffle, and through the partition I heard them put him in the priest's cell, and I heard him say, in Spanish too fluent and too tired to be a mercenary's, that he was looking for us. That he had been paid to find us. That he was a guide.

A guide. I lay on the rope cot and listened to him breathe on the other side of the wall and thought: they have sent a guide. Which meant they had finally admitted we were lost, which meant the consortium's patience had run out, which meant everything I had been afraid of for six weeks was now moving toward us with legs.

His name, I learned from the guards' gossip, was Reyes. Petra Reyes. And when I heard that name I sat up on the cot so fast the rope frame sang like a struck string, because I knew it. Every man in that consortium's orbit knew it. The guide who lost two clients in the Cordillera del Viento avalanche. The guide whose name became, in the years after, a shorthand in boardroom conversations — a cautionary tale, a way of saying *mountains kill, and the men who lead you up them are the ones who bury you.* Vane had used the story at least a dozen times in pitches I'd sat through, always with the same soft regret, the same practiced sorrow: *We learned from that tragedy. We learned that risk must be managed by professionals.*

I had never asked what Vane had actually learned. I had been paid well enough not to ask.

Now I pressed my palm against the partition wall and the plaster was cool and slightly damp, and on the other side I heard the man shift, the creak of a cot that was probably not a cot but the floor, and I understood that I had a choice to make, and that I had been making it wrong for six weeks, and that the wall between us was the last wall I had any right to hide behind.

"Reyes," I said.

Silence. Long enough that I thought he was asleep, or refusing me, or both.

"You know my name," I said. "Halvorsen. Erik Halvorsen. The expedition you were hired to find — I'm the leader. I'm alive. We're all alive, more or less. And I need to tell you something, and I need you to listen before you decide what I am."

The cot on the other side creaked. A voice came through the wall, low and roughened, the voice of a man who had been shouting against river noise for twenty years. "I'm listening."

* * *

Where do you begin a confession? I began with the river, because the river was the only honest thing in the whole affair.

"You've come up the Río Sombra," I said. "You've seen what it is. Two hundred kilometers of free-running water, the last major undammed tributary in the basin. What the consortium's brochures call an *underutilized asset.* What the people here call — I didn't know what they called it until they took our radios apart with machetes. I still don't know the word. But I've watched them look at it, Reyes. It's not a resource to them. It's an ancestor."

"I've seen the village," he said. "Downstream. The drowned one."

Of course he had. Of course they had shown him. Ines Calloway was many things I had come to fear, but she was not careless, and she would have wanted him to see it — the rooftops breaking the surface in the dry months like the backs of animals, the church bell tower that stood in eight meters of water and rang, the boatmen said, in high wind, a sound the consortium's engineers had called *a charming acoustic anomaly* in a footnote of the environmental impact statement.

"Then you know what a dam here does," I said. "You know what it did at Santa Rosa del Agua. Now let me tell you what you don't know. Let me tell you what I know."

I told him about the team. Eleven of us when we left Puerto Ladeado: four real scientists — my hydrologist, my geologist, two graduate students who had signed on for the adventure and gotten the nightmare instead — and seven who were not what their credentials said. Consortium people. Vane's people. A surveyor whose doctorate I could not verify and whose instruments I was never allowed to calibrate myself. A logistics officer who filed daily reports I later found were duplicated and couriered out by a fishing boat that wasn't a fishing boat. Two security men whose rifles were better than their cover stories. They were polite. They were competent. They called me *Doctor* and they made the coffee in the mornings and for five weeks I did not look too closely, because the consortium was funding the expedition and the consortium had asked — *requested* — that certain personnel be included for operational efficiency, and I had signed the paper, and I had told myself that science could survive inside a contract the way a river could survive inside a channel.

"Half your team," Reyes said through the wall. Not a question. His voice had gone very flat.

"Seven of eleven," I said. "I did the arithmetic in week three and made myself not believe it. That's the honest confession, Reyes, if you want one. Not that I was deceived. That I declined to do the division."

* * *

The light moved. The fish on the drying rack turned from silver to gold to a dull copper, and somewhere below the mission a woman was singing — one of the work songs, the ones with the river's name in every verse, the ones Ines's people sang while they hauled water and mended nets, and I had heard it so many times now that I caught myself humming it, and hated myself a little for that too, for absorbing the music of people I had helped condemn.

I told him about the data.

"The real data," I said. "Not the numbers the consortium people were transmitting downriver. My own. I ran my own gauging stations — four of them, hidden, off the official grid. I measured the dry-season base flow myself, with my own hands, because I had begun to distrust everything that passed through the surveyor's instruments. And Reyes — the numbers are worse than anything in the environmental statement. Worse than the villagers' worst estimate. The consortium's model assumes the tributaries hold through the dry season. They don't. I've measured them dying. The reservoir they're proposing would need the Río Sombra to carry a volume it only carries three months of the year. To hold their promised capacity, the dam height has to go up eleven meters. Eleven meters, Reyes. That's not a reservoir. That's a sea. That's every village on this river, and the next river's villages, and the mission, and the source basin — gone. Underwater. And the model that hides that fact was written by the surveyor on my own team, with data from my own expedition."

The wall was silent. I realized I had been gripping the edge of the rope cot hard enough to leave marks in my palm.

"They're not just building a dam," I said. "They're building a lie, and the lie is wearing my name. My station numbers. My signature on the preliminary report. When this dam kills the river, when the villages flood, when the fish die and the sediment chokes the channel the way it choked Santa Rosa — the record will say Erik Halvorsen verified the hydrology. Erik Halvorsen found the site sound."

"And the real data," Reyes said. "Where is it?"

There it was. The question, arriving the way I had known it would, and I made myself answer it slowly, because I understood — I had understood since the night the radios died — that this was the hinge of everything, that what I did with the next sentence would decide whether I was a witness or a hostage, a man or a ledger entry.

"In my head," I said. "And on paper. The paper is hidden. The head — the head you can't confiscate, which is why they haven't killed me. They think I don't know they know. The surveyor thinks his copies are the only copies. Ines thinks I came here to steal the river. You think — I don't know what you think. But the complete record, Reyes — my gauging data, the surveyor's own internal model with the eleven-meter correction, the courier manifests, the correspondence between Vane and the site chief — it exists, it's real, and it is the kind of document that doesn't just stop a dam. It stops a company."

* * *

I heard him stand up on the other side of the wall. The floorboards spoke his weight, and then his voice came closer, lower, right against the partition, so that I could hear the grain of it, the tiredness underneath.

"You want me to get you out," he said.

"I want you to get the data out," I said. "Me, if it can be arranged. My four real people, if there's any mercy left in this valley. But listen to me — I'm fifty-eight years old and I've spent six weeks on a rope cot learning to pray to a river I helped betray. I'm not the point. I'm *collateral.* The data is the point. Get it to a journalist, a regulator, a court, I don't care — get it somewhere it can't be buried the way Santa Rosa was buried, and I will stand in front of any tribunal on earth and say every word of what I've said to you tonight, with my name on it, and let my career burn as the down payment."

"And why should I believe you?" he said. "You're the man who brought them here. You signed the paper. You're the reason the consortium has a foothold in this valley at all — your expedition was the survey. Your name is on the stake I found in the gorge."

I closed my eyes. Through the empty window sockets the last light was going the color of old blood, and the singing below had stopped, and I could hear the river itself, far down the slope, doing the only thing it had ever done honestly, which was run.

"Yes," I said. "All of it. I found the stake you found. I drove it. I stood on that bank with Vane's people and I said, *yes, this site is viable,* because the alternative was admitting I'd been used, and I was a coward, and cowards are the cheapest material a company can buy. I have spent thirty-one years believing a number couldn't lie, Reyes, and I learned too late that a man can teach a number to lie just by choosing which numbers to count. That's my ledger. That's what I am. And I am offering you the only thing I have left that's worth anything: the true arithmetic, and my testimony, and my ruin, freely given. Not because I'm good. Because I'm guilty, and guilt is the only fuel I've got."

Silence again. But it was a different silence — I had spent six weeks learning the silences of this building, the silence of the guards' boredom, the silence of Ines's contempt, the silence of my own shame, and this one was none of those. This one was a man on the far side of a rotten wall, weighing something.

"You said the paper is hidden," he said at last. "Where?"

"Somewhere Ines's people will never find it, and Vane's people will never think to look," I said. "That's all I'll say through a wall. You get me in a room with Ines Calloway — a real room, a truce, your word and mine — and I'll draw the map. But Reyes — hear this, and hear it clearly, because I think you're the kind of man who hears things clearly. Vane knows we're alive now. Your arrival told him. Whatever he sent you, he'll send more, and next time it won't be a guide with a contract. It'll be men whose contract doesn't have a rescue clause."

On the other side of the wall, the floorboards creaked — once, twice, the slow pacing of a man doing arithmetic of his own. Then stillness.

"Get some sleep, Doctor," Petra Reyes said. "Tomorrow I get you a room with Ines Calloway. God help us both."

And far below the mission, in the dark, I heard the river running, and for the first time in six weeks it did not sound like a verdict. It sounded like a clock.
