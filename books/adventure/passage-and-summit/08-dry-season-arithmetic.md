## 8. Dry Season Arithmetic

The river had dropped another hand's width overnight.

Ines Calloway knew it the way she knew her own pulse — not by looking, though she looked every morning, standing on the flat stone above the mission house where the water ran brown-green and slow over the drowned roots of the ceiba trees — but by sound. The Río Sombra had a voice in the wet season, a full-throated roar that shook the mission's cracked windows and made conversation at the dock a matter of shouting. Now it murmured. It muttered. It was beginning to whisper, and a whispering river was a river with secrets to keep and less strength to keep them.

She crouched and touched the stone where the high-water mark lived as a dark stain, a tide line of the impossible. Two months ago that stain had been under a meter of moving water. Now it dried in the sun like an old scar, and every day the scar grew wider, and every day her arithmetic grew crueler.

Forty-one mouths in the valley proper. Eleven more at the upstream farms who would come down when the maize failed, because they always came down. Six prisoners in the mission house who ate from the same stores and drank from the same wells and gave back nothing but questions.

She counted them again, as if the number might improve with repetition. It did not.

* * *

The mission house smelled of dust and old incense and, lately, fear. Ines walked its corridor with a lamp because the generator had died in June and the fuel for the small one was rationed to the radio hut, and the morning light through the barred windows came down in yellow bars that lay across the floor like something felled.

Six doors. Six locks she had ordered installed herself, with her own hands, driving the screws while Father Anselmo's ghost — if ghosts troubled such places — presumably watched. Behind the third door, Dr. Halvorsen was pacing again. She could hear him: three steps, turn, three steps, turn, the rhythm of a man wearing a groove into his own conscience. Behind the fifth door, someone — the young one, Lindqvist, the one who cried at night and pretended not to — was singing very softly, some northern hymn about green pastures, and the sweetness of it in this dry brown place made Ines's jaw tighten until her teeth ached.

They were not monsters. That was the problem. If they had been monsters, the arithmetic would have been simple.

She stopped at the third door without meaning to, the way you stop at a grave you didn't come to visit.

"You're awake," Halvorsen said through the wood. His voice had gone hoarse over the weeks; he'd begun talking to her through the door whether she answered or not, a one-sided correspondence conducted in Norwegian-accented Spanish, as if enough words might wear through the planks.

"The river is dropping," she said.

A pause. "I know. I can hear it."

"Can you." She leaned her forehead against the door, felt the cool grain. "Then you can hear what I hear. Another month, maybe five weeks, and the lower rapids go to gravel. After that, no boat comes up from Puerto Ladeado. No boat goes down. Anyone still in this valley when the river closes is in it until the rains."

"And your point, Señora Calloway?"

"My point, Doctor, is that I have forty-one people to feed through a dry season, and six guests who eat whether they work or not, and a valley that may be at war by the time the water returns." She straightened. "So I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to answer it the way a scientist answers — with what you know, not what you hope. Are you surveyors?"

Silence. Three steps, turn. Then: "Some of us are hydrologists. Some of us are liars. I'm not sure anymore which category I'm in myself."

It was the most honest thing he'd said, and it was useless, and she hated him a little for making honesty feel useless.

* * *

The council met at dusk in the church, because the church had walls and the walls kept out the mosquitoes and, Ines suspected, because half the valley still believed that decisions made under a crucifix carried more weight than decisions made anywhere else. She did not share the belief, but she respected the architecture of it. Power needed a room.

Twenty-three adults came. That was nearly everyone whose opinion could start a fire.

She stood at the altar rail with the day's numbers chalked on a slate she'd taken from the mission schoolroom, and she read them aloud the way a judge reads charges: rainfall, zero for nineteen days. River stage, down thirty-one centimeters since the solstice. Stores of maize, cassava, dried fish, salt. And then the line she'd saved for last, written smaller than the others because writing it large would have made it real too soon:

*Guests: 6.*

"We have been calling them guests," she said. "I chose that word myself, and I chose it carefully, because words are load-bearing. But the season has come when we must decide what they actually are."

Old Mateo spoke first, as he always did, from the front pew where he sat like a fixture. He was seventy-one and had been a boy when the company men came the first time, the ones with the transit instruments and the polite smiles, and he carried that year inside him the way a tree carries a nail driven into its sapwood — grown around, never gone.

"They are witnesses," he said. "That is what they are. Witnesses to what the consortium intends. You do not release witnesses. You do not feed witnesses through a winter and then wave goodbye at the rapids and hope their gratitude holds. You keep them until there is a court, or until there is a reckoning, whichever comes first."

"And if there is neither?" This from Rosa Quintero, who ran the nursery gardens upriver and had hands permanently stained green with the dyes of her trade. "Mateo, my friend, I love you, but you are planning for a world with judges in it. Look at the slate. When the river closes, we are alone with our decision for four months. Whatever we decide, we live inside it. Can we live inside keeping six people locked in a mission house until the rains? Feeding them our children's maize? Guarding them when every able man will be needed for the harvest and the fish traps?"

"Can we live inside letting them go?" Mateo's voice rose, cracked, steadied. "Rosa. Rosa. They came with instruments. They came measuring our water. One of them — the big one, Brandt — I saw him sketching the gorge. Sketching it! You do not sketch a thing you love. You sketch a thing you intend to change."

"He sketched the birds too," said young Emiliano from the back, the fisherman, barely twenty, all elbows and certainty. "I've seen his book. Birds, plants, the church. Maybe he just — draws things."

"Maybe," said Mateo, with the weight of seven decades pressing down on the word. "And maybe the company men in my boyhood smiled beautifully right up until the day the trucks came."

Ines let it run. That was the discipline of chairing a council: you let the fire burn through its kindling before you tried to shape the flame. She watched faces in the lamplight — the fear on the mothers' faces, which was not fear of the scientists at all but fear of hunger doing mathematics against them; the stubbornness on the old men's faces, which was memory wearing the mask of strategy; and underneath everything, in everyone, the same question asked in different grammars: *what are we becoming?*

Because that was the true ledger, wasn't it. Not maize and centimeters. The valley had survived droughts before. What it had never survived was itself, turned cruel.

Tomás's wife, Beatriz, stood — Tomás himself was downriver somewhere, ferrying a stranger with a guide's weathered face, and Ines had thought about that stranger more than she liked — and said, quietly, "My grandmother always said: the river gives according to how you treat what it sends you. Even trouble. Especially trouble."

"It sent us surveyors," Mateo said.

"It sent us *people*," Beatriz said. "What they are, we haven't finished finding out. Neither have they, maybe."

The argument went on for two hours. It circled, doubled back, changed weapons. Mercy was called weakness; caution was called cruelty; both accusations were true and neither settled anything. Ines took notes she didn't need, her pencil moving in the margins of the slate's arithmetic, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the council was not going to decide anything. Councils decided things when the options were clean. These options were mud, and mud was hers to walk through alone.

At last she raised her hand, and the room stilled the way water stills when a stone settles.

"Here is what we know," she said. "We know the consortium wants this valley's water. We know they destroyed San Rafael downstream — I have seen the rooftops under the reservoir, I have stood on the church tower of a drowned town, so do not tell me what they are capable of; I have *waded* in what they are capable of. We know these six came up our river with instruments, and that at least one of them lies, and that their leader cannot look me in the eye. We know the dry season will close the river, and that whatever we choose, we will live inside it, as Rosa says." She set down the pencil. "And we know one more thing. We know that a stranger is coming up from Ladeado, following their trail, and Tomás turned back at the gorge rather than bring him here — which means the stranger is now walking alone through canyon country toward us, and I do not yet know whose he is."

"Ours?" Emiliano asked.

"Or theirs," said Mateo. "The consortium does not lose six people and send no one to look. Whichever way you count it, Ines — witnesses or burdens — the arithmetic isn't finished. Something else is coming up that river behind them."

Ines looked at the slate. At the small word *guests*. At the column of numbers that shrank daily while the human column only grew.

"Then here is my ruling," she said, and heard her own voice sound like her mother's, which frightened her more than any shortage. "No one is released, and no one is harmed. The scientists work — Halvorsen with the rain gauges, the others on the food lines, guarded, unpaid, unfed better than we feed ourselves. We hold them as witnesses until we know what they witness. And when the stranger arrives—" she paused, feeling the whole room lean toward her, feeling the loneliness of command settle over her shoulders like a wet poncho, "—when the stranger arrives, I will meet him myself. Before the sentries touch him. Because if he is ours, we need him. And if he is theirs, I want to look into his eyes before anyone decides anything on my behalf."

* * *

She walked home along the river path in full dark, the way she always did, refusing the lamp someone always offered. The Río Sombra whispered beside her, low and intimate, running shallow over stones it hadn't touched since she was a girl. She could see stars reflected in the slow pools, whole constellations lying in the water like coins at the bottom of a purse, and she thought: *this is what they want to take. Not the water. The sky lying in the water.*

At the bend below the mission, she stopped, as she stopped every night, at the place where the flood wall of the old San Rafael road emerged from the bank — a curve of mortared stone built by people who had believed, reasonably, that the river would stay in its bed. Her people had come from down there, generations of them, farmers and ferrymen and bell-ringers, until the consortium's dam closed its gates one February and the water climbed the valley walls for eleven days, patient as arithmetic, until the bells of San Rafael rang underwater and then rang no more.

Her grandfather had rowed the last boat out. He never spoke of it. He had taught her to read the river instead, and she had thought that was inheritance enough.

Now she stood between the drowned town's stone and the whispering water and did the sum one final time, honestly, the way Halvorsen had asked her to demand honesty: six strangers fed through a dry season cost perhaps a tenth of the stores. Six strangers released might cost the valley everything, if even one of them carried maps to the source. Six strangers kept might summon soldiers, lawyers, men with Vane's kind of patience. Every column balanced to ruin. There was no safe entry in the ledger. There was only the choice of which ruin to stand inside, and who she would be when the rains came and the river rose again over all of it.

Behind her, up the slope, a sentry's whistle sounded — two short notes, the signal for movement on the gorge trail.

Someone was coming down out of the canyon country. Alone, by the sound of it. Limping, by the report that followed.

Ines stood a moment longer with the stars in the water at her feet, then turned uphill toward the torchlight, toward the locked doors and the chalked arithmetic and the stranger stumbling out of the dark carrying — she could already feel it, the way you feel weather in an old wound — carrying something that was going to change the sum entirely.
