## 9. Two Kinds of Truth

### Petra

They came for him at first light with a machete and a bucket of fish guts.

Not a threat — a summons. The woman holding the machete was maybe sixty, silver-braided hair, forearms like dock rope. She set the bucket down inside the cell's threshold and said, in Spanish worn smooth as river stone: "Ines says you carry loads or you don't eat. Choose."

Petra chose. His ribs still sang from the interrogation, his wrists wore the memory of the zip ties, but three days of sitting in the mission house had taught him that captivity was mostly boredom wearing a uniform of fear, and boredom he could survive. He hoisted the pack they gave him — dried fish, cassava flour, a coil of rope, salt — and found it weighed nearly as much as his old expedition kit. Forty kilos, give or take. Enough to keep a man honest on a trail.

The trail kept a man honest by hurting him.

They went downriver, which surprised him. Downstream meant toward the world, toward the consortium's reach, toward everything Ines Calloway supposedly wanted to wall out. But the column moved with purpose: six villagers, two dugout canoes poled through the shallows, Petra carrying his share in silence at the rear where they could watch him. The dry season had begun its theft in earnest. Sandbars stood exposed where the maps showed current; the water ran thin and glassy over stones that should have been drowned, and the fish smell off the shallows was thick enough to chew.

The guide ahead of him was a boy of perhaps sixteen, barefoot on rock that would have flayed Petra's boots in an hour. The boy never looked back. Behind them all, half a day's march, came Ines.

She didn't speak to him until the second morning.

He heard her before he saw her — the particular cadence of someone giving orders without raising her voice, each sentence landing like a stone placed in a wall. Then she drew level with him on a shelf of trail above a rapids, and for a while they walked in the same silence, two people pretending the other wasn't there.

"You walk well," she said finally, in English. Her accent was strange — not local, not gringo. Something coastal, something learned and then half-forgotten. "For a spy."

"For a hostage, you mean."

"Those aren't different things here." She glanced at his hands, at the rope burns layered under newer ones. "Ochoa trained you?"

"Nobody trained me. I guided. Fifteen years."

"Guiding." She said the word the way another person might say *arson*. "You led people into places for money."

"I led people safely into places for money. It's a different profession than people think."

"And when you didn't?" She let it hang there. She knew. Of course she knew — Ochoa's name would have opened every file in the valley, and files were one thing the consortium had never managed to burn. "Two clients. A mountain called—"

"Don't." The word came out harder than he intended, and the boy ahead turned to look, and Petra made himself breathe. "You want to know what kind of man you're feeding. Ask me anything else."

She studied him with eyes the color of the river in shadow, and whatever she saw seemed to settle something, though not in his favor. "Tonight," she said. "There's something I want you to see. And then you'll show me what Halvorsen gave you. Yes — I know about the ledger pages. My people searched your boots while you slept. Don't look so offended; you'd have done worse."

That night, downstream of everything, she built a small fire in the lee of a bluff and showed him what her war was made of.

* * *

### Ines

She took him down to the water after dark, when the village below the bluff had gone quiet except for dogs and one guitar played badly and with great devotion. She wanted him to see it the way she had first seen it: at night, when the truth of a place stops hiding behind its scenery.

"This is Puerto Ceniza," she said. "Was. There are forty-one people living here now. There used to be six hundred."

The village sat on a terrace above the river, and even in starlight Petra could see what was wrong with it. The houses were built for higher ground. Doorways opened onto air. Staircases descended from front porches into water that lapped, patient as a creditor, at the third step. A church bell tower rose out of the flood itself, the bell hanging silent ten feet above the surface, green with rot, and beneath it the water held the shapes of rooftops like bodies under a sheet.

"The dam," Petra said quietly. Not a question.

"There is no dam." Ines crouched at the waterline, and her reflection broke and reformed around her voice. "That's what no one understands. There doesn't need to be. They bought the watershed above us — logging concessions, road contracts, a gravel operation in the headwaters. Every concession was legal. Every permit was stamped. And the river did the rest." She flicked water from her fingers. "Silt choked the channel. The floods came higher each year until the government declared it a natural disaster, which is the most elegant lie ever told, because nature can't be sued. Six hundred people became refugees of a paperwork accident."

Petra said nothing. There was nothing honest to say that she hadn't already survived saying herself.

"My father fished this stretch for thirty years. He drowned in it — in his own river, in water that shouldn't have been there, chasing a boat that belonged to a man who'd already lost everything else." She stood, brushing sand from her knees with unnecessary force. "I went to the capital. I filed complaints. I stood outside the ministry with a photograph of my father until security carried me away. And do you know what I learned? The law is a door that only opens from their side." She turned to face him, firelight from the bluff catching the edge of her jaw. "So I stopped knocking. I came upriver, past the gorge, past the maps, and I found people who remembered another way to hold ground. We don't ask anymore. That's what your scientists walked into. Not a kidnapping. A border."

"They're not surveyors."

"Some of them are. Halvorsen told you himself — you think I haven't listened through that wall? Half his team carried consortium instruments and consortium orders. The other half didn't know." Her voice tightened. "Do you understand what that means, Petra Reyes? It means the man who tells the truth and the man who lies came here in the same boat, wearing the same faces, and I'm supposed to sort them apart with what? Their smiles?"

"With data," Petra said. "With the ledger. With the stakes."

"Stakes."

"Tomorrow. On our way back. There's a place Tomás wouldn't go past, but you will." He met her eyes, and for the first time since the sentries had taken his pack, he felt something other than fear — the cold clean feeling of a man who has found the one thing he's certain of. "You've shown me your dead. Let me show you theirs being planned."

* * *

### Petra

The gorge again, but from the landward side this time, and with company.

Ines climbed like someone who had been arguing with mountains her whole life and winning on points — no wasted motion, no fear of exposure, just a continuous economical violence against gravity. She brought four of her people, including the boy, and left two at the rim with rifles they clearly knew how to use. At the narrows where Tomás had turned back weeks ago, she stopped only long enough to spit into the current, some private benediction or curse, and then kept walking.

The stakes were exactly where he'd left them: driven into the gravel bar at the gorge mouth, aluminum, orange-capped, stamped with the logo he had spent five years trying to forget. *Consorcio Aguas del Norte.* The letters sat in their little oval like a brand on a hide.

Ines knelt beside the nearest stake and touched the stamp with one finger, the way you touch a scar to confirm it's yours.

"How many?"

"Eleven that I found between here and the mission house. Probably more above the falls. They run a line up the valley — elevation benchmarks, flow gauges buried in the banks. This isn't reconnaissance, it's engineering. Somebody already chose the dam site."

"When."

"The gauges are calibrated. Fresh batteries. Weeks, not months." He watched her face do something complicated — fury arriving and being put to work, the way a good guide puts fear to work. "And this." He drew the ledger pages from the oilskin pouch inside his shirt, and her sentries tensed, and she waved them down. "Halvorsen's field book. The real one. Flow rates, sediment loads, seasonal minimums — numbers his own employers paid him to lose. Look at page twelve."

She read standing, lips moving slightly, and Petra watched her translate the columns into consequences faster than he could have. When she reached the bottom of the page she closed the book with exaggerated care, the way you close a door on a room where something terrible is sleeping.

"These flows," she said. "During dry season, the Río Sombra runs at—" She stopped. Started again. "Every community from the mission down depends on this river in the dry months. If they impound it upstream, we don't get flooded. We get *emptied*. Slowly. Legally. A drought with a signature on it."

"Now you know what I know."

"No." She handed the pages back, and her hand was steady, and her eyes were not. "Now I know what *you* say I should know. You were Vane's man once. These are Vane's numbers. Maybe the trap is the ledger itself — maybe the whole story, the blacklisted guide, the confession through the wall, is built to walk me exactly here, to make me trust paper because a handsome stranger carried it in his shirt." She stepped close enough that he could see the sweat tracking dust down her temple. "Tell me why I shouldn't believe that."

"Because if I wanted to destroy you, I'd tell you nothing and let the dry season do it." He held her gaze. "Because I've been destroyed by these people once already, and I know the smell of the work. And because—" He almost said *because I need you to believe me*, and swallowed it. "Because the numbers don't care who carries them. Check them yourself. You have a river. Measure it."

For a long moment the gorge filled the silence with its bell-sound water, and Ines Calloway looked at him the way you look at a route you don't want to climb but see no way around.

"Half," she said at last. "I believe you halfway. That's more than I believed you yesterday. Don't spend it all at once."

* * *

### Ines

They camped that night on a ledge above the confluence, and she could not sleep, and neither, she judged from his breathing, could he.

She lay watching the stars wheel over the canyon rim and turned the ledger over in her mind like a stone she couldn't stop rubbing. Halfway. She had told him halfway, and it was true, and the terrifying part was how much of her wanted it to be all the way. Because if the ledger was real, then the war she had built her life around had a shape she could finally fight — not the fog of legal abstractions, but gauges and benchmarks and a dam site she could stand on and refuse. And if it was false, then she had held six innocent men captive for nothing, and her people had become the thing she'd sworn they'd never be.

Both possibilities cost her something. That was how she knew at least one of them was true.

"Reyes." Her voice was low. Across the coals, his outline shifted. "The avalanche. Five years ago. Tell me what actually happened."

A long silence. Long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Vane's company cut the access road above the couloir against every warning I filed," he said finally. "I refused to take clients up that day. They replaced me with a cheaper guide and sent the group anyway. Two died. The report blamed my route plan — a forged one. The blacklist followed within the month." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "You asked what happened. What happened is I learned the same lesson you did. The law opens from their side."

"Why are you really here? Ochoa's contract. Say it plainly."

"To find them. To bring out whoever wanted bringing. To be paid, because I am broke and ashamed in roughly equal measure." The coals ticked. "And somewhere on the river, without deciding to, I started wanting something else instead. I haven't finished deciding what."

She considered that in the dark. It was, she thought, the most dangerous thing a man could say to her — not a lie, which she could catch and kill, but an unfinished truth, still moving, still capable of going anywhere.

"Halvorsen's honest people," she said. "If the ledger clears them, they go free. All of them. Even the ones whose colleagues spied for Vane. That's the price of your truth."

"Yes."

"And Vane walks? A man who flooded a valley and buried a mountain and forged your name — he gets lawyers and hearings while my father stays under that water?"

"He gets what the law gives. Which is less than he deserves and more than we can take ourselves." Petra's voice was rough. "I know how that tastes. I've been chewing it for five years."

"It tastes like Puerto Ceniza," she said. "Like a doorway full of river."

She heard him move, sit up. "Then help me make the law work for once. Not because it deserves it. Because it's the only weapon that hits them where they live — in the permits, in the funding, in the boardrooms. Your rifle can't reach those. The ledger can."

Below them the confluence murmured, two waters becoming one and losing both their names. Ines stared into the dark until the stars blurred, hating how much she wanted to believe him, hating more that wanting was the right word for it.

Before dawn, one of her sentries came down the trail at a run — the boy, breathless, face gray in the last of the night.

"Ines." He caught his breath. "The camp below the falls. The one the scientists' friends use. It's gone — struck in the night, everything burned. And there are tracks coming upriver." He swallowed. "Armed tracks. A lot of them. Heading for the source."
