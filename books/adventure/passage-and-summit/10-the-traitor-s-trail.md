## 10. The Traitor's Trail

The alarm came as a silence.

Petra had been sleeping in the granary behind the mission house, one ear tuned the way five empty years had taught him to tune it — not to sound, but to its absence. The sentry's footsteps that should have circled at midnight didn't. The dog that always complained about the wind near the corral said nothing. And when he opened his eyes to the strip of moonlight through the slats, he saw dust hanging in it, fine and slow, the way dust hangs after something has already passed through a room.

He was on his feet before he understood why, and by the time he reached the mission house door, Ines was there too, barefoot in the cold, her hand raised to stop him before he could speak.

"The geologist," she said. "Marek. His cot is empty. The window of the east cell is open."

"Which way?"

"Downriver." Her jaw tightened around the word like it was a stone she'd been chewing all night. "Toward your people."

*Not my people,* he almost said, and didn't, because the correction would have been a lie with better grammar.

They stood together in the doorway while the community woke around them — lamps kindled behind shutters, low voices, a child crying somewhere and being hushed. Petra looked at the canyon mouth where the Río Sombra bent north into country he'd crossed only once, half-dead, guided by desperation and the memory of contour lines. Marek had a six-hour head start if he'd left at dusk. He had copied data — Halvorsen had admitted as much through the wall, the ledger pages photographed or transcribed, Petra still wasn't sure which — and data didn't tire, didn't blister, didn't second-guess itself in the dark.

But men did all three.

"I can take him," Petra said. "If I leave now, I catch him before the narrows."

Ines turned to look at him fully, and in the lamplight her face was the face of someone doing arithmetic she hated. "And then what? You bring him back and we keep him until the river dies and Vane's men come for all of us anyway?"

"And then you have time," Petra said. "Time is the only thing up here anyone's ever had enough of."

She studied him a long moment. Whatever she saw — and he'd stopped trying to guess what she saw, because every guess had been wrong so far — made her nod once, sharply, and turn away to wake the council.

He packed in ten minutes. Water for two days, though he hoped for one. The ledger pages Halvorsen had pressed into his hands through the bars, wrapped in oilcloth against sweat and rain. A coil of rope he'd cut down to save weight. No gun — the valley had none to give him, and the truth was he'd never liked carrying one; a weapon made decisions faster than a man could think them, and thinking was the only thing he had left that was entirely his own.

Tomás met him at the water stair with a lamp.

"You're going past the gorge again," Tomás said. Not a question.

"Just the canyon this time. The dry route."

"The dry route is where men go to disappear." Tomás held out the lamp anyway, and then, when Petra took it, caught his wrist. His grip was surprisingly strong for a man who spent his life sitting down. "Petra. If you find this Marek. What will you do?"

It was the question Petra had been asking himself since the window had come open, and he still didn't have an answer he could say out loud. So he said the truest thing adjacent to it: "I'll decide when I see his face."

Tomás let go. "That's what my father used to say about snakes," he said, and went back inside without saying goodbye.

* * *

The canyon country above the gorge was a landscape built out of absence. The river ran somewhere below, audible more than visible, a long breathing in the dark between walls of red rock that rose on either side like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here to die a thousand years ago and hadn't finished. Petra moved along the high trail where the smugglers' path clung to the cliff face, and the moon did him the courtesy of being nearly full, laying the trail out in silver and black so that every step was a small decision made visible.

Marek was easy to track and hard to understand. That was the trouble.

The man traveled like a scientist and not a soldier — resting too long, drinking at the wrong seeps, dropping things. Twice Petra found places where he'd sat with his back to a boulder for what must have been an hour, judging by the flattened dust and the scatter of crumbs from the mission's hard bread. But he also traveled smart in ways that didn't fit: cutting across switchbacks where the rock was good, keeping off the skyline, moving in the cool hours and sleeping through heat. Someone had trained him. Or someone had written him a route.

At dawn Petra crouched over the first real message the trail had given him: a survey stake, driven into a crack in the trailbed, stamped with the consortium's mark — the stylized confluence, two lines meeting in a drop. Beside it, wedged under a stone, a strip of cloth torn from a shirt, blue, the color of the expedition's field shirts. A marker. *This way. I passed here.*

But marked for whom?

Petra sat back on his heels and let the question do its work, because it was the question he'd been carrying since Puerto Ladeado, since Ochoa's office, maybe since the avalanche, and the canyon gave it back to him with interest. If Marek was marking his route for a rescue party from the forward camp, then the man was loyal, and stopping him meant protecting the valley from the truth reaching Vane. If Marek was marking it for himself — insurance, a breadcrumb trail home in case the camp had moved — then the man was afraid, and afraid men told different stories than loyal ones.

And if — the thought arrived the way cold air arrives in a canyon, sliding down without warning — if Marek wanted to be followed?

He got up and walked on, and the canyon walked with him, and the question kept pace like a third traveler neither of them acknowledged.

By midmorning the walls narrowed and the light changed, coming down in blades instead of sheets, and Petra began to pass the signs of older traffic than Marek's. Old drill casings rusting in a side wash. A faded ribbon of flagging tape gone gray as cobweb. And then, in a place where the trail widened into a shelf above the river's murmur, a whole cache: crates stenciled with the consortium logo, split and long ago picked over, but one crate still sealed, and inside it — he pried the lid with his knife — rolls of paper. Maps. He unrolled one against the rock and the breath went out of him slowly.

It was the valley. All of it. Contoured, gridded, annotated in a neat engineer's hand, with the flood line drawn in blue ink at the level the dam would create, and the mission house a small careful symbol beneath the blue, and the village downstream — Ines's drowned village, the one she'd shown him, the one that started her war — lying under the blue like a thing already buried.

Someone had surveyed this valley years ago. Before Halvorsen. Before the disappearance. Before any of it. The consortium hadn't lost an expedition up here; it had planted one, the way you plant a seed in ground you've already bought and are waiting to flood.

Petra rolled the map with hands that were not quite steady and put it in his pack next to the ledger, and understood that he was no longer tracking a man. He was tracking a decision he'd have to make, and the decision was walking ahead of him wearing Marek's boots.

* * *

He caught sight of him in the afternoon, on the far side of a slot wash where the trail dropped toward the river.

Marek was a small figure at this distance, a smudge of blue against red rock, moving with the stiff, deliberate care of a man whose feet had failed him hours ago and who had decided not to inform the rest of his body. He was limping badly. At the rate he was going, Petra could close the distance in an hour, less if he ran the scree.

He didn't run the scree.

He sat down instead, in the shade of an overhang, and drank half his water, and made himself look at the arithmetic honestly, because the canyon offered nothing else to do and honesty was the one discipline he'd never managed to abandon even when it ruined him.

If he caught Marek, the data stayed in the valley. The valley stayed hidden. Vane stayed ignorant a little longer, and Ines's people stayed alive a little longer, and the lie they were all living upriver continued — but so did the danger, coiled and patient, because Vane would send more expeditions, better ones, armed ones, and next time there would be no Halvorsen to confess through a wall, no ledger, no warning.

If he let Marek run, the data reached Vane. Vane learned exactly how much the valley knew, exactly what Halvorsen had documented, and adjusted accordingly — and Petra knew with the certainty of a man who had watched Marcus Vane eat lunch while discussing severance packages what Vane did with inconvenient knowledge. He erased it. Erased the knowledge, erased the knowers, erased the valley if he had to. The data wouldn't free anyone. It would just tell the executioner where everyone was standing.

And yet. And yet some traitor part of him, the part that had spent five years wanting his name back, whispered the old seduction: *let it run, let Vane see the ledger, let Vane come to you, let Vane offer you what he offered before — and this time don't burn it.* The whisper sounded like his own voice. That was the worst of it. Seduction never sounds like a stranger.

Petra screwed the cap back onto the canteen and stood.

"No," he said aloud, to the canyon, to the whisper, to whatever was listening. The word fell into the red silence and didn't echo. Canyons don't give anything back. That was why he trusted them.

He went down the slope at a controlled slide, boots skidding in the talus, and cut the angle across the wash, and by the time the sun touched the western rim he was close enough to see Marek clearly for the first time: a thin man in his fifties, gray-stubbled, glasses missing one lens, shirt torn at the shoulder where the blue cloth had been sacrificed to markers. He was standing very still at the edge of a drop, staring down at the river far below, and he was not looking for a path down.

He was looking at something on the far bank.

Petra followed his gaze and felt the bottom drop out of the day.

There was a camp on the far bank. Small, disciplined, wrong. Two tents, a mast antenna, a boat pulled up on the gravel — and figures moving with the unhurried economy of professionals. The consortium's forward camp, advanced farther upriver than anyone had guessed, close enough now that the dry season's shrinking river was bringing it within reach of the mission house like a tide coming in.

Marek hadn't been running toward it blindly. He'd been running toward a signal fire he couldn't light, waiting at the edge of the water, trying to judge whether he could swim the channel with his ruined feet and his oilskin packet of copies — or whether he should wait for dark and risk the crossing when the camp's lamps would show him the rocks.

Petra lay flat behind a fallen slab and did the last of the arithmetic, the part he'd been avoiding since the granary.

He could stop Marek here. Take the packet, take the man, walk him back upriver, and the valley's secret held — for a season, maybe two. Or he could let the packet cross the river tonight, and everything Halvorsen had confessed, everything the ledger proved, would land in the hands of the one man alive who knew how to make evidence disappear.

Unless the evidence didn't reach Vane's hands. Unless it reached other hands first.

Petra looked at the packet bulging in Marek's breast pocket, and at the camp, and at the long red miles between here and the mission house, and understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like vertigo, what he had to do — not stop the truth, not free it, but *steer* it. Take the packet himself. Let Marek believe his delivery had succeeded. And carry the copies somewhere Vane would never think to look: not downriver, not to the camp, but back up — to Ines, to the council, to the one place in the world where the data might become testimony instead of fuel.

He was still forming the plan when the canyon betrayed him — a shift of wind, a scrape of boot on talus, his own body reminding him he was fifty-one years old and had been awake for thirty hours. Marek's head came up sharply, a heron startled off a bank, and his eyes swept the slope and fixed, impossibly, precisely, on the shadow behind the slab.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Marek did something Petra had not expected in any version of the arithmetic.

He smiled. And raised one hand — not in surrender, not in greeting, but pointing, deliberately, past Petra's shoulder, up the slope behind him.

Petra turned.

Three figures stood on the rim of the wash above, silhouetted against the last of the light, rifles slung, and the tallest of them lifted a hand in a lazy salute that Petra recognized the way you recognize a scar — because he'd seen it before, years ago, across a conference table in a glass tower, from a man who had said *we'll take care of you, Petra, we always take care of our own.*

Marcus Vane's men had found him first.
