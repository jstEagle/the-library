## 11. Camp Perdida

The traitor's trail ended where the canyon did.

Petra crouched at the lip of the last bend and looked down into a bowl of light. The forward camp sat on a gravel terrace above the river like something that had been dropped there from another world — canvas domes, aluminum towers, a helicopter pad stamped with an H that caught the afternoon sun and threw it back hard enough to hurt. Generators hummed somewhere below the sound of the water. Strings of work lights were already strung between poles, though the sun was still two hours from the ridgeline, as if whoever ran this place couldn't bear to let the dark arrive unmanaged.

He had followed Renner — Dr. Aldous Renner, glaciologist, liar — for three days through country that wanted him dead. Up scree chutes that moved under his boots like water deciding to flow. Across a saddle where the wind had stripped the snow to blue ice and he'd crawled on his elbows, listening to his own breath crackle in his chest. Down into this canyon, where the air went warm and still and smelled of diesel and cut pine, and the river ran green with glacier flour, beautiful and opaque, keeping its own counsel.

Renner had come down the switchbacks an hour ahead of him, walking fast, walking like a man going home. Petra had watched him pass the sentry post without challenge. Watched him shake hands with someone in a green parka whose face he couldn't see at this distance. And then Petra had done the arithmetic every guide learns eventually: one man against a camp of thirty is not courage, it's weather. You wait it out. You read the sky.

So he waited. He lay in the shadow of a granite fin above the camp while the sun crossed and the shadows swung like compass needles, and he mapped the place the way he'd map any route: hazards, anchors, escape lines. The helipad. The comms tent with its dish aimed at a sky full of other people's business. The mess dome, glowing now as the light failed, spilling yellow onto the gravel. The fuel cache, bermed and tarped. Two sentries at the lower trailhead, bored, stamping their feet. One man with a rifle walking the perimeter in a loop that took eleven minutes — Petra timed him twice, because eleven minutes was either sloppy or deliberate, and he needed to know which.

The cold came down off the icefields in a slow flood. He ate half a ration bar and let the rest melt in his mouth because chewing made noise, and he thought about Ines standing in the flooded church downstream of everything, her voice level as a survey line: *They came to measure what they meant to take.* He thought about Halvorsen behind the cell wall, whispering numbers like a man confessing sins in the dark. *Half my team never believed in the expedition. They believed in the invoice.*

And he thought about the name on the stake he'd found in the gorge, stamped deep into the aluminum so no weather would ever wear it away: **VANE HYDROLOGICAL CONSORTIUM**.

Five years, and the mountain had carried the name upriver ahead of him.

* * *

He went in during the change of watch, when the perimeter walker stopped at the mess dome for coffee and the sentries at the trailhead turned their backs to the wind. He came down the scree on the north side where the gravel gave silently, crossed forty meters of open ground with his heart going like a fist on a door, and slid under the gap in the tarped fuel berm into a corridor of diesel stink and shadow. From there it was tents and more tents, guy-lines strung low, generators breathing behind sound baffles. A camp built by people who expected to be here a long time. A camp built by people who expected no visitors.

He told himself he was looking for the data. That was the mission — his contract, his leverage, whatever word you used when you'd already decided what you were willing to become. The comms tent first. Files, drives, anything with the consortium's logo. Get out before the watch changed again.

But the comms tent was empty, and the field desk inside it held nothing but radio logs, and the top log sheet was signed in a hand he knew.

He hadn't seen that handwriting in five years. He'd have known it blind. It slanted forward like a man leaning into a deal — *M. Vane* — and beside it, in the margin, a note in the same confident script: *R. arrived w/ copies. Full set to follow. Advise source party re: timeline.*

Petra stood very still in the dark of the tent and let the generator's hum fill his ears, and the past came up the river to meet him.

* * *

The avalanche had happened on a Tuesday.

That was the detail everyone remembered afterward, the absurd domesticity of it — not a storm day, not a forecast failure, just a Tuesday in late spring, blue and windless, the kind of day guides pray for. Petra had been leading the second rope team below the icefall on Monte Serrano, four clients strung out behind him, when the slope above the couloir let go. Not a sluff. Not a release. The whole hanging glacier, a hundred thousand tons of it, coming down the mountain like a verdict.

Two clients dead. The other two pulled out of the debris with broken bones and frostbitten faces and eyes that never quite focused on him again. And Petra Reyes, who had checked the forecast twice, who had turned back from the same route in worse conditions the year before, who had done everything right — Petra Reyes became the man who led clients under a hanging glacier on a bluebird Tuesday.

The inquiry took nine months. The consortium — Vane Hydrological, which had been running its survey season on Serrano that same spring, which had helicopters in the valley — the consortium produced seismic records showing a natural release. Spontaneous calving. Act of God, act of ice, nobody's fault, certainly not the fault of any guided party. The report was thorough. The report was airtight. The report buried him, and the industry closed over his name like water over a stone, and Marcus Vane himself had called him once, near the end, to say how sorry he was, how these things happened, how if there was ever anything—

Petra had hung up. He'd spent five years telling himself the call was pity. That Vane was a stranger who ran a company that had merely been nearby, a bystander with a telephone.

The log sheet in front of him said otherwise. *Advise source party re: timeline.*

Timeline. As in: the survey schedule on Serrano required the hanging glacier gone. As in: the consortium's seismic "records" had been manufactured after the fact to explain a detonation. As in: two clients and a guide's whole life had been the cost of clearing a hazard from a work zone.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to the younger man on the rope, the one who still believed the mountain killed people honestly.

"Petra."

The voice came from the tent flap, and it was warm, and it was terrible.

Marcus Vane stood silhouetted against the work lights, silver-haired now, heavier, dressed in expedition fleece worth more than everything Petra owned. Behind him, filling the flap, two men with rifles they hadn't bothered to raise. They didn't need to. This was Vane's camp, Vane's canyon, Vane's night.

"You always were early," Vane said. "I told them you'd come in from the north scree. Nobody bet on it." He stepped inside, and the men stayed at the flap, and for a moment it was almost like the old days — two professionals in a field office, the smell of coffee and cold nylon. Almost. "Sit down, Petra. You look like hell."

"I've been following your employee," Petra said. His voice came out flat, which surprised him. "Renner. He runs well for a scientist."

"He runs well because I pay him to run." Vane poured coffee from a thermos into two cups, an old habit performed without thinking, and pushed one across the desk. Petra didn't touch it. Vane drank his own, unhurried, watching Petra over the rim. "You found the stakes. Of course you found the stakes. You were always the best route-finder I ever employed." He set the cup down. "I'm sorry about the cell wall conversations. We could have arranged better accommodations if we'd known you were coming. Though frankly, I didn't believe Ochoa would find anyone desperate enough to take this job. I underestimated the market."

"The market." Petra heard his own voice from very far away. "You mean me."

"I mean the situation has developed." Vane sat, folding his hands, and for a moment the lamplight softened his face into something almost kind. "Petra. Listen to me. What do you think you know?"

"That you blew the hanging glacier on Serrano. That you wrote the report that buried me. That you've been doing the same thing up this river ever since — buying surveys, planting scientists, drowning villages downstream and calling it hydrology."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — not guilt. Recalculation. "The Serrano matter," he said carefully, "was a scheduling conflict. The glacier was unstable regardless. Our geotechnical assessment—"

"Your geotechnical assessment killed two people who trusted me."

"Your *route* killed them." It came out fast, and then Vane caught himself, smoothed it, and Petra understood suddenly that he was seeing the real man for the first time — not the employer, not the benefactor, but the thing underneath, patient and cold as the lake they meant to build. "No. No, listen. That's not why I came out here." He leaned forward. "Here's what's actually happening, Petra. The dam gets built. That's decided — ministries, financing, signatures, all of it done before you ever took Ochoa's money. The only question is how many people get hurt in the process. I can control that. I am controlling that. The relocation packages, the compensation schedules, the timing of the inundation — that's all me, fighting for soft landings in a room full of men who'd rather just clear the valley."

"And the raid on the mission house?"

"A contractor overreach. Being dealt with." Vane waved it away like smoke. "The point is this." He opened the desk drawer and took out a folder, and laid it on the wood between them, and turned it so Petra could read the tab: **REYES, P. — REINSTATEMENT FILE**. "Full certification review. The Serrano findings reopened and revised — there are documents, Petra, real ones, that show the original seismic interpretation was flawed. Flawed through error, not intent. Errors get corrected. Names get cleared. You walk back into the profession with five years of grief converted into a wrongful-blacklisting settlement and a public apology from the association." He tapped the folder once. "All of it requires one thing. Your silence about this valley. You were never here. You found nothing. The Halvorsen retrieval resolves quietly, the community resettles per schedule, and you go back to being what you were supposed to be."

The tent was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the perimeter walker's boots crunched past, eleven minutes exactly, and faded.

Petra looked at the folder. He looked at it the way a starving man looks through glass at bread, and he hated himself for looking, and he kept looking anyway. Five years. Five years of border-town hostels and under-the-table work and clients who googled his name and made excuses. Five years of measuring his life in distances traveled because there was nowhere to stand still. A name, cleaned. A trade, returned. The avalanche rewritten from murder back into weather.

"All you have to do," Vane said gently, "is be silent. You're good at silence, Petra. You've practiced."

And that was the thing — that was the hook in the flesh of it — that Petra could feel the temptation working, feel it fit him like old gear, familiar buckles and worn-in straps. It would be so easy. It would be so *deserved*. Who was he to spend other people's names to buy justice for a valley he'd known for two weeks? Ines would trade him to the consortium herself if she learned what he was. Halvorsen was a liar with a ledger. The villagers downstream were dead either way; the signatures existed; the ministers had eaten.

But underneath the wanting, quiet as groundwater, was the image that wouldn't drown: the flooded church, the bell tower standing in brown water like a raised hand. And the sound of Ines saying *the river keeps what the river keeps*, and the knowledge — sudden, total, physical, like the mountain letting go above him — that if he took this folder, he would be the avalanche. He would be the thing that comes down on a blue Tuesday, on ordinary people, on a valley that trusted the weather.

He would finally deserve the blacklist.

"No," Petra said.

Vane sighed, and the kindness left his face like water leaving a channel. "I was afraid you'd say that. I genuinely hoped—" He stood, buttoning his fleece, and nodded once toward the flap, and the two men came in and took Petra's arms, not roughly, professionally, which was worse. "Search him," Vane said. "Then put him in stores. And Petra—" He paused at the tent flap, backlit, framed by the lights of the camp he'd built on other people's rivers. "The offer stands until morning. After that, the file becomes what it always was. Paper."

They marched him across the gravel under the work lights, past the mess dome where someone laughed at something, past the helipad with its great white H, and Petra walked with his head down and his eyes moving, cataloguing, the way he'd catalogued every route he'd ever climbed: the fuel cache, the generator shed, the comms tent with its dish aimed at the indifferent sky, the crate of satellite equipment stacked by the door with its shipping manifest still stapled to the side.

*Advise source party re: timeline.*

Eleven-minute loops. Bored sentries. One helicopter, fueled, sitting fat and patient on the pad.

In the stores tent they zip-tied his wrists to a cargo rack and left him alone with the smell of rope wax and freeze-dried food, and the darkness came down over Camp Perdida like a lid.

Petra tested the zip ties once, twice, and then went still and started counting.

Not seconds. Options.

Outside, the generators hummed. The river ran on in the dark, green and secret, carrying everything it kept toward the sea — and somewhere upstream, in the high basin where it was born, a valley full of people was sleeping, believing they had until summer.

By morning, Vane's men would learn what Petra Reyes had already learned in the dark of the comms tent, reading that slanted, forward-leaning handwriting:

Some timelines run both ways.

He began, silently, to work the ties against the edge of the cargo rack.
