## 12. Refusal

The fire Vane built was small and civilized, the way everything about him was small and civilized — a ring of stones, a kettle of river water coming to a boil, two tin cups set out on a flat rock like an offering to a god he didn't believe in. He poured coffee from a dented percolator with the ease of a man who had done this ten thousand times in ten thousand places where other people's lives were being decided.

"Still take it black," he said. Not a question.

Petra stood at the edge of the firelight and did not sit. Behind Vane's tent, the consortium camp lay quiet in the blue hour before dawn: canvas structures staked into the canyon shelf above the Río Sombra, a satellite dish aimed at the indifferent stars, generators silent because silence was cheaper than fuel and drew less attention. Somewhere in that cluster of nylon and steel were the complete hydrology files — Halvorsen's real data, the numbers that would drown this valley on paper before any water touched it.

"You knew I'd come," Petra said.

"I knew someone would." Vane handed him a cup anyway, and after a moment Petra took it, because his hands were shaking and he hated that Vane could see it. "The sentry you put to sleep at the perimeter had a distinctive way of tying off his watch rotation. Sloppy. Yours, once upon a time. I taught it to you myself."

Five years collapsed like a snowpack letting go. Petra could smell the basecamp at Cerro del Juicio again — diesel and freeze-dried stew and the particular cold of altitude before dawn. He could see Vane's face in the light of a cook tent, younger then, explaining why the incident report needed certain phrases removed. *Clients assume risk, Petra. That's what waivers are for. You want to help their families? Fine. But not by feeding the lawyers.*

"You read my file," Petra said. "You know what happened."

"I wrote your file." Vane smiled, and it was almost warm, which was the cruelest thing about him. "Sit down. We have until first light, and I'd rather give you good news than bad."

* * *

Petra sat. It cost him something to do it, and he understood, watching Vane's satisfaction settle over his face like frost, that sitting had been the point. Obedience measured in inches. Vane had always been a man who collected small surrenders and called them rapport.

"The avalanche wasn't your fault," Vane said. "I want you to hear that from me directly, not from a lawyer, not in a deposition. The route assessment was sound. Your judgment that morning was sound. What failed was the cornice loading model we were using — outdated, underfunded, a procurement decision made in an office in Denver by people whose names you've never heard. When it came apart, there was nothing you or anyone else could have done."

The words landed in Petra's chest like stones dropped down a well. He waited for the splash. It didn't come. Five years of carrying those two names — Aldana and Reyes-Kim, he still said them every morning like a penance, the guide and the geologist who'd trusted him — five years of building his whole ruined life on top of that weight, and now a man with a kettle of coffee was telling him the weight had never been his.

"And you let me believe otherwise," Petra said. "For five years."

"I contained a situation." Vane's voice didn't change, didn't harden; that was the horror of it. "Two dead clients, a consortium mid-negotiation for three concessions along that range, and a press corps already circling. If the report had said what the raw data said — that our own survey flagged that slope as unstable six weeks earlier and nobody acted on it — the consortium dies, the concessions die, and a hundred people who work for us lose their livelihoods. So I made a choice. I chose the many over the few. And you—" he gestured with his cup, gentle, almost kind— "you were the few. I'm sorry for that. Genuinely. It was the arithmetic available to me at the time."

"The arithmetic." Petra looked into the fire. A log shifted and threw sparks up toward the canyon walls, and for a moment the whole gorge seemed full of small red ascensions, brief and doomed. "Is that what you call the valley? Arithmetic?"

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — not guilt, Petra realized. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own language spoken back to him by someone who meant it as an accusation.

"The valley is a variable," Vane said. "A large one, I'll grant you. Four hundred souls downstream of the proposed reservoir, seasonal agriculture, a mission house older than the country that claims it. I've read Ines Calloway's file too. Impressive woman. Wrong, but impressive." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving his face into planes and shadows. "Here is what I can offer you, Petra, and I offer it once. Full reinstatement. Lead guide certification restored, back pay, a public correction of the incident record — published, notarized, the works. Your name cleaned in every registry from here to Anchorage. And a position running safety operations for the entire Sombra corridor, which means when the dam goes in, you're the man who decides how the relocation happens. Humanely. With your hand on the wheel instead of your face in the mud."

He let it sit. Down in the dark below the shelf, the river ran, low and thinning, the dry season eating it a centimeter a night. Petra could hear it the way you hear a clock in a quiet room.

"You're offering to clear my name," Petra said slowly, "in exchange for my silence about a valley full of people who are about to lose everything they have."

"I'm offering you absolution," Vane said. "Which is more than anyone else in your life has managed."

And that was the blade, turned just so. Because it was true. No one else had offered. The licensing board hadn't, the families hadn't, the river towns that had blacklisted him by reputation hadn't. Five years of doors closing, and here was the one man responsible for all of it holding the only door open, and behind the door was everything Petra had wanted since the morning he dug two bodies out of the snow: to be believed. To be clean. To walk into Puerto Ladeado and buy rope without the shopkeeper's eyes sliding away.

Vane watched him weigh it. That was the worst part — being watched while wanting, like a animal caught at a bait line.

* * *

"I need to tell you what I saw today," Petra said.

Vane's expression flickered. "Petra—"

"Downstream. Before I climbed up here. There's a village — or what's left of one. San Pedro de las Aguas Bajas, if the sign at the ferry crossing was right. The reservoir from the consortium's last project, the one on the Río Claro. Half the town is underwater. Permanently. There are fence posts standing in four feet of green water and a church roof you can see on calm days, and people living on the ridge above it in houses built out of whatever they salvaged, looking down at their own streets every time the wind stops." His voice stayed level, which surprised him. "I stood there for an hour. There was a boy fishing off what used to be his schoolyard. He showed me where the bell tower was. He was proud of knowing. Nobody had told him it was anything to grieve."

"That was a different project," Vane said. "Different engineers, different decade, different regulatory environment—"

"It's the same arithmetic." Petra set the coffee cup down on the rock between them, untouched, and stood. "That's the whole thing, Marcus. That's the entire offer, in one image. You clear my name, and somewhere downstream of this valley there's a church roof under green water, and some kid grows up proud of knowing where it is. And I'd be the man who decided how gently they drowned."

"Or," Vane said, and now the warmth was gone, replaced by the flat patience of a man moving to his second instrument, "you refuse, and I ruin you properly this time. Not rumor. Documentation. Trespassing on a secured site, theft of proprietary materials, association with armed insurgents holding hostages — I have photographs of you at the mission house, Petra. I have witnesses. I can make sure you never guide so much as a day hike for the rest of your life, and I can make sure the Calloway woman's people answer for kidnapping under a law that still carries twenty years in this province." He rose too, unhurried, straightening his jacket. "Absolution or annihilation. Those are the terms. I'd hoped we wouldn't need the second half of the conversation."

"You hoped wrong."

"No," Vane said quietly. "I didn't. I knew you'd refuse. I've known since the moment I saw your name on the retrieval contract — Ochoa's little off-book arrangement, did you think I didn't have him mapped? I offered anyway because men deserve to hear their choices spoken aloud before they die by them." He glanced past Petra's shoulder, toward the dark tree line at the edge of the shelf, and raised his voice slightly. "Raúl. Walk Mr. Marchetti to the lower trail and hold him there until the helicopter comes. Don't damage him. He's going to be useful as a witness either way."

Figures detached themselves from the dark — two of them, rifles slung casual, moving with the loose competence of men who had done ugly errands before breakfast. Petra's heart went very slow and very loud. He thought: *he's not going to kill me. He's going to keep me. A witness who confirms the hostage story, a cautionary tale, a leash.* Vane never destroyed what he could repurpose.

"Last chance," Vane said. "The coffee's getting cold."

"It was cold five years ago," Petra said. "You just kept pouring."

Then he ran.

* * *

Not toward the lower trail — toward the tents. It was the only direction they wouldn't expect, the geometry of it all wrong, uphill through the heart of the camp with Raúl shouting behind him and the second man cutting for the flank. Petra had spent five years learning terrain the way other men learned scripture, and he knew what Vane had forgotten: that a camp is a landscape, and every landscape has a grain. The mess tent's guy lines. The generator's fuel drums stacked against a storage module. The comms tent with its satellite dish, its cable runs taped down in neat yellow lanes —

—and its field server, because no operation Vane ran traveled anywhere without its data, and data lived in gray ruggedized cases, and there were three such cases racked inside the comms tent beside a charging station blinking soft green in the pre-dawn gloom.

He went through the flap at a shoulder and hit the rack with both hands. Two cases went down clattering. He grabbed the third — heavier, its lid stenciled with the consortium's mark, the same logo he'd found stamped on the survey stake in the gorge, the same logo from the incident report he'd never been allowed to read — and tore the tether strap free of the charging dock. Behind him the tent flap burst open and Raúl came through low and fast, and Petra swung the case in a flat arc that caught the man across the temple and put him into the folding table with a sound like a drum.

Then the world outside became noise.

The first shot went high — a warning, or panic, or both — and cracked the night open. Petra dove out the back seam of the tent as the second and third rounds came through where he'd been standing, canvas ripping, a lantern inside bursting into oily flame. He ran. The camp woke around him like a kicked hive: shouts in two languages, generator lights snapping on, muzzle flashes strobing the canyon walls so that for split seconds the whole gorge stood revealed in white — the river far below, silver and thinning; the cliff trails; the black mouth of the drainage he'd scouted at dusk because some part of him had known, even while accepting Vane's coffee, that he would need a way out that wasn't a way in.

Bullets chased him into the trees. One struck stone near his head and sprayed his cheek with grit; another kissed his outer thigh, a slap of heat and then a strange distant cold, and he stumbled, caught himself on a sapling, and kept moving because stopping was a decision and he had already made the only decision that mattered tonight. The drainage took him steeply down through shadow and wet fern, the case banging his hip, blood filling his boot with each stride, and above him the gunfire stuttered, argued with itself, and fell away — because Vane's men knew these slopes no better than the dark did, and chasing a wounded man downhill into unknown canyon at night was arithmetic even Marcus Vane wouldn't sign.

* * *

He reached the river an hour before dawn, at a place where the water spread shallow over pale stone, barely knee-deep, the dry season having stripped it to its bones. He waded in without hesitating. The cold closed over the wound and he gasped, bent double, one hand on the current, the other locked around the case's handle like a drowning man's fist.

Behind him, upstream, the fires of Camp Perdida glowed faint against the canyon walls. Ahead, downstream, the Río Sombra ran dark toward the mission house, toward Ines, toward four hundred people who would wake this morning not knowing that the paper that would drown them had just changed hands.

Petra knelt in the shallows and let the river hold him up. His leg throbbed in time with his pulse. He was bleeding, hunted, unemployed beyond any hope of employment, and by sunrise Vane would have every mercenary and every magistrate in the province looking for him. Nothing would ever be reinstated. No record would ever be corrected. He had burned the offer — not with fire, but with something hotter and less forgiving, the plain refusal of a man who finally understood that a name cleared by silence isn't a name at all, just a cleaner cage.

And underneath the fear, underneath the pain, something in him was — there was no other word for it — free. The avalanche, the blacklist, the five empty years: none of it had ever been his to carry, and none of it mattered anymore, because he had stopped trying to get back to the life before and started walking into the one after. Exile, then. Permanent, self-chosen, purchased with blood in a cold river. He laughed once, a short broken sound the water swallowed, because liberation had turned out to look exactly like losing everything, and no one had warned him they were the same door.

He opened the case in the starlight. Inside, nested in foam, a drive and a sheaf of printed tables — stage-discharge curves, basin yields, inundation maps with the valley's villages marked in red like wounds. Halvorsen's true ledger. Everything.

Petra sealed it, shouldered it, and turned downstream, limping into the current, toward the mission house, toward Ines Calloway, toward the war he had just made himself a soldier of.

Behind him, far up the canyon, a helicopter lifted off from Camp Perdida and came downriver, hunting.
