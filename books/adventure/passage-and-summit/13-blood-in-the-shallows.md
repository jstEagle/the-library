## 13. Blood in the Shallows

Ines Calloway woke before dawn to the sound of the river lying.

She had lived beside the Río Sombra long enough to know its voices — the low gossip of the shallows, the deep-throated argument of the gorge, the thin whistle it made through the rocks when the dry season was strangling it. But this morning the river was quiet in a way that had nothing to do with water. It was the quiet of a thing holding its breath, and Ines lay in her hammock in the mission house's eastern room and listened to that held breath until her own chest ached.

Then the dogs began.

Not barking — she would have known barking, half-asleep, from any dream. This was different: a rising, ragged chorus from the southern sentry post, the dogs of the Herrera family throwing their voices upriver in panic, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the cough of an outboard motor running against the current. No one ran an outboard at night on the Sombra. The boats here were poled and paddled, quiet as prayer. An engine in the dark meant someone who did not care about being heard, which meant someone who did not believe anyone upstream could hurt them.

Ines was out of the hammock and pulling on her boots before the thought finished forming.

*They came for the scientists.*

The mission house was cold stone even in the wet season, and now, in the third month of the dry, it was cold as a grave. She moved down the corridor past the cells where the Halvorsen team had spent six weeks behind bars she had ordered built with her own hands — good work, honest work, she had told herself, because prisoners who were fed and sheltered were not the same as hostages, and she had repeated that sentence so many times it had worn smooth as river stone and meant almost nothing anymore. The doors were locked. The night guard, old Benicio, was already at the end of the hall with his lantern raised, his face gray.

"Boats," he said. "Three of them, maybe four. Below the bend."

"How many men?"

"Too many to count in the dark. Armed." He said the word carefully, the way you'd set down something heavy. "Rifles, Doña Ines. Not hunting guns."

Hunting guns they could have answered. Her people knew every trail, every ford, every place the river narrowed enough to make a stand. But rifles in the dark, carried by men who came by motorboat — that was a different grammar entirely. That was the language of the consortium, and Ines had learned to read it years ago, downstream, in another village that no longer existed except as a stain of oil on flooded water.

"Wake everyone," she said. "Quietly. Send the children to the church with Rosa. Get the scientists into the cellar — no, wait." She stopped herself, hand flat against the cold wall. If the raiders wanted witnesses erased, the cellar was a tomb with better acoustics. "Move them to the orchard terraces. Behind the walls. And get word to Mateo at the upper post — tell him to light nothing, fire nothing, just watch and count."

Benicio hesitated. "And if they reach the house?"

"Then they'll find out how much stone costs to move." She heard her own voice come out flat and hard, and some small observer inside her — the one that never slept, the one that had been whispering doubts since the day she first locked a scientist behind a door — noted the sound of it without approval.

Outside, the stars were still thick over the ridge, but the east had begun to bruise. The village of San Rafael de la Frontera — forty-one souls, two hundred years of graves, one church whose bell had been cast from melted rifle barrels after the last war — lay along the riverbank in a scatter of tin roofs and whitewashed walls, its gardens already brown at the edges, its canals running thin. Ines walked the main path fast, calling names in a low voice, and doors opened ahead of her like eyes waking. She saw the fear arrive in each doorway before the understanding did: first the blank animal startle, then the calculation, then the grim settling. These were people who had fled one flood already. They knew what it looked like when the powerful decided your home was in the way.

By the time the sky went from bruise to ash, the boats had rounded the bend.

There were five of them, riding low, and the men in them wore dark clothing and did not shout or wave or announce themselves in any way, which told Ines more than any flag could have. Men who came to talk made noise. Men who came to erase did not. She stood on the mission steps with Mateo's counting report in her head — *twelve, maybe fifteen, rifles, one man with a radio* — and felt the fury rise in her like water behind a failing dam, and this time she did not try to hold it back. Fury was fuel. Fury was the only inheritance the consortium had ever given her people that kept its value.

"Emiliana." She found the young woman at the corner of the church, bow in hand, three arrows standing in the ground at her feet like planted stalks. "You see the man with the radio?"

"Yes."

"When they land, he is yours. Not to kill. To deafen. I want them blind and mute before they're off the shingle." Ines put her hand on the girl's shoulder and felt it trembling and did not mistake the trembling for weakness. Emiliana's brother had drowned in the flood below the old dam site, though the consortium's lawyers had called it an accident of recreation, a swimmer misjudging current. "Steady. We are the river today. Let them be the stones."

The first boat grounded on the shingle bar below the village with a grinding crunch that carried across the water like a bone breaking.

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do, in pieces that Ines would spend the rest of her life reassembling and never quite finish.

The mercenaries came off the boats in a spread formation, professional, silent, rifles slung low — and then the village answered. Not with gunfire. Ines had forbidden it; bullets against rifles was arithmetic, and she had done that arithmetic before and buried the remainder. Instead the village answered with everything else it had. The dogs came off their chains in a screaming wave. Emiliana's arrow took the radio man through the forearm as he raised it, and his shout of pain was the first human voice the raiders had heard all morning, and it broke their silence like ice breaking. Smoke pots went up from the far fields where Rosa had lit the damp chaff piles, and the wind off the gorge pushed the smoke across the landing in a white blindness. Somewhere a conch horn began to blow — old Benicio's signal, the same note his grandfather had blown — and it was joined by a second horn from the upper post, and the sound of it went up the valley like a pulse going up a vein.

For perhaps four minutes, it worked. The raiders hesitated on the shingle, blinded, harried, their formation dissolving among the dogs and smoke, and Ines allowed herself one breath of something that was almost hope.

Then a man's voice cut through the chaos — calm, amplified, wrong. A loudspeaker. "This does not concern you," the voice said in accented Spanish, smooth as spilled oil. "Deliver the survey team to the bank and withdraw upriver. You will be compensated. Resist, and we will treat this settlement as hostile infrastructure."

*Hostile infrastructure.* Ines stood on the mission steps and let the phrase enter her body like a bullet enters wood. Not *hostile forces*. Not *enemies*. Infrastructure. Bridges were infrastructure. Dams were infrastructure. And villages, apparently, when they stood in the way of water.

"No," she said, though she was too far away for the loudspeaker man to hear, and then she said it again louder, to her people, to the smoke, to the river: "*No.*"

The raiders stopped trying to be surgical.

The first rifle shot cracked across the shingle and took old Benicio through the chest while he stood in the open ringing the church bell — because he had refused to stop ringing it, because the bell was the signal and the signal was the village and he was eighty-one years old and had decided, with the clarity available only to the very old and the very brave, exactly how he intended to spend his final minutes. Ines saw him fall. She saw the bell keep swinging above him, struck once more by his dying hand, one last bronze note rolling up the valley like a question no one would answer.

And she saw Tomás Herrera — the boy, sixteen, not the boatman's son she'd been teasing about his first solo run just last week — go down in the shallows trying to drag Benicio behind the stone jetty. Two shots. The river took him gently, turning him once, reddening around him in a bloom that spread and thinned and spread, and Ines understood with terrible clarity that the Río Sombra, which had fed her people for two centuries, was drinking them now, and there was nothing she could do about either direction of that exchange.

After that, the fighting became retreat, and the retreat became flight, and Ines moved through it shouting the orders she had rehearsed in nightmares: *to the terraces, to the upper trail, leave the houses, leave the boats, GO.* The mercenaries pressed as far as the mission wall and no farther — whether for lack of numbers or lack of orders, she never learned — and by full light the village belonged to the smoke and the dead, and her people were strung out along the cliff trail above it like beads pulled off a snapped string.

She counted them at the second terrace, in the thin shade of the mango trees, the way you count anything you cannot bear to have lost: twice, three times, lips moving. Thirty-nine. Forty-one had woken in San Rafael that morning. Thirty-nine stood or sat or lay moaning on the terrace now, and two of those would not survive their wounds without medicine she did not have.

Two names short. Benicio. Tomás.

Ines sat down on a root, abruptly, because her legs had made the decision without consulting her, and put her face in her hands, and for one unguarded moment she was not a leader or an avenger or a keeper of a sacred valley but only a woman smelling gunsmoke in her hair and hearing a bell that would never ring again.

* * *

They burned nothing. That was the detail that frightened her most, later, when she had breath to be frightened in. The mercenaries withdrew downriver by mid-morning, taking their dead and wounded, leaving the village standing intact — houses, church, granaries, all untouched. Erasure, she realized, had never meant fire. Fire leaves evidence, leaves grief, leaves martyrs. What they had intended was quieter: take the scientists, silence the witnesses, and leave a village so cowed it would spend a generation telling itself stories about why it hadn't resisted. They had miscalculated. Benicio and Tomás had made certain they miscalculated.

But the next party would not miscalculate. The next party would bring fire.

"We cannot go back," said Mateo. He stood over her on the terrace, an old man now himself, his machete still in his hand as if the argument might turn physical. "Not this season. Maybe not ever. They know where we sleep now."

"And the scientists?" That was Emiliana, her arm bound where an arrow shaft had gashed it in the melee, her voice scraped raw. "The ones in the orchard. What do we do with *them*, Ines? March them to the source? Feed them from our own stores while our children eat less?" She spat. "Every mouth we save is a mouth taken from us. That is what mercy costs. I used to think you understood the price of things."

"I understand the price of things." Ines rose. Her knees ached; her whole body felt like a rope bridge in wind. "I have paid them since I was nine years old. Sit down, Emiliana."

But the girl's words stayed in her like splinters, because they were true, or true enough. Forty-one mouths — thirty-nine now — and a climb into country that gave nothing freely. And seven prisoners who were, by every law she had ever claimed to honor, innocent of anything except arriving in the wrong valley wearing the wrong employer's money.

She went to them in the afternoon, on the third terrace where they waited under guard, huddled among the packing crates and the last of the harvest stores. Dr. Halvorsen came to the front of the group the way he always did, placing his slight body between the guards and his people, a habit Ines had watched for six weeks with a contempt she had lately begun to suspect was something else entirely.

"You heard the loudspeaker," Halvorsen said. It wasn't a question. His eyes went past her, down the trail, toward the smoke still standing over the village. "We heard the shooting. Are your people—"

"Two are dead." She said it plainly and watched it land on him like a stone dropped into a well. "An old man ringing a bell and a boy of sixteen. They died defending the men who imprisoned them. I want you to carry that down whatever road you take out of this valley, Doctor. I want it to weigh on you the way my dead weigh on me."

"They weren't defending us," Halvorsen said quietly. "They were defending the idea that we were worth more alive than erased. There's a difference, and I'll carry both." He straightened. "The man with the loudspeaker. Did you recognize the voice?"

"No."

"I did." For a moment the scientist looked older than the mountains. "It doesn't matter. What matters is that they came to erase witnesses, which means somewhere in their ledgers, we've become liabilities instead of assets. Which means the data matters. Which means—" He stopped himself, and Ines saw him choose, deliberately, not to bargain. Six weeks ago he would have bargained. Something had changed in him, or in her willingness to hear it.

"The guide," she said instead. "Reyes. Your people say he reached the mission and was taken. Where is he now?"

"In the storeroom, under guard. He hasn't eaten since yesterday." Halvorsen hesitated. "Ines — Calloway. Whatever you decide about us, decide quickly. They'll be back, and next time they won't land on the shingle and make speeches. And you should know something else. Reyes has seen their forward camp. He's seen the man who runs it. He came back with files — real files, hydrology, the whole survey. He showed me enough through the cell wall to know they're genuine. If you're looking for a reason to trust an outsider, you won't find a better one than a man who stole from his own employers to bring you the truth."

"He stole from them," Ines repeated slowly, "or he was sent to steal *for* them, and got caught playing both sides. You don't know which. Neither do I."

"No," Halvorsen admitted. "Neither do I. But I watched him bleed getting here, and I watched him refuse a deal that would have freed him, and I am a scientist, señora — I work with evidence. The evidence says he chose."

*Evidence.* The word turned over in her like a stone in current. All winter she had built her certainty on absence of evidence — on what the survey stakes implied, on what the consortium was capable of, on the flooded village that haunted her dreams. Certainty had been armor. It had let her lock doors and call it justice. Now the armor had holes in it, shot through by an old man's bell and a boy's blood and a prisoner who spoke of evidence instead of ransom, and underneath the armor Ines found something she had not felt in years and did not welcome: doubt, alive and moving, like a fish in her chest.

She looked west, where the trail climbed out of the terraces and up toward the high basin, toward the source, toward the one place her people had sworn never to take outsiders — the place where the river was born and the dead were remembered and the laws of the valley predated every map. Her grandmother had told her: *the source forgives nothing and forgets nothing. Bring only what you can account for.*

Forty-one souls minus two. Seven scientists. One guide in a storeroom, bleeding and unproven. And behind them all, coming up the river sooner than any of them wanted, a man with a loudspeaker and a ledger full of names to cross out.

"Break camp," Ines said. Her voice came out steady, and she was grateful to it, because nothing else in her was. "Everything we can carry, nothing we can't. We walk tonight, by the high trail, and we don't stop until the tree line." She turned to Mateo. "Bring me the guide. Unbound. If he runs, that tells me something too."

Mateo studied her for a long moment — the old man reading the new decision forming in her face the way he read weather. "You're taking him to the source," he said. Not a question either. "Your grandmother will turn in her grave, Calloway."

"My grandmother," Ines said, "is part of the river now, and the river has been telling me all week which way things flow." She looked down the trail one last time, at the smoke over San Rafael, at the shingle bar where the blood had already washed away, at the water running thin and lower than it had run in living memory. "The dry season is killing us from below and the consortium is killing us from behind. There's only one direction left. We go up. We go all the way up."

That night, under a moon like a filed claw, the column of San Rafael de la Frontera began to climb — thirty-nine living, two dead wrapped in canvas for burial at the source, seven prisoners walking free among their guards because Ines could no longer remember exactly what crime they were guarding against, and one limping outsider with stolen files falling into step beside her hammock-bearers, close enough to touch, saying nothing, watching everything.

She did not trust him. She was very far from trusting him.

But when he stumbled on the first switchback and she found her own hand halfway extended to catch him before she could stop it, Ines Calloway understood that the war she had been fighting for twenty years had just changed shape — and that the enemy behind them wore a familiar face, and the stranger beside her might be the only map out of the country she was entering.
