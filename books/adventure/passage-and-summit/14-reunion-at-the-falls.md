## 14. Reunion at the Falls

The river saved him the way rivers save people — by accident, without meaning to, without caring whether he lived or drowned.

Petra had stopped thinking of it as a river somewhere in the second hour of his flight from Camp Perdida. It was a sound, a cold weight against his wound, a direction. *Downstream. Always downstream.* He had slid into the water above the second cataract with Vane's bullets stitching the surface behind him, and the Río Sombra had taken him the way it took everything — leaves, boats, men's plans — and carried him through channels he did not choose.

The bullet had gone through the meat of his left shoulder, low, missing the joint by what he later decided was the width of a prayer. At the time he hadn't decided anything. He had surfaced gasping in a pool below the falls, dragged himself onto a shelf of black stone, and lain there while the sky wheeled overhead and the water spoke its one word over and over: *go, go, go.*

He went.

He remembered the journey in fragments, the way a man remembers a fever. Tearing his shirt to bind the shoulder, the cloth blooming red and then rust-brown and then the brown of the river itself, so that he and the water matched and he could almost believe he was part of it. Walking the cliff trail in the dark with one arm useless, his boots finding the ledges his body remembered from five years ago, from the worst year of his life, from the surveys he had run for the consortium before he understood what the surveys were for. The mountain knew his feet. That was the obscenity of it. He had tried to leave this place and the place had kept his measurements, his footfalls, his blood.

He remembered eating. That was the strange mercy — hunger was a kind of clock, and as long as he was hungry, he was alive. He found wild plantains and a nest of river eggs and ate them raw, crouched like an animal, and laughed until the laughing pulled at the wound and became something else.

He remembered thinking about Halvorsen's ledger, wrapped in oilcloth inside his shirt, pressed against the skin above his heart like a second wound. The complete hydrology files. Eleven years of flow data, sediment cores, floodplain surveys — the whole long honest record of what the Río Sombra was and what the dam would do to it, and buried in the middle of it, the falsified sections, the numbers Vane's people had invented to make the valley look empty. *Empty of what?* Ines would ask. And the answer, in the language of engineers, was: *empty of anything worth counting.* No villages. No orchards. No graves. No river community older than the republic itself. Just a basin, a capacity, a number on a balance sheet.

He had to get it to Ines.

He did not examine why. There was no time, and besides, he was afraid of the answer. A week ago he had been a man under contract, owed money for the delivery of witnesses. Now he was a man with a hole in his shoulder carrying stolen property through canyon country, and the contract was a burned thing behind him, and ahead of him was a woman who had held him prisoner in a mission house and every reason in the world to put a second bullet in him.

*Go, go, go.*

On the third morning he heard the falls before he saw them — the great white noise of the upper cataract, where the river dropped through a seam in the mountains and the spray hung in the air like weather that had forgotten how to end. The column would pass there. It had to. There was no other way upriver, not with children and old people and boats hauled overland on rollers. Tomás had told him once, in the flat voice of a man stating a law of nature: *Everything that goes up the Sombra goes past the Falls of the Virgin. Everything.*

Petra came down off the ridge at noon, half-blind with fever, and saw them.

* * *

They were a river of people moving along a river of stone. Two hundred, maybe more — he counted without wanting to, the old guide's habit, heads and packs and dugouts dragged on sledges of palm logs. Women carried bundles on their backs and children on their hips. Old men walked with the deliberate care of people conserving something they could not replace. The boats came last, hauled by teams of twenty, their hulls scarred white from the portage, and behind the boats walked the sentries with their shotguns and machetes, faces turned always toward the downstream trail.

Toward him.

Petra stopped at the edge of the spray zone, where the mist came off the falls in cold gusts, and stood in the open with his one good arm raised. It was the only thing left to do. If they shot him, they shot him. He had run out of ways to be careful.

The sentries saw him within a minute. He heard the shout go down the column — a single word, passed mouth to mouth like a current — and then the line of people stopped moving, two hundred faces turning toward the wet black rocks where a bleeding man stood swaying with his arm in the air.

Then Ines Calloway came through the crowd.

She moved the way water moved, he thought — finding the gaps, wearing down what resisted. She had a machete at her belt and a boy of maybe twelve carrying her pack, and her eyes found him across forty meters of spray-slick stone and did not soften and did not waver.

"You," she said. Her voice carried. "You were in Vane's camp."

"I was in Vane's camp." His own voice came out ruined, a whisper with gravel in it. He cleared his throat and tried again. "And now I'm here. And I have something you need to see before you decide what to do with me."

"You escaped."

"I stole from him first." He fumbled at his shirt with his right hand, and the sentries' shotguns came up, and he froze, and slowly, watching Ines's face, drew out the oilcloth package and held it up. It was streaked with his blood. "Hydrology files. Eleven years of them. The real ones. Halvorsen's real ones, and the forged ones Vane built to replace them, side by side. It's all here. The proof of what the dam will do, and the proof that they knew."

The column was absolutely silent except for the falls. Petra realized he was shaking, and not from the fever, or not only from the fever.

"Ines." He said her name and it cost him something, saying it, the way it costs to set down a weight you have carried so long you have grown around it. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to look at the paper. That's all. Look at the paper, and then do whatever you were going to do to me anyway."

For a long moment she said nothing. The spray drifted between them. Somewhere in the column a child cried and was hushed.

"Rosa," Ines said at last, to the boy with her pack. "Get the old doctor." Then, to the sentries: "Nobody touches him." And then she crossed the wet stone to him, took the oilcloth package out of his bleeding hand with both of hers, and looked at him with an expression he would spend a long time failing to name — something between a woman reading a letter and a woman reading weather.

"You look like something the river spat out," she said.

"It spat me out twice," Petra said. "I'm getting better at it."

* * *

They treated him in the lee of a granite overhang while the column rested, and the old doctor — a small, fierce woman named Abuela Ríos who had probably delivered half the people now watching from a respectful distance — cleaned the wound with boiled water and agave spirit and said nothing the entire time, which Petra understood to be its own kind of judgment.

Ines sat across from him with the files open on her knees.

She read the way she did everything, completely. He watched her go through the forged survey summaries first — the ones with the consortium's letterhead, the ones that described the upper basin as *uninhabited seasonal grassland, no permanent settlements, minimal cultural assets* — and her face did not change, and that frightened him more than anger would have. Then she reached the real data, Halvorsen's raw field records, the flow measurements and the floodplain maps and the sediment cores that showed the valley floor was built of a thousand years of settled earth, orchard soil, midden, bone. And there, in the margin of one page, in Halvorsen's small precise hand, a note Petra had read four times himself and still could not read calmly:

*Consortium directive: classify all permanent habitation in the inundation zone as "transient." Do not survey the mission. Do not survey the terraces. See Vane re: final report.*

Ines closed the file. She was quiet for so long that Abuela Ríos looked up from her bandages.

"The terraces," Ines said finally. "My grandmother's grandmother built the terraces. There are graves in them. There are *names* in them." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "They wrote down that we are not here."

"Yes," Petra said.

"And you carried this to me. Through their bullets. With a hole in you." She said it not as gratitude but as arithmetic, a woman adding a column she did not yet trust.

"Halvorsen gave me the location of the files," Petra said. "The rest was mine."

"Why?"

It was the question he had been afraid of, and here it was, in the open air, with the falls roaring behind it. He looked at his bandaged shoulder, at the rust-brown cloth, at the river below the trail where he had almost died twice in one week for two different sets of people.

"Because five years ago I surveyed this valley for them," he said. "I walked these trails with their instruments and I wrote down what they told me to write down, or close enough, and I told myself it was just a job, just numbers, and then there was an avalanche on a route I'd flagged and two of my clients died and the company needed someone to blame and it was easy, because I was the guide who'd argued about the route, who'd *noticed things*, who was inconvenient. They took my license. They took my name. And I spent five years telling myself the worst thing they ever did to me was the blacklist." He looked up at her. "It wasn't. The worst thing was the terraces. I helped them write you out of the map, Ines. I didn't know I was doing it, and it doesn't matter, because I did it. So no. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to let me be useful. It's the only thing I know how to be."

The silence went on. The falls went on. A sentry shifted his weight somewhere behind Petra and Ines raised one hand without looking, and the shifting stopped.

"Bring him," she said to the sentries, standing. "Not as a prisoner. Not as a guest. He walks with the column and he eats from the common pot and if he betrays us, Abuela Ríos gets to decide what happens, and she's been wanting an excuse for thirty years." The old doctor smiled without warmth. "Move him when he can walk. We move at dusk."

* * *

He could walk by dusk. He insisted on it, and Abuela Ríos looked at him with the flat appraisal of a woman who had seen a thousand men insist on things and outlived most of them, and relented.

They found Halvorsen near the head of the column, walking between two villagers who were no longer quite his guards — Petra noticed that, the way the distance had changed, the way the old scientist now carried a share of someone else's pack. Halvorsen had aged years in the weeks since the mission house; his beard had gone from grey to white and his hands, when he took the oilcloth package back, trembled like a man holding a bird he wasn't sure was alive.

"You got them out," Halvorsen said. "All of them. Even the forged ones."

"Especially the forged ones," Petra said. "The forgery is the confession. You can't fake data unless you know what the real data says."

Halvorsen laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and when he had his breath back he sat down heavily on a sledge runner and opened the files across his knees with the reverence of a man opening a scripture he had half forgotten he believed in. Ines stood over him. The column waited. Word had traveled, the way it travels in a moving crowd, and people were drifting near — not close, never close, but near enough to watch the old gringo's face as he read.

"Here." Halvorsen's finger stopped on a page. "The flow records. Eleven years of gauge readings from the lower stations — mine, and the ones my real team ran. This is the river's own testimony. The dry season drawdown they're counting on, the one that makes the reservoir math work — it only works if the upper basin contributes what their model says. But look." His finger moved down the column of figures. "The model assumes the headwater springs are perennial. They're not. Three of the five run seasonal. In a drought year the dam produces a fraction of the promised power, and the valley drowns anyway. They *know*. Vane signed off on the model himself — there, initial and date. He knew the numbers were fiction when he signed."

"And the habitation surveys," Ines said.

"Here." Halvorsen turned pages, and his hands were steadier now, the tremor burned away by purpose. "The real surveys. My people walked the terraces in the first month, before the plants arrived, before Vane took control of the reporting. There are forty-one occupied structures in the inundation zone. Forty-one. Orchards, cemeteries, the mission, the school. All of it documented, photographed, mapped. All of it classified out of existence in the final report." He looked up at Ines, and his eyes were wet. "I let them do it. I told myself I'd fix it later. That's what cowards say — *later* — and later is where the valley drowns."

"You're fixing it now," Petra said.

"I'm a dead man fixing it now," Halvorsen said, almost cheerfully, and Ines flinched. "Vane's men saw my face in the camp. They'll have my description on every trail between here and the coast. But it doesn't matter what happens to me, because it doesn't matter what happens to *paper*." He pressed the files flat against his chest. "What matters is where this goes. A government office can lose this. A courthouse can lose this. A newspaper—" He looked at Petra. "You said you had a plan. In the camp. You said something about a beacon."

"I did," Petra said. "And it's still there. Buried at the edge of Vane's camp under a cairn I built, with a solar trickle charge and a satellite uplink and a dead-man timer. If I don't reset it every seventy-two hours, it transmits everything — the files, the photographs I took of the camp, the survey stakes with the consortium's stamp, all of it — to three newspapers and two regulators and a lawyer in the capital who owes me a favor and hates Vane independently of anything I've done." He paused. "The timer runs out in fifty-one hours. And I can't reset it. I'm done going back down there."

"Then it fires," Halvorsen said.

"Then it fires," Petra agreed. "And Vane knows the timer exists. He's been hunting for the cairn since the night I ran. Which means he has one move left: get up this valley, get to the source, destroy the original field records — your notebooks, the physical evidence, the things that prove the copies are real — and get back down before the story breaks. Or burn the valley and everyone in it and call it a security operation."

The word *burn* moved through the crowd like a wind through cane. Petra heard it pass from mouth to mouth in the valley's own language, a word he didn't know but didn't need to.

Ines had gone very still. When she spoke, it was to Halvorsen, but her eyes were on the upstream trail, where the mountains closed around the river like a hand around a throat.

"The notebooks," she said. "The original field records. Where are they?"

"With your people," Halvorsen said. "You took them when you took us. Three boxes. They're in the mission house, or they were, if your people didn't burn them."

"We don't burn records," Ines said. "We burn *stakes*." And something that was almost, almost a smile crossed her face and was gone. "The boxes come with us. Everything comes with us. We are taking this valley's whole testimony to the source, where no soldier of Marcus Vane has ever stood and where, with the river dropping the way it's dropping, no boat of his can follow."

"He doesn't need boats," Petra said quietly. "There's a pass. The western saddle, above the gorge. It's hard going, but it's going, and I ran a survey line over it five years ago, which means there's a cut trail, which means his men can move fast on it. He'll come over the top. He'll come down into the basin from behind you while you're climbing, and he'll have the high ground, and he'll have guns, and you'll have—" He stopped. He had been about to say *children*, and the word would not come out, because the column was full of them, asleep against their mothers' backs, and saying it aloud would make it real.

Ines finished it for him, flat and clear. "We'll have the truth and a river running out. That's what we'll have."

"Yes."

"Then we need more than truth," she said. "Truth is slow. Bullets are fast. And I have two hundred people and maybe forty guns, and he has—" she looked at Petra, and it was not a question, but he answered it anyway.

"Sixty, maybe seventy. Mercenaries, not soldiers. Good in a firefight, bad in the mountains, worse the longer it lasts. And Vane—" Petra looked down the trail, back the way he had come, toward the camp where a man with a familiar face was even now assembling his killers for the ascent. "Vane doesn't fight. Vane *bargains*. He'll come up that pass offering terms, because terms are cheaper than graves, and he'll offer them to whoever looks most afraid."

"Then we'd better make sure nobody looks afraid," Ines said.

* * *

They moved at dusk, the whole column, into the throat of the upper gorge, and Petra walked among them with his bandaged shoulder and his useless arm and the oilcloth package passed from Halvorsen's keeping to Ines's, wrapped now in oilskin and lashed inside the frame of the lead boat where no rain and no traitor would find it.

He was one of them now, or adjacent to them; the distinction mattered less each hour. A woman he had never seen gave him soup without being asked. A boy — Rosa, the one who carried Ines's pack — fell into step beside him and asked, with the directness of the young, whether it was true he had stolen from the bad men, and Petra said it was true, and the boy nodded once, satisfied, as if that settled the man's whole account.

Behind them the falls went on roaring, hiding their sound from anything that might be listening downriver. Ahead of them the gorge narrowed and the first stars came out above the rimrock, cold and precise as instrument readings.

And somewhere above the western saddle, Petra knew, men were lighting no fires and speaking no words and moving through the dark toward the same basin, carrying the same hunger in a different shape. Fifty-one hours. Maybe less. The timer in the cairn under the stones, ticking toward the truth; Vane's column ticking toward the source; the river itself ticking down, day by day, toward the bare bones of the dry season.

Ines appeared at his shoulder as the trail steepened. She did not look at him. She spoke the way people speak when the words have been waiting a long time.

"The pass you surveyed. The western saddle. You know it."

"I know it."

"Then tomorrow," Ines Calloway said, "you're going to show me how to close it."
