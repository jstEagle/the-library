## 15. The Forbidden Source

### Petra

The river thinned to a whisper two days into the climb, and by the third it was less a river than a rumor — a bright thread stitched through scree, vanishing under boulders and surfacing again like something shy of being seen. Petra had spent five years measuring his life in miles, and he had never walked miles like these. Every step upward was a step into country that had no name on any map he'd ever bought, borrowed, or stolen.

Ines led the column. She had not asked his permission to take the route, and he understood why when the valley walls closed in around them: this was not a trail. It was an inheritance. The villagers moved through it the way a man moves through his own house in the dark, without light, without hesitation, touching stone at certain places as they passed — a flat palm against the rock, brief, almost casual, the way you'd touch a doorframe out of habit or devotion. Petra counted eleven of them before he stopped counting.

"You're staring," Ines said, falling back beside him. Her voice was low enough that only he could hear it over the crunch of forty pairs of boots.

"Learning," he said.

"What you learn here stays here."

"I know." He shifted the pack on his wounded shoulder and did not look at the camera case strapped beneath it. "That's what I'm afraid of."

She studied him for a long moment. Above them the sky had gone the deep bruised blue of altitude, thin and merciless, and her face in that light looked carved rather than lived-in. "You understand what you're asking," she said finally. "Every outsider who has seen the source has come to take from it. That is the whole history of your kind in this valley. You want me to believe you're different because you carry files instead of stakes."

"I don't want you to believe anything," Petra said. "I want the truth to have somewhere to live besides my memory. Memory can be discredited. Memory can die in a gorge." He paused. "You've already lost two people to men who wanted no witnesses. I'm trying to make sure there are witnesses anyway. Everywhere. Permanent ones."

Ines said nothing, but she didn't tell him to put the camera away either, and in the grammar of the last week that was close to absolution.

They camped that night in a hanging meadow where the grass grew in cushions thick as mattresses and the air smelled of some flower he couldn't name — sweet and cold at once, like snow given a perfume. The children of the column, and there were children, he kept being startled by the children, ran laughing between the tents until their mothers called them in. Old women built the fire in a way he'd never seen: three stones, never four, and the first stick laid pointing uphill.

"Why three?" he asked Tomás, who had appeared at his elbow with two tin cups of something hot and bitter.

Tomás shrugged, but his eyes were serious. "Four closes the square. Three leaves a door open. For whoever's coming behind us." He handed over a cup. "Or for whatever lives up there. Depends which grandmother you ask."

Petra drank. The bitterness sat on his tongue like a warning. Around him the column settled into sleep, and above them, invisible now in the dark, the river's birthplace waited with what he could only describe as patience.

He did not sleep well. Not from pain, though the shoulder throbbed where Vane's bullet had torn through. From the sense — growing all day, undeniable now — that he was approaching something he had no right to approach, and that the mountain knew it, and that the mountain was letting him come anyway. Which was worse.

### Ines

Her grandmother had described the basin to her exactly once, the winter before she died, and had made Ines swear she would not go until she was old, until the valley needed her to go, until there was no one else. *The water is born singing,* the old woman had said, hands working wool in the firelight, eyes fixed somewhere beyond the wall. *And everything that hears it must decide what it will sing back.*

Ines had thought it a story for children. She had been wrong about many things lately.

She walked at the head of her people through the last defile at dawn, and when the walls opened she stopped so suddenly that Mateo behind her nearly walked into her back.

The basin was a bowl of light. It held the morning like a cupped hand. Grass so green it hurt sloped down from every side to a shallow amphitheater of white stone, and from the center of that stone the river rose — not from a spring, not from a glacier's melt, but from the rock itself, welling up in a dozen braided veins that caught the sun and threw it back broken into gold. The sound reached her a heartbeat later: not loud, not soft, a chord of running water layered over itself dozens of times, and underneath it, faintly, a resonance in the stone, as if the mountain hummed along.

*Singing,* her grandmother had said. Singing.

Behind her, her people were going quiet, one by one, the way snow quiets a village. Someone began to weep. An old man — Esteban, who had fished the Río Sombra for sixty years and cursed it for fifty-nine — knelt down and pressed his forehead to the grass.

Ines stood very still and let herself feel none of it, because feeling all of it would unmake her, and her people needed her made. She catalogued instead, the way she always did: water enough for ten valleys. A basin hidden by the ridge line, invisible from any approach but this one. Defensible. Cold. High enough that even Vane's mercenaries would come up gasping, if they came at all.

And then she hated herself for cataloguing, because this was holy ground and she was treating it like a fortress, and her grandmother's voice in her skull said: *this is how it starts, niña. This is how a people becomes the thing it fears.*

She turned. Reyes stood a few paces back, and to his credit he wasn't filming yet. He was just standing, hat in his hands, looking at the water with an expression she recognized because she'd worn it herself a moment ago — the expression of a man revising everything he believed about the size of the world.

"You swore nothing about this place exists in writing," she said. "Not coordinates. Not descriptions. Nothing."

"The files don't mention it. Halvorsen's data ends downstream of here." He hesitated. "But I'm going to document it, Ines. You know I am. That was the bargain."

"Not the location. Document what it means. Document that it exists and that we exist and that we will defend it." She stepped closer. "Let the world know there is a heart up here worth killing for. Just don't hand anyone the map to it."

Reyes considered that, turning his hat slowly in his hands. "A heart without an address," he said at last. "I can write that."

"It's more than most men manage."

Below them, at the edge of the white stone, Halvorsen had sunk to his knees, and Ines watched the old scientist lay both palms flat against the rock the way her people touched the walls of the defile, and she felt something shift in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to name.

### Halvorsen

Erik Halvorsen had published forty-one papers. He had been cited nine thousand times. He had stood in lecture halls on three continents and explained, with elegant slides, how watersheds worked. And in all those years he had never once, until this morning, put his hands on the actual beginning of a river.

The water was cold past cold — a cold that went through skin and muscle and settled in the bones like an old truth. His knees ached on the stone. His lungs ached from the altitude. His conscience ached from everything else, and had for six weeks, and would, he suspected, for the rest of a life that might now be considerably shorter than he'd planned.

He had brought surveyors to Eden. That was the plain arithmetic of it, however he dressed it in the language of science. Half his team had carried consortium pay stubs folded inside their credentials, and he had signed the permits, chartered the boats, led them up the Río Sombra like a shepherd delivering his flock to slaughter. *I didn't know,* he had told himself, and then, later, *I suspected,* and then, in the mission house with the smell of woodsmoke and fear, the final honest entry in the ledger: *I chose not to know, and choosing not to know is a choice.*

Now the data in Reyes's stolen files confirmed what his own instruments had whispered all along: the dam's reservoir would drown this entire watershed. Not just the lower valley Ines had shown him, the drowned village with its church roof breaking the floodwater like a rib. The source itself. They meant to take the birth of the river and turn it into a holding tank for turbines.

He cupped a handful of the water and drank it. It tasted of stone and starlight and nothing human at all.

"Dr. Halvorsen." Ines stood over him. Most of the time she looked at him the way you look at weather — something to be endured, forecast, survived. Today something else was in her face. "Your instruments. The ones the honest ones among your team carried. Did they record here? This basin?"

"No." He got stiffly to his feet. "We never came this high. The plants in my team steered us away from it — I see that now. They mapped the lower gorge and called it sufficient." He almost laughed. "Vane doesn't know this place exists. The surveys show the dam feasible without it. But if the source were documented — if the world knew the river is born here, in living stone, on land your people have kept for—"

"Longer than your science has existed," Ines said.

"Then the dam dies twice over. Legally and morally." He met her eyes. "I can't give back the drowned village. I can't give back your dead. But I can spend what's left of my credibility making sure this water keeps singing. Let me help him document it. Properly. Flow rates, temperature, the geology of the seeps — evidence, not poetry. Poetry gets dismissed. Numbers get entered into records."

"And when the hearing comes," Ines said, "you'll stand in a room full of lawyers and say these things out loud. With your name attached."

"With my name attached," Halvorsen said. "It's the only thing I have left that's worth anything. I intend to spend it."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she nodded, once, and turned away, and Halvorsen understood that he had just received, from the woman whose people he had helped betray, the closest thing to forgiveness he would ever be offered. It was not mercy. It was a work assignment. He found he preferred it that way.

### Petra

By midday the camp had taken shape around the basin's rim, and Petra worked.

He photographed everything the way Ines had drawn the line: the water, the stone, the faces of her people as they came down to the source and touched it — but never the approaches, never the ridgelines, never a single frame that could be geolocated by someone who knew what to look for. He shot video testimony with Halvorsen narrating flow measurements while villagers held the survey tape. He recorded Esteban telling, in a cracked voice, the story his own grandfather had told him, of the year the river nearly died and the old ones climbed up to sing it awake. He recorded Ines, at her insistence, reading aloud the names of the dead of the flooded village — thirty-one names, and she read them without stopping, without water, standing in the shallows where the river began, so that its first act would be to carry their names downhill forever.

It was the hardest documentation he had ever done, because half of him wanted to put the camera down and simply be inside the place, and the other half knew that being inside it was a luxury purchased by everyone who'd died below. Beauty that demands a price, he thought. He was paying installments.

Toward evening he climbed alone to the basin's eastern rim to check sightlines — old habit, new necessity — and stood where the last light hit, looking down the whole length of the world they'd climbed out of. Far below, impossibly far, the Río Sombra wound away through the gorges toward Puerto Ladeado and the sea and every kind of trouble. Somewhere down there, Vane was regrouping. Somewhere down there, men with rifles were doing arithmetic about altitude and ammunition and how much a river's birth was worth.

He raised the binoculars and swept the pass — the only pass, the notch in the southern ridge through which the entire column had funnelled two days ago.

The light was failing, gold going to grey. He almost missed it.

Movement. Small, at this distance. Figures in the notch — four, five, six — moving with the economical spacing of trained men, and the flat glint of glass catching the last sun. A scout's telescope, aimed at the basin.

Petra lowered the binoculars very slowly. His heart was not pounding; it had gone strangely quiet, the way it did before avalanches, before everything that mattered.

They hadn't followed the column. They had *anticipated* it. Which meant someone had talked — or Vane had simply reasoned it out, the same way Petra would have: if you're a cornered people with a dying river, you go to the source.

He was halfway down the scree before he realized he was running.
