## 16. Vane's Ascent

The scouts came down at first light, two of them, a boy named Rafael and an older woman called Marta whose knees had been bad for as long as Petra had known her and who had nevertheless outwalked every able-bodied man in the column. They came into the high camp the way bad news always arrived on the mountain: quietly, and all at once.

Petra was awake. He had slept perhaps two hours, wedged between a root and a boulder above the sleeping column, the way he had slept for twenty years, the way a man sleeps when he has stopped trusting the ground beneath other people's feet. The stolen files were against his ribs in a waterproof roll, and his shoulder wound had gone from a hot wire of pain to a deep, dull throb that he had decided to interpret as healing. Below him the river — what remained of it — muttered through the stones like an old man counting what he had left.

Marta crouched beside the fire pit and held up her hands before anyone could speak. "The pass," she said. "They're on the pass."

"How many?" Ines was already moving, pulling her jacket on, her hair still braided from the day before.

"Forty. Maybe more behind. Armed — not hunting rifles. Military pattern." Rafael's voice cracked on the last word, and he steadied it the way a boy does when he refuses to be a boy. "We watched them from the ridge. They have mules carrying something heavy, wrapped. And they move like they know the trail."

"They know the trail," Petra said, "because I told them the trail."

Everyone turned. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. But it was true, and in the last three days he had learned that truth spoken early cost less than truth spoken late. The route up the Río Sombra to the source basin was the route he had scouted years ago for the consortium's first feasibility study — the route he had walked with a surveyor's chain in his hands and a company logo on his jacket, back when he had believed, or had let himself believe, that measuring a thing was innocent.

"Vane has my old maps," he said. "The ones I drew before I understood what they were for. He doesn't need to find the way up. He only needs to follow it."

* * *

They gathered at the council stone above the camp, the same flat granite slab where Ines had once, Petra now understood, decided the fate of the Halvorsen expedition. The morning was cold enough to see breath. The river ran below them, thinner every day, its voice dropping by a syllable each morning. The dry season was doing Vane's work for him: every day the water fell, the gorge trails opened wider, the crossing at the shallows grew easier, and the only pass to the source — the pass that in flood season was a waterfall and in drought was a road — became a road.

Petra stood at the edge of the stone and looked at the faces. Forty-three of them now. Two had been buried two days' walk behind them, under cairns Ines had built with her own hands and would not let anyone else touch. The children had been sent ahead with the oldest walkers. What remained were the ones who had chosen not to run past the point of running.

"Vane doesn't want us," Petra said. "I want to be clear about that, because it matters to what we do next. He wants the source. He wants the hydrology data destroyed, and he wants the valley's water claimed before the dry season ends and the regulators come upriver. We are an obstacle, not an objective. That's good news. Obstacles can be moved, and objectives can't."

Ines stood across the stone from him, arms folded. "And when he's done with the source?"

"Then we're not obstacles anymore. We're witnesses. And witnesses get buried." He let that sit. "I've seen how his men handle witnesses. I was almost one."

"You were almost one of *them*," someone said from the back — Tomás's cousin, a broad man named Esteban who had not forgiven Petra for being an outsider and had stopped pretending otherwise since the raid. "You drew their maps. You ate at their table."

"I did." Petra didn't flinch from it. He had learned, in the mission house and in the canyon and in Vane's tent, that flinching was the most expensive thing a man could do. "And because I did, I know how he thinks, what he'll do, and where he'll be weak. You can throw that away because of my history, or you can use it. I'd rather you use it."

Ines raised a hand, and the murmuring stopped. She looked at Petra for a long moment, and he saw in her face the same calculation he'd seen in chapter after chapter of his own life — the arithmetic of trust, done in a currency that had been devalued by every outsider who had ever come up this river.

"Say your plan," she said.

* * *

He laid it out with sticks and stones on the granite, the way he had once laid out routes for clients, the way his father had once laid out fish traps in the shallows of a river on another continent. It was the oldest human technology: make the enemy come to you where the ground does your fighting.

"They have to take the pass. There is no other way to the basin — I made sure of that when I surveyed it, because I was making sure of it *for them*." He placed a stone at the top of the arrangement. "The pass comes down through the gorge. The Gorge of Bells — where the walls narrow to less than ten meters and the trail is cut into the cliff above the water. In the wet season it's suicide. In the dry season it's passable, barely, single file."

He moved a stone into the narrows. "Forty men, single file, in a slot canyon with a hundred-meter walls. If the walls come down at the right point, the pass is closed. Not for a day. For a season. Maybe forever, if we do it right."

Silence. Then Esteban: "The walls won't come down because you wish them to."

"No. They'll come down because the mountain wants them to, and always has." Petra pointed to the cliff trail. "That shelf is cut through a layer of rotten rock — I flagged it myself, years ago, as unstable. The consortium's engineers wanted to blast a wider trail there. The survey said no, too fragile, the whole face would let go. Vane's own documents say the gorge is the one place the valley defends itself." He looked up. "We don't bring the mountain down. We just stop holding it back."

The trail shelf was held in place, he explained, by a seam of debris — old slide material, packed by roots and seasons — that the community had cleared and maintained for generations to keep the trail open. It was their trail, their maintenance, their labor that kept the pass passable at all. No one had ever thought of it as a weapon because no one had ever thought of the gorge as anything but a passage.

"Pull the debris seam at the top of the shelf," Petra said, "and the shelf goes with it. The trail closes. Vane's men are in the narrows when it does — or they stop before it and have to turn back, and by the time they find another way, if one exists, the dry season is over and the river is in flood and the source is unreachable until next year. By then the files are in the hands of journalists and the dam is dead."

"And if the timing is wrong?" Marta asked. Her voice was flat, not hostile. She had earned the right to flatness.

"Then men die in the gorge, or men don't, and the pass stays open, and we fight them on the summit with forty against forty and no ground on our side." He met her eyes. "I won't lie to you. This plan has a knife edge. Everything from here does."

* * *

The argument lasted through the morning. Petra had expected it to be about him — his past, his loyalty, his right to propose that anyone die for his redemption. It was not. It was about the gorge itself.

"The Gorge of Bells is where we say goodbye to our dead," said an old woman Petra didn't know, her voice like dry leaves. "My mother's name is carved in that wall. My son's. We do not close it."

"We are not closing it," Ines said. "We are letting it close itself. There is a difference, and the river knows the difference."

"Does it?" The old woman looked at her. "You've been deciding what the river knows since the mission house, Ines. Two of ours are under cairns because of your deciding."

Petra watched Ines take that blow. He watched her not flinch, and he understood that she had learned it the same place he had learned his own version — from years of being the one who decided, and being alone inside every decision. When she spoke, her voice was quiet enough that everyone had to lean in to hear it, which was, he suspected, exactly the effect she intended.

"Rosa is right," Ines said. "I have decided, and people have died, and I will carry that until I am carried. So hear me: I am not deciding this one alone. Petra, tell them what happens if we do nothing. Not the plan. The nothing."

Petra told them. He told them about Vane's forward camp and the crates of explosives he had seen there, the same industrial charges the consortium had used on the lower river to open its survey corridors — charges that had, incidentally, destabilized a hillside above a village that no longer existed. He told them what Vane had said in his tent, in the tone of a man discussing freight: that the source was an asset and assets were secured, and that populations were variables. He told them about the avalanche, finally, all of it — the two clients, the report he'd been made to sign, the way the company had built a mountain of paper over two graves and called it weather.

"I spent five years being buried under that paper," he said. "I know exactly how it's done. And I'm telling you: if Vane reaches the source with his explosives and his lawyers and his forty men, this valley will be buried under paper too. You'll still be here. But you'll be here the way ghosts are here."

* * *

They voted the way Ines's people voted — not with hands but with standing. Those who stood for the plan stood on the north side of the council stone; those against, on the south. Petra stood apart, because he did not consider it his vote to cast, and Ines, without looking at him, said, "You stand too. You're in this gorge with us."

So he stood on the north side, and the count was not close, but it was not unanimous either, and Ines looked at the south side for a long moment and said, "Those who stand south, you will take the children and the elders and the files' copies east over the saddle tonight. That is not exile. That is the other half of the plan." Because that was the thing Petra was learning about her: she did not waste a defeat. She folded it into the next move.

* * *

The rest of the day was work, and work was mercy. Petra threw himself into it with the gratitude of a man who trusted his hands more than his thoughts. The debris seam at the top of the shelf had to be undermined, not collapsed — pulled loose just enough that the weight above it, a hundred tons of rotten rock, would let go at a touch. But only at a touch, and only when Vane's men were beneath it.

That was the knife edge. Too early, and the pass closed with the column still a day below, and Vane simply waited them out or found the old hunter's traverse that Petra knew about and had not mentioned to anyone, because mentioning it would mean admitting there was a way around his own trap, which would mean admitting the trap might fail. Too late, and the vanguard came through the narrows and up onto the shelf and the charge went off under the wrong feet.

He worked with Esteban, of all people, prying at the seam with bars cut from a broken mule pack frame, and the two of them said almost nothing for six hours except the necessary things — *left, harder, hold* — and somewhere in the fifth hour Petra understood that this was the most honest conversation he'd had with the man, and possibly the beginning of one.

"You drew these maps," Esteban said at last, not looking up from the bar.

"I did."

"And now you're unmaking them."

"That's the whole job," Petra said. "Everything else is details."

Esteban grunted. It was not forgiveness. It was something better: it was a working relationship, which on a mountain is worth more than forgiveness, because forgiveness is a feeling and a working relationship is a rope you can both hang your weight on.

* * *

At dusk the second set of scouts came down, and the news was worse and better in the way news is when it arrives in pairs. Worse: Vane's column had made better time than expected and would reach the gorge mouth by midday tomorrow. Better: Tomás was with them.

Petra found him at the edge of the firelight, older-looking than the river had left him, his boats gone — Tomás had burned them at the lower crossing, he said, all four, rather than let them fall into Vane's hands, and had then walked three days upriver with six of the lower-community men to rejoin Ines's column. His hands were blistered from the oars and then from the trail, and when Petra clasped them he felt the damage and said nothing, because Tomás said nothing either.

"You burned the boats," Petra said.

"I burned the boats." Tomás looked at the fire. "My grandfather burned boats. It is a family talent — knowing what has to burn." He glanced up. "You have something that has to burn, I think. Or something that has to fall. I heard the plan from Marta. Tell me where you need a boatman when there is no water."

Petra told him. The narrows had one more feature, the one that had given the gorge its name: in the dry season, the river dropped so low that a shelf of smooth stone emerged along the north wall, a natural rampart, and the community's tradition held that in the old days sentries had stood there with bells — bronze bells, rung to warn the valley of anyone coming upriver. The bells were long gone, melted or traded or lost, but the sentry shelf remained, and from it, a man could see the entire length of the narrows and drop stones on anything in it.

"I need you on the shelf," Petra said. "Not to fight. To watch, and to ring — you'll carry a steel carabiner and a piton, and when the vanguard is fully in the narrows, you strike them together. That's the signal. That's the bell. The moment it rings, we pull the seam."

Tomás considered this with the seriousness he gave to water. "A bell made of climbing gear," he said. "My grandfather would find it funny."

"Would he approve?"

"He would say the river doesn't care what the bell is made of. Only that it rings at the right time." Tomás looked up at the dark walls of the gorge, invisible now but present the way a held breath is present. "And he would say: make sure you are not inside it when it rings."

* * *

Petra did not sleep that night either. He sat above the column again, and Ines came up at some point past midnight and sat down beside him without asking, which by now was its own kind of permission.

"You knew this would happen," she said. "When you took the files. You knew he'd come."

"I hoped he'd come." Petra turned his wounded shoulder carefully toward the fire below. "A cornered man makes mistakes. Vane in his camp is patient. Vane on the march, with the dry season ending and his asset slipping away — he's a different animal. He'll push. Men who push make mistakes."

"And if he doesn't push? If he stops at the gorge mouth and waits us out?"

"Then we've bought a week and lost nothing but a week." He paused. "But he won't. I know him. He's spent his whole career believing that hesitation is the only unforgivable sin. He once told me that a delayed decision is worse than a wrong one, because a wrong one at least teaches you something. He's going to come up that gorge at the front of his column, because he always does, because he needs to see things with his own eyes." Petra looked at the black seam of the pass against the stars. "And when he does, he'll be standing under a hundred tons of rock that I flagged as unstable eight years ago. In a report he signed."

Ines was quiet for a while. "You want him dead," she said. It was not an accusation. It was a reading.

Petra took his time answering, because the answer deserved honesty and honesty deserved difficulty. "I want the pass closed," he said finally. "I want the season lost and the dam dead and the files out. If Vane is standing under the shelf when it comes down, I will not weep. But I'm not building the plan around his death, because plans built around a death are prayers, not plans." He looked at her. "You taught me that. The mission house. You kept them alive when killing them would have been easier. That's the only reason I trusted you enough to come back with the files."

"That and you had nowhere else to go."

"That too," he admitted, and for the first time in days, something that was almost a laugh moved through his chest without waking the pain in his shoulder.

Below them the river ran thin and silver under the stars, lower than it had been that morning, lower than it would ever be again this year. Somewhere down that dark thread of water, forty men and a man named Marcus Vane were sleeping in the cold with their explosives and their mules and their certainty, and tomorrow they would start up the only road to the source, and the road would be waiting for them the way the valley had always waited for the people who came to take from it: patiently, and without mercy, and with a bell that had not rung in a hundred years.

Petra checked the piton in his pocket, and the carabiner, and the fuse cord for the seam charges, and then he lay back against the boulder and closed his eyes, and for the first time since the canyon, he slept — not because the danger was past, but because for the first time in five years, he knew exactly where he was standing, and why, and what was coming, and had chosen every meter of the ground between himself and it.

The bell would ring at midday.
