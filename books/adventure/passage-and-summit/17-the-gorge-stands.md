## 17. The Gorge Stands

The gorge woke before dawn, the way it always did — bells first.

They hung from the old mission bell-tree at the narrows: rusted church bells, cooking pots, lengths of rail iron, strung on wire by hands three generations dead. When the wind came down off the high basin it set them all murmuring at once, a low bronze argument that the valley had learned to read like weather. Ines had grown up believing the bells spoke to the river. She was thirty-one years old before she learned they'd been hung as a warning system against slavers, and she had never decided which truth she liked better.

This morning there was no wind. The bells were silent. And still she heard them.

She stood on the ledge above the narrows with her father's machete at her hip and binoculars borrowed from a dead man's pack, and she watched the smoke of Vane's column rise through the pass trees like a confession no one had asked for. Forty men, maybe more. They moved badly for soldiers and well enough for mercenaries, spread along the trail in the loose file of men who expected no resistance. Behind them the river ran low and glassy, the dry season having stripped it down to its bones, and Ines thought: *they are coming for water that is barely there. They are coming to own a ghost.*

"Rearguard's stopped at the scree," said Marisol beside her, a girl of nineteen who had learned to read terrain the way other girls learned song lyrics. "Six of them. Holding the boats' landing."

"I see them."

"They're waiting for something."

"They're waiting to make sure we don't run." Ines lowered the glasses. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was everything behind them that shook. "Petra's people should be in position."

Below, hidden in the willow tangle that choked the downstream mouth of the gorge, Tomás's boats waited — four dugouts poled upriver in the night by men and women Tomás had spent two days recruiting from every landing between here and Puerto Ladeado, men who owed him money, favors, grudges, or all three. Ines had not trusted the plan when Petra laid it out in the dirt with sticks. She did not entirely trust it now. But she had watched Petra Marchetti walk into Vane's camp half-dead and walk out with the truth in his bleeding hands, and trust, she was learning, was a thing you extended like credit — not because you believed in the man but because you had no better currency.

She touched Marisol's shoulder once, and the girl slipped away down the trail to carry the word, and Ines stood alone above the narrows and listened to the river count down the morning.

* * *

Tomás heard the bells in his sleep and woke knowing.

He had moored the boats in the willows at dusk, hulls packed with stone and cut brush so they sat low and invisible, and he had told his crews one thing only: *when the shooting starts, we block the river.* Not fight. Not chase. Block. The gorge narrowed below the mission flats to a throat barely wide enough for two canoes abreast, and if that throat filled with sunken hulls, nothing Vane had — no boat, no barge, no raft of stolen timber — was leaving the valley by water. Whatever happened upstream, the river would be a door with no handle.

He lay in the wet leaves and listened to the pass.

The first sound was not gunfire. It was a whistle — three notes, falling, the signal Petra had taught them all yesterday evening in the firelight, drawing lines in ash. Then the whistle broke apart into noise: rifle reports rolling down the canyon walls, doubled and tripled by echo until it sounded like a war instead of an ambush, which had been the point. Petra had been very clear about the point. *We don't have to beat them,* he'd said, in his flat border Spanish that made Tomás's teeth itch even now. *We have to make them believe they're losing.*

Tomás rolled out of the leaves and put two fingers in his mouth.

His crews came up out of the willows like herons flushing — twelve of them, thirteen, poling the stone-laden dugouts into the current with the practiced economy of people who had fought rivers their whole lives and never once expected to fight men on one. The first boat swung across the throat of the gorge. The second wedged against it. Tomás stood in the bow of the third with a hatchet, and when the moment came he did not hesitate; he brought it down through the hull of his own boat, again and again, water boiling up through the splits, and leapt for the rocks as the dugout settled and rolled and became part of the wall.

Three boats. Four. A fifth dragged from the shallows and sunk broadside. Within minutes the throat of the Río Sombra was a reef of drowned wood, and the current, low and angry, piled foam against it and sang.

Then the rearguard opened fire from the landing, and the morning stopped being clever.

Bullets slapped the water. A man named Eusebio, who had joined Tomás because Vane's surveyors had poisoned his fishing grounds two years running, took a round through the shoulder and went into the current without a sound. Tomás hauled him out by the collar and dragged him behind the reef, pressing the wound with river mud because that was what his grandmother would have done and because he had nothing else. Eusebio looked up at him with the mild surprise of the shot and said, "I can't feel my arm," and Tomás said, "You've got another one," and kept working.

Downstream, beyond the reef, the river ran empty to the horizon. Nothing was getting out. Tomás crouched in the spray with a dying man's blood thinning between his fingers and understood that this, too, was a kind of victory, and that it tasted exactly like failure.

* * *

Petra fought the ambush the way he climbed: three moves ahead, always giving ground he could afford.

The plan had been geometry. Let Vane's column commit to the narrows trail where the cliff pinched it to single file. Hit the head of the column from the ledges above, hit the middle from the talus field, and let panic do the rest — drive them back into the pass, or forward onto the flats where Tomás's reef would pen them with no boats and no exit. He had forty-one fighters, most of them farmers with hunting rifles and one very old shotgun, against perhaps fifty professionals. Every advantage he had was borrowed from the terrain, and terrain, unlike loyalty, never changed sides mid-fight.

For an hour, it worked.

The column broke at the second bend. Petra saw it happen from the ledge — the mercenaries' neat file dissolving into cover, return fire going high and wild, an officer's whistle shrieking for order that the echoes ate alive. The valley fighters melted and reappeared, fired and moved, and the gorge filled with the particular chaos of men being beaten by a landscape. Petra put his shots low and careful, wounding rather than killing wherever he could choose it, and told himself the mercy was tactical and not the other thing.

Then the officer stopped whistling for order and started whistling for the rearguard, and Petra understood his mistake one full minute too late.

Vane hadn't split his force to hold the boats. He'd split it to bleed them. The six men at the landing were bait — a fixed position that any blocking force had to engage, drawing Tomás's crews into a firefight they couldn't win and couldn't leave, while the main body, instead of retreating up the pass, did the thing Petra's whole plan assumed no mercenary would do.

They charged.

"Up!" Petra screamed it down the ledges. "Everyone up, off the flats, NOW—" But the flats were where the noncombatants were, the old ones and the children staged for evacuation toward the source, and Halvorsen was down there.

Petra saw it all from two hundred meters and could change none of it. The scientist — sixty-three years old, bad knee, a man who had spent eight months in captivity and come out of it carrying a ledger heavier than his body — stepped out from behind the supply cache into the open ground between the charging column and the fleeing families. He had taken a rifle from somewhere. He fired it standing up, in the open, with the terrible deliberate calm of a man settling accounts, and whether he hit anything mattered less than what he was: a target. Six rounds converged on him. He folded at the waist like a coat dropped from a hook, and the column poured past him without slowing, and Petra was already moving, sliding down the scree on his heels, screaming words that tore his throat, and the distance between what he saw and what he could reach stretched out like the river itself, endless, indifferent.

He reached Halvorsen in the churned mud behind the cache. The scientist's eyes were open and finding focus with difficulty, the way a lens finds focus, and his hand came up — not to his wound, but to his breast pocket, patting it twice.

"The pages," he said. Blood at the corner of his mouth, but his voice was almost amused. "Still there. You see? I told you I'd—"

"Don't talk."

"—deliver." His hand found Petra's wrist and gripped it with astonishing strength. "Marchetti. Listen. The data is true. That was always the whole of it. The data is true, and I lied about everything else." His grip loosened by degrees, like a rope paying out. "Tell Ines — tell her the mission house — I'm sorry about the mission house. We should never have—"

He didn't finish. Petra held him anyway, forehead to forehead, in the way of men who have no ritual for this and must invent one in the space of a breath. Around them the firefight collapsed into scattered shots, then silence, then the strange birdsong of the gorge resuming as if nothing had happened, because to the gorge nothing had.

When Petra looked up, the column had regrouped on the far flats — bloodied, furious, but intact, and between two of Vane's men, wrists bound, hair torn loose, stood Ines Calloway.

She had gone down for the children. Everyone would say that afterward, and everyone would be right, and it would save no one anything.

* * *

Ines counted her captors the way her mother had taught her to count stars: without staring, by memory.

Six. No — seven, one lagging with a bloody bandage at his thigh. Professional, angry, and afraid, which was the combination she liked least. They had pulled her out of the evacuation line by the hair, and now they marched her ahead of them along the riverbank, using her body the crudest way a body can be used: as a shield, a sign, a sentence written in flesh that read *stop shooting.*

Behind them, the gorge held. She could hear it — the reef of sunken boats churning the current, Tomás's people sniping from the willows, the whole downstream door nailed shut. Ahead, upstream, Vane's main force was regrouping on the flats with their dead left where they fell and their objective unchanged. They had lost men today. They had lost the river. And they had gained her, which their commander clearly intended to spend.

"You're the one who runs the valley," said the man holding her arm. Young, under the dust. Nervous. "Calloway."

"I'm the one who lives here," she said. "You're the one visiting."

His grip tightened, and she welcomed the pain the way she welcomed all clear things now — gratefully, because pain at least told the truth about itself. Through the trees she could see the mission house on its rise above the flats, shuttered, empty since the evacuation, and she thought of Halvorsen in his cell last month saying *I didn't know what they'd do with the data,* and she thought: *he died on our ground. On the ground we took him to. Whatever else is true, that debt is ours.*

A shot cracked from the treeline and one of her captors spun and sat down hard, and for one wild second the line around her loosened —

—and then closed, and the moment passed, and the young man shoved her stumbling toward the mission house steps, and Ines understood with cold clarity that she was not being rescued and not being killed but being *kept*, carried forward into whatever bargain Vane meant to buy with her, and that the price of her life would be paid by everyone else.

Above the gorge, faint through the trees, the old bells stirred at last in the afternoon wind. They rang ragged and broken, half their hangers shot away, and to anyone else it would have sounded like ruin.

Ines lifted her chin and listened, and memorized it, and began, silently, to count the hours until dark.

* * *

Petra found Tomás at the reef at last light, both of them gray with powder smoke, and they looked at each other and neither spoke first, because there was no sentence in either language equal to the day.

"Halvorsen?" Tomás asked finally.

"Gone."

"Ines?"

"Taken." Petra crouched at the waterline, where the drowned boats made their black reef against the sunset, and picked up a shard of his own plan — a splinter of Tomás's hull, split by his own hatchet — and turned it in his fingers. "The river stands. Nobody gets out by water. But she's in their hands, and Vane knows exactly what she's worth, and by morning he'll have moved her somewhere we haven't found yet."

Tomás lowered himself onto the rocks beside him, heavily, an old man's motion though he wasn't old, only used. Downstream the river ran free beyond the reef, glittering, going on without them toward the drowned villages and the sea.

"So," Tomás said. "What does the guide do now?"

Petra watched the light die on the water. Somewhere upriver, in the dark, Ines was counting hours, and he had promised her — in the basin, over the files, in the fragile arithmetic of their alliance — that the valley would not be traded. Not for his name. Not for his life. Not for hers, if it came to that, though God knew he wanted it to come to something gentler.

"Now," he said, standing, "we find out where Vane sleeps. Because tomorrow I'm going to trade him something he wants even more than a hostage."

Tomás looked up. "What's that?"

Petra touched his breast pocket, where Halvorsen's final pages lay — the confession, the ledger, the dying delivery.

"Me."
