## 18. Summit Bargain

The pass was a throat of stone, and Vane's column came up it like smoke.

Petra watched them from the ridge above the narrows, counting through the scout's binoculars until the numbers stopped meaning anything and became simply *too many*. Fourteen men. Fifteen. Automatic rifles slung with the casual competence of professionals, packs heavy with rope and demolition charge. They moved in the staggered file of men who had done this before, who would do it again, who thought of mountains as terrain rather than as places.

At their head, unmistakable even at four hundred meters, walked Marcus Vane.

He had aged well, which Petra found obscene. The same silver at the temples he'd worn in the conference rooms of Puerto Ladeado five years ago, the same unhurried stride, the same way of holding his head as if the air around him had been personally arranged for his comfort. He carried no rifle. Men like Vane never did; they carried other people instead.

And behind the rearguard, hands bound, a rope around her wrists and a mercenary's fist knotted in her collar, came Ines Calloway.

They had taken her at the falls two days ago — the rearguard that slipped past Tomás's boats, the ambush that cost Halvorsen his life buying the column its escape. Petra had heard it secondhand from a breathless runner, and the news had gone through him like cold water through a wetsuit: grief first, then arithmetic. Because Ines was not merely a hostage to Vane. She was leverage against the only man Vane truly feared, and Vane knew it, because Vane had always known how to find the softest part of any structure and press there.

*You,* Vane would be thinking. *You'll come down off your ridge for her.*

Vane was right. That was the humiliation of it. Petra lowered the binoculars and felt the truth settle over him with the weight of snowpack: he would trade himself, and they both knew it, and knowing it changed nothing.

"You're not seriously considering this." Tomás stood beside him at the ridge crest, arms crossed against the wind coming up the basin. Behind them, the refugee column waited in the rocks — forty-three souls, what remained of the mission community, watching the pass with the stillness of animals scenting fire. "Fourteen guns. You have a pistol and a bad shoulder."

"I have something else."

"The files are already sent."

"They're already sent." Petra said it quietly, almost to himself, the way a man confirms the location of a fixed anchor before trusting his weight to it. Three nights ago, crouched in the ruins of Camp Perdida with Vane's searchlights sweeping the treeline, he had buried the satellite beacon beneath a cairn on the ridge and keyed the transmission — the complete hydrology archive, Halvorsen's real data, the consortium's internal correspondence naming every drowned village downstream by name and calling them *acceptable losses*. It had taken eleven minutes for the files to crawl skyward. Eleven minutes, and then the truth existed in places no bullet could reach: a journalist in the capital who owed Petra nothing and owed stories everything; a regulator whose fax number Halvorsen had sewn into his jacket lining like a talisman; three newspapers, two ministries, one very patient lawyer.

The world above the valley now knew what the valley had always known.

But knowledge traveled at the speed of paper, and bullets traveled faster.

"Tomás." Petra handed back the binoculars. "When I come down, take the column east along the high trail. Don't wait. Don't watch. If this goes wrong, the story doesn't need witnesses — it needs survivors who can talk."

"And if it goes right?"

Petra looked at the pass, at the thin ribbon of men ascending toward the source of everything. "Then I'll need someone to carry me down," he said, "because I promise you my legs won't work."

* * *

He went down alone, the way he'd gone into the gorge, the way he seemed to do everything that mattered — one man against the arithmetic of numbers, carrying less than he needed and more than anyone could see.

The wind dropped as he descended into the pass, and the silence that replaced it had texture, had grain, like the inside of a bell. His boots crunched on scree. Somewhere above, water sang in the rock — the river's first voice, the infant sound of the Río Sombra being born out of the glacier's dark heart. He let the sound steady him. This was his country, whatever the licenses said. He had been reading mountain ground since he was nine years old, and he read this pass now the way other men read faces: the choke point at the narrows where six men could hold fifty; the overhang that would shed rock at a shout; the angle of afternoon light that would blind anyone looking west.

He walked into their picket at the narrows without slowing, hands visible, pistol left behind on a ledge two hundred meters below. Let them search him and find nothing. The nothing was the point.

They took his pack, patted him down with rough efficiency, and marched him up the switchbacks with a rifle muzzle resting against his spine like a finger tapping for attention. At the final turn, the pass opened onto the high bench below the source, and there was Vane, standing at the edge of the glacier meltwater pool as though inspecting a property line.

"Reyes." Vane didn't turn around. "You always did have a talent for arriving exactly where you shouldn't be."

"You sent men to make sure of it."

Vane turned then, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing Petra had seen in five years, because it was warm. It was the smile from the old days, from the briefing room with its topographic maps and its coffee smell, the smile of a man who believed the world was a machine and himself its operator. "Ines Calloway is alive because I want her alive," Vane said. "She's currently enjoying our hospitality about a kilometer back down the trail. You can verify that, or you can stand there looking heroic. Your choice."

"I want to see her."

"You want to trade." Vane spread his hands, magnanimous, a man granting terms he'd already decided. "Fine. Here are mine. You walk out of these mountains with me, Reyes. You give a statement — a full statement — confirming that the avalanche five years ago was guide error. Your error. You recant whatever fantasy you've been telling yourself, and in exchange, your record gets cleaned. Reinstatement. License restored. A future." He paused, letting it land. "And the woman goes free, and your valley friends get to keep breathing, provided everyone forgets this valley has anything worth drowning."

Behind Vane, the mercenaries had gone carefully still. Professionals listening.

Petra felt the offer the way he imagined a drowning man feels a rope — the animal part of him reaching for it before the rest could object. *License restored.* Five years of doors slammed shut, swinging open. His name spoken without the flinch. And all it would cost was a lie, and a valley, and every dead person downstream whose names Vane had filed under acceptable.

"You engineered it," Petra said. "The avalanche. You cut the cornice report from my briefing packet. You knew the slope was loaded. You needed the incident to void the environmental review, and you needed a guide to blame, and I was convenient."

Something flickered across Vane's face — not guilt, never guilt, but the irritation of a craftsman seeing his joinery examined. "Proving that would require evidence," Vane said softly. "Evidence has a way of staying buried at altitude."

"Not this time."

"No?" Vane glanced at the sky, at the sun sliding toward the western wall. "Reyes. Look around you. Fourteen rifles. No radio traffic leaving this basin that I don't permit. Whatever you think you've done, it dies here with the glacier. That's not a threat. It's logistics."

"That's the thing about logistics," Petra said. "I've spent five years learning them. Distances. Loads. Rates of travel." He looked Vane in the eye. "Do you know how long it takes to move fourteen men up a pass like this? Two days. Do you know how long it takes a signal to reach a satellite?"

The wind moved through the pass. One of the mercenaries shifted his weight.

"Three nights ago," Petra said, "from the ridge above Camp Perdida, I transmitted the complete Halvorsen archive. Every hydrology reading. The flood models showing the reservoir drowns forty kilometers of valley including two villages that were never 'abandoned' — they were flooded, Marcus, with people in them. The internal memos. The survey falsifications. The name of the man who removed the cornice warning from a guide's briefing packet five years ago." He let that last sentence hang in the thin cold air. "It's in the hands of journalists. Regulators. A lawyer who has been waiting years for exactly this. By tomorrow morning, your consortium's board will know more about your operation than you do."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. Then he laughed — short, disbelieving, a bark of sound swallowed by the rock. "A beacon. In the field. With what power supply, Reyes? With what clearance? You're a disgraced mountain guide playing spy."

"I'm a mountain guide," Petra said. "Which means I know how to carry weight a long way without complaining. Ask your quartermaster what's missing from the Camp Perdida stores."

He watched it land — watched Vane's certainty meet the first cold draft of doubt, watched the man's eyes flick involuntarily toward the sky, toward the invisible arc where the truth was already falling toward earth. Vane was not a stupid man. That was the tragedy of him. Stupid men could be forgiven. Vane was doing the calculation in real time, and Petra could see each term of it arrive: the missing beacon, the eleven minutes of transmission he'd never known about, the journalist's name Halvorsen had whispered through a cell wall like a dying man passing a flame.

"Even if it's true," Vane said slowly, "killing you changes nothing now."

"Correct." Petra took a step forward, into the space between the muzzles, because the moment demanded it. "Killing me changes nothing. Killing her changes nothing. Shooting every person in this basin changes nothing. The story exists, Marcus. It's out of the mountain. You can't climb fast enough to catch it." He turned, deliberately, to face the ring of mercenaries — to face them as men, not as weapons. "And here's what your employer hasn't told you. By Friday, the consortium's accounts are frozen. The board is going to feed him to the ministries whole, because that's what boards do when the ship sinks — they name a captain. Every one of you is standing guard over a dead company, protecting a man whose protection is worth exactly nothing by the end of the week. Your paychecks die with him. Your legal exposure doesn't."

Silence. The glacier ticked and settled somewhere above them, ice speaking its slow language.

One of the mercenaries — a broad man with a scarred jaw, the same one who'd held Ines's rope — looked at his sergeant. Not at Vane. At his sergeant. Petra had spent enough years leading paid men through dangerous country to read that glance fluently. It was the glance of an employee checking whether the enterprise still existed.

"Sergeant," Vane said, and his voice had lost its warmth, "nobody is going anywhere—"

"Stand down." The sergeant said it quietly, not even loudly, the way a man closes a ledger. He shouldered his rifle. Around him, one by one, with the smooth mechanical obedience that soldiers reserve for reality, the others followed. Muzzles dropped. Safeties clicked home. Fourteen professionals, in the space of ten seconds, deciding that the truth was heavier than the money.

Vane stood alone on the high bench, and for the first time since Petra had known him, he looked small — a man dressed for a summit that had been moved while he climbed.

"You can't do this," Vane said. "I own—"

"You own nothing," Petra said. "That was always the difference between us. I never owned anything. I just knew how to read the mountain."

* * *

They brought Ines up the trail an hour later, unbound, escorted by the same sergeant wearing the expression of a man returning borrowed goods. She came up the last switchback fast, too fast for the altitude, and stopped when she saw Petra standing whole among a ring of disarmed mercenaries, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"You gave yourself for me," she said finally. It wasn't gratitude in her voice. It was suspicion, worn thin over something rawer.

"I gave myself for the valley," Petra said. "You were the price tag it used."

A ghost of a laugh escaped her — brief, unwilling, gone. She looked past him at Vane, standing under guard at the edge of the meltwater pool, and her face closed like a door. "What happens to him now?"

"That's not my call anymore." Petra nodded eastward, toward the high trail where Tomás and the column waited, toward the long descent and the world of paper and consequence waiting below. "There are ministries, and lawyers, and the families downstream. They'll want him. But this valley has its own way of settling accounts, and I suspect the valley gets a vote."

Ines considered that. High above them, half-hidden in the glacier's blue shadow, the source of the Río Sombra seeped from the rock — a thread of water no wider than a hand, beginning its thousand-kilometer argument with gravity. She watched it the way a woman watches a child sleep.

"He destroyed my village," she said quietly. "He drowned my neighbors and called it progress. There isn't a court below that holds what he owes."

"No," Petra agreed. "But there's a valley above that remembers everything."

She turned to him then, and something passed between them that had no name yet — not trust, not yet, but the ground trust gets built on. "Then we take him down," Ines said. "Downriver. To the communities. Let them decide what justice looks like when the law arrives too late."

Below them, the mercenaries began the descent in silence, escorting the man they'd been hired to protect toward a judgment no contract had ever covered. And Petra stood on the high bench with the wind rising, watching the river's first thread gather itself out of the glacier and go, and understood that the hardest climbing of his life was still ahead — not up this mountain, but down, into the lowlands, into the hearing rooms and the history books, carrying a prisoner and a truth and a name that might never be cleaned.

Ines touched his arm. "Come on, guide," she said. "Walk me down."

They started down together, into the dusk, following the water toward whatever the valley would decide.
