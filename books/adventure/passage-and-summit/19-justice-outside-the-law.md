## 19. Justice Outside the Law

The gorge gave back its dead slowly, the way a river gives back what it has taken only when it is good and ready.

Ines stood on the shingle below the falls while men she had known since they were boys waded out to where Tomás's boats held their catch. The water was low enough now that the rapids had lost their thunder; you could hear individual voices across it, could hear the slap of hands on hulls, the wet scrape of a body being passed from boat to shallows. Two of her own. Halvorsen. And three of Vane's mercenaries, drowned in the trap they had come to spring, their rifles still slung across their chests like passengers who hadn't bought tickets.

Marcus Vane was not among them.

He sat on a flat rock at the tree line with his wrists bound in front of him with the same nylon cord he'd used on Ines's sentries, watching the recovery work with the expression of a man auditing a warehouse fire. He had not spoken since Petra Marchetti had walked him down off the pass with a hand fisted in his collar like a father hauling a truant son. He had not asked for water, though his lips were cracked gray. He had not looked at Ines, which she understood was itself a kind of speech.

She had imagined this moment for eleven years. She had rehearsed it in the dark hours of the mission house, in the season after the flood took San Rafael de Abajo, when sleep came only as accusation: *you will find them someday, and then.* She had imagined shouting. She had imagined a rifle. She had imagined — though she never admitted this even to herself — begging, some crack in the machinery of these men, some moment when one of them would look at the water rising over the rooftops of her village and understand.

None of it had prepared her for the actuality of Marcus Vane sitting on a rock, small against the immensity of the valley, waiting to learn what she was.

*What we are,* she corrected herself. Because that was the question now, wasn't it. Not what she would do with him but what the valley would do, and she was the one who would have to say it aloud.

Petra came up the shingle toward her, moving carefully on the leg the consortium's bullets had opened. Someone had dressed the wound with the mission's last clean linen and he'd refused the stretcher twice, so he walked with a boatman's pole for a staff, and each step cost him something he pretended it didn't. He stopped beside her without looking at her directly. That was his way, she had learned. He offered people the courtesy of pretending not to watch them break or mend.

"He's asking for you," Petra said. "Hasn't eaten. Won't take water until someone official offers it. Says he wants terms."

"Terms." The word tasted like silt.

"He thinks he's still negotiating. It's all he knows how to do." Petra turned the pole in the gravel, pressing a hole and filling it again. "I've seen men like him on mountains. They can't stop climbing even when the summit is a cliff."

"And what do you think I should do with him?"

"I think," Petra said slowly, "that whatever I think doesn't matter anymore. That's rather the point of everything I did." He looked at her then, and there was no counsel in his face, only a tired kind of respect that frightened her more than anger would have. "This one's yours, Ines. The valley's. I just carried the files home."

He limped away toward the fires, and she stood alone with the river running shallow past her boots, carrying less water than any dry season in living memory, carrying everything else.

* * *

They convened the council at dusk in the old mission house, because it was the largest roof left standing and because there was a bitter fitness to it. The room where the scientists had been held through the long weeks of suspicion now held the man who had set those suspicions in motion, seated on a bench against the wall with two guards who would not meet his eye.

Ines counted the faces by lamplight. Forty-one adults. Every family in the upper valley was represented except the ones that were empty now — the Mendozas, both sons lost at the mission house; old Beatriz, whose grandson had died on the first night of the raid. Their absence was a presence. Grief attended the council whether it was invited or not.

She laid it out plainly, because they deserved plainness. "The police are four days downriver, if they come at all. The government that licensed Vane's survey is the government that licensed the dam. If we give him to them, he will be processed, represented, released on technicalities, and the survey will resume under a new name before the rains return. Some of you know this already because you have lived it. Others of you want him to answer to law anyway, because law is what separates us from him." She let that sit. "Both things are true. So we decide here, tonight, what justice looks like when the law has been bought and paid for by the man standing trial."

Beatriz spoke first, and her voice was dry as seed husks. "Give him to the river."

A murmur ran the length of the room, approval and horror braided together, indistinguishable in the lamplight.

"The river took my grandson," Beatriz said, unblinking. "Let it take the man who made the flood. Let the Río Sombra settle its own accounts."

"The river didn't kill your grandson," said Domingo Reyes — no kin to Petra, though everyone always assumed otherwise — a fisherman with forearms like mooring rope. "Men with guns killed him. And men with guns should answer to men, not to water. Give him to the authorities. Let it be done in daylight, with papers. Otherwise we are exactly what they said we were — savages holding hostages in a ruin."

"We *were* savages holding hostages," someone called from the back, and there was laughter in it, ugly and defensive, and Ines let it burn out before she spoke.

"You were frightened people protecting your homes," she said. "There is a difference, and the difference is that we are deciding this in council instead of with bullets. But Domingo is right about one thing. Whatever we do tonight, we will be it afterward. That is the true sentence. Not whatever happens to him. What happens to us."

Vane laughed.

It was the first sound he had made all day, and it went through the room like a knife through wet canvas. Forty-one faces turned to him.

"Forgive me," he said, and his voice was smooth as contract language, unhurried, almost gentle. "It's only — you're doing it beautifully. The deliberation. The gravity. I've sat through a hundred of these. Boardrooms, mostly. Same architecture. The elders speak, the hotheads speak, someone invokes the ancestors." He inclined his head toward Beatriz with terrible courtesy. "Madam. And at the end, someone realizes that killing me makes you murderers and releasing me makes you fools, and the compromise is always the same: hand me to someone else and let the problem become theirs." He smiled. "So let me save you the evening. My lawyers are in Puerto Ladeado. My company holds the mineral concessions on half this watershed. Kill me and my successor arrives with soldiers inside a year. Release me and I'll be generous — compensation, resettlement funds, a school with my name on it. Hand me to the police and I'll be drinking brandy in the capital by Friday. There is no version of this where you win. There are only versions where you lose *well*, and I'm authorized to help you choose one."

The silence after was total. Even the river outside seemed to hold its breath.

And Ines felt it rise in her — the thing she had fed for eleven years, the thing that had kept her warm through every cold night of command. It rose like floodwater, patient and total, and it said: *he is right about the boardrooms but wrong about the valley, and you could end him right now, and no court anywhere would ever weigh it, and your people would carry it forever.*

She thought of her grandmother teaching her to read the river's moods. *The water that destroys is not evil, niña. It is only water that has forgotten where it belongs.*

She stood.

* * *

"Here is what we are going to do," Ines said, "and then we are going to stop talking about him, because he has already taken too many of our evenings."

She crossed the room until she stood directly before Vane, close enough to see the pulse in his throat, and she made herself study that pulse the way she'd study a rapid before running it — not with hatred, with attention.

"You are right that there is no version of this where we win," she said. "You built it that way. You bought the courts and the concessions and the silence, and you are correct that killing you changes nothing, because you are not the dam. You are barely the surveyor of the dam. You are a man who carries other men's arithmetic." She crouched to bring her eyes level with his. "But you are wrong about one thing. You said there are versions where we lose well. You never considered the version where *you* lose."

Something flickered behind his composure — not fear yet, but its herald.

"You wanted our water," she said. "So you will live on it. We are sending you downriver. By boat, the whole way, through every community between here and the delta — San Rafael de Abajo first, where the flood began. They will see your face. They will hear what you did, from us, and then they will do with you what they choose. We wash our hands of you at each landing. No guards beyond our own waters. No instructions. No mercy requested and none delivered. If the communities you flooded feed you, that is their grace. If they put you off the boat in the ruins of the village you drowned, you will sleep in what's left of it. And if the law finds you at the mouth of the river — good. Let the law have what the river leaves."

"That's death," Vane said quietly. All the smoothness had gone out of his voice. "Those villages hate—"

"Yes," Ines said. "They do. Isn't it remarkable. Eleven years of hating, and you never once thought about what it would feel like from this side of the water." She straightened. "We are not killing you, Marcus Vane. We are doing something worse. We are making you go and *see*."

Domingo was nodding slowly. Beatriz stared at the floor, her jaw working, and Ines knew she was weighing the sentence against her grandson's name, and Ines could not make it balance for her, and she did not pretend otherwise. When Beatriz finally lifted her head there were tears on the creases of her face, but she said, "Downriver, then. Let him look at what he made." And the council murmured assent around her like water finding its level.

Only Petra, from the doorway where he'd been leaning unseen, spoke into the quiet. "The lower villages won't all be merciful."

"No," Ines agreed. "They may not be."

"And you're giving him to them anyway."

"I'm giving *us* to them," she said. "Our judgment, our account of it, our hands open. If they choose blood, that blood is theirs to carry, not ours. That is the difference between justice and vengeance, Petra. Vengeance travels alone. Justice brings witnesses."

He held her gaze for a long moment, and then he nodded once, the way he nodded at weather he couldn't argue with, and pushed off from the doorframe and went to make arrangements for the boat.

* * *

They launched at first light, in the gray hour when the mist stands on the water like a congregation.

Ines rode in the lead boat herself, because she would not send another woman's son to do what her word had decreed. Vane sat amidships with his wrists free — she had ordered the cord cut at the last landing above the gorge; bound men cannot row, and rowing was part of the sentence — and he pulled when told to pull and rested when told to rest, and with each kilometer downstream she watched the corporate armor flake off him the way paint flakes off a hull left too long in the sun. By the second day he had stopped calculating. By the third he had started flinching at the sound of water over shallows.

At midday on the third day they rounded the bend below the old ferry crossing, and San Rafael de Abajo came into view.

Or what remained of it. The reservoir had been drawn down since the scandal broke — the hearings, the leaked files, the consortium's slow public unraveling — and the ruins stood exposed along the shoreline like bones at low tide: foundations, a church wall with its bell arch empty, the stub of the mango tree where children used to climb. Water-stained to twice a man's height. Everything the color of drowning.

Vane stopped rowing.

Ines let him look. She had promised the council no cruelty, and she kept to it — no words, no gloating, no recitation of names. She simply let the boat drift in the current while the ruined village did its work, and she watched this man who had spoken of compensation and schools-with-his-name-on-them stare at a church foundation and slowly, slowly understand that the numbers he had carried had been people.

"There were four hundred and six of us," she said at last, quietly. "When the sirens came. I was nineteen. I counted us onto the ridge because my grandmother was too old to run and someone had to count." She turned to face him. "Two hundred and ninety-three answered the next morning's roll call. You will go ashore here. You will walk up to the ridge where we waited, and you will see the graves, and tomorrow morning the people of San Rafael will decide what your hands deserve. We leave at dawn. Whatever they choose is theirs."

They put him ashore among the foundations. He stood ankle-deep in the silt of a street that had been a street, and for the first time since the pass, Marcus Vane looked small in a way that had nothing to do with distance.

As the boat pulled away, Ines did not look back. She looked forward, down the long brown artery of the river, toward the delta and the sea and the hearing rooms where Petra Marchetti would soon stand and spend his name like his last coin. Her power sat in her chest like ballast stone — heavy, necessary, hers. She had held a man's life in her hands for three days and had chosen neither the knife nor the brandy-and-forgiveness, and the choosing had cost her something she could not yet name, some easy certainty she suspected she would not get back.

Tomás shipped his oars beside her and squinted upstream, toward the high country.

"The guide," he said. "Marchetti. He leaves for the capital tomorrow, yes? To testify?"

"At first light."

Tomás grunted, reading the water. "Then pray the river runs fast," he said, "because word came up with the fishing boats this morning — the consortium's lawyers filed an injunction in Puerto Ladeado. They're calling the stolen files theft. They're calling *him* a thief." He spat over the gunwale, thoughtful. "And a thief, señora, can be discredited. A thief's testimony weighs nothing."

Ines looked back once, at the small figure on the shore growing smaller among the ruins, and then fixed her eyes downriver, where the water ran dark and fast toward a courtroom she could not enter and a friend she could not follow.

"Then we'd better make sure," she said, "that he isn't alone when he stands up to speak."

Behind them, faint on the wind off the water, the empty bell arch of San Rafael seemed almost to ring.
