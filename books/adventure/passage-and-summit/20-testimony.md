## 20. Testimony

The hearing room smelled of floor wax and cold coffee, and Petra Reyes thought it was the least honest room he had ever stood in.

Everything in it pretended to be something it wasn't. The wood paneling was laminate. The flag behind the tribunal's bench had been ironed into a stiffness no wind would ever give it. The water pitcher on the long table sweated gently under fluorescent light, and the stenographer's fingers hovered above her keys like birds waiting for a signal to land. Even the air conditioning hummed a note of false calm, as if the building itself were trying to convince him that nothing that happened here could hurt anyone.

He knew better. He had spent five years learning exactly how much damage a quiet room could do.

They had dressed him for it, or tried to. The lawyer the journalists' consortium had retained — a narrow man named Ferreyra with reading glasses on a chain and the posture of a man who apologized for existing — had produced a jacket the night before, dark gray, discreet, and said, *You want to look like someone they can trust.*

Petra had held it up, felt the weight of it, and hung it back over the chair.

"I spent five years being someone they could trust," he said. "It didn't work out."

So he sat at the witness table in the same canvas shirt he'd worn up the Río Sombra, mended at the shoulder where Vane's rearguard had put a bullet through the fabric and very nearly through the arm beneath it, and he let them look at what he actually was: a blacklisted guide with a ruined record, a scarred forearm, and a folder of stolen hydrology data that had already been splashed across three continents.

The tribunal was three of them — two men and a woman — seated high enough that witnesses had to lift their chins to speak. The woman, Judge Arrieta, had eyes like river stones: smooth, gray, and impossible to read depth in. She had been courteous so far. Courteous the way ice is courteous.

"Mr. Reyes," she said now, after the morning's procedural weather had finally cleared, "before we continue, I'm obligated to confirm your understanding of the terms currently before this body."

"I understand them," Petra said.

"For the record, please state them."

He almost smiled. They loved the record. Everything here existed twice — once in the air, once on paper — and only the paper version counted. He had learned that lesson too, years ago, in a room much like this one, when two clients went into the mountain and did not come out, and the consortium's lawyers had built a cathedral of paper around the truth until the truth couldn't be seen from the street.

"You're offering me reinstatement," Petra said. "Full restoration of my certification. Expunged record. Back pay, retroactive license, the right to guide legally anywhere in the territory." He paused. "In exchange for limiting my testimony. I confirm the hydrology findings but decline to name individual actors. No testimony regarding Marcus Vane's personal conduct. No testimony regarding the internal communications of Meridian Water Resources. The record stays clean of names."

"And you have declined these terms?"

"Three times," Petra said. "Your counsel keeps hoping I'll get tired."

A rustle moved through the gallery — reporters mostly, a few NGO observers, and in the third row, motionless as a heron, Tomás, who had come downriver for the first time in eleven years because he said somebody ought to sit there who knew what the water actually sounded like. Beside him sat Dr. Annelise Halvorsen, freed, cleared, thinner than the woman whose voice Petra had first heard through a cell wall in the old mission house, but upright, her hands folded on her knees like a woman keeping them from doing something.

And at the end of the row, half in shadow because she preferred it there, Ines Calloway, who had traveled four days to sit in a room that smelled of lies, because she wanted to watch him do it. She hadn't spoken to him since dawn. She didn't need to. Some things were said better by presence than by words, and she had always known that about herself.

Judge Arrieta made a small mark on the pad before her. "Very well. Let us proceed to the matter of your own record."

* * *

It took them an hour to walk him through it, and he let them take their time, because the walking-through was the point.

Five years ago — six now, if you counted honestly — Petra Reyes had led a commercial expedition on the Cordillera's northern faces for Meridian-affiliated outfitters, back when the consortium's money reached everywhere like groundwater. Two clients, a honeymooning couple from the coast, had died in an avalanche on a route he had chosen. That was the official story, and it was even true, in the way official stories are true: he had chosen the route. What the story left out was why.

What it left out was the satellite phone call he'd received the night before, from an operations manager he'd never met, telling him the window was closing and the clients were expensive and the summit photo was already promised to a sponsor. What it left out was the weather data that had been quietly reclassified, the avalanche forecast that had come to him secondhand and softened, the pressure applied not with threats but with invoices. What it left out was that when the mountain came down, it came down on people who had been placed beneath it by arithmetic, and that afterward, the same company that had pushed him upward buried him — a report here, a withheld log there, a safety violation invented whole — until his name meant *avalanche* and no insurer would touch him and no outfitter would hire him and the blacklist closed over his head like water.

He had told all of this once before, early on, to an inquiry that had listened politely and concluded that a guide must bear responsibility for his route. He had stopped telling it after that. Telling it was like bleeding into a river; the river didn't care, and the blood was gone either way.

But today the river was in the room. Today the stolen files — Halvorsen's complete hydrology series, plus the internal correspondence Petra had photographed off Vane's own terminal at Camp Perdida — had cracked the consortium open like a geode, and everyone could see what had been inside it all along.

"Mr. Reyes," said the counsel for the consortium — a silver-haired man named Duarte whose firm had, Petra noted with some satisfaction, lost three of its partners since the files went public — "you've described events from six years ago. You'll understand we find it convenient that these recollections surface only now, alongside material obtained through admitted theft."

"It isn't convenient," Petra said. "It's expensive. You're offering me my life back and I'm turning it down in front of witnesses. There's nothing convenient about that."

"The documents you've provided—"

"Were stolen. Yes. I stole them. I broke into Camp Perdida, I copied Marcus Vane's files, I transmitted them to journalists from a satellite beacon while his men shot at me, and I'd do every part of it again tomorrow morning before breakfast." He heard his own voice rise and brought it back down. "You can charge me with that too, if you like. Add it to the pile. But don't stand there and tell me the timing is suspicious. The timing is that nobody believed me until I brought receipts."

Judge Arrieta lifted one hand, and Duarte sat.

"Mr. Reyes," she said, "the tribunal has reviewed the Meridian internal correspondence. We've reviewed the reclassified meteorological advisories from the season of the northern avalanches. We've heard testimony from Dr. Halvorsen regarding the falsification of survey data on the Río Sombra, and from engineers formerly employed by the consortium regarding the downstream communities flooded during the trial impoundments." Her voice never changed register; it simply proceeded, like water finding its level. "None of this is actually in dispute anymore. What remains in dispute — what you are making a question — is your own status."

"My own status," Petra repeated.

"You may restore yourself today. One sentence of limitation — decline to elaborate on Mr. Vane's personal culpability beyond what the documents already establish — and the tribunal is prepared to recommend full restoration. Your record would read clean. You could guide again. Legally. Anywhere."

The room went very quiet. Even the air conditioner seemed to lean in.

Petra looked at the pitcher of water. He looked at his hands on the table — rope-scarred, sun-cured, the hands of a man who had measured his life in miles for so long that stillness felt like a foreign country. He thought about what a clean record would mean. A laminated card. His name pronounced correctly by strangers. The right to stand at a trailhead and say *follow me* without someone checking a blacklist first. Five years of hunger, solved in a sentence.

Then he thought about the valley.

He thought about the mission house with its cell walls thin enough to hear a guilty man confess through. About the flooded village downstream — Ines's village, San Rafael de las Aguas, drowned to the spill line during the trial impoundments while its people were told it was temporary. About the two villagers who had died in the shallows defending the mission house from Vane's mercenaries, whose names Ines had spoken aloud at the burial and would speak aloud every year for the rest of her life. About Halvorsen, dead in the gorge, buying thirty minutes with his life so the boats could close the river's throat, his journal now wrapped in oilcloth and carried toward the source by a man who had hated him and then hadn't.

About the source itself — the high basin where the Río Sombra is born, sacred ground, green and glacial and utterly unphotographed until this year — which would be a reservoir floor within a decade if the dam lived, and which would live unless people stood in rooms like this one and said names out loud.

Vane's personal conduct. Vane's signature on the order to flood San Rafael ahead of schedule, before the legal challenges could ripen. Vane's signature on the operation to erase the witnesses. Vane's face across a camp table offering Petra his old life back like a man returning a borrowed coat.

If Petra took the deal, the documents would still condemn the consortium. The dam would probably still die. But Vane would retire to a consultancy, and the men who signed orders would keep signing them, and somewhere else — some other river, some other valley whose people weren't in the gallery because they didn't know yet that they needed to be — the same arithmetic would run again, quietly, in rooms that smelled of floor wax.

"Judge Arrieta," Petra said.

"Yes, Mr. Reyes."

"I want to answer your question properly, so I'm going to take a moment."

"Take it."

He turned then — the first time all day he had turned his back on the bench — and looked at the gallery. At Tomás, who gave him nothing, which was Tomás's way of giving him everything. At Halvorsen, who nodded once, small, like a woman sealing a letter. At Ines, whose chin lifted a degree, and whose eyes said what they had said on the summit ridge above the source: *Look back down the whole length of it. Then choose.*

He turned back to the bench.

"Here is what I know about records," Petra said. "I've had mine ruined by people who kept theirs spotless. Marcus Vane has never had a blemish on any document bearing his name — not when he ordered the forecast softened above the northern col, not when he signed the early impoundment at San Rafael, not when he sent armed men upriver to erase a village's witnesses. Clean paper, every page. And everything he touched came apart, and other people paid for it. My record says I killed two clients. The truth is that a company spent them, and when the bill came due, they wrote my name on it instead of theirs." He laid his scarred forearm flat on the table, the bullet scar visible below the rolled sleeve. "So no. I won't trade silence for a clean record. I've seen what clean records are worth. I'd rather carry the true one."

Duarte was on his feet. "The witness is declining—"

"I'm declining," Petra agreed. "Formally. For the record." He almost smiled at that. "Put it in twice."

* * *

They made him say the rest of it anyway, and he was glad, because saying it was the whole point.

For two more hours he walked them through it: the contract from Ochoa, the smashed radios at the lower camp, the survey stake stamped with the consortium's logo standing in the gorge like a nail driven into the valley's palm. The mission house. Halvorsen's confession through the cell wall — *half my team were plants, and the real data drowns the valley.* Camp Perdida, and Vane's offer, and the fire he'd made of it in a steel drum, and the files he'd carried out against his own bleeding. The beacon transmitting from a ledge above the falls while tracer-light stitched the dark. The gorge trap. Halvorsen falling. Ines taken, and himself traded for her, and the checkmate played not with guns but with a press list uploaded three days earlier.

He spoke until his voice went rough, and the stenographer's keys clattered like rain on a tin roof, and the paper version of the truth accumulated page by page, and this time nobody would be able to build a cathedral over it, because too many people had copies.

Near the end, Judge Arrieta removed her glasses and regarded him for a long moment with those river-stone eyes.

"Mr. Reyes," she said, "for clarity of the record. Knowing that this tribunal will accept your refusal, knowing your record will remain as it stands — blacklisted, uncertified, formally responsible for the deaths of Elena and Mateo Ferrer — do you wish to add anything to your statement?"

Petra thought of the two names. He carried them the way Ines carried hers. He had gone back, in the years between, to the northern col twice, alone, in seasons when no one guided, and sat with the snow and said their names into it. It had not fixed anything. It had kept him human, which was different and better.

"Yes," he said. "Two things. First: Elena and Mateo Ferrer deserved a guide who would have turned that phone call off and walked them down. I wasn't that guide, and I've spent six years becoming him, and if my record is the price of becoming him, then the record can stay. Second—" and here he looked past the bench, past the laminate and the flag, as if he could see all the way upriver from this windowless room, "—there is a valley that fed me when I had nothing, that sheltered me when I was hunted, and that asked me for nothing except to be left its water. If the cost of saving it is that I never guide legally again, then I want the record to show that I consider that the cheapest thing I've ever bought."

He sat back down. Somewhere in the gallery, very quietly, Tomás exhaled through his nose — the sound he made instead of laughing, instead of crying, instead of praying.

Judge Arrieta replaced her glasses. "The tribunal notes the witness's refusal," she said. "We will recess for the day. Mr. Reyes — you're free to go."

Free to go. He almost laughed at that too. He had been free to go for six years; that had never once been the problem.

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had turned golden and dusty, and the capital's traffic snarled below the courthouse steps like a river running backward. Reporters came at him on the steps — they always did now, though less to ask questions than to confirm what they'd already written — and he gave them the only quote he had.

"They asked me to buy my name back with the valley's silence," he said. "I told them the valley isn't for sale. It never was. That was the whole problem."

Then he walked, because his legs wanted to walk, down through the city's noise toward the riverfront, where the water ran brown and heavy with the season's silt, and found Ines waiting on a bench with her boots muddy — she had refused, all week, to wear anything but her valley boots in this city of polished floors — and Tomás a polite distance away, pretending to study a moored ferry with great interest.

"So," Ines said. "That's it. It stays ruined."

"That's it. It stays ruined."

"No card. No license. If you set foot on a commercial route tomorrow, they can arrest you."

"If I set foot on a commercial route tomorrow," Petra said, "they can arrest me."

She studied him the way she studied the river in dry season — reading the drop, calculating what remained. "You look," she said at last, "like a man who just lost everything."

"I know." He sat down beside her, and the bench was hard and the sun was warm and somewhere below them the brown water went on toward the sea, carrying everything, keeping some of it. "It's the strangest thing, Ines. I feel lighter than I've felt since before the col. Like I finally put down something I was never strong enough to carry."

She was quiet a moment. Then she reached into the canvas bag at her feet and drew out an envelope, soft-cornered, travel-worn, and handed it to him without ceremony, the way she handed over everything — as if it weighed nothing, as if it weighed everything.

"What's this?"

"A letter," she said. "From the council. They met last week, after the hearing transcripts started arriving upriver. All of them, the whole valley federation, from the mission settlements down to the delta villages." She watched his face as he turned the envelope over. "They voted."

Petra's thumb hesitated at the flap. "On what?"

"On whether the valley will receive a guide," Ines said, and now the corner of her mouth moved, the smallest crack in her command-stillness, the expression he had learned to read as her version of laughing. "Not a licensed one. Not a certified one. The law says you can't guide, and the law is welcome to its opinion. But the river runs through our ground, señor Reyes, and the passes are ours, and the source is ours, and the council says — read it, it's shorter in their words than mine —"

He broke the seal and read it. It was short. It was, as she promised, better in their words. At the bottom, beneath the council marks of nine communities, someone had added a postscript in a young hand, and he recognized the script of the boy who had carried water at the mission house, the one who had watched him all that summer with wide untrusting eyes:

*Come back and teach us the ropes. We will teach you the river.*

Petra read it twice. Then a third time, though the words didn't change.

"Well?" Ines said.

He folded the letter along its worn creases and put it inside his shirt, over his heart, where the important papers went — where Halvorsen's journal had ridden, where the map of the source basin lived now in his memory deeper than any paper.

"Well," Petra said, "it seems I'm still blacklisted everywhere in the country." He stood, and offered her his hand, and the late light caught the scar on his forearm and the beginning of a smile he didn't try to hide. "Except where it counts. When do we leave?"

"Tomorrow," Ines said, taking it. "There's a party of students coming up from the delta. First ones ever. They want to see where the water is born." She rose beside him, and together they looked down the long brown length of the river, all the way in their minds to the sea. "Pack light, señor Reyes. And Petra —"

"Mm?"

"Next time a judge offers you your life back," she said, "remember you already gave it away. To better people."

Down at the dock, Tomás was already untying a boat.
