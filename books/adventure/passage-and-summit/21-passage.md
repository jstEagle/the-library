## 21. Passage

Puerto Ladeado smelled the same as it always had in the late dry season: diesel and dust and river mud drying on the pilings, a sweetness of overripe mango rotting somewhere behind the fuel depot. Petra stood at the top of the boat ramp for a long time before he went down it, because he had learned that year that some arrivals deserved to be taken slowly.

Five months since the hearing room. Five months since he had said *yes, that is my signature* and *yes, I was blacklisted* and *no, I will not accept the settlement*, each word landing like a stone set carefully on a cairn. The dam was dead — not officially, not yet, but the consortium had withdrawn its engineers, and money, which is a faster animal than law, had already run. The scientists were free. Halvorsen's honest ones were testifying; the planted ones were scattered or worse. And his own record remained exactly what it had been: ruined, annotated, permanent.

He had expected to feel lighter. He did not feel lighter. He felt *accurate*, which was different, and took longer to live inside.

The ramp boards were soft under his boots. At the bottom, moored where she had always been moored, sat the *Mariposa* — Tomás's boat, Tomás's pride, her paint gone chalky and her name repainted in fresh white letters that trembled slightly, as if the hand that wrote them had been unsteady with something other than age.

Tomás was under the awning amidships, mending a net he did not use, because mending nets was what Tomás did with his hands when his heart needed somewhere to sit. He looked up. He did not stand.

"You came back," Tomás said.

"You said I could."

"I said a lot of things." The old man's face worked through several expressions and settled on none of them. "I said I'd never take you past the gorge again. I said your kind of trouble floats downstream and lands on other people's decks. I said—" He stopped. His hands kept working the net, knot after small patient knot. "I heard about the hearing. Radio. Then a man from the capital came asking questions about you, and I told him nothing, because there was nothing good to tell and nothing true either."

Petra crouched at the edge of the deck, keeping himself below Tomás's eye line, an old habit of deference between men who had once been close enough to share a fire every night for a season. "I'm not here to ask you to take me anywhere."

"Good."

"I'm here to ask if I can help you paint."

Tomás's hands went still. For a moment the only sound was the river slapping the hull, that patient applause water gives everything it passes. "You always were a terrible painter," he said finally. "You put it on too thick. You think more paint means more protection. It doesn't. It just hides the rot longer."

"I know," Petra said. "I've been learning the difference."

They painted the *Mariposa* over four days. Tomás directed; Petra scraped and sanded and laid on thin coats, one after another, waiting properly between them, and by the third day Tomás had begun to talk — not about the gorge, not about the men who had fired on them both from the cliffs while Tomás ran boats through the narrows to block Vane's retreat, but around those things, the way water talks around stones. About his daughter in Ladeado who wanted him to stop running the river. About the price of diesel. About a heron that had started fishing off his stern every morning at six, punctual as debt.

On the fourth evening, sitting on the ramp with their boots in the shallows, Tomás said, without looking at him: "Halvorsen. The old man who stayed at the falls so the rest could get away." A pause. "You were fond of him?"

"He lied to me first," Petra said. "Then he told me the truth at the worst possible moment, and then he spent it buying us twenty minutes against guns. So yes. Fond isn't quite the word. But it's the closest one I have."

Tomás nodded slowly. "My brother drowned in this river when we were boys. My father never spoke his name again. Said grief should be carried, not displayed." He turned his beer can in his fingers. "I have decided my father was wrong about that. Half wrong. You carry it, yes. But you should be able to say the name."

"Emil Halvorsen," Petra said.

"Emil Halvorsen," Tomás repeated, tasting it, approving of the weight. "So. What will you do with what he left you?"

"The journal goes back to the source," Petra said. "Where the river starts. That's where he wanted it. He wrote that down before—" He stopped. Started again. "It's sacred ground up there. Ines's people closed it to outsiders a long time ago. She's going to open it for one day, for one grave."

Tomás was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed — roughened, lowered, the voice he used on the river itself. "No one has asked me to go past the gorge since," he said. "Not because they fear it. Because they think I'm finished." He looked out at the darkening water. "Ask me."

*

Ines met them at the mouth of the Río Sombra three days later, standing in a dugout with two paddlers, and the sight of her hit Petra harder than he had prepared for. Not because she had changed — though she had, the raid and the losses and the judgment she had handed down to Vane all written now in the set of her shoulders — but because she hadn't changed where it mattered. She still looked at him the way she had on the supply run, that first grudging day: as a problem not yet solved, a man not yet dismissed.

"You brought the boatman," she said.

"He brought himself."

"I know. He signaled us from the bend. Old man still runs the narrows like he was born in them." Almost a smile. It didn't finish, but it got further than any smile of hers had gotten in the mission house, in the days when he had been her prisoner and she had been certain of everything. "The council agreed to one day. Sunrise to sunset. You carry nothing out of the basin but yourselves and what you bury. No samples. No photographs of the water."

"The journal?"

She considered. "The journal goes into the ground. That is not taking it out. That is giving it back." She stepped across into the *Mariposa* without asking permission, the way people do who have grown up on water, and settled herself at the bow like a figurehead that had opinions. "Go, then. We lose daylight arguing."

They ran the gorge in the last safe week of the season, water low and clear, the bells — those hollow stone chimes the current played in the narrows — ringing softer than Petra remembered, almost gentle, as if the gorge too had been through its own dry season and come out quieter. Tomás handled the boat with a grace that made Petra's chest ache, reading water the way illiterate men read faces, instantly and completely. Ines watched him work and said, once, loudly enough to carry over the engine: "Whoever taught him that taught him well."

"My father," Tomás said. "And the river corrected the parts he got wrong."

Above the gorge they transferred to the dugouts Ines's people had cached, and then there was only walking — two days of it, up trails that narrowed to goat-width, through forest that smelled of wet cedar and bruised leaves, the air cooling degree by degree until breath began to show. Ines led. Her sentries had melted away at the boundary of the high basin; whatever happened above the source, apparently, was not for guards. It was for witnesses, or it was for no one.

Petra carried the journal wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and the weight of it was nothing — less than a kilo of paper and leather — and also everything, and he found himself adjusting the strap constantly, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it's real.

On the second night, camped below the final climb, Ines sat across the fire from him and broke her own silence the way she seemed to do everything: deliberately, and only when she had decided the cost was acceptable.

"When we held you in the mission house," she said, "I believed I was protecting this valley. I still believe that. What I no longer believe is that I knew how." She fed a stick to the flames. "Two of my people died in that courtyard. Marisol. And the boy, Andrés, who was seventeen and thought he was immortal, the way seventeen-year-olds are permitted to think for exactly one summer." Her voice did not break. It simply went very flat and very careful, a road after rockfall. "I keep their names the way you keep your miles. Counted. Carried."

"I know," Petra said. "I saw you say them. The morning we buried them."

"And I saw you stand apart, because you understood it wasn't yours to stand inside." She looked up at him across the fire, and her eyes held the thing he had first seen in them in the interrogation room — not hatred anymore, but appraisal, ongoing, unfinished. "Vane offered you your name back. One conversation, one silence. I have wondered all these months what it cost you to refuse. Not in money. In *self*."

Petra turned his hands toward the fire. The scars from the escape downriver had healed into pale ridges across his knuckles, a map of a country he'd crossed quickly. "For five years I measured my life in miles," he said. "Distance from the avalanche. Distance from the consortium. Distance from Puerto Ladeado, from clients, from anything that could accuse me of anything. I thought if I got far enough from the worst day of my life, it would stop being mine." He shook his head. "It doesn't work. The miles don't work. You just arrive places carrying the same man."

"And now?"

"Now I'm trying to measure differently. In miles I chose." He nodded up-slope, toward the dark shoulder of the mountain where the river was born. "This one, for instance. Nobody paid me. Nobody contracted me. I chose it, and it costs me the last thing I had, which was the story where I was innocent."

Ines studied him for a long moment. "That," she said at last, "is the first thing an outsider has ever said to me about this valley that I did not need to check for hooks." She rose, folded her blanket with military precision. "Sleep. Tomorrow we give the old man back to the water."

*

They reached the source at dawn.

Petra had imagined it a hundred times during the climb — had tried to, anyway, and failed, because his imagination kept producing reservoirs, intake towers, the geometry of capture. What he found instead was a meadow the size of a cathedral floor, ringed in dark stone, threaded with channels of water so clear they were visible only by the light they bent. The river did not begin here so much as *seep into being* — rising through moss and gravel in a hundred silver threads that braided and rebraided, gathering conviction, until at the meadow's lower edge they became a stream you could step across, and then, inexorably, a river.

The light came up the valley wall and struck the water all at once, and the whole meadow lit from beneath, glowing like something held to a lamp.

Ines knelt at the edge of the stonework — and it *was* stonework, ancient, low walls marking paths the community had walked for generations, worn smooth by feet — and murmured words in her own language, brief and unhurried, an introduction rather than a plea. Then she looked back at Petra and nodded once.

He unwrapped the journal.

Halvorsen's handwriting filled it edge to edge — hydrology tables at the front, the honest data, the numbers that would drown the valley if the dam were ever built; and behind them, in a tighter, darker script, the confession, the ledger of names, the slow unraveling of a man who had sold half his team's integrity for funding and spent his last months buying it back at the only exchange rate available. Petra had read it twice on the journey north. He would not read it again. Some documents are meant to be carried until they can be put down.

There was no coffin. There was a hollow among the stones at the meadow's head, where the largest spring rose, and Ines had told him the custom: things given to the source become part of the water. Every drop that leaves this valley afterward carries a little of what was returned here.

Petra knelt and laid the journal in the hollow. The paper would not last; he knew that, and suspected Halvorsen had known it too, and had chosen the place anyway, understanding that permanence was never the point. Copies existed — in regulators' files, in journalists' archives, in the testimony transcript with his own ruined name signed at the bottom. This copy was not for the world. This copy was for the river.

"Ines," he said. "Read something. If there's something to read."

She considered, then recited — not her language this time but Spanish, plainly, so he could hold it too:

*"For the water that was measured honestly, and the men who were not. Let the numbers rest where the numbers were born."*

They filled the hollow together, stone by stone, and when it was done the spring rose around the cairn and began, thread by silver thread, to leave.

Petra stayed kneeling a long time. He was not praying. He was counting — but differently than he ever had. Not miles escaped. Not years lost. Just this: one mile, chosen, completed. Then another beginning immediately, downhill, all the way to the sea.

Ines touched his shoulder — briefly, lightly, the first time she had ever initiated contact — and said, "The light's good. We should walk while we have it."

They walked down out of the basin side by side, and behind them the source kept giving the river away, drop by drop, asking nothing back.

* * *

Three weeks later, in Puerto Ladeado, a letter arrived at the dockmaster's office addressed in a hand Petra did not recognize. Inside was a government permit — a cultural access license, the first ever issued for the upper Río Sombra — bearing conditions drafted jointly by the Ministry and, in an annex written in a familiar blunt style, the Río Sombra community council. The license named two guides. One was Petra Reyes, blacklist intact, noted in the margin as *provisional, subject to annual review*. The other was Ines Calloway.

Attached was a single sheet in Ines's handwriting: *First party arrives in six weeks. They want to see the gorge and the source. I told them the guides decide the route. Decide.*

Petra walked down to the *Mariposa*, where Tomás was painting her name yet again, whiter than ever, and held out the letter without a word.

The old man read it twice. Then he looked up at the river — high and brown and running strong with the first rains, the whole valley drinking — and smiled like a man who has just been told the season is open.

"Well," Tomás said. "Better start planning the route."
