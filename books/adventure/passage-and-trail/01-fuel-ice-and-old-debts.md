## 1. Fuel, Ice, and Old Debts

The last week of a medevac season smells like avgas and antiseptic, and I was hosing the second one off the deck of the *Kestrel Queen* when Mara Voss walked into my hangar like she still owned the lease.

Three years, and the woman hadn't learned to knock. She just pushed through the door with the wind at her back and her collar up, and stood there in the doorway letting the cold pour in around her boots while I pretended the hose in my hands required my full attention.

"Jonah Brightwater," she said. "Still hiding behind that airplane."

"Still walking into buildings uninvited." I shut off the water. "Hangar's closed. Season's over. Go home."

"I need a crew."

"You've got one. You've got three crews and a hangar twice this size."

She came down the length of the bay slow, her eyes doing inventory — the Queen's patched cowling, the oil stain on the concrete shaped vaguely like a country nobody recognized, Dez's toolbox left open where he'd abandoned it mid-curse an hour ago. Mara Voss had run cargo out of Meridian Field since before I got my license, and she read aircraft the way other people read faces: fast, unsentimental, and usually right.

"Not for me," she said. "For you. Triple rate."

I laughed. It came out wrong — too quick, too flat — and she heard it, because she heard everything.

"Triple," I said. "Nobody pays triple for anything anymore except lawyers and liars."

"Interior run. Two tons of freight, maybe more if the weather holds. Out to the Halvard research station past the divide. They're stranded up there — lost their resupply window, lost their radio mast in a storm, and they've got people going through winter stores by November." She pulled a folded paper from inside her jacket and set it on the workbench between us, smoothing it flat with one hand like she was taming something. "Contract's simple. You fly, I pay, everybody pretends we were never friends."

"That last part's not in the standard form."

"I had it added specially."

The paper sat there. I didn't touch it. Outside, the wind was working the hangar door in its hinges, that low moan the Kestrel Range makes when it wants you to know it's thinking about you.

"Route?" I asked, and hated myself for asking, because some part of me already knew.

"Alder Pass."

There it is. There's the moment in every man's life when the floor tilts a degree and he has to decide whether to say so. I picked up a rag and started drying my hands, very thoroughly, finger by finger, buying seconds.

"No," I said.

"It's the only way in. The southern valleys are already snowed under, and the long way around adds four hundred miles and two fuel stops neither of us can guarantee. Alder Pass is open maybe another ten days before it closes for the season. It's the pass or nothing."

"Then it's nothing. Find another pilot."

"There is no other pilot, Jonah. That's the whole point of triple rate. Everyone else looked at the route and said no, and everyone else is smarter than you." She leaned against the workbench, arms crossed, watching me with those gray eyes that used to watch me across a briefing table in another life. "You know the pass better than any man flying. You grew up on those approaches."

"I grew up on a lot of things."

"You crashed there."

The rag stopped moving. Outside, the wind shoved the door again, impatient.

"Three years ago," she went on, quieter now, which was worse. "Elias Thorne's plane. Your mentor. I read the report. Weather-related, they called it. Whiteout, CFIT, the whole alphabet soup. And then you sold your share of the company, moved out here to the edge of nowhere, and started hauling insulin and mail like you could outrun a map." She tapped the contract once. "I'm not asking you to forget. I'm asking you to fly over it. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes. One of them pays triple."

* * *

Dez found me an hour later in the office, staring at the contract like it might blink first. He's a big man, Dez Okonkwo, built like the airframe he maintains, and he filled the doorway the way he fills everything — completely, and with opinions.

"You signed it yet?"

"No."

"Good. Don't." He dropped into the chair across from me, the one with the broken spring that always felt like it was judging you. "Mara Voss walks in here after three years of silence, offers money nobody sane would offer, and routes it through Alder Pass. You see the shape of that? Because I see the shape of that, and the shape is a trap."

"It's a supply run."

"It's a supply run through the one valley on earth you won't talk about. I've flown with you three years, Jonah. You reroute around that airspace even when it costs us fuel. You go quiet when the weather report mentions the Kestrels. Last spring you made me take down a chart because it had the pass printed on it." He spread his hands. "And now you're telling me it's just miles?"

I looked at the contract again. The numbers were obscene. Enough to rebuild the Queen properly, replace the engine I'd been nursing along on prayer and safety wire, pay off the hangar, put real money behind Dez for once instead of promises. Enough that refusing it wasn't stubbornness anymore — it was charity toward my own ghosts, and ghosts don't pay rent.

"The station's got people on it," I said. "Scientists. They're running out of food."

"People are always running out of something. That's the business." Dez leaned forward. "I'm not saying don't do it because I'm soft. I'm saying don't do it because I know you. You'll take this job, you'll tell yourself it's about the money and the scientists, and somewhere over that valley your hands are going to remember things your mouth never told anybody. And I'll be sitting in the right seat watching you come apart at eleven thousand feet."

"That's very dramatic."

"I'm a very dramatic man. Ask my ex-wife." He stood up, took the contract off the desk, and read it all the way through, which is more than I'd done. Then he set it back down, face-up, pen beside it. "Triple rate. Two tons. Ten days before the pass closes." He shook his head slowly. "The money's too good to refuse and the route's too bad to accept. That's not a contract, Jonah. That's a riddle."

"And what's the answer?"

"Ask yourself why she brought it to *you*. Voss doesn't do favors and she doesn't do cruelty. So which is this?"

He left me alone with it. That's the thing about Dez — he knows exactly how much rope to give a man, and it's always precisely enough to hang yourself or climb down, and he genuinely doesn't care which.

* * *

I signed it at dusk, when the light through the hangar windows went the color of old brass and the mountains to the east turned into a black serrated line against a sky going violet. The pen scratched. The ink dried. Somewhere in the middle distance, the Kestrel Range sat under its first dusting of the season, white on granite, patient as a debt.

Just miles, I told myself. Coordinates and headings, fuel burns and density altitudes. A valley is a valley. Ice doesn't remember, and neither does a glacier, and certainly not a man who's spent three years practicing not looking west.

I told myself all of it, the way you tell yourself a story at night so you can sleep, knowing full well the story has holes in it big enough to fly a plane through.

Dez watched me sign without a word, then took the contract, checked the signature against my license like a notary with a grudge, and filed it in the drawer where we keep the paperwork we're not proud of.

"Wheels up Thursday," he said. "Weather window looks clean until the weekend. After that—" he nodded west, toward the pass, toward everything, "—after that, the mountain decides."

That night I dreamed about Elias Thorne's voice on the radio, calm as ever, saying the thing he said that night, the thing I'd spent three years not hearing: *Turn back, Jonah. Turn back now.*

In the dream, for the first time, I answered him.

Thursday couldn't come fast enough, and it couldn't come slow enough, and when it finally came — gray, cold, the wind out of the northwest smelling of snow — Mara Voss was standing on my apron at first light with a manifest in her hand and something in her expression I couldn't read, and the first line of the manifest, item one of two tons of freight, was written in handwriting I knew.

It was Elias Thorne's.
