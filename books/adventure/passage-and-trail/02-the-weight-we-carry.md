## 2. The Weight We Carry

**JONAH**

Loading a plane for the interior is a theology of weight. Everything goes on the scale, everything gets argued with, and in the end you pray you guessed right about what the sky will forgive.

Dez and I worked the Otter's belly like men packing a coffin — which, if you asked Dez, was exactly what we were doing. Crates of diesel, a disassembled snowmobile, medical pallets wrapped in oilcloth, and one long black case Mara Voss had personally supervised into the cargo bay without telling either of us what was inside it.

"Triple pay," Dez muttered, cinching a strap until the webbing sang. "You know what triple pay means, Jonah? It means she's paying us double to not ask questions."

"It means she's in a hurry."

"Everybody who's in a hurry is in a hurry *away* from something." He slapped the fuselage twice, our old ritual for good luck, though his heart wasn't in it. "Alder Pass, man. You said yourself you'd sooner fly through a firestorm."

I had said that. I'd also signed the contract, so my credibility was already bleeding out on the hangar floor.

Mara came across the apron with her collar up against the wind, carrying a clipboard she didn't need. She'd never needed it. It was theater, same as her crisp expedition jacket, same as the way she looked at the mountains like they owed her money.

"Weight's at limit," she said. "Weather window out of Kestrel Field closes by fourteen hundred. Can we make it?"

"We can make anything," I said. "That's what you're paying for."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, from a time when we'd been young enough to say reckless things to each other and mean them as compliments. Then it was gone, filed away behind the clipboard.

"There's something you should know before we're airborne," she said. "The cargo isn't just supplies. There's a research team camped beyond the pass. Glaciologists. They went in during the last calving window and the weather turned before they could come out. Their radio's failing, their fuel's gone, and two of them need insulin."

Dez stopped strapping. "How long have they been stranded?"

"Eleven days."

"Eleven days," Dez repeated, looking at me. "And you sat on this until the crates were loaded."

"I sat on it until I knew you'd say yes," Mara said flatly. "Because you would have. Both of you. You'd have gone for free and we all know it, and free doesn't charter planes." She met my eyes, and there it was again — that old current under the ice between us. "Jonah. I need you flying this route because you're the only pilot alive who knows Alder Pass in bad air. Whatever history you've got with that valley — I'm asking you to set it down for six hours. Just six hours. Then it's yours again."

Six hours. As if the valley were a room you could walk out of. As if I hadn't spent three years learning that some rooms you carry with you.

"Fourteen hundred," I said, and climbed into the cockpit, and told myself once more that it was just miles.

* * *

**MARA**

I'll admit what I never admitted to him: I watched Jonah Brightwater run his preflight the way other people watch church services. Every switch, every control surface, every prayer performed in the correct order. Three years since I'd seen him work, and he hadn't lost the thing that made him famous in the Range — that terrible, total attention, as if the plane were the only honest conversation left available to him.

He used to talk to me like that too. Once.

We lifted off at 13:12 with the ceiling already sagging toward the ridgelines, and for forty minutes it was the kind of flying that reminds you why people do this at all — hard blue air, the Kestrels shouldering up white on both sides, Dez in back humming off-key to keep the cargo company. Jonah flew with two fingers on the yoke and his jaw set like a man chewing on something he refused to swallow.

Then the weather closed its hand.

It came down the western saddles faster than the forecast promised — a front with teeth, the kind that eats small aircraft and files no report. Turbulence hit us in slabs. The Otter bucked, dropped fifty feet like an elevator with a grudge, and I heard Dez in back swearing with real feeling as the tie-downs groaned.

"Kestrel Approach says the field's below minimums," Jonah shouted over the roar. "We can't get back in."

"So we push through," I said.

"With this load? In this?" He shook his head once, sharp. "There's a strip. Old survey strip, twelve miles south. I'm putting us down."

"You know what's near that strip," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because his hands went very still on the controls — stiller than they'd been in the turbulence — and the temperature in the cockpit dropped ten degrees.

"I know what's near every strip in this range," he said quietly. "Strap in."

* * *

**JONAH**

The survey strip was a gray scar someone had abandoned to the alders twenty years ago, and I put the Otter down on it with the wind trying to fold our wings like laundry. Dez got the ties set in record time. Then the three of us stood in the blowing snow, engines ticking as they cooled, and I looked north up the valley I had sworn off, and there it was.

Half-buried in the icefield above the strip, one wing raised like a hand mid-protest: a wreck I would have known anywhere, from any angle, in any light. Tail number 4471. The Cessna 180 that Elias Wren had flown for thirty years, that he'd let me take the controls of when I was nineteen and full of ambition and certain the mountains were a puzzle built for me to solve.

Three years of melt and refreeze had done what rescue crews couldn't. The ice was giving him back.

"Jonah." Dez's voice, careful. "Hey. We should check the tie-downs."

"In a minute."

"Jonah—"

"*In a minute,*" I said, and it came out wrong, too loud, and Dez took a step back like I'd swung at him, and Mara — Mara just looked at me. She knew. Of course she knew; she'd been there for the aftermath, the funerals, the inquiry, the whole slow demolition of whatever we'd been building. What she didn't know — what nobody knew but me and the mountain — was the part I'd left out of every official account. The warning call Elias made eight seconds before impact. The turn he told me to make. The turn I didn't make, because I was sure, so sure, that I could hold the line tighter and save us ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds. That's what my certainty cost. Elias Wren, and the marriage I hadn't yet managed to propose, and every night since that I'd spent proving to myself I could fly anything, anywhere, perfectly — as if enough perfect landings could balance one imperfect turn.

I walked up to the wreck alone. The fuselage was scoured pale, windows frosted over from inside, and the ice held it the way amber holds a fly — gently, completely, without judgment. I put my bare hand on the cold metal and the grief came up through my arm like current, three years of it, all the miles I'd flown trying to outrun a valley that had simply been waiting for me to come back.

"He'd have hated this," I said aloud, to no one. "Being a landmark."

Behind me, boots crunched. Mara stopped a few yards off, hands in her pockets, snow gathering on her shoulders. She didn't offer comfort. She never had; comfort wasn't a currency between us. She offered presence instead, which was harder.

"The team beyond the pass," she said softly. "Two of them are diabetics, Jonah. Eleven days."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her voice cracked, just slightly, and I realized how much of her own history she was carrying up this valley too. "Because you're standing here talking to a wreck, and I understand, God help me, I understand — but there are living people past that ridge, and the window is closing, and I need to know right now whether the man flying my plane is going to make it through Alder Pass, or whether he's going to freeze up at the mouth of it like he froze up just now."

I looked at the wreck. I looked at her. Somewhere north, the wind was stacking itself into the storm that would seal the pass, and somewhere south, Dez was pretending to inspect a tie-down while watching us like a man watching two people stand on thin ice.

"Elias told me to turn," I heard myself say. "That night. He called the turn and I didn't make it."

Mara went absolutely still.

"That's not—" she started.

"It's the truth. It's the whole truth, and I've got maybe an hour of daylight to decide what to do with it." I pulled my hand off the ice, and the cold stayed in my palm like a brand. "Get back to the plane, Mara. Storm's coming early — and I don't think we're the only ones racing that window anymore."

She frowned. "What do you mean?"

I nodded north, past the wreck, past the ridge, to where — faint, almost lost in the wind-scour — fresh tracks ran along the old survey trail. Tracks made by boots that weren't ours, heading toward our fuel cache.

Someone had gotten here first.
