## 3. Calving Season

The snow came eleven hours early, which in the Kestrel Range is less a weather event than a personal insult.

I stood on the apron at Alder Pass base camp and watched it fall — fat, indifferent flakes dropping out of a sky the color of wet slate — and did the arithmetic every bush pilot does when the sky turns hostile. Fuel aboard: marginal. Fuel cached at the glacier head: supposed to be four drums. Visibility: shrinking by the minute. Window for the crossing: closing like a door with a grudge.

"Jonah." Dez's voice behind me, tight. "You need to see the cache."

The cache was a quarter mile up the moraine, and we walked it in silence because neither of us wanted to say out loud what we were both already thinking. Four fuel drums had been staged there two days ago, tarped and flagged. We found one drum, upright and smug. The other three were gone — not buried, not drifted over. *Gone.* The tarp had been cut, not torn. Clean knife work.

"Voss's rivals," Dez said. "The Corrigan outfit. Their track comes through here."

"I know whose track it is."

"They didn't even take the drums to use them. Look." He pointed, and I looked. Fifty yards off the trail, half-sunk in a drift, all three drums lay on their sides with their caps cracked open. Somebody had emptied forty gallons of avgas into the snow rather than let us have it. That's not theft. That's malice with logistics.

I crouched by the nearest drum and breathed the fumes and thought about how many ways there are to say "turn back" without using words. Then I stood up, brushed the snow off my knees, and said the stupidest sentence of my life:

"We can still make it on one drum if I fly it lean."

Dez stared at me. "Through the pass. In that." He gestured at the sky as though it were an exhibit in a trial he was already winning.

"The calving window opens tomorrow morning. Six hours, maybe eight. After that the ice bridge goes and we're looking at two weeks minimum, and the scientists beyond the pass don't have two weeks. They've got maybe five days of heat left."

"You've been listening to yourself, right? Hearing the words?"

"Every one."

"Then you know this isn't a supply run anymore. This is a penance flight."

I didn't answer that, which was its own answer. Dez has known me three years. He knows what my silences weigh.

* * *

We spent the evening prepping the plane like surgeons prepping a table. Dez pulled panels and checked lines twice; I drained water from the tanks until the sample ran clear and then ran it again because I needed something to be sure about, anything. Mara worked the radio, raising the stranded team at Thule Station — six voices, thin and crackling, grateful and scared in equal measure. She logged their supplies, their fuel, the state of Dr. Okafor's frostbitten hands. Her voice never wavered. It never does. That used to infuriate me. Now it steadied me like a handrail.

At 2100 she came out to the hangar where I was pretending to torque bolts I'd already torqued.

"The window forecast moved," she said. "It's opening earlier. 0400, not 0600."

"Then we fly at 0400."

"In near-whiteout, on fumes, past a wreck you won't talk about, to save people who aren't paying us anymore because Corrigan bought their contract out from under us this afternoon."

I put down the wrench. "Corrigan what?"

"Bought the resupply contract. Legally. Which means technically we're flying for free." She leaned against the fuselage, arms crossed, watching me with those gray eyes that had once read my whole soul across a mess hall in Fort Alder and found it wanting. "So tell me why, Jonah. And don't say miles."

The wind moaned through the hangar door. Somewhere up-valley, the glacier groaned — that deep tectonic complaint they make before they do something irreversible.

"Because they'll die," I said.

"That's true and insufficient."

"Because I left people to die once already, and I'm not doing it twice."

Mara went very still. "Once," she repeated. "You mean Alder Pass. Three years ago."

"I mean a lot of things," I said, and turned back to the engine, and she let me, because Mara Voss has always known exactly how much pressure a seam can take before it splits — and exactly when to stop applying it.

* * *

0400 arrived wearing a blindfold.

I won't pretend the takeoff was anything but ugly. Snow smoke off the strip, horizon gone, flying by the numbers and the seat of a very experienced pair of pants. Dez rode copilot running the lean mixture like a man defusing something; Mara sat behind us with the radio board, working the relay to Thule Station, her voice the only calm thing in the hemisphere.

The pass itself is a stone throat nine miles long with walls that funnel wind into something between weather and warfare. At the halfway mark the whiteout closed completely. No horizon. No valley floor. Just gray in every direction and the altimeter telling me things I had to believe on faith.

And then, dead ahead, ghosting out of the murk like a reproach: the black shoulder of Miller Ridge. My mentor's ridge. Eli Sandoval's ridge, where his tail fin still stuck out of the ice a half-mile east, pointing at the sky like an accusation that never melts.

Three years ago I'd flown this same corridor in weather just like this, with Eli on the radio from the strip below, telling me to turn back, telling me the pressure gradient was wrong, telling me — in the flat, patient voice he used for students he loved — *"Jonah, the mountain doesn't care how good you are. Turn around, son."*

And I'd said, "I've got it, Eli," because I was twenty-six and immortal, and I'd pushed through, and the turbulence over the ridge had taken the aircraft out of my hands for four seconds, and four seconds was enough. He'd scrambled a rescue party into the storm himself. He'd died doing it. His plane went down on the ridge searching for mine.

The report said weather. The report was kind. The report didn't know that the last thing Eli ever transmitted wasn't a mayday — it was *"I told him to turn."*

Nobody knew that but me. I'd kept it sealed behind the cockpit door for three years, along with everything else — including the fact that the woman I loved had waited at the strip that night, and I'd never once told her the truth either.

The plane bucked. Ice on the wings. Fuel gauge lying to me in a language I understood fluently.

And somewhere in that gray screaming nothing, with Dez white-knuckled beside me and Mara's voice threading through the static, I heard myself say, over the open channel, in a voice I barely recognized:

"Eli told me to turn. Three years ago. I ignored him. The crash was mine — all of it. He died looking for me because I wouldn't listen. That's why I stopped flying this pass. That's why I—" My voice cracked like lake ice. "That's why I never called you, Mara. You deserved the truth and I gave you distance instead."

Static. Long, terrible static. The plane shuddered through a downdraft and Dez grabbed the yoke with me and we hauled it level together, and through the headphones I heard Mara breathe once, slow, the way she does before she says something permanent.

"Jonah Brightwater," she said, "you listen to me. Eli Sandoval taught you to fly. He would be ashamed of exactly one thing tonight — and it isn't this confession. It's that it took you three years. Now fly us through, because the people at Thule Station are alive, and so are you, and both facts are yours to keep."

The cloud tore open ahead — a ragged seam of blue-black dawn, and beneath it, the glacier.

The ice bridge was calving. Great slabs the size of barns were shrugging off the face and thundering into the meltwater, and through the gap — narrow, shifting, insane — a corridor of survivable air opened and began closing even as I watched.

"Hold on," I said. "Going through."

I banked into the gap with maybe ninety seconds of window and thirty minutes of fuel, and Dez fed the engine its last honest gallons, and Mara called our heading to Thule Station in a voice that didn't shake, and we slipped through the collapsing throat of the pass like a thread through a needle held by a god who owed us one.

Behind us, the bridge finished coming down. Ahead, the lights of Thule Station burned small and gold through the last of the snow.

And on the open channel, quiet enough that only she could hear it, Mara said words I'll carry longer than any scar:

"There's something I need to tell you too, Jonah. About that night. About who else was waiting at the strip."

The wheels touched snow.
