## 4. What the Miles Return

I have heard a lot of engines in my life, but I had never heard one *beg* before.

That's what the Cessna was doing as it came through the calving window — that ninety-minute gap when the glacier finally quit groaning and held its breath, and the pass opened like a door someone remembered to unlock. The plane limped down the valley trailing a ribbon of smoke from the starboard cylinder, and every person on the ridge below the science station stopped talking at once, which for a group of glaciologists is roughly equivalent to a standing ovation.

Jonah brought it in on the packed snow strip with the delicacy of a man setting down something he wasn't sure he deserved to keep. The skids hissed, the tail swung, and then there was just silence and steam and six stranded scientists running across the ice with their parkas flapping like they'd all won something.

Dez climbed out first, gray-faced, and kissed the snow. Actually kissed it. I decided not to hold it against him.

Then Jonah stepped down, and for a long moment he didn't look at any of us. He looked back up the pass — back the way they'd come, through the whiteout, through the confession he'd made over the radio while I sat in the station listening to a man dismantle three years of silence one static-chewed sentence at a time.

*I ignored the turn,* he'd said. *He told me to turn and I didn't listen, because I thought I knew better. It was me. It was always me.*

I'd spent three years assuming the opposite. Funny how the truth sounds smaller than the lie you built around its absence.

"You're late," said Dr. Okafor, the station lead, shaking Jonah's hand hard enough to rattle his fillings. "We've been eating freeze-dried regret for two weeks."

"Traffic," Jonah said.

The scientists were loaded within the hour — instruments crated, samples sealed, one very indignant research cat in a carrier that Dez volunteered to babysit purely so he wouldn't have to make eye contact with anyone. The plane could carry four and mercy. We drew straws for who stayed behind with the emergency cache until the resupply flight, and I lost, which meant I got what I'd secretly wanted: another night in the pass, with the glacier ticking and settling around me like an old house.

Jonah stayed too. He didn't ask. He just started unloading gear, and when I raised an eyebrow at him he said, "Somebody has to keep you from naming the generator," and that was that.

We slept in shifts in the station bunkroom while the wind rearranged the world outside. Around two in the morning I heard him get up and stand at the window for a long time. I pretended to be asleep. Some vigils you're not invited to, but you can keep company with them anyway.

* * *

The rival expedition turned back the next morning, and I would be lying if I said I didn't enjoy it.

Halloran's crew came up the valley on snow machines, low on fuel and lower on excuses, having discovered that their sabotaged cache — our cache, the one they'd raided and then botched — had left them short exactly when the weather turned. They arrived at the station looking like men who'd been personally insulted by geography.

Halloran himself tried to spin it. "Conditions deteriorated faster than forecast," he told Okafor, all professionalism, while his people stood behind him radiating the specific misery of the thoroughly beaten.

"Funny," Okafor said mildly. "Forecast looked fine from here."

What actually finished them was the manifest. I'd photographed the cache before we flew out — every crate, every seal number — and Halloran's crew had been careless enough to leave their own markers on the crates they'd cracked open. Okafor forwarded everything to the expedition's funding board before Halloran's snow machines were even back over the divide. By evening there was a message waiting for him at the relay station, and by the look on his face when he read it, it contained the word *discontinued* in several expensive places.

He passed Jonah on his way out. Stopped. Looked at him for a moment with something that might have been respect if respect had been run through a wood chipper.

"You flew that pass in whiteout," Halloran said. "In a damaged aircraft. That's either the bravest thing I've ever seen or the stupidest."

"Yeah," Jonah said. "Took me about ten years to figure out which."

Halloran left. The glacier, indifferent to human drama, calved somewhere in the distance — a sound like the world clearing its throat.

* * *

The invoice came later, back at the hangar, and that's where the real fight happened.

Jonah set it on my desk himself. Printed, itemized, total circled in red pen like a man marking a debt he intended to pay in full. Triple rate, per the contract. Every mile accounted for.

"I shouldn't take it," he said. "But I'm not going to decide that for you. You earned it. You paid for it, one way or another." His jaw worked. "Mara. Take the money. Consider it — I don't know. Penance with a paper trail."

I looked at the invoice for a long time. Three years of numbers. Fuel and ice and old debts, all of it tallied up neat as anything, as if grief could be reconciled line by line.

Then I tore it in half.

Then I tore it in half again, and kept going, until there was a small blizzard of confetti on my desk and Jonah was staring at me like I'd lost my mind.

"Mara—"

"You want to pay penance?" I said. "Stay. Fix the hangar roof, because it leaks, and I've been putting it off for two years because I hate roofing. Teach Dez to stop flying by feel when he's tired. Sit down at some point — I don't care when, next week or next spring — and tell me the rest of it. Not the crash version. The whole version." I swept the paper scraps into my hand and dropped them in the stove, where they caught and curled. "Money ends things, Jonah. That's what money is *for*. I'm not done with this yet."

He stood there a while. Outside, the first real snow of the season had started coming down, fat and unhurried, the kind that means business.

"The whole version takes a long time," he said finally.

"We've got winter," I said.

* * *

We started on the roof, because of course we did. There is no problem so large that it can't be postponed in favor of a smaller one with visible progress.

Dez ran the ground crew and complained magnificently about it. The scientists sent a crate of thank-you supplies that included, inexplicably, a taxidermied ptarmigan wearing a tiny aviator cap, which Dez mounted above the tool bench and named Captain Feathers, and which I have decided is load-bearing emotionally.

Jonah worked like a man making up for lost seasons. He rehung the hangar doors, rebuilt the workbench, rewired the lights Dez had been surviving under by prayer. And slowly — the way ice gives way, not all at once but grain by grain — he started talking. Not the confession version, the radio version, wrung out of him mid-storm. The ordinary version. Small pieces, handed over between tasks.

His mentor's name was Elias. He liked bad coffee and worse puns. He used to say a pilot's job wasn't flying, it was *landing* — everything else was just getting there. Jonah told me this on the third day of roofing, passing shingles down without looking at me, voice steady except at the edges.

"He'd have hated how I lived after," Jonah said. "Three years of treating miles like they were the point."

"And now?"

He looked out at the valley, white and quiet, the pass already sealing itself shut for the season behind us.

"Now I'm trying to learn the actual job," he said.

We finished the roof the day the snow settled in for good. That evening we stood in the doorway of the repaired hangar, watching it come down over the Kestrel Range, and the light inside fell warm across the snow between our boots, and neither of us moved to break the silence first.

It was Dez who broke it, leaning out from the workbench with a piece of paper in his hand and an expression I couldn't read.

"So," he said. "Don't freak out. But there's a message on the relay from Alder Pass."

My stomach dropped. "The station? Is Okafor—"

"They're fine. Everybody's fine." Dez hesitated, glanced at Jonah, then held out the paper. "It's addressed to Jonah. Says it's from somebody named Brightwater. Says—" he squinted at the transcription, "—says she's been looking for him for three years, and she knows about the pass, and she'll be at the Kestrel relay when the lines open in spring."

Jonah took the paper. Read it once. Read it again.

Outside, the snow kept falling, patient as anything, covering every trail leading out of the valley — and for the first time since I'd known him, Jonah Brightwater didn't look like a man planning his escape.

"Spring," he said quietly. "Okay. Spring."

And then, because some habits are worth keeping: "Somebody should probably teach me how to land."
