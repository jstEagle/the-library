## 1. The Weight of Silence

The trouble with small towns, Theo Nakamura had learned, was that they remembered everything except what you wanted them to forget.

Kestrel's Landing sat at the ragged end of the road, where the highway gave up and the Serrano River took over. Four hundred souls, two bars, one general store, and a sawmill that ran on spite and diesel. Nobody here had followed the trials. Nobody here knew her name meant anything south of the border, where it had been printed in three languages beside the word *negligent*. To Kestrel's Landing she was just the quiet woman who fixed fences and cleared deadfall for the timber company, who paid cash, drank alone, and never talked about the mountains.

That was the arrangement. She'd built it plank by plank, like a bridge over bad ground, and every morning she checked it for rot.

She was checking it now, in her own way—standing at the window of her rented cabin with coffee going cold in her hand, watching fog peel off the river. Six-forty in the morning. The sawmill hadn't started up yet. In that gap between dark and noise, the valley looked almost like the Vale had looked: green walls, white water, the smell of cold stone and spruce.

Almost. The Vale had never smelled like diesel.

She set the mug down before her hands could do anything stupid with it, like throw it through the glass.

*Five years.* Five years since the collapse, since the rescue helicopters, since the men in suits who'd stood in the courtroom and said *the guide led them into an unstable zone against protocol* while their own geologists' reports sat sealed in a filing cabinet two thousand kilometers away. Five years since she'd stopped being Theo Nakamura, mountain guide, and become Theo Nakamura, cautionary tale.

The cabin was small enough that tidiness was survival. Axe by the door, boots oiled, pack hung square on its peg. Everything squared away, everything accounted for. A life you could inventory in thirty seconds. She'd told herself once that this was discipline. Lately she suspected it was just another word for hiding, said with better posture.

Outside, frost furred the fence rails. She pulled on her gloves and went to work, because work was the only prayer she still believed in.

* * *

The trail crew was four men and her, cutting winter blowdown off the access roads above the mill. Foreman was a leathery old Swede named Andersson who'd hired her without questions because the timber company paid him per kilometer cleared and he didn't care if his best choker-setter used to have a famous name. That was the other thing about Kestrel's Landing: nobody asked. Asking was a form of caring, and caring cost money.

They worked the east spur all morning, bucking a spruce the size of a church that had come down across the cut. Theo ran the saw, the bar screaming through wet wood, chips flying like something trying to escape. It was good work. Honest work. Work that ended when the sun went down and left nothing behind but a clear road and tired arms.

Except her arms weren't tired enough today. That was the problem. Some days the body burned clean and the mind went with it, and some days the mind stayed behind, pacing, scratching at the walls. Today it kept dragging out the same footage it always did: the sound the mountain made—not a roar, not a crack, but a *sigh*, deep and mineral, right before the slope let go. The way Piñero had looked at her from the rope team, half a second of pure trust, right before the world turned to gravel.

Four climbers. Two guides. One survivor, and the survivor wasn't her.

"Hey." Andersson's voice cut through. "You're drifting."

"I'm cutting."

"You're cutting and drifting. Saw's wandering." He nodded at the kerf, which had gone crooked as a snake track. "Lunch."

They ate on the tailgate, backs against the truck, thermos coffee and bread and tinned fish. The younger crew members talked about elk tags and a woman in Port Calloway and whether the mill would make it to spring. Theo ate and listened and said nothing, which they were used to. But halfway through, Andersson said, quietly, so only she could hear:

"Stranger came through the store yesterday asking after you."

The bread turned to paste in her mouth. "Asking what?"

"Name. Where you lived. Whether you'd been here long." He shrugged, eyes on the treeline. "I told him we got plenty of people and no need for more. Figured you'd want to know."

"What did he look like?"

"Clean," Andersson said. "That's the thing. Boots too clean for around here. Coat too new. Like he'd stepped out of a catalog." He glanced sideways at her, and for once the old man's face held something other than indifference. "Trouble?"

"No," Theo said. "No trouble."

He nodded slowly, unconvinced, and let it go. But the rest of the day the words sat in her chest like swallowed stones. *Boots too clean.* Men in clean boots didn't wander into Kestrel's Landing by accident. Men in clean boots came looking, and in her experience—and hers was extensive—they came looking for things that were buried.

She finished the spur line, racked the saw, washed her hands in the creek until they ached from the cold. All the while the mountain in her head kept sighing.

* * *

Her cabin stood a kilometer out of town, down a track that turned to mud in rain and ruts in frost, past a stand of alders that hid it from the road. She'd chosen it for exactly that. She walked it now in the last gray light, and everything was as she'd left it—door shut, no footprints in the mud but her own from morning, woodpile stacked, axe in place.

Everything was fine until it wasn't.

There was a smell. Faint, wrong: cigarette smoke, expensive, the kind that came in slim silver boxes instead of crumpled packs. It hung in the alder shadows like a signature. And under her door, folded once, weighted by a pebble, a piece of heavy cream-colored paper that had no business existing within two hundred kilometers of her door.

She didn't touch it right away. She stood in the failing light and listened—really listened—the way she used to listen to slopes, reading the mountain's body language. Wind in the alders. River, far off. Mill, shutting down. Nothing else. No engine idling down the track, no footsteps retreating fast. Whoever it was had left the way professionals leave: long ago, unhurried, certain.

She picked up the paper with two fingers, carried it inside, and held it under the lamp before opening it.

One sentence, typed or printed, no signature:

**AMOS GRELL IS DYING AND HE SAYS YOU'RE THE ONLY ONE HE CAN TRUST.**

Below it, an address. The old mining camp at Corben Flats, forty kilometers upstream, a ghost place even by local standards.

Theo read it three times. Then she set it on the table, lit her stove, and put the kettle on, because her hands wanted a task and if she gave them nothing they'd start shaking.

Amos Grell. She knew the name the way you know a name from a newspaper you skimmed years ago—old prospector, one of the first stakers in the upper Serrano country, gone reclusive decades back. She'd never met him. She couldn't imagine why a dying man she'd never met would trust her with anything, unless—

Unless he knew. Unless he knew what she'd been, what she'd lost, what the courts had done to her. Unless somewhere in the high country above the reserve boundary, an old man had spent years watching the river and noticing things a dying prospector shouldn't be able to ignore.

Or unless it was bait. That possibility sat there too, patient and ugly. Clean boots. An address in a ghost town. A name she barely knew, wrapped around a promise she hadn't made. If someone wanted Theo Nakamura to run for the hills carrying something incriminating, this was how they'd write the invitation.

The kettle began to sing. She poured the water over the grounds and watched the bloom rise and fall, and thought about the arrangement—the planked bridge, the invisible life, thirty seconds to inventory. She could burn the note. By tomorrow it would be ash in the stove and the stranger in clean boots would be a story Andersson told badly, and she could go on fixing fences until the fences outlived her.

But the mountain in her head was sighing again. And underneath the fear, in the place she tried hardest not to look at, something older and worse was stirring: the part of her that had spent five years waiting for exactly this. A reason. A summons. A chance to walk back upriver toward the thing that ruined her and find out, once and for all, whether the fault had been in the mountain—or in the men who owned it.

She drank the coffee standing up. Then she took down the pack from its peg, checked the contents by lamplight—rope, rack, first aid, the emergency kit she'd never stopped maintaining even though there was nothing left to climb—and hated herself a little for how ready it all was.

Forty kilometers upstream. A dying stranger. A night of frost coming down off the divide.

Theo banked the stove, shouldered the pack, and stepped out into the dark, leaving the door unlocked behind her—for the first time in five years, she found she didn't much care what the town thought of that, or what might be waiting when she came back.

The track north was black and frozen hard. She walked it alone, and the river ran beside her the whole way, saying nothing, keeping whatever it kept.
