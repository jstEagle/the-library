## 2. A Map for a Promise

The knock came at eleven, which was the first wrong thing. Nobody knocked on Theo's door at eleven at night in Kestrel's Landing unless they were drunk, lost, or dying.

She'd been awake anyway. She was always awake in the hours when the mountain silence pressed hardest against the walls of her rented room above the gear shop—awake and listening for a sound that hadn't come in five years: the deep groan of rock letting go. She kept the lamp off and sat by the window with her boots on, because a guide who couldn't sleep still knew how to be ready.

The knock came again. Softer this time. Almost apologetic.

Theo opened the door on an old man propped against the frame like a coat hung on a nail. He was soaked through despite the dry night, sweat rather than rain, his gray beard plastered to his jaw. His eyes had the glassy shine she recognized from high-altitude rescues—the shine of a body running out of arguments.

"You're Nakamura," he said. Not a question.

"Wrong door."

"Theo Nakamura. Vale expedition, five years back. I saw you work." He swallowed, and the effort of it seemed to cost him something vital. "Saw you turn back for them."

Her hand tightened on the door edge until the wood bit her palm. "You've got me confused with someone else. That guide is gone."

"She's standing right here," the old man said, "not answering her door fast enough to keep me off your floor."

He was right about that much. His knees buckled as he spoke, and Theo caught him under the arms more from reflex than choice—muscle memory from a hundred carries down scree slopes—and then she was lowering sixty kilograms of dying prospector onto her narrow bed, and whatever careful invisibility she'd built in Kestrel's Landing was coming apart around her like wet paper.

"I'll get Doc Ferro," she said.

"You'll sit down," he said, "and you'll listen. Ferro can't fix what's wrong with me. Twenty years of river silt in my lungs and something growing in there that has no business being alive." He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that Theo stood over him with her fists clenched, hating herself for wanting to help and hating herself more for being afraid to.

When it passed, he fumbled inside his coat and brought out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord. He held it against his chest the way other men held children or Bibles.

"Amos Grell," he said. "Prospector. River man. And before you say it—I know what I am asking. I'm not asking you to like me. I'm asking you to carry something."

"I don't carry things for strangers."

"You carried strangers," Amos said. "Carried three of them out of the Vale on your back, and the country called it abandonment. So we're both people the record got wrong." He watched her face while he said it, and Theo understood that he'd done his research, that he'd come to her deliberately, that this whole night had been aimed at her like an arrow loosed from far away.

"Who told you where I live?"

"A dead man told me. Man who used to run supply up the Serrano before the reserve closed. He said if I ever needed someone who could read water and keep her mouth shut, find the woman in Kestrel's Landing who won't look anyone in the eye." He smiled without much humor. "Took me two years to work up the nerve. Now I'm out of nerve and everything else."

Theo sat in the chair by the desk because her legs told her to. She told herself she was only waiting for him to die so she could call Ferro and be done with it. She told herself a lot of things that night.

* * *

The oilcloth came open under his shaking hands, and inside was a map.

Not a survey map, not anything printed. Hand-drawn on waterproof stock gone soft as cloth with handling, inked in different colors from different years—you could see the decades in it, the way you could read tree rings. The Serrano River ran down its length in blue, from the coast landing all the way into the closed reserve, past the checkpoints, past the falls, up through canyons marked with small careful notes in a cramped, patient hand: *ledge portage left. cold spring, good water. bear sign, autumn.* And at the top of the sheet, where every other map Theo had ever seen simply stopped—at the boundary line, at the closure, at the place where the world had been declared off-limits—this map kept going.

It traced the river to its source.

Nobody mapped the source. The headwaters sat inside the Vale exclusion zone, sealed since the disaster, since the collapse that had killed four climbers and ended Theo's life as thoroughly as any avalanche. The official surveys showed the upper drainage as unstable, contaminated, uninteresting. This map showed side channels, an old mining road, a canyon the ink called *Bones*, and at the very top, drawn in fresher ink than the rest, a small black rectangle with a single word beside it:

*adit.*

"What am I looking at?" Theo asked, though she already knew, and knowing made her skin crawl.

"The truth," Amos said. "Or the road to it. Same thing, up there." He tapped the little black rectangle with one yellowed fingernail. "I spent thirty years working that river. Before Halvorsen bought the claims, before they closed the valley and wrote their story. I was in the upper canyon the summer it happened. I saw what they were doing, and I kept my mouth shut, because they paid me to keep it shut, and I had a daughter to feed." His voice cracked on *daughter* and he covered it with a cough. "I've been paying that debt ever since. In silence. You know about silence."

"I know about silence."

"This—" he pressed the map flat between them on the bed, smoothing it with both palms like a man soothing a horse, "—is the last copy. There were others. Halvorsen's people have been buying them, burning them, following the old river men into their graves faster than nature intended. Mine's the last one that shows the adit. The sealed entrance. What's behind it."

"And what's behind it?"

"Proof," Amos said simply. "That the collapse wasn't geology. That it was engineered—charges set to fail the slope, to scare the claims' minority holders into selling cheap, to force the disaster that let one company own the whole drainage. Four people died so some men in clean boots could get rich." His eyes fixed on hers, and beneath the fever-glass there was iron. "I know because I helped carry the wire up the slope. I was young and broke and I told myself it was just seismic testing. Thirty years, Theo. Thirty years I've known."

The room felt very small. Outside, the river that ran through Kestrel's Landing muttered over its stones, indifferent, carrying everything downstream.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why not the regulators, the press, anyone with standing? Why a disgraced guide in a border town?"

"Because regulators get bought and press gets buried, but a promise—" he tapped his chest, twice, hard, "—a promise lives in a person. And because you of all people know what it costs when a lie stands and the truth gets blamed. Your name is mud in two countries. Mine's nothing at all. Between us we've got nothing left to lose, and that's the only currency Halvorsen can't buy."

"I'm not a courier. I'm not a hero. I do trail maintenance and I keep my head down."

"Yes," Amos said. "I noticed the head down. It looks exhausting."

Theo almost laughed. It came out wrong, somewhere between a breath and a wound.

* * *

He talked until nearly two in the morning, in pieces, the way dying men talk—sentences like rocks crossing a stream, gaps between them widening. He told her about the daughter.

"Mara," he said, and saying the name seemed to steady him and gut him at once. "Mara Grell. She's in Port Calloway. Works the harbor—charts, maps, that kind of thing. A cartographer." A ghost of pride moved through the ruin of his face. "Never taught her a thing about maps. Didn't need to. Blood."

"You haven't seen her?"

"Fifteen years." He looked at his hands. "I wrote letters I never sent. Told myself I'd go when I had something worth bringing. Then I had something worth bringing and I was too sick to make the coast, and too ashamed besides." He met her eyes. "A man can be brave in a canyon and a coward at a mailbox. Don't let anyone tell you different."

"So deliver it yourself," Theo said, and heard the cruelty in it even as she said it, and didn't take it back. "You found me. You can find a bus."

"I'm not going to see dawn," Amos said, with the terrible calm of a man who had argued this out with himself long ago and lost. "And even if I did, Halvorsen's people are watching the coast road. They've been watching me for a month. I came upriver instead, through the backcountry, because I figured they'd expect a dying man to run toward his daughter, not away from her." He smiled faintly. "Running away from her. That's been my whole life. One last trip."

"Then give it to the police. Give it to a journalist."

"They'd sit on it for a decade while lawyers chewed it. Or it would disappear, like the other copies disappeared, like the witnesses disappeared." His voice dropped. "This needs to travel by hand. By someone the river trusts. By someone with a reason to hate Halvorsen bad enough to see it through and careful enough to stay alive doing it." He pushed the map across the blanket toward her. "I'm not asking you to expose anything. I'm not asking you to go up the Serrano, God knows I'm not—that valley's taken enough from you. I'm asking you to put this in my daughter's hands. Port Calloway. Ten days downriver. That's all. What she does with it after is hers."

"That's not all," Theo said quietly. "Nothing with Halvorsen is 'all.' If they're killing old prospectors over this map, they'll kill whoever's carrying it."

"Yes," Amos agreed. "Probably."

The honesty of it undid her more than pleading would have. She stood and walked to the window and looked out at the black bulk of the mountains, the ones she'd hidden from for five years, the ones that knew everything about her and had never once lied on her behalf.

"If I say no," she asked the glass, "what happens to it?"

"It goes in the stove, and the last proof dies with me, and Mara grows old thinking her father ran out on her, and Halvorsen keeps its clean story." He paused. "Or you say yes, and maybe somebody finally gets to be innocent. Maybe even you."

"I stopped believing in innocent."

"I noticed that too," he said. "It's why I picked you. Innocent people drop things when it gets hard. Guilty people hold on."

* * *

He made her swear it properly, because he was an old-fashioned man and dying men get to be old-fashioned. No handshake. He wanted words, spoken aloud, witnessed by the lamp and the river sound and whatever else had gathered in the room.

"Say it," he told her. "Say you'll carry the map to Mara Grell of Port Calloway, into her hands, past anyone who tries to stop you."

"Amos—"

"Say it. I've got maybe an hour of being alive left, and I'd rather spend it hearing an oath than a eulogy."

Theo looked at the map. At the thin blue line of the Serrano climbing into the closed country, into the valley that had eaten her name and spat it out. She thought about the careful life she'd built here—small, silent, survivable. She thought about how it had never once felt like living.

Every instinct she had left said walk away. Every instinct she'd buried five years ago said take the map, because taking the map was what guides did: you carried people through. Even dead ones. Especially dead ones.

"I'll carry it," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, rusted. "To Mara Grell. Into her hands. Past anyone who tries to stop me."

"Witnessed," Amos said, to the room, to the dark, to whatever listened. He sagged back against the pillow, and something in his face loosened, the iron going out of it, leaving just an old tired man who had finally set down a weight. "Thank you," he whispered. "Thirty years. Thank you."

He slept after that, or something near it, and Theo sat in the chair and watched the shallow bellows-work of his breathing and did not sleep at all. Around four she wrapped the map back in its oilcloth and stowed it inside her pack, beneath the flint and the spare socks, in the pocket closest to the frame of the bag, where a load rides best.

Around five, the breathing changed.

She was holding his wrist when it stopped—not because he needed holding, but because she did. His pulse went from a tap to a flutter to nothing, unhurried, like water finding its level. Outside, the sky over the eastern ridge had gone the gray of old pewter, and the first light came down the street and lay across the floor of her rented room, touching the boots of a dead stranger, and Theo Nakamura sat very still in the chair and grieved for a man she'd known six hours, and understood that grief was the smallest part of what she was feeling.

The larger part was fear. The part after that, the one she refused to look at directly yet, felt almost like relief.

She closed his eyes. She took the oilcloth bundle from her pack and held it a moment, weighing it—less than half a kilogram of cloth and ink, and heavy as a body on her shoulders.

Downstairs, the gear shop owner's truck rattled past on early errands. Somewhere in town, a phone would soon ring, or wouldn't; someone would come asking questions about an old man no one had seen arrive. She had perhaps a day, maybe less, before the men in clean boots started making inquiries.

Ten days downriver, a daughter who believed her father had abandoned her was waking up in Port Calloway, unaware that her inheritance was already in motion.

Theo laced her boots tight, shouldered the pack, and went to find out how much a lie of silence costs—and who else in Kestrel's Landing might be desperate enough to help her pay it.
