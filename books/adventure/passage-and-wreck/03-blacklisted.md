## 3. Blacklisted

Theo woke before dawn in the loft above the boathouse, as she always did, and for a moment she didn't know which year it was. That was the trick of Kestrel's Landing—the fog came off the water and erased everything, and you could believe, briefly, that the world had started five years ago with you already in it. Then the dream finished unspooling behind her eyes: the sound of the mountain letting go. The long white roar. The radio crackling a name she couldn't answer.

She lay still and let the present reassemble itself around her. Rain on the tin roof. The smell of diesel from the marina below. Her hands, scarred and steady, folded on the blanket like someone else's hands.

Five years. Long enough that the courts had stopped writing to her. Not long enough that the mountain had stopped writing to her every single night.

* * *

The Vale had been the crown jewel of the Cordillera Serrana's northern face—a hanging valley of blue ice and granite, threaded by the young Serrano River, closed to everyone but permitted expeditions. Theo had been thirty-one, the youngest lead guide ever certified for the high routes, and the Halvorsen Extraction survey team had been her ninth client group of the season.

She remembered them by their boots. That was the detail that came back first, always, before anything else—before the faces, before the names. Nine pairs of brand-new synthetic mountaineering boots, still stiff, still squeaking on the glacier ice, worn by men who had never spent a night above the treeline in their lives. They'd come up the valley in two helicopters under a mineral exploration permit, and they'd treated the mountain like a property they were already measuring for sale.

Their site chief was a compact man with a lawyer's calm named Voss. He'd shaken her hand at base camp and said, "Ms. Nakamura, we'll need your route assessment by Thursday," as if the mountain were a form to be filled out.

She should have refused the contract. She knew that now. But the season had been thin, her bank account thinner, and a Halvorsen permit meant triple rate and a helicopter resupply. Every guide in the range would have taken it. Every guide did take it, sooner or later. That was how they got you—not with corruption, just with arithmetic.

The survey was supposed to be four days: seismic lines along the lower glacier, core samples near the old adit above the falls, nothing technical. Theo laid the route herself, walked it twice, flagged every crevasse bridge with wands. She filed her hazard assessment with the reserve office and kept a copy. She did everything right. She would spend five years learning that doing everything right was worth exactly nothing when the wrong people needed someone to blame.

* * *

On the third day, the weather turned.

It wasn't unusual. The Serrana made its own weather; every local guide carried that knowledge like a second pulse. What was unusual was the survey team's insistence on pushing to the upper sample sites ahead of the front, because their equipment window was closing and the helicopters wouldn't come back for ten days.

"We can wait out the front at Camp 2," Theo told Voss that morning, standing over the satellite forecast printout, rain hammering the cook tent. "Twenty hours, maybe less. We move after."

Voss studied the printout without touching it. "Our contract specifies completion by Friday."

"Your contract doesn't specify that anyone comes home alive. Mine does." She tapped the map. "There's a slope above the adit approach that's loading fast. I've watched it all week. You want my professional assessment? We don't go up there in a storm."

Something moved behind his eyes—she'd thought at the time it was reluctance, a man caught between pressure from above and sense from below. Later she understood it had been neither. It had been patience.

"Then give me your assessment in writing," he said mildly. "Note your objection. And note that we're proceeding."

"I won't proceed. I'll pull my clients' gear and sit it out."

"Ms. Nakamura." He smiled then, small and reasonable, and she would see that smile again in her dreams for years. "You're welcome to sit out wherever you like. But our permit lists you as route lead. If you withdraw, we're required to report it, and your certification board takes a dim view of guides who abandon contracted parties in the field. I'd hate for that to end up in your file."

She was twenty minutes into arguing with him when the first of the survey techs started packing the sample kits anyway, and she understood she'd lost. Not the argument—the argument she'd won, on paper, in writing, in triplicate. She'd lost the thing arguments are supposed to protect. They went up. All nine of them plus two of her junior guides, because the juniors wouldn't defy a client either, not with her standing right there.

She went too. Of course she went. She put herself at the front of the rope line where the risk concentrated, because that was what lead guides did, and because some stubborn animal part of her believed that if she was watching the slope closely enough, nothing could happen.

At 14:40, with the storm's leading edge swallowing the upper valley, the slope above the adit approach let go.

Not a sluff. Not a wind slab. The whole mountainside, a hundred thousand tons of rock and old snow, moving like something alive and angry. She heard it before she saw it—a deep structural groan, the sound of a building being torn open—and she had time to scream the order, *rope teams off the path, down-slope, GO*, and then the world was white noise and impact and the terrible weight of the air itself changing.

When it ended, six people were gone. Two Halvorsen techs. Three of the survey crew. And Ren Okafor, her junior guide, twenty-four years old, who had taken a client's place on the second rope team because the man's boots were giving him blisters and Ren had laughed and said he'd carry the spare pair himself.

They found Ren three days later, when the weather cleared. Theo identified him. She would not describe that, even to herself, except to say that afterward she never again wore the color of his jacket.

* * *

The inquiry began eleven days later, in a rented conference room in the valley town, and Theo learned the first lesson of institutional grief: the paperwork starts before the mourning does.

Halvorsen sent lawyers. The reserve office sent administrators. There were fourteen people in the room and one of them was Theo, alone, with a photocopied hazard assessment and a set of field notes in a plastic bag because her tent had been destroyed in the secondary slide.

"Walk us through your decision to ascend on the third day," the lead investigator said.

"That wasn't my decision. I objected in writing. Here's the objection."

"Mr. Voss has provided a signed statement indicating that no such objection was communicated to him."

The room tilted slightly. She remembered gripping the edge of the table. "It's dated. It's countersigned by my second guide. It was handed to him at 06:15 on day three."

Voss, seated across the table with his lawyer's calm intact, said gently: "I have no record of receiving any document. Ms. Nakamura was under significant stress following the incident. I understand her confusion."

Her own certification board—her people, the guild she'd bled for since she was nineteen—reviewed the case a month later and suspended her license pending investigation. The suspension became a revocation. The revocation traveled. The Cordillera Serrana sat across a border from two countries that shared the watershed, and both countries' courts took an interest, and Halvorsen's legal team made sure the interest was fed. Negligent route selection. Failure to monitor slope conditions. Unauthorized deviation from protocol. Each charge was technically arguable, which was the genius of it; none of them was true, which was irrelevant. Truth required evidence, and the evidence—the countersigned objection, the original hazard filing, the seismic logs showing the slope had been destabilized days before the ascent by unreported blasting at the adit—existed only in places Halvorsen controlled.

The blasting records were sealed. The seismic logs were sealed. Her countersigned objection vanished from the reserve office files between the preliminary hearing and the final one; the junior clerk who'd last handled it left the region abruptly and could not be located.

Two years of hearings. Two countries. Her savings gone to lawyers who promised much and delivered a settlement offer instead: sign the liability acknowledgment, accept the finding of gross negligence, and Halvorsen would not pursue damages against her personally. Refuse, and they would pursue her into the ground.

Ren's mother stood outside the courthouse on the last day, in the rain, and would not look at her. Theo didn't blame her. Someone had to be guilty. The alternative—that a company had traded six lives for a schedule and buried the proof under sealed documents—required a kind of believing that grief couldn't afford.

So Theo signed nothing, lost everything, and disappeared.

* * *

Kestrel's Landing had found her the way all hiding places find people: through a friend of a friend who asked no questions. Dez had run her downriver once, years ago, back when Dez still had a boat and a reputation, and when Theo showed up at the landing with a duffel bag and cash, Dez had looked at her for a long moment and said, "Loft's dry. Rent's whatever trail work you do. Don't tell me your business and I won't have any."

That had been three years ago. In that time Theo had become very good at being no one. She cleared deadfall. She patched the boardwalk. She taught a few kids from town to read water, quietly, off the books, and pretended not to love it. She kept her head down so consistently that the motion had worn a groove in her, and the groove had begun to feel like a personality.

But here was the thing she admitted to no one, the thing that surfaced only in the hour before dawn when the fog erased the world: she had never stopped believing she was right.

Not innocent—innocence was for children and juries. Right. The slope had been failing before her party ever touched it. She'd seen the signs and said so, in writing, and the signs had been true, and somewhere in a sealed archive there were numbers that proved the mountain had been wounded on purpose or through criminal carelessness before it ever killed anyone. Six people had died inside a lie, and she was the only person alive who knew the shape of it, and she had spent five years learning to live with that knowledge the way you learn to sleep on a bad mattress—by not moving.

Some nights she almost managed it.

* * *

This morning the fog burned off early, and the light came hard across the water, and Theo stood on the boardwalk with a cup of instant coffee watching a stranger's boat come up the channel.

It was the wrong kind of boat for Kestrel's Landing—too clean, too new, a charter rig with a covered console, the kind you rented in Port Calloway by the day. It moved with the unhurried confidence of someone reading a chart rather than guessing at the water. Theo set down her coffee and picked up the splitting maul, not because she had wood to split but because her hands wanted a job.

The boat slowed at the public dock. A man stepped out, middle-aged, gray jacket, and paused to look up the street with the particular attention of someone confirming an address. Then he started walking toward the boathouse, and his boots on the boards made a sound Theo hadn't heard in five years and recognized instantly anyway: the crisp, expensive, city sound of footwear that had never touched a trail.

He stopped ten feet away and smiled the way Voss had smiled, small and reasonable.

"Theo Nakamura?" he said. "Sorry to bother you. I'm looking for a prospector named Amos Grell. Word is you're the person who knows this stretch of river best."

Behind her ribs, the old groove shifted—the groove she'd worn into herself, the shape of being no one—and something in it cracked, just slightly, like ice going out on the Serrano in spring.

"Grell," she repeated, and heard her own voice go flat and careful. "Never heard of him."

The man's smile widened by a millimeter. "That's funny," he said. "Because he's heard a lot about you."
