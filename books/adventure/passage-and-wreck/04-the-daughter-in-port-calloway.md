## 4. The Daughter in Port Calloway

The tide tables didn't lie, but people did. That was the first thing Mara Grell had learned at her father's knee, back when his knees were something she could sit on and his voice was something she could trust: *the water keeps honest time, kiddo. It's everything else that runs on lies.*

She was thinking about that now, twenty-two years later, hunched over a drafting table in the survey office above the Port Calloway chandlery, correcting a chart of the outer harbor that some junior hydrographer had botched. The shoal off Gull Point had migrated forty meters east since the last survey. Sand did that. Water did that. Fathers did that too—drifted, shifted, went where you couldn't follow—and Mara had built an entire life on the principle that if you measured honestly enough, nothing could sneak up on you.

The letter had been sitting in her jacket pocket for three days.

She hadn't opened it. She'd told herself she was waiting for a quiet moment, but Port Calloway never offered quiet moments, and she'd stopped pretending around day two. The envelope was heavy stock, postmarked from Kestrel's Landing—a river town two hundred miles inland, a place Mara knew only as a smudge of ink on regional charts, up past the head of navigation where the Serrano turned to whitewater and cartographers gave up and drew wavy lines.

Kestrel's Landing. Her father's last known address, according to the pension office that had finally tracked him down after eleven years of silence, long enough after the fact that the news wasn't a reunion but an obituary-in-advance: *Amos Grell, residing Kestrel's Landing, health failing.* She'd written him one letter after that. One. She'd gotten no reply, and she'd decided that was answer enough, and she'd filed the whole man away in the same drawer as her mother's death and the empty Christmases and the birthday when she turned nine and sat on the porch steps until dark with a harbor pilot's cap on her head because he'd promised he'd take her out on the *Meridian* and teach her to read the water.

He'd promised. The water kept honest time. Amos Grell did not.

"Mara." Tamsin, the office manager, leaned into the loft with a clipboard and the expression of a woman delivering bad news she'd rehearsed. "There's someone downstairs asking for you."

"I'm not taking clients today. The Gull Point revision is due Friday."

"It's not a client." Tamsin hesitated. "Woman. Says she has something of your father's."

The words landed like a dropped sounding weight—straight down, no drift. Mara kept her eyes on the chart, on the little eastward creep of the shoal, and made her voice come out flat. "My father's dead."

"She knows. Says she knew him. Says it couldn't wait for a letter."

*It already waited for a letter,* Mara almost said. *It's been waiting eleven years.*

She set down her pen. Ink was still wet on the shoal line; she'd have to redo the stroke later, and she found herself absurdly angry about that, about the small ruined thing, because it was easier to be angry at ink than at whatever was standing in the chandlery below with her father's name in its mouth.

"Tell her I'm busy," Mara said.

Tamsin didn't move. "She said you'd say that. She said—" Tamsin consulted a scrap of paper, frowning, "—she said to tell you the tide doesn't wait either, but it always comes back. Does that mean anything?"

It meant everything. It meant her father used to say it every single evening of her childhood, standing at the window of their apartment over the old pilot house, watching the harbor breathe. It meant this stranger had sat across from Amos Grell while he talked about his daughter, which meant he'd talked about his daughter, which meant—

Mara pulled the letter from her pocket and tore it open so fast she nicked her thumb on the flap.

Three pages, in a hand she didn't recognize—small, disciplined print, the writing of someone who'd learned to make letters under pressure. Mara read it standing up, then sitting down, then standing again without any memory of moving.

*Ms. Grell—I'm writing because I made a promise to your father, and I intend to keep it. His name was Amos. He died three days ago in Kestrel's Landing. He asked me to bring you something of his—not a thing I can mail, and not a thing I'll describe in a letter. I know what you're thinking, because I'd think it too: stranger, story, scam. So here's what I can give you in ink. He talked about you constantly. He said you became a mapmaker, and he said it the way men say things they're proud of and ashamed they weren't there for. He called you "my Mara" even at the end, when most of his words were gone. I'll be in Port Calloway through Sunday. If you don't come, I'll leave what he gave me with the harbormaster's office, under your name, and consider my promise kept as best as distance allows. —T. Nakamura*

Mara read it twice more. Then a third time, hunting for the flaw, the tell, the seam where the con showed through. Scam artists knew things—they bought names and details cheap—but they didn't know *my Mara*. They didn't know the tide line. They didn't know to say *proud of and ashamed*, which was exactly the doubled thing she'd spent two decades refusing to feel about him.

Her thumb was still bleeding slightly. She pressed it against the envelope, leaving a small rust-colored crescent, and thought: *he was dying and he was talking about me, and where was that talking when I was nineteen and calling every harbor office on the coast looking for him? Where was it when I turned twenty-five and finally stopped?*

"He talked about me," she said aloud, to the empty loft, and heard how young she sounded.

* * *

She didn't go down. That was the decision she made, and she made it the way she made all her hard decisions: by not making it, by letting the clock do it for her. By the time she got her coat on and got as far as the top of the stairs, forty minutes had passed, and the woman—the guide, the stranger, the *T. Nakamura*—was gone. Tamsin reported that she'd left no forwarding address, only a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, signed over to Mara at the harbormaster's, plus one sentence delivered with a level look: *"Tell her the door was open from her side too."*

"That's cryptic," Tamsin said.

"That's cruel," Mara said, and took the rest of the day off, which she had not done in four years.

The parcel sat on her kitchen table all evening while she cooked a dinner she didn't eat. Oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, heavier than it looked. She circled it twice like a gull deciding whether to land. Around ten she cut the cord, and inside, wrapped in a flannel shirt she recognized—God help her, she recognized it, faded green, elbow patches, the one he'd worn mending nets on the *Meridian*—was a leather roll, and inside the roll, a hand-drawn map.

Not a harbor chart. Not anything from her world of soundings and contour lines and honest decimal places. This was a river map, inked on waterproofed linen, the Serrano rendered in a draftsman's hand that shook in places toward the upper reaches. Headwaters. A fork marked in red. Notes in her father's cramped capitals along the margins: distances, hazards, a notation that read *SOURCE — PROOF — FOR MARA* with the last two words underlined twice, hard enough to tear the linen slightly.

Proof of what?

She spread it under the good lamp and worked it the way she worked any chart, and the work steadied her the way work always did. The lower river was accurate—she checked it against her own reference sheets, and it was *accurate*, better than the published surveys in places, which meant he'd run it recently or remembered it perfectly or both. The middle river carried annotations in a different mood: *avoid north bank after rain*, *company survey team here '19*, and once, pressed so hard the nib nearly went through: *THEY LIED ABOUT THE VALE*.

Everyone inland lied about the Vale. Everyone had lied about the Vale for five years—the collapse, the dead climbers, the guide whose name had been dragged through both countries' courts until it became shorthand for negligence. Mara had followed the trial from Calloway with the grim attention of someone who needed other people's disgraces to be legible. *See,* she'd told herself, reading about Theo Nakamura, the guide who'd led people into a death trap, *some people just abandon what they're responsible for. Some people are like that all the way down.*

Now a woman named Nakamura was standing in her office lobby holding her father's last words, and her father's map was accusing a corporation of lying, and the two facts were circling each other like weather fronts.

At midnight she poured a finger of rye she didn't want and sat with the map and let herself, finally, remember him properly—not the abandonment, the other archive, the one she kept locked: his hands teaching hers to hold a parallel rule. The way he'd smell of diesel and salt. How he'd wake her before dawn on her birthdays, just the two of them, and row out to watch the fishing fleet come through the narrows, and he'd name every boat, and she'd believed she was the luckiest child alive.

Then the year he stopped coming home. The year the mining money came sniffing around the coast and her mother cried in the kitchen and her father said things like *they can't buy the river* and *I have to see it through*, and then he was gone, and there were postcards for a while, and then there weren't, and her mother got sick, and died, and the pension office letter came eleven years late like everything else.

*I have to see it through.* She'd hated those words for twenty years. Sitting there at midnight with his handwriting under her lamp, she understood them for the first time—not as an excuse but as a sentence he'd been serving. Whatever he'd found upriver, whatever *PROOF* meant, he'd stayed away from her to keep something off her. Or to keep her off something. Either way, the door she'd assumed was locked from his side had maybe been locked from hers.

The rye went untouched. At 2 a.m. she wrote a reply to Nakamura's letter, four drafts, all of them starting variations of *You should know* and ending nowhere. At 3 a.m. she folded the map closed along its original creases, careful as handling a wound.

Sunday. The woman would be in town through Sunday. Today was Thursday.

Mara had two full days to decide whether to open a door she'd welded shut at nineteen, and she discovered she was furious about that too—furious at being handed the choice at all, furious that the timing was engineered so that refusal would mean losing him twice, furious that a stranger on her father's behalf had done in one afternoon what grief counselors and a patient ex-partner and two decades of harbor fog hadn't: made the abandonment feel less like a verdict and more like a mystery.

And Mara Grell did not leave mysteries unsolved. It was the family disease. It was why she owned three sets of parallel rules and a father-shaped hole and a map with two torn words on it.

*They lied about the Vale.*

Friday morning she called in sick, which she had also not done in four years, and took the ferry across the harbor to the maritime library, and pulled the microfilm on the Vale disaster, and started reading. The official record was thin in a way that professional her noticed immediately—thin the way charts are thin when someone's paid for them to be. Collapse, structural failure, regrettable, case closed, guide blamed. But the survey dates didn't line up. There was a Halvorsen Extraction assessment of the Vale dated eight months *after* the collapse that referenced bore samples taken *before* it. Small thing. The kind of small thing that isn't.

By Saturday night she had a folder. By Sunday morning she had a decision she still couldn't make, and a ferry schedule, and a woman named T. Nakamura who would be leaving Port Calloway on the evening boat, heading back upcountry, back toward the river, back toward the Vale.

Mara stood at her window watching the harbor run its honest tide and understood, with the clarity she usually reserved for shoal lines, that she was going to miss the meeting. Not because she chose to. Because fear dressed as prudence would get her to Sunday evening exactly as unready as she was now, and the door would close, and she'd spend another twenty years telling herself the timing was wrong.

Unless she didn't wait for Sunday.

She grabbed her coat. She grabbed the folder. She left the map on the table—no. She went back for the map. Of course she went back for the map.

The harbormaster's log listed T. Nakamura as lodging at the Anchorline Rooms on Cordage Street. Mara walked there fast, the folder under her arm and her heart running ahead of her the whole way, and climbed the outside stairs, and stood in front of door 6 with her hand raised to knock, and heard, through the thin wall, a voice inside—not Nakamura's, a man's, low and pleasant and wrong, the voice of a man asking questions he already knew the answers to:

"—the Grell estate, Ms. Nakamura. Just routine. The company takes an interest in its former contractors' effects. You understand how it is. People hold onto things that should have come home to us."

A pause. Then Nakamura's voice, dry as a chart margin: "I don't recall Mr. Grell working for any company."

"Everyone worked for someone, out there." A soft laugh. "We'll be in town through the week. Do travel safe."

Boots crossed the floor—clean boots, Mara would remember that detail later, boots that had never touched mud—and the door opened, and a man in a gray coat stepped out smiling, and Mara stepped aside with the instinct of a woman who'd spent her life staying off other people's charts.

He nodded to her, courteous, forgettable, gone down the stairs.

Through the open door of room 6, Theo Nakamura looked at her—at the folder under her arm, at the oilcloth-wrapped shape now visible in Mara's satchel, at her face, which Mara knew wore the Grell stubbornness like a flag—and said quietly:

"You shouldn't have come here."

"No," Mara agreed, stepping inside and closing the door behind her. "But I brought the map. And I've been reading about the Vale. So I think you'd better tell me why a mining company cares what my dead father left me."

Outside, down on Cordage Street, the man in the gray coat paused under the lamppost, took out a phone, and dialed a number that answered on the first ring.

"She's here," he said. "Both of them."
