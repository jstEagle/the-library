## 5. Men in Clean Boots

They came on a Tuesday, which Theo would remember later because Tuesdays were when she did the supply run, and the supply run was the one fixed point in a life she'd built entirely out of avoiding fixed points. She'd been gone an hour. Forty minutes up the valley road to Ferro's store, ten minutes back with a box of rope wax and two tins of coffee and the particular small pleasure of not talking to anyone beyond a nod at the counter.

When she came over the rise above her cabin, there was a truck parked in her yard.

Not a truck like anyone in Kestrel's Landing drove. The trucks here were twenty years old at least, rusted along the wheel wells, beds full of chain saws and fence posts and the salt-scoured debris of lives lived close to weather. This truck was white, spotless, new enough that the dealer sticker had probably come off last month. It sat in her yard the way a knife sits on a kitchen table—clean, out of place, waiting to be picked up.

Two men stood beside it looking at her woodpile.

Theo stopped at the edge of the tree line, half in shadow, and took stock the way she'd taught a hundred clients to take stock before she'd ever taught them to tie a figure-eight. Two men. Suits under shell jackets that still had the creases from their packaging. Boots—she looked at the boots and something cold slid down between her shoulder blades—boots that had never touched anything but pavement and carpet. One of them held a tablet against his forearm like a waiter holding a tray. The other was looking at her woodpile with the expression of a man cataloguing evidence.

Nobody drove up the valley road by accident. The road ended at her gate. And nobody in Kestrel's Landing wore boots like that.

She considered, for about three seconds, turning around and walking back down to Ferro's, buying a beer, letting them knock on a locked door and leave. But they'd already seen the cabin was occupied—the smoke, the woodpile stacked for winter, the laundry line. Invisibility wasn't hiding behind a door. It was controlling what people saw when they looked. And right now what they needed to see was a woman coming home from the store, mildly annoyed, nothing more.

She walked into the yard.

"Can I help you?"

The taller one turned first. He had the kind of face that had been sanded smooth by good dentistry and better lighting, forty or so, hair cut short in a way that said military once, corporate now. He smiled, and the smile arrived a half-second after his eyes finished measuring her.

"Afternoon, ma'am. Sorry to intrude." His voice was warm the way a heating vent is warm. "We're looking for someone. Amos Grell. Prospector. Older fellow, gray beard, walks with a limp."

The name landed in Theo's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She kept her face doing what faces do: mild confusion, faint rural impatience.

"Don't know him."

"He's been through this area," the man said. "We have reason to believe he passed through Kestrel's Landing recently. Possibly ill." A pause calibrated to invite confession. "His family's worried about him."

*His family,* Theo thought, *is a daughter in Port Calloway who hasn't heard from him in fifteen years, and you're not family, because family doesn't send men in clean boots.*

"Kestrel's Landing's got maybe sixty people," she said. "If an old prospector came through sick, Ferro would've mentioned it, and Ferro mentions everything."

"And you are?" The second man spoke for the first time. Shorter, broader, with the flat affect of someone whose job description didn't include smiling. He hadn't put the tablet away.

"Theo Nakamura. I do trail work." She shifted the box in her arms so the rope wax showed. "Seasonal. Mostly for the county."

"Nakamura." The tall one tried the name like a key in a lock. Something flickered behind his eyes—not recognition exactly, but the motion of a hand reaching toward a file. "That name's familiar."

"It's a common name."

"Not where I'm from." He let that sit, then extended a card between two fingers. HALVORSEN EXTRACTION GROUP. RESOURCE RECOVERY DIVISION. Below that, a name—**M. Voss, Site Operations**—and a phone number with a country code she didn't recognize. "We're conducting a survey of legacy claims in the upper Serrano drainage. Grell worked some of those claims decades ago. If he left anything behind—maps, field notes, samples—we're authorized to compensate fairly. Generously, even."

"Generously" hung in the air with a number attached to it, unspoken but enormous.

"I keep to the lower trails," Theo said. "I don't know anything about mining claims."

"Of course." Voss—because it had to be Voss; the card said so and the other man deferred to him in the small ways, the half-step back, the pause before speaking—smiled again. "Well. If he turns up, or if you hear anything. It would be a kindness to his family."

He said *family* again, and this time Theo heard the seam in it, the place where the word had been stitched over something else.

They got in the truck. It turned around in her yard with careful precision, tires barely marking the mud, and rolled back down the valley road. Neither of them waved. The shorter one watched her in the mirror until the trees took the road.

Theo stood in her yard for a long time, the box getting heavy in her arms, listening to the engine sound fade toward town.

Then she went inside, set the box down, and threw the bolt on a door that had never had a bolt before she installed it.

* * *

Amos Grell had died in her bed four nights ago, coughing blood into a rag while she held a cup of water he couldn't drink, and the map had been under the floorboard beneath the stove ever since, wrapped in oilcloth, and every hour since his death she had felt it the way you feel a splinter—small, specific, impossible to ignore.

She'd told herself it was paper. An old man's obsession, ink on hide, worth whatever a collector might pay and no more. She'd told herself she'd carry it to Port Calloway in a week or two, when the roads dried, mail the letter she'd already written, be done with it. A promise kept quietly. A debt paid to a stranger.

Now she pulled up the floorboard and unwrapped the oilcloth on her table and looked at the map with different eyes.

It was beautiful work, for all its age. Amos had drawn the Serrano from the reserve boundary to the headwaters in a surveyor's hand—elevations, rapids marked with his own shorthand, portage lines, springs, the ghost of a trail that hadn't existed officially in thirty years. And past the boundary, inside the Vale itself, the detail didn't thin the way a trespasser's map should. It thickened. He'd known the interior. He'd stood at the source.

And there, near the headwaters, in ink gone brown, a notation she'd read a dozen times without understanding:

*Adit sealed '09 — NOT natural. Ask H. why the tailings ran BEFORE the blast.*

Ask H. why the tailings ran before the blast.

H. Halvorsen.

Five years ago, Theo had stood in a courtroom in another country and listened to lawyers explain, patiently, kindly, with graphs, how a mountainside above the Vale had failed due to unforeseen geological conditions, and how the guide leading a party into the restricted zone had ignored warnings, and how the deaths of six people were therefore attributable to negligence—her negligence—and how Halvorsen Extraction, which had held dormant claims in the drainage, bore no operational responsibility whatsoever because its sites had been decommissioned years prior.

She had testified that the mountain had groaned for two days before it came down. That she'd reported it. That the monitoring station at the old Halvorsen access road had been dark, unmanned, its instruments either broken or switched off. Her testimony had been entered into the record and then dissolved in acid: no maintenance logs, no staff on duty, no records retained. A company that had left. A company that couldn't be blamed for a silence.

*Unforeseen geological conditions.*

An old prospector, dying in her bed, had drawn a map that said otherwise. Said the tailings ran before the blast. Said the mountain bled before it broke—which meant somebody had known the mountain was going to break, or had helped it along, and wanted the land condemned and emptied and cheap.

She sat back from the table. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside, something was moving very fast.

They weren't looking for a dead man's effects out of filial duty. They weren't even looking for mineral data, though the map had that too—Amos had marked three showings in the headwaters that any exploration geologist would weep over. They were looking for a piece of paper that contradicted five years of sworn statements, settlement agreements, court findings in two countries. A piece of paper that could reopen everything. That could turn a settled disaster back into a crime scene.

That was worth killing for. Not metaphorically. Men like Voss did not drive four hours in a clean truck to offer generous compensation for trivia. Somewhere upstream of Voss, someone had done the arithmetic: the cost of the map versus the cost of making sure the map never surfaced, and the second number had come back acceptable.

And they knew her name.

*That name's familiar.* Not suspicion yet. A thread. But threads get pulled, and Theo knew exactly what lay at the end of hers: a blacklisted guide with a documented grudge against Halvorsen, living off the grid in a border town, last seen—when? Ferro would tell them. Ferro would tell anyone who bought coffee and asked friendly questions. She'd taken Amos in; the whole landing probably knew it by tomorrow. Sick stranger, stayed at Nakamura's place, died there. Buried up behind the spruce line where the ground was soft.

They'd be back. Not today, maybe not this week. But they'd be back with the thread pulled taut, and next time the smile wouldn't bother arriving.

* * *

She spent the evening doing what she always did when fear came: she made lists. Fear was weather; procedure was shelter. Gear inventory. River levels—early season, cold, high but manageable if you hit the drops right. Distance to the reserve boundary: six days' hard travel by water, less if the melt cooperated. The Vale closed by order, patrolled by rangers who shot first at trespassers and filed reports later—or so the stories went, and the stories had kept everyone out for five years, which was presumably the point.

Running the river alone was suicide. Running it with strangers was only slightly less suicidal, but strangers had one advantage over solitude: they could hold a rope.

She thought about who she knew. That was a short list, and most of it was dead or fictional.

There was Dez Okafor, who piloted rafts on the tourist stretch below the falls until the night he ran a commercial trip into a strainer and drowned two clients—not his fault, the inquiry found eventually, a logjam shifted by flood—but *eventually* is a word that ruins people, and by the time the finding came down Dez had lost his license, his marriage, and his belief in being believed. He drank at the Anchor Room and talked to his boat like it was a horse. He knew the Serrano's lower canyons better than any living person. He also hated Theo, or had, once, publicly, in the days when her disgrace was fresh and his was beginning, two ruined reputations circling each other like dogs.

There was Ruth Adeyemi, who had been the reserve's staff biologist before the closure, who had spent five years filing freedom-of-information requests about water quality in the Vale and getting back pages so redacted they looked like modern art. Theo had met her twice, at hearings, on opposite sides of a courtroom aisle. Ruth believed the river was still contaminated. Ruth had no proof. Ruth had been saying so into a void for five years, and the void had eaten her career and most of her savings.

And there was the kid—Ivo—who'd started showing up around Ferro's store in the spring, all elbows and quick hands, thieving small and lying cheerfully, sleeping God knew where. Nobody's kid. Everybody's problem. Theo had caught him lifting a carabiner off her porch table in March and, instead of yelling, had made him rig a haul system to earn it back. He'd done it wrong, then right, then shown up the next week asking if there was more stuff he could earn. He was maybe sixteen. He was also, she'd noticed, the fastest learner she'd ever seen, and completely unafraid of things that should frighten him, which was either a gift or a diagnosis.

A raft pilot with nothing left to lose. A biologist with everything left to prove. A thief with no one watching him at all.

It was a terrible crew. It was possibly the only crew.

She wrote the names on the back of Amos's letter draft and looked at them for a long time. Then she looked at the map, at the brown-ink line running up into the Vale like a vein, at the words *ask H. why*, and understood that the decision had already been made somewhere below her conscious mind, the way a river decides its course long before the water arrives.

Because here was the thing she couldn't say in a courtroom five years ago and could say now, alone, to a lamp: she had been right. On the mountain, in the reports, in the testimony they shredded—she had been right, and the truth had a location, and the location was at the end of a dead man's ink line, and the company that destroyed her life was driving around the valley in a clean truck trying to make sure no one ever reached it.

Fear said: burn the map, mail the daughter an apology, disappear deeper.

But fear had had five years to make its case, and all it had produced was this cabin, this silence, this careful small life spent rehearsing invisibility. Amos Grell had crawled half a country to put that map in the hands of someone who might use it. He hadn't chosen a brave person. He'd chosen an available one. But available was about to become something else.

She wrapped the map back in its oilcloth. She didn't put it under the floorboard. She put it inside her pack, against the frame sheet, where it would ride against her spine.

Then she took a fresh page and began writing letters she'd deliver by hand, starting with a man who talked to his boat.

Outside, the wind came down the valley from the direction of the Serrano's source, and somewhere far upriver, in a canyon no one was supposed to enter, water moved over things that had waited five years to be found.

Three days, she decided. She'd give herself three days to build a crew.

Which meant she had three days before the men in clean boots came back—and this time, she intended to be gone.
