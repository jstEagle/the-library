## 6. The Crew Nobody Wants

I spent two days telling myself I could run the Serrano alone.

Two days of pacing the rented room above the chandlery, the map folded into a oilcloth sleeve under my mattress, Amos Grell's promise sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone. I laid the route out in my head: ninety miles of river from the old put-in at Cold Bar to the headwaters, three canyons, one gorge nobody had run since the reserve closed, and a portage through country where the trail had probably grown back into rumor. Solo. High water season. With Halvorsen's men already asking questions in town.

It was a beautiful fantasy and I knew it was a fantasy by the second morning, when I tried to lift a loaded dry bag onto a bench and my shoulder reminded me it had been dislocated twice and was not interested in a third act of heroics.

You can't run a river alone. That's the first thing they teach you, before knots, before reading water. The river doesn't care how good you are. It only cares how many hands you have when things go wrong, and things always go wrong.

So I needed a crew. And in Kestrel's Landing, the crew nobody wanted was the only crew on offer.

* * *

I found Dez Okafor where the washed-out always end up in a river town: at the boatyard, doing repairs nobody thanked him for, drinking coffee that had gone the color of the Serrano in spring.

He'd been the best raft pilot on the coast ten years ago. I'd watched him thread the Devil's Elbow on the Corrientes with a boat so loaded it was riding its own wake, and he'd done it grinning, calling the lines like a man reading a bedtime story. Then came the season he drank through, the flipped commercial trip on the Vashon, the client who didn't come up. The inquiry cleared him—no drugs, just grief he wouldn't talk about—but permits don't care about grief, and neither do outfitters. He'd been unlicensed so long the word "pilot" had worn off him like paint.

"Vasquez." He didn't look up from the hull he was sanding. "Heard you were in town. Heard you were keeping quiet."

"Word travels."

"Word's the only thing that travels fast around here." He blew dust off the fiberglass. "You want a repair quote or you want something else? Because you've got the something-else face."

I sat on an upturned cooler and told him most of it. A river to run. Upstream, which he'd hate. Into the Vale, which he'd hate more. Off the books, which was the only part he'd like.

He stopped sanding. "The Vale's closed."

"The Vale's been closed five years. Rivers don't read signs."

"Rivers don't, but rangers do. And Halvorsen's got more lawyers than the river's got bends." He set the sander down and finally looked at me, and I saw what ten years of watching him from a distance had missed: the grin was gone, but the thing underneath it—the stillness, the reading-the-water calm—was still there. Just buried. "Why me? There are pilots in Port Calloway with clean records and current certs."

"Because they're in Port Calloway, and I've got four days. And because a clean record means a man who's never been tested." I leaned forward. "Dez. I need someone who's already been to the bottom and knows the way back up. I'm not hiring a record. I'm hiring hands."

He laughed, short and ugly, but something in it wasn't ugly at all. "That's the worst recruitment speech I've ever heard."

"It's the only one I've got."

He looked at the hull, at the boatyard, at the coffee, at the whole gray arithmetic of his life. Then he said, "Upstream through the Narrows is suicide in June melt."

"Probably."

"And the gorge after that hasn't been run since before the closure. Nobody's seen what the flood years did to it."

"Then we'll be the first to know."

He wiped his palms on his thighs, a pilot's habit, gripping a wheel that wasn't there. "One condition. If I say walk, we walk. No hero stuff. I've buried enough of that."

"If you say walk, we walk," I said, and we shook on it, and his hand was steady, and so was mine, and I thought: that's one. That's one hand.

* * *

Ivo found me, which should have told me everything.

I was checking the gear cache behind the chandlery on the second evening—carabiners, throw ropes, the repair kit I'd been assembling piece by piece like a man building a confession—when I heard the rattle of the side gate and turned to find a kid standing in the alley with my spare knife in his hand and an expression like he'd been caught between two better options.

Fifteen, maybe sixteen. All elbows and hunger, dark hair cut with what looked like a fish filleting knife. I knew him by sight the way everyone in Kestrel's Landing knew him by sight: the stowaway kid off the coastal freighters, the one who slept in the net lofts in winter and lifted what he needed the rest of the year. The harbormaster had given up prosecuting him. The shopkeepers had given up shouting. He was a fact of the town, like the fog.

"That's mine," I said.

"Was nobody's this morning." He weighed the knife, considering. "Good blade. You going somewhere?"

"That's a strange question to ask a person you're robbing."

"It's a strange knife for a person who's staying." He tossed it back—underhand, easy, dead accurate into my chest, and I caught it without thinking and hated how impressed I was. "You've bought rope for a big load. Dry bags, three of them. Repair patches. You're running water, and you're running it soon, and you're running it quiet, because you bought the rope from Sal at the fuel dock instead of the chandlery, and Sal doesn't talk." He grinned, quick and crooked. "I talk, but not to them."

I should have sent him away. Every rational cell in my body cast its vote for sending him away: he was a child, he was a thief, he was a liability, and I was about to walk into a fight with a company that had already killed people once and gotten away with it. I could hear my old instructor's voice—*you are responsible for everyone you put on the water, Theo, everyone, always*—and that voice had one opinion about a sixteen-year-old on the Serrano in melt season.

But I looked at him standing in the alley, light on his feet the way only kids and river cats are light on their feet, and I thought about the portage through the canyon, and the cache runs, and the hundred small tasks that need quick hands and a body that bounces.

"How old are you?"

"Old enough."

"That's not an answer."

"Sixteen." No shame in it. "I've been crew on freighters since I was thirteen. I can read current better than half the licensed pilots in this town—I've watched them from the water, from underneath, which is a view they never get." He stepped closer, and the grin dropped away, and what was under it was dead serious. "You're going into the Vale. Everybody in the net lofts is talking about it. The woman who got blamed for the disaster, going back up the Serrano. And I want in."

"Why? There's no cut for you. There's no glory. There's probably a fine, maybe jail, definitely cold and wet."

He shrugged, and for a second he looked exactly his age, and exactly a thousand years older. "I've been nobody's crew and everybody's problem my whole life. You're the first person whose boat I actually wanted to be on." He stuck out his hand. "Ivo. I steal things, I don't get caught much, and I cook better than anyone you've ever met, which on a river trip is worth more than a cert."

I looked at that hand for a long moment.

"Rule one," I said, taking it. "You do what Dez or I say, the first time, no debate, on the water. On the water we don't vote."

"Rule two?"

"Stop stealing from me."

"No promises." But he was grinning again, and I was—God help me—almost smiling back.

* * *

Ruth Calloway was the hardest, and not because she said no.

She said yes immediately, which was the problem, because I hadn't asked yet. I'd come to her cabin on the bluffs above the estuary with nothing but a bottle of cheap rye and a speech I hadn't finished writing, and she'd opened the door, looked at me, and said, "You're the Nakamura woman. The guide. You're going up the Serrano, and you need a scientist, and you've come to the only disgraced biologist in three hundred miles. Come in before the wind takes the door."

Her cabin was a museum of a career that had been taken from her: field notebooks stacked in date order, a spectrometer older than I was sitting under a dust cloth like a covered bird, maps of the Vale on the walls with annotations in three colors of ink. She'd been the reserve's lead water-quality researcher before the disaster. Afterward, when Halvorsen's reports came out blaming "unauthorized backcountry activity" for the tailings release, her data had been the one thing that didn't fit their story—and so her data had been discredited, her methods questioned, her funding cut, her name quietly removed from the reserve's publications. She'd spent five years in this cabin re-running her numbers and mailing them to regulators who filed them under *crank*.

I told her about Amos. I told her about the map. I told her about the men in clean boots, and she nodded like I was confirming a weather forecast.

"You understand what you're walking into," she said, pouring the rye into two cups that didn't match. "If Halvorsen wants a prospector's map destroyed, it's because the river is still telling the story they buried. And if the source is still bleeding after five years, it's not an accident that's healing. It's a wound that was made to keep bleeding." She handed me a cup. "I've been writing that sentence in reports for five years. Nobody upstream would listen. So yes. I'll come. I'll bring the spectrometer and the sample kit and every notebook I own, and I will test that water until it confesses."

"There's risk," I said, because I owed her that. "Real risk. Men have already come asking about the map, and they weren't the friendly kind. If it goes badly—"

"Theo." She said my name like she'd been using it for years. "They took my work, my reputation, and my river. I'm sixty-one years old and I have been waiting for someone to give me a reason to go get them back. Don't you dare stand in my kitchen and offer me a way out."

So that was three. A washed-out pilot who'd stopped believing in himself, a thief who'd never been believed in, and a scientist the establishment had erased. A crew nobody wanted. A crew, I thought, looking around that cluttered cabin at the only honest data in three hundred miles, that nobody had wanted because we were the ones who'd seen the truth and paid for it.

We were going to be each other's witnesses. That was the thought that settled over me on the walk back down the bluffs, the estuary going silver and then dark below, the first stars coming out over the water. Not a team. Not a company of adventurers. Witnesses. For Amos. For the climbers I'd lost. For Ruth's five years of filed-away reports. For every person the company had made invisible, starting with me.

* * *

We launched prep in Dez's boatyard bay, behind locked doors, for three days.

It was not smooth. The first evening Dez and Ivo circled each other like dogs deciding about a fence line—Ivo lifting tools to "organize" them, Dez catching him at it with a look that could have caulked a hull. Ruth and Dez argued about weight distribution until midnight, she insisting the spectrometer rode center and dry, he insisting it rode wherever he damn well said because he was the one who'd right the boat when it flipped. Ivo cooked the second night—some fish stew out of odds and ends that should have been inedible and wasn't—and I watched Dez go back for a third bowl without a word, and that was the fence line quietly decided.

On the third night we laid the map out on the workbench under one lamp, all four of us leaning over it, and I watched them see it for the first time. Amos Grell's hand-drawn Serrano, ninety miles of river in faded ink, the headwaters marked with a small, patient X and a note in a shaking script: *It never stopped. Tell Mara it never stopped.*

"Five years," Ruth said softly, her finger hovering over the source. "Five years, and it never stopped."

Dez traced the gorge with one calloused finger. "This section here. If the flood years cut it the way I think they did, this is going to be the hardest water any of us has ever seen." He looked up. "You all understand that. Once we're past the boundary, there's no walking out except downriver or over rock. We're committed."

"We're committed," I said.

Ivo was quiet, which was unusual enough that we all noticed. He was staring at the X at the headwaters, and when he spoke his voice had lost its street-edge shine. "The old man who drew this. Did he know what was up there? Before he sent you?"

"He knew," I said. "He spent his whole life knowing, and nobody listened, and it killed him. That's what we're carrying up that river. Not a map. A man's whole life, and what it cost him."

Nobody said anything for a while. The lamp hissed. Outside, the harbor slapped against the pilings, patient as water always is.

Then Dez straightened up and clapped his hands once, the sound of a man closing a hatch. "Boats are ready. Food's packed for twelve days, fourteen if we stretch. Weather window opens tomorrow night—moon's dark, and the patrol boats run their boundary sweep at dusk and dawn, not between." He looked around the circle of us, the disgraced and the discredited and the never-counted. "We launch at midnight."

Ruth began covering the spectrometer. Ivo started stacking the dry bags with a thief's economy, everything where a hand could find it blind. Dez killed the lamp over the map, and Amos's river disappeared into the dark where, a hundred miles upstream, it was waiting for us.

I stood a moment in the black with the promise in my chest, heavier now, but no longer mine alone.

Four hands had become eight. By midnight, we'd find out what they were worth.
