## 7. Upstream

The river didn't care that it was two in the morning, and neither, apparently, did Theo Nakamura.

Dez stood on the gravel bar where the Serrano widened below the old mill ruins and watched her check the rafts for the third time. She worked by feel more than light—headlamp dialed down to a red coin of glow, hands moving over the frame like she was taking a pulse. Frame bolts. Thwart straps. Throw rope coiled loose enough to grab, tight enough to throw. She'd done it all twice already, and now she was doing it again, and Dez understood something about her in that moment that he hadn't understood in the three days since she'd shown up at his door with a wad of cash and a lie about a fishing trip.

Theo Nakamura was not running *toward* anything. She was running *from* something, and the river was the only direction left.

He'd seen that look before. In the mirror, mostly.

"You going to stand there or you going to earn your cut?" she said, without turning around. Her voice carried flat across the water, no echo to it, like the night itself was holding its breath.

"I'm assessing," Dez said. "Professional habit."

"Assess this." She tossed him a dry bag. He caught it against his chest and it hit hard enough to rearrange his opinion of her upper body. "Load goes bow-heavy on the green boat. Ivo rides center, Ruth behind him. You're on oars."

Dez looked at the bag, then at her. "I thought you were rowing sweep."

"I am. Until the gorge. Then I need you on sticks and me reading water." She finally turned, and the red lamp caught the planes of her face—hollowed out, sleepless, but steady in a way that unsettled him more than panic would have. "You've run the Serrano lower canyon."

"Ten years ago. Before it was a crime scene."

"It's still a crime scene," Theo said. "That's kind of the point."

* * *

They loaded in silence broken only by the river's low conversation with itself. The Serrano ran high for the season, swollen with early melt off the Vale peaks, and even in the dark Dez could hear the difference—a deeper note under the white noise, the sound of a lot of water moving with somewhere to be.

Ivo appeared out of the willows without a footstep, the way he did everything, a skinny shadow with a duffel over one shoulder that Dez was fairly sure contained at least three things stolen in the last forty-eight hours. The kid dropped the bag into the raft and grinned at Dez, sixteen years old and cocky as a jaybird.

"Boat's ready. I checked."

"You checked."

"I checked better than her." Ivo jerked a thumb at Theo. "No offense."

"Some taken," Theo said mildly.

Ruth came last, down the trail with her sample kit hugged to her chest like an infant, hunched against a cold she clearly wasn't dressed for. A park biologist who'd lost her park—that was the whole story Dez had gotten, and he'd learned not to ask for more. Everyone on this gravel bar had a story they weren't telling. That was the crew. That was the whole recruitment pitch, if you wanted to be honest about it: four people the world had finished with, floating upstream into the one place none of them were allowed to be.

"Water temp?" Ruth asked Theo, all business, grateful for business.

"Cold enough to kill you in ten minutes. So don't swim."

"That's my plan every day," Ruth said, and climbed aboard.

Dez took the oars and pushed off, and the current took them the way it takes everything—gently at first, then completely.

* * *

For the first hour it was easy. Almost insultingly easy. The valley opened up around them, black walls of pine against a sky rinsed clean by wind, stars so thick overhead it looked like someone had spilled something precious. The boundary markers for the reserve came and went on the left bank—a weathered sign, then a newer one, then a fresh steel post with reflective tape that caught his headlamp like an animal's eye.

*RESERVE BOUNDARY — CLOSED BY ORDER — NO ENTRY*

Nobody said anything when they passed it. But Dez felt the whole boat register it, the way a body registers a drop in temperature. Behind him Ruth went quiet in a way that had weight to it. Ivo stopped fidgeting. And up ahead in the sweep boat, Theo's red lamp swung once toward the sign and then away, deliberate, like closing a door.

They were criminals now. Officially. As of a steel post and some reflective tape.

Dez found, to his surprise, that he didn't mind. Ten years since anyone had trusted him with a boat and a mission instead of a hangover and a lawsuit. The oars felt right in his hands. The water talked to him the way it used to, before, reading its mood through the shafts—*shallow there, hole there, come left, easy now*. His shoulders remembered what his file said he'd forgotten.

Then the canyon narrowed, and the river stopped being polite.

* * *

He heard it before he saw it: a change in the voice of the water, from murmur to argument. Theo's lamp flashed twice—their signal—and Dez pulled for the right eddy line as the sweep boat slid in beside him against the slack water.

"Gauge says a seven-mile stretch," Theo said quietly. "Continuous. Two drops I know of, maybe three the map doesn't. We run it dark or we scout by headlamp and lose the night."

"Run it dark," Ivo said immediately.

"Nobody asked you," Dez said.

"I'm asking him," Theo said, and looked at Dez. Just looked. Red light, black water, her eyes steady as a surveyor's plumb. "You've got the best read on big water here. Your call."

And there it was. Not a test exactly—or yes, exactly a test, but not the kind with a wrong answer. The kind where whatever he chose, he'd own it, and she'd back it. When was the last time anyone had handed him a decision like it was a tool instead of a trap?

Dez looked downstream. Even in the dark he could read the water's grammar: the smooth tongue down the middle, the haystack waves building, the white roar of something bigger around the bend. High water. Cold water. A crew with one broken wrist between zero of them so far, and a long way yet to go.

"We run it," he heard himself say. "But we space the boats. Hundred yards. You follow my line, not the river's."

"The river's line is usually fine," Ivo said.

"The river's line," Dez said, "is what killed the last four people who trusted it at this flow."

That shut everybody up. Theo nodded once, and pushed off first anyway, because of course she did—sweep boat leads, that was her logic, take the risk herself before asking it of anyone else. Dez was starting to build a whole quiet case file on how Theo Nakamura operated, and every entry made him trust her more and like himself less for trusting her.

He shoved off thirty seconds behind her.

* * *

The first rapid was a liar. It looked like nothing—just a long chute of standing waves, regular as stairs—and then halfway down the river dropped two feet where no drop was supposed to be, and the world became noise.

Dez rowed the way you row when thinking is too slow: hands ahead of mind, body remembering ten thousand eddies and holes. The raft stood up on a wave, hung there—weightless, engineless, a held breath—and slammed down into the trough hard enough to buckle his knees against the frame. Water came over the bow in a cold fist. Ivo whooped. Ruth made a sound that was either a prayer or a swear.

"Hundred yards!" Dez bellowed, though he couldn't see the sweep boat anymore, couldn't see anything but the next wave and the one after that, a black herd of them stampeding downhill. He rowed. The river argued. Somewhere in the argument they reached an agreement, and the agreement was: left of the hole, right of the rock, thread the needle, breathe.

The second drop he never saw coming.

It came as a sound—a deep, hollow boom under the roar, the sound of a lot of water going over something and landing badly. He dug for the right bank, screaming for Theo's boat, but the night ate his voice, and then the current had them sideways and there was a hole in the world ahead, a place where the stars ended and nothing began.

"High side! HIGH SIDE!"

Ivo threw himself right. Ruth threw herself right. The raft tipped past the angle where tipping stops being a scare and becomes arithmetic—weight versus water, and water always has more—and for one long second Dez felt the whole craft balanced on a knife-edge of physics with his family-of-convenience strapped inside it.

Then the wave hit them broadside and the world went white and upside down.

* * *

Cold. Cold like a verdict. Dez surfaced gasping, one hand still knotted in his safety line because some part of his brain had never stopped working even while the rest of it screamed. The raft was gone—no, there, turtle-backed in the boil, Ivo clinging to the upside-down hull with both arms, eyes huge above the waterline. Ruth—

There. Sweep boat, twenty feet downstream, Theo standing at the oars like she'd been cast in bronze, hauling the boat through a seam in the current Dez couldn't even see. She brought it alongside the flipped raft in one move, one correction, one read of water that Dez would have paid money to watch in daylight, and her hand shot out and closed on Ruth's jacket collar as the biologist swept past, and held.

Held. Held. Held.

"Line!" Theo roared at Dez. "Grab the line!"

The flip line was still rigged on the overturned raft—Ivo, the little thief, had rigged it without being asked—and Dez got a hand on it and swam for the eddy behind the rock island, boots dragging, lungs full of knives. Between the four of them and the river's own exhausted generosity, they got the raft to the shallows and rolled it, and stood there in the aftermath, breathing like landed fish.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. The rapid boomed on behind them, indifferent, already forgetting them.

Then Ivo started laughing. Wet, shaking, half-hysterical laughter, sitting in the gravel with river pouring off him. "We *flipped*," he said. "First night. We flipped the boat."

"Lost the dry bag with the stove," Ruth reported, teeth chattering, cradling her kit. "Samples are okay. Samples are waterproof. Everything else is wet."

Dez looked at Theo.

She was wringing out her sleeves, methodical, unhurried, and when she met his eyes across the gravel bar she didn't say *I told you so*, didn't say *your call*, didn't say anything at all. She just nodded at the raft. "Bail it. We've got six miles of easier water and we're losing the dark."

And that was it. No post-mortem. No blame. The call had been his; the flip had been the river's; the recovery had been hers, and she filed it away like a professional filing a completed job.

Dez picked up a bail bucket and started scooping, and somewhere between the third bucket and the tenth he admitted it to himself, in the privacy of his own soaked skull:

She was good. Better than good. He'd watched her hold a loaded sweep boat in a seam at night at high water, and that move lived maybe in a dozen people on the continent. They'd blacklisted *her*? Whatever she'd done at the Vale—or whatever they'd said she'd done—it wasn't incompetence. It couldn't be. You didn't learn water like that and then forget it. You didn't read a seam like that by accident.

Which meant the disaster five years ago hadn't been a skills problem. Which meant it had been something else. Something with a name she wasn't saying.

He bailed the last of the water and took his seat on the oars, and as they slid back into the current he caught Theo watching him—not suspiciously, just watching, the way you watch weather you haven't decided to trust yet.

"Not bad," she said. "For a washed-out pilot."

"Careful," Dez said. "I might start believing you."

"Don't," she said, but something at the corner of her mouth almost moved, and Dez counted it as the first honest thing either of them had said.

* * *

Dawn found them in easier water, the canyon opening into a long marshy valley where mist stood on the surface in columns, and the mountains ahead came up pale and enormous out of the dark—the Vale, the closed country, getting realer by the mile. Ruth stood in the bow with a vial of river water held up against the brightening sky, studying it like scripture. Ivo slept in a puddle of his own making, mouth open, one hand still curled around the bail bucket.

Dez rowed and watched the peaks and felt the dread arrive on schedule, right on time behind the exhilaration, the way it always did. Because the river was beautiful, and the running was good, and for a few hours last night he'd been exactly who he used to be. But the water under his blades was carrying them deeper into a reserve that killed people to keep closed, toward a source somebody was willing to hunt them for, and the flip in the dark had been a message he'd be a fool not to read:

The Serrano at this flow forgives nothing twice.

Ahead, Theo raised one hand—not a signal, just a stop—and pointed upstream with her chin.

Around the next bend, faint in the dawn mist, came a sound Dez knew from another life, another river, another set of mistakes.

An engine. Outboard motor, idling low. Where no legal boat had any business being.

Theo's eyes found his across the water, and she put one finger to her lips.
