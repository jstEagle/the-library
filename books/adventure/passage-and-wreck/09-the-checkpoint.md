## 9. The Checkpoint

Ivo had stolen his first thing at seven years old. A bread roll, still warm, off a vendor's cart in the fish market at Port Calloway, while the vendor argued with a man about the price of ice. He'd eaten it crouched behind a stack of crab traps with his heart hammering like he'd swallowed a bird, and even then—even at seven—he'd understood the arithmetic of it: what you take is nothing compared to what you're worth taking from.

Thirteen years later, the arithmetic hadn't changed. Only the stakes.

He lay flat on his stomach on the granite shelf above the river bend, watching the ranger patrol's firelight flicker through the pines below. Four of them. Two tents, one aluminum boat pulled up on the gravel bar, and—this was the part that mattered—a radio set up on a folding table between the tents, its little green indicator light pulsing like a slow heartbeat.

"Four," he breathed, barely a sound. "One awake."

Below him, Theo went very still. In the dark she was less a person than a shape the night had agreed to keep, all edges and patience. She'd been like that since the checkpoint signs started appearing two days ago—RESERVE BOUNDARY, PERMIT REQUIRED, VIOLATORS PROSECUTED—each one making her quieter and faster, like a blade being drawn.

"One awake," Theo repeated. Her voice came from somewhere near his elbow, so low it was almost vibration. "Can you see which?"

Ivo shifted his head a centimeter. The sentry sat on an overturned crate facing the river, a thermos cup cradled in both hands, steam rising past his face. Young. Boots unlaced. The kind of tired that comes from too many nights listening to nothing.

"Facing the water. Radio's on the table to his left. Maybe three meters."

A long silence. Somewhere below, the Serrano muttered over its rocks, cold and constant, saying the only thing it ever said: *go, go, go.*

"We drift past," Theo said finally. "No lights, no paddles until we're around the bend. If they hear us, they hear water. That's all they should hear."

"And if they don't just hear?"

"Then we're four people caught trespassing in a closed reserve with no permits." She paused. "Which is why nobody gets caught."

It wasn't a plan. Ivo had grown up around plans—the kind men made in doorways and alley mouths, quick and confident and usually wrong. This was more like a prayer wearing a plan's clothes. But Dez was already down at the raft, checking the lashings for the third time, and Ruth was sitting with her sample kit hugged to her chest like a child, and Theo had that look she got when the decision had already been made and everyone else was just catching up to it.

So Ivo did what Ivo did. He looked at the radio again.

*What you take is nothing compared to what you're worth taking from.*

Except this time it was backwards. This time the thing he wanted to take was worth everything, and the people he'd be taking it *for*—

He shut that thought down before it could finish. Thinking about them as *for* was how you got stupid. Stupid was how you got caught. Stupid was how you ended up in a holding cell in Kestrel's Landing explaining to a magistrate why your fingerprints were on government property.

"Ivo." Theo's hand found his shoulder in the dark. Just pressure, no shake. "We go on your signal. You've got the best eyes. Tell me when his attention breaks."

His attention would break. It always broke. Men on lonely watches were just loneliness wearing a uniform; sooner or later they reached for the thermos, or stood to stamp feeling back into their feet, or turned to look upriver because upriver was where trouble would come from and staring at it was the only comfort available.

Ivo waited. Below, the sentry drank, set the cup down, rubbed his face with both hands. Then—there it is—he stood, turned his back to the table, and walked to the edge of the gravel bar to relieve himself into the dark water, sighing like a man unburdening himself of something heavier than urine.

"Now," Ivo breathed.

* * *

The river took them.

That was the thing nobody told you about night water—that it wasn't silent at all, that it was a wall of sound, and you could hide a whole raft inside it. Dez held them dead center in the current, paddle feathered across his knees, steering with tiny corrections that Ivo felt more than saw. Ruth lay low against the gear with her broken-looking kit clutched tight. Theo knelt at the bow, a silhouette carved out of the dark.

They slid past the camp close enough that Ivo could smell woodsmoke and coffee. Close enough to see the sentry zipping up, yawning, turning back toward his chair.

Close enough to see him stop.

See him turn toward the water. See his head tilt, the way a dog's tilts.

Ivo's whole body went cold. They were mid-channel, exposed, a black shape on black water moving slower than the current wanted, and the sentry was looking straight at them now, and his hand was already moving—not reaching for a weapon, worse, reaching for the *radio*, and Ivo understood in one white flash exactly what happened next: a call went out, a net closed, checkpoints activated upstream and down, and four nobodies became four names in a report, and Theo's name most of all, because Theo's name was the one they were actually hunting.

He didn't decide. That was the honest truth he'd carry afterward, the part that kept him up nights: there was no decision, no noble calculation. There was just the old animal reflex, thirteen years deep, firing before thought.

The raft passed under the overhanging pine at the bend, and Ivo went up it.

Later, he couldn't have described the climb. Branches tore at his sleeves. Bark ripped his palms. He remembered the shock of cold where the river had soaked his boots, remembered Dez's strangled whisper—*Ivo, what the hell*—and then he was on the low limb above the camp, swinging down into the firelight like something falling out of a bad dream.

The sentry spun. His mouth opened.

Ivo hit him.

Not hard—that was the strange part, the part his hands seemed to know without him. A palm to the sternum, a foot hooked behind the ankle, and the kid went down onto the gravel with a whump of expelled air and no shout, because the breath to shout with had been knocked clean out of him. Ivo was already past him, already at the table, already had the radio handset in his fist and the main unit torn off the table, cables ripping free, and then the other tent flap was opening and a voice said, "Renz? What—" and Ivo ran.

He ran the way water runs, downhill and without argument, crashing downslope through the brush toward the bend where the raft would be—if it was there, if they'd waited, if Theo hadn't cut her losses—and then strong hands seized his collar and hauled him over the gunwale and he was in the bottom of the raft, gasping, with the radio crushed against his ribs and Theo's face inches from his own, furious and pale and alive.

"Paddle," she said to Dez, and the river took them again.

* * *

They didn't speak for an hour.

Dez drove them hard through a stretch of shallow braids, picking channels by feel and memory, putting distance and noise and darkness between them and the camp. Ruth held a flashlight shielded under a blanket, checking Ivo's hands—bark-raw, bleeding along the heel of both palms—without asking anything, which was its own kind of question.

Finally, in a long slow pool beneath cliffs that swallowed every sound, Theo called a rest.

She didn't yell. Ivo had braced for yelling. Instead she sat on the gear pile across from him, elbows on knees, and looked at him for a long time with those gray eyes that seemed to file everything away for later.

"You could've been shot," she said.

"He didn't have a gun out."

"You didn't know that."

"I knew the radio was the problem." He made himself hold her gaze. "You said nobody gets caught. So nobody gets caught."

"That's not—" She stopped. Rubbed her jaw. "That's not how it works. We don't get caught by being careful, not by leaving a nineteen-year-old ranger facedown in the gravel wondering what hit him."

"Nineteen?" Something twisted in Ivo's chest. He'd judged the sentry young, but hearing the number made it real, made it a person, made it a kid barely older than himself lying alone on a riverbank watching strangers vanish with half the patrol's communications in a thief's arms. "You counted his age from thirty meters in the dark?"

"I counted his boots. Unlaced boots are either very experienced or very new." She sighed, and some of the iron went out of her voice. "He'll be fine, Ivo. Bruised pride, paperwork, a week of extra shifts. Rangers don't shoot first at their own checkpoint. But the radio changes things."

"How?"

"Because now they can't talk to each other, so they'll assume the worst. And because now they know exactly who we are." She leaned forward. "A raft running dark, they might write off as poachers, smugglers, idiots. A raft that steals their radio? That's deliberate. That's someone with something to hide worth chasing." She let that sit. "You painted a target on us tonight. On all of us."

The words landed the way she'd meant them to. Ivo looked down at his raw hands, at the radio between his feet—government olive drab, a serial number stenciled on the case, a handset coiled like a sleeping snake. Twelve hours ago it had been a solution. Now it sat in the bottom of the raft like evidence.

Like what it was.

"I wasn't thinking," he said quietly.

"No," Theo agreed. "You weren't."

And here was the thing, the thing he couldn't explain, the thing that made his throat tight in a way he hated: neither had she been angry. Not really. Underneath the words, underneath the iron, there was something else—something that looked almost like recognition. Like she'd seen a younger version of somebody standing on that branch, and it hadn't been him.

Dez broke the silence from the stern, spitting over the side. "For what it's worth, the theft itself was clean. Fastest I've seen. Kid never even got a look at him."

"It's not worth much," Ruth said softly. She was looking at Ivo too, but differently—not measuring, not judging. Reading. "He did it because he thought we'd be caught otherwise. That's not nothing, Theo."

"It's not nothing," Theo conceded. "It's also not a plan. Next time, Ivo, you tell me what you're seeing and you *wait*. We survive out here because nobody acts alone. The second one of us decides he's the only one who can save the day, we're done." She held his eyes. "Are we clear?"

"Clear," Ivo said.

But inside, something had shifted loose and wouldn't settle. All his life, stealing had been simple: you took, you ran, you were alone. Alone was safe. Alone was the whole point. Nobody could betray you if nobody knew your name, and nobody could leave you if nobody ever stayed.

Tonight he'd stolen for other people. Tonight, when he'd run downhill through the dark, he hadn't been running *away* from something.

He'd been running *toward* the raft. Toward them. And they'd been there, waiting, hands out to haul him in.

He turned the radio over in his lap, and that was when he saw it—stenciled under the serial number, small and official: PROPERTY OF HALVORSEN EXTRACTION — FIELD OPS DIVISION.

The cold came back all at once.

"Theo," he said. His voice came out wrong, thin. "Theo, look at this."

She took the unit from him. Turned it toward Ruth's shielded light. For a moment nobody moved, and the river said *go, go, go,* and Theo's face in the dim glow went from confusion to something older and colder.

"Those aren't rangers' radios," she said. "Rangers carry reserve-issue comms. Government green. This is corporate field equipment." She looked upriver, back the way they'd come, toward the camp that wasn't a ranger camp after all—or wasn't only one. "Halvorsen has people on the river ahead of us. Working with the patrols, or dressed as them."

Nobody spoke. Dez's paddle stopped moving. Even the water seemed to lean in.

"They're not chasing us yet," Theo said finally, and her voice had gone quiet in the way Ivo was learning to fear, quiet like deep water, quiet like the drop-off past a calm pool. "But they will be by morning. And now they know we know."

She looked at each of them—Dez, Ruth, and last, longest, Ivo.

"Get some sleep," she said. "All of you. Tomorrow we stop running at night."

"Why?" Dez asked.

"Because," Theo said, turning back to face upriver, into the dark where the mountains were, "from here on, we're not hiding anymore. We're racing."
