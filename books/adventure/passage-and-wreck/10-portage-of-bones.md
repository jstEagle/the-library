## 10. Portage of Bones

The river told us before the canyon did.

Dez heard it first—she always heard things first, some animal inheritance I'd never been granted—and she held up a fist at the bend below the confluence. We eddied out against a gravel bar the color of old bone and listened. Under the Serrano's usual voice there was another sound, lower, wrong: a grinding, like the river chewing stone.

"Siege Rapid," Dez said. "Or what's left of it. My book's ten years old."

"Your book is older than that," Ruth said.

"My book is a prayer," Dez said. "We walk."

Ivo, perched on the gear pile in the bow of the second raft like something feral that had decided we were temporarily interesting, said, "Walk where?"

I looked up. The canyon walls rose sheer on both sides, gray granite streaked with rust-colored seeps, and high on the left wall—impossibly high—a cut zigzagged across the face like a scar. A trail. Or the ghost of one.

"There," I said.

"That's not a trail," Ivo said. "That's a rumor of a trail."

"It's a portage road," I said. "Miners cut them into canyon walls all through this range. Rail spurs, mule paths. If it goes, we go."

Dez squinted at it, then at the water, then at me, doing the arithmetic she did better than anyone I'd ever run a river with. "Two rafts, six days of food, a broken radio, and a teenager who complains more than he carries." She spat over the side, an old habit she performed with real ceremony. "Yeah. We walk."

* * *

Unloading took two hours. Everything came out of the boats: frames, tubes, dry bags, the boxed samples Ruth guarded like scripture, the map itself riding against my ribs in its oilcloth sleeve because I had stopped trusting any container I couldn't feel against my skin. We broke the rafts down to their component parts and began ferrying loads up a talus slope to the base of the cut, and by the third trip my shoulders had remembered every year of work I'd done before the fall, and hated me for all of them.

The cut, up close, was worse than it looked from the water. It wasn't a path so much as a ledge blasted out of the cliff, in places no wider than a door laid flat, with a drop on the outside edge that went down and down to the white roar of Siege Rapid. Someone had drilled and pinned a cable handline along the worst stretch once. The cable was still there, furred with lichen, sagging between anchors like the spine of something long dead.

"Handline's solid," Dez called from ahead, testing it with her whole weight. "Anchors are good steel. These boys built to last."

"These boys," Ruth said quietly, "built to extract."

She'd gone strange since the water samples. Not frightened—Ruth didn't frighten, or if she did she filed it somewhere I couldn't see—but focused in a way that made the air around her feel charged. She walked the ledge with her eyes down, reading the rock.

It took me half a mile to understand what she was reading.

The first piece of it I took for driftwood, bleached pale, wedged in a crack above the trail. Then I saw the bolt heads. Then the curve of it—the deliberate, engineered curve of a flume segment, timber-lined, iron-banded, big enough to swallow a man. It had been hauled up this canyon and anchored to this wall by people who meant it to last forever, and the river had taken it anyway, or time had, and now it hung there like a rib.

"Flume," Dez said, when she caught me looking. "Sluiced ore slurry down to a mill downstream, probably. Before your time. Before mine."

There were others. Once I knew what to look for, the canyon stopped being a canyon and became a graveyard. A boiler drum the size of a truck, split open like a seedpod, ferns growing out of its belly. Iron rail, half-melted into the rock by decades of fire and frost. A wheelbarrow, impossibly, upright in a niche forty feet above the trail, as if someone had set it down for a break a century ago and never come back. And everywhere the small bones of industry: bolts, plates, chain, a boot heel fused to a scrap of leather that might have been a sole, might have been something else. I stepped around that one carefully.

Nobody talked much. Even Ivo went quiet, which for Ivo was like a clock stopping—you noticed the silence more than you'd ever noticed the noise.

Halfway along the ledge we came to a widening, a shelf maybe thirty feet across where the blasting had opened a room in the rock, and someone had camped here, long ago. There was a fire ring of fitted stones. There were tin cans, opened with knives, rusted to lace. And nailed flat to the wall, weathered to the color of the granite but still legible, a board with painted letters:

HALVORSEN EXTRACTION CO.
SECTOR 7 — NO ENTRY BELOW THIS POINT
BY ORDER OF THE SITE SUPERINTENDENT

Below the lettering, a date.

I stood in front of it for a long time. Long enough that Dez came back for me, and Ruth, and finally Ivo, who read it twice and said, "So? It's old. That's the whole point of this place, right? Old junk."

"No," Ruth said. Her voice had gone very level, the way it did when the science and the anger arrived at the same moment and shook hands. "Look at the date, Ivo."

He looked. So did I, though I already had it memorized.

**MAY 1911.**

"The Vale disaster," Ruth said, "the collapse they closed the reserve over—the official record has Halvorsen arriving in this drainage *afterward*. Salvage. Remediation. They came in nineteen sixty-one, after the flood killed those climbers, to 'assess' the old workings. That's the story. That's the story in three regulatory filings and a corporate history that won a *regional business award*." She touched the board with one finger, gently, the way you touch a wound you're checking for infection. "This marker predates the disaster by fifty years. Halvorsen wasn't salvaging Sector 7. They *were* Sector 7. They've been in this canyon for a hundred years, and then they erased themselves out of it."

Wind came down the gorge and moved the dust at our feet, and somewhere below us the river ground on over its bones.

"They hid the whole operation," Dez said slowly. "Why would you hide a mine that old?"

"Because of what they were mining," I said.

They all looked at me. I hadn't meant to say it out loud. But Amos Grell's hands had been steady on that map in his dying, steadier than dying hands have any right to be, and he had traced the river up and up and tapped one spot above the headwaters and said, *They never stopped wanting what's under the ice.* I hadn't understood him then. Standing in front of a sign that proved a hundred years of deliberate erasure, I was starting to.

"Amos prospected these headwaters for forty years," I said. "He told me the company offered him money three times to sell his claims and forget what he'd found. He wouldn't say what it was. He just said they'd killed for less, and that the map was the only honest record left of what's up there." I looked back at the board. "A company doesn't erase itself from a canyon unless the canyon knows something."

"So the disaster five years ago," Ivo said, and his voice had lost its teenage sneer entirely, "the one you got blamed for—that wasn't the first time this company buried something here."

"No," I said. "I don't think it was even the biggest."

* * *

We made camp that night on a gravel bench above the river, in the lee of a fallen flume section that gave us a windbreak and, Ruth noted grimly, a roof of century-old Halvorsen engineering. Dez got a fire going small and screened. Ivo cooked, because Ivo had appointed himself cook on day two and defended the title with genuine ferocity; he made rice and beans and something with dried chilies he'd stolen from a supply cache in Kestrel's Landing that he still insisted he had *purchased*. We ate. The river spoke its low grinding note in the dark below us.

It was Ruth who broke the quiet, the way she often did—not with talk but with an object. She set her sample kit down in the firelight and opened it, and laid the vials out in a row on a flat stone, labeled in her small precise hand. Upstream water. Confluence water. Canyon-seep water, taken that afternoon from a rust-streaked face of rock fifty yards past the old sign.

"The seep's running four times the contamination of the main channel," she said. "And the signature matches the disaster samples. Not similar. Matches. Same tailings chemistry, same isotopic ratios." She looked up at me across the fire. "Theo. The source isn't just leaking. Whatever they sealed up there, it's been bleeding into this river for years, maybe decades, slow enough that nobody downstream ever thought to look. Except Amos Grell looked. And now we're looking."

"And when we find it," Dez said, "then what? We've got vials and a dead man's map and a kid with sticky fingers. Halvorsen's got lawyers, helicopters, and a site chief whose name was on our permits before we ever launched. You think a jar of dirty water scares them?"

"It scared them enough to send men in clean boots after a dying prospector," I said.

That landed. I watched it land, in each of them, differently. Dez turned her cup in her hands. Ivo pulled his knees up to his chest, making himself small, which was the opposite of what he usually did with fear—he usually got loud. Ruth just nodded, once, like a hypothesis confirmed.

"I keep thinking about the wheelbarrow," Ivo said suddenly.

We all looked at him.

"Some guy pushed it up that trail," he said. "Put it right there. Figured he'd get back to it." He shrugged, defensive, daring us to make it stupid. "Nobody gets back to it. That's all. This whole canyon is full of stuff people figured they'd get back to."

Nobody laughed at him. It was, I thought, the truest thing anyone had said all day.

* * *

I took first watch, as I always did, and sat with my back against the cold flume timber while the others slept in their bags in a row like spoons. The stars came out hard over the canyon rim. Below, Siege Rapid kept up its grinding argument with the dark.

I should have been thinking about tomorrow—the rest of the portage, the load count, the weather building in the west that Dez had frowned at all evening. Instead I kept seeing the date on that board. May 1911. Fifty years before the disaster that ended my life. A hundred years of a company being exactly where it swore it had never been.

Five years ago I had stood in a hearing room in two languages and sworn that the collapse in the Vale wasn't guide error, wasn't client error, wasn't anything but the mountain itself failing—and the mountain doesn't fail, I'd wanted to scream, mountains are failed *into*, and everyone in the room had looked at me with that particular pity reserved for people who blame geology for their own negligence. I had believed, most days, that I was right. Believing it and proving it were different countries, and I had lived in the first one for five years because I couldn't afford a visa to the second.

Now the proof was under my boots. Literally. Every step of that portage had been a step on Halvorsen's hidden century.

I heard movement behind me and didn't reach for the knife, because I already knew the footstep—light, careful, deliberately audible. Dez lowered herself onto the log beside me and handed me half of something. Tea, from the taste. Terrible tea.

"You felt it too," she said.

"Felt what?"

"Don't do that." She blew on her own cup. "You've been walking like a woman following a funeral procession all day. I've poled boats behind you for eleven days, Nakamura. I know your walk."

I drank the terrible tea. Across the fire, Ivo had gone down hard mid-sentence hours ago, one arm flung over his pack the way teenagers sleep, guarding even in unconsciousness. Ruth's breathing was slow and even, her sample kit zipped inside her bag, inside her arms.

"When we find the adit," I said, "they'll be waiting. Or close behind. Voss didn't turn around at the checkpoint because he lost us. He turned around because he didn't need to chase us. He knows where we're going. There's only one place this river starts."

"So why hasn't he hit us yet?" Dez asked. "Helicopter, rifle, done. Why are we still breathing?"

Because he wants the map intact, I thought. Because a body in the reserve is a problem and a negotiated surrender is a solution. Because men like Voss prefer doors to bullets.

"Because he thinks we can still be bought," I said.

Dez was quiet a moment. "Can we?"

I looked at her. She held my gaze, unflinching, and I realized the question wasn't treason—it was navigation. She needed to know what heading we were on, the way she needed to know what was under the next rapid. You can run water blind, but you shouldn't run a crew blind.

"No," I said.

"Even if he offers you everything back? Name cleared, license restored, the whole five years unwritten?" She said it without cruelty, which was the only way it could be said. "I've watched people walk back into the burning house for less than that."

"Then you've watched them learn nothing," I said. "Voss can't give me back what I actually lost. Nobody can. What he can offer is a copy of it, printed on company paper." I turned the cup in my hands. "But he's going to offer. Tomorrow, or the day after. He'll pick his ground, somewhere we're committed, nowhere to run. Men like that always want to make the offer in person. They need to watch your face while they do it."

"And when he does?"

I looked east, up the black seam of the canyon, toward the headwaters I couldn't see and could feel anyway, the way you feel a storm coming through a change in the air. Somewhere up there was a sealed adit bleeding poison into the source of everything, and a dead man's daughter waiting on a coast I'd promised to reach, and a truth a hundred years deep with my own ruined name lying on top of it like the top card of a very long deck.

"Then he's going to hear no," I said, "from a woman who finally knows exactly why."

Dez nodded slowly, satisfied, and drained her tea, and said the thing she said at the end of every hard day on the water, the thing that had become, without either of us deciding it, a kind of grace between us:

"Then we run it."

Above us, far up the canyon rim, a light blinked once—white, brief, deliberate—and went out.

Neither of us said anything for a long time. But neither of us slept, either.
