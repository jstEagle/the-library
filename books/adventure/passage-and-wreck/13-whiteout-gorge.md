## 13. Whiteout Gorge

The storm came down the valley like something with a grudge.

Dez felt it before he saw it—a pressure change in his ears, a greenish light on the water, the river going suddenly glassy and wrong. Twenty years of running water had taught him that flat calm in the middle of a rapid was never calm. It was the river holding its breath.

"Pull out," Theo shouted from the bow of the lead raft, and there was no question in her voice, no negotiation. "Now. Left eddy."

They hauled for the bank as the first drops hit—fat, cold, spaced wide like artillery ranging in. By the time they had both rafts tied off and gear dragged above the waterline, the sky had gone the color of wet slate and the rain arrived all at once, a wall of it, hammering the canyon into white noise.

"Flash flood territory," Ruth yelled over the din. She was crouched over her sample kit, shielding it with her body like a mother bird. "This drainage narrows upstream. If it dumps up high we've got maybe twenty minutes before this bank is underwater."

Theo stood with rain streaming off her jaw, reading the canyon walls the way Dez had seen her read water all week—quick, total, no wasted motion. Then she pointed.

"There. The gorge."

It was barely visible through the rain: a slot in the south wall where an old tributary cut down toward the Serrano, choked with alder and scree. Above it, maybe two hundred meters up, a shallow overhang of granite offered the only roof in sight.

"We climb or we swim," Theo said. "I don't recommend the swimming."

* * *

The gorge was worse than it looked, which was saying something, because it had looked bad.

Dez went first, because he was heaviest and because Theo said so without saying so—the unspoken arithmetic of a crew that had been together eleven days and already knew each other's numbers. He found handholds in the wet rock, tested each one twice, and called them down to Ivo, who relayed them to Ruth while Theo ferried the packs and the rafts got abandoned, lashed and prayed over, below the flood line.

Ruth climbed badly. Not from weakness—from distraction. She kept stopping to look at the water rising in the main channel below, muttering readings under her breath, and every time she stopped, the rock under her boots got slicker.

"Ruth." Ivo's voice, thin and sharp from below. "Climb now, science later."

"I'm calculating discharge rates."

"You're calculating how to die. Move."

Dez almost laughed. The kid had been a stowaway and a thief three weeks ago, all elbows and stolen granola bars, and here he was bossing a grown woman up a cliff face in a storm. Somewhere in the last hundred miles, Ivo had become crew.

Then Ruth's foot slipped.

It happened fast, the way it always does. One boot skated off a algae-slick ledge, her hands scrabbled, caught, held—and then the hold she'd grabbed crumbled, a shelf of rotten shale, and she dropped. Not far. Two meters, maybe less. But she came down on her left arm, folded under her, on a slab angled exactly wrong, and the sound her wrist made carried even through the rain.

Dez heard it and his stomach clenched. He'd heard that sound twice before in his life, once on the Kootenay and once in a hospital parking lot, and both times someone had screamed after. Ruth didn't scream. That was worse. She just made a small, controlled noise, like a door closing, and went white as the foam on the river.

"My kit," she said through her teeth. "Top of my pack. Blue pouch."

The last fifty meters of climbing happened in a blur of rain and blood-pulse. They got her up onto the granite shelf and under the overhang, where the storm became a gray curtain instead of a drowning, and Theo knelt beside her with the blue pouch open before Ruth finished lying down.

"Wrist?"

"Distal radius, probably. Maybe both bones." Ruth's voice was steady, clinical, her own voice describing her own body like it belonged to a stranger. "Don't splint yet. Check my fingers first. Color, sensation, movement."

Her fingers were pale but pink at the nail beds. Theo flexed them gently; Ruth hissed but nodded.

"Circulation's intact. We splint, we elevate, we get you warm." Theo worked as she talked, SAM splint molded around the forearm, wrapped tight, sling improvised from a dry bag strap. Her hands were sure. Dez watched them and thought: *this is what they took away from her.* Five years of blacklisting, and the hands still remembered everything.

"The rafts," Ruth said suddenly, trying to sit up. "If the flood takes the rafts—"

"The rafts are tied to a granite boulder the size of your ego," Dez said. "They'll hold."

"They better. My samples are in the dry bag on—"

"On the raft. Which is tied to the boulder." He put a hand on her shoulder and pressed her back down, gentle as he could manage. "Lie still, doc. You did your job. The water did its thing. Now let us do ours."

She stared at him for a second, and then, unexpectedly, laughed—a short, cracked sound, half pain. "'Doc.' Nobody's called me doc since the university took it back."

"Well, they're idiots," Dez said. "You're the only person on this river who knows what's in the water. That makes you the most important person on this river."

From the edge of the overhang, Ivo made a gagging noise. But he was already gathering wood.

* * *

The problem with the overhang was firewood, and the problem with firewood was Ivo.

The kid announced he was going down into the gorge to gather driftwood from the flood debris line, and Theo said absolutely not, and Ivo said the wood up here was soaked through, and Theo said then we burn wet wood, and Ivo said wet wood doesn't burn, and then he was gone—slipping down the gully into the rain before anyone could grab him, moving with that low-center-of-gravity quickness that had kept him alive on the streets of three cities.

"I'm going after him," Dez said, already reaching for his shell jacket.

"No." Theo's eyes hadn't left the gully. "Two people down there doubles the risk. He knows these conditions—he grew up in worse. Watch the line and be ready."

So they watched. Rain sheeting down the gorge, visibility dropping to thirty meters, the river below swelling visibly, brown water climbing the banks in hungry increments. Ten minutes. Fifteen. Dez's heart rate climbed with the water.

At eighteen minutes, Ivo appeared.

He came up the gully with an armload of driftwood and a grin like he'd robbed a bank, and behind him, impossibly, the rain was thinning—not stopping, but slackening, the storm spending itself against the high peaks. And then Dez saw why the kid had taken so long.

Ivo wasn't just carrying wood. He was carrying Ruth's dry bag.

He'd gone down past the debris line, past the safe zone, down to the boulder where the rafts were tied—because he'd remembered, the way nobody else had, that Ruth's sample kit had a backup dry bag lashed to the stern of the second raft, and the flood was going to take the rafts if the water rose another meter, and so he'd stripped the bag and swum the last ten meters through current that would have killed any of them, and come back with the science and the firewood both.

He dumped it all on the granite, shivering so hard his teeth chattered, water pouring off him.

"Backup kit," he said, gasping. "She always keeps a backup. I watch. It's what I do."

Ruth looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached out with her good arm and pulled him down onto the granite beside her, and held on, and didn't say anything at all, because there wasn't anything to say.

Theo got the fire going with the driftwood and a quarter-cup of stove fuel, and the flame caught and spread, and the overhang filled with orange light and the smell of smoke and wet wool.

* * *

They ate rehydrated stew and drank tea brewed from pine needles Ruth insisted had vitamin C, and the storm hammered on outside their island of firelight, and somewhere in the middle of the meal, without anyone deciding it, the conversation turned real.

"I had a daughter once," Dez said, to the fire more than to anyone. "Didn't work out. Ex-wife took her to Vancouver when she was six. I sent postcards for a while. Then I sent money. Then I sent nothing, because nothing was easier than being reminded I'd chosen the river over them." He turned his tin cup in his hands. "Eleven days with you people and I've thought about her more than I have in five years. Go figure."

Nobody rushed to fill the silence. That was the thing about this crew—they were learning each other's silences, giving them room.

"I stole my first thing when I was nine," Ivo said, apropos of nothing, staring into the flames. "A loaf of bread. My mother was sick. After she died I stopped stealing food and started stealing things I could sell, which is a different crime entirely, morally speaking." He shrugged. "On this trip I keep waiting for somebody to make me leave. Nobody has. It's messing with my head."

"You steal my radio again and I'll make you leave," Theo said, but she said it soft, and Ivo grinned.

"Ruth?" Dez asked. "What's your confession? Everyone else has gone."

Ruth was quiet for a long time. Firelight moved on her face, on the splinted wrist resting in her lap.

"I reported the contamination at Vale," she said finally. "Five years ago. Before the collapse. I wrote it up, I filed it, I flagged it to my supervisor, who flagged it to his, and somewhere between his desk and the ministry it disappeared, and then the collapse happened, and then the inquiry needed a scapegoat, and they couldn't reach the company, so they reached sideways." She looked up. "They reached Theo."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke.

"I knew," Ruth said. "All these years, I knew my report existed, and I knew what it meant, and I told myself there was nothing I could do, the paper trail was buried, my career was dead, what difference could one disgraced biologist make." Her jaw tightened. "And then Amos Grell died in a flophouse with a map in his fist, and a woman I helped ruin walked into my life and asked me to run a river, and I realized the only thing I couldn't forgive myself for was staying quiet. So I came. Not for redemption. For evidence."

"You came for family," Ivo said. "You just don't know the word yet."

Ruth looked at him. Dez looked at him. Even Theo looked at him, and the kid had the grace to look briefly embarrassed, shrugging like he hadn't just said the truest thing spoken in the gorge.

"Anyway," he muttered. "Someone had to say it."

* * *

Later, when the others slept—Ruth propped against a pack with her wrist elevated, Ivo curled near the fire like a cat, Dez on watch—Theo sat down next to him at the mouth of the overhang, where the rain had thinned to mist and the river below ran loud and high but no longer rising.

"She saved my life today," Dez said quietly. "Ruth. When she grabbed that crumbling hold instead of falling free—she redirected herself onto me. Took the wrist break so she wouldn't knock us both off the wall. I saw it happen. She chose it."

"I know."

"And the kid swam floodwater for her sample kit. For *science*, Theo. For a bunch of vials." He shook his head. "Three weeks ago I was drinking myself stupid in Kestrel's Landing, convinced the best part of my life was behind me. Now I've got a biologist with a busted wrist, a teenage thief who steals for other people, and a disgraced guide who reads water like scripture. If Halvorsen catches us, they're not catching a crew. They're catching a family. And families fight different."

Theo was quiet a long moment. Mist drifted through the firelight behind them.

"When we get to the source," she said, "whatever we find there—it changes everything. There's no coming back down the same river."

"Good," Dez said. "I'm tired of the same river."

He settled back against the stone and looked up the dark gorge, toward the headwaters they couldn't see, and that was when he noticed it—a faint, rhythmic flicker far up-valley, beyond the storm's ragged edge. Not lightning. Lightning didn't pulse. Didn't hold position.

Lightning didn't come in threes, pause, and come in threes again.

"Theo," he said slowly. "Tell me that's a reflection."

She followed his gaze. The flicker pulsed again, steady, patient, miles upstream but unmistakable now.

Somebody was signaling. Or somebody was searching.

And they were between the crew and the source.
