## 15. Cut Off

Ivo heard the plane before he saw it.

That was the thing about the interior — sound traveled strange up there, bouncing off canyon walls, arriving late or early or from directions that didn't exist. He'd spent three weeks learning the river's vocabulary: the hiss of shallow riffles, the boom of water going over the falls two miles downstream, the groan of ice forming along the banks at night like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

This was none of those sounds. This was an engine, high and thin, and it was getting louder.

He dropped the armload of firewood and scrambled up the granite shelf above camp, boots skidding on frost-slick stone. The sky was doing its November trick — low gray ceiling, no color anywhere, the kind of light that made everything look already dead. He scanned north, toward the notch where the river bent out of sight, and there it was: a fixed-wing, white with a dark stripe, flying low and slow along the drainage.

He knew that stripe. Everybody who'd ever been blacklisted, fined, or run out of a job knew that stripe.

Halvorsen blue.

"Dez." His voice came out smaller than he wanted. He tried again, louder. "DEZ."

Dez was under the raft tarp with a wrench in her teeth and her hands buried in the frame, trying to coax a patch to hold on the starboard tube. She surfaced like a badger, spitting. "What? If you've broken another—"

"Plane."

She went still. Then she was up and moving, wrench forgotten, and they both stood on the shelf watching the aircraft track downriver, away from them, following the Serrano's course through the gorge until it dropped below the rim and vanished.

Toward the lower landing. Toward the cache.

They'd cached most of their surplus food and all their spare fuel at the landing below the falls — three days' hike downstream, half a day by raft if the water cooperated, which it hadn't in a week. It had been Theo's idea, back when caching had felt like prudence instead of a bet they were about to lose. You don't run a river in November carrying everything you own. You stage your supplies. You keep your options open.

The plane banked once, far off, a white sliver against gray. Then nothing.

"They're just looking," Dez said. But she said it the way people say things they don't believe, and Ivo had gotten good, in his sixteen years, at hearing the difference.

* * *

Theo and Ruth came back into camp at dusk, faces raw with cold, and Ivo watched Theo read the situation before anyone said a word. That was the thing about her too — she walked into a space and took its temperature like she was checking for fever. Her eyes went to the sky first. Then to Dez. Then to him.

"Talk," she said.

"Plane came up the drainage around noon," Dez said. "Low. Halvorsen colors. Headed downriver."

Ruth closed her eyes. She'd been gone all day with Theo, hiking the east bench, taking samples from the tributary seeps — more evidence, more vials wrapped in cloth and packed in foam, the little archive of proof growing heavier every day. Now she stood there holding a sample bag like it had suddenly turned to lead.

"The cache," Ruth said.

Nobody answered. Nobody had to.

"We should go tonight," Ivo said. All three of them looked at him, and he realized he'd said it out loud, and kept going anyway because stopping felt worse. "If they found it, we need to know now. If they didn't, we need to move it. Either way—" He gestured at the sky, the ridgeline, the thin new ice lacing the riverbanks. "Winter doesn't care what we decide."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. Firelight moved on her face. When she finally spoke, her voice was flat and even, which Ivo had learned was how she sounded when things were worst.

"Ivo, Dez, with me. We go down at first light, fast and quiet. Ruth stays here with the samples and the recordings. If we're not back in thirty-six hours—"

"What happens if you're not back in thirty-six hours?" Ivo asked, and hated how young his voice sounded.

Theo looked at him. Something passed behind her eyes — not softness exactly, but the place where softness would have lived if she could afford it.

"Then you take Ruth and the proof and you climb out the west bench to the old survey trail, and you walk until you find people who aren't us."

* * *

They went down at first light, and the river told them before they saw anything.

Ivo noticed it as a wrongness in the sound. Two miles above the falls, the Serrano ran loud and constant, a wall of noise you stopped hearing after a while the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. But this morning there was another note threaded through it — metallic, rhythmic. A generator.

Dez heard it the same moment he did. They exchanged a look and pulled the raft into the willows, and went the rest of the way on foot, crouched along the game trail, breath steaming, hearts going.

The cache site sat in a gravel bowl below the falls, where the river flattened out before gathering itself to drop. Their barrels were still there. Their raft trailer was still there. Everything was still there, arranged just as they'd left it — except for the four men standing among it, and the helicopter squatting on the gravel bar with its rotors drooping, and the man in the center wearing a parka so clean and black it looked like a hole cut out of the morning.

Voss.

Ivo had only seen him once, across the water at the portage, when he'd made his offer and Theo had refused it in six words. He remembered thinking Voss looked like a man who did his own dishes — tidy, patient, unremarkable except for the eyes, which never moved the way other people's eyes moved. They swept. They inventoried. They counted things.

Right now they were counting barrels.

"—counted eleven," one of the men was saying as Ivo eased close enough behind a fallen spruce to hear. "Manifest from the landing office says twelve."

"Then one is missing," Voss said, "or one is sunk, or one is upriver with them. Open them. Inventory everything. Leave the site exactly as you found it."

"Leave it?" The man sounded genuinely confused. "Sir, if we know what they've got—"

"Because I don't want them to know we know." Voss crouched beside a barrel, lifted the lid, peered inside. "Desperate people guard their supplies. Hopeful people come get them. I want them hopeful." He let the lid drop. "Seal everything back up. Same dirt on the lids. And pull the fuel lines on the helicopter — we're not leaving it sitting where they can see it's ours."

One of the men laughed. "Make it look like nobody's home."

"Make it look like nobody cares," Voss corrected mildly. "There's a difference. Caring is information."

Ivo lay in the frost with his cheek against the ground and felt the trap close around him — not fast, not violent, just closing, the way ice closes over open water, quietly and completely and without any single moment you could point to and say *there, that's when it happened.*

They weren't going to burn the cache. They were going to leave it there, untouched, a lure. Because Voss had figured out the thing Ivo was only now figuring out himself: the crew couldn't go forward without supplies, and couldn't go back for them without walking into his hands, and either way, winter was coming down the valley like a lid.

* * *

They retreated upstream without speaking. There was nothing to say that the river wasn't already saying.

Back at camp, Theo listened to the report with her arms crossed, staring into the middle distance the way she did when she was running water in her head — reading rapids that weren't in front of her, finding the line through something that had no line.

"He wants us to come down," she said finally. "Or starve."

"So we starve somewhere else," Dez said. "West bench. Survey trail. We walk out."

"With what food?" Ruth asked. Not cruelly. Just factually, which was worse. "We've got nine days of rations here. The cache has the rest. The pass over the west bench is a five-day climb in good conditions, and these aren't good conditions, and we'd be doing it on empty stomachs with a broken-wristed biologist and—" she glanced at Ivo, and didn't finish, which was its own kind of answer.

"And the source is two days upstream," Ivo said.

Everyone looked at him again. He was getting used to that. He was starting to understand it wasn't because he was wrong — it was because he was fifteen and kept being right, and nobody had decided yet whether that was impressive or annoying.

"The whole reason we came up this river," he said. "It's two days. Maybe three if the ice gets worse. The adit, the tailings, the proof that actually matters — it's *right there.*" He pointed upriver, into the gray. "And behind us is a man who leaves our food where we can see it because he thinks hope makes us stupid. So we spend the food we have, we go up, we get what Amos died carrying a map about, and then—" He faltered. He hadn't thought past *then.* "Then we figure out the rest."

Silence. The falls boomed faintly in the distance, two miles of gray air away, constant as a heartbeat.

"It's not a plan," Dez said. "It's a dare."

"It's the only direction he isn't watching," Theo said.

She said it quietly, but it landed in the group like a struck match. Ivo watched each of them take it in — Dez's jaw setting, Ruth's chin lifting, the small shift in the way they stood, from scattered to gathered. He'd seen crews form before, from the outside, back in his stealing days, watching expedition groups in the staging towns: the way strangers became a unit, not through speeches but through decisions, each one a small rivet.

This was a rivet.

"Nine days of food, five people, three days up, and no idea what we find when we get there," Ruth said. "And no way back down that isn't a trap."

"Yes," Theo said.

Ruth almost smiled. "Good. I hate it. Let's go."

* * *

They broke camp in the last hour of light, and Ivo did the arithmetic while he loaded the raft, because arithmetic was the one thing that stayed honest when everything else got slippery.

Nine days of food. Five mouths. Three days up, if the river allowed it, and the river had stopped allowing things a week ago — ice pans riding the current like slow white animals, the channels narrowing, the water dropping as the headwaters froze. Every day colder meant slower water meant longer days meant more food burned keeping warm than moving.

He didn't say any of this out loud. He'd learned that on the streets of Port Calloway: the person who counts the money out loud is the person everyone blames when it runs out. So he counted silently, and stowed the rations himself, and gave himself the smallest share at dinner without anyone noticing, which was easy, because nobody watched the kid eat.

Except somebody did.

"You skipped second helping," Ruth said that night, settling beside him at the fire with her splinted wrist cradled against her chest. Above them the sky had cleared for the first time in days, and the cold came down out of it like a verdict. Stars everywhere, hard and white. "I count things. Occupational hazard."

"I wasn't hungry."

"Liar. You're fifteen. You're always hungry. It's your entire personality." She nudged him with her good elbow. "Next time, take your share. If we're going to freeze together, we do it equitably. That's science."

He stared at the fire. Something in his chest felt cracked open, and he didn't have a word for it, because nobody had ever rationed *for* him before. In Calloway you took what you could carry and slept with your shoes on. Here was a woman with a busted wrist dividing beans fairly, and a pilot who'd patched his jacket sleeve without being asked, and a guide who'd put him on the safety rope every single time they scouted a rapid, no exceptions, no discussion — and the whole arrangement terrified him more than Voss did, because Voss could only take things he'd already lost.

These people could be taken from him while he still had them.

"Ivo." Ruth's voice was quieter now. "Whatever's going on in that head — whatever you're planning to give up so nobody else has to — don't. We've all done the math. We all chose the math. That's what a crew is."

"That's what a family is," he said, before he could stop himself, and then wanted to die, and Ruth just nodded slowly, like he'd confirmed a reading she'd already taken.

"Yeah," she said. "It is."

* * *

On the third night above the falls, the river began to freeze in earnest.

Ivo woke to a silence so complete it hurt — the constant voice of the Serrano muffled, choked, the channel ahead locked edge to edge with young ice that groaned and split and healed itself in the moonlight. The raft was iced to the gravel. The spray deck was frozen stiff as sheet metal. When he breathed, the moisture crackled in his scarf.

He climbed the bank to stand watch, because someone had to, and found Theo already there, a silhouette against the stars, looking upriver.

"How bad?" he asked.

She didn't pretend not to understand. "Bad. Another week of this and the river's done. No raft, no descent, no supply run. Whatever's above us becomes the whole world until spring."

"Spring is seven months."

"Spring is seven months," she agreed.

Below them, miles down, he imagined the cache sitting in its gravel bowl, sealed and patient, Voss's hook baited and waiting. Behind them, the same. Ahead, the source — Amos Grell's map folded in Theo's jacket, the ink lines of a dead man's hand pointing at whatever truth the mountain had been keeping. And around them, closing by degrees, the cold.

"We could still climb out," Ivo said. "West bench. Like you said."

Theo was quiet long enough that he thought she hadn't heard. Then she reached into her jacket and took out the map, and held it open between them in the starlight, and Ivo looked at the hand-drawn contours, the careful annotations in faded pencil — *seep, warm even in winter* — and understood, all at once, why she was really still here.

"Warm even in winter," he repeated.

"Amos marked it twenty years ago." Theo folded the map away. "Water that doesn't freeze means the adit's still open. Still bleeding. Which means the proof is still there." She turned to look at him, and in the dark her face was unreadable, but her voice wasn't. "Voss thinks he's starving us into surrender. He's actually cornered us into the one direction he can't follow."

Ivo looked upriver, into the black throat of the valley where the ice sang and the stars came down to meet the peaks.

Two more days. Nine minus three was six, and six minus two was four, and four days of food for five people was not a number that ended well — unless what waited at the source changed the story entirely.

Above the falls, the river froze. Below the falls, the trap waited. And somewhere in the narrowing dark between them, the headwaters bled their poison into the ice, waiting for someone stubborn enough to come and see.

At dawn, they broke the raft free of the ice and went up.
