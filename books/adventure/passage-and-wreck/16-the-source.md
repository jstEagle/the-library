## 16. The Source

The river got small before it ended.

That was the thing nobody told you about headwaters—that the great Serrano, the one that could break a raft in half and had, the one we'd been fighting for nine days, would dwindle to something you could spit across. By the last morning it was barely a creek, braiding itself through a gravel fan, so shallow Dez had to walk the raft the final kilometer with a rope around his chest, hauling it like a sled.

We left it in a pool the size of a kitchen and climbed.

The map said *follow the cold*. Amos Grell had written it in his crabbed hand along the margin, and I hadn't understood it until now, until the air coming down the valley turned ten degrees colder than the air going up. Cold falling off something. Cold pouring out of the mountain like water runs downhill, because it was water—meltwater, but not glacier melt. Glacier melt has a clean mineral smell, a kind of stone sweetness. This had a different smell. Metallic. Like coins held too long in the hand.

I'd smelled it before. Five years ago, on the worst day of my life.

"Theo." Ruth had stopped twenty meters ahead, kneeling at the edge of a side channel no wider than her boot. She didn't look up. "You need to see this."

I crouched beside her. The channel ran orange. Not the orange of iron-rich spring water, which is common enough in old mining country and means nothing but old wounds. This was brighter, more insistent, the orange of fresh rust, of something actively bleeding. A fine pale sediment drifted in it like smoke in still air, catching the light.

"That's not natural," I said.

"No." Ruth dipped a finger, held it up. The sediment clung to her skin like flour. "That's tailings. Fresh tailings. Finely milled rock waste." She wiped her hand on her thigh, and I watched her not look at me, watched her choose her words the way you choose footholds on bad rock. "Theo, this stuff takes decades to oxidize this color. Decades of exposure. Unless it's coming from a source that's been oxidizing the whole time, sealed away from air, and something recently opened the seal."

"Or the seal was never good to begin with."

"Or that." She stood, wiped her hands again, more thoroughly this time, as if the ground itself were evidence. "Either way, it's coming from upslope. From the adit."

Ivo, behind us, said nothing. He'd been quiet for two days, ever since the checkpoint, ever since the stolen radio had turned from a lifeline into a target on our backs. He carried the radio in his pack like a stone he couldn't put down. But his eyes moved the way they always did—reading everything, keeping everything.

"Then let's go see what the company left behind," I said.

* * *

The valley narrowed to a throat. The walls closed in, granite streaked with the same orange stain, and the cold coming down intensified until I could see my breath even though the sun was fully up. Dez ranged ahead the way he always did, moving with that river-guide economy, reading the ground instead of the water, and after twenty minutes he stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into him.

"Uh," he said. "Theo."

The canyon opened into a bowl. And the bowl was full of the past.

I'd seen wreckage before. Every guide has—old camps, rusted stoves, the occasional plane tail sticking out of a moraine like a flag marking where somebody's luck ran out. This was different. This was a machine that had been swallowed and then, somehow, partially exhaled.

A haul road, collapsed in three places, switchbacking up the far wall. The skeleton of a tram tower, its cable still strung between stumps, sagging like a dead thing's sinew. Ore carts. A dozer with its blade half-buried, its paint gone but its shape unmistakable, the Halvorsen hex still faintly visible on the cab door, ghosted through the rust. And everywhere, under everything, the pale sediment, inches deep in the low spots, drifting when the wind moved, settling on our sleeves, our eyelashes, the map in my pocket.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

It was Ivo who finally spoke, and his voice came out younger than I'd ever heard it. "They said the mine was abandoned before the disaster. That's what the reports said. That Halvorsen pulled out and the collapse happened after, and that's why it wasn't their fault."

"I read the reports," I said. "Twice. In two countries."

"This road was used." Dez had walked to the nearest switchback and was crouching over it. "Look—these aren't erosion cuts. These are tire ruts. Deep ones. And the dozer—" he pointed "—the blade's positioned like somebody parked it mid-shift. You don't abandon a site mid-shift. You abandon it in an orderly fashion, or you run from it."

"Or," Ruth said quietly, "you seal it and walk away and let the mountain keep your secret."

I took out the map. Amos's map, the thing I'd carried through nine days of river and two nights of storm and one checkpoint I still dreamed about, the paper gone soft as cloth from sweat and spray. His lines climbed this bowl exactly, his little crabbed X sitting just below the tram tower's base, and beside the X, in letters he'd pressed so hard they'd nearly torn the paper:

*THE DOOR. THEY SAID IT WAS SEALED. IT WAS NOT SEALED.*

"Amos worked here," I said. "I told you he prospected. What I didn't say is that he prospected for Halvorsen, in this valley, the year before the collapse. He was on the survey crew. He knew where every working was. And when the mountain came down and the company said *natural disaster, sealed adit, nothing to see*, he knew they were lying, because he'd stood in that adit and he knew what was in it."

"And he spent twenty years too scared to say so," Ruth said.

"He had a daughter to think about." I folded the map. My hands were steady. I was surprised by that. "Come on. The X is waiting."

* * *

We found the adit an hour later, and I will carry the sight of it for the rest of my life, however long the truth buys me.

It was a door in the mountain, exactly as Amos had written—timbered, framed in massive rough-cut beams gone silver with age, set into the base of the valley wall where the cold poured out. The opening was maybe three meters high. Someone had sealed it once. I could see the work: a concrete plug poured behind a steel grate, the kind of closure a company does when it wants a site to look properly decommissioned for an inspector's photograph.

The plug was broken. Not crumbled—broken. A hole had been driven through the center of it, roughly a meter across, ragged-edged, and from that hole the water came.

It came the way water comes from a wound. Steady, silent, orange-bright, spilling down a channel cut into the bedrock and feeding the fan we'd followed up the valley. The cold rolled off it in a visible river of its own, chilling the air, and the smell—iron and acid and something older—filled the bowl.

Ruth approached it the way she approached everything, with a scientist's terrible patience. She took samples. She photographed. She measured flow with a method so simple it was almost childlike—a floating leaf, a stopwatch, a measured channel width—and her hands never shook until she finished the arithmetic, and then they shook, just slightly, just once.

"Four hundred liters a minute," she said. "Minimum. Of tailings-contaminated water, straight from a sealed working, into the headwaters of the Serrano." She looked up at me, and her face was the face I'd seen on her the day she told us about the contamination downstream, except worse, because now the impossible thing was standing in front of us with water running out of it. "Theo. The official record says the adit was plugged and verified in the spring after the collapse. Verified dry. If it was verified dry, then either the plug failed catastrophically and no one ever checked—"

"Or it was never dry," I said. "Or it was never properly plugged. Or—"

"Or someone broke it open," Ivo said.

We all looked at him. He stood a little apart from us, at the edge of the tailings drift, and he had his arms crossed the way he did when he was about to say something he'd been sitting on.

"Think about it," he said. "The hole's ragged, right? Blasted or hammered from the inside-out or the outside-in, I can't tell. But here's what I can tell." He crouched, pointed at the base of the plug, at the ground beneath the water channel. "There's fresh tool marks on the grate. Recent. And look—" he picked something out of the sediment with two fingers, careful as a surgeon, and held it up. A drill bit. Carbide-tipped, caked in orange, but the shank was clean where his fingers had wiped it. "This isn't twenty years old. This isn't even five years old. This is months."

The bowl went very quiet. Just the water, and the cold, and the four of us breathing.

"Months," Ruth repeated.

"Someone reopened it," Dez said slowly. "Recently. On purpose."

"Or kept it open," I said. And the thought that had been forming since the orange channel, since the cold, since the first look at the bowl, finished forming and stood up in my chest and turned around to face me. "Ruth. Your samples downstream. The contamination pattern you found at the confluence, the one that matched the disaster. You said it was impossible. You said the source should have been sealed for twenty years."

"Yes."

"What did you actually mean? When you said impossible. Say it precisely."

She was quiet for a moment. Ruth was always quiet before precision; it was one of the things I'd come to trust about her.

"I meant," she said, "that the signature we found downstream wasn't legacy contamination. Legacy contamination fades, spreads, dilutes, gets buried. What we found was concentrated, fresh, and localized to a single input point upstream. I told you it was as if the disaster was still happening." She looked at the adit. "I just didn't believe myself."

"It is still happening," I said. "It never stopped. That's the whole point. That's what Amos knew, and what he was too scared to say, and what the company has spent twenty years and two countries' worth of lawyers making sure no one could prove."

I walked to the plug. I put my hand flat against the broken concrete, and the cold came through it into my palm, and I let it.

Because here was the thing I had never said out loud, not to the courts, not to the inquiry, not to the reporters who'd camped outside my rented room in the bad year, not even to myself in the dark hours when I rehearsed the testimony one more time: I had never believed it was an accident. Not the collapse, and not the aftermath.

I had been leading a client party through the lower Vale when the mountain let go. I'd reported, in the weeks before, seepage in the old workings—orange water, the same orange, the same smell. I'd reported it to the regional office, which reported it to Halvorsen, which owned the mineral rights under the reserve through a subsidiary nobody could quite trace. And when the collapse came and the inquiries started, my seepage reports became, in the company's telling, evidence of *my* negligence—evidence I'd known the ground was unstable and led clients in anyway. My warning was turned into my confession. I have never in my life been so precisely, so elegantly robbed.

But I'd always thought the robbery was opportunistic. A company covering its ass after bad luck. What I was looking at now was something else.

"Ruth," I said. "The buyout. Tell them about the buyout."

She didn't ask which one. She'd done the research on the river, nights, by headlamp, while Ivo slept and Dez kept watch. "After the collapse, Halvorsen's subsidiary sold its mineral claims in the Vale to the government for an emergency settlement—full book value, plus remediation liability transfer. The valuation was based on the site being a closed, sealed, stable legacy operation." She paused. "If the adit was leaking then—actively, massively leaking—the valuation collapses. The settlement collapses. The liability stays with Halvorsen."

"And if the disaster itself was engineered," I said, "if the seepage I reported was the mountain telling the truth and the company decided the truth was expensive—"

"Then the collapse wasn't bad luck," Dez said. His voice had gone flat, the flatness he got in big water, when the wave ahead is bigger than the raft. "It was a business decision."

"Took a buyout," Ivo said, "and needed the site sealed and silent to get it. And needed it to stay sealed and silent ever after. And when Amos started asking questions—" He stopped.

"When Amos started asking questions," I finished, "they threatened his daughter. And he ran, and he kept running for twenty years, and he kept a map to the one piece of proof they couldn't allow to exist. And now we're standing on it."

I turned back to the adit. The water ran. The cold poured out of the mountain like a held breath finally released, twenty years long.

"Sample everything," I said. "Photograph everything. The plug, the hole, the tool marks, the drill bit, the tram, the dozer with the hex on the door, the tire ruts on the haul road. Ruth, I want flow rates and chemistry. Ivo—" he was already pulling the camera, already moving, and for a second he looked less like a thief and more like exactly what we needed. "Dez, the marker. The Halvorsen marker from the canyon. Did you bring it?"

He patted his pack. We'd pried it off a stake three days ago, down in the portage, a survey marker with a date stamped on it that predated the official timeline by four years—four years of operation the company had sworn never happened. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"Then we're done here," I said. "We have what we came for."

* * *

But I wasn't done. I stood at the adit one more minute while the others worked, and I let myself feel it—not the vindication, which would keep, but the fury, which I'd been carrying so long I'd mistaken it for a skeleton, something structural, something that was just part of how I stood up.

Five years. Five years of trail work under other people's names on the permits, of towns that got too small the moment anyone recognized me, of Kestrel's Landing and its careful gray weather and its careful gray life. Five years of being the woman who killed four climbers, when the truth was that I was the woman who *warned* them, whose warning was buried under the same rubble as the climbers themselves, buried deliberately, by men in clean boots who had never in their lives been cold.

The mountain had kept the receipts. The mountain had kept them for twenty years, running orange and cold out of a broken door, waiting for someone stubborn enough to climb to the source.

"Hey." Dez, beside me. He didn't look at me; he watched the valley mouth, the way we'd come, the habit of nine days of being hunted. "For what it's worth. I always figured you were guilty. When you hired me. Figured I didn't care—work's work." A pause. "I don't figure that anymore."

"I know," I said.

"And Theo? The radio." He nodded at Ivo's pack, where the stolen unit rode. "It's been crackling for ten minutes. Same interval. Someone's pinging a repeater, trying to raise a unit that isn't answering."

The cold came out of the mountain. The water ran orange. And down the valley we had climbed, somewhere below the tram tower and the dead haul road and the gravel fan where the Serrano began, something was answering.

"How long to the valley mouth?" I asked.

"At our pace? Half a day." Dez finally looked at me. "At theirs? Less."

I took one last look at the adit—at the proof, at the door in the mountain that had finally, after twenty years, been opened from the outside by the right people—and turned downhill.

"Then we don't give them half a day," I said. "Break camp. We're running."
