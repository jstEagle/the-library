## 17. Ambush at the Adit

I heard the helicopter before I saw it, a wasp-drone buzzing up the throat of the valley, and my first thought—absurd, professional, reflexive—was *contamination plume, flight path too low, someone's going to get cited.*

My second thought was: *they found us.*

We'd been at the adit for six hours. Six hours of Theo moving through the collapsed timber framing like a woman reading her own autopsy, and Dez hauling the pry bar from cache to seam, and Ivo perched on the spoil heap with binoculars because he had the sharpest eyes of any of us and the least sense of self-preservation. And me on my knees in the shallows with sample vials lined up like soldiers, doing the only thing I knew how to do when the world went wrong: measure it.

The water coming out of that mountain was wrong. Not subtly wrong. Not *elevated metals consistent with legacy mining* wrong. It was bleeding copper and arsenic and something I hadn't been able to identify yet—a chelating agent, industrial-grade, the kind used to strip ore fast and cheap—the kind that does not occur in nature and does not persist for five years unless someone is still feeding it into the system.

Five years. The disaster was five years old. Sealed sites don't bleed like this. Sealed sites are capped, grouted, monitored. This one had been *fed*.

I'd known it since the second vial. I'd said it out loud an hour ago, standing in front of the adit's rusted gate while Theo stared at the Halvorsen stamp on the beam above her head, and she'd said, very quietly, "Say it again." So I did. And then Ivo whistled from the spoil heap and said the word none of us wanted to hear.

"Chopper."

* * *

We scattered the way we'd drilled it, which is to say badly, because we'd only drilled it twice and one of those times Dez had been drunk on celebration and the other time Ivo had been arguing about sightlines. But we scattered. Theo pulled us behind the fallen gantry crane fifty meters downstream, its lattice giving us shadow and gaps to shoot through, and I crouched with my back against cold iron and my samples hugged to my chest like a firstborn, and I hated myself a little for that—for clutching glass vials while a helicopter circled—but the vials were the whole point. The vials were why we'd come. The vials were the only version of me that would ever be allowed to exist again if we got out of here.

Ruth Okafor, disgraced park biologist. That was the headline they'd run. *Falsified data, says agency.* I hadn't falsified anything. I'd reported data someone senior didn't want reported, and when I wouldn't retract it, they made me the story instead of the numbers. Five years watching other people do the work I loved, knowing what I knew, holding a truth so heavy I'd started to wonder if I'd invented it.

Then I'd tested the Serrano water two hundred kilometers downstream and watched my own ghost come swimming back up the sample tube.

So yes. I held the vials. Sue me.

The helicopter set down on the gravel bar upstream of the adit, rotors kicking grit into the light. Four figures came out of it in matching gray shells, clean and unhurried, rifles slung but visible. The fifth figure wore no rifle and walked like the ground owed him money.

Voss.

I'd never seen him in person. I'd seen his name on permits, on the remediation reports I'd audited after the collapse, on the letterhead of the foundation that had funded half the research station where I used to work. Martin Voss, site chief, Halvorsen Extraction, philanthropist, man whose smile in press photos reached all the way to his eyes because his eyes had never once been told the truth.

He stopped ten meters from our cover and raised both hands, palms out, a man greeting old friends across a churchyard.

"Theo," he called. "It's been a long time."

Beside me, Theo went very still. Not frozen-still. Coiled-still. I'd learned the difference on the river: frozen is fear, coiled is math. She was doing arithmetic with distances and current and the angle of the sun off the gravel, and I realized with a lurch that she'd already decided how this would go if it went bad, and that her plan probably ended with most of us wet and one of us shot.

"Don't answer him," I breathed.

"I have to," she breathed back. "He'll talk whether I do or not. Better he talks to a face."

And that's when I remembered the recorder.

* * *

I carry a field recorder everywhere. Old habit—voice memos in the field, species call documentation, the muttered observations that become field notes later. It lived in the inner pocket of my shell jacket, battery at eighty percent, and until this moment it had been recording nothing but my own breathing and the river for three days.

I eased it out. Switched from standby to record. The little red light I covered with my thumb.

Dez saw. He was crouched on my left, one hand pressed flat against his thigh where the old rafting injury ached before weather, and his eyes flicked to the recorder, then to me, then gave the smallest nod I've ever seen a human being give. *Do it,* the nod said. *Get everything.*

Ivo, wedged under the crane's crossbeam, mouthed: *what are you doing?*

*Evidence,* I mouthed back.

His face changed. Thirteen years old and he understood instantly what I meant—that words spoken in anger by powerful men are worth more than any assay, that a confession is a mineral you can't fake. He shifted his weight, silent as a cat, putting himself between me and the open ground. A stowaway thief protecting a scientist with a voice recorder. My chest did something complicated.

Upstream, Voss waited. He was patient. That was the worst part—he wasn't posturing, wasn't rushing. He stood in the wind off the glacier like a man who had paid for this view and intended to enjoy it.

"Theo," he tried again. "I know you can hear me. I'm not here for your crew. I'm here for you."

Theo stepped out.

God help me, she just stepped out from behind the crane into the open, hands loose at her sides, and stood on the gravel bar with the adit gaping black behind her shoulder like a wound in the mountain. Dez swore under his breath. I grabbed air.

"There she is," Voss said warmly. "You look well. Prison agrees with some people; exile agrees with others."

"You're trespassing," Theo said. Her voice carried flat and clear down the valley. "This is closed reserve land."

"So is every step you've taken since Kestrel's Landing. Let's not litigate each other's paperwork." He took a few steps closer, boots crunching, and I could see his face now, and the press photos hadn't lied—the warmth was real, or a flawless forgery of it. "Here's what happens next, and I'd like you to hear all of it before you decide anything. Because I'm offering you the deal of your life, and I only offer once."

"We don't want your deal."

"Your crew might." His gaze slid past her, toward the crane, toward us, and I felt it like a searchlight. "A washed-out pilot with a bum knee and a suspended license. A biologist the whole profession blacklisted. And—" a pause, delicately cruel, "—a minor. Traveling with no guardian, across international lines, into a restricted zone. I wonder what a court would make of that."

Ivo made a small sound beside me. I put my hand on his shoulder without looking. Under my palm he was shaking—not with fear, I realized. With fury.

"What do you want, Voss?" Theo said.

"The map. The samples. Everything Amos Grell carried and everything you've collected since. And then silence, permanent and total, from all four of you." He smiled. "In exchange: full reinstatement. Certified, licensed, cleared record, both countries. Your name in the guide registry again. Your reputation restored with interest. You'd lead trips within the year. You'd be *Theo Nakamura* again instead of a cautionary tale."

The wind moved through the valley. Somewhere above the adit, a chunk of ice calved off the serac field and boomed down the rockface, and nobody flinched except me, and I only flinched because I was recording and I needed my hands steady.

"And if I refuse?" Theo asked.

Voss sighed, genuinely regretful, a man disappointed by weather. "Then the same thing happens that happened five years ago. Except this time there's no trial, no inquiry, no theater. You're four people in a closed reserve in winter, armed trespassers, and my team has authorization I won't describe in detail. Accidents happen in the backcountry, Theo. You of all people know that. You built a career on them."

There it is, I thought. There it is, there it is, say more, say more—

"You mean like the accident you arranged?" Theo's voice didn't rise. It dropped. "I stood in that tunnel, Voss. Before the collapse. I saw the shoring cut away. I saw the charge holes drilled into the pillar nobody would explain. You didn't have an accident. You had a demolition, and eleven people died so Halvorsen could force the buyout and write the losses off as tragedy."

For one unguarded second—one half-second—Martin Voss's face did something I will remember until I die. It didn't crumble. It *settled*. Like a man taking off a coat he'd been wearing too long.

"You always were quick," he said quietly. "Yes. Fine. For the record, since you insist: the pillar was compromised. The vein ran through it. Eleven deaths versus a company, Theo. Versus four hundred jobs, a town, a region. I made the calculation anyone competent would make. The only mistake was leaving a witness with a conscience and a good memory." He glanced at the adit behind her. "And now you've gone and found the source. Which means the calculation has to be made again."

Behind the crane, my thumb was shaking on the recorder and tears were running down my face and I didn't make a single sound, because Ruth Okafor had spent five years being told the numbers were wrong, and here was a man saying them right, aloud, on tape, in his own voice, with his own name attached.

*Versus a company.* Say it again. Please. Say it again.

But he didn't need to say it again. Once was enough. Once, timestamped, in his voice, was a verdict.

"That's enough, Mr. Voss," said one of the gray shells, the tall one, uneasy. "You said no admissions—"

"It's fine," Voss said mildly. "No one's listening."

I was listening. I was the only person in the world listening, and I loved him for it.

"The map," Voss said, extending his hand toward Theo as if concluding a business meeting. "Last chance at the life you lost."

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, and I've assembled them since from what each of us saw, because no one sees an ambush from inside it.

Theo looked at Voss's hand. Then she said, "No," and turned her back on him—turned her *back*, the purest contempt I've ever witnessed—and started walking toward us.

"Tase her," Voss said, bored, and the world broke.

The tall gray shell raised something that wasn't a rifle after all, and Dez was already moving—I didn't see him decide, he was simply in motion, the way he moved on the water, reading the drop before it came—and he shouted "DOWN!" and shoved Ivo flat under the crane as the crack of the taser discharge snapped across the gravel.

It missed Theo. It hit Dez.

He was mid-stride, exposed, the only human being standing upright in the whole valley, and the barbs took him high in the back and the current dropped him like a cut puppet. He hit the rocks face-first and didn't catch himself, and I heard the sound his head made against stone, and I will hear it for the rest of my life.

Everything after that was noise and motion. Theo turned and charged Voss—not to reach him, to reach Dez—and the gray shells swung their rifles up and Theo screamed "THE ADIT'S STILL BLEEDING AND YOU ALL KNOW IT NOW" and one of them hesitated, actually hesitated, the tall one, the one who'd warned about admissions, his barrel drifting off-line for one crucial second.

That second saved us. Because Ivo came up from under the crane throwing rocks.

Not heroically. Desperately, wildly, a thirteen-year-old's arm and a lifetime of dodging bigger kids, chunks of granite arcing out of our cover, and one caught the nearest shooter square in the jaw and staggered him into his partner, and their muzzles crossed and tangled, and Theo slid the last five meters on gravel and got her hands under Dez's shoulders and hauled.

"RUTH!" she roared. "SAMPLES AND HIM, GO!"

I went. I don't remember deciding. I was simply running, vials jammed inside my jacket, recorder clutched in my fist with the red light still burning under my thumb, sliding down the bank to the waterline where Theo dragged Dez's dead weight, blood sheeting from his scalp into the shallows, his breathing ragged and snoring-wet, the sound a brain makes when it's been shaken hard.

"He's breathing," Theo gasped. "Ivo! Raft! NOW!"

Ivo was already sprinting for the boats, and the last thing I saw over my shoulder as we shoved off into the current was Martin Voss standing on the gravel bar with his hand raised—not to shoot, to *stop* his team, the tall one arguing with him, gesturing at the unconscious man in the water, at the blood, at the line they'd just crossed—and Voss's face turning slowly toward the adit, toward the mountain, with the expression of a man hearing, very late, the sound of his own voice.

They didn't follow into the rapids. Not yet. But the helicopter lifted off and shadowed us downriver, patient as weather, and I knelt over Dez in the pitching bow with his blood on my hands and the recorder pressed against my sternum, and I thought:

*Terror is just evidence that hasn't been filed yet.*

"Hold on," I told him, though he couldn't hear. "Hold on. We've got him. We've got all of him."

Ahead, the river bent toward the falls, white and roaring, and the meltwater was rising.
