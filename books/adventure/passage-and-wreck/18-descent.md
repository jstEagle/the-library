## 18. Descent

The bullet had gone through the meat of my left shoulder and out the back, which Ruth said later was the only lucky thing about it. Clean through. No fragments to hunt for. She splinted me with a paddle shaft and a strip of her shirt while we crouched behind the adit's collapsed timber, and I remember thinking that her hands were steadier than mine, and that I was supposed to be the one with steady hands. That was my whole job description. Steady hands on the sticks.

"Can you row?" Theo asked.

She was crouched in front of me with the river roaring behind her and Voss's team somewhere above us in the trees, and she asked it like she was asking if I wanted coffee. No panic in her voice. I'd have found that insulting a week ago. Now it was the only thing keeping me upright.

"I can row," I said.

"You can't," Ruth said.

"I can row," I said again, and Theo looked at me for a long second, and whatever she saw there made her nod once and turn away to start breaking camp at a speed that would've gotten us all killed anywhere but here.

Because here was the arithmetic nobody said out loud: we were eleven miles from the lower landing by water, the melt had come early and hard, and Halvorsen's crew had vehicles and radios and no reason at all to let four witnesses reach the coast. The samples were in dry bags lashed under my seat. The recording was in Ruth's jacket, against her chest, where she'd been carrying it like a second heartbeat since the ambush. And I was bleeding into a wetsuit that was doing a terrible job of hiding it.

Ivo appeared out of the willows with his face gray and his eyes too bright. "Boat's clear. They didn't touch it."

"They didn't see it," Theo corrected.

"They didn't touch it," Ivo insisted, and climbed in.

* * *

The first three miles were the kind of water I'd have charged money for. Big, clean, pushy wave trains down the center of a granite chute, the Serrano running high and mean with snowmelt, every hole hungry. Under normal circumstances I'd have been grinning. My normal circumstances were five years and one bad season behind me.

Theo took the bow calls and I ran the boat off her voice, because reading water one-armed is a different sport entirely. Left stroke. Hard left. Right side only. Her words came flat and even, spaced like metronome clicks, and I understood suddenly why people had followed this woman into mountains before the world decided she'd killed some of them. There was a sound her voice made when everything was going wrong — a kind of low calm, like bedrock under floodwater — and you could build a life on it if you weren't careful.

We came through the big rapid sideways, half-surfing a hole the size of a kitchen, and Ivo whooped, actually whooped, and Ruth grabbed his collar and hauled him back down without looking up from the dry bag she was checking for the fourth time.

"Blood's still flowing," she told me over the noise. "That's the good news. The bad news is you're going to be cold."

I was already cold. The shoulder had gone past pain into something electric and distant, and I knew what that meant, and I knew Ruth knew, and we both agreed silently not to say it.

* * *

The gorge narrowed after that. Walls came in close enough that the sky shrank to a blue ribbon overhead, and the river dropped into a sequence of ledges that hadn't existed on any map Amos Grell had drawn, because maps don't record what meltwater does to a canyon in five years.

"We should scout," Ruth said.

"We should not stop," Theo said.

Those two sentences contained our entire situation. Stopping meant Voss's team closing the gap; they'd lost us in the trees above the adit, but radio silence doesn't last forever and men with helicopters don't stay lost. Not stopping meant running blind water with a wounded pilot and a broken-wristed biologist and a kid who'd never seen anything bigger than a harbor.

Theo stood up in the raft — actually stood, braced against the frame, scanning ahead — and I watched her do the thing guides do, the thing they never admit to doing, which is gamble. Read the water from twenty feet up, pick a line, commit before commitment is provable.

"Right of the tongue," she said. "Then hard left past the pour-over. Dez, I need your right arm only. Ivo, ballast low-center. Ruth—"

"I know," Ruth said. "Samples."

"Samples," Theo agreed, and that single word carried the whole weight of why we were doing this instead of hiding in the willows until winter. Because the water in those bags was the confession Halvorsen had spent five years and probably several fortunes burying. Because the recording in Ruth's jacket had Voss saying *the timeline was always ours to write* in a voice he'd have denied in any court. Because evidence that stays in a canyon is just litter.

The ledge dropped us eight feet into a boiling trough and the world went white and then green and then upside down briefly, and my shoulder screamed in a way that felt almost clarifying, and then we were through, spinning, everyone aboard, everyone breathing.

"Nice call," I said.

"It wasn't a call," Theo said. "It was a prayer with paddles."

I laughed and immediately regretted it.

* * *

We eddied out at the confluence where the north fork came in, and Theo called a ninety-second halt — ninety seconds, timed on her watch, no discussion — to get food and water into people. This is another guide trick I recognized from my old life: you don't ask if people are tired or scared or hurt. You put calories in them and move. Grief and fear both travel lighter on a full stomach.

Ruth rewrapped my shoulder with tape from the first aid kit, quick and brutal, and pressed two pills into my palm. "Not the good ones. You need your reflexes."

"How bad?"

"You'll live to testify." She said it lightly, but her hands paused on the tape, and I saw her look downstream toward the falls we couldn't hear yet but all knew were coming. "Dez. If it comes down to the bags or you—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"Somebody has to."

"Theo will finish it differently," I said, and Ruth looked at me, and then looked at Theo standing watch at the eddy line with the current pulling at her boots, and didn't argue.

Ivo came back from the rock point with news delivered in his particular style, which was to state the worst fact first and let you assemble the rest. "Smoke. Two klicks back up the trail on the left bank. Camp smoke, not signal smoke."

"Voss?"

"Or rangers."

"Either way," Theo said, climbing back in, "we're done being invisible. Paddle."

* * *

The falls announced themselves as a vibration in the hull before we heard them, a deep sub-bass tremor coming up through the water itself, and my stomach dropped ten years into the past to a day on a different river when I'd misread exactly this kind of tremor and a man named Calloway had gone into a hydraulic and not come out. That was the wreck part of my own private passage-and-wreck, the story I never told anyone, the reason a washed-out pilot says yes to a disgraced guide's insane errand — because the river keeps its receipts, and so do I.

We scouted this one. Even Theo wouldn't run blind at the biggest drop on the river with the melt peaking. We tied off in an eddy below a slab of granite and climbed, and there it was: the Serrano folding over a shelf twice the height of a man, the whole river, all of it, funneling into a slot maybe twelve feet wide, the landing zone below buried in a rooster tail of spray and rebound.

"Portage," Ruth said.

"Portage," I agreed.

"There's no trail," Ivo said.

"There's a trail," Theo said, and pointed at a seam in the cliff face where a ledge ran along the wall above the drop, maybe eighteen inches wide, slick with spray. Old miners had cut it once, judging by the drill marks. It went around the falls or it went nowhere.

We ran the boats light — gear portaged separately in loads, which cost us forty minutes we did not have — and then came the part I'll be honest about: I don't remember most of the lining. I remember the ledge under my boots, wet stone the color of bone. I remember Ivo carrying the sample bags strapped to his chest like a bomb suit, moving along that ledge with the falls thundering six feet below him, small and deliberate and absolutely fearless in the way only kids and fools are fearless. I remember Ruth's face when a strap slipped and the second raft swung out over the drop and caught, held, held, held on a piton some dead miner had hammered home a century ago.

And I remember Theo, last across, unclipping the raft with one hand while the other stayed locked on the line, and the look she gave the river when it was done — not triumph. Apology, almost. Like she was sorry to be hurrying past something sacred.

Then we were below the falls, in the last stretch, and the character of the country changed. The walls fell away. Willows and gravel bars. The valley opened its hand, and somewhere down there, past the braids and the logjams, was the lower landing, and past that the road, and past that Port Calloway and regulators and press and the end of everything we'd been for three weeks.

Voss knew it too. That was the thing I kept circling back to. He didn't need to catch us anymore. He needed to slow us down until dark, and then it was over, because none of us could run this water at night and he knew it and we knew it.

Which is why the helicopter, when it came, didn't come for us.

It came for the river.

* * *

We heard it first as a change in the air pressure, a flutter in the willows, and then it swung around the bend upstream of us flying low, a Halvorsen bird, corporate blue, and I watched Theo's whole body go rigid beside me in the raft.

"What are they—" Ivo started.

The charges went off a half-mile up.

We heard them as three flat cracks rolling down the canyon, and then we heard the river answer, and I have run a lot of water and I have never heard a river make that sound — a rising, gathering roar, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. They'd blown the logjam at the narrows. Every stick of timber the Serrano had been holding for years, released at once, sent down the valley directly through the stretch we were floating.

"They're flushing us," Ruth said, and her voice was pure scientific horror, which from Ruth is the same as screaming. "They're using the river as a weapon."

"Time to the surge?" Theo asked me, and only me, because I was the one whose job it was to know things like that.

"Twenty minutes," I said. "Maybe fifteen. It'll move faster than us in the braids — we can't outrun it in open water." I looked at the banks, the willow flats, the gravel. "But we don't have to outrun it. We have to not be in it. High ground, tie off, let it pass, then run the flushed channel behind it."

"That costs us two hours."

"That costs us nothing," I said, "if they think it costs us everything. They'll assume the surge got us. Let them assume it."

Theo looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her weigh it — the samples, the recording, the deadline inside her own body that was my shoulder going septic by morning — and then she nodded and drove us for the left bank.

We hauled out on a bench above the willows with minutes to spare. Ivo got lines on three trees and Ruth got me sitting with my feet up and my shoulder packed with river-cold mud because Ruth said it would help and I was past arguing. And then the surge came through.

I want to describe it properly, because nobody who wasn't there will ever believe it. The river stood up. That's the only way to say it. A brown wall of water and timber came down the valley maybe nine feet tall, whole trees cartwheeling end over end, and the sound was not a sound, it was a condition, it was the air itself being torn. Our takeout beach vanished. The willows where we'd scouted an hour earlier went under and stayed under. For eleven minutes the Serrano stopped being a river and became a demolition, and then, gradually, it remembered what it was and settled back into its banks, higher and browner and angrier, dragging its new cargo of wreckage seaward.

Nobody spoke for a while.

"They'll have a man watching the lower landing," Theo finally said. "When we don't come through, they'll report us lost. Which buys us the night approach." She turned and looked at each of us in turn — Ruth with her splinted wrist cradled against her chest, Ivo soaked and shaking and grinning anyway, me leaking quietly into the mud — and something crossed her face that I'd never seen on a trip leader before, because I'd never been one of the crew that earned it.

"Amos picked well," she said, which I understood was as close as she got to *thank you*, and possibly as close as she got to *I love you*, though I didn't understand that part yet. That understanding was waiting for me about a mile downstream, in the last hour of light, when we rounded the final bend and Ivo, who had the sharpest eyes and the least sense of self-preservation, stood straight up in the raft and pointed.

"Lights," he said. "At the landing. That's not Halvorsen blue."

Theo raised her binoculars, and I watched her go very still in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

"It's a plane on floats," she said slowly. "Charter paint scheme. And there's a woman on the dock talking to the ranger, and she's holding up a piece of paper like it's a warrant, and—" She lowered the glasses. Her voice came out strange, stripped down to the studs.

"It's Mara. Mara Grell just walked into the middle of this."
