## 19. The Handoff

Mara had been at the lower landing since before dawn, and the fog hadn't lifted, and neither had she.

She stood on the gravel shelf where the Serrano flattened out and went wide and slow, the way rivers do when they've stopped fighting. The charter pilot had wanted to drop her at Port Calloway and be done with it—*no one goes upriver this time of year, lady*—but money talked, and her father's insurance settlement talked louder. He'd set her down on a mudbank forty kilometers from anywhere, told her he'd come back in three days or not at all, and left her with a satellite phone that had exactly two numbers programmed into it: his dispatch office, and the coast guard.

The third number was hers to find.

She'd spent the night in the plane's borrowed camp chair, wrapped in a survey tarp, watching the river for shapes that weren't logs. Every hour or so she checked the satphone. Nothing. The last message from Theo Nakamura's burner had come two days ago: *Lower landing. Three days. We're coming down fast.*

*We.* Not *I*. Mara had turned that word over all night like a stone with something underneath it.

Her father had been a *we* person once. She had photographs to prove it—Amos Grell grinning on somebody's porch, Amos with an arm around men whose names Mara had never been allowed to ask about, Amos holding a baby who was her against a backdrop of tent canvas and morning light. Then the photographs stopped. Then the letters got shorter. Then the letters stopped, and there was just the money order every month, always the same amount, always postmarked from a different town, as if he were moving away from her one postal code at a time.

She'd built a whole life out of that absence. Harbor cartographer. Good at edges, at boundaries, at drawing the line where land gave up and water began. She could chart a coastline to the meter and never once have to say what any of it meant to her.

Then a letter had arrived from a stranger—a disgraced guide named Theo Nakamura, saying her father was dying and had given her something to deliver, and would Mara meet her, please, because a promise was a promise.

Mara had refused. Had read the letter four times and burned it in the sink of her apartment over the chandlery, and then had gone digging through the box of her father's things she'd sworn never to open, and found the letters she'd never been sent—the ones Halvorsen Extraction had intercepted, the ones explaining why Amos Grell had chosen exile over his daughter's safety—and by the time she finished reading them, the sink was cold and the ash was down the drain and she was already calling air charters.

Now the fog was burning off in ragged sheets, and the river ran silver-brown and patient, and somewhere upstream, if the letter-writer was worth anything at all, five people were coming down through water that had already tried to kill them once.

Mara checked the satphone again. Two bars. One message.

She played it standing very still, the way you do when you already know what a thing is going to cost you.

*"This is Nakamura. We're past the falls. Dez took a bullet at the adit—he's stable, Ruth's keeping him together. Voss's people are behind us but we lost them in the gorge. I have your father's map. I have—" a pause, wind noise, a voice in the background, Ivo's, young and urgent— "I have everything else too. The landing. Tomorrow. Tell your pilot."*

Click.

Mara stood there a long moment with the phone against her ear after the dead tone, listening to nothing, which was more or less what she'd been doing for twenty-six years.

Then she went to wake the pilot.

* * *

They came downriver at 4:40 in the afternoon, and Mara knew them before she could make out faces, because the raft was wrong.

It was a commercial-grade expedition rig, the kind outfitters bought new and retired scarred, except this one looked like it had been through a war and lost most of it. The starboard tube rode low, patched with what looked like duct tape and prayer. Gear was lashed everywhere in no system she recognized. A kid stood at the bow—sixteen, maybe seventeen, thin as a piling, pointing at the landing with an arm that didn't stop moving even when the raft straightened out.

And at the stern oars, a woman with gray coming in at her temples and shoulders like someone who'd spent her life holding things up, pulling strokes so clean they barely made a sound. The raft came off the current and onto the flat water below the landing like it had been steered there by thought alone.

Mara walked down the gravel shelf. Her legs felt like they belonged to someone taller.

The raft ground in against the stones. The kid jumped out first and grabbed the bowline, and a broad-shouldered man with a bandaged arm sat propped against the gear pile, gray-faced, smiling anyway. A woman with mud to the knees and a field kit strapped across her chest started handing things ashore with brisk, competent movements, like unloading was a form of medicine.

Theo Nakamura stepped out last, into maybe eight inches of water, and looked up at Mara, and did not smile, and did not look away.

Up close she was younger than her reputation and older than her face. There was a scrape healing along her jaw and a tiredness in her eyes that wasn't the ordinary kind—it was the kind you earn.

"You're her," Mara said. Stupidly. "You're the guide."

"I'm the guide." Theo held out a hand, and Mara took it, and the grip was dry and hard and brief. "I'm sorry it took this long."

"It took five years," Mara said. "Since his first letter. Since—" She stopped. Her throat had closed around something. "He wrote me every month. Did you know that? Before. Before whatever happened between him and the company. Every month, and then nothing, and I thought—"

"I know what you thought." Theo's voice was quiet. "I read his letters. All of them. He kept copies. He kept copies of everything—that was the problem, in the end. That was the whole problem."

Behind her, the kid—Ivo, it had to be—was watching Mara with open, unembarrassed curiosity, the way kids watch weather. The wounded man coughed once, wetly, and the biologist woman shot Mara a look that said *he needs a hospital inside twelve hours, so let's not do the whole scene on the beach.*

"Is he—" Mara couldn't finish it.

"He died in my kitchen," Theo said. "In Kestrel's Landing. Four months ago. It was fast. He was lucid at the end, and he was angry, and he was—" she paused, choosing, "—he was proud of you. He said you drew coastlines better than he ever drew mountains. He said it like it was the finest thing he knew how to say."

Mara's vision blurred. She stood on the gravel with river water soaking into her boots and let it happen, because twenty-six years of not crying in front of strangers seemed suddenly like a rule written by someone who'd never met her father.

When she could see again, Theo was holding out a roll of oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, worn soft at the edges from years of a pocket.

"His map," Theo said. "The Serrano headwaters. He drew it himself, over eleven seasons. It's the reason we're all still alive, and it's the reason some of us almost weren't. He asked me to put it in your hands, and I'm putting it in your hands."

Mara took it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"There's more," Theo said. "There's what the map led to. The adit above the falls—Halvorsen sealed it themselves, before the collapse, before the disaster, before they blamed me for it. They sabotaged their own workings to force a buyout of the independent claims, and the tailings have been bleeding into the source ever since. Your father found the first evidence nine years ago. That's why they ran him off. That's why he stayed away from you—they threatened you, Mara. You were the leverage. He chose the distance so you'd never be close enough to hurt."

The words landed one at a time, each with its own small detonation. Mara looked down at the oilcloth roll in her hands, at her father's careful handwriting visible where the cloth had frayed—*Serrano R., headwater bench, do not trust the company survey*—and understood, finally, completely, what the monthly money orders had been. Not guilt. Not abandonment.

A ransom paid in absence.

"He let me hate him," she said. "For twenty years. He let me hate him so I'd be safe hating him instead of loving him somewhere they could reach."

"Yes," Theo said simply. "And I think it cost him more than the dying did."

* * *

They got Dez up to the plane on a litter improvised from a thwart and two paddles, Ruth directing, Ivo carrying the heavy end without being asked twice. The pilot radioed ahead to Port Calloway for a medevac meet and got a response that made his face go strange: there were already people at the Calloway airstrip asking questions. Men in clean boots. A woman with a corporate lanyard who claimed to be waiting on a fishing party.

Voss's people. Ahead of them now, not behind.

Mara stood by the plane's door with the map roll in her jacket and watched Theo take this in. Watched her go very still, the way water goes still right before it drops.

"They'll have lawyers at the hospital," Mara said. "They'll have injunctions. They'll say the samples are contaminated, the recording is inadmissible, the witnesses are criminals—you especially. A blacklisted guide with a grudge. They've done it before. They did it to you."

"They did it to me," Theo agreed.

"So we don't play it their way." Mara heard her own voice come out level, and was surprised by it, and decided to keep it. "My father spent nine years being quiet because quiet kept me safe. I'm done being the reason good people stay silent. I'm a harbor cartographer. Do you know what that means? I know every customs officer, every harbormaster, every press boat skipper and salvage diver in Port Calloway. I know which regulators actually read their mail. And I know the tide tables—which means I know exactly when the media pontoon can reach the public dock and when Halvorsen's security launches can't follow without making a spectacle."

Theo looked at her for a long moment. Something shifted in her face—not gratitude, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman finding out the promise she'd been carrying had grown hands of its own.

"You don't have to do this," Theo said. "You got the map. The promise is kept. You could take the plane, take your father's ashes' worth of peace, and disappear. Nobody would blame you."

"My father disappeared for twenty years to protect me," Mara said. "It bought him a kitchen floor in a border town and a stranger's promise. I'm not doing it again. Whatever happens next happens with all of us in the frame. That's the only way it holds."

From the litter, Dez raised his good arm, weak as a fledgling. "For the record," he rasped, "I vote with the scary daughter."

"Seconded," said Ivo.

Ruth just nodded, already repacking her sample kit, because some people's yes is a sealed container.

Theo exhaled, long and slow, like a rope paying out. "All right," she said. "Calloway. Public dock. High tide tomorrow, 0940." She looked at Mara. "You get us the audience. We bring them the truth."

"And then?" Ivo asked.

"And then," Theo said, "we stop running."

The pilot fired up the engine. The rotors began to turn, flattening the grass, bending the river's surface into rings. Mara climbed in last, the map roll pressed flat against her ribs under her jacket, over her heart, where twenty years of unanswered letters had finally, finally gotten their reply.

Below them, as the plane lifted, the Serrano unspooled toward the sea—muddy, wounded, carrying its poison quietly toward everything downstream.

By this time tomorrow, she thought, it wouldn't be carrying anything quietly ever again.
