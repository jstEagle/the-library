## 21. Passage

The plane out of Port Calloway carried nine passengers and one urn, and Mara Grell counted both the whole way north.

She had her father's ashes in a canvas duffel at her feet, wedged between a dry bag and a case of surveying instruments she no longer needed but couldn't leave behind. Old habits. She'd been charting coastlines since she was nineteen, drawing the edges of things, always the edges, never the interior. Her father had gone the other direction—upriver, inland, into the country she'd spent half her life pretending not to care about.

Now she was flying into it with his bones on her lap.

"You're doing the breathing thing," Theo said from the seat beside her.

"I don't have a breathing thing."

"You do. Four counts in, hold, four counts out. You did it through the whole indictment reading too."

Mara looked out the window. The coast was falling away below them, the gray-green shelf of Port Calloway giving way to forest, then to the first pale scars of the high country. "I'm allowed a breathing thing. I'm about to scatter my father over a river I've never seen, in a valley that tried to kill you."

"The valley didn't try to kill me." Theo's voice was even, the way it always was when she corrected the record. "Halvorsen did. The valley just told the truth afterward. Rivers are like that. They keep everything and give it back on their own schedule."

Six months since the broadcast. Six months since Ruth's recording had gone out to three regulators, two newspapers, and a whistleblower hotline, and the world had rearranged itself around the sound of Voss saying *the adit was supposed to fail* in a voice as calm as a man discussing freight schedules. Six months of hearings, of Halvorsen's officers led away in good coats, of Theo Nakamura's name cleared in two countries—and of photographers on the courthouse steps who hadn't cared about the Vale disaster until it turned out there were faces to attach to it.

Mara had learned to recognize the breathing thing because she'd started doing it herself somewhere around month two.

Below them, the Serrano appeared—a bright thread stitched through green, running the way her father's map said it ran. She'd had the map framed after the handoff. It hung above her drafting table now, that careful inked line climbing from the lower landing to the headwaters, annotated in Amos Grell's cramped hand: *bend at the split rock, camp above the falls if the water's up, the source is where the noise stops.*

*The source is where the noise stops.* She'd read that line a hundred times and still didn't know what he meant. She hoped she'd know it when she heard it.

"Dez says the melt's early this year," Theo said. "We'll make the falls by tomorrow noon if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we camp and your father waits another day. He waited twenty years to come home. He can wait a tide."

Mara almost smiled. That was the thing about Theo nobody prepared you for—the flatness that wasn't coldness at all, just a woman who'd learned to say true things without decoration. Mara had spent six months learning to hear the warmth underneath, the way you learn to hear a second melody under a main line.

Behind them, Ivo was arguing with Dez about something—knots, probably, or whose turn it was to haul the food barrel—and Ruth was ignoring them both with the serene concentration of a woman annotating water samples. Their crew. Not hired hands, not exactly. More like the family none of them had managed to build the ordinary way.

The plane dropped toward the upper airstrip, and Mara put her hand flat on the duffel.

"We're close, Dad," she said, so quietly only she could hear it.

The engine noise swallowed everything else.

* * *

They launched at dawn, the way Theo liked to—gray light, cold air, the river still holding the night's hush. Two rafts. Dez piloting the gear boat with Ivo up front, all elbows and attitude, and Theo running the passenger raft with Mara beside her and Ruth riding the stern frame with her splinted wrist tucked against her chest, healed crooked and proud of it.

Mara had been on rivers before. Ferry crossings, harbor work, once a tourist float down a tame stretch of the Calloway. Nothing like this. The Serrano inside the reserve moved with intent, green water stacking up against rock and folding over itself, and every bend delivered a view that made her want to stop and draw it and made her afraid to look away from the water long enough to try.

"Left channel," Theo said, not loudly, and Dez's raft slid left ahead of them, and the river took them.

By midmorning Mara had stopped being afraid and started being furious—at herself, mostly, for the twenty years she'd spent believing her father had chosen this country over her. It was easier to be angry at him than at the truth: that Halvorsen had written him letters with a company letterhead and a threat inside, and that he'd stayed away because staying away was the only way to keep her safe. She'd found the letters in his effects after the funeral. She kept them in a waterproof bag now, in the bottom of her own dry bag, and last night at the airstrip camp she'd read them again by headlamp while Theo pretended to sleep nearby and let her pretend not to notice.

*I never stopped watching for you, little map,* the last one said. *Every survey crew that came through, I asked if they knew a Grell girl in Port Calloway. One day I'll walk into your office and embarrass you in front of your colleagues.*

He'd died four hundred miles from her office, in a rented room in Kestrel's Landing, with a stranger holding his hand.

"Talk to me," Theo said. They were in a slow stretch between rapids, the kind of water that let you think, which Mara suspected was why Theo had timed her question for it. "You've been quiet since the airstrip."

"I'm processing."

"You're cataloguing. You do it when you're hurting. You make lists in your head."

"How would you know what I—"

"Because I've watched you do it for six months." Theo's eyes stayed on the water, hands easy on the oars. "You catalogue the hearings, you catalogue the reporters, you catalogued the entire contents of my gear bag the third week we knew each other. It's how you stay upright. I'm not asking you to stop. I'm asking what's on today's list."

Mara looked at the river. A kingfisher went past low and fast, electric blue, gone before she could point at it.

"Today's list," she said. "Item one: I haven't spoken to my father out loud since he could answer. Item two: I don't actually know how to do this. Scattering ashes. There should be a protocol. I'm a cartographer, we love protocols. Item three—" She stopped.

"Three?"

"Three is not list material."

Theo let it go, which was its own kind of fluency. But an hour later, pulling into an eddy for lunch, she said, "There's no protocol. You just open your hands. Everything else is yours."

* * *

They camped above the falls, exactly where the map said to, and Mara stood at the lip looking down at water that fell away in white steps into a gorge full of mist. The noise was enormous. It got into your ribs.

Ivo built the fire with the showmanship of someone who'd finally found a legal outlet for larcenous skill. Ruth produced a bottle of terrible whiskey from somewhere in the gear boat and refused to explain its provenance. Dez told a story about cap-sizing in the Whiteout Gorge that made himself the hero and made Ruth throw a pinecone at him, and Theo sat slightly outside the firelight the way she always did—not apart, just angled, keeping watch on a dark that held nothing anymore except dark.

Mara found her there later, after the others had rolled into their bags. Theo was sitting on a boulder above the falls, and Mara climbed up and sat close enough that their shoulders touched.

"He wanted to be buried here," Mara said. "I found it in the letters. He wrote that if anything happened to him, he wanted to go back to the source. He said it was the only place he'd ever been where he understood what he was for."

"That sounds like Amos."

"You knew him eight days."

"Eight days is enough with some people." Theo was quiet a moment. "He talked about you. First night, before he gave me the map. He said he had a daughter who drew coastlines, and that she'd inherited his eyes and her mother's stubbornness, and that he'd failed her in every way a man can fail a person and she'd be right never to forgive him. Then he asked me to promise anyway."

"He was right," Mara said. "About me being right."

"Were you?"

"I don't know anymore. That's the strange part. I built twenty years of anger and it turns out the foundation was a lie somebody else poured. I don't know what to do with all this—" she gestured vaguely at her own chest— "material. It's load-bearing and it's rotten."

Theo laughed, short and real. "Welcome to the crew. We're all renovating."

Below them the falls roared. Mara leaned into Theo's shoulder, and Theo, after a moment, let her weight rest back.

"I used to think duty was the highest thing," Mara said. "You keep your word, you finish the chart, you deliver the map. And then I met you, and you kept a promise to a dying stranger across four hundred miles of river with people shooting at you, and it wasn't duty at all, was it? By the end it was something else."

"It was you," Theo said simply. "After Portage of Bones it stopped being his map and started being your father. After the handoff it stopped being a delivery and started being—" She paused, and Mara felt her choose the word carefully, the way she chose lines through rapids. "Started being us."

The fire crackled below. Somewhere across the valley a wolf spoke once and went unanswered.

"I'm not good at quiet," Mara said. "I want you to know that going in. I narrate. I make charts of feelings. I will absolutely draw a diagram of this relationship at some point and you'll pretend not to find it charming."

"It's already charming," Theo said. "I've seen your field notes."

And that was all—no declaration, no turning, no music. Just two people on a rock above a waterfall, shoulders touching, the night not cold between them. Love arriving the way the river arrived at things: without announcement, wearing down whatever was in its path, patient as geology.

Mara closed her eyes and, for the first time since the funeral, didn't catalogue anything at all.

* * *

The source, it turned out, was exactly where the noise stopped.

They left the rafts at the last navigable pool and walked the final mile, the canyon narrowing until the walls pressed close and the water shallowed to a clear rush over clean gravel. Then the canyon opened like a door, and the sound fell away all at once, and they were standing in a bowl of meadow under peaks still white from winter, and the Serrano was coming out of the ground.

Not dramatically. That was the thing that undid her. The river simply rose—seeps welling through moss, a spring pooling in a hollow and spilling over, ten small beginnings braiding into one sure current. The air smelled like stone and snowmelt and, faintly, impossibly, like the pines of a childhood she'd thought she'd invented.

Ruth knelt at the nearest seep with her sample vials, working one-handed, and pronounced the water clean with the reverence other people saved for scripture. Dez took off his hat. Even Ivo went still, which Mara had learned was the boy's version of kneeling.

The adit was visible up-slope—a dark mouth in the hillside, sealed now with new concrete and a regulator's placard, the wound finally closed. Mara looked at it for a long moment. Her father had walked past that hole for years, carrying what he knew, alone, so that she wouldn't have to carry any of it.

"Dad," she said aloud.

Her voice sounded strange in the silence. Everyone found somewhere else to be—Dez herding Ivo toward the far seeps, Ruth suddenly very interested in a lichen, Theo walking a slow perimeter with her eyes on the ridgelines out of habit, though there was nothing up there anymore but sky.

Mara unzipped the duffel and took out the urn. It was lighter than she'd expected. Twenty years of a man, and he weighed less than her instrument case.

She'd planned words. Of course she had—she'd drafted them in her notebook, three versions, cross-indexed. Standing there with the wind moving the grass, she threw all three versions away.

"You embarrassed me in front of my colleagues," she said. "You never came. You're late. I'm a senior cartographer now, I have a window office, and you missed all of it, and I am so angry at you, and I miss you so much, and I understand, and I don't forgive you yet but I'm on my way, and I brought you home." Her voice cracked on the last of it. "I brought you home, Dad. The source is where the noise stops. I get it now. I get it."

She opened the urn.

The ashes went out gray-white against the green, catching the wind, drifting over the seeps and the moss and the small sure water. Some of them landed on the current and moved. She watched one pale fleck ride the newborn river over a riffle and out of sight, heading for the sea, heading, in its own unhurried time, for the harbor where her office window faced the water.

She stood there until the wind was done. Then Theo came and stood beside her, and took her hand, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left that needed saying out loud.

Downstream, the river ran clean toward everything.

It was Ivo who broke the silence, calling from the meadow's edge with the particular urgency of a teenager who has found something: "Hey! Hey, you guys need to see this. Somebody carved something on the rock up here—it's got your dad's name on it."
