## 1. The Weight of Still Air

The wind in Puerto Deseado had a way of getting inside you, Elodie thought, of finding the seams in a man and working them wider. It came off the South Atlantic in long gray shoves, rattling the corrugated tin on the cantina roof, driving grit through the gaps in window frames, carrying the smell of salt and diesel and old fish into every room in town. The locals didn't fight it anymore. They'd built their houses low and their fences crooked and they walked leaning into it like men wading upstream.

Elodie Okonkwo had been in Puerto Deseado eleven days, which was ten days longer than his rule allowed.

He sat in the back corner of the Cantina del Faro with his back to the wall and both eyes on the door, nursing a coffee that had gone cold an hour ago because refills meant conversation and conversation meant names. Outside, the afternoon light lay flat and metallic on the harbor. A rust-streaked trawler knocked against its moorings. Gulls hung motionless in the wind, pinned there like specimens.

Eleven days. He told himself it was the weather. The Ruta 3 north was flooded out; the pass south was closed by a truck fire; any number of reasonable explanations. But he knew the truth when he looked at it, the way you know your own face in a mirror: he was tired. Bone-tired, in a place where the wind never stopped and nobody knew his name, and some traitorous part of him had started to wonder what it would feel like to stop.

Stopping got people killed. Stopping was how the Vane Syndicate found men—found *him*—because a man in motion left only a smear, but a man at rest left a shape.

The door banged open and the wind came in with it, along with the old man.

Elodie had noticed Tomás Reyes three days ago, the way you notice anything in a small port town: by elimination. Everyone else belonged here. The stranger didn't. He was gaunt in the way that wasn't dieting but disease, with a yellow tinge under his brown skin that no amount of wind-flush could hide, and he wore a canvas coat too heavy for the season and boots worn through at the outer edges, the way boots wear on a man who walks ridgelines instead of streets. Every day he came to the cantina, sat at the same table near the window, drank one glass of wine without tasting it, and watched the door the way Elodie did.

A man watching doors in a town this small was either running from something or looking for someone. Elodie had spent four days hoping it wasn't him.

Tonight the old man crossed the room directly to his table, and hope died quietly, like a match in wet grass.

"Okonkwo." The voice was gravel and river silt. "Storm chaser."

Elodie didn't move. "You've got the wrong table."

"I've got the only table." Tomás lowered himself into the opposite chair without being asked, and up close the sickness was worse—the tendons standing in his neck like cables, the breath whistling faintly at the bottom of each inhale. He set a folded oilcloth packet on the wood between them. "They said in Comodoro there was a man here who read weather the way priests read scripture. Who could take a truck across the steppe where the map says drown. That's you."

"Whoever said that is selling something."

"They are. I am." The old man's eyes were dark and steady and burning with something that looked, uncomfortably, like faith. "I'm dying, Okonkwo. Four months, maybe five. The doctors in Buenos Aires used words I won't repeat. So I'm not going to waste what's left sitting in a port town drinking bad wine."

"Condolences." Elodie reached for his cold coffee, mostly to have something to do with his hands. "Doesn't make me your guide."

"It makes me honest." Tomás leaned forward. Up close, Elodie could see the fine tremor in his hands, and beneath the illness something else—a coiled patience, the stillness of a man who had spent decades alone in empty country and found it good company. "I need to go inland. West, past the rain line, into the dry country. There's a place on a map my father gave me. Coordinates. Something worth more than gold, he said, and he wasn't a man who lied about land."

"Everyone's father found something worth more than gold."

"Everyone's father didn't die broke because he refused to sell it." For a moment the old man's composure cracked, and Elodie saw the fury underneath, banked and ancient. "I've spent forty years telling myself I'd go back when I had time. Now time's the one thing I don't have. I'm offering money. Real money. Half now, half when we stand on the coordinates."

"How much?"

"Enough to make a man's problems disappear."

Elodie laughed, short and humorless. "My problems have lawyers."

"Then enough to make lawyers disappear too." Tomás unwrapped one corner of the oilcloth—not far enough to show the map, just far enough to show the edge of a banknote, crisp and heavy. "I know who you're hiding from, storm chaser. This town has eleven hundred people and I've been here six days. You think I haven't heard about the man in the corner who pays cash and flinches at the word 'Vane'?"

The name went through Elodie like a fishhook. He kept his face still, but his hand had stopped moving, and Tomás saw it, damn him.

"Here's what I know," the old man continued, quiet now, conversational, terrible. "The Syndicate doesn't chase debts. They chase *people*. If they wanted your money they'd have taken your truck in Bahía Blanca. If they wanted you dead you'd be dead. Which means what they want is for you to keep running, scared, alone, always one night ahead of them. And you've been doing a beautiful job of it for two years."

"You don't know anything about me."

"I know you're sitting in the windiest town on the coast going nowhere." Tomás tapped the oilcloth once. "I'm offering you the opposite of running. A direction. Inland, west, away from roads and phones and men in gray cars. Six weeks in country so empty the Syndicate couldn't find God in it. And when you come out—" he slid the packet across the table until it touched Elodie's knuckles, warm from the old man's grip, "—you come out paid. Free. Or you sit here another winter, drinking cold coffee, waiting for a gray car."

Outside, the wind slammed a loose sheet of tin somewhere down the street, a gunshot sound, and neither of them flinched, which told Elodie they were the same kind of animal.

He looked at the packet. He thought about the ledger in his head: the numbers he owed, the interest that grew while he slept, the phone call two years ago from a soft-voiced man named Corvo who had said, *we'll be in touch, Mr. Okonkwo, no rush*—and how the not-rush had been worse than any threat. He thought about the rule. One night, never two. Eleven broken nights already.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, he thought about the way the old man had said *inland*, and how something in Elodie's chest had turned toward the word like a compass needle, hungry, before his fear could weigh in.

"What's out there?" Elodie asked. "Really. On those coordinates."

Tomás smiled, and it transformed his ruined face into something young. "That," he said, "is the wrong question. The right question is whether you want to spend the rest of your life asking what's behind you or what's ahead."

"That's a fortune cookie."

"My father wrote it on the map." The old man stood, slow, joints cracking like the cantina's floorboards, and left the packet on the table. "Truck's at the estación. A Toyota, ugly, indestructible. We leave tomorrow at first light, before the wind comes around from the southeast. If you're not there, I'll find another guide and you'll find another town." He paused at the door, silhouetted against the flat silver light, and looked back. "One thing, Okonkwo. Whatever happens out there—you don't leave me in the desert. Dead or alive, I come home. Swear it, or don't come."

The door banged shut behind him. The wind took his shape and erased it.

Elodie sat alone with the packet for a long time. Around him the cantina filled with the evening shift from the fish plant, men loud with beer and warmth, belonging to the town in a way he never would. He turned the oilcloth over in his hands. Heavy. Old. It smelled of dust and engine grease and, faintly, of some other decade.

He should walk away. One night, never two. The rule had kept him breathing through two years of gray cars and soft voices on telephones. Every instinct he'd sharpened since Bahía Blanca said the old man was trouble wrapped in money wrapped in a dying man's obsession, and obsessions got guides buried.

But he was so tired of towns. Tired of cold coffee and false names and the specific loneliness of a man who knows the exit routes of every bar he drinks in. Tired of running from a debt that never seemed to want collecting—as if the owing itself were the point.

Elodie finished the cold coffee in one swallow, grimaced, and tucked the packet inside his jacket, against his chest, where he could feel its weight with every heartbeat.

First light, then. One last job. In and out in six weeks, paid and gone, somewhere the mail couldn't follow.

He told himself that, walking back through the screaming wind to the hostel where his bag was already packed, and for the first time in two years, almost believed it.

* * *

He was at the estación twenty minutes early, which surprised him. The Toyota sat in the gray dawn light, ugly as promised, loaded with jerry cans and crates and a spare tire bolted absurdly to the roof rack. Tomás stood beside it breathing hard, watching the eastern sky where the clouds were breaking apart over the water, letting down thin blades of gold.

"Changed my mind twice," Elodie admitted, coming up beside him.

"I know. I watched you not sleep." The old man handed him the keys. "You drive. I'll navigate. And Okonkwo—" he nodded west, toward the horizon where the land climbed away from the sea into country neither of them could see yet, flat and brown and enormous, "—that wind you hate? It stops past the mountains. Rain shadow. Nothing out there moves unless it wants to."

Elodie put the keys in the ignition and thought: *a whole desert where nothing moves unless it wants to.* It sounded less like geography than like a warning.

He turned the engine over anyway.

Behind them, Puerto Deseado woke to another day of wind. Ahead, the steppe opened like a held breath—and somewhere out in all that stillness, at coordinates inked by a dead man's hand, waited whatever the Reyes family had refused to sell.

Neither of them would come back the same. But that morning, with the sun rising and the tank full and the road running west, it felt—for one dangerous moment—like beginning instead of fleeing.

Elodie drove into the dry country, and did not look back.
