## 2. What the Rain Never Reaches

**Elodie**

The rain never came past the mountains. That was the first thing the steppe taught you—how a wall of white peaks could rob an entire country of weather, leaving two hundred miles of yellow grass and gravel that cracked like old crockery under your boots. Elodie had driven through deserts before, but this one felt less like absence and more like theft. Somewhere west of them, whole storms were dying against the cordillera, spending themselves on snow they'd never deliver.

He drove the lead truck with the windows down because Tomás said the wind smelled honest. Behind him rattled the second vehicle: Ines Okafor at the wheel, a geologist who'd signed on for fuel money and stayed, she claimed, for the company; and beside her, wedged among crates, Mateo Lindqvist—drone pilot, ex-journalist, a man who asked questions the way other people picked locks. Four strangers and a dying man's map. It should have felt like a mistake. Instead, by the third day, it had started to feel like a crew.

"Left fork," Tomás called from the passenger seat, his voice thin as the air. He kept the map folded in his breast pocket, against his heart, as if it might keep the organ beating. "There's a guanaco trail. Follow it."

"There's no trail," Elodie said.

"There is if you believe in it."

Elodie believed in tire pressure and water rations and the exact weight of what he owed. But he turned left anyway, and to his professional irritation, there it was—a thread of beaten earth winding toward a line of low hills the color of rusted iron.

They made camp that night in the lee of a basalt ridge, and Ines produced a bottle of malbec she'd smuggled in her tool kit, and Mateo got a fire going with the efficiency of a man who'd once cooked for a news crew in worse places than this. Tomás sat closest to the flames, wrapped in two blankets, coughing into a handkerchief he pretended Elodie couldn't see him hiding.

"You ever chase storms back home?" Mateo asked, passing the bottle.

"Oklahoma. Kansas. The whole alley." Elodie turned the wine, watched it catch firelight. "People think chasing is about speed. It's not. It's about knowing when to stop. When to plant yourself and let the thing come to you."

"And did you know when?"

"I knew when," Elodie said. "I just didn't stop."

Tomás laughed then, a dry sound like wind through wire. "That's why I hired you. A man who knows when to stop but doesn't—he'll go all the way."

Across the fire, Ines raised the bottle in a mock toast. "To going all the way." And Elodie, who had spent three years making sure no toast ever included him, heard himself say, "To going all the way," and meant it, and hated how much he meant it.

* * *

**Tomás**

Tomás had buried two sons and a wife in the wet country north of here, where the rain fell so hard it seemed personal. He had come south to the dry side to die somewhere the sky kept its promises. What he had not planned was to find himself, at sixty-eight years and four months, sitting around a fire with three people who laughed at his jokes and argued about his map and looked at him—this was the unbearable part—looked at him like he was worth following.

The coughing was worse now. He could feel the shape of it in his chest, a stone growing where his lungs used to be. He had perhaps weeks. Maybe days, if the altitude or the cold decided to hurry things along. Which was why he lay awake that night listening to Elodie check the trucks in the dark, moving between them with a flashlight cupped in his palm like something holy.

"You should sleep," Tomás said when the man paused near his bedroll.

"In a minute."

"You've said 'in a minute' every night since Puerto Deseado."

"Somebody has to watch the perimeter."

"We're two hundred kilometers from anything with a grudge."

Elodie crouched beside him. In the starlight his face was all angles and caution. "Habit," he said finally. "When you run long enough, you start watching even when there's nothing to see."

"And what are you running from, muchacho?"

A long silence. Wind moved the grass with a sound like pages turning.

"People who don't accept 'in a minute' for an answer."

Tomás nodded slowly. He knew about debts. He knew about men who collected them. That knowledge sat in his breast pocket next to the map, heavier than paper had any right to be, and he told himself—as he had told himself since Puerto Deseado—that he would tell Elodie everything before they reached the coordinates. There was time. There was still time.

He slept, and dreamed of rain that never arrived.

* * *

**Elodie**

On the fifth day the truck died.

Not the lead truck—the backup, Ines's rig, its fuel pump seizing with a metallic shriek fifty kilometers short of the mapped hills. They limped it off the track, and while Mateo filmed the engine bay with the grim fascination of a man documenting his own funeral, Elodie and Ines stood in the wind arguing about options.

"We tow it," Ines said.

"With what? We'll burn half our fuel."

"Then we walk."

"It's forty klicks to the site. With supplies, with him—" Elodie glanced at Tomás, who was pretending not to listen while very obviously listening. "No."

"So we split. I stay with the truck, you three push on, come back for me."

"Nobody splits," Elodie said, harder than he intended. "Nobody splits. That's rule one."

Ines arched an eyebrow. "Since when do we have rules?"

"Since now."

She studied him for a moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her drop it. Later, elbow-deep in the dead engine, she said quietly, "My brother used to say that. Nobody splits. He ran aid convoys up north." She wiped grease on her jeans. "He didn't always make it back either. But nobody ever died alone on his watch."

Elodie handed her the wrench. "Then we're agreed."

It took them nine hours and a fuel filter cannibalized from the drone kit, but near midnight the engine caught with a cough and a roar, and Mateo whooped loud enough to scatter guanacos a mile off, and Tomás stood by the fire with tears running openly down his weathered face, laughing at nothing, at everything. Elodie watched them celebrate over a repaired fuel pump and understood, with a feeling like vertigo, that he would drive this truck into the sea for any one of them. Three years of running had taught him everyone leaves. Five days with these people were busy teaching him otherwise, and he didn't trust the lesson, and he wanted it anyway.

* * *

**Tomás**

The hills rose out of the steppe on the seventh day like the backs of sleeping animals, and Tomás knew them instantly—not from the map but from memory, from thirty years ago, when he'd been young and greedy and working for men whose names he'd sworn never to speak again.

He made Elodie stop the truck a kilometer out.

"Not yet," he said. His hands were shaking, and not entirely from the illness. "I have to tell you something before we go in. All of you. Because after, there won't be any pretending left."

They gathered by the tailgate. The wind combed the grass flat. Tomás took out the map, unfolded it, and then reached deeper into his pocket and brought out a photograph gone soft at the corners: two young men standing before a drilling rig, arms slung around each other's shoulders, grinning like the future owed them everything.

"My brother, Aurelio. And me." He let them look. "We worked for the Vane Syndicate. Not as soldiers—as surveyors. We found things for them. Water, minerals, and—" his voice caught, steadied— "places to put things they wanted buried."

Elodie had gone very still. Tomás watched the recognition move across his face like weather.

"The Syndicate doesn't lend money, muchacho. It lends silence. Every debt is a leash." He tapped the map. "These coordinates. Thirty-one years ago, Aurelio and I helped bury a convoy out here. Trucks full of ledgers. Names, payments, routes, grave sites—everything the Syndicate never wrote down twice. Men who signed their own death warrants in triplicate. Aurelio couldn't live with it. He hid copies of the cache records inside the burial vault itself, and he made me memorize the location, and then the Syndicate found out what he'd done, and my brother went into the ground with the rest of them."

The wind filled the silence. Nobody spoke.

"They think I'm dying anyway," Tomás said simply. "They let me run because a dying man leads them straight to whatever he's been holding back. My debt bought this expedition, Elodie. You were hired with bait money. I'm sorry. I am so sorry. But everything I told you about wanting to see it one last time—that part was true. Aurelio's proof has been sitting under this dirt for thirty-one years, and I mean to dig it up and give it to people who can use it, before I follow my brother down."

Elodie stared at him. Behind him, Mateo had lowered his camera; Ines's knuckles were white on the tailgate.

"How long," Elodie said quietly, "have they known where we are?"

Tomás met his eyes and could not lie to him anymore.

"Since Puerto Deseado," he said. "Maybe longer."

Far to the east, at the edge of sight, dust was rising off the steppe. Too straight to be wind. Too deliberate to be anything else.

Three vehicles, moving fast, closing the distance across the dry country where the rain never reached.
