## 3. Debt Collectors

The wind died at dawn, and that was the first wrong thing.

For six days the steppe had howled—a constant, grinding voice in our teeth, the coihue branches bent like old men into the wind. Then, on the seventh morning, nothing. Silence lay over the scrubland so complete I could hear Tomás coughing inside his tent, forty meters away. I stood with my coffee going cold and watched the horizon and felt the hair rise on my arms.

Storm chasers learn to read stillness. Stillness isn't peace. It's a held breath.

"Elodie." Marisol's voice behind me, low. She'd been on watch since four. She didn't point; she just looked east, where the land rose into a long basalt ridge, and I followed her eyes to the dust.

Not the kind of dust the wind makes. This came in a straight line, patient, three vehicles wide.

I counted them the way you count waves before they break. Two pickups, white, unmarked. A third vehicle behind them, boxy, riding high—communications gear, maybe worse. They weren't hurrying. Men who hurry are afraid. These men knew exactly what they were driving toward.

"Wake them," I said. "Quietly."

* * *

Tomás was already sitting up when I ducked into his tent. He'd heard it too, or his body had—the old prospectors' instinct for trouble arriving before the mind catches up. His skin had gone the color of the salt we'd crossed two days ago, gray-yellow, translucent. The illness was eating him from the inside, and he knew it, and he looked at me with terrible clarity.

"They followed me," he said. Not a question.

"From Puerto Deseado?"

"From before that." He laughed, and it turned into a wet rattle. "You think a dying man walks into a port town bar and hires the first stranger with a truck? Elodie. They let me run. They wanted to see where I'd go." His hand found mine, grip shockingly strong for a man two weeks from dead. "The debt was never money. It was an address. I led them to you to lead them here."

The words landed like stones dropped down a well—I heard each one hit. All those nights I'd lain awake calculating what I owed the Vane Syndicate, running the numbers, hating myself for taking Tomás's cash. And the whole time the ledger had been theirs, not mine. We hadn't found the cache. We'd been the cache's delivery service.

Outside, I could hear the others moving now—Marisol waking Dev and Priya, boots on gravel, the soft metallic sounds of people deciding what to carry. Nobody screamed. That was its own kind of grief: we'd all run from something, all of us, and none of us needed the danger explained.

"How long do I have?" Tomás asked.

"That's not—"

"How long."

I looked at him. The ridge was twenty minutes out, maybe less. The trucks would reach camp in ten if they pushed it. There was one track out of here, west, toward the flats, and someone had to make sure it stayed open long enough for five people instead of four.

"The pass through the scree," I said. "If a man stood at the narrows with the rifle—"

"I know which narrows." He was already reaching for his jacket, movements deliberate, a man folding up his life. "My father mined this country forty years. You think I don't know how to die in it properly?" He pressed something into my palm: the map, folded small, oilcloth-wrapped, and beneath it a brass cartridge casing, heavy, sealed. "The coordinates were only half of it. This is the other half. Don't open it until you're somewhere safe. If there is anywhere safe."

"Tomás—"

"You spent your whole life chasing storms." For a moment his eyes were young, bright, the prospector who'd walked into a bar and seen not a debtor but a fellow runner. "Here's yours. Go be magnificent in it."

* * *

I won't write down everything about the next hour. Some arithmetic stays private.

What I'll say is this: the narrows held. Three shots, then silence, then one more shot that I will hear for the rest of my life. By the time we crested the western rise and looked back, smoke was rising from our own camp—they'd burned the tents, the trucks, everything that said we'd ever existed—and there was no movement at the pass. None.

Dev drove. Marisol navigated. Priya sat in the bed of the pickup with the records crate strapped beside her and a shotgun across her knees, and I rode shotgun in the cab with the brass casing in my jacket pocket, pressing against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

Behind us, the white pickups crested the ridge.

* * *

The salt flat is called the Salar de la Sed—flat of thirst—and it earns the name. Sixty kilometers of blinding white crust over black mud, heat shimmering off it in sheets, no water, no cover, no shade. In the old days the sheep drovers crossed it at night. We hit it at noon because we had no choice; the eastern tracks were cut, and the flats were the only way to the Chilean border road.

By two o'clock the pursuers were a dust smear on our back trail, gaining. Their vehicles were heavier, newer, built for this. Ours was a rented Toyota with a cracked windshield and a fuel gauge that lied.

"We should burn it," Dev said suddenly. His knuckles were white on the wheel. "The records. Shred them, bury them, drive into the middle of this flat and light a fire. They stop chasing. We live."

Nobody answered for a long moment. The engine buzzed. Somewhere under the crust, brine shifted and settled.

"He's right," Marisol said quietly. She wasn't looking at either of us; she was watching the mirror. "Four names on those pages are people I drank with in Punta Arenas. But I'm not dead yet, and neither are any of you, and that's worth more than justice." Her jaw tightened. "It has to be. Say it's enough."

"It's not," Priya said from the bed, through the rear window. Her voice was hoarse with dust. "You want to know why? Because Tomás gave us the map knowing it was bait. Knowing they'd come. He bought us time with the only thing he had left so this—" she slapped the crate "—gets out. If we burn it, he died for ash."

"And if we keep it, we might all die for paper!" Dev's voice cracked across the flat. The truck swayed. "There are five graves' worth of names in that box and every one of them is a reason for these people to put a bullet in us!"

"Then let them chase us to the border and shoot us on camera," Priya said. "Let there be witnesses. That's all justice ever is—someone refusing to look away."

I turned in my seat and looked at her, this woman I'd known nine days, sunburned and bleeding from a split lip, holding a shotgun over a crate of dead men's secrets like it was a sleeping child. And I understood that the choice had already been made—not by me, not by vote, but by whatever we'd become out here. Found families aren't built from agreement. They're built from the moment staying together costs more than leaving, and everyone stays anyway.

"We keep it," I said. "But we don't all keep it."

They went quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

* * *

The border station at Paso Roballos appeared at dusk like a mirage gone wrong: floodlights, a low concrete building, a single road funneling between wire fences. And parked outside it, unmistakable even at distance, a third white pickup. Waiting.

They'd guessed our route. Of course they had. There was only ever one way out.

I made Dev kill the headlights two kilometers out and we sat in the dark while the engine ticked, and I laid it out fast and flat, the way you brief people on a storm cell: one road, one checkpoint, men on both sides. Two of us couldn't get through that way. Maybe none of us could.

"But there's another crossing," Marisol said slowly. "The old drover's ford, north. My grandfather used it. It's not legal, but it's real—if the truck can take the riverbed."

"The truck takes the ford," I said. "All of you, and the crate. Tonight, before moonrise." I pulled the brass casing from my jacket and set it in Priya's hands, closing her fingers over it. "And this goes with the records. Whatever's inside, it reaches journalists, prosecutors, someone with a badge and a spine. Promise me."

"What are you doing?" Dev asked, though his face said he already knew.

"I'm going to walk up to that checkpoint," I said, "and give them something to chase. Loud, stupid, memorable. Every eye on me is an eye off that riverbed." I smiled, and it surprised me how easy it was. "I've been running from these people for three years. Turns out the trick was always just to stop."

Marisol caught my arm. "Elodie—"

"Sixty kilometers of salt flat," I told her, "and nobody left anybody behind. Not once. So I'm not leaving you. I'm going ahead." I stepped down from the truck into the cold coming off the flats, and the last thing I saw as they pulled away was Priya's face in the rear window, mouth moving—*we come back, we come back*—until the dark took them.

Then I turned toward the floodlights, put my hands where they could be seen, and started walking.

Behind me, far to the north, faint as a heartbeat, I thought I heard a river.
