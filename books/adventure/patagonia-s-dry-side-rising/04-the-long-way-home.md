## 4. The Long Way Home

The border crossing at Paso Roballos was a gravel scar in the steppe, a customs shed, a barrier arm, and forty kilometers of nothing on either side. Elodie saw it from the ridge at dawn and knew, the way he'd known storms before they broke, that it was where this ended.

He could feel them behind them—two trucks, patient as vultures, holding back since the salt flats. Not closing. Herding. The Syndicate didn't chase; they funneled. And every road south of here ran through that shed.

"Fuel's enough to make it," Marisol said beside him, binoculars steady in her cracked hands. She'd driven thirty hours without sleep and looked carved out of it, all edges and dust. "If we run the barrier."

"And if they shoot?" Ines asked from below, where she sat with the case open between her knees, rewrapping the records in oilcloth for the fourth time. She'd stopped crying somewhere outside Gobernador Gregores. Now she just worked, small and precise, Tomás's granddaughter in every line of her.

"They won't," Elodie said. "Not here. Too many witnesses, even if it's only three customs officers and a dog." He lowered the binoculars. "They want me alive. I'm worth more alive. The debt collector's whole religion is that you pay what you owe."

Marisol snorted. "And what do we owe?"

"That's the thing." He turned to look at them both—Marisol with her mechanic's hands and her brother's grave back in Puerto Deseado, Ines with the map folded into her jacket like a second heartbeat. "I spent two years thinking the debt was money. It never was. They wanted silence. Tomás figured it out too late, and now—" He gestured at the case. "That's everything they buried. Names. Payments. Six graves outside Sarmiento that nobody ever counted because nobody was allowed to count them."

"So we take it across," Ines said. Not a question.

"You take it across." Elodie crouched, level with her, and made himself hold her eyes. "Both of you. Through the crossing, clean papers, nothing on you but grief and a dead man's map. You go north to Buenos Aires. There's a journalist—Rocío Ferrán, *La Nación*, she's been hunting the Syndicate for years and getting nowhere because there's never been paper. You give her this, and you let her burn it all down."

"And you?" Marisol's voice was flat, but her jaw was working.

"I'm the distraction."

The silence stretched long enough that the wind filled it, moaning through the coironé grass.

"No," Ines said.

"Ines—"

"No." She stood, hugging the case to her chest like a child. "Tomás died so we could carry this. You don't get to die so we can carry it faster. That's not how family works."

Elodie felt something crack behind his ribs. He'd been running so long he'd forgotten the word could be aimed at him.

"It's exactly how family works," he said quietly. "Family is who stays behind so the others get out. My father did it. Probably yours did too, one way or another. Somebody always pays the toll." He stood, knees popping, forty-one years old and suddenly very tired. "Listen to me. They'll take me. They'll want to know where the records went, and I'll tell them nothing, because I'm good at silence—I learned it from the best teachers. By the time they believe me, Rocío will have printed page one. After that, killing me is a liability, not a lesson. I survive this. Maybe not free. But alive."

"You don't know that," Marisol said.

"No," he agreed. "But I know what happens if we all run together. One truck, low fuel, two hours of head start against men who've been paid to be patient. We die in the steppe and the Syndicate keeps its graves." He took the case gently from Ines's arms, pressed it back into them. "You carry them. All of them. Tomás, and the six in Sarmiento, and everyone whose name is in that oilcloth. That's the job now. Not me."

* * *

They argued for another hour, and then they stopped arguing, which was worse, and started planning, which meant they'd agreed.

Ines sewed the thinnest documents into the lining of her coat—the names, the payment ledgers, the things that would damn them fastest. Marisol stripped the truck of anything that smelled of the expedition and repacked it with camping gear, tourist maps, a woman's lie built out of zip ties and optimism. Elodie wrote two letters: one to Rocío Ferrán, naming himself and his crimes alongside theirs, because a confession made the story unkillable; one shorter, addressed to no one, that he folded into his pocket instead of sending anywhere.

At the ridge above the crossing, they shook hands like strangers, because that was the cover, and then Ines broke it and hugged him hard enough to hurt.

"You come back," she said into his shoulder. "I don't care how. I don't care when. You come back, and we finish Tomás's expedition properly. There's supposed to be a lake past the coordinates. He always wanted to see it."

"There's no lake," Elodie said. "It's a rain shadow. Nothing grows there."

"Then we'll dig one," she said, furious and wet-eyed, "and it'll be the ugliest lake in Patagonia, and you'll be there."

Then she walked down toward the barrier with the case under her arm, and Marisol followed, and Elodie watched them go with his heart going out after them like a kite string pulled taut.

He waited until they were through—until he saw the customs officer wave them past, bored, and the little hatchback turn north onto the ripio and shrink to a speck—and then he climbed into his own truck, turned it loud, and drove straight at the Syndicate's two vehicles like a man with nothing left to hide.

Which, for the first time in two years, was true.

* * *

They took him at the crossing, professional and quiet, flex-cuffs and a hood that smelled of diesel. In the dark of the truck bed he counted turns by the sway of the chassis and gave up around forty. A voice he recognized—Vasquez, the collector who'd found him in Puerto Madryn two years ago, polite as a bank notice—sat beside him for a while.

"The records," Vasquez said.

"Burned on the flats," Elodie said. "You were too slow."

A pause. "The girl has them."

"The girl has a dead grandfather and a grudge. That's heavier than paper."

He felt rather than saw the man smile. "We'll see."

They drove him east, then south, then somewhere with gulls, which meant coast, which meant a boat, which meant he might simply disappear, and Elodie lay on the cold aluminum and discovered something strange in himself: no panic. Just the wind off the water, and Ines's voice—*you come back*—and the thought that whatever came next, he had already done the bravest thing available to him. He had stopped.

* * *

Three weeks later, Rocío Ferrán's byline ran across four columns: **THE VANE FILES: GRAVES OF THE RAIN SHADOW**. Payment ledgers. Land titles signed over corpses. Photographs of the Sarmiento site, dated, geotagged, undeniable. The Syndicate's lawyer resigned by fax. Two senators developed sudden amnesia. An arrest warrant went out with Elodie Okonkwo's name on it—not as victim, but as accomplice, and he read it in a concrete room in Comodoro Rivadavia and laughed until the guard knocked on the door.

Because a warrant meant process. Process meant courts, lawyers, daylight. The Syndicate couldn't make him vanish anymore; he was evidence now, protected by the very machinery he'd spent his life dodging.

Marisol came first, on a Tuesday, smelling of engine grease and the same brand of cigarettes she'd sworn off in the steppe. She sat across the table and told him about the hatchback, sold, and the new truck, ugly and reliable. Then Ines, twice a month, letters at first and then visits, always with news of the trial, of Rocío, of the fund being raised for the families in Sarmiento.

On the fourth visit she slid a photograph across the table. A shallow basin of grey earth beyond the mapped coordinates, ringed by their old camp chairs, someone's terrible attempt at a rock border.

"We started digging," she said. "It fills a little when it rains. Which is almost never. It's the ugliest lake in Patagonia."

Elodie held the photo a long time. His eyes were burning and he let them.

"When I walk out of here," he said, "I want to see it finished."

"It'll be ready," Ines said. "We're not going anywhere."

And for the first time since Puerto Madryn, since the debts, since the running began—Elodie believed her.
