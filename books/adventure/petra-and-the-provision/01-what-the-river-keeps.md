## 1. What the River Keeps

The river gave up its dead slowly, and it never gave them back whole.

Elodie Ashgrove had learned that in her ninth year, hauling on a grapple line beside her mother while the Provision's winch screamed and the current argued every foot of rope. What came up that day was a drowned ferryman, bloated and silver-eyed, and her mother had looked at him a long moment before saying, *Remember this, little bird. The river keeps what it keeps. It only lends.*

Eleven years later, Elodie still worked the same stretch of the Vell, three miles below the ruins of Corvane Bridge, and she still heard her mother's voice every time the hook snagged something heavier than driftwood.

This morning it snagged something much heavier than driftwood.

"Line's fast," called Tam from the stern, his knuckles white around the brake lever. "Fast and deep. That's no log, Captain."

"No," Elodie said. "Logs don't fight."

She cut the engine to idle and let the Provision swing on its anchor, the current shouldering the hull downstream in a long lazy arc while the grapple line hummed taut off the port davit. The Vell ran high with early snowmelt, brown-green and muscular, carrying its usual cargo of branches, fence rails, one entire henhouse roof, and things nobody spoke about too loudly—things the spring floods pulled out of the low villages upstream. Salvage rights on this stretch belonged to whoever could hold the line, and Elodie held lines better than anyone between here and the sea.

"Could be a safe," Tam said hopefully. "Banker's safe off some flooded manor."

"Could be a boiler plate." She took the tension gauge down with two fingers. "Could be a bridge footing, and then we lose the davit and half the rail."

"Could be you stop talking like my grandmother and pull it up."

Elodie grinned despite herself. Tam was sixty if he was a day, missing two fingers to a winch cable, and had been telling her what to do since before she was tall enough to reach the wheel. She fed him slack, watched the gauge, felt the boat's whole character change as the load came off the bottom—the way a horse changes when a heavy pack comes off its spine.

Then the weight broke free of the mud and the river took an interest.

It didn't come up easy. It came up the way a body comes up, turning, resisting, showing first one face and then another. Twenty feet down the water went from green to black, and whatever hung on the hook was pale where the silt hadn't claimed it. Elodie leaned over the rail, one hand on the davit post, and watched it rise through the murk by inches: a flat plane, then an edge, then corners—true corners, machined, deliberate.

Not a safe. Not a boiler.

A chest. Lead-lined, by the gray weight of it, banded in iron gone to lace, and stamped on the lid with a seal pressed so deep the silt hadn't filled it: a tower above crossed keys, ringed in script worn nearly smooth.

Elodie stopped breathing for exactly one second.

She knew that seal. Every child on the Vell knew it, the way they knew plague marks on doorposts—not because anyone taught it, but because it lived in the stories adults told each other when they thought the children were asleep. The tower and the keys. Vessia. The kingdom that wasn't anymore, drowned twice over—once when the Chancellery burned its capital and scattered its court, and again when the new dam waters swallowed the old river towns whole. A hundred years gone. Its last heirs put to the sword, or so the schoolbooks said, in a tidy sentence that closed the subject like a coffin lid.

"That's—" Tam started.

"I see it."

"That's trouble, is what that is. That's trouble with a capital T and a wax seal on it."

"It's salvage." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Salvage is mine by right of line and labor. You know the law."

"I know the law," Tam said. "I also know that nobody stamps their luggage with a dead country's crest unless somebody living wants it back."

The chest broke the surface and swung against the hull with a sound like a struck bell, muffled and wrong. Water sheeted off the lead. Up close the seal was clearer: the tower, the keys, and beneath them a motto in old Vessian that Elodie couldn't read but recognized from a coin her mother used to keep in a locked drawer—a coin her mother never explained, and which had gone into the fire the week before she died, along with a box of papers and a letter Elodie had been forbidden, in writing, ever to open.

*Burn it unread,* the letter said. Elodie had obeyed. It was the only instruction of her mother's she had ever obeyed without argument, and she had never once, in eleven years, stopped wondering why.

"Captain?" Tam said quietly. "Your call."

The current pushed at the chest where it hung, turning it slow and patient against the Provision's side, and the river seemed to look at her with all its flat green eyes.

Dump it back. That was the sane move. Cut the line, let it sink into the channel silt where it had slept however many decades, and run cargo like an honest woman until the end of the season. Because Tam was right: nobody lined a chest in lead unless they wanted what was inside kept dry and kept dark, and nobody sealed it with Vessia's crest unless it mattered enough to kill for, and the Chancellery's men were not a story. They were toll collectors and inspectors and quiet gentlemen in gray who asked questions at locks, and the Vell was a long road with very few ways off it.

Her hand was already on the hatchet that hung by the davit.

One stroke. Clean through the wet rope. The river would take back its loan and the ledger would balance, and Elodie Ashgrove would go on being nobody, which was a kind of wealth her mother had died believing in.

The hatchet stayed on its pegs.

Because—and she would examine this later, honestly, in the small hours, and find it ugly and true—it wasn't curiosity that stayed her hand. Curiosity was a clean thing. This was older and meaner. This was eleven years of seasons barely broken even, of selling the brass fittings off her own cabin, of watching Chancellery barges ride the river fat and flag-bright while the Provision hauled scrap. This was the arithmetic of a woman who had grown up poor on a rich river, and the sudden knowledge that the river had finally, after all these years, offered to pay out.

Or it was her mother's face, whichever story she chose to tell herself. Both were lies. Both were true.

"Grappling iron," she said. "Get the net under it before the bands go. If it opens on the lift we lose everything to the current."

Tam looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him sigh the sigh of a man who has already lost an argument he hasn't finished having. He got the net.

They brought it aboard by main strength, four of them—Elodie, Tam, the Ferro twins from the deck crew—and even so it nearly went over the side twice, swinging like a hanged man as the Provision rolled. When it finally thumped down on the main hatch, the whole boat listed to port and complained about it. Lead-lined, iron-bound, big as a coffin for a large man. The seal stared up at the sky, dripping.

Nobody touched it for a full minute.

"Well," said Ivo Ferro, wiping his hands on his thighs. "Is it locked?"

"It's sealed," Elodie said. "Look. Soldered shut, all round the lid. Somebody wanted this shut for good."

"Somebody failed," said Ryn Ferro, because there was already a hairline crack in the solder along one edge, bright as a scar, where the river's long patience had begun the work.

Elodie crouched beside it. Up close she could smell it—river mud, old iron, and underneath, faintly, something else. Wax. Paper. The dry ghost-smell of a room that had been closed a very long time.

Her mother's voice, clear as if she stood at the rail: *The river keeps what it keeps. It only lends.*

"Lend me this, then," Elodie said, too low for the others to hear. "And send the bill whenever you like."

They opened it in the cabin, doors battened, lamp lit, though it was noon. Some instincts don't consult the clock. The solder gave to a cold chisel along the cracked seam, and the lid came up with a suck of trapped air that smelled of dust and tallow and something sweeter, something like dried flowers, and Elodie's skin crawled before she knew why.

Inside, packed in rotted wool, lay papers. Bundles of them. Hundreds. Letters, mostly, tied in packets with ribbon gone to thread, and ledgers, and a slim book bound in green leather with the tower-and-keys stamped blind on its cover. And on top of everything, laid straight as a corpse in state, a single envelope addressed in a fine antique hand:

*To the heir of Vessia, wheresoever the river shall find them.*

Tam read it upside down, which was a skill, and went gray.

"Captain," he said. "I want to be on record as saying we throw it back."

"Noted for the record," Elodie said. Her fingers were already on the packet strings. "Overruled."

The first letter she drew out was dated a hundred years past, in the year the schoolbooks said the bloodline ended. The handwriting inside was cramped and hurried, the ink brown with age, and the opening line was only six words long—but six words were enough to make the cabin tilt, the lamp flare, the river outside the porthole go suddenly loud.

Six words, and her mother's name among them.

Elodie sat down slowly on the locker, the letter trembling in her hands, and understood that the river had not given her a gift at all.

It had served papers.

* * *

By dusk she had read nine letters and burned one.

The burning was practical—that was the word she used, alone in the cabin, feeding the page into the stove and watching the flame take the old ink and turn it to nothing. Practical, because that letter named names. Living names, or names whose grandchildren were living, ministers and magistrates and one signature she'd seen before on Chancellery proclamations nailed up at every lock from here to the delta. A letter like that wasn't evidence. It was a lit fuse, and Elodie had no intention of carrying fire she couldn't control.

The rest she wrapped in oilcloth and hid beneath the false floor of the coal bin, and if her hands shook while she did it, there was no one to see.

Nine letters remained. Nine letters that said, in the dry formal language of a dead court, that the bloodline had not ended on any scaffold. That a daughter had been carried out of the burning city in a fish cart. That the line had gone to ground, and to the river, and to a woman named Ashgrove who kept a coin in a locked drawer and a letter her child must burn unread.

Elodie climbed to the wheelhouse and stood a long time looking upstream, where the Vell narrowed toward the gorges and the forbidden miles and, somewhere at the top of the world, the source. The evening wind came down the valley cold, smelling of snowmelt and pine, and it seemed to her that the whole river was running one direction—past her, past the Provision, toward the sea—carrying everything away except the one thing it had just delivered into her hands.

Behind her, far downstream, a light showed at Corvane Landing where no light had shown at dusk for years. Steady. White. Deliberate.

Someone was signaling someone, and the Provision sat squarely in the middle of the message.

Elodie turned the lamp down to a ember-glow and rang for Tam.

"Cast off," she said. "We're running tonight."
