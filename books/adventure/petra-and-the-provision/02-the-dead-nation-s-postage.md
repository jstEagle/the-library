## 2. The Dead Nation's Postage

The chest sat on the deck of the *Provision* all night, and by morning it had grown heavier.

Not truly—lead doesn't gain weight—but Elodie had learned long ago that some cargo behaves like a living thing. It breathes under tarps. It shifts its weight when you're not looking. By dawn she'd caught herself twice standing over it with a cup of coffee gone cold in her hand, watching the tarp rise and fall with the river's swell as though the thing inside were sleeping.

She should have dropped it back.

That was the sane move, and she knew it the way you know the location of every rock in your own channel: without thinking, without looking. A sealed chest stamped with the crest of Vessia was not salvage. It was a message. The river didn't send messages; it swallowed them. Someone had put that chest in the water—or lost it there—and either way, whoever wanted it found would be someone with the reach to make finding it expensive.

But she hadn't dropped it back. She'd hauled it aboard with the winch groaning like a dying man, stowed it under her bunk canvas, and told herself it was because lead was worth money. Told herself it was because the seal might fetch something from a collector. Told herself a dozen practical lies while the chest breathed under its tarp and the river ran dark past the pilothouse windows.

Now, moored in the willow shadow below Miller's Bend with no traffic on the water and fog burning off slow, she knelt beside it with a pry bar and told herself one more lie: that she only wanted to know what she was carrying before she decided where to carry it.

The seal was wax over lead, crimson gone black at the edges, pressed with a crowned tower above three wavy lines—the mark of Vessia, drowned kingdom, dead two generations and still showing up in schoolbook maps with a dotted border and an asterisk. She'd seen it on coins sold to tourists in river towns. She'd never seen it this close, this fresh, as if the wax had been poured last week by hands that believed the kingdom still existed.

The pry bar bit under the lid's lip. The hasps were brass, green with river rot, and they gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside: oilcloth, wrapped tight and tied with cord gone soft as string cheese. Beneath the oilcloth, letters. Dozens of them, bundled in packets, each packet tied with ribbon that had once been white. And beneath the letters, flat and heavy, a ledger bound in leather the color of dried blood, stamped with the same crowned tower.

Elodie sat back on her heels.

Her first thought, absurdly, was postage. That was what it looked like—a nation's mail, undelivered, returned to sender after the sender drowned. Somebody's whole correspondence, sunk. There was something obscene about it, the intimacy of it. Letters are private things. Even state letters. Even treasonous ones.

She picked up the topmost packet and turned it over. The address side faced up, written in a careful clerk's hand:

*To Her Serene Highness, the Princess Maren of Vessia, in hiding —*

Elodie stopped breathing.

Her mother's name had been Mara Ashgrove. Plain Mara, who mended nets and drank her coffee bitter and sang Vessian lullabies in a language she claimed she'd picked up from a wet-nurse. Who never spoke of where she came from, who flinched at uniforms, who made Elodie promise—eight years old, small fingers in her mother's calloused grip—*never tell anyone our name means anything.*

*Maren.* Not Mara. The clerk's hand had rendered it in full formal style, but underneath the ink, underneath forty years, Elodie heard her mother's voice saying the word, and the two names lay against each other like a key and its lock.

She opened the packet with hands that did not shake, which surprised her. Later she would understand why: the shaking was waiting somewhere deeper down, patient as the river.

The first letter was dated eleven years before the fall. It was from someone signing himself only *T.*, and it read like a report from a spy who had forgotten he was writing to royalty and not a handler:

*Your Highness asks whether the Chancellery suspects. They suspect nothing, because they suspect everything, which amounts to the same paralysis. I have placed the archive beyond their doctrine; they believe knowledge is a building, and so they guard buildings. Your grandmother understood that knowledge is a current. It moves or it dies. When the time comes, the Provision will know the way.*

Elodie read the phrase again. *The Provision.* Her boat's name. Her mother had named the boat, laughing, the day they'd bought her hull-deep and rotten—"a provision," she'd said, "is what you carry because you don't trust the road." Elodie had thought for twenty years it was a joke about her mother's habit of overpacking.

It was not a joke. It was a designation. Somewhere in a dead kingdom's files, her boat had a mission.

She set the letter down on the deck planks very carefully, the way you set down something venomous, and pulled the next from the bundle. Then the next. The fog burned off. The sun came up the color of weak tea. She read until her eyes ached, and the picture assembled itself piece by piece like a wreck rising out of green water:

A royal line that had known, decades before the end, that Vessia was rotten through—that the kings poisoned rivals at banquet tables, that the ministers starved whole provinces to keep the capital fat, that the archives held proof enough to hang the dynasty twice over. A princess smuggled out ahead of the purge, stripped of her name, planted on a river like a seed in a crack of pavement. Instructions, coded in the bland language of commerce, for a vessel that would one day run upstream to a flooded archive at the river's source. A contingency plan written by people who were already dead, addressed to a woman who had spent her whole life making sure her daughter would never need to open it.

And now the daughter had opened it, sitting in the willow shadow with mud on her knees, and the kingdom everyone said was exterminated had reached up out of the water and put its crown in her lap.

*Never tell anyone our name means anything.*

"Mara," Elodie said aloud, testing it. "Maren." The names didn't fit together. They fit perfectly. Both things were true at once, and both things were unbearable.

She was still kneeling there when the interlude came—not a sound, not a sight, just a memory surfacing the way bodies surface, unbidden: a night two years ago, a tavern in Corvane Landing, a young archivist with quick gray eyes buying her a drink and talking too much about paper.

*You know what I love about letters?* Wren had said, turning a beer glass by its base. *They're honest in ways people can't afford to be. A person lies to your face. A letter lies on purpose, and if you hold it right, against the light, you can see the shape of the truth it's hiding. Every forgery is a confession if you're patient enough. That's the whole trade, really. Patience and a lamp.*

*I'll remember that,* Elodie had said, meaning it as flirtation, meaning nothing by it, meaning—she understood now—everything by it.

Because there was a letter in the chest that wasn't like the others. She'd found it wedged beneath the ledger, folded differently, addressed in a different hand—not the clerk's, not T.'s, but a slanted, hurried script that looked like it had been written standing up, or running. It was newer. The paper was whiter. And it was addressed not to the princess in hiding but forward, into the future, to whoever finally pried this chest open:

*If you are reading this, then the river kept its bargain longer than we dared hope. Know that the woman you called Mara chose this for you. Know also that choice is a debt like any other, and someone will come to collect. Burn what you cannot carry. Carry what you cannot burn. And do not trust the Chancellery's patience, nor its mercy, nor especially its archivists—they train them to be likable.*

Elodie laughed. It came out wrong, half a sob, alone on the deck of a boat named for a mission she'd never agreed to. *They train them to be likable.* Gray eyes. Quick hands. Beer glass turning, turning.

No. That was paranoia. Corvane Landing was full of archivists the way it was full of mosquitoes, and Wren had talked about paper the way other people talked about lovers, and none of that meant—

None of that meant anything. Our name means nothing. She could still make it mean nothing.

* * *

The compromise happened the way most compromises happen: quickly, selfishly, and dressed up as prudence.

She told herself it was practicality. That was the story she built while she worked, plank by plank, and it was even mostly true. You don't burn evidence wholesale; you curate it. Anyone who'd run cargo on this river knew that what got you killed wasn't possession—it was possession of the wrong item at the wrong checkpoint. So: triage. Keep what was deniable. Destroy what was damning.

The problem was deciding which was which, and the problem under that problem was that she didn't want to burn any of it, because every page was another sentence of her mother's voice, and her mother had been dead six years, and Elodie had thought she'd heard the last of her.

She burned exactly one letter.

She chose it carefully—the earliest one, the one naming the archive's location in plain terms, coordinates and depths and the snowmelt window, the single document that could lead anyone straight upriver to the source. She fed it to the cookstove page by page and watched the crowned towers curl and go orange and come apart into flakes that rode the stove-draft up the stack and out over the water. It felt less like destroying evidence than like burning a bridge behind her, and she stood there watching the ash spiral and understood, with the cold clarity of someone reading their own chart wrong, that she had just made a decision about her entire life in the space of one letter's combustion.

Because keeping the rest meant the rest mattered. Keeping the correspondence meant she was corresponding—with the dead, with the claim, with whatever waited at the source. Burning the map but keeping the mail was not neutrality. It was a reservation. A seat held on a journey she was telling herself she hadn't bought a ticket for.

"Practical," she said to the stove, and the stove said nothing, and the ash went downstream toward Corvane Landing, where a likable archivist was presumably having breakfast.

She rewrapped the chest in its oilcloth, retied the ribbons, and stowed the ledger separately—in the false bottom under the wheelhouse floorboards, where she kept the papers that kept the *Provision* legal, because irony deserved a berth. Then she went forward, cast off the willow lines, and took her boat out into the channel with the current pulling gently, patiently, downstream.

Downstream was home. Downstream was Corvane Landing, and fuel, and the market, and Wren's gray eyes if Wren was still there, and every reason in the world to point her bow that way and let the river decide she'd never opened anything at all.

Elodie stood at the wheel and looked upstream, where the water came from, where the gorge narrowed, where a flooded archive waited under snowmelt for a vessel named *Provision* to come collect a nation's mail.

Then she looked downstream, at the easy miles, and pushed the throttle forward, and hated herself a little for how relieved she felt to be running away.

Behind her, in the wheelhouse floorboards, the ledger lay in the dark with its crowned tower facing up, patient as postage, waiting for delivery.

At the landing, she would learn, someone had been asking after her boat by name.
