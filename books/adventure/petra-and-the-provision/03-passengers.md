## 3. Passengers

The Chancellery had given Wren a forged surveyor's commission, a brass theodolite they didn't know how to use, and a single instruction: *recover the chest at any cost, by any means, and report everything.*

Wren had read the instruction four times on the coach down from the capital, and each time the word *everything* had sat in their stomach like a swallowed coin. Everything meant the boat. Everything meant the captain. Everything meant writing down, in a neat archival hand, the precise dimensions of a person's life so that men in grey offices could decide what to do with it.

Corvane Landing smelled of tar and frying fish and the particular sourness of a river town that had stopped believing in its own future. The *Provision* was moored at the third berth, and Wren saw her before they saw Elodie Ashgrove—a low, work-worn riverboat with patched gunwales and a wheelhouse window fogged from the inside, like something breathing. She looked less like a vessel than an animal that had learned to tolerate captivity.

Then the captain came up out of the hold with a coil of rope over one shoulder, and Wren forgot, for three full seconds, what they were there to do.

Elodie Ashgrove was not beautiful in the way the capital understood beauty. She was beautiful the way a lock gate was beautiful—functional, scarred, absolutely certain of where it stood. She coiled the rope without looking at it, her hands doing the work while her eyes did something else entirely: reading the water, the sky, the faces on the dock. A woman who catalogued threats for a living. Wren knew the type. Wren had been trained to be the type.

*Don't stare,* Wren told themselves, and stared anyway, until Elodie's gaze swept past them and snagged—the way a hook snags—and moved on with the deliberate indifference of someone who had decided not to be interested yet.

Wren adjusted the theodolite on their shoulder, put on the face they'd practiced in a hundred mirrors—the mild, slightly bored competence of a government man who measured rivers for a living—and walked down the gangplank of the world into the captain's attention.

"Captain Ashgrove." They held out the commission papers. "Wren Calloway. Chancellery Hydrographic Office. I'm charting the fall in the waterline above Corvane. I need passage upstream as far as you'll take me."

Elodie took the papers without hurrying. She read them the way she'd read the dock—thoroughly, and with visible disbelief kept politely behind her teeth.

"The Chancellery doesn't charter private boats," she said. "The Chancellery owns boats."

"The Chancellery's boats draw too much water for the gorge this time of year." True enough; Wren had checked. "Yours doesn't."

"My boat," Elodie said, handing back the papers, "does whatever I tell it to. That's not the same as saying yes."

"I can pay."

"Everyone can pay."

"I can pay well, and ask no questions, and stand exactly where you put me and touch nothing I'm not told to touch." Wren smiled, and hated how easily the smile came, how good the lying felt in the mouth, like a language spoken since birth. "I've been told I make an excellent piece of furniture."

Something moved behind Elodie's eyes—not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a gambler deciding whether a card was worth holding.

"Cabin's aft," she said. "Deckhands eat at six. You miss it, you go hungry. And if that—" she nodded at the theodolite, "—goes over the side, I'm not fishing for it."

"It's not mine to mourn."

She turned away, and Wren stood on the deck of the *Provision* and felt the mission shift underfoot like a deck in weather.

* * *

The first two days were surveillance, and surveillance was easy, because watching Elodie Ashgrove required no effort at all.

Wren wrote the reports in their head as they watched, because that was the discipline—observe, record, transmit nothing until safe. *Subject maintains irregular schedule, awake before dawn. Subject handles cargo personally rather than delegating. Subject's crew consists of two: an engineer called Brannoc, older, deferential but not servile, and a boy called Pip who is either twelve or very small and thirteen.* All of it useful. All of it going into the report that would eventually travel downriver ahead of them, into the grey offices, into the files.

And all of it, increasingly, feeling like theft.

Because here was the thing the Chancellery briefings hadn't mentioned: Elodie Ashgrove worked. Not performed work, not commanded work—*worked*. On the second morning Wren came up early and found her waist-deep in the engine compartment beside Brannoc, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of signal cord, arguing about a bearing in language that would have gotten her fined in three jurisdictions. When the bearing was seated she climbed out, wrung river water from her trouser cuffs, and immediately began re-rigging a cargo net whose knots were wrong, muttering to herself, correcting them one by one with fingers that knew every knot's name and opinion.

"You're staring," Pip said, appearing at Wren's elbow with a mug of something brown and hot.

"I'm observing."

"That's staring with paperwork." The boy grinned and vanished aft.

Wren looked down at the notebook open on their knee. The page was full of observations, all accurate, all damning in aggregate, and none of them the truth, which was that Wren had spent two days memorizing the way Elodie's voice dropped half a register when she gave an order she expected obeyed, and the way she touched the wheelhouse doorframe twice—always twice—before entering, some superstition worn smooth by repetition, and the way she'd paused, yesterday evening, at the rail, looking downstream toward Corvane with an expression Wren recognized because Wren wore it in mirrors: the expression of someone carrying something heavy and pretending it weighed nothing.

The chest. It had to be aboard. Somewhere below decks, lead-lined and sealed, stamped with a dead nation's crest, it sat in the dark of the *Provision*'s hold, and Wren's entire purpose was to find it and take it—or, failing that, to mark the boat and let others do the taking.

*Recover at any cost.*

Wren closed the notebook.

* * *

On the third night, anchored in a willow-hung reach above the toll locks, Wren couldn't sleep and went up on deck, and found Elodie already there.

She didn't startle. That was the first thing Wren noted, professionally, and then unprofessionally admired. She simply glanced over, registered them, and returned to the river, making room on the rail beside her without a word—an invitation issued by geometry rather than speech.

Wren took it.

The river ran silver-black under a half moon. Somewhere across the water a night bird made a sound like a hinge. For a long while neither of them spoke, and the silence wasn't the armed silence of two people circling—it was something rarer, and more dangerous, which was comfortable.

"Your theodolite," Elodie said finally. "You set it up this morning on the foredeck."

"Surveying."

"Mm. You spent forty minutes measuring the angle between our masthead and a hillside with no benchmark within ten miles." She turned her head, and the moonlight caught the flat, patient intelligence in her face. "In my experience, Mr. Calloway, men who measure things that can't be measured are measuring something else."

Wren's heart performed a slow, controlled roll, like a boat taking swell. Every training instinct said *deflect, smile, offer the prepared answer.* Instead—God knew why—they heard themselves say:

"And in your experience, Captain, what are they usually measuring?"

"How long until they have to stop pretending." She looked back at the water. "I've carried passengers before. Chancellery ones. They always have the same hands."

"What kind of hands?"

"Clean ones." She flexed her own, rope-scarred and tar-stained, against the rail. "Hands that haven't decided yet whether they're willing to get dirty, and are waiting to see which way the money goes."

The night bird hinged again. Wren became aware of every point of contact between their body and the boat—the rail under their palms, the deck through their boot soles, the faint vibration of the river working at the anchor chain—as though the *Provision* itself were taking their confession.

"If I asked you something," Wren said quietly, "would you answer it honestly?"

"Depends what it costs me."

"Nothing. That's the strange part. It costs you nothing and it costs me—" They stopped. Started again. "Is there cargo aboard this boat that isn't on your manifest?"

Elodie was silent for a long moment. Long enough for the current to carry a fallen leaf past the hull, spinning slowly, lit silver.

"There's cargo aboard this boat," she said at last, "that isn't anyone's business but mine and the dead's. And if you're the man your papers say you are, you'll forget you asked. And if you're not—" she turned, and her eyes in the moonlight were the exact color of deep water over rock, unreadable and fathomless and entirely aware, "—then you should know that I've thrown better liars than you off this boat moving at full current, and the river did the rest."

Every survival instinct Wren possessed screamed at them to laugh, deny, produce the prepared deflection. Instead they heard themselves say, barely above the water:

"And if I told you I haven't decided yet which man I am?"

Something flickered across Elodie's face—not softening, nothing so simple. Interest. The interest of a pilot reading water she'd never run before, marking the rocks, calculating the line.

"Then you'd better decide soon," she said. "We pass the toll locks tomorrow. And the Chancellery keeps its books at the locks." She pushed off the rail, unhurried, and paused beside Wren close enough that Wren could smell tar and woodsmoke and river. "Sleep well, Mr. Calloway. Whatever you are."

She went below. Wren stayed at the rail until the moon set, the notebook untouched in their pocket, the forged commission feeling heavier than the theodolite ever had, and understood—with the clarity that comes only too late—that somewhere between Corvane Landing and this stretch of black water, the cage had changed places.

They had boarded the *Provision* to catch a smuggler.

They were beginning to suspect they'd boarded it to become one.
