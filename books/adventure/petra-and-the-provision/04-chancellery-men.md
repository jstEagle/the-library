## 4. Chancellery Men

The toll lock at Marrow's Gate announced itself by smell before sight—coal smoke and creosote drifting downriver on a wind that had turned cold overnight. Elodie saw the gray stone towers rise out of the morning haze, the Chancellery pennant hanging limp above them like something drowned.

She killed the Provision's engine to a polite murmur and began the ritual of looking innocent. Deck swept. Cargo manifest squared under a brass weight on the chart table. The chest itself—God help her—sat in the bilge beneath three feet of ballast chain and a false floor she'd built herself from salvaged hatch covers, sweating in the dark with its dead kingdom's secrets.

"Easy," she told the wheel, or the river, or herself. "You've lied to worse."

She hadn't, actually. But the lie was already in her mouth, warm as a coin held under the tongue.

* * *

Two men waited on the lock's lower quay, and they were wrong in the way Chancellery men were always wrong: too clean for the river. Their boots were dry. Their coats were wool the color of wet slate, cut close, no salt stains, no tar. One was tall and narrow as a mooring post, with a face like an unpaid bill. The other was shorter, softer, with the patient eyes of a man who enjoyed paperwork the way other men enjoyed drink.

"Captain Ashgrove." The tall one didn't ask it as a question. He consulted a leather folio. "Provision, out of Corvane Landing. Salvage license, class three." His voice had the flatness of a document read aloud. "We'll inspect your hold before you transit."

"Toll's paid at the window," Elodie said, leaning on the rail. "That's how locks work. You put money in, the water lifts you up. It's almost religious."

"It is," said the short one pleasantly, "when the Chancellery asks."

He came up the gangway without waiting for permission, and his partner followed, and Elodie counted her heartbeats and found them annoyingly fast. She made herself smile. She'd learned smiling young, on docks where a woman alone with a boat had to be either charming or invisible, and charm kept the crew hired.

"Class three salvage," the short one said, wandering her deck with his hands clasped behind him, touching nothing, which was somehow worse than touching everything. "Riverbed dredging, wreck recovery, that sort of thing. You must find all manner of curiosities down there."

"Mud," Elodie said. "Bottles. Somebody's grandmother's sewing machine last spring, still threaded. The river keeps what people throw away and gives it back when it feels like it."

"And if it gave back something valuable? Something with a crest on it, say?"

The question landed soft as a mayfly. Elodie let two full seconds pass, then laughed—a real laugh, because the absurdity of it genuinely reached her somewhere underneath the fear. "A crest? Captain, I pulled up a chamber pot stamped with the Duke of Halloway's arms once. Crests are cheap. Half the drowned things in this river were lying about who they belonged to before they ever sank."

The tall one wrote something in his folio. The scratch of the pen sounded loud.

"Hold inspection," he said.

* * *

They went through the Provision like customs men going through a widow's house. Elodie stood at the top of the hold ladder with her arms crossed and narrated cheerfully, pointing out the coil of dredging cable, the crates of tinned peaches bound for a trading post upstream, the spare propeller blade wrapped in canvas. The tall one rapped knuckles along the hull plates. The short one opened her icebox, read the labels on her preserves, and smiled at her rhubarb as though it might confess.

Under their feet, under the ballast chain, under the false floor, the chest sat silent as a grave.

Elodie thought about Vessia. Everyone did, now and then—the drowned kingdom was a story mothers told, a cautionary tale with a moral like a fishhook. A nation so rotten it had eaten itself. Kings who poisoned brothers over dinner. Ministers who taxed provinces into famine and called it loyalty. And then the flood, whether engineered or divine nobody agreed, and the river taking the capital whole, and the Chancellery rising on the ruins with a doctrine of never again that mostly meant *never anyone else*.

Her mother had sung her lullabies about it. That was the strange thing she kept circling back to, sleepless in the small hours: not history lessons, but songs. Songs in a language Elodie hadn't known until last week was Vessian.

"Captain." The tall one stood at the ladder's foot. "Your ballast chain. Why is it stowed over the forward bilge rather than distributed?"

"Because my stern rides heavy with the crane winch, and I'd rather list than porpoise. You're welcome to check my trim yourself, but you'll want a cup of coffee first. It takes an hour."

Something moved behind his eyes—calculation, weighing the hour against whatever schedule lived in his folio. The short one appeared at his shoulder and murmured something too low to catch. Then the tall one closed his folio with a snap like a trap.

"You'll transit," he said. "The lock master will raise you through. Safe passage, Captain Ashgrove."

They went down the gangway. They stood on the quay while the gates groaned open and the lock chamber filled, lifting the Provision toward the high water of the upper river, and both men watched her the entire time—not the boat, Elodie noticed. Her. Their eyes stayed on the pilothouse windows until the walls of the lock cut off the view, and even then she felt it, the pressure of being looked at, like a hand flat against the glass.

* * *

She ran the upper river hard all afternoon, telling herself the crawling feeling between her shoulder blades was just weather coming down out of the hills. By dusk she'd put twenty miles behind her and tied up in a bend thick with willows, where the current slid past quiet and brown and the only lights were fireflies starting up in the reeds like slow sparks.

Wren cooked. Of course Wren cooked. The archivist had been aboard four days now, ostensibly a surveyor paying passage to map the upper channel, and in four days had produced better meals than Elodie had managed in four years, asked intelligent questions about river conditions, and touched nothing on the boat without asking. It was the touching-nothing that bothered her most. Honest passengers rummaged.

"Rice with the good oil," Wren said, setting a bowl beside the helm. "I found saffron in your stores. I have questions about the saffron but I've decided not to ask them."

"A salvage boat picks things up." Elodie took the bowl. Their fingers brushed; Wren's smile did something complicated to her pulse that she logged and filed under *later*. "Where's yours?"

"I ate on deck. Watching the fireflies." Wren leaned against the rail, face tipped up, and for a moment the lamplight caught them just so—cheekbone, throat, the loose collar of a shirt that had seen better decades—and Elodie thought, with genuine irritation, *nobody looks that good eating rice in the wind*.

She ate. She listened to the river. And underneath the rice and the fireflies and the treacherous warmth of Wren's shoulder near hers, she kept turning over the tall agent's parting words. *Safe passage.* Chancellery men didn't grant safe passage. They granted permission, and permission always had teeth somewhere in the fine print.

"I'm going to check the bilge," she said. "Pump packing's been weeping."

"At this hour?"

"The river doesn't keep hours. Neither do leaks."

Wren's eyes held hers a beat too long—curious, or cataloguing, she couldn't tell—and then they nodded and went below to wash dishes, humming something. Elodie filed the hum away too. It had sounded, faintly, like one of her mother's lullabies.

* * *

The bilge was black and smelled of iron and old water. Elodie went down with a lantern and a spanner she had no intention of using, and worked her way aft on her knees, running her palms along stringers and frames, feeling for anything that hadn't been there yesterday.

She found it under the transom bench, stuck to the hull plate with resin: a disc the size of a coin, brass-cased, warm. Not engine-warm. Alive-warm. When she held it to the lantern she could see the hairline seam around its edge and, etched inside the casing, so fine you'd need a jeweler's eye, the sigil of the Chancellery Office of Records.

A tracker. Riding home in her hull, whispering her position to every listening station on the river.

Elodie crouched in the dark and breathed slowly, and let the fear finish arriving, because it wasn't done yet. The agents had searched her hold for two hours and found nothing—that was certain; she'd rebuilt that floor herself. So the tracker hadn't come aboard at Marrow's Gate. Which meant—

Which meant it had been aboard before. Which meant someone had carried it past the first checkpoint at Corvane Landing, past the bored inspector who'd waved the Provision through with barely a glance, and planted it here, in the dark, in the place only someone living aboard would know was safe.

Someone aboard.

There were exactly two someones aboard.

She climbed up into the night air and stood very still on the afterdeck, lantern doused, listening. Forward, the dishwater sounds had stopped. From the galley came Wren's voice, low, talking to themselves—or to no one—or into something small enough to cup in a palm. She couldn't make out words. Just the cadence, gentle and even, like a report.

Elodie stood in the dark with a brass tracker burning in her fist and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Corvane Landing. Four days of perfect rice. Four days of questions about river depth and channel markers and *where exactly are you headed, Captain, how far upstream does your license run*. Four days of a charming stranger who touched nothing and noticed everything and hummed her mother's lullabies like they were weather.

The tracker was warm in her hand. The river slid past, black and patient, keeping what it kept.

Above the galley lamp, Wren's shadow moved against the curtain—pausing, Elodie thought, the way a person pauses when they've heard a floorboard shift somewhere in a quiet house.

She slipped the tracker into her pocket, and began, silently, to plan.
