## 5. Confrontation at the Weir

**WREN**

The Provision ran dark above the weir, no lamp burning at the wheelhouse glass, and Wren knew what that meant before they knew it in words. A boat did not go dark to travel. A boat went dark to disappear.

They had been aboard nine days. Nine days of watching Elodie Ashgrove work the deck like a prayer—hands sure on the winch, voice low to the engine, that particular economy of motion that came from being alone with a vessel so long the vessel had opinions. Nine days of lying by omission, which was still lying, and nine days of a feeling Wren had no archive classification for.

Now they stood at the rail in the blue hour after sundown and watched Elodie lower the skiff over the stern on the davit lines, slow as a held breath. Into the skiff went the oilskin satchel. Into it went the letters—the ones Elodie hadn't burned—and a tin of hardtack and the river charts for the upper gorge, the uncharted miles, the place where every map Wren had ever filed simply stopped and wrote *here be water*.

Abandoning ship. No—not abandoning. *Relocating the treasure.* The Chancellery's own phrasing, from the briefing in Corvane: *the asset will attempt flight; recover the asset and the correspondence intact.*

Wren had signed for the mission with a fountain pen that felt, even then, like someone else's hand around theirs.

The skiff touched the current. Elodie swung down the lines, quick and silent, and Wren made their choice the way most choices are actually made—not grandly, but in the space between one heartbeat and the next, when there is only time to be who you already are.

They stepped off the rail into the shallows near the weir's apron, waded out, and caught the skiff's painter before the current could argue.

Elodie came up with a knife. Of course they did. It was at Wren's throat before Wren finished saying, "Wait," and the blade was cold and steady and Elodie's eyes above it were wide and furious and lit by the last grey light like coins at the bottom of clear water.

"You," Elodie said. "You've been on my boat nine days."

"I have."

"Surveyor." The word came out spat. "Chancellery surveyor with soft hands and a trunk full of instruments you never once opened where I could see inside them."

"That part was true. I do survey."

"For them."

"For them," Wren agreed. The knife pressed closer. They kept their hands open, palms out, the way you'd approach a spooked animal—or a captain with nothing left to lose. "And then not for them. It changed. I can't tell you exactly when. Somewhere around the third day, when you sang to the engine because you thought I was asleep."

The knife didn't move. "That's not an apology."

"It's not. It's a confession, and it's going to get worse before it gets better. There's a gunboat behind us, Elodie. Two days back, maybe less now. They know you have the chest contents. They know about the upper gorge. And they know—" Wren swallowed, and found the truth sitting in their throat like a stone they'd swallowed weeks ago, "—they know because I told them. Reports. Encoded, sent from your own wheelhouse while you slept. Coordinates. Course headings. Everything."

* * *

**ELODIE**

There is a moment when the world tilts and you learn which things were load-bearing.

Mother's letters in the lead-lined chest: load-bearing. The river itself, gray-green and patient under the stars: load-bearing. Wren—Wren with their clever mouth and their careful hands and the way they'd started leaving coffee on the wheelhouse sill without being asked—Wren had been load-bearing too, and now the whole structure groaned.

Elodie kept the knife where it was because moving it would mean deciding something, and they needed another moment first. The skiff rocked beneath them both. Behind Wren's shoulder the weir murmured its long white syllables, water going over the lip in the dark like a page turning.

"You let me trust you," Elodie said. Their own voice sounded far away, like it was coming up through the hull. "You watched me show you the letters. You read my mother's handwriting. You said—" and here their throat closed, because Wren had said *she must have loved you very much*, and Elodie had believed it came from somewhere real.

"It was real. All of it except the reports." Wren's voice cracked down the middle, honest as split wood. "I'm an archivist, Elodie. Do you understand what that means? I've spent eleven years cataloguing what power does to people. The Chancellery doesn't want your mother's letters because they're evidence. They want them because a living claimant is a lever, and levers are how the Chancellery loves people—loves them into service, loves them into graves. I knew all of that when I took the assignment. I took it anyway, because taking it meant staying close to the asset, and the asset was you."

"So I'm the asset."

"You were." Wren's eyes didn't leave theirs. In the dark they were steady, and steady was worse than pleading. "Somewhere on this river you stopped being an asset and started being the reason I sleep badly. And I kept sending the reports anyway, because the alternative was becoming a person the Chancellery hunts, and I am apparently a coward who needed nine days and a knife at my throat to admit it."

The knife trembled. Elodie hated that it trembled. "Give me one reason not to put you in this skiff alone and let the current decide."

"Because I can help you vanish." Wren said it fast, the words tumbling like spilled cargo. "The next report goes out tonight—I have the cipher wheel in my cabin, I'll send it from here if you want to watch me do it. But it doesn't have to be true. I can feed them false coordinates. Send them chasing up the Marrow fork, into the shallows where their draft won't let them follow. Buy you three days, four. Enough to reach the source."

"The source." Elodie laughed, and it came out ugly. "You know about the source too. Of course you do. Is there anything about me you don't have filed?"

"No," Wren said simply. "That's the whole problem. I know everything about you and none of it matters compared to knowing you. That's not in any report. That's the thing I'd burn the archive to keep."

* * *

**WREN**

The gun came out of Elodie's coat then—a short river pistol, the kind sold at toll locks to people who expected trouble from toll locks—and Wren thought, with strange calm, *so this is how the chapter ends,* and then thought, more usefully, *the cipher wheel is in my left breast pocket, and if I'm shot it goes in the water.*

But Elodie didn't raise it. They turned it in their hands instead, looking at it the way they'd looked at the chest when the crane first brought it dripping into the light—like a gift that felt like a debt.

"My mother traded something," Elodie said quietly. "For me. I don't know what yet. But everyone who's ever protected me has paid for it, and now you're standing in a river offering to pay too, and I am so tired of being worth something to people."

"Then be worth nothing," Wren said. "Be a boat pilot with a leaky salvage rig and bad coffee. Give it all back. Burn the letters, sink the chest, let Vessia stay drowned. I'll send the report saying the asset scuttled everything at the weir and went to ground downstream. They'll believe it, because it's the report a coward writes to save himself, and they know exactly what I am."

"And what are you?"

Wren looked at the pistol, at the knife still loose in Elodie's other hand, at the woman holding both, and told the truth with nothing left to spend on anything else:

"Yours. If you'll have something that lied to you. If not—then the Chancellery's, right up until the moment I stop being useful, and we both know how that ends."

The river filled the silence. Over the weir the water went on falling, patient, indifferent, carrying everything downstream eventually.

Elodie lowered the pistol. Didn't holster it—just lowered it, the way you lower a quarrel you intend to finish later.

"Send the report," they said. "False coordinates. The Marrow fork. And Wren—if one word of it is true, if there's one true bearing in that cipher, I will know. I grew up reading water. I can read you."

"You can," Wren agreed. "You already have. That's why I'm still breathing."

They climbed back aboard together, soaked to the waist, and Wren dug out the cipher wheel with shaking hands and began composing the lie that would keep them alive. Elodie stood at their shoulder the whole time, close enough that their wet sleeve touched Wren's, watching every letter.

Halfway through encoding, Elodie spoke again, eyes on the dark water ahead.

"There's a town upstream. Exiles. People who might remember my mother." A pause. "If you're coming with me, you should know I'm not turning around."

Wren finished the final group of figures, sealed the message cylinder, and set it in the launcher trained on the empty eastern dark.

"Neither am I," they said, and fired the lie out over the river, and turned the Provision's bow toward the narrowing gorge and whatever waited at its drowned end.
