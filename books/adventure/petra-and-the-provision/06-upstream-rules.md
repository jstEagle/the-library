## 6. Upstream Rules

The last charted mile ended at a stone pillar with a Chancellery survey mark chiseled into it, and beyond that the river stopped being a river in any sense the mapmakers had bothered with. It became a rumor with a current.

Wren stood at the Provision's bow rail and watched the gorge swallow the daylight ahead of them. The walls rose sheer on either side, close enough that Elodie could have thrown a line to the rock if they'd wanted to moor, which nobody sane would want to do. The water between the walls ran green-black and fast, braided around boulders the size of tollhouses, and the sound of it came up through the deck plates like something muttering under its breath.

"You're doing the thing," Elodie said from the wheel.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at a stretch of water like it's a document you're translating." They spun the wheel a half-turn; the Provision leaned into the current with the easy obedience of a boat that had spent thirty years learning this river's moods. "What does it say?"

"It says we're idiots."

"Good. As long as it's not saying anything worse."

Wren had been aboard for eleven days now—eleven days since the weir, since the gun between them and the confession after it—and they had learned the grammar of Elodie Ashgrove's silences. There was the silence of concentration, which was comfortable. There was the silence of grief, which Wren tried not to interrupt. And there was the silence they were in now, which was the silence of someone who had traded everything familiar for a rumor at the top of a gorge and was refusing to think about it too loudly.

The pilot's journal lay open on the chart table behind the wheelhouse, weighted down with a brass lamp and a coffee mug that said CORVANE LOCKSKEEPERS' ASSOCIATION, 40 YEARS OF SERVICE. It belonged to one Ambrose Vell, dead forty years, who had run cargo up this stretch when running cargo up this stretch was still legal—or at least still survivable. His handwriting deteriorated as the pages went upstream, as though the gorge itself were pulling the pen out of his fingers.

*Third bend,* he'd written, *the channel splits. Left is deep but the overhang will take your mast. Right is shallow but honest. Take the right. The left has eaten two boats I know of and both of them deserved better.*

And then, three pages later, in ink that looked hurried:

*Marked the spring pool today. Water's down near two feet since meltmelt began. The door only opens when the snow gives back what the mountain took. If you come in low water you'll find nothing but stone and disappointment. Wait for the swell. Everything worth having upstream waits for the swell.*

Wren had read that passage nine times. *Wait for the swell.* It was hope, but it was rationed hope—the kind that came with arithmetic attached. The dry season was tightening its grip on the whole watershed. Every day the waterline dropped another few inches, and every inch was a sandbar surfacing somewhere behind them, a rapid growing teeth somewhere ahead.

"How many days?" Elodie asked, without looking away from the channel.

"Vell says the spring pool opens at high snowmelt. The gauges at Corvane were already falling when we passed through. My guess—" Wren did the arithmetic again, hoping it had improved overnight. It hadn't. "Four days. Maybe five before the melt surge reaches the source. Maybe six before the channel gets too shallow for us to be standing here having this conversation."

"So we have a window that closes from both ends."

"That's about the shape of it."

Elodie nodded once, the way they accepted bad news: completely, immediately, without ceremony. "Then we don't waste daylight arguing about it. Watch the right side of the third bend. Vell says the left eats boats."

* * *

The third bend ate a patrol boat instead.

They heard it before they saw it—the flat percussive slap of an engine running hard against shallow water, echoing off the canyon walls so that it seemed to come from everywhere. Wren flattened against the wheelhouse roof, field glasses up, and found it threading the left-hand channel a quarter mile ahead: a Chancellery cutter, low-slung, gray hull, the twin-stripe pennant snapping at its stern. It was taking the left fork exactly as Vell had warned, and it was taking it too fast, and the overhang—

The sound carried even over the current. A crunch like a wagon axle giving way, then shouting, thin and human and very far from any help.

"They're aground," Wren called down. "Hard aground. Port hull's opened on the overhang footing."

Elodie brought the Provision around the bend on the honest right-hand channel, slow, and for a long moment they could see the whole tableau: the cutter heeled over against the rocks, water already brown around her stern where the river was drinking her, four Chancellery men scrambling along her deck in their neat gray uniforms, one of them waving his arms at the Provision with the frantic authority of a man whose paperwork could not save him.

"Don't," Wren said quietly. They didn't know they'd said it aloud until Elodie glanced up.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to think about it."

"I think about lots of things." But Elodie held their course, mid-channel, neither closing distance nor fleeing, just passing—because fleeing was a confession, and stopping was a gift. The shouting followed them around the next bend and faded into the mutter of the water.

"They'll walk out," Wren said. "There's a ranger trail on the north wall. They'll lose two days and report a wreck, and by the time anyone believes them, we'll be—"

"Past caring." Elodie's hands were steady on the wheel. "You know what bothers me? I felt glad. When she went aground. Four men nearly drowned and my first feeling was *good*, because it buys us time." They shook their head. "My mother used to say power does that to people. Makes other people's disasters feel like weather."

Wren thought about the transmission they'd sent from the weir—the false coordinates, the lie they'd fed to their handlers, the career burning behind them like a bridge with the planks pried up. "It's not power doing that," they said. "It's fear. Fear feels like weather too. You get used to reading it."

"And what am I reading right now?"

Wren looked at them—at the salt-stiff hair, the hands that knew every inch of this boat, the face of a person who'd learned that morning that their inheritance was built on a bargain struck over their own infant life, and had steered through a gorge anyway. "Somebody deciding who they're going to be," Wren said. "It's my favorite thing I've ever archived."

That earned a short laugh, quickly swallowed, but real. In eleven days Wren had learned to count those laughs like coins.

* * *

They ran the narrows at dusk, because Vell's journal insisted the patrols anchored at night and the shallows were easier to read in low slanting light—every submerged bar showed as a smooth dark tongue where the current wrinkled differently. Wren stood at the bow with a lantern shuttered to a slit, calling depths by eye while Elodie worked the wheel by ear, listening to the water the way other people listened to music.

"Twelve feet. Nine. Nine and a half—hard to starboard, there's a rock shadow."

"I see it."

"Six feet off the port quarter, doesn't matter, hold your line—"

"I *see* it, Wren."

There it was again: the partnership assembling itself out of necessity, joint by joint, until Wren realized they had stopped thinking of themselves as a passenger, or a spy, or even a guest. Somewhere in the last hundred miles they had become crew. The realization sat in their chest with a weight that had nothing to do with guilt and everything to do with how much it would cost to lose.

They cleared the narrows as full dark came down and tied off in a slot canyon Vell had marked as a safe harbor—a crack in the south wall wide enough for the Provision, deep enough to hide her hull from the river, walled high enough to hide the lamplight. Elodie killed the engine and the silence that followed was enormous, broken only by the drip of the gorge and the far-off conversation of the current.

They ate cold rice and pickled fish in the wheelhouse, and Wren read Vell aloud while Elodie patched a chafe-worn mooring line, because Elodie's hands needed work the way other people's needed sleep.

*"Day nineteen,"* Wren read. *"Met a man going downstream who told me the archive at the source is a tomb and a treasury and a trap, and that no good ever came of opening tombs. I told him no good ever came of leaving knowledge underwater either. He said that depends whose knowledge. I've been thinking about that all day."*

Elodie's hands went still on the rope.

"Whose knowledge," they repeated softly.

"The record of Vessia," Wren said. "Everything your mother hid. Everything the Chancellery wants drowned forever. It's not neutral, Elodie. It never was. It's the account book of a kingdom that poisoned itself, and whoever holds it holds a weapon."

"I know." Elodie coiled the rope with sudden precision. "I've known since the toll of names. I'm not going up there to claim anything. I'm going up there to *read*. There's a difference. One of them is what my mother refused to do, and one of them is what killed her."

Wren set the journal down. "And after you've read it?"

"After I've read it, I'll decide." They looked up, and the lamplight made their eyes very steady. "That's the rule upstream, Wren. Nobody decides for anybody else. Not the Chancellery. Not the dead. Not you."

"Not me," Wren agreed, and meant it, and understood—as they hadn't fully understood at the weir, guns out, hearts out—that meaning it was the whole of what they had to offer. Not safety. Not certainty. Just this: two people in a hidden slot of a gorge, agreeing that each of them got to choose.

Outside, the river dropped another inch in the dark, patient as arithmetic.

Wren woke once in the small hours to the sound of Elodie standing at the rail, looking upstream, and heard them say—to the water, or to their mother, or to no one—*"I'm coming. Wait for me."*

And far above, past the last of the light, the mountain held its snow, counting down the days toward the swell.
