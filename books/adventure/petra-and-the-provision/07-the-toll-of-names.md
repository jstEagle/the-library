## 7. The Toll of Names

The town had no name on any chart Elodie owned, which was how she knew they'd found the right place.

It clung to the river's east bank where the gorge widened just enough to permit a village, a scatter of slate roofs and drying nets wedged between cliff and current. The water here ran the color of old pewter, cold and fast, and the people who moved along the stone landing moved the way water did—quick, quiet, and never straight toward you.

"Vessians," Wren said, from the rail of the *Provision*. Not a question.

"You can tell from a hundred yards?"

"From the way they're pretending not to look at us." Wren's mouth curved, but their eyes didn't. "Nobody watches strangers that carefully unless strangers once cost them something."

Elodie brought the boat in herself, Wren on the line, and the villagers let them moor without a word of welcome or refusal. A woman with a face like weathered oak watched from the fish-house door, arms folded, and when Elodie met her gaze the woman jerked her chin toward the path above the landing—the universal grammar of *come, then, if you must*.

They climbed through terraces of onion beds and beehives to a house built half into the cliff, its door painted a blue so deep it was nearly black. The woman followed them without hurrying. Inside, the room smelled of smoke and tallow and, beneath it, something fainter—dried lavender, the kind that grew in Vessia's river gardens, if the letters in Elodie's chest could be believed.

The woman set out three cups and poured something clear and harsh that Elodie drank without tasting.

"You fly no colors," the woman said. Her accent was old, vowels shaped by a court that had been mud for thirty years. "But your boat's lines are Vessian. Pre-purge yard, that stern. And you have his chin."

"Whose chin?"

"The king who drowned." She said it without ceremony, the way one might say *the bridge that fell*. "You have his chin, girl, and you came upriver in a boat built by his shipwrights, and you're asking whose chin. Either you're a fool or you're her daughter."

The room went very still. Outside, the river said its one long word against the stones.

"You knew my mother."

"I knew what she cost us." The woman sat, folding her hands on the table as if settling accounts. "I am Sefa Andrel. My family kept the toll of names—every Vessian soul registered, every heir in hiding, every promise made in the last days. I am the last of the keepers, and I am old, and I have waited a long time to be angry at the right person." Her eyes, pale as river ice, fixed on Elodie. "Sit down. You'll want to be sitting."

* * *

Elodie had built a story of her mother over thirty years, the way a boat is built: plank by plank, each piece fitted to the last, the whole of it made to float. Her mother had fled the purge. Had run with a child and a false name and had never looked back, because looking back was how you drowned. Elodie had built her life on that hull and sailed it every day.

Sefa Andrel took it apart in an hour.

"She didn't flee," the old woman said. "She *negotiated*. The Chancellery's men had the archive's location by the third week of the purge—they had it from a minister who sang like a kettle. One night of fire would have ended everything: the records, the proof of bloodlines, the last legitimate claimants. All of it." Sefa's cup sat untouched before her. "And then it didn't. The Chancellery burned the palace, burned the registry, burned the names—but the archive itself, the deep one at the source? Untouched. Sealed. Why would men like that leave a thing like that?"

"Because they couldn't find it."

"Because they were *paid* to lose it." Sefa leaned forward. "Your mother went to them. Walked into the Chancellery's river camp with an infant on her hip and gave them the one thing they wanted more than her head—a way to make the claim go dormant forever. Not destroyed. Dormant. Buried where no one would look, guarded by water and forgetting. In exchange, the child was struck from every list. Not hunted. Not watched. *Nothing*. A girl who did not exist could not inherit, and so was allowed to live."

The fire ticked in its grate. Somewhere above the house, a hive hummed, fat and indifferent.

"She traded the archive," Elodie said. Her voice came out flat, a plank laid down over water. "For me."

"She traded *you*," Sefa said, and did not soften it. "Your life for their silence. The archive stays lost, the Chancellery stays blind, and you grow up a salvage captain who thinks her mother was a refugee. Everyone keeps their bargain. Everyone lives." She finally lifted her cup. "It was, by the standards of that year, a mercy. I have watched kinder people do crueler things."

Elodie stood. She didn't remember deciding to. Her legs did it the way the river does things—without asking, all at once.

"Then why tell me?"

"Because the chest surfaced." Sefa's calm didn't waver. "Because the Chancellery's boats have been on this water for a month, asking after salvage, and because the bargain is already broken whether you meant to break it or not. Because you are going upstream to that archive, girl—I can see it in you, you have her jaw when she'd made up her mind—and you should know what it *is*. It isn't a treasure. It's a debt she left in your name. And debts," she said, "are the only inheritance Vessia ever managed to pass on intact."

* * *

Wren found her at the landing an hour past dark, sitting on the mooring bollard with her boots nearly in the water. They sat down beside her without speaking, shoulder to shoulder, and let the cold come up through the stone.

"You heard," Elodie said.

"Sefa Andrel's granddaughter talks in her sleep." Wren's voice was gentle. "Half the village probably knows by now. They'll know by morning that the heir's on their landing."

"Then we leave at first light."

"Elodie." Wren turned toward her. In the dark their face was mostly voice. "Talk to me."

"She sold it." The words came out of her the way a hook comes out of a fish—tearing something on the way. "Thirty years I thought she ran. That she was afraid, that she was *small*, that she did what any of us would do. And the whole time she was making deals with the people who drowned a kingdom. She sat down with them and she *traded*, Wren. And what she traded was—" Elodie's hands closed on the bollard's wet iron. "She traded me. My life for their blindness. She decided what my life was worth, and she spent it, and she never asked. She never *asked*."

"No. She didn't."

"I would have paid it." Elodie heard her own voice crack and hated it. "If she'd come to me—any year, any hour of any year—and said, *your life for theirs*, I'd have paid it. That's not the wound. The wound is that she decided I was hers to spend. That's what an inheritance *is*, isn't it? Someone else's decision, wearing your name."

Wren was quiet a long moment. The current pulled at the mooring lines, patient, certain.

"When I was a boy," they said at last, "the Chancellery gave me my first archive assignment. A family record. I opened it and found my own grandfather's name in it—listed as an informant. He'd sold a neighbor to save his sons. I hated him for years. Carried it like a stone." A breath. "Then I got old enough to have someone I'd trade everything for, and I understood him all at once, and I've never decided which understanding is worse."

"You think I should forgive her."

"I think forgiveness is a luxury and I'm not asking for it." Wren's hand found hers on the cold iron, warm against the river chill. "I think you should be angry at the right thing. Not at her for saving you. At them—for building a world where love has to be spent like coin. At the throne that made her choose. Because that's the thing you're sailing toward, Elodie. That archive isn't proof you're a queen. It's proof the crown is a debt collector. It always was."

Elodie looked upriver, where the gorge narrowed into blackness and the last charted mile waited beyond it. Somewhere up there, sealed in snowmelt water, sat the record of everything—her mother's signature on the bargain, the kings' poisons, the whole rotten ledger of Vessia. The thing her mother had paid so much to bury. The thing the Chancellery would drown the river to keep buried.

And the thing, she understood now with a clarity like cold water, that she could not leave where it was. Not anymore. Because a debt left buried was a debt her own child would inherit someday, or some stranger's child, dredged up like a chest from the mud, and the toll would be paid again and again down the current of years.

"Then we don't go to claim it," Elodie said slowly. "And we don't go to bury it again. We go to *read the whole account*. Every name. Every price." She stood, and the river's cold fell away from her boots. "And then we decide what a kingdom's word is actually worth."

Wren rose beside her. "The Chancellery will decide too. There was a gunboat at the last lock, Elodie. Grey hull, no name painted. They're not asking after salvage anymore."

Elodie looked at the dark water, and for the first time since the chest broke the surface of her life, she wasn't afraid of what it held.

"Then we'd better be faster than they are," she said, and went to wake the *Provision*.
