## 8. Dry Season

*Wren*

The river had been shrinking for three days, and Wren had been counting.

It was the kind of arithmetic no one taught in the Chancellery's archive school: how many inches of water a boat like the *Provision* needed under her keel, how fast a dry-season channel could fall in an afternoon of wind and heat, how long a lie could hold its shape before it went soft at the edges. Wren had learned all of it in the last week, standing at Elodie's elbow with a surveyor's notebook full of false figures, watching the waterline drop past marks on the pilings like a fever breaking in reverse.

"Sandbar ahead," Elodie said. "Starboard side. See how the current wrinkles?"

Wren saw. The river's skin had changed—smooth glass over deep water giving way to a shivering, braided texture where the channel split around something shallow. The morning light came down the gorge sideways, gilding the wrack lines on the rocks: pale bands of dried silt marking where the water used to be, each one higher than the last, a staircase leading down into drought.

"I see it."

"You keep seeing it wrong," Elodie said, but there was no edge in it. There hadn't been since the weir, since the guns, since the confession that should have ended everything and instead had become this strange shared cargo they carried together. "You chart the safe side. I'll take us through the middle. Middle's deeper than it looks."

"Middle is never deeper than it looks. That's what my grandmother said about men."

Elodie laughed—a short, surprised sound—and for a moment the gorge was just a beautiful terrible place and not a trap closing by inches. Then the bend opened ahead of them, and Wren stopped laughing too.

The gunboat sat in the wide pool below the narrows like a knife laid across a throat.

Grey hull, Chancellery pennant snapping in the upstream wind, a swivel gun lashed amidships and covered with canvas that fooled no one. It was anchored, patient, exactly where the channel pinched—the one place the *Provision* could not pass without coming inside easy range.

"They've been reading my reports," Wren said quietly.

"Your false reports."

"Someone's comparing them against someone else's true ones." Wren's mouth had gone dry. "There's another asset on the river. There always was. I told you the Chancellery doesn't send one archivist after a crown."

Elodie's hands moved on the wheel, steady, bringing the bow around toward the northern bank where the water looked shallowest and the rocks looked worst. "Then we don't go through the middle."

"There is no around. Look at the banks." The gorge walls had stepped back here into a floodplain of gravel and dead reeds, exposed for thirty feet above the current line—a beach of the river's own making, a shoreline that said plainly: *I am not what I was.* "The channel's the only door, and they're sitting in the frame."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The engine idled beneath their feet, a heartbeat held in reserve. Somewhere upstream a bird cried out, once, and got no answer.

"I can go ashore," Wren said.

Elodie turned. The light caught the grey at their temple, the salt-stiff collar of the borrowed shirt, the eyes that had lied so beautifully at Corvane Landing and now could not manage even a small deception.

"I have credentials," Wren went on, faster now. "Real ones. I walk up their gangplank, I tell them I've tracked the chest to a tributary two miles south—I show them my notes, my charts, all the lovely fiction we built together. They'll want to verify. That means sending the launch, maybe the whole boat, up a dead channel while the sun's still high. It buys you the narrows. It buys you hours."

"No."

"It buys you the source, Elodie."

"*No.*" The word cracked across the deck like a whip over water. Elodie's knuckles were white on the wheel spokes. "You are not walking onto that boat. You know what they do to assets who turn. You told me yourself—you told me what happened to the last archivist who fed them bad coordinates. They didn't even—" Elodie stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, which was somehow worse. "I didn't spend three weeks learning the sound of you breathing in the next cabin just to trade you for a head start."

Wren felt something in their chest come loose, some final mooring line, and knew it showed on their face.

"Then we think of something else," Wren said, "because the water's dropping, love, and it won't wait for us to be brave in a comfortable way."

* * *

*Elodie*

The plan, when it finally came, came from the river itself.

Elodie stood at the rail and watched the current braid around the sandbar below the gunboat, and watched the way the shallows shone—not brown, not green, but the pale milk-gold of water running thin over white gravel. Two feet. Maybe less, in the channels that forked off from the main stem. No pilot alive would take a loaded boat through them.

But the *Provision* wasn't loaded. Not anymore. They'd burned the ballast stone at the weir, stripped her to frames and planking, and Elodie had spent twenty years learning every inch of her draft the way other people learned prayers.

"She floats in eighteen inches," Elodie said. "Empty. Maybe sixteen if I'm honest with myself, and I'm feeling honest."

Wren came to stand beside them, close enough that their shoulders touched. "The main channel's four feet if it's a foot."

"The main channel has a gunboat in it." Elodie pointed, tracing the line with one finger. "There. North of the bar. It looks like a gravel field, but the old pilots cut through there during the drought year—there's a scoured trench along the bluff side, the journal mentioned it, remember? 'The river remembers its bones.' We run the bones."

"And if the bones have filled in since the journal was written?"

"Then we find out very quickly, and very loudly, and we swim for it." Elodie turned to face them fully. "And you stay aboard. That's the price. That's the whole price. If we're doing this, we're doing it together—all the way to the bottom if it comes to that."

Something crossed Wren's face—fear, and underneath the fear, relief, and underneath the relief, that thing they had both been careful not to name out loud because names made things real and real things could be taken away.

"Together," Wren said. "All the way to the bottom."

Elodie rang the engine bell twice and put the wheel hard north.

* * *

*Wren*

The gunboat saw them immediately. Of course it did. A riverboat turning hard for the shallows was either desperate or insane, and the Chancellery had opinions about both.

The canvas came off the swivel gun. A voice crackled across the water through a speaking-trumpet, distorted by wind and distance: "*Vessel Provision, heave to for inspection by authority of the Chancellor's Office—*"

Elodie did not heave to. Elodie opened the throttle until the whole hull shuddered, and the *Provision* leapt forward like a dog slipping a chain.

They came into the shallows at speed, which was madness, which was the only way. The first hundred yards were fine—water peeling away from the bow in bright fans, the gravel bar sliding past to starboard close enough to touch. Wren stood at the bow sounding with a boathook, calling depths like a litany: "*Three feet. Two and a half. Two—*" and felt each number land in Elodie's hands through the wheel, through the hull, through whatever it was that had grown between them, wordless and load-bearing as a keel.

Then the boathook hit bottom and stayed there, nearly tearing from Wren's grip, and the channel began to fail.

"*Eighteen inches! Fifteen—Elodie—*"

"I feel it!" Elodie's voice came back fierce and level. The engine note climbed, screaming, the propeller chewing water that wasn't there. The boat slowed by degrees, the river rising up around them like a hand closing. Ahead, the scoured trench bent along the bluff, visible now as a darker ribbon—but between them and it lay fifty yards of gravel shoal shining in the sun like a dare.

Behind them, the gunboat's launch was in the water. Oars flashing. Men shouting. But the launch drew three feet if it drew an inch, and it was already wallowing at the edge of the bar, its crew realizing, in real time, that the river itself had chosen a side.

"*Twelve inches,*" Wren called, and then stopped calling numbers, because the numbers had stopped mattering. What mattered was momentum, and faith, and the woman at the wheel who refused, absolutely refused, to sacrifice anything she loved—including, Wren understood with a clarity that felt like sunlight, including herself.

The *Provision* ground once—a long, horrible shudder, gravel kissing the keel—and Wren threw their whole weight to port, and Elodie swung the wheel, and the boat slewed sideways, half-sliding, half-floating, surfing on its own wake into the dark ribbon of the trench.

Water rose under the hull. Two feet. Three. The propeller bit clean water and the engine dropped from scream to roar, and they were through.

The narrows swallowed them. The gunboat fell behind, anchored fast in water too deep to move quickly and too proud to admit it, its launch floundering on the bar like a beetle on its back. Wren stood at the bow shaking, boathook still in hand, and laughed until their ribs hurt.

* * *

*Elodie*

They ran until the light went long and copper, until the gorge narrowed to a slot of stone and sky, until the patrols were a memory and the only sound was the engine and the water and their own two hearts refusing to slow down.

At dusk they tied off in a side slough willow-shrouded and secret, and Elodie sat down on the deck plates and discovered her legs would not hold her any longer. Wren sat beside her. Their shoulders found each other again, the way compass needles find north.

"That was the worst piloting I've ever seen," Wren said. "And the best."

"It was the only piloting there was." Elodie tipped their head back against the cabin trunk. Above them the first stars were coming out over the rim of the world. "The water's still falling. You know that. Every hour we sit here, the source gets shallower, the archive gets further away, and the snowmelt window gets smaller."

"I know."

"And they'll come again. Not the gunboat—something lighter. Something that draws less water than we do." Elodie closed their eyes. In the dark behind their eyelids: her mother's face, the letters, the bargain she'd never consented to, the throne waiting upstream like a debt collector. "We have maybe two days of river left in her. Maybe less."

Wren's hand found theirs in the gathering dark. Did not squeeze. Just held on, the way you hold a rope over deep water.

"Then we'd better not waste tomorrow," Wren said.

Elodie opened their eyes. Downstream, far behind them, a light was moving along the bluff tops—not a campfire, not a lantern. Too steady. Too purposeful. Someone was walking the ridge in the dark, keeping pace with the river, keeping pace with them.

"Wren," Elodie said quietly. "Who walks ridgelines at night?"

Wren followed their gaze, and Elodie felt them go still.

"Nobody good," Wren said.
