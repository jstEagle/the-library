## 9. The Source

The river ended the way all rivers end—not with a wall but with a whisper.

Elodie brought the *Provision* in on the last of her steam, paddles turning slow as a dying heart, and let the hull kiss the shallows below a shelf of black stone. Above it, veiled in spray and morning mist, the spring came out of the mountain like something being born: a wide, cold mouth of water pouring from a cleft that no chart admitted existed, feeding the river that fed everything downstream—Corvane Landing, the toll locks, the weirs, the drowned kingdom itself.

Wren stood at the rail beside her, saying nothing. They had said very little for two days. Some silences were wounds; this one was a splint.

"High snowmelt," Elodie said. "The pilot's journal wasn't lying."

The cleft was open. All winter, Wren had explained, the archive behind it sat drowned under thirty feet of standing water, sealed by its own builders so that only the flood would give it up—and only at the crest of the melt, for a handful of hours, did the water drop enough to walk in. The old pilot had called it the Library That Breathes. It was breathing now. Elodie could hear it: a slow exhalation through stone, water finding its way out of chambers that had held their secrets for twenty years.

She tied off at a root-bare alder and went over the side into knee-deep cold that stole her breath. Wren followed, carrying the lamp and the oilcloth-wrapped bundle of letters she'd kept from the chest—the ones she hadn't burned, back when burning one had seemed like a compromise instead of a confession.

The entrance was exactly where the journal's crude map promised: beneath an overhang furred with moss, a doorway cut square and deliberate, its lintel carved with the Vessian crest—a crown above crossed keys, half-eaten by lichen. The water inside stood waist-deep and falling as they watched, draining away down channels cut into the floor, as if the archive were emptying its lungs.

"Hours," Wren said quietly. "Maybe three. The journal says the seal closes at dusk regardless."

"Then we don't waste them arguing about who goes first." Elodie waded in.

* * *

Inside, the air smelled of stone and iron and time. Niches lined the walls in ordered ranks, hundreds of them, each holding a lead canister stamped with the same crowned keys. The Vessians had built their archive to be read by whoever the flood chose. There was no lock. There had never been a need for one; the river itself was the gatekeeper, and the river answered to no chancellor.

Elodie lifted the first canister free. It came out trailing water like a living thing, and the wax seal cracked under her thumb with a sound like a knuckle popping.

She had come expecting vindication. She had told herself the story all the way upstream: her mother, hidden heir, betrayed by history; the proof waiting here to set the record straight; the Chancellery's hunters exposed as thieves of a birthright. She had imagined reading her mother's name in a royal hand and finally understanding why Marisol Ashgrove had run a salvage boat and never once spoken of where she came from.

The first canister held inventories. Grain shipments, dated across three years of famine in the southern provinces. Receipts signed by a minister whose name Elodie recognized from Wren's histories—Lord Cassel, architect of the Reconciliation. The grain had not gone south. It had gone to the capital, to feed the court's winter festivities, while the provinces ate bark and clay. There was a marginal note in a clerk's careful hand: *His Majesty directs that the shortfall be attributed to banditry.*

"Banditry," Elodie repeated aloud. Her voice fell flat in the stone room.

Wren had opened a canister two niches down. They read in silence for a long moment, then set it down very carefully, the way you set down something venomous.

"What is it?"

"The king's private correspondence." Wren's voice had gone thin. "He poisoned his brother. Then his brother's sons. There are payments here—to an apothecary, itemized. Dosages." They looked up, and the lamplight made their face strange. "Elodie, there are eleven years of these."

She should have stopped then. She knows that now. But the archive was open and the water was falling and some part of her still believed that if she read far enough she'd find the corner of it where her mother lived—some letter, some order, some single clean page that would make the blood in her veins mean something other than this.

So she read.

Canister after canister. The kings of Vessia had been meticulous record-keepers of their own rot. A queen who drowned a rival's petitioners in the harbor and paid the harbormaster to log it as an accident. A war started deliberately against a weaker neighbor to distract from a currency collapse, with casualty projections in the margins, corrected upward in a second hand. A purge—*the* purge, the one that had supposedly exterminated every legitimate heir—authorized not by invaders or revolutionaries but by the crown itself, against its own cousins, because a bastard line had grown popular in the provinces and legitimacy, the king's minister observed, was *a commodity best kept scarce.*

Elodie sat down on a submerged bench cut into the rock, the water soaking through her trousers, and held a page in both hands because they were shaking too hard to hold it any other way.

"They weren't murdered," she said. "They were pruned."

"Yes." Wren crouched at the edge of the lamplight, keeping their distance as though she were something that might go off. "I'm sorry. I read the summaries in the Chancellery files and I thought—I thought it was propaganda. Exiles telling stories. But it's all here. In their own hands."

"My mother knew this." Elodie heard her own voice as if from outside. "She grew up in this. This was her inheritance. Banquets during famines. Poison bought by the ounce." She looked up. "And she traded it. Didn't she? Downstream, in that town—they told me she traded the archive's location for my life. I hated her for it. I've been hating her for it since Corvane Landing."

"And now?"

"Now I think she looked at all this—" Elodie turned slowly, taking in the niches, the hundreds of leaden throats full of dead men's handwriting, "—and she understood what I'm only understanding now. It was never a kingdom. It was a machine for making other people pay for one family's comfort. And the crown isn't the prize, Wren. The crown is the disease. Every heir who ever wore it caught it. Every minister. Every clerk who wrote *attributed to banditry* in a neat little hand and went home to his supper."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere deep in the archive, water moved—a chamber closing, or opening; the building breathing around them.

"There's more," Wren said softly. "There's always more. Do you want to keep reading?"

Elodie almost laughed. "What I want is to burn this whole mountain down. What good is truth that only proves the throne was rotten from the first stone?" She stood, water streaming off her. "The Chancellery wants this archive because whoever holds it holds the claim. Proof of legitimacy. My legitimacy. And I could walk out of here with an armful of canisters and a lawyer's lifetime of argument, and in ten years I'd be signing requisitions during someone else's famine, telling myself it was necessary, writing it all down in my own neat hand because that's what this place does—it teaches you to keep records of your sins so you can call them history."

Wren said nothing. They had learned, somewhere on the river between the weir and the gorge, when to let her talk.

* * *

In the last hour, with dusk gathering in the cleft behind them and the water beginning its slow return, Elodie found the final canister. It sat apart from the others, in a niche at chest height, unmarked except for a single scratched initial: *M.*

Her mother's hand. She knew it before she touched the paper—the same slanted script that had addressed the letters in the chest, the same pen that had written *my daughter will owe no one* across a page Elodie had carried against her heart for weeks.

It was a deposition. Dated two years before the purge, witnessed by three names Elodie didn't recognize. Marisol Ashgrove—Marisol of Vessia, then, nineteen years old and already heir to everything—had entered this archive and read what Elodie had read, and had written down what she concluded, and had sealed it here for the flood to decide.

*I have read the record entire,* it began. *I am the last of the line and I find nothing in it worth lastness. If anyone reads this, know that I do not renounce the crown out of fear. I renounce it out of arithmetic. Count the dead in these canisters. Count the grain. No throne built on this foundation can be repaired by a better mason. It can only be inherited, and inheritance is how the rot keeps its grip.*

*I have a daughter coming. She will never owe this house anything. That is the only gift our name can give her.*

Elodie read it twice. The second time, the water was at her knees and rising, and Wren was calling her name gently from the doorway, and the light in the cleft had gone the color of brass.

She folded the deposition along its old creases and put it inside her coat, against her heart, next to where the burned letter's ashes had once stained the lining. Not because it was a claim. Because it was a map. Her mother had walked this exact floor, felt this exact horror, and chosen—and Elodie needed, just for one more hour, to stand where she had stood.

"Elodie." Wren's voice, closer now. "The water's turning. We have to go."

She took the lamp and looked back once at the niches, the hundreds of them, the whole drowned weight of a nation's sins filed and sealed and patient. The river was coming back to take custody of them, as it had for twenty years, as it would have forever if the Chancellery's dredges had never come sniffing.

But the river, she thought, had also given them up. To her. Which meant the choice had always been hers, and her mother had known that too—had trusted the flood to deliver the record to whoever was strong enough to carry it and sane enough to refuse it.

"Coming," Elodie said.

They waded out together into the last of the light, and behind them the water rose over the threshold, sealing the archive's throat, and the spring above poured on indifferent and eternal, feeding the river that would carry whatever Elodie decided downstream to everyone.

She stood in the shallows wringing out her sleeves, and the decision arrived not like thunder but like a boat settling at its mooring—quiet, inevitable, hers.

"Wren," she said. "How much lamp oil did we bring?"
