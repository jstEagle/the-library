## 10. Provision

The spring came up on them like a held breath.

Elodie had imagined it a hundred ways since Corvane Landing—a cataract, a geyser, some theatrical wound in the mountain—but the source of the Vessa was none of those things. It was a pool the size of a threshing floor, ringed in black stone, so clear that the eye went down and down into blue-green dark and found no bottom it could trust. Snowmelt fed it from seams high on the gorge walls, thin white threads unraveling into water that did not seem to move at all. And rising from the center of the pool, half-drowned, half-revealed by the year's highest water, stood the archive.

It was smaller than Elodie had expected. A tower, or what remained of one—three stories of pale masonry, its lower floors swallowed whole, its upper windows open to the air like the eye sockets of something patient. A stair emerged from the waterline and climbed the outside wall, each step furred with moss except where the recent flood had scrubbed it clean.

"It's open," Wren said quietly, beside her at the rail. "The door. Look."

The great doors of the archive stood ajar above the waterline, wedged wide by whatever mechanism her mother's people had built to admit the river—and only at this season, only for these few days, when the snowmelt lifted the water high enough to unseal the locks below. An archive designed to drown. Designed to be entered once a year, briefly, by whoever knew the way, and sealed again against all the rest.

"Your mother knew," Wren said. "She must have come here."

"She never told me she'd been anywhere." Elodie killed the engine. The Provision drifted the last fifty yards in silence, and the silence was enormous—no lock-klaxons, no patrol engines, no Chancellery horns. Just water moving over stone somewhere beneath them, patient as arithmetic. "Bring us alongside the stair. Gently. I don't want to crack the hull on anything under the surface."

They worked the boat in together without speaking, the way they'd learned to across nine hundred miles of narrowing river—Wren at the bow line, Elodie at the wheel, a language of small gestures replacing speech. The Provision kissed the black stone and held there, snubbed fast, rocking gently against the current that poured invisibly out of the pool and began, from this point forward, to be the Vessa.

The stairs were slick but sound. Elodie climbed with the satchel of letters on her shoulder—the ones she'd kept, the ones she hadn't burned that first night in her cabin when burning had still felt like a choice she could make piecemeal. Behind her, Wren carried the oil and the boat's signal flares, because fire wanted help up here where everything was wet.

The archive's upper chamber was one long room under a vaulted ceiling, its shelves carved directly into the walls, and every shelf was full.

Not ledgers. Not treaties. Not the dry instruments of state she had braced herself for. Books. Thousands of them, swollen with damp, their spines lettered in gold that had not yet tarnished: histories, poems, seed almanacs, children's primers, medical texts, song collections annotated in three hands. Maps of canals that had never been dug. A botanical survey of provinces Elodie had known only as names on Chancellery charts, drawn in loving watercolor by someone who had clearly walked every field.

"They weren't what the stories say," Wren said from the doorway. Their voice had gone strange.

"No," Elodie said. "They were worse than that. Or better." She pulled a volume at random—a baker's ledger, decades of daily bread prices in a neat merchant's hand—and understood suddenly what she was looking at. Not the corruption. The country. The whole ordinary, breathing, quarrelsome, baking, singing country that the Chancellery had drowned along with its kings, and then spent eighty years insisting had deserved it.

Somewhere in these shelves, she knew, was the other record too. The poisonings. The starved provinces. The kings who had made Vessia a thing that could be broken. She didn't go looking for them. She had read enough of that history in the letters, in the exile towns, in her mother's silences. Both records were true. That was the whole horror of inheritance: you never got to inherit only the good shelves.

She set the satchel on a reading table whose top was a slab of green marble, cold as riverbed under her palms.

"You don't have to do this alone," Wren said.

"I know." She looked at the last letter—the one she'd kept unburned through everything, the one addressed in her mother's hand to *whoever the river chooses*. She had promised herself she'd read it here, at the source, where it would mean the most. Now, standing in the drowned library of a dead nation with the claim to its throne in her satchel, she found she already knew what it said. It said *I'm sorry*. It said *I chose you*. It said *do what I couldn't*.

Her mother had traded the archive's location for her daughter's life, and had spent the rest of her years piloting a salvage boat up and down the river that had eaten her kingdom, dredging its bones, keeping its memory alive in the only way left to her—by touching it, hauling it, hauling it up into the light and letting it go again.

*Oh,* Elodie thought. *That's what you were doing. All those runs. You weren't hiding. You were grieving.*

She took the letter to the window and read it anyway, because her mother deserved to be heard twice.

Then she folded it small and put it inside her shirt, against her skin, and turned to the shelves.

* * *

Wren watched Elodie work and understood, finally, why the Chancellery had been wrong about everything.

They had sent Wren upriver to recover a chest of documents—a legal instrument, a threat, a weapon to be confiscated and locked away. They had spoken of the Vessian claim the way they spoke of smuggled ordnance. But watching Elodie move through the archive, pulling books down and stacking them with a salvager's efficiency, an odd tenderness in the rough economy of her hands, Wren saw that the Chancellery had never understood what they were chasing. You could confiscate a crown. You could not confiscate this—the moment when a woman who had every right to a throne stood in its antechamber and started building a pyre.

"The flares won't take on damp paper," Wren said. "We need to get a real fire going first. There's a hearth."

There was. Of course there was—an archive built to be visited once a year would have a hearth, and dry kindling laid in it by the last hand to pass through, eighty years ago or five, and the kindling caught on the second strike as if it had only been waiting.

They worked the fire outward from the hearth, shelf by shelf, and the archive began to give up its warmth. Eighty years of damp fought them; the books burned slowly, grudgingly, hissing like living things. Elodie fed the flames with a poker taken from the stand, methodical, working from the far wall toward the door so the fire would have nowhere to go but through the record and out the windows.

Halfway down the third shelf she stopped. Her hand rested on a slim volume bound in blue, and Wren saw her shoulders change.

"This one's a hymnal," she said. "Children's songs." She opened it. Read. And then, instead of throwing it on the fire, she sat down on the cold stone floor with it in her lap, ash drifting around her like grey snow, and read it cover to cover while the rest of a nation burned behind her.

Wren sat down beside her and waited.

"She wrote in the margins," Elodie said at last. Her voice was wrecked and steady at once. "My mother. These are her notes. She came here—she must have come here the year I was born. She copied out two of the songs in her own hand, on the last page. For me." She closed the book. Held it a long moment. Then she stood, walked to the hearth, and put the hymnal on the coals—and took it back off again before the flame caught, tearing out only the last page, folding it with her mother's letter.

"The rest burns," she said. "This comes home with us. Someone should sing them."

Wren nodded, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the fire wasn't already saying better.

By late afternoon the vaulted ceiling was shedding flakes of soot and the heat had driven them both out onto the exterior stair. Below, the pool had gone strange—ash on water, a grey skin spreading from the base of the tower where the flooded lower floors were giving up their contents at last, page by page, decade by decade, rising through thirty feet of impossibly clear water like slow snow falling upward.

Elodie stood at the rail and watched her inheritance dissolve.

"There," Wren said softly.

The current took it. That was the thing Elodie had counted on without letting herself name it: the source was not a pool, it was a throat. Everything that entered the spring went downstream—to the weirs, the locks, the toll stations, the landings, the exile towns, all the way to the delta and the sea. Every scrap of ash that rose from the drowned archive now began the long journey Elodie had just finished making in reverse, past every place that had ever tried to stop her, carrying the end of the claim to everyone who had ever feared it or hoped for it.

Let the Chancellery dredge *that*, she thought. Let them drag the river for the next eighty years and pull up nothing but the ashes of bread prices and children's songs.

She stayed until the last of the light went off the water. Then she touched Wren's elbow, and they climbed down to the boat together.

* * *

They ran the gorge by night because the water was dropping and every hour mattered, and because Elodie had discovered she loved the river best exactly now—in the dark, at speed, with the banks blurring past and the stars doubled in the black water ahead. The dry season wind had come around at last, blowing steadily downriver out of the north, and the Provision ran before it with her engine at half-throttle and her wake singing, doing better than she had any right to.

Wren had the wheel. Elodie sat on the cargo hatch with the chart case open on her knees, though she no longer needed it; she knew every bend of the lower gorge now the way she knew her own hands. The gunboat, if it was still hunting, was hunting a ghost—the false coordinates Wren had transmitted from the weir had sent the Chancellery's last patrol grinding up a dead channel toward a source that no longer existed. By morning even that fiction would be unnecessary. There was nothing left upstream worth a single agent's time.

"You're smiling," Wren said, not taking their eyes off the channel.

"I'm not."

"You are. I can hear it."

Elodie laughed—an actual laugh, unguarded, startling her own ears—and leaned back against the warm deckhouse. Below in the cabin, the salvaged page of the hymnal lay drying flat under glass on her chart table, next to her mother's letter. Two pieces of paper. That was the entire estate of the Kingdom of Vessia, and it fit in an envelope, and it was enough. More than enough. The river had given her a chest of lead and a name she couldn't keep, and it had given her this—wind at her back, a good boat running free, and Wren at the wheel with the stars coming out above the gorge—and the river gave nothing back, and that was finally, exactly, enough.

"What will you tell them?" she asked. "When you file your final report."

Wren considered the question with the seriousness Wren brought to everything, even jokes. Especially jokes.

"That the archive was destroyed by seasonal flooding before recovery was possible," they said. "That the claim is extinguished. That the heir—" a pause, and the wheel turned gently, following the river's long curve south toward home— "the heir was never located. Some things are easier to find if nobody's looking."

"And after you file it?"

"After I file it," Wren said, "I was hoping there might be a position open aboard this vessel. Salvage work. Hard hours, poor pay, constant pursuit by hostile governments." The stars were thick overhead now, doubled in the water, and the wind pushed them on. "I hear the captain takes all kinds."

Elodie looked at the river running silver ahead of them, all the way down to the sea.

"She does," she said. "But the interview's brutal. Nine hundred miles. You barely passed."

Ahead, the gorge opened its arms, and the Provision ran home.
