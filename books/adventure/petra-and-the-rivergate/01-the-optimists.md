## 1. The Optimists

The storm had been lying to Ivo Winterbourne all afternoon, and he'd known it, and he'd chased it anyway.

That was the thing about weather on the Meridian lowlands—it didn't lie so much as it exaggerated. The wall cloud over the salt flats had looked like a twister factory, all rotation and menace, and instead it had dumped forty minutes of rain the consistency of cold soup and moved on to terrorize some other county. Ivo sat in the cab of his rig with the engine ticking, watching the last of it crawl east, and did the arithmetic he always did after a bad chase.

Fuel: eleven marks. Repairs to the anemometer mast, snapped like a celery stalk by a gust that hadn't even been the interesting part: maybe thirty. Two days lost he couldn't bill anyone for, because nobody bought data from a man whose instruments were in pieces.

Against that: what the Syndicate expected at the end of the month.

He didn't finish the arithmetic. He never finished it. Finishing it meant arriving somewhere, and Ivo Winterbourne had built twenty years of his life on the principle that you never arrive anywhere—not a town, not a decision, not a number you couldn't drive away from.

He was still not-arriving when the headlights came up the gravel track behind him. Two vehicles, running dark until the last hundred yards, which told him everything before he saw the paint. Nobody else ran dark on these roads. The Meridian Syndicate's men liked the drama of appearing out of nowhere; they'd been trained on it, or maybe it just came naturally to people who enjoyed their work.

Ivo got out of the cab. No point pretending he hadn't heard them. The rig was a big gray box of a truck with a sensor mast folded down along its spine and STORMWORKS—WINTERBOURNE stenciled on the door in letters that had faded from white to the color of old bone. It was the only thing he owned outright, and it wasn't even that, not really. The Syndicate held the paper on it. They held paper on most things between the lowlands and the pass.

The first vehicle stopped ten yards back. The second swung wide, casual as a herding dog, and cut off the track south. Doors opened. Four men, and the one who climbed out of the passenger side first was Tam Brase, who was smiling before his boots hit the gravel, because Tam Brase was always smiling. That was the whole horror of him.

"Ivo." Tam spread his arms like they were old friends at a ferry landing. "There he is. There's the man."

"Tam."

"You're hard to find this month. We looked in Corvess. We looked in Dunlow. Somebody said you were up past the toll bridge doing wind work for the county." He shook his head, wounded, delighted. "You know what that tells me? That tells me you don't want to be found. And when a client doesn't want to be found, it makes us *want* to find him. It's human nature."

"I'm not hiding," Ivo said. "I'm working."

"Same thing, with you." Tam walked a slow half-circle around the rig, admiring it, laying a hand on the fender like he was blessing it. Behind him the other three spread out without being told—one by the fuel drums, one leaning against the second vehicle, one drifting toward the rear of the cab where the cash box lived under a false floor. Ivo noted the positions and did nothing about them. There was nothing to do about them. Four was four.

"The thing is," Tam said, "and I say this with love, Ivo—the thing is, we're optimists. That's what people don't understand about the Syndicate. Everybody thinks loan sharks, broken knees, grim men in wet coats. But no. We're optimists. We lend money because we believe people are good for it. Every mark we gave you, that was us believing in you." He turned, smile intact. "And you've made us look foolish."

"I'm good for it."

"You're good for *stories* about it." Tam's voice stayed warm as bathwater. "You're good for 'the season turns, the chase pays, I've got a buyer in the capital.' Meanwhile the number does what numbers do. It grows. Interest isn't punishment, Ivo. It's just time, priced honestly. And you've had a lot of our time."

The man by the fuel drums shifted his weight. Rain dripped off the mast. Somewhere east, the dead storm grumbled like a dog turning over.

"How much," Ivo said.

Tam told him. The number had grown again—not hugely, but the way ice grows on a branch, invisibly and then all at once. Ivo kept his face still. Twenty years of debt had taught him that the worst part was never the number itself; it was watching another man enjoy saying it.

"And if the month runs out?"

"Thirty days." Tam said it brightly, like announcing a festival. "Thirty days, full balance, or we take the rig. And Ivo—" he stepped closer, and for the first time the smile narrowed into something with edges, "—if there's any fuss about the rig, any paperwork filed, any cleverness with the county assessor, then we also take your health. Not all of it. Just enough. You'd be amazed how little of a man's health it takes to end his driving career. A wrist. An ankle. One good eye."

Behind Tam, the man at the rear of the cab had found the false floor. He looked up and nodded once, almost apologetically, and Ivo felt the particular cold of a man learning exactly how thoroughly he'd been mapped.

"There it is," Tam said, following his gaze. "See? We know you, Ivo. That's the difference between us and a bank. A bank doesn't care where you sleep. We care very much." He clapped the fender twice, a friendly drumbeat. "Thirty days. Pay it, and we part friends, and I mean that sincerely—I *like* you. You're the only client who's ever outdriven Doss there." He tipped his head at the man by the second vehicle, who regarded Ivo with the flat professional interest of a farrier looking at a horse's teeth. "Pay it, and you keep the rig, and we never have to have this conversation in a place with worse lighting."

They left the way they'd come, lights dark until the last bend, and then the lowlands were just the lowlands again: wet gravel, dying thunder, a sky scrubbed down to nothing.

* * *

Ivo didn't sleep in the rig that night. Sleeping in the rig meant waking in the rig, and waking in the rig meant being findable, and being findable was the one sin he'd never committed against himself. He drove north two hours on roads he knew by their potholes, parked in the lee of a grain elevator outside a town whose name he made a point of forgetting, and slept three hours with the driver's seat reclined and a pistol under his thigh that he'd never once fired at anything but a rattlesnake and a radio.

He woke before dawn out of habit, not rest. Habit was most of what he had. He boiled water on a camp burner and drank it bitter, standing in the elevator's shadow, watching the sky do its early gray thinking.

Here was the creed, the one he could have recited in his sleep, because mostly he did: Never stay long enough to be found. Never owe anyone more than distance. Never let a place get its hooks in—no lease, no letters, no woman who asked where next week went. A man who kept moving couldn't be cornered. A man with no address couldn't be served papers. A man who belonged nowhere owed nothing to anywhere.

It had worked for twenty years. It had worked so well that he owned a truck, a debt, and a set of instruments, and not one other thing in the world that would notice if he stopped existing.

He thought about that sometimes, usually around this hour, usually with the bitter water going down. How the freedom had a shape to it, and the shape was a box the exact size of a truck cab. How he'd spent two decades making himself unfindable and had succeeded completely, and how the success felt, at five in the morning in the shadow of a stranger's grain elevator, a great deal like being lost.

He put the thought away. That was the discipline. You put the thought away the way you put away a chart in weather—you'd need it later, but not now, and now was the only thing that mattered.

Thirty days. The number sat in his chest like swallowed ballast.

He could run. That was the honest instinct, the old reliable one—point the rig south, cross into the coastal counties, sell the mast to some fishery outfit, start the name over. He'd done it before. Twice, actually. New names fit him like borrowed coats; after a season he stopped answering to them anyway.

But thirty days was different from forever. Thirty days was a number, and numbers could be chased down the way storms could—if you knew where the energy was, if you read the pressure right, if you drove straight at the ugly part instead of around it. He didn't have eleven hundred marks. He didn't have a hundred. What he had was a rig, a talent for reading skies nobody else wanted to stand under, and a stretch of flooded country between here and the river towns where every ditch and slough was running high after three days of rain.

Flooded water paid. That was the oldest rule of the trade. When the rivers came up they took things—fences, stock, cargo, coffins—and what the rivers took, they eventually gave back to whoever was patient enough and greedy enough and stupid enough to go looking. Salvage men worked the shallows with dredges and grappling lines, and the county paid bounties on recovered property, and a man with Ivo's winch and Ivo's patience could clear a season's debts in a week of good mud.

It wasn't chasing storms anymore. It was picking through what storms left behind. It was, in the honest taxonomy of the trade, scavenging.

Ivo drained the cup, broke down the burner, and got behind the wheel.

"It'll do," he told the windshield, because there was no one else to tell.

He drove south toward the river, toward the drowned fields, telling himself it was just work—two weeks of mud and winch-cable, pay Tam Brase, roll on, stay unfindable, stay free. The floodwater lay flat and brown to both horizons, and somewhere under it was whatever the river had decided to give back.

He had no idea yet that the river had been keeping something for forty years, and that it had been keeping it for him.
