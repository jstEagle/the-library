## 2. What the River Kept

The storm broke at dawn and Ivo Winterbourne went fishing.

Not with a rod. With the dredge—a grapple rig he'd welded himself from a plow blade and three lengths of chain, good for dragging the flooded shallows of the Rivergate where high water always gave something back. Fence posts. A wagon axle once. A strongbox that had turned out to hold nothing but river stones and somebody's disappointment. The Meridian Syndicate's loan officers called it salvage. Ivo called it the only honest work left to a man who owed them four hundred crowns and thirty days.

He worked the shallows below Harrow Bend, where the river had jumped its banks in the night and swallowed half the low pasture. The water was the color of steeped tea, running fast and brown, carrying branches, a drowned henhouse door, the top rail of somebody's fence like a man waving goodbye. Ivo stood on the deck of his rig—the *Vagrant*, a flat-bottomed barge with a winch and a crane arm and twenty years of patch jobs—and paid out cable while the current shoved against the hull.

He liked this part. He'd admit that much. The not-knowing. Every cast of the dredge was a question the river might answer any way it pleased, and none of the answers could find him afterward. That was the whole architecture of his life: keep moving, keep owing, keep nothing. A man with no address was a man nobody could corner. It had worked for two decades. It was working right up until the moment it wasn't, which was how most of his plans went.

The cable went taut. Then it went *stuck*.

Ivo killed the winch and leaned over the rail. The line ran down into the murk at an angle that said snagged, but the way it held—steady, dead-still, no shudder of brush or rolling log—said heavy. He fed it slack, felt for the give. Nothing. Whatever was down there weighed as much as a horse and sat as patient as one.

"Come on, then," he told the river, and threw the winch into gear.

The *Vagrant* listed hard to port. The cable groaned, the boom dipped, and the river gave up its secret in stages: first a boil of brown water, then a shape, then a thing breaking the surface trailing weed—square, iron-banded, lead-sheathed along its edges so the water couldn't have it. A chest. Not a big one. Big enough.

Ivo's heart did something stupid and hopeful, the way it always did at this stage, because every chest was treasure until you opened it. He hauled it over the gunwale with the boom, set it on the deck plates, and stood over it dripping, breathing hard, telling himself it was probably full of bricks.

It was stamped with a sunburst.

He knew the mark the way you know a face from an old photograph—half-remembered, wrong in the details, unmistakable all the same. Sixteen rays radiating from a ring, struck deep into the lid's iron band, worn but legible. Vellmark. The nation that had been on maps when Ivo was a boy and wasn't on any now. Erased forty years back, borders dissolved into three neighbors, name scrubbed from the atlases so thoroughly that schoolchildren learned it only as a riddle: *what country used to be where the high passes are?*

Nobody salvaged Vellmarki chests out of the Rivergate. Nobody salvaged anything of Vellmark at all. The river had kept it, though. Rivers kept everything; that was their whole profession.

He got the lid open with a pry bar and a bad temper. The lock was long gone or long rusted; the lead lining had done its job, and inside, dry as the day they were shut away, lay paper.

Deeds. A dozen of them, folded into oilcloth, each bearing the sunburst seal in wax gone dark as dried blood. Land grants, river rights, easements along the waterway—along *this* waterway, the Rivergate itself, from the lowlands clear up to the pass. Beneath them, letters bundled with string, and a map, linen-backed, hand-inked, showing the range passes in detail Ivo had never seen anywhere—and he'd chased storms through those mountains for twenty years. Three of the passes on the map didn't exist on any chart he'd ever bought. Or rather, they existed and someone had made them stop existing.

He sat down on the deck because his legs suggested it.

Then he picked up the topmost deed and read it properly, and the morning changed shape.

The grant described a concession—river tolls, dredging rights, moorage fees—granted by "the lawful authority of Vellmark" to a trading concern. The date was forty-one years old. The signature was flourished and confident. And across the bottom of the page, faint as a watermark held to the light, ran the mark Ivo knew better than his own handwriting, because he'd seen it pressed into every contract he'd ever signed and every threat he'd ever been handed:

The Meridian Syndicate's seal. On a grant supposedly issued by a government that had already ceased to exist when the ink dried.

A forgery. Not just a forgery—an old one, a founding one. The Syndicate's entire claim to the Rivergate concessions, every toll booth, every moorage fee, every crown they'd wrung out of every barge and ferryman for forty years, rested on paper signed by a country that was dead before the pen lifted. These deeds in his hands weren't just older claims. They were the real ones. And real ones meant the Syndicate held nothing but muscle and momentum.

Ivo laughed. It came out wrong, more cough than laugh, and he made himself stop.

Because here was the arithmetic, and it was simple, and it was terrible. A chest like this didn't surface by accident. Somebody had sunk it—sunk it *deep*, lead-lined, weighted—when Vellmark fell, betting the papers would matter again someday, or wanting them never to matter at all. Either way, somebody might come looking. And if the Syndicate ever learned these deeds existed, they wouldn't send a letter. They'd send men. Cheerful men, patient men, men who smiled while they explained your options, and Ivo had spent the last week being introduced to exactly that species.

Thirty days to repay four hundred crowns. And here he sat holding papers worth more than everything the Syndicate owned between the lowlands and the pass—or worth nothing at all, depending entirely on whether he could get them into the right hands before the Syndicate's concession permits came up for renewal in the capital. Renewal season was close. Weeks, maybe less. After renewal, the forgery would be laundered another decade deep and no registry clerk alive would want to touch it.

He looked at the deeds. He looked at the river, calm now, going about its business, keeping its secrets.

Every instinct he had said *put it back*. Sink it deeper. Walk away. Twenty years of never staying, never signing, never owning anything a man could take from him—all of it screaming the same lesson: the moment you pick something up, it picks you back. Papers like these didn't belong to whoever found them. They belonged to whoever survived them.

But underneath the dread, something else was moving. Something unfamiliar. He'd felt its cousin before—on the flats before a storm breaks, when the air goes green and still and every hair on your arms stands up. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The feeling that the sky has finally noticed you and decided to make you part of the weather.

Hope. Reckless, badly timed hope. He hadn't felt it since he was young enough to be embarrassed by it.

Because here was the other arithmetic, the one he tried not to do and did anyway: the Syndicate held his debt. But if the Syndicate's grants were forged—if the concessions were void—then maybe the debt was void too. Maybe the whole machine, the smiling enforcers and the cheerful ledgers and the thirty days, was built on the same rotten paper as everything else they touched. He wasn't just holding a chest. He was holding a lever, and the fulcrum was in the capital, and the permits renewed at dawn on a date he could almost count down to.

Or he could throw it all back in the river, pay his four hundred crowns like a good little debtor, and spend the rest of his life being found whenever they felt like finding him.

Ivo stood at the rail a long time. The water slid past, brown and patient. Somewhere upstream the sun was burning the storm off the ranges, and the high passes—the real ones, the ones on the linen map—caught the light like exposed bone.

"All right," he said to the river, to the chest, to nobody. "You kept it. Now it keeps me."

He stowed the deeds in the oilcloth, wrapped the map around them, and wedged the lot under the floor plates of the *Vagrant*, beneath the bilge boards where no searcher ever bothered looking because no searcher expected a debtor to own anything worth finding. The empty chest he considered, then rolled overboard—it was evidence, and evidence floats, and he'd learned long ago that the safest place for dangerous things was wherever they could never be traced back to you.

The river took it without comment.

He fired the engine and turned the *Vagrant* downstream, toward the border towns, toward the people he'd need and the people he'd wronged, doing the math as he went. Deeds to file. Passes to cross. Snows coming, sooner than anyone wanted. And somewhere behind him, already, he could feel it—the particular pressure of attention, the sense of a large organization beginning, slowly, blindly, to turn its head.

He'd spent twenty years making sure no one could find him.

Now he had six weeks to make sure the right ones did.

* * *

The first border town was called Latch, because everything on the Rivergate was named for what it held or what it lost. Ivo tied up at the public dock past dark, paid a boy to watch his rig, and walked into the one tavern in town with the deeds' location memorized and his pockets deliberately empty of anything incriminating.

He needed a mapmaker. Not for the capital road—any fool could read a road sign—but for the passes. If the Syndicate came for him, and they would, the lowland roads would close like a fist. His only way through would be the routes that didn't officially exist, and there was exactly one person alive who knew why they didn't, because she was the one who'd erased them.

Odile Vasse. The best cartographer of her generation, until the Syndicate bought her and she falsified the range charts and the falsification got people lost, and lost people, in the high country, got dead. She drank in border towns now. He knew which ones. He knew because he was the reason she'd taken the Syndicate's coin in the first place—he'd sold her name to save his own rig, years back, and had been paying for it in the currency he understood least, which was memory.

She'd hate him. Good. Hate was reliable. Hate showed up on time.

Ivo pushed open the tavern door, letting out noise and lamplight, and scanned the room the way he'd scan a sky going green—looking for the exact spot where the weather was about to break.

There. Corner table. A woman with ink-stained fingers and a bottle that had seen better decades, sitting alone with a chart spread out before her that she was correcting from memory, line by line, correcting lies she'd told on paper, as if the paper could hear her apologize.

Ivo Winterbourne took a breath, put on the face of a man with nothing to lose, and started walking—carrying, under his coat and under his skin, the weight of a drowned kingdom's paperwork and the first hope he'd had in twenty years, both of them heavier than lead, and neither of them staying buried.
