## 3. A Cartographer's Regret

The rain in Corvess came down like an apology nobody meant, and Odile Vasse sat in the back of the Drowned Compass with her third brandy and her first regret of the evening, which was that there were only two more bottles after this one.

She had been drinking in border towns for six years. They were all the same—mud streets, toll bells, a tavern with a name that promised more than the building delivered. She'd learned to pick towns by their distance from the Meridian Syndicate's survey offices, and Corvess was far enough. Barely.

The barkeep knew her by now. He didn't ask questions. That was the whole appeal of border towns: everyone had left something behind on the other side of the river, and asking about it was considered rude the way staring at a scar is rude.

She was halfway through cataloguing the evening's regrets—the map, the trial, the letter she'd never sent—when the door opened and let in a man shaped like weather.

Ivo Winterbourne.

He looked worse than the last time she'd seen him, which had been from the window of a Syndicate hearing room while he testified that the falsified pass route on Chart 114-C had been her idea alone. Six years ago. Her license had gone into the fire that same afternoon; his rig had driven out of the yard untouched. She remembered thinking, as they led her out past him, that he hadn't even looked sorry. He'd looked *relieved*, the way a man looks when he's set down a weight and found it lighter than he feared.

Now here he stood, dripping on the floorboards, hat in his hands like a penitent who'd forgotten the words.

"Odile."

"No." She turned back to her glass.

"Five minutes."

"The last five minutes you spent in my company cost me my career." She kept her voice level, which took work. "You can buy the next hour at the standard rate. It's steep."

He sat down anyway, uninvited, because Ivo Winterbourne had never once respected a boundary that wasn't marked on a chart. Up close he smelled of river water and engine oil, and there was a scrape along his jaw that hadn't been there six years ago, or maybe it had and she'd never been close enough to see it.

"I need a cartographer," he said.

"You have eyes and a road. That's a cartographer."

"I need one who knows the range." He leaned forward. "Who knows what's *not* on the charts, and why."

Her hand stopped halfway to the glass.

There it was. Not an apology—he still hadn't said the word, and she understood with a cold settling certainty that he never would, that some men apologized in cargo rather than language—but something worse. Something tailored.

"Chart 114-C," she said quietly.

"The Sable Fork route." His eyes didn't waver. "The one you drew off the maps. The one they made you draw."

"They didn't make me do anything," she said. "That's the lie we both told the tribunal. They paid me. I took the money. You took the blame I handed you because your rig was worth more to you than my name, and I let you, because—" She stopped. Because she had needed someone else to be the villain, and he had volunteered so readily. "Because it was easier."

"And now I'm offering you the chance to put it back."

"Put what back?"

"The route. The truth. All of it." He set something on the table between them—a flat oilcloth packet, tied with cord, damp at the corners. "I pulled a chest out of the shallows below the flood line. Vellmarki seal on the lid. Deeds inside, Odile. Land grants going back sixty years, and at least one of them carrying the Syndicate's watermark where a government seal should be. Forged concessions. The whole river trade built on paper somebody invented in a back room."

She stared at the packet without touching it. "You're lying."

"I wish I were. Because if I'm right, every permit they hold is void, and they know it, and there are men between here and the pass who get paid to make problems disappear." He tapped the oilcloth once. "I need to cross the range before the snows. The official passes will be watched. But the Sable Fork—"

"—doesn't exist," she finished. "Officially. I erased it. I redrew the contours, moved the ridge line forty meters east, and buried the only true copy under a falsification order that burned with everything else I owned." She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "Do you know what they had me erase? A viable crossing. A good one. Better than the toll routes. I stood in a room with three men in gray suits and they slid a chart across the table and said, *this pass inconveniences our concession boundaries*, and I picked up my pen."

The barkeep, wisely, found somewhere else to be.

"So erase it again," Ivo said. "Un-erase it. Lead us through."

"Why?" The word came out harder than she intended. "Why me? There are a dozen guides who know those mountains."

"Not one of them knows where the lies are. You do. You drew every one of them personally." He paused, and for the first time something moved behind his eyes that wasn't calculation. "And because I owe you. I know what I owe you. This is the only currency I have."

She looked at him for a long moment. Six years of rehearsing this conversation, and in every version she'd said something devastating. In none of them had he shown up soaked, broke, and holding stolen deeds like a man bringing flowers.

"If I say no," she said, "you'll find another way over the range."

"Probably. And probably die trying, and the deeds die with me, and the Syndicate keeps its river." He shrugged. "But yes. I'd manage. Badly."

Honesty. That was the unforgivable part. If he'd flattered her, she could have refused cleanly. Instead he sat there admitting he was useless without her, and the guilt she'd been drinking around for six years shifted in her chest like ballast.

*Guilt offered a second chance.* She'd imagined it would feel like absolution. It felt like being handed a live wire and told to carry it uphill.

"What's in it for me?" she asked, though she already knew. "Money?"

"Some. Not much. I'm in debt to people who smile when they threaten you."

"Wonderful."

"But there's this." He untied the cord and opened the oilcloth partway. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, lay a single sheet—old, foxed at the edges, bearing a sunburst stamp faded almost to nothing. "Vellmarki land grant. Original. There may be others. When this goes to the capital, the true charts go with it. Every route they buried. Every boundary they moved. And the testimony of the cartographer who was ordered to falsify them."

Her breath caught. Testimony. Rehabilitation. Her name cleared in a registry office instead of a tavern.

Or a noose, if the Syndicate got to her first.

"You understand what you're asking," she said. "They didn't just take my license, Winterbourne. They own the survey offices, the registries, half the magistrates on the river. You're proposing to walk into the teeth of it with a satchel of paper."

"I'm proposing to walk fast."

She drained her glass. Set it down. Looked at the brandy bottle, then away from it, which was the hardest thing she'd done all night.

"I'm not doing this for you," she said. "Understand that. I'm not forgiving you, and I'm not your friend, and if you betray me again I will leave you on a glacier with a broken compass and a clear conscience."

"Fair terms."

"And I want it in writing. My full account of 114-C, entered into the record. My name attached to the corrected charts."

"Done."

She stood, gathered her coat, and counted out coins for the bottle she wouldn't finish—the first time in six years she'd left a tavern with anything unfinished except a grudge.

At the door she stopped. Rain hammered the street beyond, silver in the lamplight.

"One question," she said. "The chest. Out of everything the river kept, why did it surface now? Why you?"

Ivo looked at the oilcloth packet the way a man looks at a debt notice.

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe the river finally got tired of holding it. Maybe it wanted someone reckless enough to carry it." He pulled his hat down against the wet. "Ask me again when we're over the pass."

* * *

They left Corvess before dawn, north by the old drover's road, the deeds sewn into the lining of Odile's coat because Ivo trusted her stitching more than his own pockets. The rain followed them for two days and then gave up, and the mountains came out ahead of them, white-capped and indifferent, exactly as she'd drawn them and exactly as she hadn't.

On the third morning she spread her reconstruction on a flat rock and weighted the corners with stones. The Sable Fork route ran up the page in her own old linework, restored from memory—every contour, every switchback, the narrow notch below the eastern ridge where the trail threaded between scree and sky.

Ivo studied it in silence. Then: "It's real. All of it."

"It was always real. That was the problem." She rolled the chart and handed it to him. "There's a waystation on the far side. Old woman runs it. Ilves, the name was—Vellmarki, if I remember right. If anyone can confirm what you're carrying, it's her."

"How do you remember that?"

Odile looked north, where the clouds were already stacking themselves against the peaks like unpaid bills.

"Because I mapped her valley too," she said. "Before they told me to stop mapping things that were true."

Behind them, far down the drover's road, a rider appeared—gray coat, moving fast, kicking up a plume of dust that had no business being in such a hurry.

Odile squinted at it, then at Ivo.

"Tell me that's not who I think it is."

Ivo was already rolling up the chart.

"It's who you think it is," he said. "Saddle up."
