## 4. The Exile of Vellmark

Sera Ilves knew the sound of a heavy engine laboring up the last switchback before anyone could see it. Forty years of listening to the mountain had taught her the difference between a freight hauler downshifting and something lighter, hungrier, climbing too fast for the season. This was the second kind. She put down the kettle, took the shotgun down from the pegs by the door, and broke it open to check the shells out of habit rather than fear. Then she went out onto the porch to watch the road.

The waystation sat at the throat of the old Vellmark road, where the valley narrowed and the river ran loud enough to swallow conversation. It had been a toll house once, before the war, before the maps changed. Now it was a waystation in name and a tavern in fact, and Sera was both its keeper and its only permanent resident, unless you counted the ghosts, and she did, most nights.

The vehicle that ground into her yard was a salvage rig with a storm-chaser's mast folded down and a dent in its flank like a bite mark. Two people climbed out of it. A tall man with the weathered look of someone who had spent twenty years being rained on professionally, and a woman in a cartographer's coat with the posture of someone carrying a weight that wasn't in any of her packs.

The man came to the bottom of the porch steps and stopped there, which she approved of. Men who came all the way up before being invited were a certain kind of man.

"We're closed," Sera said.

"We saw smoke," the man said. "And the sign says rooms."

"The sign is forty years old. So is the lie."

He almost smiled. "We'll pay."

"Everyone pays. That's not the question. The question is what you're paying for." She looked past him to the woman. "You're Odile Vasse."

The cartographer went still. "You know me?"

"I knew your work before I knew your name. The 3rd Edition survey of the range. The one where the eastern passes grew teeth and the old Vellmark road grew a landslide it never had." Sera stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before the light goes. And put that rig under the lean-to. It'll hail by dark. It always hails by dark, this time of year. That's the mountain telling you it hasn't decided about you yet."

* * *

Inside, the waystation smelled of pine smoke and dried herbs and forty years of one woman's cooking. Sera fed them barley soup and dark bread without being asked, because feeding people was how she learned what they were. The tall man—Winterbourne, the storm chaser, she'd heard the name on the lowland frequencies for years—ate like a man who'd forgotten eating was a pleasure. Vasse ate like a woman doing penance.

It was Vasse who opened the oilcloth on the table, and it was the oilcloth that changed the temperature of the room.

The chest's contents were laid out with a salvager's care: deeds, correspondence, a survey map of the range passes drawn in a hand Sera had not seen since she was a girl, and at the bottom, wrapped in waxed paper, a grant bearing the Meridian Syndicate's watermark and the signature of a Vellmarki land commissioner who had died two years before the document was dated.

Sera didn't touch anything at first. She walked around the table once, the way her father had walked around a snared animal, reading it before deciding whether to free it or finish it.

"Where," she said.

"Flooded shallows below the Rivergate," Winterbourne said. "After the storm."

"The river keeps what it's owed." She sat down. She put her hands flat on the table on either side of the deeds, not touching them, framing them. "Read me the parcel numbers."

Vasse read them. Sera listened with her eyes closed, and behind her eyelids was a valley she had left at nineteen, running, with her father's hand in hers and the sunburst flag folded small against her chest.

"These are the Ilves holdings," she said. "All of them. The orchard terraces. The mill rights on the upper water. The grazing commons." She opened her eyes. "These are my family's deeds. Or copies. Or the originals that were supposed to have burned with the registry."

"They're originals," Vasse said quietly. "I've handled enough forged paper to know the difference. The forgeries are the Syndicate's grants. Look at the watermark. It's the 4th Series mark. It wasn't issued until three years after this grant is dated."

"Which means," Sera said, "the Syndicate's concessions were built on paper that didn't exist when it was supposedly signed." She laughed, once, a short hard sound with no joy in it, the sound of a door slamming after forty years. "We always said it. Every exile said it in every border town in every language we learned. *The grants are false. The land was stolen twice—once by soldiers, once by clerks.* And the world said: yes, dear, of course, have some soup."

Winterbourne was watching her the way men watch weather. "You believe it's genuine. The deeds."

"I believe it's my grandmother's handwriting on the mill rights. I believe the seal wax is Vellmarki blue, which no forger bothers with, because forgers work for people who've never heard of Vellmarki blue." She finally touched the deeds, one finger, the parcel numbers, tracing them like braille. "I believe the river kept these for forty years because the river is the last honest official in this country."

"And the passes?" Vasse asked. "The map."

Sera unfolded the survey. Her breath caught, and she let it catch, because there was no one in the room she needed to perform strength for. The passes were marked in the old hand. Including the one.

"The Kurgan Gap," she said. "They cut it off the charts after the annexation. Officially it doesn't exist. Unofficially it's the only way over the range that the Syndicate doesn't own the tolls on—because the Syndicate doesn't believe in it. Your cartographer here helped them stop believing in it."

"I drew the landslide," Vasse said. Her voice was flat, a confession delivered like a survey note. "Under orders. I was young and I told myself the alternative was worse. I've spent eleven years telling myself that."

Sera looked at her for a long moment. Grief, she had learned, came in two kinds: the kind that made you cruel and the kind that made you precise. She chose precision.

"Then you're the only person alive who can lead someone through a pass you erased," she said. "The mountain has a sense of humor. It's cruel, but it's organized."

Winterbourne leaned forward. "You know what we're doing. You know what's chasing us."

"I know the Syndicate's enforcers wear yellow armbands and smile too much. I know they stopped at my door in the spring asking if any Vellmarki had come through. I told them the last Vellmarki died in the war." She stood, and went to the shelf above the hearth, and took down a small tin box, dented, older than the waystation. "I know what's in this box. My father's ashes. He carried the deeds out of Vellmark—copies, he thought, the last of the registry—and he died on this side of the range still believing the paper would matter someday. He waited for a someday that never came, and then he was ash, and the ash waited with me."

She set the tin on the table next to the deeds.

"I'm sixty-three years old. I have a shotgun, a waystation worth nothing, and a valley full of ghosts who don't pay for their rooms." She looked at Winterbourne, and her eyes were the pale gray of the range in winter, the color that comes before the snow decides. "You're going to the capital. You're going over the range before the first snows, through a pass that doesn't exist, carrying the only proof that my people were robbed. And you were going to do it without a guide, through country I've walked every year of my exile, following a map drawn by a woman who's been apologizing to it for a decade."

"Yes," Winterbourne said.

"Then you need me, and you don't have the sense to know it yet, which is the most dangerous thing about you." She began clearing the table, stacking the dishes with the efficiency of a woman who had decided something and would not be argued with. "You'll sleep here tonight. You leave at first light. I know the Kurgan Gap in October. I know which snowfields lie and which scree slopes sing before they slide. I know where the old Vellmark cairns are, and I'll show you, because the cairns matter. A country that's been erased from the maps still exists in its stones."

Vasse spoke carefully. "Why? You have a life here. Whatever it is. Why carry your father into that?"

Sera stopped, a bowl in each hand, and considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"Because he waited forty years for the paper to matter," she said. "And I'm done waiting. Because the Syndicate took the valley, and the valley took my name off the maps, and the maps took my country, and I have spent four decades being from nowhere. I am from *Vellmark*. I will be from Vellmark when I'm ash too, and someone will carry me over that pass, and I intend to make the road for them." She set the bowls down. "Also, because you'll die without me. That's the smaller reason, but it's the true one."

Winterbourne offered his hand across the table. She looked at it.

"I don't shake hands on promises," she said. "I shake them on arrivals. You'll get your handshake at the registry in the capital, if we live to see it."

She took his hand anyway, briefly, and her grip was iron wrapped in old skin.

* * *

They left at first light, as ordered. Sera locked the waystation behind her without ceremony—she had locked it before, for weeks at a time, walking the high country to stay fluent in it—and hung the tin box from her pack's shoulder strap where she could feel it knock against her hip with every step. Her father, keeping time.

By midmorning they had left the treeline. The range rose ahead of them, white-capped already, the first snows sitting on the high shoulders like creditors at a door. Vasse navigated by her own false map, reading her own lie like a codebreaker, marking where the phantom landslide should be and therefore where the real road ran clear. Winterbourne drove the rig as far as the rig could go, then they loaded what mattered onto their backs and the mountain took over the conversation.

It was near dusk, pausing at the last cairn below the Gap—a stack of stones older than the annexation, bearing a sunburst carved so worn it was more memory than mark—that Sera saw it.

Far below, back along the valley road they had climbed, two points of light. Moving. Not following the road. Cutting across it, taking the low route toward the Gap's southern approach, the way a man travels when he knows where you're going and has no interest in knocking.

Yellow armbands caught the last of the light.

"Company," Sera said.

Winterbourne shielded his eyes, and she watched him do the arithmetic—the hours, the snow, the head start—and watched the answer come up short.

"How long do we have?" he asked.

Sera shouldered her pack and felt her father's ashes knock once against her hip, like a knock at a door, like a man clearing his throat before saying something that had waited forty years to be said.

"If the snow holds off," she said, "half a day. If it doesn't—" she looked up at the Gap, where the first flakes were already coming down, fine as sifted flour, "—we'll find out together."
