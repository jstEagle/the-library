## 5. The Assessor

Corin Hale had been a government assessor for eleven years, and in that time he had learned three things: the law was a machine, machines required maintenance, and maintenance was his job. He did not think of himself as clever. Clever men ended up in the capital, arguing before committees. Hale was thorough, and thoroughness, he had found, outlasted cleverness the way stone outlasted weather.

The orders had come by courier three days ago, sealed with the assessor-general's private stamp rather than the ministry's. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the wording. *Retrieve the documents in question and resolve the matter quietly.* Not *secure evidence.* Not *preserve chain of custody.* *Retrieve* and *quietly* — words that belonged to debt collectors, not to the Registry.

He had read the order four times on the road from the lowlands, and each reading left him more certain that he was not being sent to enforce the law. He was being sent to bury it.

The toll bridge at Rivergate spanned the Vessa where the gorge narrowed to forty feet of black water. It was the only crossing for sixty miles, which made it the obvious place to wait, and so Hale waited — badly, he suspected, because he had never been good at waiting. He sat in the toll house's cramped parlor with a cup of tea going cold and watched the road through rain-streaked glass.

The toll-keeper's daughter brought him a fresh cup without being asked. She was perhaps nineteen, quick-eyed, with ink stains on her fingers that no toll-keeper's daughter should have. She set the cup down and looked at the warrant case chained to his wrist the way other people looked at weather coming over the ridge.

"You're the third one this month," she said.

"The third what?"

"Man sitting in my father's parlor pretending to drink tea while watching the bridge." She smiled. "The other two were Syndicate. They wore better boots."

Hale glanced down at his own boots. Serviceable. Government issue. "I'm not Syndicate."

"No," she agreed. "You're worse. You're official."

She introduced herself as Nessa, poured herself a cup, and sat across from him uninvited, which no one did. "Who are you waiting for?"

"I'm not at liberty—"

"A storm chaser named Ivo Winterbourne. A cartographer called Odile Vasse. An old woman who talks to her father's ashes." Nessa sipped her tea. "And before you ask how I know, everyone on this river knows everything. The river carries gossip better than water."

Hale said nothing, which she correctly took as confirmation.

"They'll come tonight or tomorrow," she said. "The Syndicate's enforcers are a day behind you, traveling heavy. So really, assessor, the question is whether you're here to help them or to beat them to it."

"I'm here to do my duty."

"Of course you are." She said it kindly, the way one speaks to a child explaining that the stove is hot. "Everyone says that. The Syndicate men said it too, when they came through last spring collecting 'road assessments' that never appeared in any ledger I've seen. They were very dutiful about it." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "You know what I've learned about duty, assessor? It's like coin. Everyone carries it, but if you look closely enough, most of it's counterfeit."

"That's a cynical education for a toll house."

"It's an accurate one. My father collects tolls. Half the traffic on this bridge pays twice — once to us, once to whoever holds the concession. And the concession—" she stopped, smiled again, and stood. "More tea?"

She left before he could answer. Through the window, the rain kept falling on the black water, and Hale thought about the word *counterfeit*, and about forged grants, and about why a government that believed in the law would send a man to make it disappear.

* * *

They came at dusk the next day, four figures leading two mules up the eastern approach, mud to their knees and the look of people who had slept badly for a week. Hale recognized Winterbourne from the description — tall, weathered, moving like a man perpetually about to leave a room. The cartographer walked beside him with a surveyor's stride. Behind them came the old woman, and a girl of maybe sixteen carrying a pack too big for her.

Nessa was already on the bridge, lantern lit, hand out. "Toll's doubled after dark," she announced cheerfully. "Syndicate regulation."

"We don't have Syndicate money," Winterbourne said.

"Then you have a problem."

Hale stepped out of the parlor into the rain. He had rehearsed this. Warrant presented, documents surrendered, party detained pending review. Clean procedure. He had done it a hundred times.

"Winterbourne," he said. "Corin Hale, assessor for the Interior Registry. I have a warrant concerning certain documents in your possession."

Everything stopped. The old woman's hand went into her coat. The cartographer moved half a step in front of the girl. Winterbourne just looked at him, and there was something in the look that Hale hadn't expected — not fear. Recognition, almost. Pity, even.

"You're the one they sent to make it quiet," Winterbourne said.

"I'm the one they sent to retrieve evidence."

"Same thing, from where they're standing." Winterbourne didn't reach for anything. He just stood in the rain, hands loose, and waited. "Read your warrant again, assessor. All of it. Then tell me whose seal is at the bottom."

Hale had read it five times. He knew whose seal it was. He knew, too, that the assessor-general's private stamp appearing on an order to suppress evidence was itself evidence — of exactly the kind his oath required him to pursue.

"Give me the deeds," Hale said, "and I'll see you're treated fairly."

"By whom?" It was the old woman who spoke, her voice dry as old paper. "By the ministry that took our land? By the men whose names are on the forged grants? Boy, I've watched your government work for forty years. It works very well. That's the problem."

The girl with the big pack was staring at Hale with open contempt. "You're going to take the one thing that fixes everything, and put it in a drawer, and go home, and tell yourself you followed procedure."

"I'm going to follow the law."

"The law has a price tag," Nessa said from the middle of the bridge, and Hale turned to find her holding up a ledger, bound in oiled leather, fat with years. "Want to see it? Every bribe the Syndicate paid on this river for six years. Names, dates, amounts. Two of your colleagues from the Registry. One magistrate. And an entry from last autumn — a payment authorized by the assessor-general's own office, marked *bridge maintenance*." She let that sit in the rain between them. "Your machine needs maintenance, assessor. This is what the maintenance looks like."

The rain drummed on the planks. Somewhere downstream, thunder muttered.

Hale looked at the ledger. He thought about the sealed order in his case, the private stamp, the word *quietly*. He thought about eleven years of procedure, and about how a man could keep every rule and still be working for the people who wrote the rules for themselves.

"If I let you pass," he said slowly, "I'm in violation of my warrant."

"Yes," Winterbourne said.

"And if I detain you, the deeds reach the Registry the day after the permits renew, and none of this matters."

"Yes," Winterbourne said again, gently, the way you confirm a diagnosis.

Hale stood on the wet planks of the toll bridge and understood, with terrible clarity, that there was no version of his duty that survived contact with the facts. The law he served had been sold. What remained was the part underneath — the part with no office, no seal, no maintenance contract. Just a question: what was true, and who was owed?

He unchained the warrant case, took out the order, and held it over the railing above the black water.

"This never existed," he said. "And I was never here. And if anyone asks, I lost you at the ford below Kessel's bend."

"You'll lose your position," the cartographer said. Not a warning. An observation.

"I'm beginning to suspect," Hale said, "that the position was never mine to lose. It was rented, and the rent was paid by someone else."

He dropped the order into the river. It vanished without a sound.

Nessa was smiling at him — not triumphantly, he noticed, but with something almost like respect. Then she turned to Winterbourne. "Bridge toll's waived. But I'm coming with you."

Winterbourne blinked. "What?"

"I know every back road from here to the pass, I've got six years of that ledger memorized, and my father will be furious, which is a bonus." She hoisted her pack. "Besides, you need someone who can read a balance sheet. You've got a mapmaker, a guide, and a man who chases weather. None of you can add."

Behind her, the girl with the big pack raised her hand. "I can add."

"Then we're two," Nessa said.

Hale watched them cross the bridge into the dark, four of them now, then five counting the girl, and felt the strange vertigo of a man who has just stepped off the edge of his own life. He had come to intercept them. Instead he stood aside and let the future walk past him in the rain.

Then he heard the horses.

Down the western road, still distant but closing fast, hooves on wet stone — many of them, riding hard, riding heavy. The Syndicate's enforcers, a day early.

Nessa turned back, lantern high, and her smile was gone. "They burned the ferry at Marlow's Landing," she said. "Which means when they get here, they're going to burn this bridge too — with us on it, unless we move now."

Winterbourne was already running.
