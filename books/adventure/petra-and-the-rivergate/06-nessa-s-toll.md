## 6. Nessa's Toll

Nessa had been stealing from the Meridian Syndicate for six years, and in all that time she'd never once been afraid of it.

That was the trick of it, she'd learned young. Fear was a thing you could ration. Her father rationsed his like a miser—counting every coin twice, flinching at every rider on the bridge road, dying by inches behind the toll house window until there wasn't enough of him left to bury. Nessa spent hers all at once, the day she turned sixteen and found the Syndicate's concession ledger in her father's strongbox and understood that the men who owned the bridge were paying him a toll of their own: a monthly envelope, marked *maintenance*, that never once went to maintenance.

So she'd raised her own rates. Syndicate couriers paid double. Syndicate freight paid triple. She kept her own book, in her own hand, with dates and amounts and the names of the men who signed the envelopes, and she told herself it was leverage, though some nights—most nights—it was just the only interesting thing in her life.

Then Ivo Winterbourne came across her bridge with three strangers and a government assessor trailing him like a bad smell, and fear stopped being something she could spend.

* * *

She watched them from the toll house window while Hale argued with Ivo in the middle of the span. She couldn't hear the words over the river, but she knew the shape of them. She'd seen assessor's work before—a man in a good coat doing arithmetic with people.

The tall woman was Odile Vasse. Nessa knew the name; her father had kept a falsified map framed behind the bar he used to drink at, a joke between men who'd never done anything worth framing. The old one was Sera Ilves, who stood at the bridge rail looking downstream the way you look at a grave. And Winterbourne himself looked exactly like the stories: lean, weathered, restless even standing still, a man built out of miles.

Nessa had made up her mind before the argument ended. That was another trick she'd learned: decide fast, then don't look back, because looking back is how they catch you.

She took the ledger from under the floorboards where her father had hidden the envelopes, wrapped it in oilcloth, and put it in her satchel next to the deed to the toll house—which she burned in the stove, because a thing you mean to leave should not be waiting for you anywhere.

Her father's chair she left alone. Someone else could sit in it or no one would. She was done keeping seats warm for dead men.

* * *

They were loading the wagons when she walked out onto the bridge, satchel over her shoulder, and planted herself in front of Ivo like a toll gate.

"Bridge fee," she said.

Ivo looked at her, then at Odile, then back. "We already paid."

"You paid the old rate." She held out her hand. "New rate is one passenger. Me."

Odile laughed—one short, disbelieving note. Sera said nothing, but her eyes went to the satchel and stayed there, the way an old soldier's eyes go to a weapon.

"Nessa," Ivo said carefully, the way you talk to someone standing on a ledge. "The Syndicate has men coming. Maybe hours behind us. You want to walk toward them?"

"I want to stop paying rent to them," Nessa said. "There's a difference."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you pay triple like everyone else, and I tell the enforcers which way you went when they get here, because they'll get here either way, and I'd rather be gone when they do." She shrugged. "I'm not asking to join your adventure, Winterbourne. I'm telling you the toll's gone up."

Hale, still sour from losing his argument on the span, muttered something about obstruction. Nessa smiled at him with all her teeth.

It was Sera who settled it. The old woman came forward, took Nessa's chin in a hand dry as birch bark, and turned her face to the light like a buyer checking a horse. Then she looked at Ivo and said, in her flat Vellmarki accent: "A girl who robs the Syndicate for six years and lives. Take her. Luck like that doesn't walk across your bridge twice."

Ivo rubbed his jaw. Nessa watched him do it—the little war behind his eyes, the man who wanted to be alone so badly he'd rather lose than be helped—and felt a flicker of recognition so sharp it was almost pain. She knew that face. She saw it in her mirror every morning.

"Fine," he said finally. "But you carry your own weight, and when this goes wrong—"

"When," Odile said sweetly. Not *if*. Nessa decided she liked her immediately.

"—when it goes wrong," Ivo repeated, "you're on your own. I don't have room in this for anyone else's funeral."

"Nobody does," Nessa said. "That's what makes them funerals." She stepped past him onto the far bank and didn't look back at the toll house. Mostly didn't. A little. It had been her whole life, after all, even if it had been a small one.

* * *

They made nine miles before the smoke.

Sera smelled it first—an old woman who could smell woodsmoke through a storm, which told Nessa everything about what forty years of exile taught a person. They topped a rise above the river valley and looked back, and there it was: the toll bridge, burning end to end, the Syndicate's riders small and orderly around it like ants around a killed thing.

"They cut the road," Odile said quietly. "No retreat now. Anyone following has to come the long way through the pass."

"That was the point," Sera said. "Burning a bridge tells you two things. What they want to keep you from returning to, and how much they fear what you're carrying." She glanced at Nessa's satchel. "You'll show me what's in that. Later."

Nessa's stomach did something complicated. She'd thought the ledger was her armor. Standing on that ridge, watching her whole life burn behind her, she understood it was also a target—and that she'd just strapped it to four strangers.

"I'm sorry," she started. "If I'd known they'd—"

"Don't," Ivo said.

She braced for anger. It wasn't there. He was still watching the fire, and his voice, when it came, was strange—almost gentle, almost angry, mostly tired.

"Don't apologize for them burning things. That's theirs. They lit it." He turned away from the smoke. "We move. Snow's coming early this year, and we've got a pass to cross that isn't on any map worth trusting."

* * *

They walked until dark, then made camp in the lee of a granite shelf, and that was where it happened—not with a speech, not with a decision, just with a pot of Sera's barley soup passed hand to hand and Odile arguing about contour lines and Nessa catching herself laughing for the first time since her father died.

Ivo sat apart, as she'd expected. He always sat apart; she'd bet the ledger on it. But he sat apart *facing them*, which was different, and when Sera refilled his bowl without asking, he let her.

Nessa watched him watch them, and understood what she was seeing, because she'd been seeing it in the glass for years: a man cataloguing exits. Counting the ways out. Telling himself that people were cargo you eventually had to unload, that staying light was the same as staying safe, that the ache you felt at night was freedom and not its opposite.

He caught her looking. Neither of them looked away.

"You could still turn back," he said. "Hale will write you a statement. Protective custody, whatever they call it."

"The last man who offered me protection was selling it," Nessa said. "You're not selling anything, Winterbourne. That's the first thing I liked about you." She hugged her knees. "Besides. You think I crossed six years of ledgers so I could end up sitting in a government office being *protected*?"

"It's not too late to be sensible."

"Sensible got my father into a chair by a window," she said, "waiting for envelopes until the envelopes stopped coming. I've done sensible. It's a slow way to drown." She nodded at the others—Sera humming something low and foreign, Odile sketching the ridge line by firelight, correcting a mountain that had wronged her. "Look at them. When's the last time you had that?"

"I've never had that," Ivo said, and the honesty of it knocked something loose in Nessa's chest. "I've had crews. Hired hands. People who ride with you as far as the money goes." He turned his bowl in his hands. "This isn't that. This is—" He stopped, visibly wrestling with the word.

"Family," Nessa said, just to watch him flinch.

He flinched. "That word gets people killed."

"So does having none." She lay back against her pack. "Get some sleep, Winterbourne. Tomorrow we climb."

* * *

She woke once in the black hours, the way she always did in new places, listening. The fire was down to coals. Sera slept like the dead. Odile had drawn maps until her pencil gave out and now slept on top of them like a dragon.

And Ivo was awake, sitting up, looking at the four of them spread around his fire like something he'd stolen and hadn't figured out how to return.

Nessa closed her eyes before he could see she was watching. But she heard him sigh—a long, defeated sound, the sound of a man losing an argument with himself—and when she risked a glance, he had pulled his coat off and laid it over Odile's shoulders where the cold came through the rocks, and gone back to sitting watch over all of them.

*Found family,* she thought, drifting off. *Assembling itself against his will.*

Above the pass, unseen in the dark, the snowline crept down the mountain to meet them—and somewhere below, on the long way around the burned bridge, lanterns moved in file through the valley like a procession of patient, cheerful men.

The enforcers were coming. And for the first time in twenty years, Ivo Winterbourne had something to lose.
