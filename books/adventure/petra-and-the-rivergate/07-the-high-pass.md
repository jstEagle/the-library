## 7. The High Pass

The snow came three weeks early, and Odile Vasse knew it before she saw it. She could smell it—the iron-and-cedar tang of a storm front stacking itself against the range like a debt coming due.

She stood at the tree line with her hands jammed in her coat pockets and studied the peaks the way she'd once studied them for the Syndicate: not as mountains, but as problems to be solved on paper. Forty years of surveying had taught her that a map was never a lie about the land. It was a lie about what mattered.

The pass was there. She'd drawn it out herself, at twenty-six, with a steady hand and a sick stomach—then erased it, line by line, because a Meridian man in a gray coat had explained, very politely, what happened to cartographers whose maps inconvenienced the concessions. The Kestrel Pass vanished from every chart she touched afterward. The Vellmarki who'd used it for generations to cross into the lowlands found their road gone as thoroughly as their country.

Now it was the only way through.

"We can't take the southern route," Ivo said beside her. He said it flatly, the way he said everything, but she heard the arithmetic underneath. The southern passes would be choked with snow by tomorrow night, and the Syndicate's enforcers were already two days behind them and closing.

"No," Odile agreed. "We can't."

Sera Ilves came up the trail with her pack riding high on her shoulders, the small tin box lashed to its top with cord gone soft from handling. She looked at the peaks, then at Odile, and something passed across her weathered face—not quite recognition, but close.

"You're going to take us over Kestrel," Sera said.

Odile didn't pretend otherwise. "You know it?"

"I crossed it twice as a girl, before they closed the border." Sera's eyes stayed on the mountains. "My father carried me on his back through the narrows. He said the mountain forgives nothing, but it forgets even less." She turned. "Can you find it? They wiped it off your charts."

"I put it there," Odile said. "I'm the one who took it off."

The silence stretched. Nessa, crouched by the fire ring feeding sticks to the flames, looked up. Even Ivo went still, which for Ivo meant something.

"Then you know where it is," Sera said finally.

"Better than anyone alive." Odile pulled her gloves tighter. "That's the trouble."

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the snow arrived at noon like an ambush of its own.

It came sideways first, then thick, erasing the world in white handfuls. Odile navigated by memory instead of sight—which landmarks survived, which cairns she'd ordered removed twenty years ago and which the mountain had kept anyway. A split boulder shaped like a molar. A shelf of granite where the wind always screamed through a crack, a sound she'd described in her field notes as *a woman singing badly*. Nessa laughed the first time they heard it, a bright startled bark in all that white.

"That's you," she told Odile. "That's definitely you."

"Keep walking, toll-keeper."

But something had loosened in Odile's chest since the bridge, since the girl had slipped them past Hale with a smile and a forged manifest and thirty crowns of the Syndicate's own money. Odile had spent two decades believing her crime was a wound that only time could scar over. It turned out time did nothing. What worked was this: putting one boot in front of the other along a line she'd erased, making the lie walk backward into truth.

Ivo ranged ahead. He always did—he couldn't help it, the way some men couldn't help checking exits. But he kept looking back now. That was new. Twenty years of miles, and the man had finally learned to count heads.

The narrows came at dusk.

Odile had warned them about the narrows. A slot between two faces of rock where the trail narrowed to a shoulder's width and the drop fell away four hundred feet into a white throat of fog. In summer it was a corridor. In snow it was a trapdoor waiting to be stepped on.

"Single file," she said. "Rope. Nobody stops in the narrows, nobody hurries either. We move like we've got all the time in the world and none of it to waste."

Sera went first, light as a bird despite her years, her boots finding holds her father's hands had shown her forty years ago. Then Nessa. Then Ivo with the packs. Odile took the rear, because that was where the guilt belonged—at the back of the line, watching everyone else risk their lives on her memory.

They were halfway through when the rocks started falling.

Not snow-slide. Not wind. Rocks, thrown.

"Ambush!" Ivo's voice cracked up the gorge. "Down—get against the wall!"

The enforcers had climbed the south face while the storm covered their approach. Three of them, maybe four, positioned above the narrows where the trail pinched tightest. The first volley missed wide, stones skipping off into the fog, but the second caught the rope.

Odile felt the line go slack in her hands and understood, in one cold flash, exactly what was about to happen.

Nessa was mid-step on the exposed ledge. Without the rope's tension she swayed, arms wheeling, one boot skidding on ice-glazed stone—and then she was down, sliding, her fingers scrabbling at rock that gave her nothing, the drop opening beneath her like a mouth.

"Nessa!" Ivo lunged, belly-flat on the ledge, and caught her wrist at the last possible inch. Her whole weight swung him toward the edge. His boots scraped. Held.

Above them, Sera did something Odile would remember for the rest of her life. The old woman stood up straight in the killing wind, unslung the tin box from her pack—her father's ashes, forty years carried, the last thing in the world she owned that mattered—and threw it at the enforcers.

Not at them, precisely. At the scree slope above them.

The box struck, burst, and a gray plume of ash bloomed across the slope. And the slope, disturbed, moved.

It wasn't an avalanche. It was barely a slide—a hiss, a shudder, a hundredweight of stone and old snow letting go. But it was enough. The enforcers scattered, their throwing stopped, their position broken. One of them lost his footing entirely and went down the face with a scream that the wind ate whole.

In the chaos, Ivo hauled Nessa up. Odile grabbed the rope ends and knotted them around her own waist, anchoring the line with her body, and for ten seconds that lasted a year the five of them—four of them, Odile corrected herself, the ashes were gone—clung to the mountain while it decided whether to keep them.

Then the slide settled. The wind resumed. They moved.

* * *

They made the far side of the narrows by full dark and sheltered beneath an overhang Sera remembered from her childhood, a hollow in the rock where the wind couldn't reach. No fire. Too visible. They sat shoulder to shoulder in the dark, breathing steam, alive.

Nessa's wrist was wrenched and her palms were shredded. Ivo wrapped them by feel, his hands steadier than Odile had ever seen them, and Nessa made jokes through her teeth the entire time because that was how she bore things—loudly, and sideways.

Sera sat apart, facing the storm.

Odile went to her. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be an insult, so she said nothing. She just sat down in the snow beside the old woman and let the silence be what it was.

"He's home," Sera said at last. Her voice was rough but level. "Vellmarki soil. There hasn't been any for forty years, and now—" she gestured at the mountain, the pass, the whole erased country they were crossing, "—now there's this much again. Enough for him."

"Sera—"

"Don't." But it wasn't harsh. "Don't apologize. You didn't throw it. And if you hadn't led us here, my father would have died in a tin box in a waystation pantry, and no one would ever have known his name." She was quiet a moment. "He used to say a map is a promise. That whoever draws it owes the truth to everyone who walks it." She turned and looked at Odile, and her eyes in the dark were very clear. "You broke that promise. And today you paid it back. Both things are true. My people believe both things can be true at once."

Odile found she couldn't speak. Twenty years of self-forgiveness refused, hoarded like a coin she didn't deserve to spend—and here was this woman, dispossessed and grieving, handing it to her in the middle of a blizzard as though it cost nothing.

It didn't cost nothing. It had cost blood. It had cost a father's ashes flung against a scree slope, and nearly a girl's life, and Odile understood suddenly that this was what atonement actually was—not a feeling, not a clean reckoning, but payment rendered in the only currency the mountain accepted. She had falsified a map, and the truth had come back for her with interest, and she had paid, and the account was—she tested the word carefully, like ice underfoot—closed.

"I'll draw it again," she said. "Kestrel Pass. Every contour. Every cairn. Under my own seal this time."

"I know," Sera said. "I'll walk it first."

* * *

Near midnight, the storm eased just enough to show stars through tearing cloud, and Ivo crawled back under the overhang from the lookout point with an expression Odile had learned to read as bad news wearing a calm face.

"They're not all accounted for," he said quietly. "Two got past the slide. They'll have circled ahead—we'll meet them on the descent, or worse, they've gone for the waystation at the foot of the pass. The one Sera's cousins run."

"The one we need for supplies," Nessa said.

"The one they'll be holding when we get there," Ivo said.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Snow ticked softly against the rocks. Odile looked at the four of them—Ivo already running distances in his head, Nessa flexing her bandaged hand, Sera gazing north toward a country that existed again—and felt the strange, unfamiliar weight of something she hadn't carried in twenty years of drawing other people's roads.

People she'd follow anywhere. People who'd followed her up a mountain on the strength of a crime.

"How far to the waystation?" she asked.

Ivo checked the sky, the wind, the angle of the moon. "If we move now, we beat the storm's tail. We arrive at dawn."

"Then we move now," Odile said, and reached for her pack.
