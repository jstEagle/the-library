## 10. Miles and Years

The waystation looked better than it had any right to.

Ivo stood in the yard with a hammer still warm from the morning's work and let himself look at it properly for the first time. New roofline. New shutters, painted the deep blue Sera insisted was Vellmarki and Odile insisted was just blue. The stable rebuilt on its old footings, the forge chimney drawing clean, and the long porch — Sera's idea, Nessa's design, everyone's labor — running the whole front of the building like an open arm.

Snow still clung to the high shoulders of the pass above them, but down here the melt was running loud in every gully, and the first green was coming up through the mud like something escaping.

"You're staring at it again," Sera said, coming around the corner with a basket of seed packets she'd been hoarding all winter. "A man who stares at his own house is either very proud or very surprised."

"Both," Ivo said.

She handed him the basket because her hands were full of nothing else to do with them, which was her way of giving him work instead of conversation. Forty years of exile had taught her that grief and chores could share a body without arguing. She'd taught him that too, this winter, though he'd needed longer to learn it.

Inside, the main room smelled of pine shavings and bread. Odile had claimed the east table months ago and refused to surrender it; her maps were everywhere now, pinned and weighted and half-finished, the honest ones, the ones she drew the way she'd once been afraid to. On the wall above her workspace hung the first one she'd completed since the High Pass: the range rendered true, every pass marked, including the one she'd erased forty years ago under Syndicate orders. She'd labeled it in small careful letters. *Restored.*

Nessa came down the stairs two at a time, sixteen going on thirty, already wearing the coat she'd outgrown twice since autumn. "Toll's open," she announced, which was a joke, because the new toll house at the river crossing charged nothing at all. It kept a fire burning, a kettle hot, a ledger where travelers wrote their names if they felt like it, and a locked drawer where Nessa kept the Syndicate's old bribe ledger like a relic in a shrine. Pilgrims came now. Actual pilgrims, come to see the toll that took no toll.

"How many today?" Ivo asked.

"Eleven. A wool merchant who cried when I wouldn't take his coin, so I made him fix the fence instead." She grinned. "And a surveyor asking after the passes. I sent him to Odile."

Odile didn't look up from her drafting pen. "He'll get truth or nothing."

"That's what I told him."

Ivo set the seed basket by the door and felt the strange gravity of the room settle over him again, the way it did every morning and still hadn't worn off. For twenty years he had built his life out of departures. He'd measured distance the way other men measured time — in miles behind him, in storms outrun, in towns left before anyone learned his middle name. Debt had been the only thing that ever followed him, and he'd assumed that was the natural order of things: everything you love stays put, everything you owe comes along.

Then the deeds went into the registry. Then the concessions voided. Then the enforcers scattered like crows off a dead elk, and Hale resigned his commission in a letter so dry it was practically a legal document, and the courts opened the Vellmarki claims, and spring came, and nobody left.

That was the part he still couldn't get used to. Nobody left.

* * *

They drove up to the border meadow on the last day of thaw, all four of them plus the mule, which Nessa had named Assessor as an act of forgiveness she claimed was complete and everyone else suspected was not.

Sera carried the urn herself. She'd carried it four thousand miles and forty years, and she carried it the last two miles the same way: close, quiet, both hands. Her father had died in exile in a rented room in a lowland city that never learned to pronounce his name, and Vellmark had been a word on no map when he asked her to take him home someday, back when home was still a place and not a memory with borders.

The meadow sat just inside the old frontier, on land the courts had provisionally returned pending settlement — the lawyers' phrase, *pending settlement*, which Sera had repeated once, flatly, and then never again. The sunburst on the boundary stone was weathered nearly smooth. She knelt and cleared the moss from it with her thumb, gently, the way you'd wake someone.

She said words in Vellmarki. None of the rest of them understood them, and none of them needed to. Then she opened the urn and gave her father to the soil, and the wind came down off the pass and moved through the grass the way wind does when it has somewhere to be, and Sera Ilves, who had nothing left to lose, discovered she had something again and had to sit down on the cold ground and hold onto the boundary stone until it passed.

Nessa knelt beside her. Odile stood with her hat off. Ivo stood a little apart, because some griefs want witnesses and some want company, and he'd learned the difference from a woman who'd waited forty years to bury her father in his own country.

Afterward Sera pressed her palm flat against the turned earth and said, in the trade tongue, "He would have liked the blue shutters. He hated gray."

"We'll paint the barn blue too," Nessa said immediately.

"The barn will be blue," Sera agreed, "and the mule will hate it."

Assessor, grazing nearby, flicked an ear at his own name, which settled the matter.

* * *

The buyer came for the rig in late April.

Ivo had listed it honestly: storm-chased, axle-welded twice, one winch that sang in B-flat when it worked at all. The man who bought it was young, restless, in debt to somebody — Ivo recognized the look the way you recognize your own handwriting. He counted out the coin on the tailgate and asked, casual, "You getting out of the life?"

"I'm getting into another one," Ivo said.

The young man laughed like that was a good joke and drove the rig away south in a plume of dust, and Ivo stood in the road watching until the dust settled, waiting to feel the old itch, the pull of the horizon, the creed he'd lived by for twenty years: *never stay anywhere long enough to be found.*

It didn't come. What came instead was the smell of bread from the waystation, half a mile off, and the knowledge that if he wasn't back by the time it came out of the oven there would be no scene, no ultimatum — just a plate kept warm and a question at supper about whether he'd found anything worth finding out there.

He walked back slow. He let himself be found.

* * *

The house with walls went up through May and June on the rise behind the waystation, close enough to hear supper called, far enough to have his own roof. He built it himself, badly at first and then adequately, and when he got to the windows he put in more glass than any sensible man in storm country would, because Odile told him light was a material and he'd decided to believe her.

On the longest day of the year, they all ended up on his unfinished porch with a barrel of cider Nessa had acquired through means she declined to specify, watching a storm build over the far range. It was a big one, gorgeous, the kind Ivo would once have chased across three counties with his heart hammering and his debts hammering harder.

"You could still go," Sera said, watching his face watch the lightning. "Nobody here would stop you. That's rather the point of the place."

"I know." He turned his cup in his hands. "That's why I don't need to."

Odile snorted softly at her sketchbook. Nessa threw a pebble at him, affectionately. Lightning walked the ridgeline, and thunder arrived a long time later, unhurried, like something that knew exactly where it was going.

Ivo realized then what had changed, and it wasn't courage or debt or even the deeds. It was arithmetic. For twenty years he'd measured his life in miles — miles behind him, miles to the next town, miles between himself and whoever might come looking. But miles reset to zero every time you stopped. They couldn't accumulate. They couldn't mean anything except motion.

Years accumulated. Years stacked up like cordwood, like seasons, like suppers eaten at the same table with the same stubborn, impossible people. You could count them. You could run out of them. And standing on his own porch under his own roof with the storm passing harmlessly to the north, Ivo Winterbourne counted what he had — one year, almost exactly, since a flooded river had given up a chest that ruined him and saved him in the same afternoon — and found that for the first time in his life, the number felt like wealth.

"One," he said aloud.

"What?" Nessa said.

"Nothing." He drank his cider. "One year."

Sera looked at him for a moment, and then at the others, and whatever she saw made her smile the fierce private smile she otherwise saved for the boundary stone. "Give it time," she said. "They get longer."

Down in the valley, the new toll house lamp came on against the early dark, charging no one, turning away no one. The storm rolled east. The bread would be ready soon.

Ivo stayed.
