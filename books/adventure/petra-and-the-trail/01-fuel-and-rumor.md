## 1. Fuel and Rumor

The engine coughed twice on the climb out of San Rafael de los Andes, and I did what I always do when she coughs: I talked to her.

"Not today," I said. "We have a schedule."

The *Avispa* — my Cessna 185, twenty-two years old, older than my flying license and considerably better maintained than my finances — cleared the ridge with a margin I'd describe as adequate and my mechanic would describe as insane. Below us the town shrank to its essentials: the airstrip, the market square, the bank where a man named Ferrero kept a folder with my name on it and a number in it that grew like mold in the wet season.

I don't like thinking about that number before eight in the morning. So I turned north and let the Cordillera del Silencio do what it does, which is make everything human look temporary.

Here's what you need to know about my job, if you're the kind of person who needs things explained: I haul medicine, mail, and gossip across four hundred kilometers of mountains that the government maps but doesn't really believe in. The medicine goes up-valley. The mail goes everywhere. The gossip comes back down with me, because gossip is lighter than cargo and pays about as well.

There are six settlements on my circuit that can't be reached by road, or can be reached by road only if your definition of "reached" includes three days of switchbacks and a river crossing that eats one truck per year, on average, usually in March. The state sends a doctor twice annually. The state sends a census taker every ten years. The state sent a priest in 1987 and he stayed eleven months before the altitude got his heart. Everything else — insulin, penicillin, letters from granddaughters in the capital, spare parts for water pumps, the occasional chicken — arrives by me, or not at all.

Mostly by me. I'm not going to pretend my record is spotless. There was the crate of vaccines I had to jettison over the Río Blanco gorge in '09 when the downdrafts came off the face like a fist. There was the mailbag I left on the tailgate of a pickup in San Rafael while I argued with Ferrero's clerk, which someone stole, which means somewhere in the valley there's a family that thinks their son stopped writing to them from the coast. I've made my peace with it the way you make peace with weather. You log it, you learn from it, you fly anyway.

My compass sits on the panel where the artificial horizon should be, because the artificial horizon died two years ago and cost more to replace than the plane was worth on paper, though the plane's worth on paper has never once matched its worth to me. The compass is brass, older than the plane, older than me. It belonged to my grandfather, who used it not in the air but underground, walking the adits of a mine called La Merced, back when our family owned a piece of that mountain instead of the other way around.

I keep it because it points north no matter what, and because some mornings I need an object in my life that doesn't owe anybody money.

* * *

First stop was Yerba Buena, which isn't a town so much as an argument between a mission, a schoolhouse, and forty hectares of potatoes. The strip there is a shelf of packed earth some missionary graded into the hillside in the fifties, and landing on it is like setting a teacup down on a moving table. You come in over the river, you flare over the fence line, and you pray the goats have moved, because the goats never move.

Sister Angélica was waiting with her list. She's been at Yerba Buena for thirty years and she runs the dispensary with the gentle tyranny of a woman who has outlasted every official who ever tried to tell her how.

"Insulin," she said, taking crates from me before I'd finished tying down. "And the antibiotics. And—" she peered into the hold— "is that a typewriter?"

"It's for the school."

"The school asked for chalk."

"The school asked for chalk and got a typewriter. That's the system working as intended."

She smiled the way she does, which is to say briefly and without surrendering anything. Sister Angélica is the closest thing I have to a confessor, which is to say she knows my debts, my mother's health, and the name of every man who's ever bought me a drink, and she disapproves of precisely none of it except the debts, which she considers a failure of imagination rather than character. I appreciate that in clergy.

"Any word?" she asked, meaning: any news from down-country, any gossip worth the freight.

"Fuel's going up again. Ferrero's clerk got married. The mayor of San Rafael is being investigated for something everyone already knew about."

"That's not news, that's climate." She signed my manifest against the wing strut, her handwriting small and certain. Then, folding the carbon copy along lines worn soft: "You'll be going past Quebrada Honda?"

"Over it, probably around it, depending on the cloud."

"There were men through here last week. From the capital. Government people." She said it lightly, the way you mention rain coming. "They asked about landing strips. High ones. In the interior valleys."

"Surveyors?"

"Something like that. They had instruments and good boots and they didn't smile enough for geologists." She handed back the manifest. "One of them asked who else flies up here."

"And you told him?"

"I told him God and the diocese." Her eyes went flat and pleasant. "He seemed satisfied."

I laughed, because it was expected, and because most of it was even genuine. But something ticked over in the back of my head, the way it does — a small instrument needle swinging off zero. Men from the capital asking after high strips. Asking who flies them.

I've built a whole life on a simple principle: in the Cordillera, questions are cargo too, and you don't carry what you can't land safely. People tell me things because I don't ask. A woman in Pucará told me where her brother hid during the troubles; I flew his letters to him for six years and never once asked why he couldn't post them himself. A muleteer told me which pass the army watched in October; I changed my route and never asked what moved through it instead. This is not cowardice. It's ballast management. Every question you carry is weight, and weight is fuel, and fuel is money, and money is the thing I don't have.

So I didn't ask Sister Angélica anything else. I drank the coffee she insisted on, admired the new roof on the dispensary, refused lunch twice, accepted bread for the road, and took off into a sky that was doing that pale washed-out thing it does before weather decides what it wants to be.

* * *

Pucará next, then Chacalluta, then the long leg up the Río Blanco to Alto Miraflores, dropping parcels the way other pilots drop bombs — carefully, and with apologies to whoever's standing underneath. At Chacalluta an old woman named Doña Rosa gave me a letter for her daughter in the capital and a bag of dried peaches I didn't ask for and couldn't refuse without insulting three generations of orchard-keeping. At Alto Miraflores the schoolteacher met me with a water pump impeller that needed machining in San Rafael and a rumor that needed nothing at all.

"You heard about the survey team?" he said, helping me shove a crate of roofing nails out the door.

"Which survey team?"

"The government one. Mapping the high valleys — mineral assessment, somebody said. Six of them, maybe seven. They flew into Santa Ana de las Nieves two weeks ago with gear for a month." He squinted up at the cloud deck. "Big contract, apparently. Somebody in the ministry wants to know what's up there."

"Rocks," I said. "Ice. Wind. Forty families' worth of grazing rights that nobody's ever bothered to count."

"Maybe they're counting now."

Maybe. I filed it next to Sister Angélica's men with the good boots, and the file was getting thicker. Two independent mentions of a survey team in one day, on a circuit where news normally travels at the speed of mule and arrives pre-decayed. Something was stirring up-country, and whatever it was, it had budget.

Here's the thing about the high valleys: everybody wants something from them and nobody wants to live there. Ranchers graze the low shoulders. Prospectors scratched the streams for a decade after the war and found just enough gold to ruin themselves honestly. And underneath all of it, under the scree and the old workings, lies the fact nobody says out loud anymore: that mountain used to belong to people. My grandfather among them. La Merced and a dozen claims like it, worked for sixty years, and then one season the deeds were "irregular," and then the claims were "consolidated," and then a company that existed for exactly as long as it took to strip the easy ore folded itself into nothing, and the families were left holding memories and grudges in roughly equal measure.

I was born after all that. I inherited the compass and the grudge and not much else. I've spent my whole life being careful not to want the mountain back, because wanting things in this country is how you end up in a riverbed with your boots on backward. I fly. I pay Ferrero — slowly, insultingly slowly, but I pay. I keep my nose out of other people's geology.

That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement.

* * *

Weather closed over the pass at Chacalluta like a hand closing over a coin, so I sat it out on the ground for two hours drinking terrible coffee with the schoolteacher and learning more about the survey team than I wanted to, which is to say: their leader was a woman, which apparently surprised everyone; they had a radio and a supply schedule; and they'd contracted with a pilot out of Santa Ana for resupply drops, since the valley they were working had no strip at all.

"No strip," I repeated. "Then how do they get out?"

"Mule train, I suppose. Or they walk." He shrugged. "Government people. They like walking. It makes them feel thorough."

When the ceiling lifted I climbed back into the *Avispa*, and the needle in the back of my head swung again, because a thought had landed and wouldn't leave: a high valley, no strip, a month's supplies, and a team that didn't smile enough for geologists. I knew that country. Everybody who flies the interior knows it in the way you know a face across a room — the shape of it, the way the light falls, the places the wind lives. And I knew that if I were contracting resupply for a team working the high valleys east of the divide, there was exactly one pilot based close enough and foolish enough to take the job.

Me. It would be me. It's always me, because the alternative is explaining to Ferrero why the number in his folder stopped growing.

I didn't like the thought. I liked it the way you like a stone in your boot: it meant the boot still fit, and it meant every step would remind you.

* * *

Last stop of the day was Santa Ana de las Nieves, which calls itself a town the way a rowboat calls itself a fleet. One street, one cantina, one airstrip cut into the river terrace, and a fuel drum situation best described as aspirational. I landed at half past four with the light going amber on the glacier faces and the wind coming down-valley cold enough to mean something.

Emilio runs the strip, the fuel, and the cantina, in descending order of competence. He was dragging a hose toward my wing before I'd shut down, which either meant business was slow or he had something to sell.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm early. Tomorrow's flight is cancelled."

"Tomorrow's flight." He fed the hose. "That depends."

"On what?"

"On whether you're busy." He glanced at me sideways, and Emilio glancing sideways is a whole telegram. "There's a party looking for charter. Up-valley work. Resupply run."

"To the survey camp."

His eyebrows performed a small shrug of confirmation. "You heard."

"I hear things. It's my job." I watched the fuel gauge needle climb and did arithmetic I didn't enjoy. A resupply run meant hard cash. Hard cash meant Ferrero's folder getting thinner. It also meant flying into whatever those men with the good boots were actually doing, in a valley with no strip, in October, when the weather up there turns on a whim and holds grudges.

"How much?" I asked, because I am a professional and professionals ask before they decide.

Emilio named a figure. It was a good figure. It was, frankly, an indecent figure, the kind of figure that suggests the client has more money than patience, or more urgency than sense.

"When?"

"They wanted yesterday. I told them you'd be along today." He coiled the hose. "They're at the cantina. The woman — Vasco, she said her name was. Elena Vasco. She seems..." He hunted for the word. "...serious."

I looked north, where the valley was, where the clouds were stacking themselves against the divide like furniture pushed against a door. Somewhere up there, seven people with instruments and good boots were mapping rocks that had already been mapped, by men whose names were on deeds my grandfather's hands had signed, and the state was paying indecent money to know what was up there, and men in the capital were asking who else could fly.

Every instinct I own said: go home, drink wine, sleep, mind your ballast.

But the figure was good, and winter was coming, and Ferrero's folder has a winter rate.

"Buy me a coffee," I said. "And point at the serious one."

Emilio grinned and pointed, and through the cantina window I saw a woman at the corner table spreading a map flat with both forearms, pinning the corners with a tin cup, a rock, and a pistol she set down like it was a paperweight, and I felt the needle in the back of my head swing all the way over and stay there.

She looked up and caught me looking. She didn't smile. She beckoned.

And that, I would think later, many times, in circumstances considerably worse than a cantina at dusk — that was the moment the mountain reached up and put its hand on my tail.

I walked inside anyway.
