## 2. The Last Drop

The call came in on the radio at dawn, which should have been my first warning. Government people don't call bush pilots at dawn. They call at ten, after coffee, when the paperwork has been properly admired. Dawn calls are for people who want something done before anyone can ask why.

"Quintana." The voice was clipped, official, the kind that reads from a card even when it's memorized. "Ministry of Natural Resources. We have a supply requisition for the Cordillera survey. Can you be at the depot by eight?"

I was already at the depot. I'd been there since six, arguing with a fuel pump that had opinions about cold mornings, but I wasn't going to tell him that. A pilot who's always available is a pilot who's always cheap.

"Survey," I said. "I didn't know there was one."

"There is." A pause, the sound of a card being consulted. "Cordillera del Silencio, upper valley. You'll have coordinates. Medical supplies, instruments, mail."

"Mail," I repeated. "For a survey team that didn't exist yesterday."

"Quintana, do you want the contract or not?"

I wanted the contract. I wanted it the way a man in a card game wants one more hand, which is to say I wanted it and I already suspected how it would end. My fuel account at Herrera's was three weeks past polite and entering the region of ugly. The medicine run to San Rafael had paid barely enough to cover the oil change, and the oil change had been the kind where you cross your fingers and hope the mechanic finds nothing.

"Coordinates," I said, and wrote them down.

* * *

The valley didn't appear on any of my maps with a name. It had a number, the way unloved children used to have numbers in orphanage ledgers, and the number meant nothing except that someone, somewhere, had decided the valley wasn't worth naming. The Cordillera is full of such places. Mountains don't care what we call them. We name things so we can pretend we own them, and the mountains let us believe it right up until the moment they don't.

I flew south along the river, then west where the river bent, climbing through passes that I knew the way you know the stairs in your own house—by feel, by the particular way the light falls at seven in the morning, by the places where the wind comes over the ridge and slaps the plane sideways like a drunk uncle. The Cessna and I have an arrangement. I keep her fueled and patched, and she forgives me my shortcuts.

The valley opened below me like a held breath. Green going gold at the edges, a river braiding through the middle of it, and on the east bank, on a shelf of flat ground that some glacial hand had smoothed ten thousand years ago for exactly this purpose, a cluster of tents. Orange and gray. A radio mast. A stack of supply crates arranged with the kind of geometry that says *engineers live here*.

I circled once. Standard practice. You look for obstacles, for wires, for the rock or deadfall that will ruin your day. What I noticed instead was the quiet. No figures moving between the tents. No smoke from the cook stove, and it was past eight, and survey crews are like farmers—they eat when the light tells them to.

Then a man stepped out of the lead tent and looked up, and I felt foolish. They'd been inside. It gets cold up there in the mornings even in summer. Cold enough that you stay in your bag until the sun clears the ridge, and the sun hadn't cleared the ridge yet. The valley floor was still in shadow, blue and silver, and I was reading absence into what was only patience.

I set her down. The strip was better than most—firm, dry, long enough that I had her stopped with room to argue about it. The man walked out to meet me, and behind him, one by one, the other tents produced their occupants, blinking, buttoning jackets, the small theater of a camp waking up.

He introduced himself as Vasco. Elena Vasco. I'd assumed the tall man in the canvas jacket was the boss, because the tall man in the canvas jacket had the posture of authority, all shoulders and chin. But it was the woman who came toward me with her hand out, and it was her name the others used when they spoke, and it was to her the tall man looked when he wanted permission to say something.

"Petra Quintana," I said. "You're the ones who don't exist."

She smiled. It was a good smile, quick and a little crooked, but it didn't reach whatever she kept behind her eyes. "We exist," she said. "We're just not discussed."

"Welcome to the Cordillera. Nobody here is discussed. It's why the mountains like us."

The crates came off in relays. Six of them, plus the mail sack and a wooden box marked FRAGILE in three languages, which in my experience means it will be dropped by the second person to touch it. The crew was five: Vasco, the tall man whose name was Ibarra, a young geologist who talked to himself while he worked, and two others whose names I caught and immediately lost, the way you lose the names of people you meet on buses. They were efficient. Too efficient, maybe. Supply drops usually involve a certain amount of cheerful chaos, crates opened on the spot to see what got broken, someone complaining about the coffee. These people unpacked the way soldiers unpack. Everything checked. Everything counted.

"You're not on my circuit," I said to Vasco while the young geologist wrestled the FRAGILE box. "Who arranged that?"

"The Ministry."

"The Ministry doesn't arrange things. The Ministry permits things, after they've been denied twice."

"Then someone in the Ministry arranged it," she said, "and I'd thank you not to look so pleased about the contradiction."

I wasn't pleased. That was the thing. There was a small cold weight settling behind my sternum, the one I get when the numbers don't add and I can't yet find the error. A survey team that wasn't discussed. A requisition that came at dawn. A camp that unpacked like a garrison. None of it was wrong, exactly. All of it was slightly wrong, the way a portrait is slightly wrong when the painter has moved the eyes two millimeters too close together. You can't say what it is. You just know the face is watching you differently.

"Weather," I said instead. "You're at nine thousand feet with a south-facing valley. You've got maybe six weeks before the first snow that means anything, and after that you're here until spring. Is that the plan?"

"That's the plan."

"Radio check-ins?"

"Every third day, six in the evening. You'll hear us on the San Rafael relay."

Every third day. I filed that away the way I file everything, in the drawer that has no lock and no key and no organization whatsoever, but that has never once lost anything.

"Six weeks," I said. "I'll come back before the snow. Somebody always needs something the Ministry forgot to requisition."

"Somebody always does," she agreed.

* * *

I should have taken off then. The wind was still sleeping, the barometer was steady, and every hour I lingered was an hour the morning weather could spend deciding to become something else. But the young geologist had knocked a fuel cap loose on one of the crates and diesel was seeping into the gravel, and there was a whole ceremony of absorbent cloth and official language that had to happen, and by the time it was done the sun had cleared the ridge and everyone had eaten and the camp had become, briefly, a place where people lived rather than a place where people were stationed.

Vasco walked me back to the plane. The others stayed behind, and I remember noticing that too—the way they stayed, the way none of them drifted within earshot, the way Ibarra suddenly found the radio mast fascinating and needed to inspect it from a distance of exactly far enough.

She didn't speak until we were standing at the Cessna's door, with my body between her and the camp and the camp's eyes politely elsewhere.

"Quintana. Do you know this valley? The old valley, I mean. Not the number. The name it had before."

I looked at her. "Before?"

"Before the relocations." She said it evenly, the way you'd say *before the war* or *before the fire*, the words worn smooth from being handled. "Thirty years ago. There were families up here. Farms. A mine."

"Everyone knows about the mine," I said, which was true and also a way of saying nothing. Everyone knows about the mine the way everyone knows about a relative who disgraced himself—acknowledged, unexamined, never mentioned at dinner.

"Do you know what happened to the deeds?"

The cold weight behind my sternum shifted. My grandfather had held a claim in these mountains. My grandfather had held a claim, and then one autumn the claims had been *regularized*, which was the word they used, and by the following spring the Quintanas were farming a smaller piece of poorer ground in a different valley, and my grandfather had spent the rest of his life being polite about it in a way that curdled everything he touched. I was four when he died. I remember his hands and his silence, and I remember my mother saying once, when she thought I was asleep, that the paper had been the thief, not the men. Paper doesn't leave tracks either.

"Nobody knows what happened to the deeds," I said. "That's rather the point of what happened to the deeds."

"Yes," she said. "It is."

She reached into her jacket. What she brought out was a piece of paper folded into eighths, soft at the creases, the color of old bone. And here is the part I have turned over a thousand times since: she didn't hand it to me. She opened my map case herself—reached across and unclipped the flap, the way you'd do for a child or a patient—and slid the folded paper in among my charts, and clipped the flap shut, and patted it once, flat, the way you'd pat a wound you'd just bandaged.

"For later," she said.

"Later what?"

"Later is when you'll know." Her crooked smile again, and this time it reached her eyes, and what was in her eyes was not friendliness. It was the look of a woman putting something valuable into the one place she's decided is safe, and hoping to God she's judged right. "You fly everywhere and owe everyone. Nobody watches a woman like that. It's the best hiding place in the Cordillera."

"I don't like being a hiding place. Hiding places get burned down."

"Which is why I'm asking instead of telling." She stepped back. "Fly careful, Quintana. Weather's building in the south. I can smell it."

"So can my left knee. It's been in government service longer than either of us."

But I checked the sky anyway, because she was right. South and west, over the ridgeline, the air had gone the particular bruised color that means the mountains are brewing something and haven't decided on the menu. I had maybe two hours of decent flying. Maybe less.

* * *

I took off with the valley at my back and the folded paper riding in my map case like a splinter. I didn't look at it. That's a discipline, not a virtue. You don't read other people's documents in a moving aircraft; you read them on the ground, with time, and by the time I was on the ground the weather had made its decision.

It came in behind me like a debt collector. I'd turned north up the river valley with the sun still on the water when the southern sky simply closed, the bruise spreading, and the wind arrived ten minutes later with the smell of rain that hadn't fallen here yet but intended to. I ran the river low and fast, the way the old pilots do it, under the weather rather than through it, and the Cessna grumbled about it and held together, and behind me the last I saw of the high valley was the cloud swallowing the ridge whole.

I put down at San Rafael an hour later with rain hammering the strip and my hands aching from gripping the yoke harder than the flying required. Herrera was in his shed, as always, a man who had achieved perfect union with his machinery by becoming one.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I look like a woman who just outran a storm."

"Same thing." He wiped his hands on a rag that was more rag than cloth. "You were up south. High country?"

"Supply run. Survey camp."

His rag stopped moving. It was a small thing, a half-second pause, but Herrera has been in the Cordillera longer than I've been alive, and small things are his whole language. "Survey camp," he said. "In the old valley?"

"Nobody calls it that."

"Nobody calls it that *now*." He went back to wiping, but slower. "Who requisitioned the flight?"

"The Ministry."

"Hm," said Herrera, and the *hm* contained an entire conversation we did not have, and I understood that he knew something and had decided, in his way, that knowing it out loud would be bad for both of us.

I walked back to my room over the depot in the rain, and I took out the map case, and I sat on the cot with the folded paper in my hands for a long time before I opened it.

It was a fragment. A corner of a larger document, torn, not cut—torn in anger or in haste, the fibers ragged. A seal, half of it, stamped in wax gone the color of dried blood. A line of formal script: *...con los linderos que siempre han poseído...* — with the boundaries they have always possessed. And below it, a name. Not my grandfather's. A name I didn't know. Herrera's father's, though I wouldn't learn that until later, and by later everything would be different.

*For later*, she'd said.

I sat there while the rain came down on the tin roof like applause for something I hadn't done yet, and I tried to name the feeling in my chest, and I couldn't. It wasn't fear. Fear has a shape, a direction, something to run from. This was the other thing, the one that visits before fear arrives, the one that stands in the doorway of the house and doesn't come in and doesn't leave.

The radio in the corner hissed. Every third day, six in the evening, the San Rafael relay.

Tomorrow was a third day.

I put the paper back in the map case, among the charts where it would ride with me everywhere, weightless and enormous, and I lay down on the cot with my boots on, because something in me had already decided that I would not be sleeping long.
