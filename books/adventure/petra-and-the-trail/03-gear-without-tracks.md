## 3. Gear Without Tracks

Ten days is a long time to carry a question you can't afford to ask.

I spent most of them the way I spend everything: in the air, hauling other people's necessities and none of my own. Insulin to San Rafael de los Andes. A crate of schoolbooks that smelled so strongly of ink the whole cabin tasted like a printing press. Two hundred kilos of flour for the co-op at Vilcabamba, plus a side of gossip about the co-op treasurer's second wife, which I delivered free of charge because gossip is the only cargo that appreciates in value the longer you hold it.

The folded paper rode in my map case the entire time. I didn't open it. That's not discipline—that's superstition with better posture. Elena Vasco had said *for later*, and *later* has a way of arriving whether you invite it or not, and some part of me wanted to keep one thing unsaid, unread, unopened, like a coin under the tongue. As long as it stayed folded, the survey team was still just a team doing survey things on a mountain, and I was still just a pilot who'd made an honest drop and flown home through weather that would've scared a lesser woman into a career change.

Also, I'll admit it: I was busy not thinking about my debts. The Banco Agrario sends letters now instead of telegrams, which they seem to think is a kindness. Four pages of formal language to say what a telegram says in ten words: we own your airplane, and by extension your legs, and by further extension whatever's left of your pride. I keep the letters in the same drawer as my mother's rosary, which she would have found either touching or blasphemous, depending on how much wine she'd had.

So: ten days of flour and insulin and ink-smell, and then the schedule said survey camp, and the sky said maybe, and I said yes, because I always say yes to the sky. It's the only yes I've never regretted at the time.

* * *

The valley looked wrong before I could say why.

I came in from the south, the same way as before, down the throat of the river gorge with the walls rising on both sides like the covers of a book nobody reads anymore. The weather was clean—high thin cirrus, wind out of the west at fifteen knots, visibility you could cut into strips and sell. Good flying. The kind of day where the mountains show you everything they have, all their scree and shadow and old snow, holding nothing back.

And the valley held nothing back, and that was the problem.

A occupied camp has a texture. Smoke, first of all—a thread off the stove pipe, leaning east in a west wind, the most reassuring crooked line in the world. Laundry if they're feeling domestic. A figure or two moving between tents, small as ants but *moving*, and movement is the thing. From five hundred feet up, people look like weather. They drift. They gather and disperse. A camp without them looks like a photograph of itself.

This camp was a photograph of itself.

I circled once, telling myself I was checking the wind sock. The sock hung dead still, which matched the wind I was feeling at altitude about as well as a fish matches a bicycle. Then I told myself I was checking the strip, and it was fine, gravel and grass exactly where I'd left it, my own tire tracks from ten days ago still printed in the mud near the edge like a signature nobody had bothered to erase.

Nobody had walked on them since. That was the detail that put the cold in my stomach, though I didn't understand it yet. Ten days. A camp of six people. And the ground around my landing tracks lay smooth as fresh paper.

I set her down anyway. Of course I did. You don't fly two hours into the Cordillera del Silencio, see something wrong, and fly away from it—not if you're the kind of person who ends up flying into the Cordillera del Silencio in the first place. The tires touched, rolled, stopped. The engine ticked itself quiet. And then there was just the sound of the valley, which was no sound at all, except a stream somewhere below the moraine talking to itself in a voice too steady to be natural.

"Hello the camp!" I called, because that's what you do, and because my voice needed to hear itself work.

Nothing answered. Not even a bird. Though to be fair, at this altitude the birds keep their opinions to themselves.

* * *

The stove was warm.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had that morning, standing in the middle of a camp that had been abandoned by ghosts. I pulled off my glove and laid my palm flat against the stove's iron flank, and it held the kind of warmth a body leaves in a chair—not hot, not cold, but recently *occupied*. Somewhere between an hour and three, my hand told me. My hand is not a scientific instrument, but it has never lied to me about engines or stoves, and I trust it more than I trust most men.

The tents were pitched. All six, guy-lines taut, corners staked with the neatness of people who expected to come back to them. Inside the largest, bedrolls were rolled. A tin cup sat on the supply crate where someone had set it down mid-drink—I checked; there was a ring of dried tea at the bottom, half a cup's worth, abandoned. A paperback novel lay face-down on a cot, spine cracked, holding its place at page two hundred and something for a reader who hadn't returned.

The gear was stacked. That was the part that made my skin crawl, though it took me a minute to know why. It wasn't scattered, the way things are when people leave in a hurry or get taken in one. It was *stacked*—rope coils squared, ice axes crossed in pairs, the rock samples in labeled canvas bags lined up on a tarp with military corners. Someone had organized this. Someone had stood here, after whatever happened, and tidied.

You don't tidy after an avalanche. You don't tidy after a crevasse takes your colleague. And you especially don't tidy, then walk away leaving the stove lit and your book at page two hundred and eleven.

I walked the perimeter twice. The second time I did it properly, bent over, reading the ground the way Tomás Aguilar taught me to read it years ago, back when he still took paying clients and I still thought of him as a guide instead of a rumor with a walking stick. Ground tells you everything if you let it. Grass bends in the direction of passage and stays bent for days. Gravel rolls under a boot and leaves a bright scar against the weathered stone. Mud remembers.

The mud remembered nothing.

No tracks leaving the camp. None. Not toward the pass to the north, not down the gorge to the south, not up the talus slopes east or west. Six people had been here—the six sleeping bags said so, the six cups, the six places set at the mess table under the kitchen fly—and six people had apparently been raptured off the face of the mountain sometime in the last few hours, leaving behind a warm stove and a half-drunk cup of tea.

Except the tea. I kept coming back to the tea. If they'd vanished that morning, the stove might still hold heat, sure. But the cup should have been full. People don't pour tea and abandon it unless they poured it and then something interrupted them—and nothing interrupts six trained surveyors all at once, quietly enough that every one of them leaves their gear stacked and their beds made.

Unless they left on purpose. Unless they left *fast*, on purpose, in such a hurry that the tidying was done first and the tea got forgotten in the last thirty seconds. Unless somebody came up that valley whose arrival was worth abandoning a hot drink over.

I stood in the middle of it all with my hands on my hips and gave the valley my best professional assessment, which was: *Petra Quintana, you are standing inside a sentence with the verb missing.*

* * *

Here's the thing about me that the officials later found so inconvenient: I notice things, and then I make the mistake of noticing that I've noticed. Most people have a mercy clause built in—they see something strange, and their mind files it under *strange* and moves on to lunch. Mine files it under *evidence* and starts building a case I never asked to prosecute.

So I did what I do. I went through the camp the way I'd want someone to go through mine if I ever went missing, which is thoroughly, respectfully, and with an eye for what's absent rather than what's present.

What was present: everything. Radios—both of them, sitting in the equipment tent, batteries in. That alone should have called a search party. Survey teams don't lose radio contact voluntarily; the radio is the umbilical cord, and Elena Vasco struck me as a woman who kept her cords attached. What was present: the theodolite, the barometers, the sample bags, the medical kit, the food for another three weeks. What was present: boots. Six pairs of spare boots, dry and waiting.

What was absent: personal papers. Every notebook, every field journal, every map case gone. I knew because I'd seen Elena's map case ten days ago—leather, scarred, held shut with a brass clasp shaped like a condor—and the table where she'd worked had four pale rectangles in the dust where papers used to live.

They'd taken their records and left everything else. Including, notably, anything that could tell me where they'd gone.

Which is when I remembered that I was carrying one of their records myself.

* * *

I sat down on the supply crate with the tin cup beside me and took the folded paper out of my map case, and for a moment I just held it. There's a weight a thing acquires when the people who gave it to you stop being reachable. It was the same piece of paper it had been ten days ago—cheap, soft-edged, folded in quarters—but it had gained mass somehow, the way a letter gains mass after the sender dies.

I opened it.

It was a fragment. Torn, not cut—the edges ragged, one corner missing entirely, as if it had been ripped from a larger document by someone in a hurry or someone with strong feelings. The paper was old, older than the survey, older than me: heavy rag stock gone amber at the folds, the kind they don't make anymore because the trees it came from are all furniture now.

The writing was copperplate. Beautiful, formal, the handwriting of a man who'd been paid to write beautifully and formally. Most of it was torn away, but what survived said:

*...estando presentes las partes, y en nombre de los herederos de la familia Quintana, se confirma y ratifica el derecho de propiedad sobre la veta y sus pertenencias, comprendiendo el socavón conocido como La Boca del...*

I read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you touch a bruise to confirm it hurts.

*La familia Quintana.* My family. My grandfather's family, spelled out in a dead man's copperplate, in a document confirming—ratifying, the word was *ratifica*, a word with teeth in it—a property right. A mine. *La Boca del* something, the name torn away, but I didn't need the name. My grandfather had a name for the hole in the mountain that ate forty years of his life and then swallowed itself: he called it *la mina que Dios cerró*, the mine God closed, and he said it the way you talk about a wife who left you, with love and grievance in the same breath.

His claim was seized the year my mother turned fourteen. Everyone knew the story, the way everyone knows a scar's origin: the new mining law, the inspectors from the capital, the paperwork that appeared one morning declaring his title irregular, his deed void, his mountain suddenly belonging to a company whose directors had never seen it. He fought for nine years and died poor, and the families who lost land alongside him—the Roldáns, the Chumbes, the Vasco—

I stopped.

Vasco.

Elena Vasco. Who pressed a torn fragment of a pre-seizure land deed into my map case and said *for later*, and whose team had now evaporated off a mountainside leaving a warm stove and no tracks, taking every scrap of paper they owned except the one they'd already sent down the mountain in the custody of a bush pilot too indebted and too curious to throw it away.

I sat very still on that crate for a long time. The stream talked to itself. The wind sock hung dead. Somewhere above the valley rim, a cloud built itself slowly over the western peaks, white and innocent as a lie.

Here's what I understood, sitting there: I was not holding a curiosity anymore. I was holding evidence. Evidence that the seizures—the ones that broke my grandfather and forty families like ours—had been contradicted by documents that existed, that someone had kept, that a government surveyor had found again and judged dangerous enough to smuggle out in pieces, through a pilot, marked *for later*.

And *later* had arrived, and the people who'd mailed it were gone, and the only address on the package was me.

* * *

I did three things before I flew out, and I did them fast, because the stove's warmth was fading and with it my estimate of how much lead time I had.

First, I photographed the camp—not with a camera, which I don't own, but with my eyes, which are the only instrument I've never crashed. Tents, gear stacks, the pale rectangles in the dust, the dead sock, the unbroken mud. I filed it all where I file everything, in the drawer behind my sternum that doesn't lock and can't be burned.

Second, I left a mark. Nothing clever—just my boot heel dragged across the mud by the airstrip in the sign every pilot in these mountains knows: a slash and a dot. *Was here. Found nothing. Moving on.* If anyone from the team came back, they'd know a friend had landed and gone. If anyone else came back, it would read exactly like what it was: a routine check, nothing found, nothing worth reporting.

Third, I took the fragment out of my map case and moved it. Map cases get searched—it's the first place anyone looks on a bush plane, after the fuel receipts. I sewed it into the lining of my flight jacket instead, behind the patch on the left shoulder where the fabric was already doubled, using thread from the emergency kit and ten minutes of the steadiest stitching of my life. My grandmother taught me to sew. She'd have laughed herself sick to know the skill finally paid for itself.

Then I flew out, south down the gorge, low over the stream that never stopped talking, and the valley closed behind me like a mouth.

* * *

The thing about dread is that it doesn't feel like fear. Fear is a animal thing—fast, hot, over quickly, useful. Dread is slower. Dread is arithmetic. It sits in the back of the cockpit with you for two hours and does sums.

*Six people, gone without tracks.* Sum: they didn't walk out. *Stove warm, papers taken.* Sum: they left deliberately, within hours, and they didn't plan to come back for their gear. *Deed fragment, my family's name, marked for later.* Sum: Elena Vasco knew something was coming for her, and she'd known it at least ten days ago, and she'd chosen me—the pilot with the debts and the dead grandfather and the habit of not asking questions—as the one person careless enough and stubborn enough to keep a secret alive.

And underneath all of it, the sum I didn't want to do: *if they took their papers and left no tracks, then either they had a way out that isn't a trail—or someone made sure there were no tracks, and the warm stove was left by whoever arranged the leaving.*

By the time the capital strip came up out of the haze, I had stopped doing arithmetic and started making decisions, which is dread's final gift: it hardens. It goes in like a soft thing and comes out like a keel.

I taxied in past the fuel bowser, shut her down, and sat for a moment in the ticking quiet, my hand resting on my left shoulder where the paper slept against my shoulder blade.

Ten days ago I'd have said my job was to stay out of things. Fly the medicine, haul the flour, deliver the gossip, ask nothing, owe everyone, keep my head below the snowline where the weather and the politics both live.

But my grandfather's name was stitched into my jacket now, in a dead man's copperplate, and there were six empty tents on a mountain with a warm stove in the middle of them, and I had looked at that mud and the mud had told me the truth, and the truth was that somebody wanted this story to end in silence.

I have carried medicine, mail, flour, and gossip for eleven years. I decided, rolling up to the tie-downs with the afternoon sun flat on the windscreen, that I could carry one more thing.

I just hadn't yet met the officials who'd decide my silence was worth more than my testimony—or the strangers with cameras who were already, at that moment, walking across the apron toward my airplane.
