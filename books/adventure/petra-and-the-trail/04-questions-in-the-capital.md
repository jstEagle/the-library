## 4. Questions in the Capital

The capital smelled of diesel and eucalyptus, which is to say it smelled like every town that had ever decided it was important. I tied down the Cessna at the government strip—newer asphalt than mine, painted lines still bright—and walked into a building with a brass plaque that said *Dirección de Asuntos Territoriales* in letters polished by people who wanted you to know they mattered.

I'd been summoned. That was the word they used on the radio, *summoned*, as if I were a mule that might otherwise wander off. The voice on the frequency had been polite about it, the way a fence is polite.

The man waiting for me was called Señor Ibáñez, though he introduced himself so softly I mostly caught the shape of the syllables. He had a desk the size of a dining table and nothing on it but a telephone, a blotter, and a folder with my name on it. The folder was thin. I remember being relieved by that, and later I understood why I shouldn't have been. Thin folders mean they already know everything worth knowing. Thick folders are for people who are still interesting.

"Señora Quintana. Thank you for coming."

"You didn't leave me much choice."

"We like to think of it as an invitation." He smiled like a man who had practiced smiling at people who owed him things. "Coffee?"

"I've had coffee."

"Of course." He opened the folder anyway, unhurried, and I realized the coffee question hadn't been a question at all. It had been a test of how I answered questions. "You made a delivery to the Vasco survey camp on the fourteenth of last month."

"A supply drop. Weather window closed behind me. It's all in my logbook, which I brought."

"We have your logbook." He said it gently, and something cold went through me, because I had left the logbook in the plane. Which meant someone had taken it out, read it, and put it back—or hadn't put it back, and this one was a copy, or an original, or whatever version suited them. "Unscheduled stop. You spoke with the team leader. Elena Vasco."

"Thirty seconds. She signed for the cargo. She asked if I could carry mail out and I told her no, weight limits. Standard."

"What did she give you?"

And there it was. Not *did she give you anything*—*what*. I kept my face doing what faces do when they're bored, which is nothing. "A signature on a waybill."

Ibáñez looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere down the hall a typewriter clattered like rain on a tin roof. "Señora Quintana, three trained surveyors are missing from a valley with one approach. No tracks. No radio call. Their equipment is intact. You are the last person known to have contact with them. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Tired," I said. "It makes me tired. I flew them their rice and kerosene. If I knew where Elena Vasco was, I'd be telling her family, not sitting in a room with a man who calls a summons an invitation."

He wrote something down. I watched him write it and thought: he's not writing what I said. He's writing how long I took to say it.

"Tell me about the weather," he said.

"The weather?"

"On the fourteenth. When you left the valley."

"Closing fast. Front off the western cordillera, cloud deck dropping, maybe forty minutes of margin. Why does the weather matter?"

"Because," he said, folding his hands, "a pilot with your experience would not have lingered. And yet your engine log shows eleven minutes on the ground. Eleven minutes is a conversation, Señora Quintana. Eleven minutes is a paper changing hands."

I want to tell you I laughed it off. I didn't. I sat very still and did arithmetic I didn't like: someone had pulled my engine log, cross-checked it against my fuel purchase, and timed my stop on the ground to the minute. That's not an investigation. Investigations are messy and slow. This was bookkeeping.

"Elena Vasco asked me to take a letter to her daughter," I said finally. "I told her no. Weight limits. She kept it. That's the whole story, and I'll sign anything you like saying so."

"Her daughter has been asking questions too," Ibáñez said, and his voice had changed—not warmer, exactly, but more careful, the way a man's voice changes when he steps onto ice. "In the south. Asking about the survey. About the valley. You wouldn't know anything about that."

"I don't know anyone's daughter. I know airstrips and weather."

"Mm." He closed the folder. Just like that, done, as if we'd discussed freight rates. "Thank you for your time. You're free to go. We may need to speak again."

"People usually do," I said, and walked out before my face could betray me, because here's what I knew and he didn't know I knew: I had a folded piece of paper in my map case with names on it. Old names. Names on a deed fragment that matched a mine my grandfather worked before men with stamps and seals took it away. And a bureaucrat who times your ground stops to the minute doesn't ask *what did she give you* unless somebody above him already knows what she gave.

* * *

The memo, I learned later, went up two floors that same afternoon. I never saw the original, but Tomás—of all people, months afterward, over a fire I'll tell you about another time—recited it nearly word for word, because he'd spent thirty years around men who wrote memos and he never forgot a sentence that frightened him. This is what it said:

> **INTERNAL — DIRECCIÓN DE ASUNTOS TERRITORIALES**
> **RE: SUCESO VALLE ALTO / EQUIPO VASCO**
>
> 1. Pilot P. Quintana interviewed 1100 hrs. Cooperative in manner, evasive in substance. Denies receipt of documents. Ground time at camp (11 min) inconsistent with stated purpose. Recommend continued monitoring.
>
> 2. Subject's financial position precarious. Note outstanding obligation against family property (ref. Banco Agrario file Q-114). Leverage exists should cooperation become necessary.
>
> 3. Survey team status: recovery operation suspended pending weather. No further resources allocated.
>
> 4. Recommend no public statement regarding valley access restrictions until matter resolved discreetly.
>
> 5. Third party interests have been notified. Advise against direct action at this stage.
>
> — Ibáñez

Read that again, the way I read it a hundred times afterward. *Recovery operation suspended.* Three people missing in the mountains and they suspended looking for them after nine days, in September, before the snows. And then item five, which is the sentence that should have kept me awake for the rest of my life: *third party interests have been notified.* The government wasn't hunting whoever made those surveyors vanish. The government was passing messages to them.

And item two. *Leverage exists.* My grandfather's land went into a bank file with a number, and my family's debt note lived in a drawer in some office, and a man I'd met once for eleven minutes knew exactly how heavy it was.

* * *

I spent that night at the boarding house near the strip, the one with the sagging porch where pilots drank cheap pisco and lied about headwinds. I told myself I'd sleep. Instead I lay on a mattress that smelled of other people's tobacco and listened to the dogs argue about garbage, turning the deed fragment over in my mind. Forty families, Tomás would say later. But that night I only knew it was old, and real, and that Elena Vasco had pressed it into my map case with fingers cold from altitude and said *for later* in a voice that meant *because there may not be an afterward for me.*

Around midnight I gave up and went out to check the plane. Habit. A pilot checks her plane the way a mother checks a sleeping child—not because she expects trouble, but because not checking is unthinkable.

There was a man leaning against the fence at the edge of the strip.

Not doing anything. Just leaning, in the loose way of a man who has nowhere particular to be, wearing a canvas jacket and a hat that had been fashionable for nobody ever. There was a camera hanging against his chest. Tourists carried cameras. Nobody had been a tourist within two hundred kilometers of this town since the road washed out in '61.

I stood in the shadow of the hangar and watched him. After a while he straightened, raised the camera, and took a photograph of my Cessna. Not of the sunset behind it—the sun had set an hour ago. Of the plane. Then he turned and photographed the tail number specifically, close, the way you photograph a document.

Here's a thing about me: I don't spook easy. You don't spend fifteen years landing on riverbars and ridgelines if you flinch at shadows. So instead of walking back inside, I walked toward him, boots loud on the gravel, and he didn't move, which told me more than the camera did. Spies scurry. Men with permission stand still.

"Lose something?" I asked.

"Just admiring the aircraft." His Spanish had gravel in it from somewhere else. North, maybe. Or nowhere—somewhere they train accents out of you.

"She's not much to look at. Older than both of us, probably."

"Reliable, though. I hear she goes places other planes won't." He tipped his hat. "High valleys."

"The high valleys are full of weather and nothing else."

"That right?" He smiled, and it was a perfectly friendly smile, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all day. "Then I guess she flies them empty."

He walked off toward the town lights without hurrying, and I stood there in the dark with my heart going like a lazy piston and understood, finally and completely, what had happened. For fifteen years I'd been the one who saw everything and reported nothing—the eyes of the cordillera, the woman who knew whose wife was sick and whose husband was drinking and which estancia had lost sheep to wolves. Information passed through me like water through a channel. I was safe because I was useful and harmless.

Somewhere between that valley and this fence, the water had stopped passing through. Now it was pooling behind me. I was carrying something—a scrap of paper, a name, a fact—and the moment you carry something, you're not a channel anymore. You're a target.

The hunter becomes the hunted. My father used to say it about pumas, standing on our stolen land we still called ours. *When the puma starts watching the trail behind her, mija, she's not hunting anymore.*

I went back to my room and took the deed fragment out of my map case, and I'm not proud of what I did next, but I'll tell you anyway: I sewed it into the lining of my flight jacket, under the collar, where a seamstress's hands had already mended things twice. Then I packed my bag, because staying in that town one more night felt like standing in a riverbed watching the water rise.

In the morning I filed a flight plan south. Routine circuit, medicine run, nothing unusual. The clerk stamped it without looking up, and I noticed—I always notice—that the man with the canvas jacket was drinking coffee across the street, and he watched me load the plane, and he did not write anything down, because he didn't need to. He already knew where I was going. What he didn't know was who I'd be meeting there.

Because taped under my seat cushion, where only a mechanic or a desperate woman would ever look, was a telegram I'd picked up at the mission strip two days earlier, addressed to nobody, marked *hold for pickup*, in a hand I didn't recognize:

**PILOT WHO FLEW TO EMPTY CAMP. MY MOTHER IS ELENA VASCO. I AM COMING SOUTH ON THE ROAD. I WILL FIND YOU. — M.V.**

I climbed in, ran the checks, and flew south into a sky the color of hammered tin, wondering which of us was following whom.
