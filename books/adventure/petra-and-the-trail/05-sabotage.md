## 5. Sabotage

There's a saying among bush pilots in the Cordillera del Silencio: the mountain doesn't kill you, it just holds the door open and waits to see what you'll do. I'd always found that comforting. That morning I would have traded it for a simpler proverb, like *check your fuel twice* or *trust no one with soft hands*.

I was up before the mission bells, which in San Rafael de Abajo is a competitive sport. The Franciscans ring at five-thirty sharp, and every pilot in the valley has learned to be airborne by five-twenty just to say they slept through it. My Cessna—*La Golondrina*, the Swallow, though she flew more like a drunk pigeon with opinions—sat on the strip under a skin of frost, and I walked her wings the way my father taught me: heel of the hand along the leading edge, feeling for dents, ice, anything that shouldn't be there.

Everything felt right. That's the part I keep coming back to. Everything felt right.

The mechanic on duty that week was Chelo Ferreyra, a bowlegged man of about sixty who had once rebuilt an engine using parts from three different aircraft and a prayer, and who charged accordingly. He'd serviced the Swallow two days earlier after my run down from the northern missions—oil change, filter, the usual confession and absolution. He waved at me from his hangar with a wrench held aloft like a tiny scepter, and I waved back, and if there's a moment in this whole story I'd give back to have again, it might be that one. Two people waving. Nothing wrong in the world yet.

I loaded light: mail sacks for the river settlements, a crate of penicillin for the clinic at Puerto Escondido, a bolt of cloth Doña Imelda had ordered four months ago and would complain about receiving at all. And in the map case, flat against my knee where it had ridden for ten days now, the folded paper Elena Vasco had pressed on me at the survey camp. I hadn't opened it. I told myself that was discipline. It was closer to superstition—the way you don't say the word *crash* in a cockpit, the way you don't read a letter that might make you a witness.

The officials in the capital had asked me about that paper, actually. Not directly—they were too smooth for direct—but the one with the rimless glasses had let his gaze rest on my map case a half-second too long, the way a man looks at your wife across a dinner table when he thinks you won't notice. I'd smiled and talked about weather. Weather is the great conversation of pilots; it can fill any silence and commit you to nothing.

Five-twenty-two. Throttle forward. The Swallow rolled down San Rafael's strip with her usual grumbling reluctance, tail coming up, airspeed alive—and then, at maybe forty knots, with the trees at the far end beginning to look less like scenery and more like an appointment, the engine did something I had never heard an engine do.

It didn't quit. Quitting would have been honest. Instead it stumbled, caught, stumbled again—a ragged, coughing misfire, like a man trying to sing while being strangled—and then died altogether with a sound I can only describe as offended.

Forty knots. Trees ahead. Riverbar to the right, downstream, where the Río Blanco braided out wide and shallow and gravelly. I've made that calculation a hundred times in drills I hoped never to use, and here it was arriving uninvited, and I will tell you honestly: my hands knew what to do before my fear finished reporting for duty. Nose down to keep the speed. Carb heat, out of habit, though the engine was already past caring. Flaps when the bar was sure beneath me. And the little cold voice in my head that sounds like my father saying, *fly the airplane all the way into the ground, mija, because the ground is not flying it for you.*

The Swallow hit gravel at the upstream end of the bar, gear absorbing what it could and then folding like a bad argument. We slewed left, wingtip plowing a furrow you could plant potatoes in, and stopped with a shudder and a hiss and a smell of hot metal and snapped composure.

Then there was quiet, except the river, which had been running the whole time and hadn't noticed me at all. There's a lesson in that, if you want one.

I sat there for a moment doing inventory. Head: attached. Teeth: all present, tongue bitten but serviceable. Left shoulder: bruised, complaining, functional. Pride: total loss. I climbed out through the broken door because the other one wouldn't open, stood on the riverbar in the early light, and looked at my aircraft lying there with her nose in the gravel like a bird that had flown into a window.

I said a word I won't repeat here. Then I said several more.

* * *

Chelo arrived two hours later, having heard over the radio and driven the river road as fast as his truck's dignity allowed. By then I'd dragged the mail sacks clear, covered the engine with a tarp, and eaten half a brick of hard cheese out of spite. He took one look at the Swallow, one look at me, and said, "You walked away."

"I walked away."

"Then we'll drink tonight. First, we find out why." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, and went straight for the fuel system, because a pilot who crashes on takeoff with a freshly serviced engine has only so many suspects, and gravity isn't one of them.

I watched him work. Chelo is a man who talks to engines the way some men talk to horses, low and reasonable, and for a long time the only sounds were the river and the small metallic vocabulary of his tools. The sun climbed. My shoulder throbbed in time with my pulse. Somewhere in the middle of the morning I realized I wasn't thinking about the crash at all. I was thinking about the rimless glasses in the capital, and the half-second glance at my map case, and the stranger with the camera who had photographed the Swallow on the apron while pretending to read a newspaper that was three days old and upside down.

"Petra," Chelo said.

He was holding the fuel line up to the light. Inside it, visible against the brass fitting where the line met the filter housing, was something that should never be anywhere near an aircraft: grit. Not sand, not the ordinary dust of an unpaved strip. This was finer, darker, deliberate-looking—like someone had taken a handful of the mountains themselves and ground it fine and fed it to my engine a pinch at a time.

"That's not contamination," I said. It wasn't a question, but I needed to hear him say it.

"No." He turned the fitting in his fingers. "Contamination comes in with bad fuel, and it shows everywhere. This is placed. Someone put this in your line, and they put it where it wouldn't stop you on the ground. It stops you in the air, or it doesn't stop you at all." He looked at me over the top of his spectacles. "You understand what I'm telling you."

I understood. A sabotage meant to look like maintenance failure. Meant, if I was lucky, to ground me. If I wasn't lucky—

"You're supposed to be dead," Chelo said, "or walking."

The river kept on not caring. I found that I was gripping the wing strut hard enough to hurt my hand, and I made myself let go, finger by finger, because a person who breaks her own hand squeezing a strut is a person who has stopped being useful.

"Who?" I said. And then, because Chelo deserved better than a rhetorical question: "No. Don't answer that. You don't know, and I don't either, and neither of us wants to guess out loud on an open riverbar."

Chelo nodded slowly. He put the fitting down on a clean rag with the care of a man handling evidence, which is exactly what it was. "I serviced her two days ago," he said. "Line was clean. So it came in after. Either at night, here—" he gestured at the strip, the fence, the dark — "or before she ever reached me."

Before. Before the filter, before the oil change, before the northern run. Which meant whoever did this had done it days ago, calmly, while I was hauling medicine and gossip and believing myself invisible. While I was practicing the family art of not asking questions.

My grandfather used to say our family survived the seizures—the mine taken, the deeds gone, the whole valley redistributed to men with signatures—by keeping our heads down and our mouths shut. He said it like a strategy. Looking at that grit in the fuel line, I understood for the first time that it had also been a surrender, and that the surrender hadn't even worked. They'd come for us anyway. They always come anyway. Keeping quiet doesn't make you safe; it just makes you easy.

Something shifted in my chest, and I want to describe it precisely, because it wasn't courage. Courage came later, if it came at all. What shifted was simpler and colder: the understanding that staying invisible was never an option. It had only ever been a delay. The men who answer to no one had decided I was worth an engine failure on takeoff, and you cannot out-quiet a decision like that. You can only decide what you're going to be when they come looking. Cargo, or pilot.

"Chelo," I said. "How long to fix her?"

He snorted. "She's not a *her* today, she's a pile of *it*. Airframe's bent, prop's done, gear's a memory. Engine might be salvageable if the grit didn't score the cylinders—I won't know until I open her up." He wiped his hands on a rag that was itself mostly grease. "Two weeks. Three, if parts have to come from the capital, and parts will have to come from the capital, because everything does."

Three weeks. Winter doesn't wait three weeks. The first snows in the high country come early some years, and everyone from Tomás the guide to the oldest nun at the mission had been saying this was going to be one of those years. Three weeks was the difference between a pass being a pass and a pass being a grave.

And somewhere up in that high country, past the cairns nobody had explained to me yet, past the shrine I couldn't have named, there was a folded piece of paper in my map case that I suddenly wanted very much to read.

* * *

We drank that night, as promised, at Chelo's kitchen table, an aguardiente so harsh it qualified as structural engineering. His wife, Rosa, fed me soup and told me I looked terrible, which from Rosa is a term of endearment. Chelo said little. He was a man who processed things slowly and completely, like a good engine, and around the second glass he finally spoke.

"When I was young," he said, "before you were born, there were men who came through the valley buying silence. Not with money—with fear. Cheaper, and it kept." He turned his glass. "My father fixed machines for the mine at Quebrada Honda, before the seizures. Afterward he fixed tractors and said nothing for thirty years. I used to think he was a coward." He looked up. "Now I think he was waiting."

"For what?"

"For someone to stop waiting." He drank. "You crashed today because somebody decided you mattered. Congratulations. It's the worst compliment in the world."

I laughed—actually laughed, which surprised both of us—and then it turned into something else halfway out, and Rosa put her hand on my shoulder, the bruised one, gently, and I sat there in a warm kitchen full of soup smells and let myself be furious. Not the hot flash of the riverbar. A slow fury, banked like a coal, the kind that keeps. I thought of Elena Vasco pressing that paper into my map case with snow coming down behind me, and the look on her face—not fear, exactly. Urgency. The look of a woman handing off something heavy because she trusts the next pair of hands.

They had tried to kill me for carrying it. Whatever it was, it weighed more than paper.

Rosa went to bed. Chelo and I sat with the lamp between us, and finally I took the map case off my belt—it had been there all day, riding my hip through the crash and the walk and the soup, and I realized I'd been touching it the way you touch a bruise—and unfolded Elena's paper on the table.

It was old. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, soft at the folds, and the ink had faded to brown, but the writing was formal, notarial, the kind of script that was designed to survive arguments. A title of some kind. A name I recognized with a jolt that went through me like the impact of the gravel: *Quintana.* My grandfather's name, spelled out in a dead official's careful hand, attached to a parcel of land above the Río Blanco, with boundaries described in terms of peaks and river bends and a mine called— the word was smudged, but the shape of it was there.

A deed. Or a piece of one. Torn, the edge ragged, as if it had been ripped from a larger document in a hurry, or hidden in pieces so no single theft could destroy them all.

Chelo leaned over and read it upside down, which mechanics can do. He was quiet a long moment. Then he said, "Your grandfather's claim. The one they took."

"The one they took," I said.

"And the survey team found this." He tapped the paper. "Up in the high valley. And now their camp is empty, and someone put grit in your fuel line, and men are asking questions in the capital about a pilot who landed where she shouldn't have." He sat back. "Petra. You are not invisible. You may never have been."

"No," I said. "I'm starting to think I was never invisible. I was just unread." I folded the deed along its old creases, carefully, and put it back in the map case, and buckled the flap. "That changes. Starting tomorrow."

"Tomorrow you're grounded. Three weeks, minimum."

"Tomorrow I start walking."

Chelo looked at me for a long time across the lamp. Outside, the wind had come up from the south, smelling of snow that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it. Somewhere up in that whiteness, a woman named Elena Vasco was either dead or waiting, and a man with rimless glasses was either hunting me or reporting to whoever was, and an old mine called something-I-couldn't-read sat under the rock like a debt thirty years overdue.

"You'll need a guide," Chelo said at last, in the tone of a man who has already lost an argument with himself. "And I know exactly which old fool to send you to."
