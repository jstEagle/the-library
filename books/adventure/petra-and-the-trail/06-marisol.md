## 6. Marisol

My mother taught me three things before she left: how to read a topographic map upside down, how to argue with a bureaucrat until he wishes he'd never been born, and how to pack a bag in under four minutes. She did not teach me what to do when all three skills turn out to be useless, which is roughly the situation I found myself in on the morning I stepped off the mail truck in San Rafael de los Andes with a duffel bag, a forged letter of introduction, and a photograph of a woman who had stopped answering my letters ten days ago.

The town smelled like wet adobe and diesel, the way mountain towns do when the rains come early and nobody has money for drainage. I asked at the cantina, the church, and the police post — in that order, because my mother also taught me that authority is best approached last, after you've already collected your version of events.

The cantina gave me nothing but a landlord's shrug. The priest gave me sympathy, which is worth less than nothing because it comes with obligations. The police post gave me a sergeant who looked at my photograph of Elena Vasco for a long time and then asked me, very gently, whether I was sure she hadn't simply decided to start a new life somewhere without a twenty-two-year-old daughter to complicate it.

I want you to understand that I did not hit him. I have grown as a person.

"She's a government surveyor," I said instead. "She has a contract. She has a team. She has a daughter who reads her mail."

"Teams go quiet sometimes," the sergeant said. "Radio batteries die. Weather closes passes." He slid the photograph back across the desk like he was returning something fragile. "You should talk to the pilot."

"What pilot?"

"The one who flies up there. Quintana. She was the last one to see them, they say." He said *they say* the way people say it when *they* is actually just one man in an office who told him to say it. "Though between us, señorita, I wouldn't get my hopes up. She's been grounded. Engine trouble."

*Grounded.* The word sat wrong in my stomach, though I couldn't have told you why yet. A bush pilot with engine trouble is not news; it's Tuesday. But the way he said it — with that small, practiced finality, like a lid going on a pot — made me think of every other sentence in this country that ends a conversation before it starts.

So: the pilot. Grounded, possibly sabotaged, definitely the last person to see my mother alive. I picked up my duffel.

*

Finding Petra Quintana took less time than finding a bathroom in the capital, which tells you something about how many secrets a town of four hundred streets can hold. Everyone knew where she lived — the house at the end of the lane with the windsock on a pole, the yard full of engine parts arranged with the tenderness most people reserve for gardens. What nobody wanted to do was introduce us.

I knocked anyway. That's the thing about doors: they only work if somebody's stubborn enough to use them.

She opened it on the second knock, and I'll be honest, my first thought was that the sergeant had sent me to a graveyard by mistake. Petra Quintana was maybe thirty, with a bruise going yellow-green along her jaw and a splint on two fingers, standing in her doorway like a woman who had recently lost an argument with gravity and was still deciding whether to file a complaint.

"You're the pilot," I said.

"And you're selling something." Her voice was dry as old rope. "I don't buy anything."

"I'm not selling anything. I'm looking for my mother. Elena Vasco."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Come inside before the whole street writes a play about it."

The house inside was small and ferociously organized. Charts rolled and labeled. Tools hung in silhouettes painted on a board, so any missing tool announced itself by its absence. On the table, a map case lay open, and I confess I looked at it before she invited me to sit down, because I am my mother's daughter and manners are for people whose mothers answer their letters.

"That's government property," Petra said, not moving to close it.

"So is my mother."

That landed. I watched it land. She pulled out a chair for me with her good hand, and we sat across from each other like two people playing cards for stakes neither would name yet.

"Tell me about her," she said. "Start anywhere."

So I started anywhere. I told her my mother had been gone eleven weeks on a survey contract — mineral assessment, watershed mapping, the usual bureaucratic euphemisms for men in offices wanting to know what's under mountains they don't intend to share with anyone. I told her the letters came weekly, then every two weeks, then stopped entirely, and that the last letter had been strange. Not frightened. Strange. My mother didn't frighten, but she economized, and the last letter was written like a woman counting words.

"She mentioned a supply pilot," I finished. "Said if anything happened to her, I should find the pilot. She didn't say why. She said you'd know."

Petra was quiet long enough that the windsock outside creaked twice against its pole.

"Did she say anything else? Anything at all?"

"She said—" I stopped. This was the part I'd been turning over for nine days, the part I'd decided to show no one, and here I was, showing it to a stranger with a bruised face because grief makes you stupid and hope makes you worse. "She said, *keep the paper dry.*"

Petra stood up so fast her chair skidded.

*

Here is what I learned in the next hour, sitting at that table while Petra Quintana laid things out with the precision of a woman who pre-flights her own life: ten days ago she had flown an unscheduled drop to the survey camp in a valley called Quebrada Honda, and my mother had pressed a folded paper into her map case with instructions that were not instructions — *for later,* nothing more. Ten days later, Petra returned on schedule and found the camp intact. Tents pitched. Stove warm. Gear stacked in neat rows, the way gear gets stacked when someone plans to come back for it.

And no people. No tracks out. Nothing.

"They didn't leave," Petra said. "People leaving leave signs. Broken grass. A dropped mitten. There was nothing. It was like the mountain opened its hand and took them."

"And the paper?"

She hesitated. Then she reached into the map case and unfolded it on the table between us, smoothing it flat with her splinted fingers, and I felt the air in the room change temperature.

It was a fragment. Heavy paper, foxed with age, torn raggedly along one edge as if ripped from a larger document in haste or anger or both. Handwritten in faded iron-gall ink, with a wax seal impression stamped into the corner — broken, but legible enough. And names. Columns of names, forty of them, each followed by measurements and boundary descriptions in a legal hand.

I had brought mine out before she finished speaking. Of course I had. I'd carried it from the capital sewn into the lining of my coat, because my mother raised me paranoid and then vanished, which is the kind of parenting that vindicates itself.

Two fragments. Same paper stock. Same ink. Same seal — hers from the upper left corner of whatever document it had been, mine from the lower right. And where hers ended mid-sentence, mine began, the torn edges fitting together across the table like a wound being closed.

"*...parcel designated La Mina de la Virgen, together with all workings, adits, and water rights appurtenant thereto, granted in perpetuity to the undersigned families of the Cordillera del Silencio...*" I read it aloud because reading aloud makes things true. "*In perpetuity.*"

"Do you know what this is?" Petra asked. Her voice had gone strange.

"It's a land grant. An old one. Pre-revolutionary, maybe older." I traced the columns of names. "These are family grants. Forty families held this valley — the mine and everything around it — before the state reassessed the titles. Before the seizures."

"My grandfather worked a mine up there." Petra said it flatly, the way you say a thing you've spent your whole life not saying. "La Mina de la Virgen. He worked it with his brothers until I was six years old. Then men came with papers, and the papers said the claim belonged to the state, and the papers were signed by officials, and my grandfather could read but he couldn't fight, and there's a difference." She looked down at the joined fragments. "He died still paying off a debt for equipment the state confiscated. We still have the debt note. My mother keeps it in a tin with the funeral photographs."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Outside, the rain started again, soft and businesslike, drumming on the tin roof.

"Your mother knew," I said slowly. "She found something up there. Something about the mine. And she gave you half of it because—"

"Because she wanted it somewhere the survey team couldn't lose it." Petra's jaw tightened, and the bruise went white at the edge. "Because she already knew they might disappear."

"Why would knowing about an old mine make people disappear?"

"Because it's not about the mine." Petra stood and paced to the window, and I watched her decide how much truth to spend on a stranger. "It's about who owned it. Because if those grants were never legally extinguished — if they were just *taken*, with forged paperwork — then everything built on that theft since is standing on nothing. Every lease. Every concession. Every company that ever paid the state for rights to that valley paid thieves." She turned back to me. "And the people who profited can't afford for anyone to find the original documents."

"The original documents." I looked down at our two fragments, torn apart on purpose, scattered on purpose — hidden, but not destroyed. "Someone tore these up and hid the pieces. You don't destroy evidence you want destroyed. You tear it up and hide it if you're planning to need it later."

"Or if you couldn't bring yourself to burn it," Petra said quietly.

We sat with that. Two pieces of a document that some hand, thirty years ago or more, had refused to kill. I thought about my mother pressing her piece into a stranger's map case with two fingers, quick, like passing contraband. *For later.* She had known. Not suspected — known. And she had trusted a bush pilot she barely knew, which meant she had watched Petra long enough to know exactly what she was trusting.

"She chose you," I said.

"Don't make it sentimental."

"It's not sentimental. It's tactical. She evaluated the available assets and selected you." I gathered the fragments, fitted them together once more, then separated them and handed hers back. "Which means I'm choosing you too. Whatever you're doing next, I'm in it."

"You don't know what I'm doing next."

"You don't either. But you're doing it, and you have a plane, and I have a reason." I put my mother's photograph on the table between us, face up. Elena Vasco, squinting into sun, mid-laugh, caught by a camera she hadn't posed for because she never posed. "Eleven weeks ago she walked into that valley. Ten days ago she vanished from it. Somebody in this country knows why, and it isn't the sergeant at the police post, and it isn't the nice priest, and it isn't the minister's office that sent me a form letter saying inquiries were ongoing. So it's you and me, Pilot, until somebody better comes along."

Petra looked at the photograph for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its dryness, or rather the dryness had cracked and something warmer showed through.

"There's a man in the next valley," she said. "Old guide. Tomás Ferrer. He carried ore out of that mine when it ran, before the seizures. If anyone knows where the rest of that document went — where the *originals* went — it's him." She paused. "Fair warning. He doesn't like questions."

"Neither do I," I said. "But I ask them anyway."

Almost, almost, she smiled. Then she glanced past me toward the window, toward the road, and the smile died before it finished being born.

"But first," she said, "we're going to want to be somewhere else. Because that car has passed this house twice since you arrived, and it doesn't belong to anyone in this town."

I looked. A gray sedan, government-plain, idling at the end of the lane in the rain with its wipers going and nobody getting out.

My mother's voice, in the last strange letter, came back to me: *keep the paper dry.*

I buttoned my coat over mine, and we went out the back.
