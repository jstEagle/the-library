## 7. Tomás Remembers

The girl knocked on my door the way young people knock on everything—like the world owed them an answer and they'd come to collect. Three sharp raps, then a pause, then three more, as if she'd read somewhere that persistence was a virtue and intended to exercise it until it was.

I knew who she was before I opened it. A village this size has no secrets, only delays, and word of Petra Quintana's arrival had reached me before her boots did. The pilot herself I'd seen once from a distance, years ago, at her father's funeral—a thin girl in a black dress too big for her, standing very straight, not crying. Now she stood on my step with that same straightness, older and weathered by altitude, and beside her a young woman with Elena Vasco's jaw and a look in her eyes like a lit fuse.

"Tomás Arriaga?" the pilot said.

"Depends who's asking."

"Petra Quintana. This is Marisol Vasco. We need to talk about the mine."

I looked at them for a long moment. Forty years of carrying loads over passes had taught me to weigh things by feel—the weight of a pack, the weight of a silence, the weight of a question you don't want asked. These two carried something heavy between them. I could feel it from where I stood.

"There is no mine," I said.

"There's a shrine," Marisol said.

And there it was. Thirty-five years I'd kept that door shut in my head, and this girl with her mother's jaw walked up and put her hand on the latch without even knowing it existed.

"You'd better come in," I said, because the alternative was standing in the street having this conversation, and some conversations deserve walls around them.

* * *

My house is small and smells of rope and dried meat and the particular dust that comes off old leather. I put water on to boil because a man needs something to do with his hands when his past walks through his door, and I set out cups I hadn't used since my wife died, which tells you something about how often I have visitors worth using cups for.

They sat across from me—Petra with the stillness of someone used to reading instruments, Marisol leaning forward like a dog on a scent—and for a while nobody spoke. The kettle worked itself up to a whistle and I took it off before it could get loud enough to say anything embarrassing.

"The survey team went missing ten days ago," Petra said finally. "Elena Vasco among them. Before they vanished, she gave Petra a piece of paper." She nodded at Marisol, who produced a folded sheet, soft with handling, and smoothed it flat on my table.

I didn't want to look at it. That's the honest truth. My eyes went to it anyway, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

It was a deed fragment. Surveyor's ink, official border, but the hand that had written the names—that was older. I knew the hand. I'd seen it on documents I helped carry up a mountain when I was nineteen years old and stupid with loyalty, documents wrapped in oilcloth and hidden behind a stone in a shrine that no one living was supposed to remember.

"Where did you get this?" I asked, though I knew.

"Elena gave it to Petra at the survey camp," Marisol said. "Ten days ago. Then the camp emptied itself."

"Gear stacked. Stove warm. No tracks," Petra added. "In or out."

I turned my cup in my hands. Outside, a mule complained about something in the general direction of nothing, and I envied it.

"I carried ore from that mine," I said. "When I was young. Before your time, before either of your times. Mule trains, down to the valley road, forty kilos a trip. It paid better than anything else in these mountains, and everyone said the mine was legitimate, and everyone believed it because believing was easier than checking." I looked up. "The claims weren't legitimate. They were taken. Forged seizures, signed by men in the capital, witnessed by men who were paid to witness. Forty families lost their ground in under two years. Some of those families are still here. Most scattered."

Marisol had gone very still. "Whose signatures?"

"Aurelio Sanch," I said. "Magistrate. He declared the deeds invalid on grounds of improper registration—paperwork errors, he called them, technicalities. Then the deeds themselves disappeared from the registry. Poof. As if the land had never belonged to anyone." I drank my tea, which had gone bitter. "The families appealed. Appeals take money. Money takes land. You see the arithmetic."

"And the original grants?" Petra asked quietly. "The ones before the seizures?"

I set the cup down. There it was. The question I'd been waiting thirty-five years to be asked and dreading every day of the wait.

"When it became clear what Sanch was doing—when the first families got their eviction notices—some of us understood the originals were the only proof left. The registry copies would vanish; we knew they would. So a few men carried the true grants up into the high country and hid them. Above the tree line. In a shrine the old people used, back before the church decided the mountains belonged to God instead of the other way around." I met their eyes. "I was one of the carriers. Nineteen years old. Strong back, weak judgment."

"Where?" Marisol said. Just that. One word, worn smooth by repetition.

"You understand what you're asking. If I take you there, I'm not taking you to a place. I'm taking you to thirty-five years of—" I stopped. Found another word. "Of debt."

"We know about debt," Petra said, and something in her voice made me look at her properly. "Both of us."

* * *

They stayed the night—I had two spare blankets and a floor that had hosted worse—and I lay awake listening to the wind work at the eaves and doing arithmetic of my own.

Here is what I did not tell them, sitting at my table with my dead wife's cups: that Aurelio Sanch married my sister. That I ate at his table. That when the seizures began, I told myself my sister's husband was a man of law and law must be followed, right up until the day I watched Doña Ines Quintana—Petra's grandmother, though I doubt the girl knows it—stand in the registry office holding a document older than the republic and hear Sanch explain, gently, reasonably, that paper means nothing without proper registration, and proper registration meant his stamp, and his stamp cost what it cost.

That I carried ore for him afterward. That I told myself a man feeds his family first and philosophizes later. That when the chance came to carry the deeds up the mountain instead—to do one thing with my strong back that wasn't theft—I grabbed it like a drowning man grabs driftwood, and I've been telling myself ever since that one good load balances forty bad ones.

Arithmetic again. It doesn't work. I've had thirty-five years to check the sums.

Toward morning I heard the girl—Marisol—turn over in her sleep and say her mother's name once, softly, the way you say a thing you're afraid will stop being true if you say it loud. And I thought: this child's mother may be alive on that mountain, and the only map to whatever happened to her runs through a shrine I've spent half a life pretending I couldn't find again.

Some debts you pay in coin. Some you pay in kilometers.

* * *

At dawn I had bread and tea on the table when they woke, and while they ate I laid out my old survey maps—the real ones, hand-drawn, predating anything the government printed—on the cleared end of the table.

"The shrine sits above the tree line, in a cirque below the western ridge of Cerro Blanco," I said, tapping the paper. "Three days' walk in fair weather. Five in foul. After that, the pass beyond it is the only way down the far side, and the far side is where your survey team went missing—or where whoever moved them took them."

"You'll guide us," Petra said. Not a question. She had her father's way of stating things.

"I didn't say that."

"You laid out the maps."

I looked at the maps. They looked back at me, the way old debts do.

"I'll tell you why I shouldn't," I said. "I'm sixty-eight years old. My knees report directly to the weather and ignore my instructions entirely. Anyone following me becomes a target of whatever took that survey team, and I can't protect anyone, including myself. And the last time I climbed toward that shrine, I was carrying something for a man I should have refused, and I've regretted the walking ever since."

Marisol set down her bread. "Then don't come for us," she said. "Come for yourself."

There it is. Sixty-eight years old, and a girl half my age hands me the one sentence I've been avoiding since my hair went grey. Come for yourself. As if guilt were a mule that needed walking, and I the only one who knew the trail.

I gathered the maps into a roll and tied them with cord, slower than the task required.

"Two conditions," I said. "First: we leave within the hour, ahead of any weather and ahead of anyone asking questions. Second:"—and here I paused, because the second condition was for me more than them—"nobody asks me why I'm going. When you figure it out, keep it to yourselves."

Petra studied me the way she probably studies weather fronts. "Fair," she said.

We were on the trail by full light, three shadows moving north through the frost, and I won't pretend my heart was light. But I'll tell you what I felt, walking out of the village with thirty-five years finally strapped to my back where I could see it: lighter than I'd felt since the day I hid them.

Guilt is a strange cargo. Carried alone, it crushes. Shared out on the trail, it's just weight—and weight, at least, is honest.

By noon we'd reached the tree line's ragged edge, and that was where Petra, scanning the ridgeline with a pilot's eyes, stopped and caught my arm.

"Tomás," she said quietly. "How long since anyone burned wood up here?"

I followed her gaze to a thread of smoke rising thin and gray against the far slope, from a place where no fire had any business burning.

"Too long," I said.
