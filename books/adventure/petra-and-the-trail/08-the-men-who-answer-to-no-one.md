## 8. The Men Who Answer to No One

The mission airstrip at San Rafael de Arriba was the kind of place that existed because nobody had ever bothered to argue it out of existence. A grass strip mowed by sheep, a chapel with a bell that rang flat, and Father Anselmo, who kept fuel in drums behind the vestry and gossip in his head like a man hoarding grain against a famine. I'd been landing there since I was nineteen years old. He'd never once asked me what I carried, which in my experience was the highest form of hospitality the Cordillera offered.

We came in low over the river at midmorning, three days out from Tomás's cabin, with the plane light and my nerves lighter than they had any right to be. Marisol rode up front beside me—she'd earned the seat by not complaining once during two days of walking—and Tomás sat in back with the deed fragment wrapped in oilcloth inside his jacket, next to his heart, like a saint's medal he didn't quite believe in.

"Smoke," Marisol said.

I saw it before she finished the word. Not chimney smoke—the thin, domestic gray of a cookfire—but a black, greasy column standing off the tree line south of the strip, leaning east with the wind.

"That's not the village," I said, though my hands were already moving, throttle back, flaps thinking about it. "That's the drums."

I put the plane down on the near end of the strip anyway, because there are times when the smart thing and the necessary thing shake hands and agree to disagree, and because if Father Anselmo was alive, he'd be where the fire wasn't, and I needed to know which side of that sentence we were standing on.

He was alive. He came out of the chapel at a half-run when our wheels touched, cassock tucked into his belt like a man who'd stopped caring about dignity somewhere around dawn, and he grabbed my arm through the window hard enough to leave marks.

"Petrina. Petrina, you cannot stop here."

"I stopped."

"Yes." He looked at the sky as if it might apologize for him. "Yes, you did."

Behind him, the fuel drums were still burning—a controlled sort of blaze, set well clear of the trees, the way you burn something when you want it destroyed but not the whole valley with it. Two of the six were gone entirely. The others had been dragged apart and lit individually. That detail lodged in me like a splinter: whoever did this wasn't careless. Careless men burn down valleys.

"Who?" I asked.

"Four riders. Armed—not hunting rifles, the short kind, the kind you don't carry unless you mean to use it. They came yesterday evening." Father Anselmo crossed himself, then seemed embarrassed about it. "They asked after a pilot. The pilot who landed at the empty camp."

Marisol made a sound beside me, small and involuntary, and I felt it land in my own chest too. *The empty camp.* So they knew. They knew the survey team was gone, knew I'd been the last one in, and they'd traced the thread backward through every airstrip in the range until it led here—to an old priest and six drums of avgas.

"What did you tell them?"

"The truth." He met my eyes. "That many pilots land here. That I do not keep a ledger of faces."

"And?"

"And one of them smiled at me, Petrina, in a way I did not care for, and said that was a very convenient kind of memory, and then they burned the fuel anyway. As a—" he searched for the word, found it, disliked it— "*as a lesson.* About memory."

Tomás had climbed down and come around the wing, and now he stood looking at the smoke with an expression I couldn't read, or maybe could read too easily. "They're working outward from the camp," he said. "Valley by valley. It's how you hunt a man in mountains—you don't chase him, you close the doors." He looked at me. "There are four doors out of this country, and you've just landed in one of them."

"Thank you, Tomás. I always enjoy your weather reports."

He almost smiled. Almost. It was progress, of a kind.

Father Anselmo pulled me toward the chapel, talking fast and low. "You must go. Now, today, before they circle back—they went downriver, but they will come back, they told me so themselves, they wanted me to know they would be asking again. And Petra—" he gripped both my hands now, and his palms were shaking, this old man who had buried half the valley's dead and never once trembled at a graveside— "they asked about a girl. A young woman traveling with the pilot. And about an old guide. They described Tomás."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with altitude. "How did they describe him?"

"An old man who used to pack ore. Who knew the high trails." Anselmo's voice dropped further. "Petrina, that is not a description. That is a *file*. Somebody gave them that. Somebody who knows these mountains and knows its people, and is feeding them names."

I thought about the mechanic kneeling in the gravel beside my dead engine, holding up a fistful of grit. I thought about the stranger photographing my plane in the capital, casual as a tourist. The picture assembled itself without my permission: someone patient, someone inside, someone handing out pieces of us one at a time to men on horseback.

"All right," I said. "All right. We go. But not before—"

"Petrina." Marisol had come around the other wing, and her voice had that flatness I was learning to fear, the flatness she got when feeling had been compressed past the point where it could afford volume. She was looking at the burning drums. "That was your fuel. For getting out."

"It was."

"So how do we get out?"

I opened my mouth to say something competent and reassuring and discovered the cupboard was bare. Sixty liters in my tanks, maybe less after the crossing—that was enough to fly out, if I flew out *now*, alone, straight to the capital, and left the two of them standing in a valley with armed men circling it. Or it was enough to fly deeper in, higher, colder, toward a shrine above the tree line and whatever waited there, with no fuel waiting anywhere on the far side of the decision.

The arithmetic sat there between us, ugly and patient. It has a way of doing that, arithmetic. It doesn't raise its voice. It just waits for you to finish lying to yourself.

"We walk," Tomás said.

"We just walked three days," Marisol said.

"And we'll walk thirty more if that's what it costs." He said it without drama, the way you'd note that rain was coming. "The plane is done, girl. The moment those men learned a pilot was involved, every strip in the range became a trap with wings. Anything that flies is a thing they can find. Feet don't show up on anyone's list until they're already past you."

"He's right," I said, and hated the sentence even as I signed it. "Anselmo—can you hide the plane? Not fly it. Hide it. If they find it, they'll know we came through; if they find it *empty*, they'll know we're on foot, and on foot means the high country, and there's only one thing in the high country worth walking to."

The old priest considered this. Behind him the bell rope hung slack in the doorway, and I thought about all the funerals that bell had rung flat for, and decided I did not intend to provide it with work.

"There is the old drying shed above the falls," he said. "It has a roof that leaks and a floor that rotted in '89, but it has a roof. I'll say nothing. I have said nothing for forty years, Petrina; I am very good at it." Then, quieter: "Take food. Take the wool blankets from the sacristy—God forgives practical loans. And take this." He pressed something into my hand: a small tin medal, worn smooth, of some saint whose name had rubbed away along with most of his features. "My predecessor carried it across the pass in the year of the big snows. He said it was for finding the way when the way stops being visible. I have never needed it. Perhaps that was always what it was for—for whoever came after."

I closed my hand around it. It was warm from his pocket, and absurdly light, and I pocketed it next to Elena Vasco's folded deed fragment, so that my jacket now contained a stolen inheritance and a borrowed miracle, which struck me as roughly the correct inventory for the situation.

* * *

We were out of San Rafael within the hour, on foot, on the north trail, with blankets, hard bread, dried meat Anselmo pressed on us like he was paying a debt, and a water skin that leaked in a way Tomás pronounced "characterful." Behind us the smoke from the drums had thinned to a smudge, then to nothing, and I caught myself looking back at it the way you look back at a door you've just locked, checking.

The first hour I was afraid. I want to be honest about that, because later—when people started telling the story, and they did, everyone always wants the story—I noticed the versions where I strode into the wilderness fearless, jaw set, eyes bright, and I want it on record that the first hour I jumped at a woodpecker and my hands shook so badly I dropped the water skin twice.

But somewhere in the second hour, on the switchbacks above the river, something shifted. I've thought about what it was. Partly it was Tomás setting the pace—unhurried, relentless, a metronome made of gristle and stubbornness—and discovering that if I matched him, there was no room left in my body for trembling. Fear takes energy. Walking takes energy. You get to choose where the energy goes, and choosing is itself a kind of courage, or at least its rough draft.

Partly it was Marisol. She walked the way she did everything, with her chin up and her grief converted directly into forward motion, and around the third switchback she said, without turning around:

"They burned the fuel to teach him a lesson about memory."

"Yes."

"So they think memory is a thing you can burn." She said it almost conversationally, but her knuckles were white on her pack strap. "Then they haven't met my mother. Elena Vasco remembers everything. Phone numbers, coordinates, birthdays of people she's met once. When I was small I thought it was a trick." Her voice caught, steadied. "She's alive. People who remember everything don't die quietly in a valley. They'd leave a trail. She'd *make* them leave a trail."

And partly—it shames me a little to admit it, but honesty is the one luxury I've never managed to give up—it was the anger. Anger is a marvelous fuel. It burns dirty and it burns hot and it gets you up a mountain when bread and dried meat won't. Those men had burned sixty gallons of my fuel, frightened an old priest, put my name in the mouth of every rider in the range, and described my grandfather's trade to strangers with short rifles. Somewhere in the second hour the fear and the anger finished their argument, and anger won, and I discovered that fear sharpened into discipline looks, from the outside, exactly like a woman walking steadily uphill and saying very little.

Inside, I was making lists. That's what discipline turned out to be, for me: lists. Water sources Tomás named as we passed them. Altitudes. Which ridgelines gave cover and which exposed you to the sky. How much food remained and how many days it bought. Where the riders had last been seen and therefore where they weren't. Every fact filed, cross-referenced, kept. *Memory is a thing you can burn,* they'd said. Fine. Then I would be the thing they couldn't burn.

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow below the ridge, no fire, sharing blankets against a cold that arrived early and stayed late. Tomás took first watch without being asked, sitting against a boulder with his hat brim low, and I lay awake listening to the forest do its nighttime bookkeeping—wind, water, something small and busy in the litter—and thinking about doors.

Four doors out of the country, he'd said. The riders were closing them one at a time. Which meant somewhere behind us, men who answered to no one—not to the governor, probably not even to whoever paid them, because men like that answer finally only to the job—were riding circuits with our descriptions in their pockets. *A pilot. A young woman. An old ore-packers.* And somewhere ahead of us, above the tree line, a shrine full of paper that forty families had bled for, guarded for a generation by whoever had hidden it, hunted now by whoever wanted it ash.

And in between, us. Three people, one leaking water skin, a saint's medal of unknown efficacy, and a combined age that Tomás contributed more than half of.

"You're awake," Tomás said, without turning. His watch-voice, low. "Good. Then hear something."

I sat up. Across the hollow, Marisol slept with her back against her pack, breathing slow, and the moon had come up enough to silver the ridge line.

"When I packed ore out of that mine, I was seventeen," Tomás said. "Strongest summer of my life, and I spent it carrying other men's wealth down a trail my grandfather helped cut. I told myself it was just work. Work is honest. The ore isn't responsible for who owns it." He was quiet a moment. "That lie lasted me fifty years. Don't waste time building yours, Petra. Whatever you're telling yourself tonight about why you're doing this—about the money you owe, about staying out of things, about being just a pilot—set it down now. It'll be heavy enough later without pretending it's lighter than it is."

"I'm not telling myself anything."

"No," he agreed mildly. "You're making lists. I can hear it from here. Lists are fine. But a list isn't a reason." He adjusted his hat. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we climb above the trees, and after that, everything we do, the sky sees."

I lay back down. Above the black teeth of the ridge, the stars were out in their usual obscene quantities, indifferent and beautiful, and I found Elena Vasco's folded paper in the dark and held it without unfolding it, the way you hold a hand.

*For later,* she'd said. Well. Later had arrived, wearing boots, and it was climbing.

Somewhere down the valley behind us—or perhaps already on the flank of the ridge ahead, closing the third door—I heard it: a sound I might have taken for wind, except that wind doesn't repeat itself at intervals, and doesn't travel uphill against the current.

A whistle. Two notes, low, then silence. Then, fainter, answering from another direction, two notes more.

Tomás had gone very still against his boulder. In the moonlight I saw his hand move, slowly, and rest on Marisol's blanket, and squeeze once.

"They're not behind us," he breathed. "They're ahead. They've been ahead all day."

Above the tree line, in the direction of the shrine, the night whistled back a third time.
