## 9. Cairns

The tree line gave up on us the way everything up here gives up on you—gradually, then all at once. One hour we were ducking under branches heavy with last night's meltwater, and the next we were walking through a world of gray stone and gray sky with nothing between them but wind. The forest didn't end so much as surrender.

"Cheerful country," Marisol said behind me.

"It gets better," Tomás said, which was a lie, and the three of us all knew it was a lie, and somehow that made it easier to keep walking.

We had been climbing for two days since leaving the burned mission airstrip behind us—two days of Tomás navigating by means he refused to explain, Marisol asking questions he refused to answer, and me doing the arithmetic I'd been doing since the riverbar: how many days to the shrine, how many days of food left after that, how many days until the first snows that every almanac in the Cordillera said were still three weeks out and my own bones said were much closer than that. The numbers never came out comfortable. Numbers like these never do.

My ribs had stopped complaining about the crash and started negotiating instead—a dull, persistent ache that flared whenever I twisted wrong, which on this terrain was roughly every fourth step. I'd strapped them tight that morning without telling the others. There was no version of this story where telling them helped.

* * *

It was Marisol who saw the first one.

"Petra." She'd gone very still, the way she did when she was trying not to hope too loudly. "What is that?"

I followed her arm. Maybe two hundred meters upslope, half-swallowed by scree, stood a column of stones. Man-high, maybe a little less. Stacked—not piled, *stacked*, each stone chosen and seated on the one below it with the kind of patience I associated with cathedral masons and people who had nothing else in the world to do.

"A cairn," Tomás said.

"I know what a cairn is," I said. "I've seen a thousand cairns."

"You haven't seen these cairns."

He was right, and I hated how quickly I knew it. The trail cairns I knew—the ones shepherds built, the ones pilots used as unofficial waypoints when the fog came down—were sloppy things. Practical. A traveler in a hurry stacking rocks because rocks were what the mountain offered. They leaned. They slumped. They looked like what they were: an argument between a tired person and gravity, which gravity usually won.

This one didn't lean. This one had been *built*. The base stones were the size of my torso, sunk into the slope like foundation work. The courses above narrowed with a deliberateness that spoke of someone standing back, squinting, adding a stone here, taking one away there. Somebody had cared about this pile of rocks. Somebody had cared about it the way you care about a thing you expect other people to depend on with their lives.

"How old?" Marisol asked.

Tomás walked up to it and put his palm flat against the stones, the way you'd touch a wall to feel whether a house was lived in. Lichen had gotten into the seams—thin gray-green crusts, the slowest handwriting in the world.

"Older than me," he said. "Older than the seizures. Older than the mine, probably." He stepped back and looked along the line of the slope, and I watched his eyes do something they hadn't done in two days of walking: they *focused*. All this time he'd been leading us by some private compass, some memory he carried like a debt, and now the memory had put on a body. "There'll be another one. And another after that. Spaced so you can see the next from the last, even in weather. Even at night."

"Spaced by whom?"

"By people who walked this route before there was a route." He was already moving. "Come on. If the next one's where I think it is, we've been drifting west all morning."

* * *

Here is a thing about being a bush pilot that nobody tells you before they hand you the keys to an airplane: most of your job is not flying. Most of your job is *reading*. Reading weather that hasn't arrived yet. Reading a riverbar to know whether it will hold your wheels or eat them. Reading the face of the man buying your fuel to know whether today's price is today's price or today's price plus whatever he thinks your desperation weighs.

So I read. It's the only skill I have that translates cleanly to the ground, and I used it now.

The second cairn sat exactly where Tomás said it would, on a shoulder of rock where the slope briefly relented, and from its base you could see the third, a dark finger against the skyline two kilometers on. From the third, the fourth. Whoever raised these stones hadn't just known the way—they'd known the way the way I know approaches into short strips. Not as geography. As *procedure*. Left of the cairn, the ground fell away into a gully full of loose rock that would have broken an ankle or worse; right of it, a ramp of solid bedrock climbed at a grade even Marisol, carrying the tin box wrapped in her jacket, could manage without cursing. Every cairn stood at the mouth of the safe line, like a door held open.

"Somebody spent years learning this," I said. "Then spent more years marking it. You don't do that for a shortcut."

"No," Tomás said. "You do it for an escape."

Marisol and I both looked at him. He kept walking.

"The ore road comes up from the south," he said, after long enough that I'd almost accepted he wasn't going to answer. "Wagons, mules, later trucks. That road was for taking things *out*. Wealth goes down the easy path. Always has." He touched the third cairn as he passed it, lightly, the way you'd touch a shoulder. "But if you're coming *up*—if you're running, or hiding, or carrying something you can't let the road see—you come this way. And somebody who loved whoever came this way after him built stones so they wouldn't die of a turned ankle in the dark."

"And who was coming up?" Marisol asked quietly.

Tomás didn't answer, but his silence had a shape, and the shape was forty families.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of the fifth cairn, because it was the only windbreak for a kilometer in any direction and because I told myself the decision was practical and not superstitious. There is something about sleeping inside a structure built by dead hands that makes you check your boots for scorpions you know aren't there.

I took first watch, partly because my ribs made lying down a negotiation I wasn't ready to win, and partly because I wanted the dark to myself for a while. Above the tree line the night comes down clean. No insects humming, no leaves moving—just wind and stars and the enormous indifferent breathing of the range. In the plane I love this altitude. On foot, it loves me considerably less.

That was when I saw the smoke.

Not near. Not a campfire's worth. Far off to the north, past the saddle we'd have crossed tomorrow if tomorrow went well, a thread of gray rose against the last violet band of dusk and bent with the wind. One thread. Thin. The kind of smoke a single fire makes—the kind a careful person makes, small and hot and fed a little at a time.

Nobody lives above the tree line. Nobody camps up here in October without a reason that involves either desperation or pursuit, and I had recently become well acquainted with both.

I woke Tomás without a word, just a hand on his shoulder, and pointed. He looked for a long time. Longer than the smoke deserved, which meant he was looking at what it *meant*.

"One fire," he said finally. "One person, or a group keeping discipline." He glanced back the way we'd come, down the long green dark of the valley we'd climbed out of, where two days ago armed riders had burned a mission airstrip to ask a question about a pilot. "They're not following our track. Our track goes here." He tapped the ground. "Their fire is on the line of the cairns. Ahead of us."

"They know the route."

"Or they're reading it the same way we are." He was quiet a moment. "Or they built it."

I thought about that all through my watch, and most of Marisol's, staring at a sky full of cold fire while a thread of warm smoke bent and straightened and bent again on the horizon. Hope is a strange cargo. I've hauled medicine and mail and once, memorably, a wedding dress and a live rooster in the same load, and none of it ever shifted in turbulence the way hope shifts. All day the cairns had been handing it to us handful by handful—*someone knew this route, someone marked it, someone meant for people like us to survive it*—and now the same evidence pointed the other direction, and I couldn't make the stones mean only one thing. A trail built for fugitives can be read by hunters. A door held open doesn't ask who's coming through.

* * *

Morning brought wind and a sky the color of unpolished steel, and with it, at the seventh cairn, the proof.

Marisol found it because Marisol found everything—she had her mother's eye for the detail that doesn't fit, though neither of us said that out loud yet. She crouched at the base of the stones and made a sound low in her throat, and when I got there she was holding something up between finger and thumb with the care you'd give a live coal.

A boot print. In the fine volcanic grit that collects in the cairn's wind shadow, crisp-edged, unfaded by any rain—because there had been no rain, not up here, not for a week. A day old. Two at the outside.

And beside it, pressed deep, the print of a walking staff or a rifle butt, and beside *that*, smaller, lighter, the print of a second boot. Two people. Moving fast enough to drive their heels in. Heading north, toward the saddle, toward the smoke.

"Recent," I said, stating the obvious because sometimes the obvious needs saying out loud to become real.

"Very." Marisol turned the grit gently with a fingertip. "And look—they stopped here. Both of them. Facing the cairn." She pointed to a scatter of prints at the base, a little knot of disturbed gravel. "They stood and looked at it. For a while."

"Why would anyone stop to look at a rock pile?"

"Because they recognized it," Tomás said. He had come up behind us without making a sound, which at his age and with his knees should have been impossible, and he was looking at the cairn with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. "Because they knew what it was. Or—" and here his voice did something complicated, "—because they were checking whether it was still standing. Whether anyone had knocked it down."

"Who knocks down trail markers?"

"People who don't want anyone using the trail." He crouched, wincing, and studied the prints without touching them. "These are government-issue boots. The tread pattern—I issued this pattern myself, thirty years ago, to the survey crews working the boundary commission." He looked up at me. "Your surveyors wore this boot, Petra. I checked, that first night at the mission, when you showed me Elena Vasco's supply manifest. I didn't say anything because a boot print proves nothing except that someone owns boots."

"And now?"

"Now there are two sets, a day old, on a route that appears on no map, walking toward a fire that shouldn't exist, above a valley where six people vanished without leaving tracks." He stood, slowly, and the wind pushed at him like it was testing his balance and finding it acceptable. "Now I'm willing to call it evidence."

Marisol had gone white around the mouth. I knew the arithmetic she was doing because I was doing it too: six missing surveyors, two sets of prints, one thin thread of smoke. The math had too many possible answers and every one of them hurt.

"It could be the men who took them," she said. "Moving prisoners."

"It could be survivors," I said. "Moving themselves."

"It could be a trap," Tomás said, with the gentleness of a man who has decided you deserve the truth even when the truth is a hammer. "Both of those and a trap. Up here they're rarely mutually exclusive."

* * *

We argued about it for an hour, which in expedition time is a constitutional crisis. Marisol wanted to push hard for the saddle and be at the smoke by midday. Tomás wanted caution—wide spacing, water stops, the discipline of people who expected the mountain to kill them for one careless hour. I wanted both things at once, which is the pilot's condition generally: you want to get there and you want to arrive.

In the end we split the difference, which is to say we did what Tomás said, faster.

The cairns kept coming. Eight, nine, ten—and at ten, something new. The tenth cairn stood on a knife-edge ridge with the whole northern basin spread below it, and someone, recently, had built onto it. Fresh stones, unlichenied, wedged into the old stack to raise it by a course and a half. And on the newest stone, placed flat on top like a paperweight on a ledger, a small triangle of flat slate.

Not natural. Slate doesn't walk uphill and arrange itself.

Marisol reached for it and Tomás caught her wrist—not hard, just fast. He picked the slate up himself, turned it over, and held it out to us without a word.

Scratched into the underside, shallow, hurried, with something harder than the stone itself, was a mark. A V with a line through it. And beneath the mark, in letters small enough that I had to hold the slate close and tilt it against the light:

**4 ALIVE — CAVE — FOLLOW CAIRNS — TRUST NO RIDERS**

Four words of instruction and four words of hope, cut into a rock by someone with numb fingers and no time, and left under a stone that had been standing guard over this route since before anyone alive was born.

Marisol made a sound I'll hear for the rest of my life—half laugh, half sob, strangled in the middle by the part of her that remembered the fall, the cover-up, the rescue that never came. Four alive. Out of six. Her mother might be one of the four, or might not, and the slate didn't say, and the mountain didn't say, and the wind certainly didn't.

"Vasco," Tomás said quietly. "The V. It's her mark—it's on the manifests, the corner of every field map. Surveyors sign their grids." He handed the slate to Marisol, whose hands were shaking too badly to hold it steady, so I held it with her, our four hands together around a scrap of stone thinner than a promise.

"She was here," Marisol whispered. "Days ago. She climbed to this ridge and she left a message for—" Her voice caught. "For whoever came. She trusted someone would come."

"Someone built the cairns so people could come," I said. "Seventy years ago, a hundred, who knows. And she trusted them anyway." I looked north, toward the saddle, toward the thread of smoke that this morning had been a threat and was now, impossibly, a lighthouse. "Trust no riders. Fine. We're not riders. We're a grounded pilot, a stubborn daughter, and a guide with a guilty conscience, and we are going to follow these stones to the end of the line."

Below us, the basin opened northward, and the eleventh cairn stood on its far rim, patient as everything else up here, pointing the way.

And beside it—small, distant, unmistakable in the clean high air—a figure rose from behind the stones, stood watching us for a long moment across two kilometers of empty rock, and then, deliberately, raised one arm and beckoned.
