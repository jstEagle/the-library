## 10. The Shrine

My mother taught me to read maps before she taught me to read words. She'd spread them on the kitchen table in the capital, her finger tracing the blue veins of rivers, and say, *The land tells you where it wants you to go, Marisol. Your job is to listen without arguing.* I was seven. I thought she meant it as a game.

She meant it the way priests mean things.

Now I stood at the tree line with my hands jammed in my pockets, looking up at a slope of grey scree that went on until it became sky, and I understood that everything in my life had been an argument with this mountain. The mountain had won every round so far. It had taken my mother's letters—shorter each year, then none at all. It had taken her voice off the radio, mid-sentence, ten days ago. And now it wanted me to climb it, because a seventy-year-old man with bad knees said there was something buried near the top worth dying for.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," I said.

Tomás didn't turn around. He was adjusting the straps of his pack with the patience of a man who had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. "Because the paper is real," he said. "And real paper is heavier than any of us."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have left." He hoisted the pack. "The shrine is two hours above us if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't. We should be arguing while we walk."

Petra came up beside me, breathing hard from the last stretch of forest, her face still carrying the yellow-green shadow of the bruises from the crash. She looked like a woman who had been folded up wrong and never quite unfolded. But her eyes were doing that thing they did—scanning, measuring, filing away exits.

"The weather's going to break by noon," she said. "After that we're climbing in cloud. So we go now, or we go never."

"You sound very sure for someone whose plane is currently a sculpture on a riverbar."

"I'm sure about clouds." She almost smiled. "Clouds are honest. They tell you exactly what they're going to do to you."

We went.

* * *

The forest gave way the way forests do—not all at once but in stages, trees shrinking and twisting, then giving up entirely, until we were walking through a world of stone and lichen and wind. The wind up here had a voice. Not a howl—that would have been dramatic, cinematic, bearable. It was worse than a howl. It was a mutter, constant, like an old man reciting grievances he'd been nursing for decades. My grandfather's wind. My mother's wind. Maybe mine, eventually, if I lived long enough to have grievances worth reciting.

Tomás set a pace that was insulting to all three of us—young, half-young, and old—and refused to apologize for it. He walked the way he talked: in short sentences, uphill, without looking back to see if anyone followed. Twice Petra had to call a halt just to let me catch my breath, and both times Tomás stood facing up-slope, hands on hips, watching the ridge line like a man waiting for a bus he didn't trust.

At the second halt, Petra crouched next to me and passed me the water bottle.

"How are you doing?" she asked. The question was casual. Her eyes weren't.

"My legs are fine."

"I didn't ask about your legs."

I took a drink instead of answering. Above us, maybe four hundred meters, a shape broke the skyline—a low wall of fitted stones, half-swallowed by lichen, with a gap in the middle like a missing tooth.

"There," Tomás said, and for the first time since we'd left the riverbar, his voice cracked open a little. Just a hairline fracture. But I heard it.

"What is it?" I asked. "Really?"

"A chapel," he said. "Or what's left of one. The families built it when the grants were new. Before my time. People used to bring offerings up here before the planting season. Candles. Corn. Sometimes a chicken, if the year had been good." He paused. "Sometimes a child's shoe, if the year had been bad."

"And the documents?"

He looked at me then, and his face did something complicated—the look of a man opening a door he'd nailed shut years ago. "When the seizures started, my father carried the grant papers up here in a flour sack. Forty families' deeds. He sealed them in a tin box and put them under the altar stone, and he told God that if the law wouldn't protect them, the mountain could. Then he came down and signed his name to the transfer papers anyway, because they threatened his family, and he spent the rest of his life not talking about either thing." Tomás picked up his pack. "I was eleven years old. I helped him carry the sack."

Neither Petra nor I said anything. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't have been either a lie or a cruelty.

We climbed.

* * *

The shrine was smaller than it had looked from below, which is the way of most holy places. A ring of stones, waist-high, enclosing a space barely big enough for three people to stand in. The altar was a flat slab resting on two uprights, and the whole structure leaned eastward, the way old men lean into the wind. Someone had built a small cairn beside it—recently, by the look of the stones, unweathered—and my stomach did something unpleasant when I saw it.

"Somebody's been here," Petra said quietly. She'd seen it too. Of course she had.

"Recent," Tomás agreed. His jaw had gone tight. "But not recent enough. Look at the lichen starting on the underside. Two years, maybe three. Not weeks."

"Who builds cairns at shrines?"

"Pilgrims," Tomás said. "Or people saying thank you. Or people saying sorry." He didn't elaborate, and I got the distinct feeling he could have.

Petra circled the structure once, reading it the way she read weather. Then she stopped at the altar slab and put her palm flat against the stone. "Cold," she reported. "Dry underneath, though. The overhang protected it." She looked at Tomás. "How do we know which stone?"

"We don't lift anything," Tomás said. "We ask first."

I thought he was joking. He was not joking. He knelt down in front of the altar, knees cracking like green wood, and pressed his forehead briefly against the cold rock. His lips moved. Whatever he said, he said it fast, like a man paying off a debt in installments.

Then he stood, turned to face downslope, and addressed the empty air in a carrying voice:

"We come for the papers of the forty families. We come to carry them down and give them back their names. If anyone objects—" he paused, and something in his voice made the hairs on my arms stand up, "—now is the time."

The wind muttered. Nothing else did.

"All right," Tomás said, sounding like himself again. "Petra. The slab. Lift from the east side—it's lighter there. Marisol, be ready to reach under. And whatever you find, don't drop it."

The slab weighed about what a dead man weighs. We got our fingers under the eastern edge and lifted together, and the stone came up with a grinding reluctance, like it had been asked to move once before in its long life and hadn't enjoyed it. Underneath: dark earth, packed hard, and the ghost of a cavity where something box-shaped had rested for fifty years.

Empty.

For one full second nobody moved. Then Petra said, very calmly, "It's fine. It's fine. Look at the earth—there's a depression. Something was buried deeper. Keep lifting."

She was right. Beneath the first cavity, half-hidden by a mat of root-fiber, there was a second hollow, lower, better concealed. My father—no. My mother's colleagues, the survey team, had found nothing here; they'd never gotten this far. This hiding place had been dug by someone who understood that the first hiding place is always found, and the second is only found by people who already know it exists.

Tomás reached in to the shoulder. His arm disappeared into the earth up past the elbow. His face went through several expressions in rapid succession—concentration, effort, doubt, and then a kind of stunned blankness—and then he drew out a tin box the size of a bread loaf, rusted orange along its seams, heavy as sin.

Nobody spoke. The wind muttered its grievances. Somewhere below us, a condor turned a slow circle, riding the thermals with the patience of something that knew it would outlive everyone present.

Tomás set the box on the altar slab. His hands were shaking. Not much. But visibly.

"Fifty-two years," he said. "It's been in the ground fifty-two years."

"Open it," I said. My own voice sounded strange to me. Thin. Like it belonged to somebody standing further away.

He worked the latch. It fought him, rusted half-shut, and then gave with a crack that echoed off the rocks above. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were papers.

Not fragments. Not scraps. Complete documents, dozens of them, tied in bundles with cord that crumbled when touched. Land grants bearing dates older than the republic itself in some cases. Signatures. Seals. Names—my God, the names. I untied the first bundle with fingers that wouldn't behave, and read aloud, because silence felt unbearable:

"Quintana. Vasco. Arriaga. Chumbe. Sanch—" I stopped.

"Sanch," Petra repeated.

I held the page closer. The name was written in a firm hand, the ink browned with age but perfectly legible. Aurelio Sanch. Holder of grazing rights, upper valley, parcel fourteen. And beneath his name, in the same hand, a different document altogether—a ledger page, columns of figures, payments recorded across years, and at the bottom of the column, a signature I recognized from history books and courthouse walls and the faded photographs in government offices.

Aurelio Sanch. Magistrate of the Cordillera del Silencio. Declared dead thirty-one years ago. Lost in a river crossing, body never recovered, mourned in official memorials, his portrait hung in the provincial courthouse where I'd stared at it as a schoolgirl during civics lessons, learning how justice worked.

Dead men don't sign ledgers.

"He's alive," I said. The words came out flat, factual, absurd. "The magistrate. He's alive."

Petra took the ledger page from me. Her eyes moved down the columns—dates, amounts, recipients—and I watched her face change as she understood what she was holding. Not just proof of theft. Proof of *administration*. Payments made, systematically, year after year, to officials whose names meant nothing to me and everything to the state. This wasn't a crime that had happened once. It was a crime that had been maintained. Tended. Budgeted.

"They've been paying for it," she said slowly. "All this time. Somebody's been making payments to keep this quiet, and somebody's been receiving them, and the paperwork—" she tapped the page, "—the paperwork is *here*. In a tin box. Under a rock. While the actual money moved through banks in the capital."

"Forty families," Tomás said. He was staring at the bundle of deeds like a man staring at a grave. "Forty names. Every family in the valley. Mine among them." He sat down heavily on a stone, all at once, like his legs had resigned without notice. "My father hid these. I helped him. And then I spent fifty years walking past this mountain, guiding hunters and geologists and government men up and down these trails, and I never once came back up here to check whether the papers were safe. Because checking would mean admitting I remembered where they were. And remembering would mean doing something."

"Now you've done something," Petra said.

"I dug a hole in the ground and took out a box."

"You dug up forty families," I said. "That's not nothing."

He looked at me then, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't wipe them, and I understood that this old man had been carrying a weight so long he'd forgotten it was there until the moment someone offered to help set it down.

* * *

We sorted the papers inside the shrine wall, out of the worst of the wind, working fast because the cloud was coming down the valley behind us like a tide. Petra photographed what she could with the battered camera she kept in her jacket—she'd explained earlier that film could be confiscated, cameras could be smashed, but memory plus documentation was harder to kill. I copied names into my notebook. Tomás identified signatures, murmuring, "Arriaga, yes, the arriero family, they lost the mule trail concession... Chumbe, the weavers, their allotment ran to the river..." Each name was a household. Each household was a theft. Each theft had a signature at the bottom of it, and the signatures kept circling back, again and again, to offices in the capital.

And then, near the bottom of the ledger, the final entry. The most recent. Dated—Petra checked it twice against her almanac—eleven months ago.

Eleven months ago, a dead man had authorized a payment.

"To whom?" I asked.

Petra read the entry twice. "No recipient named. Just 'the valley matter,' and an amount, and the signature." She looked up. "Sanch isn't just alive. He's *active*. He's still managing it. Whatever happened to Elena's team—"

She stopped. We all stopped. The wind filled the pause with its muttering.

"—whatever happened to Elena's team," Petra finished carefully, "happened because they found this. Or something like this. The mine records, the seizure trail, the payments. They were getting close to the same thread we're holding right now."

My mother had been getting close to the same thread.

I closed my notebook. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Inside, something had shifted—not grief, exactly, because grief was familiar furniture by now, but something colder and more useful. An inheritance, my grandmother used to say, is whatever you can't put down.

This was what I couldn't put down.

"We take everything," I said. "Every page. We don't leave a scrap. If they come looking, they find an empty hole and a story."

"Agreed," Petra said. "And we move. Now. That cloud is dropping faster than I'd like, and I want to be below the tree line before it eats the trail."

Tomás began rewrapping the bundles in the oilcloth, slower now, careful, the way you handle things that belong to other people. Forty families. Forty names. Somewhere down in the valleys, descendants of those names were herding goats and hauling water and paying taxes on land that had been stolen from their grandparents, never knowing that their inheritance had been sitting under a rock the whole time, patient as bone.

I helped him fold the oilcloth. The paper smelled of earth and rust and time. It smelled, faintly, of my mother's kitchen table, where maps had been spread and fingers traced rivers, where a woman had once told her daughter to listen to the land without arguing.

I was listening now, Mother. I finally was. And the land was telling me things I wished to God it had kept to itself.

We repacked the second hollow with stones, replaced the altar slab, and left the shrine looking undisturbed—which was a lie, but a polite one, the kind you tell the dead. Tomás added one stone to the recent cairn beside the wall. Just one. He didn't explain it, and I didn't ask.

We were twenty minutes below the tree line when the cloud swallowed the shrine behind us, and Tomás, walking point, stopped so abruptly I nearly collided with his pack.

Ahead of us, down-slope, half-hidden in the thinning trees: smoke. A thread of it, rising straight in the still air, from a place where no fire should be.

Petra eased up beside him. Her face had gone very still.

"That's between us and the riverbar," she said quietly. "And we didn't light it."
