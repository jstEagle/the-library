## 11. Snowline

The snow came at dawn, the way bad news does in the mountains—quietly, from a direction you weren't watching.

I knew it before I opened my eyes. There's a particular hush that falls when the first flakes land on canvas, a softness in the air like the whole world holding its breath, and I had heard it once before, thirty-one years ago, on a morning I have spent every morning since trying to forget. I lay in my bedroll and listened to it fall and thought: *well, old man. Here is your bill.*

The girls were still asleep. Petra slept the way she flew—efficiently, one ear open, ready to move. Marisol slept like her mother must have, all in, as if sleep were a country she'd been denied entry to for weeks and intended to make up for. I envied them both. My own nights had been short since 1979, and no amount of altitude was going to change that.

I got up quietly, fed the little stove, and put water on. Above the tree line now, three days out of the shrine, two days below the pass we meant to cross. The tin box sat between us like a fourth passenger—forty families' worth of paper, names I knew, signatures I knew better than I wanted to.

The snow thickened. By the time the girls woke, it was coming down in earnest, fat flakes with intent behind them, the kind that don't stop for politeness.

"That's early," Marisol said, staring out at the white.

"Two weeks early," I said. "Maybe three."

Petra looked at me the way she looked at weather reports she didn't believe. "Can we still make the pass?"

I could have lied. I'm good at lying; I've had practice. But there's a limit to how much weight a lie can carry, and we were already overloaded.

"We can try," I said. "But if we're crossing in this, there's something you both should know first. And I'd rather say it warm than frozen."

* * *

We sat around the stove with our hands around our cups, and I looked at the two of them—Petra Quintana with her grandfather's stubborn jaw, Marisol Vasco with her mother's eyes—and I understood that what I was about to do was either the bravest thing I'd done in three decades or the stupidest, and that at my age those are often the same thing.

"You know I carried ore," I began. "From the mine. I told you that."

"You told us," Petra said.

"I told you some of it. The part that makes me useful." I turned my cup. The tea was terrible; I'd brought terrible tea on purpose, because good things are for men who've earned them. "Here's the rest."

"In '78, when the government came through with their survey maps and their stamps, I was twenty-six years old and I thought I knew everything. I'd been guiding since I was fifteen. I knew every goat track on this side of the range, and I made sure everyone knew I knew it. Vanity. That's the seed of most betrayals—not money, not fear. Vanity. Somebody tells you you're important, and you believe them."

Marisol had gone very still. Petra hadn't moved at all.

"The man who told me was Aurelio Sanch."

I let the name sit there. Outside, the snow kept falling, indifferent.

"He was the magistrate's clerk then—soon to be magistrate himself, though nobody knew how soon. Sharp suit, softer voice, and he looked at me like I was a man of consequence. He said the valley families' claims were irregular. Old-fashioned. Holding back progress. He said if I helped the survey teams find the boundaries—the *real* boundaries, the ones drawn in his office—there'd be work for me. Steady work. Respectable work." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "He was right about that. I've had steady work ever since. Guiding hunters and geologists and lost tourists across ground that used to belong to forty families, including the Quintanas, and telling myself every year that I didn't know what the papers really said."

"And did you?" Petra asked. Her voice was flat, which from her means furious.

"I knew enough. I watched Sanch's men take the deeds from the mission archive—boxes of them, carried out under my own eyes while I pointed the way down a trail I'd walked a hundred times. I told myself they were going to be copied, filed properly, returned. You can tell yourself anything at twenty-six. The trouble is you keep telling yourself the same thing at fifty-seven, and by then it isn't innocence anymore. It's just a habit."

"Why are you telling us this now?" Marisol asked. Not cruelly. She had her mother's gift for asking the necessary question without sharpening it first.

"Because of the snow." I nodded at the tent wall, where the light had gone the color of old bone. "If this keeps up, the pass closes. If the pass closes, we're not getting these documents anywhere, and neither is anyone else. And if we're stuck here—" I stopped. Started again. "There's more you need to know, and I'd rather you hear it from me than from him."

"Him?" Petra said.

"Aurelio Sanch is alive."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the rocks with a sound like a long exhale.

"The records say he died in 1984," I said. "Drowned, they said, in the Río Blanco. No body recovered, but bodies wash away up here; nobody questioned it. His widow grieved beautifully. She was my sister." I looked at my cup. "She died believing it. That was the cruelest part, and I helped maintain it. Every year, on the anniversary, I'd go drink at her table and tell her the mountain had taken him, and the mountain takes what it takes, and there's nothing to be done. Meanwhile he was living up here. Above the snowline. Watching the mine he'd stolen."

"How do you know?" Petra asked. "How do you know he's alive?"

"Because I've seen his smoke." I said it plainly; there was no other way left to say it. "Twice in thirty years, from the high ridge east of the shrine—a thread of chimney smoke where no shepherd goes, where nothing legal exists. And because last spring a supply cache I keep near the tree line was raided. Nothing taken but coffee, salt, and matches. A thief takes meat. A man hiding in the high country takes coffee, salt, and matches. I know his tastes. I taught my sister to buy them."

Marisol set her cup down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "You think he took the survey team."

"I think your mother found the ledgers, and the ledgers name him, and a dead man cannot afford to be named." I met her eyes. "I don't know that he hurt anyone. I've spent three days hoping he hasn't. But I know he's up here, and I know this snow will drive us toward shelter, and the only shelter on this side of the pass is his."

* * *

Petra stood up. She has a way of standing that ends conversations she doesn't want to continue sitting through.

"You've known this the whole time," she said. "Since the shrine. Since before the shrine. You agreed to guide us and you didn't say any of it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because if I told you at the trailhead, you'd have gone anyway—you're your grandfather's blood, both of you, and blood like that doesn't turn around—and you'd have gone without me. And without me you'd walk into his country blind." I spread my hands. "So yes. I used you. I told myself I was protecting you. Both things were true, which is the worst arrangement truth ever invented."

The silence went on long enough that I could hear each flake land, or believed I could.

It was Marisol who spoke first. "My mother trusted someone too," she said. "Someone who knew the mountains. Someone who told her where to look." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on *look*. "Was that you? Did you send her up here?"

"No." And this I could give her clean. "Elena Vasco found the trail herself—I read that much in the camp ledger before we left, the survey notes, the cairn bearings. She was better at this than I've ever been. But I'll tell you what I can do." I reached over and tapped the tin box. "I can get that, and her, off this mountain. Or die explaining why I couldn't. It's the only payment I have left, and I intend to make it."

Petra studied me for a long moment with those pilot's eyes that weigh everything—fuel, load, weather, men.

"If you're lying," she said, "about any part of this—"

"I'm not lying anymore. That's rather the point." I almost smiled. "Lying is easy work. I'm tired of easy."

* * *

By midmorning the snow had buried the cairns to their waists. We broke camp anyway, because staying put in the open at eleven thousand feet is just dying with extra steps, and we moved toward the rock shoulder Tomás—toward *I*—knew waited two hours east, a shallow overhang where the stone held the day's warmth like a grudge.

I led. Behind me the girls followed, and behind them the box rode in its oilcloth, forty families' worth of inheritance bumping against Marisol's back. I thought about my sister as I walked. I thought about how she used to sing while she cooked, badly, joyfully, and how the last thing I ever gave her was a comfortable lie, and how lies are the only debts that compound faster than interest.

The snow eased near noon, the way storms do when they're pausing to reload. In that pause, from the shoulder of the ridge ahead, I saw it again after thirty years: a thin gray thread of smoke, rising straight up from a fold in the mountain where no map admits anything lives.

Petra saw it too. She stopped beside me, and I felt her arrive at the same arithmetic I had: smoke meant shelter, shelter meant warmth, warmth meant survival—but smoke also meant him.

"There," she said quietly.

"Yes," I said. "That's his fire."

Marisol came up between us, breathing hard, snow in her hair, her mother's deed strapped to her back. She looked at the smoke, then at me, and her chin came up in a way that made my chest ache, because I'd seen that exact gesture on another face, years ago, across a magistrate's desk.

"Then we walk toward it," she said.

And the snow, having considered its options, began to fall again—harder now, with the settled patience of winter come to stay.
