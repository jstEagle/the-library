## 12. The Pass That Isn't

The pass was called La Garganta del Viento—the Wind's Throat—and on the map it looked like a mercy: a low saddle between two shoulders of granite where a sensible person could cross from the eastern valleys to the high basin without touching glacier ice. Tomás had traced it with one cracked fingernail the night before we left the shrine, and I'd believed him, because Tomás had forgotten more about these mountains than the survey office ever knew.

I should have known better than to trust a map drawn by men who'd never carried anything heavier than a pencil over that ground.

We came up out of the last stunted forest in the grey half-light before dawn, Marisol ahead of me with the deed box strapped to her back like a hump she'd grown overnight, Tomás behind me moving with the deliberate economy of a man who has learned that every step costs something he'll want later. The tin box from the shrine rode against my spine, wrapped in my spare shirt, and I could feel its corners through the canvas even through everything else I was feeling, which was mostly cold and a headache the size of the Cordillera itself.

Forty families' names in a box on my back. I kept thinking about that. My grandfather's name among them—*Quintana*, inked in a clerk's careful hand thirty years before I was born, on a document declaring what was already true and then made false by everyone who signed beneath it.

"Smoke," Marisol said.

She said it quietly, the way you say *snake* when you're not sure yet whether it's a stick. We all stopped. Above us, maybe six hundred meters up the valley wall, a thin grey thread rose off a shelf of rock and bent east in the wind, unraveling into nothing.

"That's not a campfire," I said.

"No," Tomás agreed.

"It's too steady."

"Yes."

A fire that burns steadily at dawn, above the tree line, in a valley no one is supposed to be in—that's not a fire someone lit to keep warm. That's a fire someone lit to be seen by. Or it's a signal. Or it's a kitchen. Any of those possibilities was worse than the others, depending on the hour.

We stood there a moment, three people breathing white into the cold, and then we did what sensible fugitives do: we stopped looking at it and started walking faster.

* * *

The Wind's Throat announced itself an hour later as a narrowing—a place where the valley pinched between two walls of stone so sheer they seemed to lean toward each other overhead, as if the mountain were trying to whisper across the gap. The trail, if you could call it that, funneled down to a corridor perhaps twenty meters wide, scree on both sides, and through it the wind came hard enough to make me walk at an angle, leaning into it like a drunk leaning on a fence.

Tomás stopped first.

He didn't say anything. He just stopped, the way he did when his body knew something before his mind caught up, and I watched him go still in that particular way—not frozen, but *listening*, his whole frame turned into an instrument. Then he took two more steps, and two more, and then he sat down abruptly on a boulder as if his legs had been repossessed.

"Oh," he said.

That's all. Just *oh*, in the flat tone of a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate.

I came up beside him and saw what he saw, and my stomach did something complicated and unhelpful.

The pass wasn't there anymore.

Where the map promised a saddle—a clean notch of sky between the walls—there was instead a wall of broken rock. Not a little slide, not a few tons of stone that a determined party might climb over. A *field*. Hundreds of tonnes of it, spilled down from the western wall like something poured, filling the throat of the pass completely, blocks the size of trucks jumbled with gravel and shattered slabs, still settling, still shedding small streams of dust that drifted in the wind like the mountain exhaling after effort.

And it was fresh.

I've seen old slides. Old slides have edges worn round, lichen starting its slow yellow work, snowmelt channels cut through them, plants colonizing the margins. This had none of that. The fracture faces were pale and raw, the color of bone. Dust still hung in the air at the top of the field. And at the base of the western wall, where the rock had let go, there was a scar—a clean vertical wound in the mountainside, maybe forty meters tall, exposing stone that hadn't seen daylight since before there were people to name it.

Nobody had climbed that wall and pried it loose. But somebody had known exactly which seam to drill, exactly where to set a charge, exactly how much powder it would take to bring down enough mountain to close a pass.

"They blew it," Marisol said. Her voice came out very level, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "They blew the pass."

"Yesterday," Tomás said. "Maybe the day before." He got up off the boulder slowly, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and limped toward the slide field with the expression of a man checking whether a corpse is really dead. He picked his way twenty meters out onto the rubble, tested a slab with his boot, listened to it grind, and came back shaking his head. "Even without loads we'd lose a day getting over this. With Elena—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. We weren't carrying Elena yet, but we would be, or her survivors would be, and none of us weighed what we had a week ago.

"So we go around," Marisol said.

"There is no around." Tomás pointed west, where the wall ran unbroken toward a skyline of peaks wearing their first white of the season. "South is the glacier. North is four days of country nobody crosses in autumn, and the snow's early this year. This was the way through. This was the only way through."

I looked at the scar on the wall again. At the dust. At the arithmetic of it.

"They knew our route," I said. It wasn't a question, so nobody answered it. "Or they knew *a* route, and they closed it anyway, because closing a pass costs nothing and tells you everything. If we come at it from another direction, they'll know we didn't come through here. If we double back, we're slower. Every option we have now is worse than the one they took away."

"They're herding us," Marisol said.

"Like sheep," Tomás said, and spat, which for Tomás was a speech.

Here's the thing about being herded: the animal doesn't usually know it's happening until the corral walls are already there. The dogs work the flanks, quiet, patient, and the flock drifts the way pressure wants it to drift, and by the time the gate appears the animal is almost grateful for somewhere to go.

I stood in the wind at the mouth of a pass that wasn't, and I thought about the smoke.

It had been rising off the shelf to the east, up-valley, in roughly the direction we'd have gone if the pass had been open. A fire that shouldn't exist, burning steadily, visible from below, in country where a fire is either a mistake or an invitation.

"Sheep don't think," I said.

Marisol looked at me. "What?"

"The dogs herd the sheep because the sheep only run. They run from the noise, away from the dogs, wherever the pressure points. They never once think about walking *toward* the thing they're afraid of." I nodded up the valley, toward the thread of smoke. "Whoever set that fire expects us to see it and turn. Expects us to bolt south, or north, or back the way we came, into whatever's waiting there. They've spent money and dynamite making sure we have nowhere good to go. So the one direction nobody's planned for is straight at them."

"You don't know anyone's waiting anywhere," Marisol said. "You're guessing."

"I'm guessing with my life," I said. "Which concentrates the mind wonderfully."

Tomás hadn't spoken. He was looking at the smoke with an expression I couldn't read—something older than fear, some weather system moving behind his face. When he finally turned to me, he said, "There's a route up to that shelf. An old one. Ore trail, from before the seizures. It's steep, and it's exposed in two places, and if the wind swings north we'll wish we'd never been born. But it goes."

"You've walked it?"

"Once. Carrying ore down it, when I was younger than Marisol." His jaw worked. "I swore I'd never climb it again."

"Why?"

"Because the last time I stood on that shelf, I could see the mine." He said it simply, the way you'd report the time. "And I've spent thirty years arranging my life so I wouldn't have to look at it."

The wind gusted, hard enough to stagger Marisol a step, and somewhere above us a pebble ticked down the slide field and landed with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

"We're burning daylight," I said. "Decision's now."

* * *

Courage gets described wrong in most of the stories. In the stories it's a feeling—heat in the chest, a trumpet note, the blood singing. Maybe it is, for some people. For me it has always been colder and quieter and much less pleasant: courage is a decision made by a tired person who does not feel brave, who feels in fact like turning around, and who walks forward anyway because the arithmetic says forward is the only direction with a future in it.

I want to be honest about what I felt on that ore trail, because honesty is the one luxury I've never been able to afford and I'm told it appreciates in value.

I felt sick. I felt the specific dread of a pilot on the ground, which is the worst kind, because in the air fear has work to do—it keeps your hands moving, your eyes scanning, your mind running numbers—and on the ground it just sits on your chest like a cat that won't be fed. Every step up that trail I thought about turning back. I thought about it the way you think about a warm bed when you're standing in freezing rain: constantly, tenderly, with real longing.

I climbed anyway. That's the whole secret. There isn't another one.

The trail was exactly as bad as Tomás promised. It switchbacked up the eastern wall on a grade that made my calves burn, on footing that alternated between solid rock and gravel that moved underfoot like water with opinions. Twice we crossed ledges where the path narrowed to less than a boot's width, with nothing on the outside but air and a drop long enough to give you time to reconsider your entire biography on the way down. On the second ledge the wind swung north, exactly as Tomás had feared, and pushed at us sideways, and I pressed myself against the rock face and breathed through my teeth and thought about my grandfather's name in the ledger, and put one foot in front of the other, and then did it again.

Marisol climbed like she did everything—angrily, competently, as if the mountain owed her money. She had the deed box cinched tight and she never once asked to stop, though I saw her hands shaking on the rock and knew it wasn't only from cold.

Tomás climbed like a man returning to a house he'd burned down. Silent. Careful. Looking at everything and letting himself look, which I understood now was the harder labor.

Near the top the trail crossed a gully filled with old mine tailings—grey waste rock spilling down from workings higher up, decades of someone's greed ground fine and dumped on the mountain. Tomás stopped there and picked up a piece of it, turned it in his fingers, and let it fall.

"This came out of the Quintana adit," he said. "Your family's tunnel. The waste dump runs two kilometers down this gully. I used to count the cartloads. Forty-one a day, in the good years."

"In whose good years?" Marisol asked.

Tomás didn't answer. He started climbing again.

* * *

We came up onto the shelf in the middle of the afternoon, and the wind hit us like a hand shoving us back down the trail, and I dropped to a crouch behind a rib of rock and got my first look at the fire.

It sat maybe four hundred meters along the shelf, tucked into a hollow below an outcrop—a proper campfire, built in a stone ring, burning dry scrub and something darker that smoked steadily. Beside it, a shape. A figure, sitting on a rock, wrapped in something heavy. Not moving. Not hiding, either. Placed, the way you'd place a piece on a board.

One person. Alone. Sitting at a fire in the middle of nowhere, at the edge of winter, waiting with the patience of furniture.

"That's a trap," Marisol breathed beside me.

"Obviously it's a trap."

"Then why are we here?"

"Because traps have two ends," I said, "and I want to know what's holding up the other one."

But even as I said it, I was looking harder at the figure, because something about the posture was wrong for an ambush. Ambushers crouch. They face the approach. This figure sat slumped, angled slightly away from us, facing out over the valley to the east—facing the view, if you wanted to call it that, the grey fall of the ranges going down into cloud. Facing it the way a man sits when he's done waiting for something and is deciding what to do with the rest of the day.

Tomás had gone very still beside me. I mean *still*—stillness like the moment between lightning and thunder, the whole air pulled taut. He was staring at the figure at the fire, and his lips had gone grey, and when he spoke his voice came out sanded down to almost nothing.

"I know that coat," he said.

"What?"

"The coat. Grey wool, military cut, brass buttons replaced with horn. I know that coat." He swallowed. "I helped him buy it. Thirty-one years ago, in the capital, the week before they buried an empty coffin and called it Aurelio Sanch."

The wind came across the shelf and flattened the smoke from the fire, and the figure at the flames turned his head—slowly, without alarm, without surprise—directly toward the rib of rock where the three of us crouched, as if he'd heard Tomás's voice carried up the wind, or as if he'd been counting our footsteps the whole way up the ore trail and had simply been waiting for us to arrive.

He raised one hand. Not a threat. Not a welcome, either. Something older than both—an acknowledgment, the gesture of a man greeting guests he'd expected and dreaded in equal measure.

"He knows we're coming," Marisol whispered.

"He's always known," Tomás said. "That's the trouble with Aurelio. He's known everything for thirty years. It's the only thing he's ever been any good at."

The man at the fire stood up, unhurried, and I saw that he was old—old the way the mountains are old, worn down but structurally sound—and that he moved with the care of someone carrying an injury or an age he'd stopped apologizing for. He nudged the fire with his boot, sending up a fresh column of smoke, and I understood suddenly why the fire burned so steadily, why it had been visible from the valley floor.

He hadn't lit it to signal his own people.

He'd lit it so we could find him. Because at his age, on this shelf, in this wind, he couldn't come looking—and there was something he needed to give us, or something he needed to ask, and winter was coming down the mountain behind us like a debt collector.

"Well," I said, and stood up out of cover, into the open, into the wind, with forty families' names on my back and no plan worth the name. "Let's go meet the dead man."
