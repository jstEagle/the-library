## 15. Terms

He came out of the snow with no warning at all, which was how I learned that the smoke I'd chosen to walk toward had a chimney, a keeper, and thirty years of practice at being unseen.

One moment Marisol was helping Elena sip from the water bottle, Tomás was rigging a windbreak from survey stakes and prayer, and the next there was a man standing at the mouth of the cave as if the mountain had simply decided to produce him. Old. Straight-backed. Dressed in wool that had been mended so many times it had forgotten its original color. A rifle hung from his shoulder the way a tool hangs from a man who has carried it so long it's part of his skeleton, and his eyes went around our party once—Elena, the two surviving surveyors, Marisol, Tomás, me—and stopped on Tomás with a weight I could feel from across the cave.

"Hola, Tomás," the man said.

Tomás didn't answer. He had gone the color of the rock behind him, and his hands, which had been steady on the windbreak all afternoon, were suddenly very busy with a knot that didn't need tying.

I put my hand inside my jacket, which is where I keep the knife, and also where I keep the habit of pretending I'm calmer than I am.

"You'd be the pilot," the man said to me. His voice was dry and even, a voice used to being obeyed in rooms with better furniture. "The one who found the camp."

"I'd be a lot of people," I said. "Who's asking?"

"Aurelio Sanch," Tomás said, and the name came out of him like a tooth.

I knew the name. Everyone in the Cordillera knew it the way you know the name of a disease—Magistrate Aurelio Sanch, dead eleven years, buried in the capital with honors, his signature on half the seizure orders that stripped the high valleys of their land grants a generation back. A dead man was standing in a cave above the snowline, looking at me with the patience of someone who had already decided how this conversation would end and was merely waiting for me to catch up.

"You're supposed to be in the ground," I said.

"I'm supposed to be a great many places." He crouched down, slowly, the way old men crouch when they've made peace with the cost of it, and looked at Elena. "Señora Vasco. You found the mine."

"We found a mine," Elena said. Her voice was a rasp; eleven days in a cave with two dead companions and no rescue will do that to a voice. "We found ledgers. We found your signature."

"Yes." He said it without flinching, which I hated. A man who flinches you can read. "And that is why you are all going to die here, unless we negotiate."

* * *

Here is what I knew by then, and what I didn't.

I knew that three of the six surveyors were alive, and that two of the three couldn't walk—Elena's ankle was less an ankle than a suggestion, and the younger one, Iriarte, had a fever that had been climbing all day like it had somewhere to be. I knew the third, Quispe, was running on something deeper than strength and would run out soon. I knew the first snows had come early and were not a weather event anymore but a deadline.

I knew that the documents were in the tin box, wrapped in oilcloth, sitting under my jacket against my ribs where they'd been since the shrine. Forty families. A ledger of payments. Signatures, including one belonging to the dead man crouching in front of me.

I knew that Tomás had helped seize those claims when he was young and strong and stupid in the way the powerful make young men stupid, and that he'd been carrying it for thirty years the way you carry a stone in your boot—learning to walk with it, never learning to put it down.

And I knew that I did not trust Aurelio Sanch as far as I could throw the tin box, and I'd been flying in thin mountain air for fifteen years, so I could throw it farther than most, but not far enough.

"Negotiate," I said. "That's a funny word for a man with a rifle."

"The rifle is for the winter," he said. "For whatever comes down the valley looking for me. It is not for you. If I wanted you dead, pilot, you'd have died at the shrine, or on the ridge, or in the whiteout two days ago, and I'd never have had to look at any of you." He said it without heat, which was worse than heat. It was arithmetic. I recognized it, because it was the same arithmetic the officials in the capital had used on me, sitting behind their desks, deciding what my silence was worth.

"So talk," I said.

* * *

He talked. He was, I'll give him this, good at it. Thirty years of talking to no one but himself and the mountain, and he still had the magistrate's gift for making a thing sound reasonable.

He could not carry them down. That was the spine of it. He was seventy-three years old, he had a bad hip, and even in his best years he had never been able to carry a grown woman over a pass. The survivors would die up here—the fever, the cold, the wound on Quispe's leg that had gone bad at the edges. He'd watched the survey team through his glass for days before he'd worked up the nerve to come down, and by then two were already dead in the fall, and he had buried them, he said, with a prayer, which was more than he'd given most people.

"I can guide," he said. "I know these routes better than anyone living, because I am the only one living who has needed them. But I cannot carry. You have the strength, pilot. You have the plane, if the riverbar holds. What I have is the way down through the rock, and the knowledge of where the riders are, and—" he nodded at the box against my ribs, "—apparently, the past."

"And what do you want for it?" Marisol asked. She hadn't spoken since he'd appeared. She'd been standing over her mother with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and when she finally did speak, her voice cracked down the middle like river ice in spring. "The documents. You want the documents."

"I want the documents destroyed," Sanch said.

The cave went quiet in the way caves do, absorbing the sound and giving nothing back.

"No," Marisol said.

"Then hear the rest before you decide I'm a monster. I may be a monster, but I am a monster with information." He settled himself against the rock, and for a moment he looked tired in a way that no performance could fake—tired down to the geology of himself. "The men on the horses. You've seen them."

"We've seen what they left of the mission airstrip," I said.

"Then you know they are not police. They are not soldiers. They are the loose ends of a bargain I made eleven years ago, when I decided to die instead of testify." He looked at Tomás. "You know which bargain."

"I know you signed," Tomás said. His voice was flat, but his hands had finally stopped with the knot. "I carried the papers. You signed them. Thirty years, Aurelio. Thirty years I've told myself you were a young magistrate doing what the government told him to do, and that maybe you hated it. And the whole time you were up here, alive, sitting on top of what you took."

"Yes," Sanch said. "And I have spent every one of those years making sure no one else could take it again. You think I stayed above the snowline for my health? The mine is still there. The ledgers are still there. Every year, men come looking. Every year, I make sure what they find is nothing. I am not your villain, Tomás. I am the villain's janitor."

"That's a lovely speech," I said, "for a man who wants to burn forty families' land grants."

"I want to burn the *ledger*," Sanch said, and for the first time his evenness cracked, just a hairline. "The ledger has my name on payments to twelve officials, two of whom are still in the government, one of whom is now the man who employs the riders. If that ledger comes down this mountain, those men do not lose their careers. They lose nothing, because men like them never lose anything—they send the riders. And the riders will follow the ledger. To the judge who holds it. To the families who file on it. To the pilot who carried it." He looked at me. "To your airstrip. To your debts. To whatever is left of your life after they're finished with it."

"And the grants?" Marisol said. "The grants you leave alone?"

"The grants I leave alone," Sanch said, "because the grants are the only part of this that was ever true. The seizures were forged. The deeds were never legally destroyed—only hidden, by people smarter than I was at the time. The land belongs to the forty families. It has always belonged to them. I have known that for thirty years, and I have kept it safe the only way a coward knows how: by keeping it secret." He spread his hands. "So. My terms."

* * *

His terms were these.

The ledger—the pages with the payments, the names, the signatures—would not come off the mountain. He would burn it himself, here, tonight, in front of us, and keep the ashes. The land grants, the forty names, the proof of ownership, would go down with us, with the survivors, with Marisol and me, to whatever court and whatever judge we could find brave enough or ruined enough to hear the case.

In exchange, he would guide us down the far side, through the rock and the routes only he knew, ahead of the snow, past the riders, to the riverbar. All of us. The wounded, the grieving, the guilty, and the pilot.

"And then?" I asked. "After the riverbar. You just wave goodbye?"

"And then I surrender," he said.

That I had not expected. Neither, from the look on his face, had Tomás.

"You'll what?" Tomás said.

"I will walk into the capital, present myself to the court, and testify to every forgery I signed and every payment I took, in writing, under my own name, which will require the court to first establish that I am alive, which will be its own entertainment." He said it the way a man announces a weather forecast. "The riders are tracking me, not you. As long as I am alive and moving, I am the story they follow. When I stop moving—in a courtroom, in front of clerks and journalists and whatever is left of the law in this country—I stop being useful to the men who paid me, and I become dangerous to them instead. It is the only weapon I have left, and I have been saving it for thirty years. I had hoped to use it differently. The mountain has other plans. The mountain usually does."

I looked at Elena. She was propped against the cave wall, gray-faced, her ankle strapped and useless, and she was watching Sanch with the expression of a surveyor reading a contour line—measuring, not believing.

"You buried my people," she said.

"I did."

"You watched us for days."

"I did. I was a coward, and then I was a slower coward." His voice didn't waver, and I understood suddenly that the evenness was not calm. It was a man holding something down. "I will not ask your forgiveness. I am asking for your legs, señora. Yours have eleven days left in them at this altitude. Mine have none to spare. Between us, we might get you to the riverbar."

* * *

I want to tell you that I reasoned it out like a pilot—weight and balance, fuel and weather, the cold arithmetic of it. But the truth is that I stood there in a cave above the snowline with a dead man's bargain in my ears and my grandfather's land against my ribs, and what I felt was mostly exhaustion wearing the costume of judgment.

Because here was the thing. He was right about the ledger. I had been carrying it for two days, and I had been carrying the knowledge of it like a second heartbeat, and every time I thought about bringing it down the mountain I saw the mission airstrip burning, and I saw the stranger photographing my plane outside the government office, and I saw the mechanic's face when he pulled the grit from my fuel line and said, quietly, like a man telling me a diagnosis: *Someone wanted you to fall, Petra. Out here, that's the same as wanting you dead.* The ledger was a bomb. I'd known it since the shrine. I'd just been too tired and too angry to say it out loud.

And he was right about the grants. That was the part that got under my skin and stayed there. He could have lied. A dead man with a rifle and thirty years of practice could have told us any story, and who would check? But he'd told us the one story that cost him—the one where the families were right and the law was a forgery and he was the forger. Liars don't spend their capital on true things that hurt them. I've known enough liars to know the exchange rate.

But he'd also buried two surveyors and watched three more starve in the dark for days before his conscience finished its slow commute. And Tomás—Tomás, who had confessed his own guilt on the snowline with his head bowed like a man at a graveside—Tomás hadn't said a word since the terms were laid out, and his silence was louder than the wind.

"Tomás," I said.

He looked at Sanch for a long time. Thirty years looked back.

"She was your sister," Tomás said finally. "Rosa. My wife. You sat at my table. You held my daughter when she was born. And the same year, your signature took the Quintana claim, and the Vascos', and the forty." His voice didn't rise. It went down, which was worse. "I have hated you for thirty years, and I have missed you for thirty years, and I have never once been able to do both at the same time until right now."

"I know," Sanch said.

"The ledger burns," Tomás said. "The grants go down. And you walk into that courtroom, Aurelio, and you say all of it, with my name beside yours, because I helped carry the papers and I will help carry the truth, and God can sort out the difference." He turned to me. "Petra. He guides, we carry, the grants go to a judge. That's the deal. It's a bad deal. It's the only one on the table, and the snow doesn't negotiate."

I looked at Marisol. She had her mother's hand in both of hers, and her mother was looking at her, and whatever passed between them, I wasn't invited to it. But Marisol's jaw unclenched by one degree, which from her was a signature on a contract.

"The ledger burns tonight," I said to Sanch. "In front of me. Page by page, so I can see the names one last time. Because if I'm going to spend the rest of my life looking over my shoulder for the men in that ledger, I'm at least going to know whose shoulders they are." I pulled the tin box out from under my jacket and held it, and my hands were steadier than I had any right to expect. "And Sanch. If we get to the riverbar and this was a trap—if the riders are yours, if any part of this was a funnel—you should know I keep a flare gun in my survival kit, and I am a very good shot with it at close range, and I have been having a very bad month."

For the first time, something moved across the dead man's face that might have been, in a better climate, a smile.

"Noted, pilot," he said. "Build the fire. It's going to be a long night, and we have a mountain to argue with in the morning."

I opened the tin box. The oilcloth came away, and the pages inside were yellow and soft as moth wings, and the first name on the first grant was Quintana, in my grandfather's hand, in ink that had outlasted the government that tried to erase it.

Outside, the snow kept falling, patient as a creditor. And somewhere down the valley, I would have bet my one remaining engine on it, the riders were climbing.
