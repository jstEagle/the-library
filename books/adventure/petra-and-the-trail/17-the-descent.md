## 17. The Descent

The storm broke the way a fever breaks—not gently, but all at once, leaving everything wrung out and pale. I woke in the cave to a silence so complete it felt like an insult after two days of wind screaming through the pass like a creditor at the door. The kind of silence that means the mountain has finished arguing and expects you to act.

I crawled to the mouth of the cave. The sky above the ridge was rinsed blue, hard and clean, and the snow on the far slope lay unbroken from crest to cloud line, a white page waiting for someone stupid enough to write on it.

That someone was going to be us.

"Petra." Sanch's voice behind me. Thirty years above the snowline had given him a voice like gravel poured slowly. "We move today or we don't move until spring."

"Then let's not stand here admiring the view."

He almost smiled. Almost. On Aurelio Sanch's face, a smile was like a rumor of gold—everyone had heard of one, nobody living had seen it.

* * *

There were five of them to carry. Elena was the strongest of the survivors and even she could walk only with a shoulder under her arm; the others—Ríos with his ruined ankle, the young geologist whose name I still couldn't say without Marisol flinching, two more hollowed out by weeks of cold and half-rations—would have to go down on stretchers lashed from tent poles and the survey team's own rope. Tomás cut poles with his machete and said nothing, which for Tomás meant he was thinking hard about something he'd rather not think about.

Sanch stood over the wreckage of our map and drew routes in the snow with a stick. Not the mapped pass—that was still blocked, still watched, probably. His routes went down the far side, over shoulders and along ledges that existed nowhere on paper.

"You've walked these?" I asked.

"Every one," he said. "Some of them alone. Some of them carrying things I shouldn't have been carrying." He didn't elaborate, and I'd learned by then that Aurelio Sanch's silences were load-bearing. You didn't pull at them unless you wanted the whole structure coming down on your head.

"Ledger says you were a magistrate," Tomás said quietly, from across the fire pit. "Magistrates don't walk goat trails."

"This one did," Sanch said. "For thirty years. Ask me why some other time, old man. Or don't. We both know you already know."

The look that passed between them was long and old and had thirty years of unpaid bills folded into it. I busied myself checking lashings. Some conversations you're not invited to, and some you're actively grateful to be excluded from.

Marisol came and knelt beside me, testing knots. Her mother was going down on my stretcher party, and she'd appointed herself to it by the simple method of not asking permission. She had Elena's jaw and none of Elena's patience.

"She'll make it," I said.

"I know." A pause. "Say it again anyway."

"She'll make it. And so will you. And when we get to the bottom, I'm going to teach you both to fly, because clearly this family has a problem with staying on the ground."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of her, which was worth more than the deed box sitting there in the snow, worth more than forty families' names on brittle paper. Grief makes strange arithmetic, but laughter is the only currency it accepts at face value.

* * *

We left at first light, six walkers and five carried, Sanch in front reading the mountain like a ledger he'd written himself. And I will say this for the man: whatever else he was—a thief, a fugitive, a magistrate who'd buried his own name in the snow—he knew this range the way I knew my engine. Every sound it made, every place it might fail.

He took us off the shoulder of the peak along a ledge no wider than a coffin, angled just enough that the snow couldn't hold, and told us to walk where the rock showed dark. "Snow lies," he said. "Rock tells the truth. Walk on truth."

"That's a sermon," Tomás muttered behind him.

"It's a route description. Sermons are longer."

By midday we'd dropped below the wind line and the air thickened around us, warmer, denser, smelling of wet stone. Elena rode my stretcher with her eyes open the whole way, cataloguing, always cataloguing—I understood now that this was how she survived, by turning everything into data, including her own rescue. At a rest stop she looked up at me and said, "You fly the circuit alone? All year?"

"All year."

"My God," she said, with genuine feeling. "And they sent *us* up here with a support budget."

* * *

It was the second day that nearly killed us.

The weather came back around noon, sliding in off the western peaks as fog first, then snow, then that particular white horror where sky and ground surrender their border and the world becomes an unwritten page. Whiteout. I'd flown above cloud my whole life; I'd never been inside one that ate the ground beneath my feet.

Sanch called a halt and got us huddled against a rock wall, counted heads twice, and then his face changed. He counted again.

"The second stretcher," he said. "Where's the second stretcher?"

Gone. Ríos and his carriers—Tomás among them—had been twenty meters behind us, and the whiteout had simply closed its hand around them like water closing over a stone. No sound. No tracks. Nothing.

Marisol started forward and I caught her arm. "You go out there, you're gone too. That's how it works. That's the whole trick of it."

"So we just—" Her voice cracked. "They're *right there*, Petra. Twenty meters."

"Were," I said. "Twenty minutes ago."

Sanch was already tying rope to his waist. He didn't ask for volunteers, didn't explain, didn't do anything but coil the line and step backward into the white like a man stepping off a dock. I grabbed the end and paid it out, and Marisol and I anchored ourselves against the rock and fed him rope, arm over arm, into nothing.

"How does he know where they went?" Marisol shouted over the wind.

"He doesn't," I shouted back. "That's what the rope is for."

Ten minutes. Fifteen. My hands went from pain to a warm numbness I recognized as the beginning of something bad. The rope ran out through my gloves in slow pulses—moving, stopping, moving—and each stop was a small death I had to sit through. Marisol prayed, or recited names, or both; I couldn't tell and didn't ask.

At twenty minutes the rope went taut and stayed taut, three heavy pulls—the signal Sanch must have taught someone, sometime, for exactly this. We hauled. It was like hauling a drowned river, dead weight and drag, and then shapes came swimming out of the white: Sanch first, bent double under Ríos's stretcher slung across his shoulders, Tomás behind him with the other carrier's collar in his fist, dragging both men like sled dogs refusing the harness.

They collapsed against the rock wall in a tangle of rope and breath. Sanch set the stretcher down with exaggerated care, straightened up, and vomited into the snow. Then he wiped his mouth and said, in a voice of perfect bureaucratic calm:

"The ledge drops away another fifty meters on. They walked past the turnoff. In this, anyone would."

"You went out there," Tomás said. He was shaking. I'd never seen Tomás shake. "Sixty years old, blind as the rest of us, on a rope."

"Yes," Sanch said.

"Why?"

Sanch looked at him for a long moment. Snow gathered on both their shoulders like the mountain trying to bury the question before it got answered.

"Because I put them on this mountain," he said finally. "All of them. Elena's team, your families, every name in that box. Thirty years I've been paying interest on that debt, and today I found out it can be paid in something other than standing still." He turned away. "Rest ten minutes. We move before it sets."

Trust, I thought, watching him retie the rope with fingers I could see were frost-nipped. Trust isn't a thing you decide. It's a thing that happens to you while you're hauling on the other end of a line.

* * *

We made the tree line at dusk, dropping out of the white world into a forest so sudden and green it felt like surfacing from deep water. The injured groaned with relief they were too weak to express any other way. Marisol cried without noise, the way she did everything—efficiently, furiously, as if crying were a task on a list.

We camped under a granite overhang, and Sanch built a fire with wood he seemed to know by address. I sat next to him, mostly because everyone else was afraid to and partly because I wanted to see his hands.

"They're frozen," I said.

"They'll thaw."

"Frostbite doesn't care about your schedule."

"No," he agreed. "Neither did I, once. That was rather the problem." He flexed the fingers, wincing, watching the fire. "Your grandfather worked the Quintana claim, didn't he. Below the shrine."

The question landed like a dropped wrench. "How do you know that?"

"I signed the seizure order." He said it flatly, the way you'd read coordinates. "Forty-one claims in eleven days. Your grandfather's was the ninth. He argued longer than most. He called me a word I won't repeat in front of the girl." Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but its fossil. "He wasn't wrong."

I should have hated him right then. I'd had days to prepare the hate, packed it like gear, kept it dry. But sitting there with his burned hands and his thirty-year ledger of penance, all I could manage was the exhausted honesty of someone too tired to hold a grudge upright.

"When we get down," I said, "you're going to testify. All of it. On the record."

"Yes."

"And then they'll hang you, or whatever it is they do now."

"Probably something modern and humiliating," he said. "Yes."

"Good," I said, and he nodded, and that was the closest thing to a handshake we ever exchanged.

Across the fire, Tomás was telling Marisol about the mine as it had been—the carts, the songs, the smell of ore dust—and I realized he was doing it deliberately, building the girl an inheritance out of memory since paper hadn't been enough to keep one. Elena slept. The fire ticked. Somewhere below us, far down in the dark, a river ran toward the lowlands, and beyond the river, a riverbar, and beyond the bar—

I stopped myself. I'd spent my whole life not borrowing trouble from tomorrow, and I was good at it. But tonight the habit wouldn't take. Because tomorrow was the riverbar, and the riverbar was the one place on this entire mountain range where we would be visible, exposed, funneled—exactly the kind of ground that men who answer to no one choose for their work.

Sanch caught me looking south. He didn't say anything. He just added wood to the fire, and the flames stood up taller, and neither of us slept much.
