## 18. Betrayal at the Riverbar

The riverbar looked different from below than it had from above. Everything does. From the air, the gravel shelf where my mother's team had once been dropped off was a pale comma against the green water, a place you could cross in three seconds of engine noise. From the ground, after nine days of carrying human beings down a mountain on stretchers made of tent poles and prayer, it was the Promised Land.

We came out of the trees at mid-morning, and I remember thinking: we made it. That was my mistake. I let myself think in complete sentences again.

Petra stopped so suddenly that Tomás walked into her back, which for a man his age and a woman her stubbornness should have been fatal to at least one of them.

"Don't," she said.

I looked past her shoulder. There were four horses tied at the far end of the bar, saddled, standing hipshot in the way horses do when they've been waiting a while and have opinions about it. And there were men. Three of them, dressed like the kind of hunters who never bring anything home but trouble, rifles slung with the casualness of men who have used them enough that the gesture has worn smooth.

"Ah," said Tomás, in the tone of a man confirming a long-held theory about the weather.

"You knew?" I whispered.

"I suspected. Now I know. It's an important distinction in my line of work."

Behind us, Elena stirred on her stretcher. She'd been drifting in and out since the pass, fever and lucidity trading shifts like dockworkers. One of the other survivors—Ríos, who had a broken arm and the composure of a man who had already decided death was overrated—lifted his head.

"Company?" he asked.

"Company," Petra confirmed. She set down her end of the stretcher with exaggerated care, the way you do when you want your hands free and everyone to pretend otherwise. "Stay here. All of you. If this goes badly, take them downriver along the bank. There's a fisherman's hut two bends south. Tomás knows it."

"I know it," Tomás said. "I also know that walking away is not what these men are here to permit."

"Then walk fast."

She started forward. I did not stay behind. This will surprise no one who has spent more than ten minutes with me, and it didn't surprise Petra either; she glanced back, saw me coming, and gave up on me the way you give up on weather.

* * *

The three men watched us come. The one in the middle was older, gray at the temples, with the patient stillness of someone who had been paid to wait before and had gotten good at it. He didn't reach for his rifle. He didn't need to. The rifle reached for itself, metaphorically speaking, just by existing.

"Pilot," he said to Petra. "You're hard to kill. We've noticed."

"Flattery," Petra said. "From a man holding a gun. My week is complete."

"We're not here for you." His eyes went past her, past me, to the stretcher party at the tree line, and something in his face tightened—not conscience exactly, more like a man doing arithmetic he'd hoped would come out differently. "Where is he?"

"He who?" Petra asked, and she was very good, she was so good, but the older man had been doing this longer than she'd been flying.

"Sanch."

The name sat on the riverbar between us like a dropped stone. Behind me I heard Tomás come up slowly, his boots crunching gravel, and I understood then—I finally understood—why the riders were here, why they hadn't simply shot us on the mountain or burned us out of the cave. They weren't tracking us. They had never been tracking us.

They were tracking their paymaster's loose end.

"He's not with us," Petra said.

"No," the man agreed pleasantly. "But he was. Snow doesn't lie, pilot. Six sets of tracks out of that valley, and one of them walks like a man who learned to march before any of you were born. He led you through the pass. He carried a stretcher through the whiteout—we found where he set it down." He tilted his head. "He's good. Thirty years up there, you'd expect him to be feral. He isn't. He's careful. Careful men are the worst kind to hunt."

"So don't hunt him," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I hated that, hated being twenty-four years old and audible. "He's done. Whatever you think he owes—he's spent thirty years paying it. He lives above the snowline alone. He buried himself alive up there. What more do your employers want?"

The man looked at me with something almost like kindness, which was worse than cruelty. "Señorita, they don't want anything. That's the whole point. Wanting is how you get caught. They just need the account closed." He turned back to Petra. "Here's the offer, because I'm told you're a woman who deals. You brought papers down off that mountain. Deeds, ledgers, whatever the old man kept warm all these years. You hand those over, and you fly these people out, and nobody follows you anywhere ever again. And Sanch—" he spread his hands, "—Sanch comes with us. Quietly. Cleanly. He's an old man. It'll be gentle."

"And if I say no?"

"Then it gets loud, and loud is bad for people on stretchers."

* * *

I want to record, for whoever eventually reads this—and Marisol, if you're reading your own account, stop editorializing—but I want to record that in that moment I did the math too. Everyone did. It was right there on the bar, in the gravel, in the arithmetic the man had laid out so reasonably: documents against lives. Papers against my mother. Papers against Ríos and Delgado, who could barely stand. Papers against Petra's plane, sitting in plain view at the south end of the bar, unguarded, full of fuel.

One box of tin and paper, weighed against everything.

And Petra Quintana stood there with the wind pulling at her jacket and did not even blink.

"No," she said.

The man sighed, genuinely regretful, an actor who had rehearsed regret until it fit him like a glove. "Pilot—"

"No. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to say louder things." She stepped closer, close enough that the two flanking riders shifted their weight, and she ignored them with the magnificent contempt of a woman who had landed tail-draggers on riverbars narrower than her own self-regard. "Those deeds belong to forty families. To her family"—she jerked a thumb at me—"and to mine, though it took me thirty years and a dead engine to learn it. They are not currency. They are not leverage. They are not for sale, trade, or safekeeping. And as for Aurelio Sanch—"

"He's nothing to you," the man said.

"He's the man who saved my passengers when I couldn't," Petra said. "That makes him mine. You don't get to close accounts on my mountain, with my people, on my riverbar. Find another auditor."

There was a silence. Somewhere upstream a bird made an idiotic, cheerful sound, entirely unaware of the moment.

"You're choosing a dead magistrate over your own skin," the man said, not unkindly. "That's rare. Usually people choose their own skin over their own mothers."

"Usually people haven't met my mother," I said.

And that was when Sanch walked out of the trees.

* * *

He must have hung back since the last camp, following our track at a distance, the way he had done half the descent—present, unseen, unwilling to be seen until he had no choice. He came out onto the bar with his hands empty and open at his sides, an old man in a coat gone gray as ash, and the effect on the three riders was immediate and ugly. Rifles came around. Horses stamped.

"Aurelio," the leader said. Not surprised. Relieved, maybe, the way a surgeon is relieved when the tumor presents itself.

"They were never going to give you the papers," Sanch said. His voice was quiet and carried anyway, the way voices do in big empty places. "You know that. You were always going to take them. So let's skip the part where anyone pretends this was a negotiation." He walked forward, deliberately, placing each step like a man crossing ice. "Me. Unarmed. No chase, no shooting, none of your mess on a public bar where fishermen pass. In exchange, they walk to the plane and they fly, and you lose sight of them by noon."

"That was the offer," the man agreed.

"It was my offer first," Sanch said. "You borrowed it. You always borrow. That's what you are—you're what's left when borrowing becomes a profession."

"Sanch, don't—" I started.

"Marisol." He didn't turn around. "Your mother is alive because I showed your pilot the route through the scree. Let an old man buy one thing outright before the end. It's the only purchase I'll ever make clean."

Tomás moved then. I hadn't even seen him come up beside me, but suddenly he was there, and his face—Tomás's face, which I had seen tired, guilty, wry, afraid—was none of those things. It was terrible in its calm.

"Aurelio," he said. "Brother-in-law."

Sanch stopped. For the first time, the magistrate's composure flickered, a lamp in wind. "Tomás. You shouldn't have come down with them. You should have stayed hidden like me. Cowardice keeps, brother. Courage spends."

"One of us had to spend," Tomás said. "We drew lots thirty years ago and I cheated. I've been meaning to mention it."

Whatever that meant, it landed on Sanch like a blow. And the riders were watching this exchange—their target and some ancient guide having a reckoning in front of them—with the growing irritation of men whose script had been torn up.

"This is touching," the leader said. "It changes nothing. Aurelio, come here. Slowly."

Sanch took one step. Two.

"No," Petra said again.

"Pilot," the man said, exasperated now, rifle lifting off its sling, "you are a bush pilot with a debt note and a dead engine and no army. Stand aside."

"The engine's not dead," Petra said.

It took me a second. Then I saw it—the way she wasn't looking at the riders at all anymore. She was looking past them, down the bar, toward the plane, and there was something in her face I'd only seen once before, on the mountain, when she'd decided to walk toward smoke that shouldn't exist.

Courage as a decision. Not a feeling.

"What did you do?" the leader asked, and for the first time the patience in his voice developed a crack.

"Nothing yet," Petra said sweetly. "But while you three have been standing here admiring yourselves, my mechanic friend—" she nodded at Tomás, who had drifted, casually, terribly, ten feet to the left, "—has been within reach of my tool kit. And I keep a flare pistol in it. And my fuel drums are stacked about forty meters from your horses."

"You wouldn't," one of the flankers said. "Burn four horses?"

"I'd burn the whole Cordillera before I hand over one more person to men who answer to no one," Petra said. "Ask the mission airstrip. Oh—wait. You were there."

The silence that followed had teeth. And in it, Sanch did the thing I will think about for the rest of my life: he smiled. A small, real smile, aimed at Petra's back.

"There it is," he said softly. "Thirty years I wondered what the families' blood looked like when it finally stood up. There it is."

"Walk to the plane," Petra said to the rest of us, without turning her head. "All of you. Now. Slowly."

Nobody argued. Ríos got Delgado up. I took my mother's stretcher handles, and Elena opened her eyes—fever-bright, lucid for the moment—and looked at the tableau on the bar, the old magistrate with his empty hands, and said, in a voice like a scalpel:

"Aurelio Sanch. You will not die on this riverbar. I forbid it. I have testimony to take."

"Magistrate Vasco," Sanch murmured, "even now, giving orders."

"Always."

We moved. Ten meters. Twenty. The riders' rifles tracked us and Petra's hand hovered near the tool kit and the whole world balanced on a knife-edge of bluff, because I would learn later that the flare pistol was back at the cave, lost in the storm, and Petra had been prepared to throw a wrench at a horse and hope for a miracle.

And then Sanch walked forward into the circle of rifles, arms wide, and said the words that broke the day open:

"Take me. But hear this first—all of you, and your paymaster too, wherever he hides. The deeds were never destroyed. The grants stand. Forty names, witnessed, sealed, and by tomorrow morning they'll be in front of a judge who can't be bought, because I've spent thirty years making sure of one honest man in that capital, and his name is in my pocket." He patted his coat. "Shoot me and it goes to the newspapers anyway. Let me live to testify, and maybe—maybe—your employer dies of paperwork instead of gunfire. Choose."

The leader's rifle did not lower. But his eyes did the arithmetic. I watched him do it. Papers that survive a dead man versus a loose end that could be closed quietly later, except now it couldn't, not quietly, not ever—

"Horses," he said finally, to his men, and it was not mercy, it was retreat wearing mercy's coat. "Mount up."

They took Sanch anyway. That was the bargain, and he walked into it with his spine straight, glancing back once—at Tomás, at me, at the mountains he had guarded like a wound for thirty years.

"Fly well, pilot," he said. "Land better."

Petra watched them go until the horses were dots on the far trail, and then she turned around and put both fists on her hips and shook, just once, head to toe, the shake of a woman who had been absolutely certain of nothing for nine days.

"Everyone in the plane," she said hoarsely. "Before I change my mind about any of this."

Tomás lingered at the edge of the bar, looking down the trail where his brother-in-law had vanished, and said quietly, to no one, to the river:

"Hold on, Aurelio. I cheated the lot, remember. I'm owed one more round."

Then he climbed in after us, and Petra's engine caught on the third try, and the riverbar fell away beneath us like a page turning.
