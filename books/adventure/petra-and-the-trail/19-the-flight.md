## 19. The Flight

The wreck of the supply cache looked like something a giant child had built and then lost interest in. A fuselage half-sunk in gravel, wings folded at angles wings don't go, and scattered around it like shed skin: crates, drums, a coil of rope, and — if Tomás's memory served, and so far it had served better than my map — tools.

"Tell me again why you're smiling," Marisol said. She had Elena's arm over her shoulder and Elena's weight on her hip, and she was still managing to look at me like I'd gone soft in the head.

"Because that Cessna has a magneto," I said, "and mine doesn't."

My own plane sat at the far end of the riverbar where we'd dragged her through the willows two nights before, nose low, tail high, looking less like an aircraft than a very expensive sled. The riders had taken her carburetor apart with more enthusiasm than skill and left the pieces in a coffee can, which told me two things: they wanted her flyable eventually, and they didn't know what they were doing. Both were gifts. I intended to accept them rudely.

Tomás crouched by the wreck and began pulling panels without ceremony, the way he did everything, as though the mountain had already forgiven him for it. "How long?"

"Give me three hours."

"We may not have three hours."

"Then give me two and a half and don't talk to me while I work."

He almost smiled. Almost. Tomás rationed his smiles the way the mountain rations its sunlight, and I'd learned not to waste them.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fixing an airplane under threat of death: your hands are steadier than you'd expect. Fear, it turns out, is mostly fuel. It burns hot and dirty, but it burns.

The problem was this. My engine ran — I'd heard it turn over that morning, a sick, grinding cough, but it turned — but the fuel system was full of whatever grit those men had introduced back in chapter five of my life, and the carburetor jets were scored. The supply cache Cessna, bless her dead heart, had a clean carburetor, a spare magneto still wrapped in wax paper, and a fuel pump whose diaphragm hadn't rotted through. Also forty gallons of avgas in drums, which was either salvation or a bomb depending on how carefully I siphoned.

I started with the carburetor. Laid both units side by side on a crate lid, swapped jets, blew through passages until my lips went numb, and held the rebuilt unit up against the gray sky like a jeweler appraising a stone that might be glass.

"You'll want to check the float," Tomás said from somewhere behind me.

"I said don't talk to me."

"The float. If it's saturated it sits wrong and floods the—"

"I know what a saturated float does, Tomás."

"Your grandfather taught you that."

It wasn't a question. He'd known my grandfather. He'd carried ore beside him, maybe, back when the mine still belonged to us on paper and in fact. I let that sit between us for a moment, heavy and warm, and then I checked the float. It was saturated. I hated that he was right, and I loved him a little for it, and I said neither thing out loud because there was no time and because some feelings are like nitroglycerin: true, valuable, and best not shaken.

* * *

Marisol worked beside me without being asked, holding parts, fetching the screwdriver I kept losing into the gravel, reading torque specs off a maintenance card she found in the wreck's glovebox with the particular satisfaction of a woman whose mother had made her study for a pilot's license she never finished.

"She'd have finished it," Marisol said once, quietly, nodding at Elena, who lay wrapped in every blanket we owned near the fire, feverish, her broken leg splinted with tent poles and prayer.

"I know," I said.

"She used to land on glacier strips. She told me about it like other mothers talk about gardening."

"Elena Vasco could probably land on a glacier strip in a crosswind while filing a complaint with the ministry." I tightened the last fitting on the fuel line. "She gets it from you."

That earned me half a laugh, which in Marisol's currency was a fortune. Then her face went back to the flat watchfulness she'd worn since the cave, since the stretcher, since the word *riders* stopped being hypothetical.

We both knew the arithmetic. Three of us who could walk, one who couldn't, one old man with knees that forecast weather, a wounded woman who needed a hospital or a miracle, and six armed men somewhere behind us who had already burned one airstrip to send a message. The message was: *we are thorough.* The only exit was up. And up required one engine, salvaged from two wrecks, assembled by a woman who'd been awake for thirty hours.

I've flown worse odds. I just usually got to choose them.

* * *

By midday the sun came through thin enough to cast shadows, and I had the engine buttoned up. That left the airframe, and the airframe was where the day got ugly.

The riders, in their thoroughness, had slashed both tires on my plane. Not shot them — slashed them, neat and economical, the way men slash tires when they want you mobile later but humbled now. I stood over the ruin of my port tire and did the math on patch kits, which came to zero.

Then I remembered the cache Cessna's tires. Same size. Worn to the canvas on one shoulder, but canvas beats slash.

"Wheels," I called, and Marisol and Tomás spent the next hour wrestling a wheel off a dead airplane, which is exactly as fun as it sounds, involving a jack improvised from a fuel drum and a length of plank, a lot of Portuguese-adjacent swearing from Tomás that predated the republic, and one moment when the drum shifted and nearly took his fingers and I learned what my own voice sounded like at panic volume.

But it went on. The wheel went on. I pumped it fat with a hand pump until my arms shook, rolled the plane level, and stood back.

She looked terrible. Patchwork cowling, mismatched tire, fuel stains down her flank like old tears. She looked like every aircraft I'd ever loved, which is to say like a promise made out of scrap.

"One engine," Tomás said beside me. Not a question either. He was getting good at that.

"One engine," I agreed. "Everything riding on it."

"And if it quits over the ridge?"

"Then everything rides on something else. Wind, luck, the Virgin, my grandfather's ghost. I'm not picky about co-pilots."

* * *

We loaded her as the light went long and gold. Elena first, laid across the removed rear seats on a litter lashed to the frame, her head toward the front so I could hear her if she woke and so she could hear me, because I had decided — without consulting anyone — that hearing my voice would keep her anchored the way a compass needle stays anchored to north even in a shaking hand.

Then the deed box, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped where it couldn't shift. Forty families' worth of names, a ledger of bribes, a magistrate's signature on paper that should have burned thirty years ago. Heavier than anything else aboard, and it weighed nothing at all.

Marisol climbed in beside her mother. Tomás stood at the wing with his hands in his pockets, and I realized he wasn't getting in.

"Tomás."

"Somebody walks out and leads them wrong," he said. "If they find tracks going one way and no plane, they follow the tracks. You go south over the pass; I take the horses west along the river. Buy you the afternoon."

"That's insane."

"It's arithmetic." He smiled then, fully, spending one of his hoarded coins. "Besides. This mountain owes me a walk. I intend to collect."

I grabbed his collar before he could turn away. "You come down off this bar alive, old man. You hear me? There's a hearing coming. There are forty families who need to see your face stand next to Sanch's. You don't get to die buying me time. That's not how debts work anymore. We're done with that economy."

For a long moment he looked at me, and something moved behind his eyes like water under ice — thirty years of guilt finding, at last, a reason to stay thawed.

"Two days," he said. "Mission strip. I'll be drinking your coffee."

"Three days. I'm flying slow. Everything's riding on one engine."

* * *

I ran up the engine at dusk.

She caught on the third blade — coughed, cleared, and settled into a rumble that sounded like a man with a chest cold insisting he's fine, thank you, perfectly fine. Oil pressure crept up. Temps held. The needle on the fuel gauge did something heroic and honest for the first time in days.

And then, across the river, on the trail we'd come down yesterday: movement. Horses. Six of them, small in the failing light, moving fast, and above them the flat glitter of glass catching the last sun — binoculars, or a scope, pointed our way.

They'd seen the smoke from our fire. Or heard the engine. Or simply followed the river, because rivers are honest and always lead somewhere, and thorough men know that.

"Time to go," Marisol said from behind me. No fear in it. Just fact.

I swung up into the seat. Straps. Mixture rich. Flaps set for a short, ugly, overloaded takeoff off nine hundred feet of riverbar gravel with a tailwind I didn't like and a tree line at the far end that I liked even less.

Elena stirred as I ran the throttle up. Her eyes opened, found mine, and even feverish, even gray, she managed the look of a woman grading my technique.

"Vasco," I said over the noise, "if you can hear me — your daughter says you land on glaciers."

A ghost of a smile. "Full throttle first," she whispered. "No hesitation. Hesitation kills on short strips."

"Yes, ma'am."

I held the brakes, brought her to full song, felt the whole airframe shudder like a dog shaking off a river, and let go.

The bar rushed at us. Gravel sang against the belly. The tail came up heavy — too heavy, we were overloaded past any number in the book — and the trees at the end grew taller with every second, patient and green and absolutely certain of themselves. Fifty feet. Thirty. The wheels left the ground so late I felt them kiss a tussock on the way out, a little *goodbye* tap, and then we were climbing at an angle that would have made my instructor weep, dragging ourselves into the air on one borrowed, mended, blasphemous engine.

Behind us, through the rear window, I saw the riders reach the bar. Saw one of them dismount and fire, twice, the muzzle flashes small and furious and far too late. The rounds went past like rude thoughts.

Ahead of us: the pass, the dark coming down, and a hundred and forty miles of mountains between here and the capital strip.

"Petra," Marisol said, leaning forward, her hand on her mother's blanket, her eyes on the ridgeline ahead. "The fuel gauge."

I looked. The needle, that honest hero of ten minutes ago, had begun — slowly, thoughtfully, like a man changing his mind — to fall.

Faster than it should.

Somewhere behind the panel, in a line I'd spliced by lantern light, the mountain was taking its cut early.
