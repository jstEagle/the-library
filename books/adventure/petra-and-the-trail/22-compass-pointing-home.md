## 22. Compass Pointing Home

Spring came late to the Cordillera that year, the way it always does, arriving like a relative who complains about the journey and then stays too long. The snowline retreated up the valleys in grudging stages, leaving brown stubble and the bones of last year's flowers, and when the passes finally opened enough for a plane to fly them without insulting the weather, I was the first one through.

Of course I was. I'm the only one who flies them at all.

The Cessna came back from the dead, which is more than I can say for some. The mechanic—Roldán, who talks to engines the way other men talk to horses, in a low voice and with a great deal of flattery—rebuilt her from the riverbar wreck over four months, and when he was done he stood back, wiped his hands, and said, "She'll never be the same."

"Neither will I," I said, "and I'm flying anyway."

He took this as a compliment to his work, which it was, mostly.

So on a morning in late October, when the sky over the capital strip was that washed blue that only exists at high altitude in spring, I loaded my circuit the way I always had: quinine and penicillin for the mission at San Rafael, a crate of schoolbooks for the settlement at Puerto Frío, mail, a sack of coffee, a radio battery the size of a coffin lid. And then, on top of all that, a leather document case, government-stamped and heavier than anything else in the plane, though it weighed almost nothing at all.

Legal papers. Forty families' worth. Certified copies of land grants that had spent thirty years in a tin box above the snowline while the mountains kept the original secret and the courts kept the rest.

It's strange, what a plane is for. For eleven years I thought it was for hauling medicine and gossip, and I suppose it was, but it turns out it was also for this: for carrying the truth at two hundred kilometers an hour over terrain that had swallowed it whole, and delivering it to doorsteps that had stopped waiting.

* * *

The first stop was San Rafael. The mission airstrip had been burned the previous autumn by men who answered to no one, and I half expected to find it still a scar. But the brothers had rebuilt the fuel shed with new timber, and someone—Tomás, I suspected, though he'd never admit it—had planted a row of alders along the windward edge to break the gusts off the ridge. They were saplings, thin as whips, but they were planted in a line, which is the most hopeful thing I'd seen in the Cordillera since the hearings began.

Father Benítez met me at the strip with his sleeves rolled and ink on his fingers.

"Medicine," he said, taking the crate, "and I suppose the rest is gossip."

"The rest is jurisprudence," I said. "The Vásquez claim was approved Tuesday. All of it. Every hectare."

He set the crate down carefully, the way you set down something that might break, though it was just plywood and penicillin. Then he crossed himself, which for Benítez is not a small gesture. He's a practical man. He saves the crossing for things that matter.

"Forty families," he said.

"Forty families." I let that sit a moment. "The Quintanas were the eleventh signature on the original grant. My grandfather's hand. The archivist showed me. He had beautiful handwriting for a man who was about to be robbed."

"And yours?"

"Mine looks like a spider fell in the inkwell. But it's on the new filing, all the same. Petra Quintana, heir, co-signatory, witness." I shrugged, because if I didn't shrug I was going to have to feel something about it, and I had a circuit to fly. "The state even apologized. In writing. A whole paragraph."

"Did you keep the letter?"

"I'm having it framed. I'll hang it in the outhouse where it belongs."

Benítez laughed, and the alders leaned in the wind, and I went back to the plane with my hands steadier than they had any right to be.

* * *

The high valley took two hours to reach, and I flew most of it in silence, which for me is a kind of prayer.

I won't pretend I don't still look at that valley the way you look at a room where something happened. The survey camp was long gone—the state had sent a crew in December to pack out the gear, very quietly, very officially—but I could have landed on that riverbar blindfolded and known where every tent had stood. I could have told you where the stove had been warm on the morning I found no tracks, where the gear had been stacked in that neat, terrible order, like a sentence someone had stopped writing mid-word.

Three of them came back. Four didn't.

The fall took two on the day it happened—Oyarzún and the young one, Cifuentes, whose name I only ever learned afterward, from the inquiry, which is a shameful way to learn a man's name. The cold took the fourth, Delgado, in the cave, eleven days in, while Elena held his hand and Marisol's mother rationed the last of the chocolate into pieces too small to taste. Elena told me this once, in the hospital in the capital, in a voice with no drama in it at all, and I have never been able to put it down since. Some things you carry the way you carry a compass bearing. You don't decide to. You just don't lose it.

The inquiry had asked me, at one point, why the survey team had covered up the deaths instead of signaling for rescue. I'd said what Elena said: because they'd found the mine, and the ledgers, and they understood—correctly, as it turned out—that a rescue party might arrive with different orders. Because staying hidden was the only way any of them stayed alive. Because in the Cordillera, sometimes the bravest thing a person can do is nothing, loudly, for weeks.

The commissioners had written this down without expression. Bureaucrats are the only people I know who can hear a ghost story and take minutes.

* * *

I'd built the wreath myself, which was a mistake, because I am many things and none of them is a florist.

Marisol had helped. She'd come to the strip the evening before with armloads of ichu grass and the last of the high-country flowers—the small purple ones that grow out of rock scree and have no business being as stubborn as they are—and we'd woven them around a frame of willow withes while Tomás watched from the fuel shed and offered unsolicited criticism.

"Too loose," he said. "It'll come apart in the slipstream."

"It's a wreath, Tomás, not cargo."

"Everything is cargo," he said, "if you love it enough to worry about it."

This is the kind of thing Tomás says now. Since the pass, since the cave, since the day he stood in a courtroom beside his brother-in-law and told the truth about what he'd done as a young man—carried the forged papers, guided the men who seized the claims, kept silent for thirty years while the mountain kept his shame—since all that, he has developed a habit of saying things that sound like wisdom and daring you to call him on it. I let him have it. A man who confesses to a nation has earned the right to be occasionally profound.

He'd come with me this morning, too, as far as the valley. He sat in the right seat with his hands on his knees, wearing the same canvas coat he'd worn above the snowline, looking out the window at terrain he knew better than any map, and he didn't say anything for two hours, which from Tomás is a eulogy in itself.

"There," he said finally, as the valley opened beneath us. "The shrine shelf. You can see it still."

You could. The rockfall that had half-buried it was still there, and the ledge above it, and the scree slope where we'd dug out the tin box with our bare hands while the first snow came down around us like a curtain being drawn. The box was in a courthouse vault now. The mountain had kept it thirty years and given it up in an afternoon, and I have decided not to have an opinion about which of them is more reliable.

I came around over the riverbar, into the wind, the way you're supposed to, and slowed her down until she was barely flying—hanging on her prop, the way Roldán would have hated—and Tomás opened the window and we dropped the wreath.

It fell the way everything falls in the mountains: slowly at first, then all at once, the willow frame tumbling, the ichu grass streaming, the purple flowers holding on with the stubbornness of their kind. It landed on the riverbar, on the exact place where the survey tents had stood, and it did not come apart, and I heard Tomás make a small satisfied sound that I will charitably interpret as grief and not vindication.

We circled once. Just once. I wanted to do more, but there's a thing about the dead in high country—you honor them by leaving, not by staying. Staying is how you join them.

"Oyarzún," Tomás said, as we banked out. "Cifuentes. Delgado." He said each name the way you set a stone.

"Elena lived," I said. "Marisol's mother lived. That's not nothing."

"It's not nothing," he agreed. "It's also not them."

And that was Tomás all over—refusing the arithmetic that lets you feel better. We flew south in silence, and I let the valley shrink in the window until it was just another fold in the range, holding what it holds.

* * *

Puerto Frío was next, and the schoolbooks, and then the long leg home with the light going gold behind the western ridges.

I flew it thinking about debt.

I'd burned mine in the capital square in December—my family's debt note, the leverage the riders' paymaster had bought to keep me quiet, twenty years of interest and threat that I fed into a coffee can and lit with a match while half the market watched. I'd expected to feel free. What I actually felt, standing there while the paper curled and the smoke went up, was something closer to vertigo—the specific dizziness of a person who has been bracing against a weight so long that when it lifts, she staggers.

But it passed. That's the thing nobody tells you about freedom: it's mostly a matter of the body recalibrating. For the first month I kept reaching for the fear the way you reach for a handrail on stairs that aren't there. Then one morning in February I woke up and it was simply gone, and I lay in bed for a while listening to the absence, and then I got up and went to the hearings, because that was what my days were for now.

The hearings are still going. They'll go on for years—forty families, forty claims, forty sets of boundaries to be walked and measured and argued over by men in the capital who have never walked anything steeper than a courthouse stair. Some families will get their land back whole. Some will get pieces. Some, like mine, will get land that has been worked by strangers for a generation, and will have to decide what a deed is worth against a neighbor's planted fields. There's no version of this where everybody comes out clean. Justice, I've learned, isn't a verdict. It's a chore. It's a thing you do with your hands, season after season, and it never fully finishes, and that's not a flaw in it. That's just what it is.

Aurelio Sanch is in the penitentiary at Cusco Alta now. He surrendered voluntarily, testified to every forgery, named every name, and asked for nothing. The judges gave him fifteen years, which he received the way he seemed to receive everything, with the mild attention of a man who had already served a much longer sentence somewhere above the snowline. He wrote to Tomás once, in the spring. One page. Tomás read it at my kitchen table, put it down, and said, "He wants to know if the alders took."

They took. I told him so. It seemed like enough.

Elena Vasco walks with a stick now and has stopped pretending she won't always. She's been appointed to the commission that's redrawing the survey boundaries—properly, this time, with the families at the table—and she runs it with the same flat competence she'd used to keep three people alive in a cave through a Cordilleran winter. She told me once, over coffee, that she dreams about the valley. "Not nightmares," she said. "Just the valley. Being there." I said I knew what she meant. I dream about it too. I think everyone who was up there does. The mountain gets into you like altitude. It takes a while to come down.

And Marisol—Marisol is studying law. She announced it at the airstrip in January, in the same tone she'd used for everything since the day she'd walked into my hangar with a copy of a deed fragment and a jaw like a door slamming: as a fact, not a request. "Someone in this family," she said, "is going to know what the papers say before they sign them." She'll be terrible at it, in the best way. She'll be the kind of lawyer who makes the comfortable people uncomfortable, and the Cordillera has been waiting for one of those for thirty years.

* * *

The last leg home, I flew low over the river, because the light was doing that thing it does in late afternoon, turning the water to hammered copper, and because home is the one place you're allowed to arrive slowly.

The strip was empty when I came in—my strip, the one I'd leased off the same families whose claims had just been restored, which makes me, by one legal reading, a tenant of my own grandfather's land, and by another, exactly where I always belonged. I flared, touched, rolled out, and shut her down, and the silence after the engine is always the same: enormous, ringing, full of everything the flight had been too loud to hear.

Tomás was there, of course. He'd come down on the mail truck an hour behind me, because Tomás does not fly if he can avoid it, and he was sitting on the fuel drums with a thermos, watching the ridgeline the way old guides watch ridgelines, which is to say not really watching them at all, just keeping the habit of it.

"Well?" he said.

"Well what?"

"Did it come apart?"

"It didn't come apart."

He nodded, satisfied, and poured me coffee I didn't ask for and absolutely wanted. We drank it sitting on the drums while the light went out of the valley, and neither of us said anything for a long time, which is the kind of conversation I've come to value most.

Then he said, "Next month, the boundary walk. The Quintana parcel. They'll want the heir present."

"I'll be there."

"And the Vásquez parcel after. Marisol will want to walk it herself."

"She'll want to walk it at a pace that kills us both."

"Yes," Tomás said, with something dangerously close to fondness. "She's her mother's daughter."

The stars were coming out over the Cordillera, the way they do—sudden, total, like a ledger being opened—and I looked up at them and then south, to where the high valley was, invisible now, holding its wreath and its dead and its tin box's empty ghost, keeping what it keeps.

I used to think my compass pointed at home because home was where I kept the plane. I know better now. It points there because home is a thing you carry, the way you carry a bearing—through weather, through doubt, through the long white stretches where nothing confirms you're right and you go on anyway. Forty families are carrying it now. Some of them are walking their boundaries this very season, planting stakes in ground their grandparents were robbed of, and every one of those stakes is a compass point.

I drained my coffee, and Tomás gathered the thermos, and we walked to the hangar together under the stars, two survivors of a story that mostly isn't over, arguing amiably about whether the alders would need fencing before the goats found them.

They will. Everything in the mountains needs fencing, or tending, or carrying, or mourning. That's not a burden.

That's the inheritance.
