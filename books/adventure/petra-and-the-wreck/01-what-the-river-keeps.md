## 1. What the River Keeps

The river did not want her there. Ulyana Halloran felt it in the way the current pressed against her shoulders like a hand, in the way the silt rose at her approach, blinding and resentful, as though the water had spent forty years keeping its secret and resented her intrusion. She hung at nine meters, weight belt snug, regulator hissing, and let the gray-green murk settle around her like a curtain drawn by a patient hand.

Below her, the wreck of the *Vespera* lay on its starboard side in the channel's deepest cut, half-buried, its hull furred with zebra mussels and a soft black sediment that swallowed light. The navy charts called it a cargo scow lost in a storm, nothing more. But the navy had also closed the site within a day of the sonar survey, posted a patrol boat with orders to turn back civilian divers, and filed the whole affair under a classification number that made no sense for a rusted barge. Ulyana had been running salvage contracts on this river for eleven years. She knew what it meant when men in pressed uniforms got nervous about mud.

She also knew she shouldn't be here. The contract money was gone — spent, or rather never paid, after the navy voided every permit in the district three weeks ago. What she had left was stubbornness and a dive plan sketched on the back of an invoice, and the particular hunger of a woman who had already lost everything else worth losing.

Her brother Tomas had drowned too, in a manner of speaking. Not in this river — in a border war four hundred kilometers east, when he was twenty-two and she was nineteen. They'd sent her a folded flag and a letter with a stamp she still kept in a drawer, and the words *killed in action, body not recovered*, which was the army's polite way of saying they didn't know and weren't going to look. For eleven years she had dived rivers and lakes and one cold stretch of sea, pulling up other people's losses for other people's insurance claims, and some part of her had always been looking down into the dark for him.

The wreck's cargo hold gaped where the plating had torn away. She swam in sideways, lamp beam cutting a cone through the suspended silt. Crates, long rotted to slurry. A winch drum. And against the inner bulkhead, wedged beneath a collapsed shelf, something that was not cargo.

It was a chest. Iron-banded, sealed with wax that had gone the color of old blood, and stamped on its lid with a seal she recognized from her grandfather's stories but had never seen outside of a museum: the double-headed eagle of Veloria, the border nation that had ceased to exist before she was born — erased, redrawn, renamed, its archives scattered or burned. The navy hadn't classified a sunken scow. They'd classified this.

Her heart was hammering against the mouthpiece. She worked the chest free with a pry bar, muscles burning in the cold, and got a lifting bag on it. It came up slow, heavier than it looked, as though the river were reluctant, as though it were handing over something it had only ever meant to keep.

* * *

The surface was white noise and wind. She surfaced behind the breakwater at the old quarry dock, half a kilometer downcurrent from where the patrol boat sat its lazy circuit, and hauled the chest onto the stones with hands so numb she could barely feel the iron bands. The sky was the color of dishwater. Somewhere inland, a train sounded its horn, long and low.

She should log it. That was the rule, her own rule: find, record, report. Eleven years of discipline. Instead she knelt on the wet stones and looked at the seal, and thought about classification numbers, and broke the wax with her dive knife.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: paper. Bundles of it. Letters, dozens of them, tied with cord, and beneath them a small tin box holding photographs and a child's wooden horse with one ear chipped.

She untied the first bundle with fingers that shook, and the cold stopped mattering entirely.

The handwriting hit her before the words did. She knew that hand the way you know a voice in a dark room — the slanted capitals, the way Tomas crossed his sevens, the little hook he put on the letter *r* because a teacher had once shamed him for his penmanship and he'd spent a summer correcting it out of spite. She had letters from him from basic training. She had read them until the folds wore through.

The date on the first page was six years after his death.

*Dear Lena,* it began — her childhood name, the one only family used — *I don't know if these will ever reach you, and I've stopped expecting anything. I'm writing anyway, because the schoolmaster here says writing is how we refuse to disappear...*

She read standing, then kneeling, then sitting in the wet gravel without noticing she'd sat. He had crossed the border. He had survived — not escaped, not hidden, but *lived*. There was a village called Zelenmoor. There was a schoolhouse with a leaking roof. There were children whose names he listed with obvious affection, a woman referred to only as M., a garden, winters, a life. Letter after letter, year after year, in his living hand, dated and dated and dated, while back home the army had sent her a flag and a lie.

Grief turned inside out. That was the only way she could describe it, later, when she tried: eleven years of mourning picked up and shaken like a snow globe, everything she'd built her stillness on suddenly swirling. He had been alive. He had written to her. He had grown older somewhere across a line on a map, teaching children their letters while she pulled corpses' belongings out of rivers and called it a living.

And none of it had reached her. Which meant someone had decided it wouldn't.

She sat with the tin box open on her knees. In the last photograph, Tomas stood in front of a whitewashed building, squinting into sun, a chalkboard under one arm. He was laughing at whoever held the camera. He looked like their mother. He looked happy. She touched his face with one cold fingertip, and the sound she made didn't have a name.

* * *

Dusk came down over the quarry like a lid. She rewrapped the letters, latched the chest, and stowed it under the tarp in the bed of her truck, beneath a false layer of chain and tackle. Her mind was moving fast now, past grief into the colder country beyond it: who else knew the chest existed? Why close the site? Who had ordered it buried — and buried how thoroughly?

She was cinching the last strap when she saw it.

Across the channel, on the far bank where the pines came down to the water, a figure stood among the trees. Too far to make out a face, wrong angle for headlights — just a shape, man-shaped, motionless, watching the dock. Watching her. Not the casual pause of a passerby; the stillness of it was wrong, deliberate, the stillness of someone who had been there a while.

Ulyana went very quiet, the way she did underwater. Slowly, casually, she pulled the tarp flat, climbed into the cab, and started the engine without looking toward the trees again. When she checked the mirror as she pulled out, the figure was gone — no movement, no crunch of gravel, just absence, as if the pines had opened and closed around him.

Her headlights swept the road home. Behind her the river ran dark and indifferent, keeping whatever else it kept. In the truck bed, under chains and canvas, her brother's voice rode in an iron box, dated years after his grave.

She drove faster than she needed to, and did not stop once, and at every intersection she caught herself checking the mirrors — because the watcher on the bank had raised one hand as she'd left. Not a wave.

A salute.
