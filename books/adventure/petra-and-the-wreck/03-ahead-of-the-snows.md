## 3. Ahead of the Snows

The pass had a name on Ilya's maps and no name in anyone's mouth, which was how he liked his passes. He called it the Kettle, because the walls rose on either side like iron and the wind came down the throat of it whistling, and by the time you reached the top you were boiled dry. He had drawn it twice in his old life — once for the army that owned him then, once for himself, in pencil, in a notebook he kept sewn into his coat lining — and both drawings agreed: the Kettle was passable until the first hard snow, and after that it was a grave with good acoustics.

They climbed it on the third day, with the sky already the color of dirty wool.

Ulyana carried the chest. She would not surrender it to a mule, would not let it ride strapped to anything that might bolt or fall or be taken from her at a river crossing, so she bore its weight herself in a frame pack that cut grooves into her shoulders she would feel for years. Forty pounds of sealed oak and iron, forty pounds of her brother's handwriting, riding against her spine like a second heart. When the trail steepened she bent nearly double under it, and Ilya walked behind her with one hand hovering near her belt, ready to catch, saying nothing, because he understood that some weights are not meant to be shared, only witnessed.

Petra Voss came up the mountain behind them at a distance of half a kilometer, and she was not alone.

* * *

They had seen the helicopters at dawn — two of them, black against the pale east, flying the valley floor in slow grid patterns like men searching a floorboard for a dropped coin. There was no mistaking what they searched for. Ulyana had stood in the frozen grass outside the waystation where they'd slept and watched them, and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very cold, the way it went when she was deep underwater and the light began to fail.

"They're not looking for us," Ilya had said. "Not exactly. They're looking for the chest. We're just the current address."

"That's worse," Ulyana said.

"Yes," Ilya agreed. "It is."

And Petra — Petra had been given the operation's endgame two nights before, in a burst transmission she read by red lamplight in a border post that smelled of kerosene and old cigarettes. *Recovery of materials is priority one. Personnel associated with materials are priority two. Disposition of priority two at commander's discretion.* She had read the sentence four times. It was written in the language of her profession, clean and bloodless, and she knew precisely what *disposition at discretion* meant, because she had written such sentences herself, for other people, in other mountains. She had sat with the paper in her hands and thought about a letter she should never have read — a letter dated eleven years after a funeral, describing snow falling on a schoolyard, describing children learning their letters — and she had folded the paper along its creases until it was small enough to swallow.

She had not radioed confirmation. That silence was already a kind of treason, small as a seed, and seeds grow.

* * *

The storm found them at the treeline.

It did not arrive; it assembled. Clouds came over the western ridgeline like an invading army, gray and organized, and the temperature fell ten degrees in as many minutes. Ilya stopped, tasted the air, and said one word: "Now." They moved faster after that, Ulyana hunched under her burden, breath tearing white from her mouth, boots punching through the thin crust into the powder beneath. The chest rode her back and she talked to it without meaning to — *almost there, almost there, hold on* — the way she used to talk to her dive tanks, the way her mother used to talk to the stove.

Above them the Kettle narrowed. The walls closed in, sheer rock furred with ice, and the trail became a shelf barely wide enough for two. Below, the valley they had climbed out of filled with cloud like water filling a basin. Ilya led now, reading the mountain the way other men read newspapers, and twice he stopped and put his bare hand flat against the snowpack and frowned at whatever it told him.

"Bad?" Ulyana asked the second time.

"The slope above us is loaded," he said. "It's been waiting all winter for an excuse. Keep your voice down."

So they climbed in silence, three figures on a white shelf, and half a kilometer below and closing, six figures in gray, moving with the unhurried competence of people who had done this in worse places.

* * *

It happened at the narrows.

Later, Petra could never reconstruct the sequence — only the fragments, the way a wreck diver surfaces with pieces. A crack, deep and wrong, like a bone breaking inside the mountain itself. The whole slope above the trail shuddering, shrugging, beginning to move. Her team leader shouting *rope, rope, rope*. And then the world going white and enormous and loud beyond sound, the avalanche coming off the upper bowl not as snow but as a decision, tons of it, erasing the trail, the shelf, the argument between the mountain and everything standing on it.

It took her team. She saw them go — saw Vasik turn and reach, saw the white wall take him mid-stride, saw the others scattered like letters from a torn envelope. And it took her, but not all the way. The edge of it caught her, spun her, and threw her down-slope into a couloir choked with debris, and then the mountain settled into its new shape and the silence afterward was total, ringing, absolute.

She was buried to the chest. Her left arm was free; her right was pinned at a bad angle; her radio was somewhere under the snow that pressed against her ribs with every breath. The cold came through her gear immediately, patient and professional, and she understood with a strange calm that she had perhaps twenty minutes, maybe thirty, before the cold finished its paperwork.

She shouted. The mountain ate the sound.

* * *

Ulyana heard the avalanche from above the narrows and turned to look back into a valley that no longer contained a trail. Where the gray figures had been there was now a smooth white scar, beautiful the way wounds are beautiful, smoking faintly in the cold.

Ilya grabbed her arm. "We can't—"

"I know."

"We can't," he said again, gentler. "That woman has spent three days trying to put us in the ground. Every hour we stand here, the net closes. You have the letters. You have the only copies of the truth in the world riding on your back. Go."

Ulyana stood in the wind and looked down at the smooth white scar, and every instinct she owned — the diver's instinct, the survivor's instinct, the sister's instinct, sharpened by grief into something almost like a blade — told her to go. The navy had killed her brother once on paper. This woman had come to kill him a second time, by burying the proof he'd lived.

She went down anyway.

She found Petra by sound — a faint scraping, rhythmic, deliberate, someone digging with one hand. Ulyana dug her out with her bare fingers and the edge of Ilya's entrenching tool while Ilya stood watch above them, cursing softly in two languages, watching the upper slopes. Petra came free in stages, gasping, her right arm hanging wrong, her lips already graying, and when her eyes focused on Ulyana's face the first thing she said was:

"Why."

"Not here," Ulyana said. "Can you walk?"

Petra tried. She made forty meters before her legs quit, and after that they took turns carrying her, Ulyana taking most of it, the chest still on her back, the officer across her shoulders like a sack of grain, climbing through the gathering dark while the storm arrived in earnest and erased the world behind them stroke by stroke.

* * *

They reached the shelter cave near midnight — a shallow overhang Ilya remembered from his mapping days, floored with dry scree, walled against the wind. They got a stove lit. They stripped Petra's frozen outer layers and wrapped her in everything they had, and Ilya splinted her arm with a ski pole and a bandana, working quickly, not meeting her eyes.

Her radio had survived. It was in her inner pocket, cracked but intact, and it lay against her hip now like a second splint, and she could feel its weight the way Ulyana must feel the chest's.

Through the storm-static she could raise Command. One transmission. *Team lost in avalanche. Subjects presumed dead in same. No further pursuit possible this season.* Two sentences. A lie. The first lie of her career, nineteen years of unblemished service ending in a snow cave with two strangers and a stolen box of letters.

Or the truth. *Subjects alive, in my custody, location as follows.* Extraction by helicopter when weather lifted. Disposition at discretion.

She thought about Vasik, who had a daughter. She thought about the sentence written in bloodless language, and how easily she had once written such sentences herself. She thought about a man who had died as a schoolteacher instead of a soldier, and whose sister was asleep sitting up, ten feet away, her arms around a wooden chest like a child around a dog.

Petra took out the radio. Her hands shook — cold, she told herself, only cold.

She keyed the transmit switch, and in a voice flattened by static and exhaustion and something else, something new, she reported: avalanche, team lost, subjects lost, route impassable, requesting withdrawal of assets pending spring assessment. Request confirmed. Transmission ended.

She set the radio down. Across the cave, Ulyana was watching her. Had watched the whole time. Neither of them said anything, and the storm roared on outside like a sea, and somewhere below them, past the passes, past the borders, a newspaper office waited with its lights off for morning.

"We descend tomorrow," Ilya said quietly, into the dark. "If the weather holds."

It did not hold. But they went anyway.
