## 1. The River Keeps What It Takes

The river had been wrong since dawn, and Isandro Ishikawa trusted it the way other men trusted their mothers.

He stood at the lip of Kestrel Gorge with his boots planted on wet limestone, watching the water move beneath him with the particular attention he gave to nothing else in the world. The Kestrel was supposed to run green this time of year—glacier-fed, glassy in the slow stretches, loud and white in the drops. Instead it ran the color of old tea, brown and heavy, swollen past its banks in places where the trail hugged the stone. It moved with a new authority, like something that had been given permission.

"You're staring again," said Ferro, behind him, shifting the weight of his pack. Ferro was one of the three clients Isandro was walking across the border this week, a soft-bellied man with soft hands who had paid too much and asked too little, which in Isandro's experience meant he was carrying something worth more than either.

"The river's high," Isandro said.

"Rivers get high."

"Not this river. Not in the dry season. Not brown." He crouched, touched two fingers to the current where it eddied against a boulder, and brought them to his nose. Silt, yes—but under it something else. A mineral smell, sharp and cold, like the inside of a locked room. And beneath that, faintly, the sweet-rot stink of things that had drowned and were still drowning.

He said nothing else about it. That was the discipline. In eleven years of walking people through unmarked passes, Isandro had learned that fear was a contagion and a guide was its first carrier. So he drank from his flask, checked the sky—clear, hard blue, no weather anywhere near them—and said, "Two hours to the crossing. We move fast and we don't stop at the water."

They moved. The gorge narrowed and the river rose beside them, chewing at the trail in places, and twice Isandro chose routes that added distance rather than trust the flooded path. Ferro complained. The other two, a brother and sister with the flat patience of professionals, said nothing and watched Isandro's face for the tells he didn't give them.

It was on the second hour that he saw the first of the things the river carried.

A door. A whole door, varnished blue, still on its hinges, turning slowly in an eddy like a thing deciding which way to go. He watched it a long moment. Then, fifty meters on, a shuttered window frame. Then a roof beam, black with water, trailing what looked like thatch. Then—this was the one that put a cold wire down his spine—a wooden cradle, small, empty, rocking gently against a rock shelf before the current took it again.

Nobody said anything. Ferro had gone quiet in the way of men doing arithmetic they don't like. The sister looked at Isandro once, a question in her face, and he answered it with a small shake of his head: *not weather, not here, not from any valley I know.*

Because that was the thing. There was no valley upstream. Not anymore. The Kestrel rose in the high country, and the high country had been closed for as long as Isandro had been walking these mountains—closed by decree, closed by checkpoints, closed by the particular silence of a place removed from maps. The drowned valleys of Vessia lay up there, or what had been the drowned valleys, flooded forty years ago behind a dam no one was allowed to name, in a nation that no longer appeared on any atlas he'd ever been able to buy. He had grown up two ranges over, and even as a boy he had understood that the upstream country was a direction you did not point at in company.

The river was carrying a village down out of that silence.

* * *

He delivered his clients at dusk on the far side, at the stone shepherd's hut that marked the safe edge of his territory. Ferro pressed an envelope into his hand with damp fingers and asked, too casually, whether the water level would hold for his return trip in the spring. Isandro told him the mountain would decide, and watched the three of them descend toward the low towns until the dark took them.

Then he did the thing he always did after a delivery, the thing that had kept him alive through two blacklists and one border incident that had never happened the way the tribunals said it happened.

He went back.

Not for sentiment. A guide who left things behind on his routes left witnesses, and witnesses left trails. He moved fast through the blue hour, alone, his lamp off until full dark, and he meant only to sweep the trail and be gone by midnight. He meant to sleep in a different valley, as he always did, and never think about the blue door again.

The river had other ideas.

* * *

He smelled the shallows before he saw them—a broadening of the gorge where the Kestrel slowed and spread, depositing what it had grown tired of carrying. In the moonlight the water lay flat and brown and quiet as spilled coffee, and the debris field stretched along the gravel bar like the aftermath of a market day: planks, a chair, a child's rubber boot, a section of fence with wire still twisted in it. All of it waterlogged, all of it old, all of it wrong.

And snagged against a fallen pine at the bar's edge, half-sunk, sat a chest.

He should have walked away. He told himself that later, and it was true, and it had never once in his life stopped him. Isandro crouched at the waterline and studied it the way he studied a suspect snow slope: shape, weight, the way it sat against the current. It was a strongbox, iron-banded, and it was heavy—he could tell by how deep it rode even in the shallows, how the current broke white around it. It was not the kind of thing a flood carried by accident. It was the kind of thing a flood carried because someone had put it in the water, or because it had been stored somewhere the water had never been meant to reach.

He waded out. The cold went through his boots and into his bones immediately, glacier-cold, and the current tugged at his knees with more strength than the flat water suggested. He got a shoulder under it and lifted, and the chest came free with a reluctant suck of mud, and he carried it to the gravel bar and set it down and stood over it, dripping, breathing hard, feeling like a thief at his own graveside.

There was a seal on the lid.

He wiped the silt away with his sleeve, and the moon did the rest. Stamped into the lead—lead, he realized, lead-lined, which meant someone had wanted it to survive water, fire, and time—was a sunburst. A circle with rays, worked in a style too old and too formal for any of the border towns, and beneath it a word in a script he had never been taught and had no right to recognize and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a lullaby sung in a language you don't speak.

*Vessia.*

He sat down on the gravel. The river ran on beside him, brown and patient, and he found that his hands were not steady.

He knew the sunburst because his mother had known it. She had kept a tin at the back of a cupboard in the house above Serrano, and once—once, when he was nine, when he had pried it open expecting coins—there had been a cloth inside embroidered with this exact rayed circle, and his mother had taken it from him with a speed that had frightened him more than any anger ever had, and had said, in a voice he had never heard her use before or since, *Where did you find this?* And he had said, *It was in the cupboard,* and she had looked at him for a long moment with an expression he would spend thirty years not understanding, and then she had sewn the cupboard shut, and they had never spoken of it, and she had never spoken of much else, ever, not about where she came from, not about the years before Serrano, not about the father who was a shape in no photograph, and Isandro had learned early that some questions were doors his mother kept locked, and he had become, without ever deciding to, a man who kept moving.

The chest sat in the moonlight with its sunburst seal, and the river that had carried it down from the forbidden country ran beside him, and Isandro understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own death notice, that the water had not brought him a curiosity.

It had brought him an address.

Upstream. The flood had come from upstream, from the closed valleys, from the drowned nation, from the direction no one pointed at and no one was allowed to go—and something up there had broken open. A dam, a vault, a grave. The river was emptying a house forty years sealed, and it had chosen, out of everything it carried, to set this one box at his feet.

He worked the seal with his knife, and stopped, and looked at the water for a long time.

Then he wrapped the chest in his ground tarp, shouldered it—it weighed as much as a wounded man—and climbed out of the gorge under the stars, walking fast, not because he had a destination but because staying still had suddenly become the most dangerous thing he could do.

Behind him, the Kestrel kept running brown, and kept bringing down pieces of a country that did not exist, and somewhere in the low towns, Isandro would learn too soon, men were already asking questions about the flood, and about the seal, and about a mountain guide who had once had a mother who sewed her cupboards shut.
