## 2. A Name in Wax

The chest sat where he'd left it, half-buried in river gravel at the mouth of his tent, and all day it had watched him.

Isandro told himself that was nonsense. Lead didn't watch. But he'd spent the morning re-hanging the rope bridge over the secondary channel with his hands on the wire and his eyes drifting back to camp, to the dull grey gleam of it among the cook pots and coiled line, out of place as a tombstone in a kitchen. The sunburst seal on its lid had caught the light once, briefly, like something blinking.

He worked until the shadows of the gorge walls climbed the eastern face and turned blue, then violet, then nothing. He ate standing up. He banked the fire small, the way he always did—small fires, short nights, no valley twice—and then he sat down across from the chest the way you sit down across from a dog you suspect of biting.

The river spoke behind him. It had been speaking wrong for three days now: too full, too brown, carrying its cargo of impossible things. Fence posts from farms that weren't on any map he guided through. A child's boot, small enough to cup in one hand. A door, once, whole and painted yellow, swinging slowly downstream like a man wading with his arms out. The Kestrel took things—that was its nature—but it took them from places along its length. This was different. This was the river arriving already full, already grieving, from somewhere upstream where no one was allowed to go.

He'd told himself flood, landslide, seasonal melt. He'd told himself a lot of things today.

Now, in the dark, with only the coals for company, he ran his thumb along the seal's edge. Wax, black as tar, pressed deep into the seam of the lid, and under the wax a lead lip folded over itself—the kind of closure you needed shears for, or patience, or a reason. The sunburst stamped into it was worn but legible: sixteen rays, and at the center a circle that wasn't quite a circle, more an eye, or a well viewed from above.

Vessia. Everyone knew the word the way everyone knew the word for plague. The drowned nation. The valleys behind the Meridian Dam that had gone under the water forty years ago, taking eleven towns and their church bells and their graveyards and, eventually, their names. There were no Vessians anymore, officially. There were reservoir maps, and there was a fishery, and there was a memorial plaque at the overlook that tourists photographed without reading.

Isandro had never thought about Vessia. That was not an accident; it was a discipline. He did not think about borders either, or tribunals, or the word *incident*, or the particular way his mother had gone quiet whenever anyone upstream was mentioned. His whole life was built on the principle that some doors stay shut if you never touch the handle.

He touched the handle.

The wax cracked with a sound like a knuckle. Underneath, the lead gave to the pry bar he used for pitons, folding back in a curl, and cold air came out of the chest—not stale air but *cold* air, river-cold, as though the thing had been breathing water until this morning and hadn't learned yet how to breathe anything else.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were papers. Dozens of them. Bundles tied with cord, folders swollen and dried into waves, a leather ledger whose cover had bloomed white with mold in the shape of continents. And on top, laid flat as if placed last, deliberately, by a hand that wanted it found first: a single sheet under glassine, yellowed, typed on a machine with a failing letter E.

He held it up to the coals.

It was a birth record. Registry format, the old Vessian civil style—he knew the format the way he knew knot formats, from documents he'd ferried for men who paid in silence. Name of child. Name of mother. Place of birth. Date.

The date was his.

The name of the child was his own, spelled correctly, which was somehow worse than if it had been wrong. Isandro Ishikawa, born in the town of Velhava, District Nine, Vessia—born four months before the dam came down. Four months before the water arrived and erased the word *Velhava* from every map that mattered.

And the name of the mother was Mireia Ishikawa, née Osta, of Velhava.

His hands were steady. He noticed that about himself the way you notice a strange sound in your own house—with a kind of detached alarm. His hands should not have been steady. His mother's name looked up at him off government paper, in a government hand, filed in a government archive of a country that officially did not contain her, and his hands were steady because some part of him had been waiting thirty years for exactly this and had trained itself, without telling him, not to shake.

*She never said.*

He made himself think it plainly, because his whole life he'd been good at not thinking things plainly, and look where plainness had gotten the chest: open.

Mireia Osta, who sang songs in a language she claimed was just "mountain talk." Who cooked a festival dish every spring that she called "my grandmother's" and would not say whose grandmother. Who, when he was nine and asked her where she was born, had looked at him for a long moment with an expression he had spent decades filing under *tiredness*, and said: *In a place that isn't there anymore, little one. Eat your soup.* He had laughed. He had eaten his soup. He had been raised, without ever being taught, in the family craft of looking away.

And the border incident—the tribunal, the blacklist, the two countries that had closed their passes to him—had come when he was twenty-six, guiding a party through a corridor he'd walked fifty times, when shots he never heard were reported from a checkpoint he never reached, and a manifest he never signed bore a signature that was almost his. Almost. Like his name on this page. Correctly spelled. Filed by other hands.

He set the birth record down very carefully, as if it were sleeping.

Then he pulled the bundles toward him and opened the first one, and understood what the chest actually was. Testimony. Page after page of sworn statements, depositions, carbon copies of letters between engineers and ministers, marked CONFIDENTIAL in stamps so faded they were becoming ghosts. Names of men who had signed off on water levels. Dates. Numbers. One document repeated a phrase that snagged in him like a barb: *the reservoir will fill regardless of relocation timelines.* Regardless. As if the towns were weather.

He read until the coals went to red eyes and then to ash-veins, and the gorge got so quiet he could hear the river thinking.

* * *

The radio woke him at 04:10 with Dario's voice coming through static like a man shouting up a well.

"Kestrel station, Kestrel station, you reading? Isandro, pick up, I know you're up there."

He rolled out of the bag and keyed the handset. "Reading. This better be a weather report."

"It's a people report." Static chewed the edges of Dario's voice. "I'm in Low Ferren. There are strangers here. Not the usual buyers—no one's buying, that's the thing, the passes are shut till melt-off. These ones aren't buying anything. They're asking."

"Asking what?"

"About you." A pause long enough for the static to fill with teeth. "Two men and a woman, decent boots, decent gear, money that doesn't match their clothes. Showing a photograph. They're asking about a guide named Ishikawa, and they're asking about salvage." Another pause. "They're asking about salvage out of the river, Iso. Anything washed down. Sealed containers."

The fire was dead. The tent was dark. Somewhere in that darkness, inches from his knee, the chest sat with its curled-back lip of lead and its breath of cold water, and Isandro became aware of it the way you become aware of a wound—suddenly, totally, the whole body rerouting around the fact of it.

"How sure are you they mean me?" he said. "Ishikawa isn't rare."

"They had your year of birth, man." Dario's voice dropped, went almost gentle, which from Dario was more frightening than shouting. "Your exact year. And they had a description of your mother."

The handset felt suddenly very heavy, or his arm very light. Outside, the river ran on in the dark, brown and burdened, bringing things down from a place that wasn't there anymore.

"Dario," he said. "Who sent them?"

"If I knew that I'd have led with it. Nobody official. Official people carry paperwork. These ones carry cash and don't write anything down." Static surged and fell. "Look—I can be up there in two days with Sefa and the boys. Say the word. Whatever you've got in that camp, maybe it shouldn't be in that camp."

"I don't have anything," Isandro said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

A laugh came down the wire, thin and wrong. "Yeah. You always were the worst liar on the crew. Two days. Keep your fire small." The channel clicked, hissed, went to the long empty tone.

He sat with the handset in his lap while the sky over the gorge rim went from black to iron. The strangers had his birth year. They had his mother's description. Which meant someone, somewhere, had opened a file that had been closed for forty years—or had never let it close—and had started walking it upstream, town by town, following the same current the chest had ridden down.

The past he had outrun for thirty years had finally gotten an address. It had taken its time. It had read the river right, the way he would have: everything the water takes, it delivers.

He looked at the chest. He looked at the birth record sleeping under its glassine, at his mother's maiden name, at the town that wasn't there, at the word *regardless*. Then he began, methodically, in the grey pre-dawn, to pack—rope, stove, the oilcloth bundles, the ledger with its mold-white continents—because whatever was coming up the valley had started downstream, and downstream was no longer a direction he trusted.

By full light he had struck camp entirely, leaving the gravel scuffed and cold, and was standing at the treeline with sixty pounds on his back and the chest wrapped in canvas, looking down at the river road where, very far off, two figures had appeared, moving upstream.

Not hurrying. People who hurry are people who might lose you. These walked like the outcome was already decided.

Isandro turned uphill, toward the passes, and began to climb.
