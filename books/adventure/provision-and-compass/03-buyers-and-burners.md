## 3. Buyers and Burners

The lamp in Wren Osei's workroom had a stutter in it, a half-second hesitation every time she switched it on, as if the light itself were considering whether to tell the truth. She had learned to work in that flicker. Most of her life was spent in the space between what a document claimed and what it actually was, and she had come to think of that gap as a kind of country—a borderland where she held citizenship but no rights.

The item on her bench tonight was a bill of lading from the port at Sarnholt, dated forty-one years ago, purporting to list grain shipments out of a valley that no longer appeared on any map. The paper was right. The ink was right. The watermark showed the twin-tower mill that had stood on the Sarnholt quay until the fire of '09. Everything about it was authentic except its reason for existing, because the valley it shipped *from*—Vessia—had been drowned, erased, unmade so thoroughly that even the grain merchants had been paid to forget they'd ever loaded barges there.

Wren pressed her loupe to the signature and felt the old sickness rise in her throat. Not disgust. Recognition.

She had once been the youngest senior conservator in the Halvard Archive, the woman who could date a rag-paper fiber by its tooth alone. Then she had authenticated a set of land grants for a client whose name turned out to belong to a man who used them to evict four hundred families from a river delta, and when the scandal broke, the Archive had discovered that its shining young archivist had taken private commissions for six years. They did not ask why a junior researcher with her salary had needed the money. They did not ask about her mother's medical bills, or the letters Wren had written to the oversight board that went unanswered. They asked for her keys, and she gave them, and after that she worked the other side of the river: authenticating looted documents for dealers who never asked where things came from, because not asking was the entire business model.

*Buyers and burners,* her old mentor used to say. *Every archive serves one or the other eventually.* He'd meant it as a joke. It had stopped being funny around the time she realized which one she'd become.

The bill of lading was a test piece, she understood now. Someone upstream was moving a collection—something large, something sealed—and this single sheet had been let loose into the market like a drop of dye into water, to see who would notice the color. And Wren noticed. Because the lading wasn't just Vessian. It bore the counterstamp of the Office of Public Testimony, the body that had spent the last years before the flood collecting sworn accounts from valley residents about the dam, the cracks in it, the engineers' warnings, the men who came down from the capital and told everyone the water was safe.

Testimony meant witnesses. Witnesses meant names. Names meant that somewhere, someone had decided the dead should be allowed to speak—and someone else would pay anything to keep them silent.

She set down the loupe. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Outside the workroom window, the low town of Corren's Landing stank of fish meal and diesel, and beyond it the Kestrel ran high and brown with snowmelt and something else, something that had come loose upstream where no one was allowed to go.

Wren had spent three days tracing the dealer who'd shown her the lading, and three more tracing the rumor behind it: a chest, lead-lined, seal intact, pulled from the shallows by a mountain guide with a blacklisted name. Isandro Ishikawa. Two countries wanted him gone; every smuggler in the gorge knew his routes; and he was currently camped, according to a boy who sold him rope, above the old quarry road with a fire small enough to hide from satellites.

She packed her bag with the tools of her former life—calipers, fiber tweezers, a loupe, a camera with no network chip—and one tool of her current life: a pistol she hoped never to touch. Then she wrote a note for her landlord, in case it mattered later, saying nothing at all, which was its own kind of testimony.

* * *

She found his camp by the smell of juniper smoke and the discipline of it. No fire ring twice in the same shape. Bedroll positioned against stone so body heat reflected back. A man who had made invisibility into a craft, and who heard her thirty meters out, because when she crested the last granite shelf he was already standing with a knife held low and casual, the way you hold a tool you've used often enough to stop performing with it.

"That's far enough," he said.

"I know who has the chest," Wren said.

The knife didn't move. His eyes did—one quick flick toward the shadowed hollow behind him where, she guessed, the lead box sat wrapped in oilcloth, and then back to her, reassessing. He was younger than his reputation. Tired in the specific way of people who sleep in shifts against their own nerves. There was river mud dried on his boots, brown water stains up to the knee, and she thought: *he went back for it alone, at night, after delivering whatever cargo bought his bread.*

"Everyone knows who has the chest," he said. "I have the chest."

"No." She kept her voice level, her hands visible. "You have what's *in* the chest. What I know is who else will have it, soon, unless you move first. The Meridian Compact. Cartographic office, humanitarian mandate, very quiet, very well funded. They're the ones who redrew the maps after the flood. They're the ones who bought the grain merchants' silence—" she nodded at the memory of the lading "—and they are already in Corren's Landing asking after a guide named Ishikawa."

Something moved behind his face. Not surprise. Confirmation. Whatever warning had reached him, hers fit it like a key fits the lock it was cut for.

"Who are you?"

"Wren Osei. I authenticate documents. Stolen ones, mostly. I'm good enough that I can tell you your chest is real—not a plant, not a forgery seeded to flush you out—because I've handled Vessian provenance before, and the wax on that seal was poured over a cord of Vessian hemp, and nobody counterfeits hemp." She crouched slowly, showing empty hands, and sat back on her heels like a traveler resting. "I'm also good enough to tell you what happens next. The Compact doesn't send soldiers for paper. They send clerks. They'll offer you money, then a new name, then they'll offer the people around you trouble, and if all of that fails, there will be an accident on a mountain, and it will be very sad, and the file will close."

"You sound like you've read their mail."

"I've authenticated their burn piles." The words came out flatter than she intended, and she let them. Let him see the scar tissue. "Twice. Charred fragments, reassembled, sold to collectors as curiosities. Both times Vessian. Both times testimony-shaped. I didn't understand what I was looking at until the third shipment came through and I finally read the fiber instead of the ink."

The wind came up the gorge and carried the smell of the river—that wrong, swollen smell, silt and pine pulp and something mineral, like the mountain bleeding. Isandro looked past her, downstream, the way a man looks at a debt.

"Why are you here?" he said. "Not to steal it. You'd have tried while I slept."

"Because I spent ten years helping looters put prices on other people's histories, and I told myself it was just paper, just authentication, just a skill." She stood, brushing grit from her knees. "Then a chest floats out of a drowned country with my whole profession's crime scene inside it, and it lands in the hands of the one man alive with a blood claim to open it. That's not coincidence. That's a door. I'm tired of being on the wrong side of doors."

He studied her for a long moment. She watched him do the arithmetic smugglers do—what she cost, what she carried, what she knew that he didn't. She could almost hear the ledger turning.

"The documents name me," he said at last. Quietly, like a confession to the dark. "My mother. My birth. Vessia." His jaw tightened. "I need someone who can tell me if that's true, or if someone built a beautiful lie and floated it down a river to catch me."

"It's true," Wren said. "I'd stake what's left of my name on the wax alone. But I'll verify every page, because if we're going to do this, we do it clean. No forgery claims. No provenance disputes. Nothing for them to burn the truth with."

"Do what?" he said. But his hand had lowered the knife, and his eyes had gone upstream, toward the source, and she knew he'd already answered himself.

"We take it to the light," she said. "All of it. Before the buyers get there—or the burners."

He laughed once, without humor, a short bark that the gorge swallowed. "You know what's upstream? Checkpoints. Water that kills. A country that doesn't exist anymore except in the papers you want me to carry through it."

"And you know what's downstream," Wren said. "I've lived there. It's worse."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the river filled the silence, running high and brown and full of everything it had taken. Then Isandro crouched, pulled back the oilcloth in the hollow behind him, and let the firelight find the sunburst stamped in the lead—the same sunburst that had been struck from every atlas, every textbook, every gravestone registry in two nations.

"Radio said strangers in the low towns," he murmured, mostly to himself. "Dario warned me yesterday." He looked up at her, and there was something raw under the wariness now, something that might have been hope wearing armor. "How long do we have?"

Wren thought of the test-piece lading, the dye already spreading through the market. Thought of clerks with soft voices and hard ledgers booking passage north.

"A week," she said. "Maybe less. So we leave tonight."

Far below them, the Kestrel kept pulling at its banks, patient as a creditor, carrying its stolen cargo toward the sea—and somewhere upstream, in the dark where the river began, something waited that both of them had spent their whole lives running from: the truth, in writing, with names attached.
