## 4. What Dario Owes

The radio woke him before dawn, crackling through the static the way Dario's voice always had—half signal, half grin.

*Ozzy. Three days out. Bringing company. Don't shoot.*

Isandro lay in the dark of the lean-to and listened to the river. It had not gone back to sleep since the chest came down. Even at this hour it ran loud, brown-throated, muttering about things it had swallowed upstream. He'd buried the documents again under the floor stones, wrapped in oilcloth, but he could feel them the way a man feels a cracked rib: not visible, just present, every breath reminding him something inside was wrong.

Three days later they came up the goat track from the south, single file, and Isandro watched them from the ridge with binoculars older than some of his clients. Dario first, broad as ever, gray shot through his beard now, walking like a man who owned the ground instead of renting it. Behind him, Petros and little Anselm, twins of trouble from the old Kestrel days. And last, Sefa—Sefa, who had carried Isandro's pack out of the White Saddle when his knee tore, who had never once asked him a question he didn't want to answer.

He should have felt glad. He told himself he did.

They came into camp with the easy noise of men who had shared too many cold nights to bother with ceremony. Dario embraced him hard enough to crack ribs, smelling of woodsmoke and cheap tobacco. "You look terrible," Dario said, holding him at arm's length. "You look like a man being haunted."

"I am."

"So I heard." Something moved behind Dario's eyes—quick, shuttered, gone. "Strangers in the low towns asking after you. Compact people, or dressed like it. Figured you'd want friends around when they come looking."

"And you came all this way out of friendship."

Dario grinned, and for one second it was fifteen years ago and they were splitting a bottle on the roof of a customs house in Val Corvane, young and stupid and certain the world would never catch them. "Out of debt," he said. "Same thing, almost."

They made camp below the overhang where the chest had lain. Isandro distributed tasks because that was how he kept his hands steady—Petros to the snares, Anselm to the waterline, Sefa to re-lashing the lean-to against the wind that came down the gorge each evening like a tide. Dario he set to nothing, which was itself a message, and Dario accepted it with a shrug that said *I see your message and raise you silence.*

For two days it was almost good. Almost the old crew. They ate marmot stew and argued about routes and Sefa laughed at Petros's impression of the customs sergeant at Kestrel Bridge until he wheezed. Isandro let himself forget, in pieces, the way ice lets go of a rock—one warm hour at a time.

On the third night he forgot entirely, and that was the mistake.

He woke past midnight needing water and found Dario's bedroll empty. Not alarming—not yet. Men walked at night in the mountains; men pissed in the dark. But the fire had been banked low, and by its embers he saw the oilcloth was folded wrong. He had wrapped the documents with three turns and a slipknot, an old habit from carrying contraband through wet country. The knot now was a bowline. Dario's knot. The one he used for loads he expected to drop.

Isandro did not move. He stood in the shadow of the lean-to and breathed the way he taught clients to breathe at altitude—slow, even, invisible—and watched.

Dario crouched at the edge of the firelight with a camera no smuggler owned. Small, matte-black, silent. He turned pages with gloved fingers, photographing, and the red recording light pulsed like a small patient heart. Isandro knew the papers by their shapes as they passed through the light—the testimony folios, the tribunal minutes, and last, held longest, the birth record with his mother's name written in faded iron-gall ink. His own name beneath it. Dario photographed that page twice.

Then Isandro stepped into the firelight.

He didn't remember deciding to. Later he would reconstruct it as instinct, the same instinct that had once pulled him off a cornice two seconds before it failed. Dario froze, camera half-raised, and for a long moment neither man spoke, and the river filled the silence with its brown gossip.

"Put it down, Dario."

"Ozzy—"

"*Put it down.*"

Dario looked at him across the coals, and Isandro watched something happen in his friend's face that he had seen only twice before: once on a client whose rope anchor had failed above a four-hundred-meter drop, and once in a mirror, years ago, at a border post. The look of a man doing arithmetic with his own life.

Dario set the camera on the stone. Slowly. Like disarming something.

By then the others were awake. Sefa came out of the lean-to first, took in the scene—the camera, the open oilcloth, Isandro's face—and went very still, the stillness of a man standing on snow he has just heard crack. Petros drifted closer with a hand near his knife, reading the room the way he read weather.

"You sold us," Isandro said. It wasn't a question. Questions were for things you could still doubt.

"No." Dario's voice came out rough. "I sold the *route*. Years ago. The Kestrel run, the customs rotations, the blind passes. Not you. Never—" He stopped himself, because they both knew the sentence was a lie wearing good clothes.

"The border incident." Isandro heard his own voice go flat and distant, the voice he used for delivering bad news to families. "Val Corvane. The manifest that didn't match. The guards who were waiting at a crossing only four people alive knew about." He counted them off like knots on a rope. "Me. You. Sefa. And whoever you told."

Sefa made a sound low in his throat. "Dario. Tell him he's wrong."

Dario didn't tell him anything. He sat down on the stone beside the camera, heavily, like a man setting down a load he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it. "I owed money," he said. "Not gambling money. Blood money. My brother's debts, after he died—the kind that come collect in person if you don't pay. The Compact found me in Val Corvane and offered to clear it. All they wanted was a route map and a schedule." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and steady at once, which was somehow worse than either alone. "I told myself it was paper. Lines on a map. Nobody dies of lines on a map."

"Somebody died of lines on a map," Isandro said. "Me. Everything I was. Eleven years, Dario. Eleven years I've slept in a different valley every night because of what happened at that crossing."

"They weren't supposed to take you." For the first time Dario's voice broke. "The deal was the route, the schedules, the cargo windows. Then they staged the incident and I understood what I'd done, and by then they owned me. That's how it works, Ozzy. You don't sell them one thing. You sell them the key, and then every door after is theirs." He gestured at the camera, at the documents glowing faintly in the firelight. "They have my sister's address. They have my crew's names. They came to me three weeks ago and asked me to find you, and I said yes, because saying no meant they send someone who doesn't love you."

"And the photographs?"

"A condition. Proof of delivery." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "I told them I'd need to find you first. I've been buying you time. Every day I reported a false trail was a day they weren't here. But they stopped believing me two days ago." He looked east, toward the dark shoulder of the gorge. "There's a tracker on the river already. Ahead of us, not behind. And more coming up the valley road."

Petros swore softly. Anselm had appeared at the edge of the light, snare wire still in his hands, looking younger than his years.

Isandro crouched, slow, and picked up the camera. It was warm from Dario's grip. He weighed it, thinking about throwing it into the river, thinking about how far sound carried down a gorge at night, thinking about eleven years of different valleys.

Instead he opened the camera and pulled the memory card free and closed it again and handed it back. "You'll give them nothing else," he said. "And you'll tell me everything they gave you. Routes, codes, faces. All of it."

"Ozzy." Dario took the camera like it might bite. "You can't outrun this one. There's no pass they don't have. There's no valley I haven't already drawn for them."

"Then we stop running." Isandro looked at the documents, at the birth record with his mother's careful hand, at the testimony that named the drowned nation and the men who had drowned it. Upstream, somewhere above the reservoir line, the river was born out of ice, and everything it had taken was waiting there. "We go to the source. We finish what she started."

"She?" Sefa asked quietly.

"My mother," Isandro said, and the word tasted like meltwater, cold and mineral and older than his grief. "She was Vessian. She never told me because telling me would have killed her, and it did anyway, just slower." He stood, and the decision settled over him with the strange calm of a man stepping onto a bridge he knows will hold. "Pack for high country. We leave before first light."

Dario rose too, slower, an old man in a strong body. "If I come with you, I'm choosing sides they can see."

"You chose years ago," Isandro said. "This is just the first time you get to choose the right one."

For a moment Dario looked at him with something that was not quite forgiveness asked or given—something rawer, the look of two men on a rope team who know that if one falls, the other must decide, in half a second, whether to jump or cut.

Then, from downstream, faint but unmistakable on the night air, came the sound Isandro had dreaded since the chest washed into his life: an engine, climbing the valley road, running without lights.

* * *

They struck camp in the dark, moving with the economy of men who had broken camp under pursuit before. Isandro gave the documents to Sefa—Sefa, whose loyalty had never once required arithmetic—and took point himself, choosing a route west out of the gorge that existed on no map, because the maps had been drawn by people who believed the cliff bands were impassable, and Isandro had spent a hungry winter proving otherwise.

Behind them, the engine climbed. Ahead, the river ran loud and brown toward the sea, carrying its secrets down while Isandro finally, after eleven years, began to carry his up.
