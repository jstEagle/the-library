## 5. Upstream Rules

The checkpoint appeared the way weather does in the high country—first a rumor, then a certainty. Wren saw it from the ridge at dawn: a knot of grey canvas and greyer men where the river road narrowed between two walls of scree, a drone the size of a heron circling above it with the patient hunger of a thing that had all morning.

"They've been there since yesterday," Isandro said. He crouched beside her in the shadow of a boulder, and the wind coming off the glacier pulled at his collar as if it wanted to read the map too. "Two vehicles. Rotating watch. That's not a roadblock, that's an invitation to be seen."

"Then we don't take the road," Wren said.

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. "There's a route," he said, and the *almost* was doing a great deal of work. "Above the falls. My father used it. Nobody uses it."

"Nobody uses it," Wren repeated, "because it's a route that kills people."

"Because it's a route that *has* killed people," Isandro said. "There's a difference. The mountain keeps records too."

Sefa, who had been silent through all of it, spat over the edge of the ridge and watched the wind take it sideways. "I've followed this man up three cliffs that weren't cliffs," she said. "I'm still here. Statistically, we're owed a fourth."

Wren had known Sefa for four days and understood already that this was how she prayed.

* * *

The route began behind a curtain of water. That was the first thing Wren had not been prepared for—that the falls did not fall so much as *hang*, a grey silk sheet of meltwater two hundred meters high, and that Isandro walked into it the way other men walked into doorways.

"Stay against the rock," he shouted, and the water ate his words and gave them back broken. "The ledge is behind the second fold. Count your steps. Don't count the drop."

Wren counted her steps. She counted forty-one of them with her back flat against stone so cold and so wet that it felt less like touching the mountain than being absorbed by it, the falls thundering past close enough to fill her ears, her sleeves, her mouth. The world reduced itself to pressure and noise. Somewhere ahead Sefa's red pack glowed like a coal in the grey. Somewhere behind, the Compact's drone circled a road that no longer mattered.

Then they were through, on a shelf of wet granite no wider than a kitchen table, and the falls were a wall at their backs, and the drop was everything else.

Wren made the mistake of looking.

The river ran below them through the gorge like a vein of tarnished silver, and beyond it the terraced shadows of the old Vessian valley—drowned forty years ago, its church towers rising out of the reservoir in drought years like the fingers of something reaching up. She had authenticated documents about that valley for six years. She had catalogued its census records, its land grants, its dam inspection reports, all of them stamped with the sunburst seal, all of them filed under designations designed to make a reader's eyes slide off. She had never once stood above it and felt it *breathe*.

"Keep moving," Isandro said. "The ledge ends in a chimney. You climb chimneys by trusting your heels, not your eyes."

"My eyes are what I make my living with," Wren said.

"Then close them and trust your heels, archivist."

She climbed the chimney with her eyes open anyway, because she was who she was—because some stubborn, ruined part of her refused to stop witnessing even when witnessing was the thing most likely to get her killed. The rock was slick with spray and older than language. Twice her foothold crumbled into the void and her whole weight hung on her fingers, and both times Isandro's hand found her wrist from above, unerring, as if he had mapped the exact coordinates of her failure in advance.

"You've done this before," she gasped at the top, hauling herself onto the high shelf.

"I've done this running," he said. "It's easier when you're not thinking."

* * *

They made the high traverse by afternoon, and that was where the mountain nearly took Sefa.

It happened without drama, the way the worst things do. A section of slope where the rock had shed its skin, a field of loose plates over ice. Sefa went first because she always went first. Ten steps in, the mountain shrugged, and she went with it.

Wren would remember the sound—the dry *clack* of Sefa's boot dislodging stone, the half-second of absolute silence, and then the rope snapping taut and Isandro dropping to his knees behind a flake of granite, both hands locked on the line, his whole body become a single argument against gravity. Sefa swung out over the void, penduluming, her boots skating uselessly down the rock face, and Wren threw herself onto the rope beside Isandro because there was nothing else her hands knew how to do.

"Feet on the wall!" Isandro roared. "Sefa! *Feet!*"

Sefa found the wall. Found it the way a drowning woman finds anything—blindly, desperately, with a scream she didn't let out. Isandro took in rope hand over hand, and the mountain gave them nothing, and then, grudgingly, it gave them everything: Sefa's boot jamming in a crack, her weight coming onto her own legs, her hands finding the flake, and then she was up, over the lip, lying on her back and laughing in the flat, airless way people laugh when the alternative is screaming.

"Fourth cliff," she said to the sky. "We're even now."

Wren sat down hard on the stone. Her hands would not stop shaking. She looked at them—archivist's hands, ink-stained, soft, made for vellum and climate-controlled rooms—and understood that she had left that life somewhere behind the falls, and that whatever was on the other side of this mountain, she would arrive at it a different person than the one who had started.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below the ridgeline, a cup of stone that held their small fire like a secret. Below them, far below, the Compact's checkpoint was a scatter of lights on the river road, and the drone had gone home to whatever roost drones go home to. The stars came out the way they only do at altitude—brutal, unfiltered, close enough to be accusatory.

Wren took the documents out of the waterproof sleeve and held them toward the firelight, because she had promised herself she would explain, and because she was done with the kind of archives that existed to be unread.

"You should hear all of it," she said. "If we're going to the source, you should know what the source is."

Isandro sat across the fire with his forearms on his knees. Sefa lay with her boots toward the flames and her eyes open, watching the dark.

"Vessia didn't drown," Wren said. "That's the first thing. Everyone says *the dam failed*, the way you'd say *the weather turned*. But dams don't fail. They're failed. Someone decides, and then physics does the paperwork."

She turned the pages. The firelight made the old ink swim.

"The Meridian Compact built the Vessia Dam. That part's public—buried, but public. What isn't public is the inspection record from the final year. The engineer—your grandfather, Isandro, though I didn't know that when I first read it—filed four formal warnings. Four. Seepage in the abutment, hairline fractures in the core wall, water levels held above safe operating height for *months*. And here's the part they sealed." She looked up. "The water was held high deliberately. There was a downstream contract—a power purchase agreement with the Compact's own subsidiary, contingent on maximum reservoir output. Every meter of extra head was worth money. The engineer's warnings were answered in writing. By the regional director. Do you want to know what it says?"

"Say it," Isandro said quietly.

"'Noted. Proceed as scheduled. The valley's claims are under review.'" Wren let the words sit in the firelight. "The valley's claims. Four thousand people. Their homes, their graves, their orchards—their *claims*. Under review. Eleven weeks later, the dam failed, the valley drowned, and the review was closed for lack of interested parties. Then the Compact erased the maps. Redrew the borders, reassigned the valley to three different administrative regions so it belonged to nowhere, paid out settlements with gag clauses, and reclassified the inspection record. Your mother's family got out early because your grandfather saw it coming and wouldn't stop saying so. That's why the birth record exists. That's why *you* exist on paper as Vessian-born. He registered you before the erasure, when the ink was still wet and the country still had a name."

Isandro was silent for a long time. The fire cracked. Below them the river went on doing what rivers do—carrying, carrying, carrying.

"The border incident," he said finally. "The one that blacklisted me."

"Wasn't about the border," Wren said. "I've spent two years pulling threads, and every thread ends at the same spool. Someone flagged your file six years ago. Someone made sure you'd never have standing, never have credibility, never be in a room where anyone would believe you. They weren't punishing you for anything you did. They were *pre-clearing* you. In case the record ever surfaced. In case the engineer's blood ever came looking."

The wind moved through the hollow. Isandro stared into the fire, and Wren watched him do the arithmetic she had watched a hundred people do and never once watched anyone finish: the sum of a life re-read as someone else's handwriting.

"They didn't ruin me," he said at last. "They *quarantined* me."

"They quarantined the truth," Wren said. "You were just the vector."

Sefa spoke without turning her head. "So we go to the source. We get the ledger. And then what? We can't outrun them forever. There's three of us and they own the maps."

"We don't outrun them," Wren said. "That's the whole point. That's what they've never understood about archives. You can burn a document, but you can't burn the fact that it existed. Somewhere in the world there are a hundred people like me—disgraced, obsessive, unemployable—who've spent years wondering what was in the Vessia file. You don't outrun the Compact. You *publish*." She looked at Isandro. "But it has to be the complete record. The testimony in the chest names the director. The ledger proves the orders. One without the other is a rumor. Both together is a tribunal."

Isandro reached into his jacket and took out the wax-sealed corner of the birth record, turning it over in his fingers. His mother's name. His own. A country that no longer appeared on any map, and a boy from it who had spent his whole life sleeping in different valleys so that no valley could claim him.

"My whole life," he said, "I thought I was running from something I'd done."

"You were running from something that was done *to* you," Wren said. "There's a difference. And there's a direction." She nodded upstream, into the dark where the glacier breathed its cold down on them. "Downstream is theirs. Every road, every checkpoint, every map. But upstream—" she smiled, and it was not a gentle smile, "—upstream there are no maps at all. Just the source."

Isandro fed the birth record back into its sleeve with the care of a man handling a live coal. "Then we go up," he said. "And we don't come down until the whole world knows what the water took."

Sefa rolled onto her side, facing the fire at last. "There's a village," she said. "Above the reservoir line. My aunt's cousin trades with them—people who never signed the settlements, never took the money. If they're still there—"

"If they're there," Isandro said, "they'll know the caves."

The fire burned low. Wren lay back against her pack and watched the stars wheel, and thought about how she had spent six years authenticating the dead, and how it had taken a flooded chest and a wanted man to teach her that some documents aren't records of the past at all.

They are instructions. They are addressed to whoever is still alive to read them.

Above them, at the head of the valley, the glacier creaked in the dark like a door being opened somewhere, very slowly, by a hand that had been waiting a long time.
