## 6. The Village That Isn't There

The trail above the reservoir line was not a trail. It was a rumor of one—a seam of goat-traffic and old landslide scree that Isandro had found years ago while running contraband over the shoulder of Mount Halya, back when he'd told himself the work was just work. He climbed it now with Wren a rope-length behind him, Sefa last, and the drowned valley opening below them like a held breath.

The water sat green and still in the morning light, so calm it looked solid, a slab of jade laid into the mountains. Somewhere under it were orchards, a chapel, streets with names. Isandro had crossed this reservoir a dozen times in his life without once thinking about what lay beneath. Now he could not stop seeing it: rooftops under forty meters of cold water, doors that would never open again.

"You're doing it again," Wren said from below, breath ragged on the thin air.

"Doing what."

"Looking at it like it's going to apologize."

He didn't answer. The sun came off the water hard enough to hurt, and he turned his face to the rock and climbed.

* * *

They smelled woodsmoke before they saw anything else—woodsmoke and something baking, yeast and grain, a smell so ordinary at this altitude that it stopped Sefa mid-step like a hand on her chest. "Nobody lives up here," she said. "I've run these ridges for six years. Nobody lives up here."

"Then we're smelling a ghost," Wren said.

Isandro knew better. He had spent his whole life learning where people hid, because hidden people paid best and asked fewest questions. There were always more of them than the maps admitted. He cut left off the seam of the trail, following the smoke's ghost-thread through a stand of wind-bent pines, and came out onto a shelf of meadow that no surveyor had ever bothered to name.

There were eleven houses. He counted them twice. Low stone buildings roofed with turf, windows small and deep-set, a millrace running bright and quick from a side-stream into a wheel that turned with a sound like slow applause. Goats. A field of barley, absurdly high, absurdly green. Children—three of them—frozen between the houses with their mouths open, staring at the strangers as if the mountain itself had grown faces.

An old woman came out of the nearest house wiping flour from her hands, took one look at them, and said something in a language Isandro did not know but understood anyway, the way you understand a door slamming. It was not a welcome.

"We're not Compact," he said in trade-tongue. "We're not anyone."

The woman looked at him for a long moment. Then she said something else, softer, to a man who had appeared behind her carrying a scythe with the unhurried ease of someone who had used it as a weapon before or was prepared to. The man stared—and then the scythe dipped, and his face did something complicated, grief and recognition fighting for the same ground.

"He says—" began a young man stepping forward, the only one dressed in cloth woven anywhere near a city, "he says you have your grandfather's face."

Isandro's mouth went dry. "I don't have a grandfather."

"Everyone has a grandfather," the young man said. "Yours was named Emeric Ishikawa. He was the assistant chief engineer on the Meridian dam project. And when they wouldn't listen to him, he walked out of the compound with everything he knew and tried to give it to the valley." He paused. "You have his face. My grandmother kept a photograph. She burned it when the water came, but she never forgot it. None of us forget faces. It's how we survived."

* * *

They fed them first. That was the rule here, the young man—his name was Teodor—explained, because hunger made liars of everyone and the village had learned long ago that you fed a stranger before you questioned him, so that at least the lie would be told on a full stomach. They ate barley bread and hard cheese and beans cooked with some herb Isandro didn't know, sitting on benches worn smooth by generations of hands that officially did not exist.

Around them the village watched. Not hostilely—that was the strange part. Watchfully, the way a family watches a doctor read results.

"How long?" Wren asked quietly. "How long have you been up here?"

"Since the water." Teodor turned his cup in his hands. "My great-grandparents were Vessian. When the tribunal declared the valley uninhabited—before the flooding, mind you, they declared it empty *first*, that was the trick—they moved everyone out on paper. So the ones who refused to leave became paper ghosts. No census. No registry. No right to exist, which meant no obligation on anyone's part to drown us." He smiled without any humor in it. "The Compact is very thorough with documents. It's their weakness too. If you don't exist in their files, sometimes they forget to look for you."

"Fifty years," Sefa said. "You've been hiding for fifty years."

"We've been *living* for fifty years," an old voice corrected.

The elder had come in without sound. She was small and straight-backed, hair white as glacier milk, eyes the color of wet slate, and when she sat down across from Isandro the whole room rearranged its attention around her. She studied his face for a long time, unembarrassed by it, turning it over like a page.

"Emeric had that scar," she said at last, touching her own brow above the left eye. "From a climbing fall, he said. He laughed about it. He said the mountain had marked him so the tribunal couldn't pretend he was one of theirs."

Isandro's hand went to his own scar without his permission. "Who are you?"

"I am Mirela. I was nine years old when my mother carried me up this mountain on her back. And I knew your grandfather, child, because he came here—right here, to this room—to warn us. Three weeks before the flood. He brought maps. He brought the inspection reports they buried. He stood where you are standing and he begged us to leave, and we thought he was the one trying to trick us, because the Compact men had come before him with kind words too." Her voice did not waver, but her hands closed around each other until the knuckles whitened. "Forty-one families stayed. Eleven houses are what's left of them."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside a goat complained at the length of its tether.

"He went back down," Mirela said, "to get the last of the evidence—the ledger, his own hand, every order, every falsified report, every payment. He meant to carry it out of the country, to a press, to the world. The flood came early. Or it came exactly when they intended it to, which is the same thing." She looked at Isandro, and there was something terrible in the steadiness of her gaze, something that had waited fifty years to be delivered. "His daughter survived. Your mother. We sent word upriver for years, after. She never answered."

"She couldn't have," Isandro heard himself say. His voice sounded far away. "She never talked about any of it. I thought—I grew up thinking we were from nowhere. She wanted it that way. She changed our names, moved us every time I got comfortable, taught me to sleep in a different valley every night before I even knew why." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "She wasn't teaching me tradecraft. She was teaching me to survive a war I didn't know we'd lost."

Wren's hand found his forearm under the table and stayed there.

"The chest," Mirela said. "The river gave it back to you. Do you understand what that means? Nothing comes down from the source unless the source lets it go. We have waited fifty years for the water to make a decision." She rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the hearth. From behind a loose stone—one of hundreds, indistinguishable, a hiding place that had outlived everyone who remembered choosing it—she drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and waxed leather, tied with cord gone soft with age.

She set it on the table between them but did not untie it.

"This is a copy of his field notes. The ledger itself he hid where no flood could reach it and no clerk would think to climb—the source caves, above the spring, where the river is born. He told me the location in the last hour I ever spoke with him. I was nine. I have kept it for sixty-one years." She pushed the bundle toward Isandro. "Inside is the key to reading what he wrote. His cipher. Without it the ledger is just an old man's arithmetic. With it, it is a confession signed by every man who ordered the water."

"Why give it to me?" Isandro asked. "You don't know me."

"I know your face," Mirela said. "And I know what it costs to carry a thing like this. Your grandfather died carrying it. Your mother died having fled it. You have been running from it your whole life without knowing its name." She leaned close, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "The river does not choose carelessly, child. It kept what it took. Now it has given it back. The only question left is whether you will be a keeper or a runner."

* * *

They left at dusk, because Mirela insisted—the Compact's satellites passed less often in the dark, and the village had learned the sky's schedule the way farmers learn weather. Teodor walked them to the edge of the meadow with a lamp shuttered to a coin of light, and pressed food into their packs, and would not take thanks.

"When you come back down," he said, "come back this way. Whatever happens." He glanced back at the eleven houses, the turf roofs black against the last light. "Fifty years is long enough to be a rumor. We'd like to be a place again."

Sefa cried a little, quietly, after the lamplight fell behind them. She pretended she hadn't. Isandro let her.

They climbed through the dark by Isandro's memory and the moon's grudging help, up past the tree line, across the shoulder of scree where the wind spoke in a dead language, and camped at midnight in the lee of a boulder with the glacier humming somewhere above them like a struck glass.

Isandro unwrapped Mirela's bundle by headlamp. Field notes in a small, precise hand—Emeric Ishikawa's hand, though Isandro had nothing to compare it to except the sudden, awful certainty that it was true. Diagrams. Dates. Payments recorded against initials. And on the first page, in the margin, a line that stopped his breath:

*If my granddaughter's son ever reads this: the water remembers. Be worth remembering.*

He sat with it a long time. Wren watched him from across the fire ring, saying nothing, giving him the silence like a gift.

"My mother ran her whole life," he finally said. "And it bought her nothing but a son who inherited the running. Fifty years, and the Compact still owns the story, and the valley's still drowned, and those people up there are still ghosts." He closed the notes. "I'm done moving."

"That's not like you," Sefa said softly.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He looked up toward the glacier, where the source waited, white and blind and patient. "But I know where I'm going now. That's the difference. Every valley I ever slept in, I was running *from* something. This is the first time I'm walking *toward* it."

Wren fed the fire another stick. "The caves above the spring," she said. "Emeric picked well. Cold, dry, above flood line, and the Compact's clerks don't climb."

"They'll have to learn," Isandro said.

It was Sefa, on last watch, who woke them an hour before dawn—not with words but with a hand flat on Isandro's chest, her other hand pointing up-valley.

Lights. Three of them, maybe four, moving along the glacier's flank with the disciplined spacing of people trained to search. Too high for climbers out of the low towns. Too deliberate for chance.

"They beat us to the ice," Sefa whispered.

Isandro watched the lights crawl, and felt something settle in him that was not fear—or not only fear—but the cold, clean clarity of a man who has stopped looking over his shoulder because he has decided what's ahead matters more.

"Then we climb faster," he said.
