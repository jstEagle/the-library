## 7. White Water, Black Ledger

Wren felt the gorge change before she heard anything. The air pressure shifted the way it does before a confession—something held too long finally giving way. The walls narrowed here, grey granite sweating with melt, and the river below them had gone from brown to a churning, arterial red-brown, thick with the silt of a landscape being torn apart upstream.

"Storm in the high country," Sefa said, squinting at the sky through the notch of rock where they'd stopped to drink. "That's flood water. That's *coming* water."

Isandro crouched at the edge, reading the current the way Wren had seen him read everything—not with his eyes so much as with his whole body, some animal ledger of weight and angle and threat. "Two hours," he said. "Maybe less. We need to be out of the narrows before it crests."

"Then we don't stop," Wren said.

"We don't stop," he agreed, and they didn't, not until the cave.

It announced itself as a seam of darker stone in the cliff face, thirty feet above the waterline, half-hidden behind a curtain of frozen seep. The elder's directions had been precise in the way of people who had spent generations hiding things: *where the mountain weeps, look behind the tear*. Inside, the air was cold and mineral, and their headlamps threw shadows that seemed to move independently of them, as though the cave had its own population of the drowned.

The ledger was where the elder said it would be. Behind a slab that took both Isandro and Sefa to shift, wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff as bark, inside a tin box that had been soldered shut by hands that never intended it to be opened by anyone still living. Wren's fingers shook as she worked the seal with her knife—not from cold, though the cold was considerable, but from the specific vertigo of an archivist touching a document that has outlasted every institution that tried to destroy it.

She had spent eleven years authenticating looted papers in back rooms for men who paid in untraceable currency. She knew forgery the way a doctor knows disease—intimately, clinically, without illusion. This was not forgery. The ink chemistry alone told her that; the iron gall had oxidized in the pattern of genuine decades, not simulated age. And the hand that had written it—precise, engineering-tight, with a draftsman's habit of underlining figures twice—matched the sample of Tomás Ishikawa's script she'd seen once, years ago, in a Compact auction catalogue that was never supposed to reach the open market.

"It's real," she said, and heard her own voice crack on the second word. "Isandro. It's real. The diversion orders, the pressure tests he flagged, the memos from the Compact's hydrological board telling him to sign off anyway. He refused. That's why they—"

She stopped. Some truths you deliver gently. Some you can't.

Isandro stood very still in the lamplight. "That's why they drowned it," he finished. "And buried him in the story."

"Your grandfather didn't fail to stop the dam," Wren said. "He was the only one who tried."

Sefa made a sound low in his throat—not quite a word—and turned away to stand watch at the cave mouth. Isandro said nothing at all. He sat down on the stone floor with the ledger open across his knees, and Wren watched a man read his own inheritance and be remade by it, the way water remakes stone: slowly, all at once, irreversibly.

* * *

They heard the engine before they saw anything, which meant the engine wanted them to hear it.

"Off the trail," Isandro breathed, and they flattened into a fold of rock above the path as the vehicle came around the bend below—a Compact all-terrain, matte grey, unmarked, the kind of vehicle that existed officially nowhere. It stopped. A single figure climbed out, and even at that distance, even across twenty years and a betrayal Wren had only heard about secondhand, she recognized the set of the shoulders.

"Dario," Isandro said. Not a question.

The man below them raised a hand—not a weapon, just a hand, palm out, the universal gesture of *wait*. He was older than the photographs in Isandro's stories had prepared Wren for, grey at the temples, moving with the careful economy of someone whose body had been a tool for too long. He carried a Compact-issue tracker's rifle slung loose, and a radio at his hip, and he stood in the middle of the trail like a man standing in the ruins of his own house.

"I know you're up there," Dario called. His voice carried strangely in the gorge, flattened by stone. "I know you found the ledger. I know what's in it, because I'm the one who told them what to look for."

Sefa's hand found his knife. Isandro's found Sefa's wrist.

"Twenty years," Isandro called down, and his voice did something Wren had never heard it do—wavered. "Twenty years I ran because of what you sold."

"I know." Dario's head bowed. "I know what I did. I know what the incident cost you—your licenses, your name, every valley you ever loved. I know because I helped them build the file. I was there when they staged the crossing. I signed the statement they wrote for me." He looked up, and even from above, Wren saw that his eyes were wet. "And I have spent twenty years telling myself I had no choice, because the debt was going to kill my sister, and the Compact doesn't lend, Isandro. It *invests*. And it collects."

The river spoke then, a long rolling boom from upstream, and every face turned toward the sound. The flood pulse was coming. Wren could feel it in the rock under her palms, a tremor like a heartbeat accelerating.

"You have orders," Isandro said. It wasn't a question either.

"I have orders to contain the documents and the carriers." Dario's hand drifted toward the rifle, and Sefa moved—fast, too fast, angling down the scree toward the trail in a flanking line that made no tactical sense unless the plan was to draw fire. "Sefa, *don't*—"

The shot came from the vehicle. Someone else had been in it. Wren never saw the second man clearly—just a shape in the cab, a muzzle flash, and then Sefa pitching sideways off the scree with a sound that Wren would hear for the rest of her life: not a cry, just a short exhalation, the sound a body makes when it stops believing it can hold itself up.

He fell thirty feet onto the trail and did not get up.

Everything after that happened in pieces. Isandro going down the rock face like water going downhill, fast and controlled and terrible. Wren clutching the ledger to her chest and following because following was the only thing she knew how to do. The second Compact operative climbing out of the vehicle with his weapon up, and Dario stepping between them—physically between them, his back to Isandro, his body suddenly a wall.

"Stand down," Dario said, and his voice was nothing like it had been a moment before. It was the voice of a man who had finally finished a calculation twenty years long and did not like the total. "Stand down or I put you in the river."

"You're relieved," the operative said. "Voss, you're relieved. I have the authority—"

"You have nothing," Dario said. "You have a radio and a rifle and a boss who will disown you the moment this goes wrong, and this has *already* gone wrong. The flood's coming. Get in the vehicle or the flood gets you."

The river decided the argument. The flood pulse arrived as a wall of sound first, then a wall of water, brown and enormous, swallowing the lower trail, lifting the all-terrain like a toy and rolling it. The operative scrambled for high ground and lost his rifle doing it. Wren lost her footing on the wet rock and was caught—hauled up by the collar by Dario, of all people, who held her with a grip like iron and a face like a man looking at his own verdict.

"Go," he said to Isandro. "Upstream. The source trail, past the narrows. I'll carry the boy."

"You'll come with us," Isandro said. "You'll testify—"

"I'll carry the boy," Dario said again, and bent and lifted Sefa out of the water's reach with a gentleness that broke something open in Wren's chest. "I owe twenty years, Isandro. This is the first payment. Go. The Compact will have clerks at the source by morning—they always send clerks, they always think a signature can fix what a signature broke. Be there first. Be *loud*."

* * *

They climbed.

The narrows above the flood line were a nightmare of wet granite and freezing spray, and Wren climbed them on arms that felt borrowed, the ledger sealed in waxed canvas against her spine, Sefa's blood still on her hands from where she'd pressed them to his shoulder before Dario took him. Isandro moved ahead of her like a man climbing out of his own grave, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing language could do with what had just happened.

At the top, where the gorge opened onto the high valley and the air went thin and clean and glacial, Wren finally turned and looked back. Far below, tiny as a miniature, Dario was climbing the far bank with Sefa across his shoulders, away from the vehicle, away from the road, away from everything that could save either of them—carrying the boy he had helped ruin toward whatever shelter the drowned country's edges still offered.

"Will he make it?" Wren asked.

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. "He's the best route-finder I ever knew," he said finally. "And he knows he's not allowed to die yet. That's usually enough."

They stood in the wind, two people and a book, at the top of the world. Above them, the valley narrowed to a white thread, and at its end, barely visible, the glacier—the source, the spring, the beginning of everything the river had carried down to them: a chest, a name, a grandfather's refusal, a friend's blood.

"Listen," Wren said.

Isandro tilted his head. Beneath the wind, beneath the water, faint and rhythmic, came the sound of rotors. More than one. Coming up the valley from downstream.

"They're not sending clerks by morning," Wren said, and pulled the ledger tighter against her back, and started to climb.
