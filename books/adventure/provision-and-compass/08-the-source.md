## 8. The Source

The river ended the way all rivers end—not with a roar but with a thinning, a letting go. For three days they had climbed beside it as it shrank from a brown beast to a silver thread, and now, in the last blue hour before dawn, Isandro stood at the place where the thread simply stopped being a river at all: a glacial spring pooling out of the rock like something spilled from a broken cup, so clear it seemed less like water than like absence.

He had imagined this moment for years without knowing it. Every smuggler's map he'd ever drawn had a blank space at its top, and every blank space had been this—the source of the Kestrel, the vein that ran down through the gorge, past the drowned valleys of Vessia, past his camp, past everything. He had spent his whole adult life downstream of a thing he'd never seen.

The meltwater pool lay in a bowl of granite scoured smooth by ten thousand winters. Ice floes drifted on it, small and patient, turning slowly like sleeping animals. Above, the glacier hung in its cirque, blue-veined, groaning once in a while with a sound like a door opening very far away.

Wren crouched at the water's edge, arms wrapped around her knees, the ledger strapped to her chest under her jacket. She hadn't let go of it since the caves. Her lips were cracked from the altitude and she was breathing in the shallow, rationed way of someone who has learned that air is not free.

"It's smaller than I thought," she said.

"Everything is," Isandro said. "From far away."

Sefa was asleep against a boulder, her ankle bound with strips of shirt, her face gray with pain she refused to name. They had made her walk on it for two days because stopping meant dying, and she had walked, swearing at them in three languages, and now that they had stopped she slept like a stone dropped into deep water. Isandro looked at her a long moment. The cost ledger. He kept it whether he wanted to or not.

"We should keep moving," Wren said, but she didn't move either. Neither of them believed the words. There was no moving left. The route ended here; the maps ended here; the world, more or less, ended here. Whatever came next would come to them.

It came an hour after sunrise.

* * *

They heard no engine—there were no engines this high—but Sefa woke with a hand on Isandro's arm and said, "Smoke," and there it was: a single thread of woodsmoke rising from behind a spur of rock downstream of the pool, thin and domestic, the smoke of people making tea.

Isandro counted weapons in his head and found the inventory short. His knife. Wren's flare gun, two flares. Sefa, who could barely stand. Against whatever the Compact sent, it was nothing, and he knew it, and he found to his surprise that he felt calm—not the calm of resignation but the strange flat clarity that had always come to him in the worst places, on rotten cornices, in whiteout, when fear had burned through itself and left only attention.

"They've been following us since the village," Wren said. "Since before."

"I know."

"You knew?"

"I've known since the checkpoint." He had felt them the way a guide feels weather—not seen, but present, a pressure on the back of the neck. "You don't climb the Kestrel headwall with someone watching and not feel it."

"Why didn't you say?"

"Because you'd have done something desperate." He almost smiled. "And because I wanted to see what they'd do first."

What they did was make tea.

Four figures came around the spur at midmorning, walking unhurried, and they were not what Isandro had braced for. No armor. No rifles slung across chests. Four people in good gray expedition clothing, one of them carrying a kettle, another a folding table under one arm like a man arriving at a picnic. The oldest was a woman with white hair cut close and spectacles that caught the sun; the youngest looked barely old enough to have finished school, clutching a leather folio the way Sefa clutched her ankle—as if it hurt.

"Clerks," Wren breathed, and there was something worse in her voice than there would have been if she'd said soldiers. "Oh, Isandro. They sent clerks."

"Do clerks kill people?"

"Clerks erase them." She stood, brushing ice from her knees. "Soldiers are for people who matter. We're paperwork to them. That's why they brought a table."

The woman with the spectacles stopped a courteous ten meters away and inclined her head, the way one traveler greets another at altitude, and said, "Mr. Ishikawa. Miss Osei. You've made a remarkable climb. My colleagues in logistics said the headwall was impassable this season. I confess I enjoyed telling them otherwise."

"Who are you?" Wren asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"Halloran. Records Division, Meridian Compact." The woman said it the way other people said *good morning*. "We're here to help you file your findings."

* * *

They set up the folding table on the granite shelf above the spring, and Halloran set out cups, and poured tea from the kettle, and it was absurd and it was terrifying and it was, Isandro had to admit, effective. You could not fight a picnic. You could only sit at it or walk away, and there was nowhere to walk.

"Before we begin," Halloran said, "I want to be honest with you, because I understand you've both been lied to by institutions and I imagine honesty is scarce currency." She folded her hands. "The Compact will not permit the Vessia testimony to enter public circulation. That is fixed policy, older than everyone in this conversation, including me. It is not negotiable, and no amount of courage on your part will change it. What *is* negotiable—what I climbed here to negotiate—is what happens to the three of you afterward."

Wren laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "You're offering us terms for our surrender before we've conceded there's a war."

"There was a war," Halloran said mildly. "That's rather the point of the documents. This is the peace conference."

She nodded to the young clerk, who opened the folio with hands that trembled slightly, and laid out papers on the folding table, weighting their corners with small stones because there was no wind but he did it anyway, and Isandro understood that the boy had rehearsed this and was terrified anyway, and felt an unexpected flicker of pity.

"Mr. Ishikawa." Halloran turned a page toward him. "Your blacklist. Both entries—the Kestrel Republics and the Meridian corridor states. Full expungement. Not suspension. Expungement, with formal letters of apology from both tribunals, delivered publicly. Your guiding license reinstated at the highest tier. And—" she slid a second paper forward— "a new identity, should you want one. Clean history, clean passport, funds sufficient to start anywhere on earth. The Compact is very good at beginnings. It's rather our specialty."

"And the documents?" Isandro asked.

"The chest goes into the spring." Halloran said it gently, the way you tell someone a diagnosis. "Lead sinks. Glacial till covers it within a season. In fifty years the pool will have moved and no one will ever find it. The testimony dies quietly, in the place it was born, and no one is hurt. No one else is hurt," she corrected herself, with a glance at Sefa's ankle, at the bandages, at the price already paid. "You've lost enough. All of you. That's not a threat—it's arithmetic. Continue downstream with those papers and people will die for them. Stop here, and the dying stops today."

The tea steamed. The glacier groaned its far-off door-sound. Isandro looked at the papers on the table—at the word *expungement*, at the word *clean*—and felt the offer slide into him like a knife going into warm wax, finding no resistance anywhere.

Because here was the truth he could not say aloud: he wanted it. God help him, he wanted it. Twenty years of never sleeping twice in the same valley. Twenty years of leading men with hidden cargo through passes that didn't exist on any map, eating cold food, keeping his back to walls, learning the faces of clients well enough to know which ones would shoot him if the money went wrong. Twenty years of being a man the world had agreed to forget, and here was a woman with spectacles offering to remember him differently. A new name. A clean passport. The right to stand in a town square without calculating sightlines.

His mother had lived that life too, he thought suddenly—the running, the silence, the name changed at borders. She had carried Vessia inside her like swallowed glass and told him nothing, and he had hated her a little for the silence, all those years, without understanding it had been mercy. She had known about offers like this one. She must have refused one herself, once, somewhere, in a language he'd never learn.

"Isandro." Wren's voice, low. "Look at me."

He didn't. He looked instead at the spring.

The morning sun had come fully onto the pool now, and the ice floes had begun to turn faster in the melt-current, and where the light struck the water he could see down—further than seemed possible—to the pale floor of the basin, and floating on that mirror-bright surface, framed in drifting ice, was a reflection. His own. He leaned over it the way you lean over a well.

The man in the water looked calm. Capable. Unmarked. A man with a clean passport and a new name and twenty good years of mountains ahead of him, none of them haunted, none of them watched. A man who had never been born in Vessia. A man whose grandfather had never built levees against a dam he knew would fail. A man whose mother's silence had worked.

*They're offering me the life I've been living,* he thought. *They're just asking me to sign for it.*

That was the seduction of it—that it asked for nothing new. Only that he make official what he had already practiced for two decades: disappearance. He had been erasing himself professionally since he was nineteen. The Compact wasn't proposing to change him. It was proposing to pay him for it.

The reflection rippled as an ice floe ground past, and broke, and reassembled, and Isandro watched it reassemble and understood that it would always reassemble, that water keeps no record, that this was precisely the Compact's religion—they were clerks of forgetting, priests of the pool, and the whole drowned nation lay beneath waters exactly like these, clear and beautiful and holding nothing.

Except it wasn't holding nothing. It was holding everything. That was the whole secret of rivers; he had known it since the flood first washed the chest into his camp. *The river keeps what it takes.* It had taken his mother's country and kept it. It had taken the testimony and carried it to his door. Water forgets nothing; it only waits, and moves things downhill until someone reads them.

Halloran was watching him watch the water, and something in her expression shifted—a flicker, quickly mastered, of a woman who had sat at many tables like this one and knew the exact moment the wax began to cool.

"Take your time," she said. "The glacier isn't going anywhere."

"No," Isandro said. "But the river is."

He straightened up. Behind him, Wren had gone very still, one hand pressed flat against her jacket, over the ledger, over her heart. Sefa had opened her eyes and was watching him from the boulder with the level, unflinching look of a woman measuring a rope before she trusts it with her weight.

Isandro picked up his cup of tea, drank it—it was good tea, insultingly good—and set it down on the folding table with a small precise click, directly on top of the word *expungement*.

"Here are my terms," he said. "You burn nothing. You drown nothing. And you can start by telling me who signed the order for the dam—because I'm going to read it into every radio on this mountain before nightfall."

Halloran closed the folio, softly, like a book ending.

"I was afraid you'd say that," she said, and snapped her fingers, and four figures rose from behind the spur of rock where no four figures had been before.
