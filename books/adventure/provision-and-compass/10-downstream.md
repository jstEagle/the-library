## 10. Downstream

The cell they gave him had a window, which was either mercy or a mistake.

Isandro had decided, after four months, that it was neither. It was architecture—the old tribunal building predated the Compact, predated the blacklists, predated the era when men like him were filed under *unresolved* and shelved. The window was simply where a window had always been, and through it he could see a slice of the city: slate roofs, a bell tower, and beyond the rooftops the blue shoulder of the mountains where he had spent his whole adult life learning never to stay.

He stayed now. That was the strange part. Not the walls—he had slept in worse places, in snow caves and smugglers' lofts with the wind coming through the boards like a second voice—but the staying itself, the way each morning he woke and did not move, and the world did not punish him for it. His body still expected the punishment. Some mornings he lay with his boots half-laced before he remembered there was nowhere he was permitted to run to, and nowhere, anymore, that he needed to run from.

The guards called him *the Vessian*. Not prisoner. Not the border criminal of two countries' worth of warrants. The name had changed the way a river changes when the season turns—imperceptibly, then all at once.

He learned the shape of what he'd done the way everyone else had learned it: from outside, in fragments carried up the corridor by guards who thought he couldn't hear. The broadcast had gone out from the spring in ninety-one seconds of transmission before the Compact jammed the relay, and ninety-one seconds had been enough. Wren had built the archive to survive compression, to survive truncation, to survive exactly the violence that was done to it. He had not understood, crouched in the meltwater spray with Dario shouting and the clerks' men closing in, that what she was doing was not copying the testimony but *distributing* it—seeded, redundant, everywhere at once, a document designed the way water is designed, to find every low place.

The low places had found it. Within a week there were readings of the engineer's ledger in four languages. Within a month there were tribunals—not his, not yet, but ones like it in three countries, convening to ask how a nation had been drowned on purpose and why every map had agreed to forget it.

And him. They asked about him too. The border incident, the one he had sworn for eleven years he hadn't caused, turned out to have a paper trail after all—Dario's confession, extracted from the wreckage of what Dario had become at the source, and corroborated by Compact routing records that Wren had pulled from the same archive. The blacklist had not so much dissolved as been revealed as what it always was: a fiction with excellent paperwork. The warrants were withdrawn. The two countries sent letters of apology so carefully worded they read like threats.

He was still in the cell because he had walked into the tribunal hall at the source and given himself to the first clerk who would take the surrender, and because the truth, having been buried once, was being handled now with the paranoia of people who had learned what happens to things left in the open. He was evidence. He was the living seal on the chest. They kept him the way an archive keeps a fragile document—in the dark, at controlled temperature, until the reading was done.

He could not bring himself to resent it. That was the part he turned over at night, staring at the ceiling. He had spent eleven years paying for a crime he didn't commit, and now he was paying, willingly, for one he had: the crime of knowing and not saying. The sentence felt, for the first time in his life, correctly sized.

* * *

The guards let Wren come on Thursdays. He never learned why Thursdays. Perhaps it was in her file. Wren Osei struck him as a woman whose life was largely in her file, and who had made her peace with that, or weaponized it.

She came in with the same canvas satchel every week, and every week the guard made her empty it onto the steel table, and every week it held nothing but paper, which was, Isandro had come to understand, the only thing Wren had ever needed to smuggle.

"You're on the front page again," she said, the first Thursday of the thaw. She laid the paper down and turned it toward him. There he was—the tribunal photograph, taken the day of his surrender, looking thinner and older than he felt. The headline was in a language he read slowly, but the word *Vessia* needed no translation. It had stopped being a dead country's name and become a question, and questions, unlike countries, could not be drowned.

"I'm not on the front page," he said. "The river is. I'm just standing in it."

"That's the correct reading." She sat, folding her long frame into the chair like a compass closing. "The mapping commission published the first bathymetric survey of the reservoir last week. They're calling the drowned valleys by their old names again. Lower Vessia. The Kestrel terraces. There's a village—what's left of it—above the line, and the elder you met, the one with the ledger, she's been asked to advise on the survey. She sent something."

Wren took an envelope from the satchel and slid it across. Inside was a photograph, printed on heavy stock: the elder standing at the reservoir's edge with a survey crew, pointing at water. Beneath the water, the sonar imaging showed the ghost-lines of walls, a bridge, an orchard laid out in rows. His grandfather's terraces. The engineer's terraces.

Isandro held the photograph for a long time. The cell was quiet in the way cells are quiet—no wind, no water, no birdsong, only the hum of pipes and the far bell. He had not realized until that moment how much of his life had been scored by moving water, and how strange silence was, and how he had come to prefer the silence, because silence was what staying sounded like.

"She looks well," he said finally.

"She looks *vindicated*," Wren said. "There's a difference. It has better posture."

He almost laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a cough, and Wren waited it out with the patience of someone who had spent months in caves and gorges with him and knew all his weather.

"Sefa?" he asked. It was the question he asked every Thursday, first or last, and every week the answer was the same and every week he had to hear it again anyway.

"Healing. The leg is pinned, not gone. The surgeons say he'll walk. Whether he'll climb—" She shrugged. "He says the mountains owe him one. He's keeping a list."

"And Dario."

The silence this time was Wren's. She looked at the table, at her own hands, at the window with its slice of slate and bell tower.

"There's a memorial service next month," she said. "The Vessian families are holding it at the reservoir. They asked for his name to be read with the others. The ones who were lost before, and the ones who were lost after." She met his eyes. "I told them I thought he'd have hated that, and they said that's exactly why it should be read. That a man who spent his life owing debts should have one paid to him that he can't refuse."

Isandro turned the elder's photograph over. On the back, in the elder's careful hand, was a line in Vessian script and, beneath it, a translation Wren had clearly added: *The river keeps what it takes. It also gives back, in time, to those who wait on the bank.*

His mother's handwriting had looked nothing like that. But then, he had only ever seen her handwriting on grocery lists and forged waybills, and perhaps everyone's true hand only came out when they wrote about what they had lost.

* * *

The trial, when it finally came, lasted nine days, and he remembered almost none of it except the sound.

He had expected argument, accusation, the theater of blame. Instead there was mostly reading. Clerks and survivors and archivists stood in the well of the tribunal hall and read the testimony aloud, name by name, ledger line by ledger line, the engineer's figures and the Compact's orders and the voices of the drowned, and the reading went on for days until the words stopped being evidence and became something else—a country, rebuilt out of its own paper, standing in the middle of the room.

On the ninth day they asked him to speak. He had prepared nothing. He stood where the witnesses had stood and found that his hands, which had never shaken on a rope, on a ledge, on the wet stone above the source, were shaking now.

"I spent eleven years moving," he said. "You don't stay anywhere when you're guilty, even if you don't know what of. You sleep in a different valley every night and you tell yourself it's the work." He looked at the tribunal, at the galleries where the Vessian descendants sat, at the empty chair where the Compact's senior clerks would have sat if any of them had been brave enough to attend, and were not. "I'm not going to move anymore. That's the whole of what I have to say. The river kept what it took. So will I. Whatever you decide I owe, I'll stay and pay it."

They gave him four years. The lawyers called it light. The newspapers called it complicated. Isandro, walking back to the cell that had a window, called it the first fair trade of his life: his freedom for the truth, the truth for the drowned, the drowned for the living who would now, at last, be allowed to say where they were from.

* * *

On the last Thursday of spring, Wren came with the satchel and the guard emptied it and found, as always, only paper—and one thing that was not paper. A small brass compass, old, the hinge worn bright.

"Yours?" he asked.

"His." She set it down between them. "Your grandfather's. It was in the ledger's wrapping, in the cave. The elder sent it down with the last of the survey mail. She said an engineer's tools should go to whoever finishes the work."

Isandro picked it up. It was lighter than he expected. The needle swung, settled, pointed north with the calm of a thing that had never once doubted where it was going, which he supposed was the difference between a compass and a man.

"The mapping commission starts the source expedition in the autumn," Wren said. "Above the reservoir line, all the way to the spring. They'll need guides who know the unmarked passes. Guides with clean papers." She let that sit. "I've been asked to write the official history of the Vessia testimony. The whole account. The chest, the gorge, the crew, the source. I told them I'd only do it if every name was in it. All the names. The ones that were read at the memorial, and the ones that weren't."

"Dario's is."

"Dario's is." She looked at him steadily. "And yours will be in it the way it actually happened. Not the blacklist story. Not the martyr story. The true one, which is worse and better than either."

He turned the compass in his hands. Through the window, past the bell tower, the mountains stood in their spring snow, and somewhere among them was the pass where he had first led men in the dark for money, and the gorge where the river had run wrong, and the spring where he had looked into the meltwater and seen the life he'd been living offered back to him like a coat in his own size. He had refused it. Some mornings, in the cell, he still felt the refusal like a wound. Most mornings now, it felt like a door.

"When I get out," he said, "I'd like to see the valleys. The ones with their names back."

"You will." Wren began repacking the satchel, paper by paper, with the economy of a woman who had never owned anything she couldn't carry. "The river's already on its way down to meet you. It's had a head start."

The guard came for her at the end of the hour, as he always did, and she went, as she always did, without ceremony—except that at the door she stopped, and looked back, and said the thing she had evidently carried up four flights of stairs and through three checkpoints to say.

"Your mother named you right, you know. Isandro. I checked the old registers. It's Vessian. It means *the one who stays*."

Then she was gone, her boots ringing down the stone corridor, and Isandro sat alone with the compass and the photograph and the window full of mountains, and for the first time in eleven years, everywhere he was not going felt exactly like home.
