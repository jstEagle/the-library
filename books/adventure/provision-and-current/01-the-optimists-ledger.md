## 1. The Optimists' Ledger

The coffee at the Blue Spruce Diner tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration, and Wendell Quintana drank it anyway, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

She'd chosen the booth at the back on purpose. Back to the wall, sightline to the door, and — more importantly — a clear view through the plate glass to the gravel lot where the *Vagrant Sky* sat gleaming under the sodium lights, her crew visible as silhouettes moving around the truck's open bay. Tomas was up on the roof rack re-lashing the anemometer mast. Priya had the bay doors open, the blue glow of her diagnostic rig spilling out onto the gravel like a puddle of arctic light. Dev was arguing with the fuel pump again, which meant Dev was winning, which meant they'd be paying for diesel at a rate that would make a banker weep.

Her family. All of them, right there, forty feet away and completely unaware.

The two men slid into the booth across from her without asking. They moved like men who'd done this before — one in front, one behind, a choreography of quiet intimidation. The one in front was older, silver at his temples, wearing a jacket too nice for a roadside diner off Route 9. He set a leather folio on the table between them, square and neat, the way you'd set down a chess clock.

"Ms. Quintana." His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. "Thank you for meeting us."

"I didn't meet you," Wendell said. "I was drinking bad coffee and you sat down."

"And yet here we are." He opened the folio. Inside, printed pages, crisp as frost. "My name is Mr. Aldous. This is my associate, Mr. Fenn. We represent the Meridian Syndicate's recovery division."

"I know who you represent."

"Then we can skip the pleasantries." Aldous turned the folio around so she could read it. She didn't touch it. She'd learned a long time ago never to touch their paper; touching it made it yours. "Your outstanding balance, including accrued interest and service fees, stands at two hundred and fourteen thousand, six hundred dollars. As of this morning."

The number landed in her chest like ballast dropped from altitude. Two hundred fourteen. Last spring it had been one-eighty. She'd been paying it down — she'd *been paying* — three hundred a month scraped from chase-season contracts and Priya's data sales and the occasional consulting gig where some insurance company wanted a storm verified by someone whose name still carried weight in certain narrow circles.

Three hundred a month against interest that ran four figures.

"You've been very diligent," Aldous said, watching her face with something that might have been respect in a better man. "Small payments, always on time. It's almost admirable. But the ledger doesn't run on admiration."

"The season starts in six weeks," Wendell said. Her voice came out level. Ten years of practice, keeping her voice level while the inside of her skull screamed. "We've got contracts. Tornado alley runs are booked through June. I can bump the payments to—"

"To eight hundred a month." Aldous smiled faintly. "Which brings your balance to zero in approximately thirty-one years. Ms. Quintana, I'm fifty-four years old. I'd like to see this resolved within my lifetime."

Fenn hadn't said anything. He just sat there, younger, broad-shouldered, hands folded, watching her the way she watched a supercell on radar — patient, tracking, waiting for rotation.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"We want what anyone wants. Resolution." Aldous produced a pen — heavy, matte black — and laid it across the folio. "Thirty days. One payment of two hundred fourteen thousand, six hundred dollars, or the Syndicate exercises its collateral claim."

Wendell went very still. "There is no collateral. The truck's leased, the equipment's—"

"The collateral isn't the truck." Aldous's voice stayed gentle, almost apologetic, and somehow that was worse than a threat. "It's the business. The name. *Quintana Storm Verification.* Your contracts, your client list, your reputation — all of it signed over when you refinanced, did you forget? You were in a difficult place. People sign things in difficult places."

She remembered. God, she remembered. Three years ago, after the hail season gutted them, after the transmission blew in Kansas and the hospital bills from Dev's rollover came due all at once, and the man from Meridian had sat across from her in a hotel bar in Amarillo saying exactly the words she'd needed to hear: *just a formality, just paper, we believe in you.*

They always believed in you right up until they didn't.

Outside, Dev slammed the fuel pump's access panel shut in triumph and threw both arms up. Even through the glass, Wendell could see him mouthing something triumphant at Priya, who gave him a thumbs-up without looking up from her screen. Tomas climbed down off the roof rack, tested a guy-line with two sharp tugs, nodded to himself. Forty feet away. A whole life, forty feet away, and she couldn't cross the distance without carrying this.

"If I miss the payment," Wendell said slowly, "you take everything. The crew walks away with nothing."

"The crew walks away employed elsewhere, I'm sure. People with skills land on their feet." Aldous closed the folio. "But I'll tell you something, off the record, because I think you strike me as a woman who appreciates information." He leaned forward slightly. "The Syndicate doesn't actually want your little operation. Trucks and radar rigs and five people who love each other — it's not worth our overhead to run it. What we want is the debt resolved. Which means if someone walked in here today with a way to make two hundred thousand dollars appear in thirty days, we would consider ourselves satisfied, shake your hand, and never trouble you again."

"That's not information. That's a fantasy."

"It's an incentive structure." He stood, buttoning his jacket, and Fenn rose with him, smooth as a drawbridge closing. "You're resourceful, Ms. Quintana. You read weather other people can't see. Find the opportunity in the forecast." He set a card on the table — plain white, a phone number, nothing else. "Thirty days. We'll be in touch weekly. Not to pressure you. Just to remind you that time is also a form of weather, and yours is turning."

They left cash on the table for the coffee — overpaid, of course, everything about them overpaid and precise — and walked out past the window where the *Vagrant Sky* caught the light. Fenn paused beside the truck. Looked at it. Looked at the crew. Made a small note in a small book.

Then they were gone, a gray sedan pulling onto Route 9, taillights shrinking into the dusk coming down off the hills.

* * *

Wendell sat in the booth for another four minutes. She knew because she watched the clock above the register, counting the way she counted seconds between lightning and thunder, measuring how close the danger was.

Four minutes to put her face back together. She'd gotten good at that too.

When she pushed through the diner door into the cold, the smell hit her first — diesel and pine and the ozone tang of Priya's electronics — and then the noise, Dev already talking, Tomas already laughing at whatever Dev was talking about, and the whole warm ridiculous machinery of her life running on without her, oblivious, and she loved it so much it felt like a wound.

"There she is!" Dev spread his arms. "Tell these people the pump was rigged against us. Tell them I performed surgery."

"He wiggled a hose clamp," Priya said, not looking up.

"I *performed surgery* on a hostile fuel pump, and now we have full tanks at a price that constitutes theft in our favor, and I expect recognition."

"You'll get a plaque," Tomas said. He was standing by the truck's rear bumper, and he looked at Wendell a beat longer than usual — Tomas always looked a beat longer than usual, that was his whole gift, noticing things people tried not to show — but he let it go, the way he always did, filing it somewhere behind his eyes for later. "Fuel's handled. Priya fixed the uplink. We're ready for the Kestrel scouting run whenever you give the word."

And there it was, sitting in the middle of the evening like a stone in a riverbed: the Kestrel Range. Six weeks early. A scouting contract that had come through her inbox nine days ago from a client she'd never heard of, offering money that was almost insultingly good for what was, on paper, a simple job — site survey, sensor placement, weather-window assessment in the high passes before winter sealed them.

On paper, it was the answer. On paper, it was two hundred thousand dollars for three weeks' work.

On paper.

Because Wendell had opened the attachment, seen the coordinates, and felt ten years fall on her at once like a collapsing roof. Because those coordinates marked a basin she knew better than her own heartbeat, and a station she'd sworn on the graves of the dead never to look at again. And because the contract's terms had been strange — payment structured in tranches, a confidentiality clause thick enough to hide a body, and a line item for "archive retrieval" that no legitimate survey job had ever included.

She hadn't told the crew any of that. She'd told them it was a survey contract, good money, off-season work. They'd cheered. Dev had immediately started a spreadsheet.

Now she looked at them — Dev still glowing over his fuel-pump victory, Priya finally lifting her head, Tomas watching her with those quiet eyes — and she made the only choice available to a woman drowning: she smiled like the water was fine.

"Give me the word tomorrow," Wendell said. "Tonight we eat actual food, we check the tire chains, and nobody talks about work until morning."

"Amen," said Dev.

They loaded up, and Wendell took the driver's seat last, and as the *Vagrant Sky* rolled out of the lot onto the darkening highway, she caught her own reflection in the mirror — calm, competent, captain-shaped — and behind it, faint as a watermark, the number ticking down.

Thirty days.

Behind them, far up the valley, the first clouds of the season were stacking themselves against the Kestrel Range like a debt coming due.
