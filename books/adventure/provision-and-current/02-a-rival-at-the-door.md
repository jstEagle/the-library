## 2. A Rival at the Door

The collectors' truck was still a dust plume on the highway when Ilsa Voss's Land Cruiser came up the diner's gravel lot, and Wendell understood, in the way she understood barometric drops before the instruments confirmed them, that the day was not finished ruining her.

She was standing at the window with a coffee she'd bought to have something to do with her hands, watching the dust settle where thirty days of her life had just driven away. Behind her, the crew occupied their usual corner booth — Tomas reading a paper topo map he refused to replace with a tablet, Priya elbow-deep in a radio housing, Dez telling some story with his whole body, fork for a prop. They were laughing about something. She let the sound of it wash over her like a warm front, thin and temporary, and tried to memorize it the way you memorize a sky that's about to turn.

The Land Cruiser door opened and the cold came in with her.

Ilsa Voss had not aged the way people aged. She'd aged the way weather stations aged — a little more equipment, a little more rust, the same structure underneath, load-rated for storms most buildings would never see. Silver at her temples now, cropped short. Same field jacket, older, the Meridian Syndicate patch removed but the sun-faded rectangle where it had lived still visible, like a scar from a tattoo she'd regretted. She scanned the diner once, cataloguing exits and occupants the way she catalogued everything, and her eyes found Wendell's in the window reflection before Wendell turned around.

"Quintana." A nod, almost respectful. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive." Wendell set her coffee down. "New rig?"

"Leased." Ilsa's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Everything's leased. That's rather the point of me."

* * *

They took the booth at the back, the one under the failing heater, because Ilsa asked for it and because Wendell wanted the crew in her sightline. Through the window she could see them in the reflection — Tomas now watching her with the particular stillness he got when he was counting something. Counting her heartbeats, maybe. Counting the lies she wasn't telling.

Ilsa laid a tablet on the table between them, face down, like a card she wasn't ready to show.

"I'll be brief, because you strike me as a woman who's done being handled today."

"You'd be right."

"Meridian gave you thirty days. I know because Meridian tells me things. Meridian tells me a great many things, Wendell, that would surprise you." She turned the tablet over. A contract glowed on the screen, dense with clauses, and above the clauses, a single line of figures that made Wendell's breath go shallow. Enough. More than enough. Enough to erase the debt, buy back the rig's title, put a year of fuel and salaries under the crew, and still have a margin left over — a margin, which was the truly obscene part. Debts didn't come with margins. Debts came with less than zero.

"What's the job."

"Retrieval. The Kestrel Range. Upper atmospheric research station, decommissioned eleven years ago. There's a sealed archive in the sublevel vault — magnetic media, hard copy, the whole institutional memory of a five-year monitoring program. Meridian wants it recovered before first snowfall seals the passes. That's a window of roughly three weeks, and shrinking."

"Why does a finance syndicate want a dead weather station's filing cabinet?"

"Because the data has value to parties I'm not cleared to name, and because I'm paid not to be curious, and I've gotten very good at both." Ilsa slid the tablet a few inches closer. "You're the best high-altitude recovery crew in four states, you're local to the range, and you're the only crew I know of that's run the Kestrel access roads. The fee is what you see. Half on signature, half on delivery of the archive to a point I'll designate. Non-negotiable, and yes, I know how that word lands with you."

Wendell didn't look at the number again. She was looking at the coordinates in the header — the site designation, the grid reference, the elevation in meters — and her body had gone cold and far away, the way it did in the last seconds before a cell collapsed, when the sky stopped being weather and became a decision.

She knew those numbers. She knew them the way she knew her own blood pressure. She had written them on her arm once, in marker, the morning of, because the radio was failing and she'd been afraid of forgetting. Eleven years, and she had never forgotten. She had only ever pretended to.

Station Kestrel-4. The one they'd evacuated. The one Marisol had gone back up for.

The diner noise receded. The heater ticked. Somewhere behind her, Dez laughed at his own punchline, and the sound arrived from a great distance, from another country, from a life she'd been building carefully on top of a hole for a decade.

"Wendell." Ilsa's voice, quieter now. "You've gone somewhere. I need you here."

"You knew." Wendell heard her own voice come out flat and dangerous. "You knew what those coordinates were when you walked in here."

"I suspected. I confirmed when your face did that." Ilsa folded her hands on the table. She had always had that gift, Wendell remembered — the ability to be cruel with tremendous gentleness, to deliver a wound like she was wrapping it. "I need someone who knows that station. Not a map crew. Someone who's stood inside it. The vault access is nonstandard, the approaches are nonstandard, and I have three weeks. If I'd sent you a job for any other mountain, you'd have said no in a sentence and we'd both have saved an afternoon."

"And if I say no now?"

"Then I take the contract to Halloran's outfit in Casper, and they'll get it done badly, and you'll spend your thirty days watching the number on your head grow interest." A pause. "I'm not threatening you, Wendell. I'm describing the weather."

That was the worst of it. That was the thing about Ilsa Voss: she never lied, not exactly. She just held up the sky and let you look at it.

Wendell looked at the reflection in the window. Tomas had stopped pretending to read. Priya's hands were still. Dez was watching the back of Wendell's head like he could see through it. Her crew. Her found, chosen, hard-won family — the people who'd taught her that a home could be a thing with wheels, that belonging was a thing you did rather than a place you had. The people she'd sworn, around a campfire eleven years ago with ash still in the air and Marisol's name still unsayable, *never again*. Never that range, never that kind of contract, never the arithmetic where a person's weight got weighed against a deadline.

And thirty days. Thirty days, and collectors who had looked at Dez's hands and Priya's throat and priced them the way you price salvage.

"You said half on signature." Wendell's voice sounded like someone else's. Calm. Professional. The voice she used on the radio when everyone was scared and someone had to not be. "Half on delivery."

"Standard."

"And the archive comes to you sealed. I don't open it, I don't inventory it, I don't ask what's in it."

"That's the shape of it."

"Then I have one condition that's not in your contract." Wendell turned the tablet face down again, her hand flat on its back, over the number that could buy them all free. "If we go in, we go in as a crew. My call on the mountain, my route, my go/no-go. You ride along or you wait at base, but you don't command. Not on that mountain. Not anywhere above the tree line."

Ilsa studied her for a long moment, and Wendell watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Wendell had just watched the collectors do in the parking lot, except Ilsa was pricing something else. Trust, maybe. Or leverage. Or the exact weight of a decade of guilt and what it could be made to carry.

"Agreed," Ilsa said. "Your call on the mountain."

They shook on it. Ilsa's grip was dry and cool and strong, and she held it one second longer than a handshake needed, and said, softly, so only Wendell could hear: "I am sorry it's this mountain. I want you to know that part's true."

Then she was gone, the Land Cruiser pulling out in a wash of gravel, and Wendell sat alone in the back booth with the contract glowing on the table between her hands and the heater ticking and ticking, and outside the window the western sky had gone the color of a bruise healing, high thin cloud streaming off the range like smoke from something buried and still burning.

The crew came over together, no discussion, the way they did everything. Tomas slid into the booth across from her. Priya stood. Dez leaned on the table.

"Who was that?" Tomas asked.

Wendell looked at the tablet. At the number. At the coordinates she could recite from memory, from a morning written on her own arm in dying marker while the wind took the roof panels off station by station, panel by panel, while Marisol's voice on the open channel said *going back up for the cores, Wendell, twenty minutes, I'll beat the cell* —

"Pack for cold," Wendell said. "We leave at first light."

She didn't tell them yet. She'd tell them at the trucks, or on the road, or maybe she'd let the mountain tell them, because some truths you could only say once and she needed to find the words that would keep them from walking away, and because — she admitted it, in the cold private ledger of herself — some part of her needed to be already moving, already committed, before they could see her face and read in it what she'd just signed them all into.

Tomas didn't move. He looked at her the way he looked at a sky that was lying to him.

"Where are we going, Wendell?"

She picked up the contract. The coordinates sat at the top of the page, small and exact, the most honest thing on it.

"Kestrel," she said. "Station Four."
