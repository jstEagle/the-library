## 3. Crew of Small Mercies

Tomas Reyes counted the crew the way he counted everything: twice, and with suspicion.

Wendell first, of course. She stood at the tailgate of the lead truck in the gravel lot behind the shop, a clipboard she never actually read held against her chest like a shield. She'd been back from the diner for two hours. Two hours was long enough for a woman like Wendell Quintana to bury whatever had happened in there under six feet of competence, but Tomas had navigated by her for nine years, and he knew her tells the way he knew contour lines. She'd come back too calm. Calm was what Wendell did instead of screaming.

Priya was second, cross-legged on the truck bed with the radio harness open around her like surgery, soldering iron hissing. She'd rebuilt their comms three times since spring and would rebuild them three more before they rolled, because Priya did not trust equipment that hadn't recently suffered.

Marta and Dee were arguing about fuel cans by the shop door, which meant everything was normal, which meant Tomas could stop counting and start worrying.

He crossed the lot and leaned on the tailgate beside Wendell. "Thirty days," he said quietly.

Her jaw tightened. Not a question then — she'd told him nothing, and he'd gotten it anyway, because Marta's cousin worked dispatch at the diner and collectors didn't drink coffee for free without leaving a story behind.

"Who told you?"

"Does it matter?" He watched her profile. The scar through her eyebrow, the one she'd never explained and he'd never asked about, caught the morning light. "Thirty days, Wendell. What's the number?"

"You don't want to know the number."

"I want to know why you took Voss's contract." He kept his voice low, pitched under the argument about fuel cans, under Priya's soldering. "I've watched you turn down three jobs this month that paid actual money. Then Ilsa Voss walks into our yard — *Ilsa Voss* — and you say yes before she finishes the sentence. You knew the coordinates before she said them out loud. I saw your face."

For a long moment Wendell said nothing. A crow worked at something in the gravel. Somewhere down the highway, a truck ground through its gears.

"The Kestrel station," Wendell said finally. "The archive they buried there. It has recordings from that night."

Tomas felt it land in his chest like a dropped anchor. Ten years, and she'd never once said *that night* without immediately talking about something else.

"Recordings of what?"

"Of the forecast calls." Her voice stayed flat, professional, the voice she used for weather. "Official ones. And some that weren't official. If they're intact — if they survived — they show what I called and when I called it. They show I didn't—" She stopped. Started again. "They clear me, Tomas. For the first time in ten years, there might be a version of that night that isn't just my word against a burned station and a syndicate report nobody let me read."

"And Voss wants them to sell."

"Voss wants them to sell." Wendell finally looked at him, and there it was under the calm — not fear exactly, something older and tireder. "But I'm not going to sell them. I'm going to get them out, and I'm going to use them. That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

"It's not the whole difference," Tomas said. "The other difference is that Kestrel killed four people, and you swore — we all swore — we'd never go back."

"I know what I swore."

"Do you? Because from where I'm standing, it looks like the debt made the decision and you're just riding along in your own truck."

That landed. He saw it land, saw the flinch she couldn't quite hide, and hated himself a little for throwing it even as he knew someone had to.

* * *

He found Priya still elbow-deep in the radio harness an hour later, after Wendell had gone inside to pull route maps and pretend the maps required that much attention.

"You heard," he said.

"I hear everything." She didn't look up. "Kestrel Range. Before snowfall. Voss paying enough to buy us all new trucks and Wendell a clean slate." She set the iron down and finally met his eyes. "You're going to tell me it's a bad idea, and then you're going to ask me to come anyway."

"I wasn't going to tell you it's a bad idea. You already know it's a bad idea. We both know it's a bad idea." He sat on the truck bed beside her. "I'm going to ask you why you'd say yes."

Priya wiped her hands on a rag, slow, thinking. "My sister's clinic in Alvarado closes in March if the loan doesn't restructure. Voss's advance covers it twice over." She shrugged, defensive. "There. Ugly reason. Your turn."

"My reason isn't ugly. My reason is standing in that kitchen right now reading route maps like they're scripture, about to drive herself back into the worst night of her life alone because she thinks that's what owing people looks like." He shook his head. "She doesn't get to do it alone. That's my reason."

"She'll hate that."

"She'll hate it and survive it. Alone she might not do either."

Priya looked at the radio harness, at the little green lights coming up one by one as the board tested itself. "Channel checks are good," she said, which was Priya for *yes*. "If we're doing this, I'm pulling the backup units from storage, and I'm hard-mounting the repeater, and nobody argues with me about weight limits."

"Nobody argues with you about anything. It's one of your best qualities."

* * *

By late afternoon the yard had turned into the organized chaos that passed, in their outfit, for planning. Marta had the fuel situation resolved through means she declined to explain. Dee had the winch apart and back together and was now testing it on the shop's anchor post with the grim satisfaction of a woman who expected the world to require pulling.

It was Marta who raised it, eventually, over the tailgate while they loaded chains. Marta, who never raised anything.

"So we're really doing this," she said. "Kestrel. In October."

"We're really doing this," Tomas said.

"Then somebody should say the thing everybody's thinking." Marta set a fuel can down harder than necessary. "This is the line. Loyalty's one thing. I'd follow Wendell into a hail core, I have followed her into a hail core, twice, and both times she got us out because she knew the storm better than the storm did. But Kestrel isn't a storm. Kestrel is a mountain that already beat her once, and this time she's not chasing it, it's waiting for her. There's a difference between loyal and suicidal, and I'd like somebody to tell me which side of it we're standing on."

Dee shut off the winch. The sudden quiet was loud.

"That's fair," Tomas said. "So here's my answer. Suicide is when you go in knowing you can't come out. This isn't that. This is going in knowing exactly how bad it can get, with the best navigator, the best tech, the best mechanic, and the meanest driver I've ever met, to bring home the only thing that's ever mattered enough to Wendell to be scared of." He looked around at them. "We're not choosing the mountain. We're choosing her. And if that line between loyalty and suicide exists, it's drawn by whether you leave together or leave alone, and I'm not leaving her alone on either side of it."

Silence. The crow again, somewhere. Then Dee snorted and threw him a ratchet strap.

"That was almost poetry, Reyes. Don't make a habit of it."

"He makes a habit of it," Priya called from the radio bench. "You just haven't been listening."

* * *

Dusk came early that time of year, blue shadows pooling in the yard, and Wendell emerged from the shop with the route maps finally rolled and a look on her face that Tomas recognized: the face of a woman who had argued with herself all day and lost to the version of herself that was honest.

She stopped at the tailgate where the four of them had gathered without arranging to, the way they always did.

"You could still say no," she said. "Any of you. Individually. Right now, no questions, no consequences. I'd sign the advance over—"

"Shut up, boss," Dee said.

"—because what's up there—"

"Shut *up*, boss," Marta said, gentler than Dee, which from Marta was practically a blessing.

Wendell looked at Tomas last, because she always did, because he was the conscience and consciences get consulted. He just nodded once.

She exhaled, long, like she'd been holding that breath for ten years. "First light, then. Priya, radios?"

"Green across the board. Backup units loaded."

"Marta, fuel?"

"More than legal. Less than I wanted."

"Dee?"

"Winch will pull God himself out of a crevasse, assuming God weighs less than eight tons."

Wendell almost smiled. Almost. Then she looked north, where the Kestrels were already wearing their first white like a warning, and the almost-smile went away.

"Tomas. Routes?"

"Two ways in," he said. "The east approach is faster but it funnels — one rockslide and we're a cork in a bottle. The river road adds six hours but gives us three escape lines. I'd take the river road."

"Take whichever keeps us alive longest," Wendell said. "That's the only navigation order I'll ever give you."

He was rolling the maps when Priya's radio crackled — the base unit, the channel they never used, the one only one other person had the frequency for. Static, then a voice, clipped and cold and familiar:

*"Quintana. Change of plans. I'm sending coordinates for a different staging point. Don't be late — the mountain won't wait, and neither will the people I answer to now."*

Tomas looked up. Wendell had gone very still, the way she went still reading a sky that had just turned wrong.

"That," Marta said quietly, "is not the voice of a client."

"No," Wendell said. "It isn't."
