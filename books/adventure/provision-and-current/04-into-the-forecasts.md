## 4. Into the Forecasts

The sky over the Kestrel Range was doing something it shouldn't.

Wendell stood at the shoulder of the road with her coffee going cold in her hand and read it the way other people read scripture — line by line, looking for the verse that would damn them. The lenticular clouds were stacking over the massif in three distinct layers, smooth as poured wax, and the topmost layer was streaming east when everything below it was flowing west. Wind shear. The mountain was wearing two different weathers at once, and only one of them intended to let them through.

"Beautiful, isn't it," Ilsa Voss said behind her.

"It's a warning," Wendell said. "Beautiful is what it looks like from a distance."

She'd been saying things like that all morning, and Ilsa had been absorbing them the way sand absorbs rain — politely, without any visible effect. The woman had ridden up from the diner in the second truck with Priya and the gear, and somewhere in the first hundred miles she had begun asking questions. Not the blunt kind. Ilsa's questions came dressed as small talk, wrapped in concern, delivered with the gentle persistence of water finding a crack.

*You were up there ten years ago, weren't you? What was the station like? Did you know the men who ran it?*

*I heard there was a storm. I heard it was bad.*

*Wendell. You can talk to me.*

She hadn't talked to her. She'd talked about barometric pressure instead, and jet stream behavior, and the difference between a chinook arch and a foehn wall, and Ilsa had smiled each time like Wendell had told her a secret anyway.

Now the convoy idled at the last switchback before the valley road, four vehicles in a line: Tomas's rig in front with the plow mount, the supply truck, Priya's comms van with its forest of antennas, and Ilsa's rental SUV bringing up the rear, too clean, too new, its paint still holding the memory of a showroom. Ahead, the road climbed through scree fields the color of old bone, and above the scree the mountain rose into cloud and kept going.

"Front's moving faster than the models said," Tomas said, coming to stand beside her. He had his binoculars up, watching the ridgeline. "Priya's pulling the updated feed. She says the low's deepening off the coast."

"Models always say what you want them to say until they don't." Wendell drained the coffee anyway. It tasted like the bottom of the pot, which was to say, like the morning deserved. "We make the lower station by dark or we don't make it at all. After tonight, that road—" she nodded at the switchbacks, where snow already dusted the highest curves like flour on a baker's hands, "—stops being a road and starts being a rumor."

Tomas lowered the binoculars. He looked at her the way he did when he was deciding whether to say something, which was often, and which she had learned over six years to respect and dread in equal measure.

"You've been quiet since Voss showed up," he said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *quiet* quiet. There's a difference." He folded the binoculars with a click. "The others see the money. I see the coordinates. You recognized them before she finished reading them out. You went gray, Wendell. I've seen you gray exactly twice, and both times the sky was about to kill somebody."

Wendell watched the clouds. The middle layer was thickening, boiling up from the west in towers that would, by afternoon, be a wall. Somewhere above all of it, the jet stream was doing its long patient work, peeling moisture off the Pacific and hurling it at the range like a man throwing buckets at a fire he hated.

"When we're through the pass," she said, "I'll tell you everything."

"You said that at the diner."

"And I meant it at the diner."

Tomas held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, and went back to the truck, and Wendell stood alone with the mountain and the cold and the verse she still hadn't read aloud.

* * *

They climbed.

The valley road gave way to gravel, the gravel to graded dirt, the dirt to a track that was more suggestion than infrastructure, and the trees began to lean away from the wind like witnesses turning from a crime. Wendell drove the lead truck with the window cracked despite the cold, because she wanted the air on her face, wanted to taste what was coming. Pine and snow and stone dust. Underneath it, faint and getting stronger, the mineral smell of a storm loading itself.

Priya's voice crackled through the radio. "Updated feed's in. That low's a monster now — nine-sixty-two and deepening. Landfall on the coast in eighteen hours, and the moisture plume is aimed straight at us like it's got our address."

"How much time before it reaches the range?" Wendell asked.

"Best case, thirty hours. Worst case—"

"Don't say it."

"—the orographics accelerate it and we've got twenty."

Wendell did the math without wanting to. Twenty hours to reach the lower station, assess the vault, and decide whether to push for the high camp. Thirty hours if the mountain was feeling generous, and the mountain had never once been generous in her experience. She keyed the radio. "Everyone tops off fuel at the cache marker. Check chains. If you've been meaning to fix something, fix it now, because there's no fixing after dark."

"Copy," said Tomas.

"Copy," said Priya.

A pause, then Ilsa's voice, smooth even through the static: "Copy. And Wendell? The vault — you've been inside one of these before, yes? The Kestrel stations?"

Wendell let the silence stretch until it became an answer, and then let the silence become static, and drove on.

* * *

The rockslide came an hour past the tree line, on a stretch of road Wendell had been watching with particular dislike.

It wasn't a bad stretch, objectively. It was cut into the mountainside with a sheer wall on the left and a drop on the right, maybe two hundred meters down to a river that ran gray with glacier flour. The problem was the wall itself — fractured granite, frost-shattered, the kind of rock that held itself together out of habit and spite. Ten years ago, in a different season, on a different road but the same mountain, she had watched a slope like it let go.

She had been thinking about that — not thinking, exactly; *hearing* it, the way the memory lived in her ears more than her eyes, the deep cough of the slope failing, the sound like the mountain clearing its throat — when she noticed the dust.

A thin ribbon of it, sifting from the wall two hundred meters ahead, right at the base of the fracture where the freeze-thaw had been working all autumn. Small stones first, pattering across the road like the first fat drops before rain. Wendell's foot was on the brake before her mind finished the sentence, and she had the radio up and was shouting "STOP — everyone stop, back up, NOW" while the ribbon of dust thickened into a curtain.

The convoy halted. In her mirrors she saw Priya's van slew and stop, saw Ilsa's SUV brake hard, saw Tomas already reversing with the calm of a man who had driven behind her through worse.

Then the wall coughed.

It wasn't the whole slope. It was maybe forty tons of granite, a house's worth of mountain, and it came down in a single grinding exhalation and buried the road where the lead truck would have been twelve seconds later. The impact came up through her boots. Dust rolled over the convoy in a brown wave, and for a moment the world was nothing but the smell of stone, ancient and indifferent.

When it cleared, the rockpile stood across the track like a door slammed shut — except it had missed. It had missed by a truck's length and a half, and beyond it the road continued, and past a shoulder of scree they could all see a way around, a steep gravel fan the trucks could crawl over if they respected it.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Priya's voice over the radio, thin: "Everyone okay?"

"Okay," Tomas said.

"Okay," said Ilsa.

Wendell keyed her mic and found her voice was steadier than she'd expected. "That's the mountain telling us it knows we're here. We go around on foot first, probe the fan, then one vehicle at a time. Nobody's a hero."

She climbed out into the dust-thinned air and walked the edge of the slide, and the others followed, and it was Ilsa who stopped closest to the fresh rockpile, looking up at the raw scar on the wall, and said, quietly, almost to herself:

"You knew. Before it came. You were already braking."

"I read the slope."

"You read it like you were *expecting* it." Ilsa turned, and her eyes in the thin light were the pale gray of the cloud deck, patient and cold and very awake. "Ten years ago, Wendell. The Kestrel station. There was a slope like this, wasn't there. There was a road like this. And there was a call that had to be made, and you were the one who made it."

The wind came down the valley and pushed between them, carrying the first dry sting of snow.

"Ilsa," Wendell said, "ask me what the barometric gradient is doing. Ask me about the moisture plume. Ask me anything about the sky, and I'll answer until you're sick of hearing it. But you don't get the other story on a road. You don't get it in a minute. That story costs more than you've budgeted."

For a long moment Ilsa said nothing. Then she smiled, small and unhurried, and looked back up at the scar on the mountain. "Everyone keeps telling me you're the best reader of weather they've ever seen," she said. "I'm starting to think what you actually read is *consequences*."

They crossed the fan one vehicle at a time, chains clattering, engines screaming in the thin air, each truck tilting toward the drop with the river far below, and Wendell drove last so she could watch the others through. When her own wheels found the far side and the road bent north again, the first snowflakes came down — not the storm yet, just the mountain's outriders, big slow flakes that landed on the windshield and did not melt.

Above the cloud deck, invisible but certain, the jet stream bent its long gray back, and the wall of weather began its march inland, and Wendell drove into it with the coordinates burning in her head like a coal she couldn't put down: the lower station by dark, the vault, the recordings, the voice.

The voice everyone thought was silenced.

She had almost let herself believe Priya had been wrong about that. Almost. But the mountain had just tried to close the road behind them, and in Wendell's long and expensive education, mountains only got aggressive like that about one thing.

They were expected.
