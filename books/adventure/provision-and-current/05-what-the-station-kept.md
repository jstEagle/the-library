## 5. What the Station Kept

The lower station announced itself the way ruins do — not all at once but in pieces, a rusted weathervane first, then a corner of roofline bent like a rib, then the whole gray skeleton of it rising out of the scree with its antenna mast snapped at two-thirds height and pointing back down the mountain as if in accusation.

Priya killed the engine and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel of the support truck, listening to the tick of cooling metal and the wind worrying the guy-wires that still stood. Behind her, Tomas's rig idled, headlights cutting cones through the thin afternoon light. Above them, maybe four thousand feet up a switchback trail that no longer existed except as suggestion, the high station waited under the glacier line. That was where Ilsa's coordinates pointed. This place was just the front door.

"Nobody's been here since," Wendell said.

She had come around to Priya's window without Priya hearing her, which was unusual. Wendell moved loud on purpose; she liked people to know where she was. But today she'd been quiet all morning, all through the last hundred miles of switchbacks, watching the mountain come down to meet them with an expression Priya couldn't file anywhere.

"Ten years," Priya said. "Maybe eleven. The corrosion on the mast says the paint failed fast — high UV, high sulfur soil. Nobody came back to repaint." She said it as data because data was safe. "Which means whatever they left here is exactly how they left it."

Wendell looked at the station for a long time. The wind pulled at her jacket hood.

"Priya."

"Yeah?"

"If you find something in there that isn't weather data —" Wendell stopped. Started again, quieter. "You tell me before you tell anyone else. Before Ilsa. Understood?"

Priya understood that this was not a technical instruction. She nodded anyway, and Wendell walked off toward Tomas's truck, shoulders squared like a woman walking into weather she'd already read.

* * *

The vault door was the only thing in the whole structure that hadn't rotted.

That was the first wrong note. Priya catalogued wrong notes the way other people catalogued faces — it was why Wendell kept her, why the crew kept her, though they mostly expressed it by leaving her alone with machines and bringing her coffee without being asked. Everything in the lower station had surrendered: the interior walls furred with mold, the instrument benches swollen and split, a coffee mug fused to a desk by a decade of freeze-thaw cycles, its glaze crazed like old porcelain. But set into the bedrock at the back of the server room, behind a collapsed rack of dead equipment, the vault door stood clean-edged and true, its seal unbroken, its keypad dark and waiting.

"Intact," she called out, and heard boots in the corridor, and hated herself a little for the relief in her own voice. Ten years of abandonment should have meant water intrusion, condensation cycling, every seal degraded. Instead: pristine. Someone had maintained this door. Someone had wanted what was inside it to survive.

Rigged was the second wrong note, and it found her forty minutes later.

Tomas held the flashlight while she worked, his breath fogging in the cold, his big hands steady on the light the way they were steady on everything. He was the crew's conscience and its navigator, and right now he was also its wall, planted between Priya and the doorway so that if anything went wrong it would go wrong through him first.

"You're frowning," he said. "You only frown when it's bad."

"I'm frowning when it's interesting." She traced the conduit running from the vault's maintenance panel up into the ceiling void. It was new. Not new-new — years old, oxidized, but newer than the building, newer than the accident. And it didn't terminate anywhere sensible. It looped back into itself, a circuit with no load, no indicator, no purpose she could name until she put her meter across it and watched the numbers hold steady against all logic.

"There's power on this line," she said. "Trickle current. Off-grid, battery bank somewhere, probably buried. It's a tripwire, Tomas. Not a bomb — I don't think it's a bomb — but if we cut the wrong conductor or force the door without the handshake sequence, it screams. Cellular burst transmitter, probably. Maybe satellite. Somebody gets a text message with our coordinates the second we touch this wrong."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. Outside, the wind changed pitch, dropping into the register that meant snow within hours, and Priya saw him hear it the way he heard everything, with his whole body.

"The syndicate," he said. "They knew about this archive before Ilsa did. You think they left a bell on it?"

"I think somebody did." She wiped her hands on her thighs. "Ilsa's contract says recover and deliver. It doesn't say who else knows it exists. It doesn't say why a dead station in a dead range has a live alarm on a vault nobody's opened in ten years." She looked up at him. "I can beat the handshake. Give me an hour. But Tomas — when I open this, we're telling the mountain we're here."

He considered that with the gravity he gave everything, even jokes, even breakfast.

"Wendell already told the mountain we're here," he said. "She's been telling it since the diner. Open your door, Priya. We'll deal with whoever's listening."

* * *

The handshake took fifty-one minutes, most of them spent with her heart going harder than any soldering iron deserved. She spoofed the maintenance cycle, bled the tripwire's capacitor through a resistor she built from salvage, and at 4:17 in the afternoon, with the light going copper outside the broken windows, the vault seals released with a sound like a long-held breath finally let go.

Inside: dry air, the smell of paper and cold electronics, and racks of storage drives in environmental cases, each one labeled in a neat engineer's hand. KESTREL-1 THROUGH KESTREL-9. Below them, a single case marked differently — no number, just a date, and Priya's stomach dropped through the floor before her brain caught up, because she knew the date. Everyone in this crew knew the date. It lived in the silences Wendell carried around like ballast.

"Ambient," she said aloud, to no one, steadying herself. "It's ambient audio. Station log. They recorded everything, all channels, continuously. Weather telemetry, comms traffic, room mics." Her fingers were cold and clumsy on the drive caddy. "This is the whole night. This is — Tomas, this is the entire night, from the inside."

He didn't ask which night. He knew. He'd signed onto this crew three years after the accident and he still knew, because Wendell's ghosts were load-bearing in all their lives.

"We shouldn't play it here," Tomas said slowly.

"No."

"But you're going to look at the index."

"But I'm going to look at the index."

She slotted the drive into her field reader with hands that almost didn't shake. The directory bloomed across her screen: telemetry streams, calibration files, forecast bulletins received and transmitted — and audio, hour after hour of it, timestamped, continuous. She scrolled past the daylight hours. Past evening. To 21:00 on the worst night of Wendell Quintana's life, and the hour after it, and the hour after that.

And there, in the channel list, a source tag that made her go completely still.

UPSTAIRS MIC — R. OKONKWO.

Ray Okonkwo. The fourth voice on the mountain that night. The forecaster whose body they never recovered, whose name was on the memorial Wendell drove a hundred miles around every year, the man the official report said had died instantly when the upper station's communications array came down — the man whose silence had let the report stand, had let the blame settle where it settled, had let Wendell spend a decade paying for a call that everyone said was hers.

"He was recording," Priya whispered. "The room mic was live. His mic was live. He was alive up there, after the collapse, and the station was still writing to disk, and nobody ever —"

She stopped, because the next thought arrived all at once and it was enormous: nobody ever retrieved this. Which meant nobody ever listened to it. Which meant Ray Okonkwo might have spent his last hour talking into a machine, saying things that had been sitting in the dark for ten years while the world blamed Wendell Quintana for his death.

Or — and here the awe curdled, here the suspicion came up cold through her chest like frost through soil —

Or somebody had known. Somebody had always known. Somebody had kept the vault sealed, powered, alarmed, waiting. And then a woman named Ilsa Voss had shown up with a contract and too many questions about the accident, asking for this exact box, in this exact range, before the snows closed.

"Tomas," Priya said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Why does Ilsa want this?"

He crouched beside her, read the screen, and she watched the same arithmetic move through his face.

"Payment enough to erase everything," he quoted quietly. "That's what she offered Wendell. Enough to erase everything." He looked at the drive. "You don't pay that much for weather records, Priya. You pay that much for leverage. For something that can clear a name — or bury one. Something worth more to the people who built the lie than to the woman it was built against."

Outside, the light had gone from copper to ash. Somewhere above them, four thousand feet up, the high station waited with whatever Ilsa actually wanted waiting inside it, and the first flakes were starting to come down through the beam of Tomas's headlights, fat and slow and early, weeks early, the mountain closing its hand.

Priya copied the drive twice — once to the encrypted array in the truck, once to a chip small enough to sew into a jacket seam — and then she went to find Wendell, because she had promised, and because some news you don't carry alone.

She found her standing at the tree line, looking up at where the storm was being born.

"Wendell," Priya said. "There's something you need to hear. And I mean that literally. There's a voice on these drives, and it isn't yours."
