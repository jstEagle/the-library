## 6. The High Camp Bargain

Ilsa Voss had learned long ago that the mountains did not care who you were, only what you carried. She stood at the mouth of the high camp tent and watched the snow come down in fat, deliberate flakes — the kind that meant business, the kind the Kestrel Range used to close its accounts for the season. Three weeks early. The forecasts had said ten more days of clear windows, but forecasts were a currency she had spent her whole life learning to counterfeit, and she should have known better than to trust anyone else's.

Behind her, the camp was a small economy of misery: two tents, a stove burning through fuel faster than the plan allowed, and five people pretending they weren't doing the same arithmetic she was.

Wendell Quintana crouched at the radio, turning the dial through static with the patience of a woman tuning a wound. Priya had the vault's control module open on her knees like a patient on a table. Tomas sat with his hands wrapped around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from. The other two hovered near the gear wall, saying nothing, which was its own kind of noise.

Ilsa pulled her gloves on and stepped out into the white.

* * *

The snow was already ankle-deep and coming down sideways off the ridge. Above the tree line there was nothing to break it, nothing to slow it, nothing to hide behind. She walked the perimeter out of habit, checking anchors, checking the sled lines, checking the lie of the drifts against the slope above them. The mountain here was a long gray shoulder rising toward the col, and somewhere past that col, two miles as the wind flew, sat the upper station and the vault beneath it.

The archive. Forty pounds of drives and reels in a titanium case that represented, depending on how you counted it, either her salvation or her coffin.

She had signed with the Meridian Syndicate eleven months ago. People assumed men like Aldous Renk collected debts with crowbars, and sometimes they did, but the elegant ones worked differently. Renk had sat across from her in a booth at the Meridian Club with a folder open between them — photographs of her sister's clinic in Cusco, the loan documents, the interest schedule that bent like a fishhook — and he had smiled the way a banker smiles, which is to say not at all.

"The Kestrel archive," he'd said. "You know the range. You know Quintana. Bring us the case, and your sister's ledger closes."

He hadn't mentioned Wendell's debt. He hadn't needed to. The syndicate liked its hooks distributed; it meant no single fish could pull free without snagging the others. Ilsa had understood then that she wasn't being hired so much as conscripted, and that the woman she'd spent ten years competing against was already drowning in the same current.

She'd told herself it didn't matter. That Wendell had made her choices. That everyone in this business was running from something, and the only sin was getting caught.

Now she watched the snow bury the trail behind them and understood that the window had closed on all of them equally.

* * *

She went back inside. The stove ticked. Wendell looked up from the radio, and for a moment their eyes met across the tent — ten years of rivalry compressed into a single look, and underneath it, unacknowledged, something older and more tired than rivalry. Recognition. Two women who had both learned that competence was cheaper than honesty.

"Nothing?" Ilsa asked.

"Static." Wendell set the handset down. "Storm's eating the repeaters. We're dark until it breaks."

"Will it break?"

Wendell's jaw tightened. She read weather the way priests read scripture, and everyone in the tent knew what her silence meant.

"How long," Tomas said quietly.

"Four days. Maybe six." Wendell rubbed her eyes. "We have fuel for three if we're careful."

The arithmetic landed on all of them at once. Priya closed the control module with a soft click, very final, like a door.

"So we go up tomorrow," Priya said. "Hit the vault, come back down before—"

"In this?" Tomas gestured at the tent wall, where the fabric shuddered under gusts like something breathing. "The col in a whiteout is a coffin with a view."

"We don't have a choice," Priya said.

"There's always a choice," Tomas said. "That's the problem."

Ilsa let them argue. She had learned that the best moment to make an offer was when other people were busy discovering the limits of theirs. She waited until the voices frayed, until Wendell raised a hand and the tent went quiet, and then she spoke.

"There's another way."

Every head turned. Good. Attention was a resource too, and she intended to spend all of it.

* * *

She took Wendell outside. Not because she wanted privacy exactly — the storm would take care of that, the wind shredding their words before they traveled a meter — but because she wanted Wendell away from the others, away from Tomas's steady gaze and Priya's quick suspicion. Some bargains curdled in front of witnesses.

They stood in the lee of the supply sled, hunched against the white. Snow gathered on Wendell's shoulders and she didn't brush it off. She just waited, arms crossed, the way she'd waited across a hundred briefing tables, across a dozen finish lines, across the wreckage of a decade.

"You want to tell me why you really took this job?" Ilsa said.

"You hired me."

"I hired a crew. You accepted for reasons of your own." Ilsa tilted her head toward the tent. "Your navigator doesn't know. Your tech doesn't know. They think you're doing this for the money, and they're following you into a killing storm because they love you, and you're letting them believe a lie because the truth is heavier."

Something moved behind Wendell's eyes — not anger. Anger would have been easier. It was the flinch of a woman hearing her own secret spoken aloud in the open air for the first time.

"Say what you came to say, Ilsa."

So she did. She told her about Renk, about the club booth, about her sister's clinic and the fishhook curve of the interest schedule. She told her that the syndicate had hired them both, that the archive wasn't payment — it was collateral, one piece in a portfolio of leverage, and that Wendell's own debt was probably sitting in some Meridian filing cabinet next to hers, filed under *assets, recoverable.*

"They never meant for us to be paid," Ilsa said. "They meant for us to be owned. The archive is just the collar tightening."

Wendell was quiet for a long time. Snow built up on her hood, on her lashes. When she finally spoke, her voice was flat and cold as the ridge above them.

"And your way is what, exactly? Walk away?"

"My way is smarter than that." Ilsa stepped closer, and she kept her voice gentle, almost kind, because she had learned that cruelty worked best dressed as mercy. "The storm gives us four days. The syndicate expects delivery through the eastern pass — that's what my contract says, and Renk trusts his own paperwork. But there's a helicopter service out of Valderra, on the western side. Expensive. Discreet. They fly weather, not paperwork."

"You want to steal the archive from the syndicate."

"I want to *sell* it to someone else. There are three news organizations and one federal investigator who would pay very well for whatever Meridian wants buried badly enough to send collectors into a mountain range." She let that land. "A cut each. Enough to clear your debt outright — not pay it down, *clear* it. Enough that you never take another call from a collector again. And enough left over to keep your crew flying for five seasons."

"And them?" Wendell nodded back at the tent. "My crew. Do they get a cut, or do they get abandoned at altitude while we fly out?"

Ilsa had prepared for this question. She had prepared answers the way Priya prepared tools, each one honed. "They get flown out too. Everyone goes home. But the decision can't be made by committee, Wendell. Five people keeping a secret is four more failure points than two. We take the case tomorrow, we tell them the syndicate took it, we walk them down to the tree line, and we're gone before they understand what happened. They grieve for a week, they hate us, and then they're alive, and alive people get over things."

"That's not a plan. That's a betrayal with logistics."

"It's survival with logistics," Ilsa said. "There's a difference, and the difference is who's still breathing in the spring." She reached into her jacket and produced the satellite phone, held it between them like a card laid face-up. "Valderra is one call away. The window closes tonight. After that, we're all just waiting to see whose debts collect them first."

The wind screamed across the col. Somewhere above them, buried in the white, the old station held its secrets, and somewhere below them, in the lowlands, the syndicate's collectors were already moving — Ilsa was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of weather. Renk never sent one hook when two would do.

Wendell stared at the phone. Then at her. And Ilsa saw her weigh it — saw the arithmetic run behind those storm-reader's eyes, the debt and the crew and the decade of ruin, all of it balanced against a single act of treachery that would buy everything back.

"I need to think," Wendell said.

"You have until first light."

"No." Wendell's voice hardened, and for a second Ilsa saw the woman from ten years ago, the one whose calls had ended lives, or so the world believed. "I mean I need you to answer me one thing first. The night of the accident. The station recordings. Did Renk tell you what's on them?"

And there it was — the question Ilsa had been avoiding since Cusco, since the club, since the folder opened. Because Renk had told her one thing, casually, the way powerful men say terrible things: that the archive contained audio logs from the Kestrel station's final night, and that Meridian had spent considerable money making sure those logs stayed buried, and that the person who'd falsified the forecast feeds that night had been *on Meridian's payroll.*

Which meant Wendell Quintana might be innocent. Which meant the debt crushing her had been built on a lie the syndicate itself had authored. Which meant the archive wasn't collateral at all.

It was a bomb.

"First light," Ilsa said, and turned back toward the tent, because she could not stand in the falling snow and watch Wendell Quintana realize that the truth was worth more than any cut Ilsa could offer — and that Ilsa had known, and had offered her the money anyway.

Behind her, the storm thickened. By morning, the drifts against the supply sled would be waist-high, and the radio would be dead, and the choice would no longer belong to either of them.

Because that was when they heard the engines — low, wrong, diesel-heavy, climbing a road that should have been impassable — and Wendell turned toward the sound with an expression Ilsa had seen once before, ten years ago, on the worst night of both their lives.

Headlights bloomed through the whiteout below the camp.

The collectors had found them first.
