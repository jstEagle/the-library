## 7. Whiteout

The engine turned over twice before Ilsa let it catch, and in those two seconds Wendell understood everything.

They were at the high camp, the tents sagging under a foot of new snow that hadn't been in any forecast worth trusting. The archive case sat on the passenger seat of the lead truck like a sleeping animal — forty pounds of drives and sealed canisters that held, among other things, the sound of a night ten years gone. Ilsa stood in the driver's door with one boot on the running board, and she wasn't even pretending anymore.

"Storm's here early," Ilsa said. "You can see it coming down the valley. You're the one who taught me to read that, if you remember."

"I remember." Wendell's voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were using her mouth. "I also remember you don't drive the Kestrel descent in a whiteout. Nobody does."

"Nobody smart." Ilsa pulled her goggles down over her eyes. "But I'm not going down the descent, am I? I'm going around. East ridge road, the long way. It adds six hours and subtracts all of you." She smiled then, and it was almost the old smile, the one from the years when they'd chased storms together out of love instead of debt. "You could come. There's room for one more. There was always room for one more, Wendell. That was your whole problem."

Behind Wendell, Priya said something low and urgent to Tomas. The wind took most of it.

"The crew comes or nobody comes," Wendell said.

"That's what I thought you'd say." Ilsa revved the engine once, a small cruelty, a promise. "Then here's the arithmetic. The syndicate gets their archive, I get my ledger cleared, and you get a walk. Sixteen kilometers down to the tree line, in that." She nodded at the sky, where the western wall had gone the color of wet slate and the wind had begun to carry grit with its snow. "You've done it before."

The words landed exactly where she aimed them. Wendell felt the old scar open along its whole length.

"You don't know what happened on that route," Wendell said quietly.

"I know what everyone knows. Your call, your storm, four people who didn't come home." Ilsa's hand moved to the gearshift. "Maybe the archive says different. Maybe it doesn't. Either way, I'm not waiting around to find out with you standing behind me holding a wrench."

She dropped the truck into gear. Gravel sprayed. Then the taillights shrank down the east road, two red embers swallowed by the thickening gray, and the high camp went quiet except for the wind beginning to learn its own strength.

* * *

Priya got the radio working for eleven minutes.

That was enough to learn three things: the east road had already drifted shut behind Ilsa, so there would be no following; the lower station still had power and shelter, sixteen kilometers down the old survey route; and no rescue was coming until the front passed, which the forecast band put at somewhere between thirty-six hours and never.

"So we walk," Tomas said.

"We walk," Wendell agreed.

Nobody argued. That was the thing about her crew that strangers never understood — they argued about everything, fuel ratios and radio codes and whose turn it was to cook, but when the sky came down they folded themselves into her decisions like water finding a channel. She didn't know whether to be proud of that or afraid of it. Probably both. Probably always both.

They stripped the camp in twenty minutes. Tents, rope, stove, first aid kit, the emergency cache of ration bars Priya had hidden against exactly this kind of day. Wendell stood a moment longer than she needed to at the edge of camp, looking west, where the storm wall had arrived in earnest — a solid moving mass, white on gray on white, erasing the ridgeline the way an eraser takes a pencil line.

Ten years ago she had led four people down this same slope in weather just like it, because she had trusted a forecast that told her she had twelve hours and she actually had forty minutes. She had made every correct decision available to her with the information she had, and it hadn't mattered, because the information itself was a lie dressed up in official letterhead. She hadn't known that yet, then. All she'd known was the silence on the radio afterward, and the names, and the way the mountain kept what it took.

Now she knew. Now she had heard the fragment Priya had played her, the falsified feed, the voice on the recording that should have been dead. And knowing changed nothing about the fear. It only changed what the fear was for.

"All right," she said, and turned back to them. "Same rules as ever. Rope spacing, nobody breaks formation, and if I say drop, you drop where you stand. I've walked this route before. Not in this. But before."

"You walked it *after*," Tomas said gently. "That counts double."

Wendell didn't answer that. She clipped into the front of the rope and stepped off the edge of camp into the white.

* * *

The whiteout came for them fully within the hour.

It wasn't snowfall, exactly — it was the world being taken apart and reassembled wrong. Up became a rumor. The horizon dissolved. Wind hit from the northwest in gusts that felt physical, like hands shoving, and the snow moved horizontally, stinging, filling every seam of clothing, packing into goggle vents until the world shrank to a porthole. Wendell navigated by things no map recorded: the lean of the slope under her boots, the pitch of the wind against her left ear versus her right, the memory of a cairn she'd built herself a decade ago out of guilt and grief, three stones stacked at the top of the first switchback because she couldn't think what else to do with her hands.

She found the cairn. Or her boot found it, catching the base stone, and she stood over it for a moment with the wind screaming, feeling something in her chest crack open that she'd welded shut years ago.

"Quintana!" Priya's voice through the storm, muffled, frightened. "Talk to us!"

"Switchback's here," Wendell called back. "We're on course. Move."

They moved. Hours passed that might have been minutes. The temperature fell through numbers Wendell preferred not to name. They stopped once to drink, once to eat, and once because Priya's goggles iced completely and they had to clear them inside a huddle of bodies, four people breathing warmth onto glass in a circle while the storm tried to peel them apart.

It was on the third section, the traverse above the old moraine, that Tomas went down.

Wendell heard it before she saw it — a cry cut short, the rope going taut. She anchored herself and hauled, and they pulled him up from a shelf of hidden ice where his left leg had folded beneath him at an angle legs don't go. He was conscious, which was something. He was white-lipped and swearing steadily, which was more something. But the break was real. Tibia, maybe fibula too, the kind of fracture that ends a march unless you end it differently.

"Splint it," Wendell said, and her hands were already moving, already tearing the foam pad, already lashing, because competence was the only prayer she'd ever learned. "Priya, pole. Marco, take his pack."

Tomas gripped her wrist while she worked, hard, through two layers of glove. "Say it," he said. "Whatever you're deciding right now, say it out loud so we all hear it."

Wendell tied the last knot. She looked at him — navigator, conscience, the man who'd rallied the others when she couldn't ask — and she said it out loud.

"Nobody gets left on this mountain. Not today, not ever again. We carry him."

* * *

They carried him.

Marco took the front, Priya the back, Tomas slung between them on a litter of tent poles and rope, and Wendell broke trail ahead, reading the slope by feel, calling hazards back into the wind. The pace became brutal in a way the word barely covers. Fifteen hundred meters of descent that should have taken three hours took seven. Every step Wendell took, she took twice — once forward, once checking the line behind her, counting heads like a woman counting heartbeats.

And somewhere in the fifth hour, in the worst of it, when the storm reached down and shook them like a dog shakes a rag, Wendell realized she wasn't afraid anymore.

Not because the fear was gone. Because it had changed jobs. For ten years the fear had sat in her chest like a debt of its own, interest compounding, and every job she'd taken had been a payment against it, and every payment had failed. But this — hauling her people down the same slope, in the same kind of killing weather, refusing to lose a single one — this was the first time the fear had ever pointed forward instead of back. Terror transmuted. Resolve. Whatever the word was, she felt it settle into her bones like ballast.

At the top of the last pitch, where the moraine funneled toward the tree line and the old station lights should have been visible below, Wendell stopped and counted her crew. Four. All four. Breathing, moving, cursing, alive.

"Tomas," she said over her shoulder. "How's the leg?"

"Ask me when I can feel it," he called back, weak but grinning somewhere under the ice on his face.

Wendell laughed — actually laughed, a short wild sound torn away by the wind — and raised her head to check the descent.

Which was when she saw the lights. Not the station's steady amber glow, but moving lights, vehicle lights, three of them, climbing the access road from the valley floor. Headlights cutting cones through the horizontal snow, deliberate, searching.

She knew those headlights. She'd spent thirty days learning them outside a diner window.

"They found the pass," she said, and her voice went cold and level, the storm suddenly the smaller danger. "Get down. Get him down. Now."
