## 8. Debt Collectors on the Pass

Tomas counted them out of the white like a man counting his own bad luck: four figures, roped together, moving with the deliberate economy of people who had done this before. They came up the pass from the east side, out of the teeth of the wind, and they did not wave.

"Company," he said, and Wendell turned.

She'd been walking point for six hours, breaking trail through drifts to her thighs, and even now, half-frozen, she read the situation faster than any of them. Her eyes went to the figures, then to the sky — the bruised, boiling ceiling of cloud pressing down on the col — and back again.

"Meridian," she said. Not a question. "Four of them. Roped."

"How can you possibly—" Priya started.

"Their gear's too good for guides and too ugly for tourists." Wendell pulled her balaclava down and Tomas saw only her eyes, steady and terrible. "Everyone stop. Nobody runs. Running in avalanche country is how you die tired."

The crew stopped. Six of them now, strung along the pass like beads on a frayed wire — Wendell, Priya, Marisol hauling the sled with their salvaged supplies, Dev limping on a sprained ankle he hadn't complained about once, old Ruthie with her frost-nipped fingers tucked in her armpits, and Tomas himself, carrying the map of this route in his head because every paper copy was buried under Ilsa's theft or burned for warmth. Behind them, ten miles of whiteout they'd crossed on memory alone. Ahead, the descent that led to the far trailhead, to Ilsa, to whatever was left of the truth.

And between: four collectors who had chased them across two counties and one mountain range.

Tomas watched them come and thought about the strange arithmetic of debt. He'd grown up watching his mother count change at the end of every month, deciding which bill got paid and which got a promise, and he'd learned early that money was never really about money. It was about who could make you afraid. The Meridian Syndicate had made Wendell afraid for a decade, and she'd carried it the way she carried everything — silently, competently, until it nearly killed her.

Now the fear had caught up at eleven thousand feet, and the storm didn't care about anyone's ledger.

* * *

The lead collector stopped ten meters away. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, but his eyes were hard as drill bits, and his breath came out in ragged flags of vapor. His three companions spread slightly, flanking, professional about it even here, even now.

"Quintana." His voice carried flat over the wind. "Marcus Reyes. We met at the diner."

"I remember," Wendell said. "You ordered the pie."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, maybe, that she'd noticed anything about him at all. "You have something Meridian wants."

"We have frostbite and a dead radio," Wendell said. "Ilsa Voss has your archive. She took our vehicle and went down the west side. If you want it, you're chasing the wrong people."

Reyes smiled without warmth. "Voss is already handled. Contract's voided. You—" he gestured, taking in all of them, the sled, the ruined state of them— "you're the ones still holding what matters. The station keys. The access codes. Whatever she couldn't carry."

Tomas saw it land on Wendell like a weight. Because it was true. Priya had stripped the vault's authentication module when they'd realized the archive could be re-locked remotely — a paranoid precaution that had suddenly become the most valuable thing on the mountain. It rode in an inner pocket of Priya's jacket, small as a cigarette case.

"You're going to freeze to death standing there," Wendell said. "All of us are. So here's my offer. We walk down together, we share shelter at the tree line, and nobody does anything stupid."

"Our instructions say—"

"Your instructions were written by men in warm rooms." Wendell's voice didn't rise; it dropped, and somehow that was worse. "Look at the sky, Reyes. That's not weather anymore. That's a freight train. You want to stand on this col arguing about collateral?"

For a long moment nobody moved except the wind. Then Reyes looked up — actually looked, the way a man looks when he finally reads the page he's been pretending not to see — and Tomas watched the calculation happen behind his eyes. Pride against survival. Orders against arithmetic.

"Move," Reyes said. "Together. Any tricks and we leave you here."

They moved.

* * *

An hour later, descending the eastern shoulder, the storm caught them fully.

It came down the valley like something released rather than arrived — a wall of white that erased the world in seconds. Visibility collapsed to arm's length. The temperature fell so fast that Tomas felt the moisture in his eyelashes crackle. They roped everyone into one long line, collectors and crew together, because there was no other way to move, and that was its own kind of dark joke: bound to the men hunting them, sharing breath and rope and the small heat of human bodies in a killing cold.

Dev stumbled. A collector named Okafor — young, quiet, the only one who'd spoken a full sentence since the pass — caught him without being asked, hauled him upright, checked his rope. Their eyes met through ice-crusted goggles. Neither said anything. There wasn't language for it.

That was the thing about the cold, Tomas thought, planting his boots in the rhythm of the line. It didn't care whose side you were on. It collected from everyone at the same rate. And in its presence, the whole idea of sides started to feel like something invented by people who'd never been outside.

Wendell called a halt in the lee of a granite spine, and they crowded into it, backs to the rock, eight people breathing in the blue dimness. Reyes dug out a thermos of coffee that had gone lukewarm hours ago and passed it around without ceremony. Wendell drank, handed it back, and said, quietly, so only he and Reyes could hear:

"The archive isn't just weather data."

Reyes went very still.

"It's recordings," Wendell said. "From Station Kestrel-Four. From the night of the accident. Internal comms. Forecast transmissions. Everything the station logged before it went dark." She let that sit. "Including forecasts that didn't come from the station. Forecasts that were fed to it. Falsified. By people with Meridian letterhead."

Tomas's heart slammed. She was spending it — the truth they'd bled for, offered up like chips at a table — and he understood why even as it terrified him. It was the only currency she had left.

"You're lying," Reyes said, but his voice had changed.

"Am I? Ask yourself why the syndicate wanted that vault so badly it sent Voss, then sent you. Ask yourself why my debt was structured the way it was — why a storm-chasing outfit with one bad season owed more than three good ones could cover. Somebody built that debt on purpose, Reyes. Somebody needed me broke and quiet, because I was there that night, and I know what the instruments said."

The wind screamed past the granite. Somewhere above them, Tomas heard it — faint, wrong, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

"Snowpack's loaded," Ruthie said softly, to no one in particular. "Been listening to it settle all afternoon. This slope's a held breath."

"Then we keep moving," Reyes said, but he'd gone pale under the windburn, and Tomas knew the words had found their mark. Men like Reyes served the ledger, but they weren't stupid. They knew what falsified data meant. It meant the debt itself was rotten — and debts built on lies don't survive daylight.

They moved.

* * *

It happened forty minutes later, on the traverse below the corniced ridge, and afterward Tomas would never be able to assemble the sequence cleanly. Memory gave it to him in fragments, the way trauma always does.

A sound first — deep, percussive, felt in the chest before the ears. Then Wendell screaming "Slope! Off the rope, off the rope!" and the knife coming out, and the line cutting, and the world tilting.

The slide took the lower flank of the traverse. It took Okafor.

He'd been sweep, last in line, and when the fracture line propagated above him he did the right thing — dove sideways toward the rock spine, toward shelter — and the mountain simply outvoted him. Tomas saw him go under, saw Reyes turn and lunge for the rope that no longer connected to anything, saw the white close over the place where a twenty-six-year-old man had been breathing thirty seconds before.

Then it was over, and the silence after the avalanche was the loudest thing Tomas had ever heard.

They dug for two hours. Eight people digging with hands and helmets and a shovel, in the dying light, following beacons and probes, and they found him at a meter and a half, and he was already gone — cold takes what cold takes, and the mountain does not negotiate. Reyes knelt beside the body for a long time, his bare hands shaking, and none of his people spoke, and none of Wendell's either.

Finally Wendell crouched across from him.

"He tried to save my navigator's ankle an hour ago," she said. "I saw it."

Reyes looked up. His face had aged ten years in an afternoon. "Doesn't matter now."

"It matters to me." She pulled off her glove and pressed something into his hand — the station key fob, the physical master, the one piece of hardware they'd kept separate from Priya's module. "Take your people down. Take him home. When you get to signal, call this number and ask for Elena Marsh at the regional forecast authority. Tell her Quintana said to play the Kestrel tapes. All of them."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you go back to Meridian knowing what they built your paycheck on." Wendell stood. "You owe a dead man a reason, Reyes. This is the only one on offer."

Tomas watched the collector close his fist around the fob, watched him look at Okafor's body wrapped in a tarp, watched something behind his eyes tip over — not forgiveness, nothing so clean, just the plain arithmetic of a man who had seen what his employers' lies weighed.

"We'll dig him a cairn," Reyes said hoarsely. "Then we're gone."

* * *

They buried Okafor under stones at the tree line as the storm eased to a hard, steady fall, and the collectors descended south with their burden while the crew turned west, toward the trailhead, toward Ilsa.

Tomas walked beside Wendell in the blue dusk. His broken leg screamed with every step — he'd set it himself in the aftermath, splinted with a ski pole and Marisol's belt, and now he rode the sled part-time and hobbled the rest, refusing to be cargo entirely. Ahead, the valley opened toward the far trailhead, where a stolen vehicle sat and a woman with the archive thought herself safe.

"She'll run the moment she has signal," Priya said, checking the battered receiver. "If she hasn't already."

"She hasn't," Wendell said.

"How do you know?"

"Because the files are locked. She needs my codes, and she thinks she can trade for them." Wendell's mouth curved, and there was nothing warm in it. "She doesn't know I already gave the key away. To a man with a dead friend and a phone number."

Tomas laughed — a short, cracked sound that hurt his ribs — and then stopped, because ahead, where the trail bent around the shoulder of the mountain, headlights swept through the falling snow.

Someone was waiting at the trailhead. And they weren't alone.
