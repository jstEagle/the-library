## 9. The Recording

The far trailhead was a gravel scar at the mountain's knee, and Ilsa Voss was already there.

She stood beside the stolen truck with the archive case open on the tailgate, her back to us, as if she'd known we'd come — as if she'd left the door open on purpose. Snow came down in fat, unhurried flakes, the storm's vanguard, softening every edge of the world. Behind her the tree line began, black spruce marching downhill toward the valley and everything that waited there: roads, radios, people who believed what they were told.

Tomas leaned on my shoulder, his splinted leg wrapped in a sleeping bag, his weight heavier than it should have been. Priya flanked us with the signal rig cradled against her chest like an infant. Marsh brought up the rear, ice axe in hand, saying nothing. Four of us, half-frozen, ten days out from the high camp, walking out of a whiteout that had tried very hard to keep us.

Ilsa turned. Her face was wind-scoured and calm, and that calm made me want to hit something.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You should see the other guys," Tomas said. "There weren't any."

Her eyes went to the case on the tailgate, then to Priya's rig, and something flickered behind the calm — the first honest thing I'd seen on her face in twenty years of knowing her. "You walked out," she said. "Through that." She gestured at the white wall behind us, where the Kestrel Range had erased itself entirely. "You actually walked out."

"We had a good navigator," Priya said. "She just wouldn't admit it for ten years."

* * *

Here is what I want you to understand about that moment, because I have replayed it enough times to know all its rooms: I could have taken the case. It sat right there, three steps away, and Ilsa was alone, and whatever else she was, she wasn't a fighter. The collectors were scattered somewhere up the pass under new snow, one of them dead, the rest retreating or frozen or both. Nobody was coming. The mountain had cleared the board.

And inside that case was my name, clean. Ten years of running, of diners where I counted other people's money out of the corner of my eye, of crews who deserved better than a captain who flinched at weather reports — all of it sitting in a waterproof box on a tailgate.

"Play it," I said.

Ilsa tilted her head. "Wendell—"

"Play it. All of it. Here, where we can hear." I looked at Priya. "Patch it through the rig. Maximum gain."

"Why here?" Ilsa asked, and for once her voice wasn't smooth. "Why not somewhere warm, somewhere safe, with lawyers—"

"Because the last time someone listened to this station alone, they decided what the rest of us got to hear." I stepped closer. "Because you've been deciding things for other people your whole career, Ilsa, and I'm done being decided about. Play it."

The snow fell between us. Somewhere above, the mountain groaned — a deep tectonic complaint, snowpack settling, the range talking in its sleep.

Ilsa held my gaze for a long moment. Then, to my genuine surprise, she reached into the case and lifted out the drive.

"I wanted you to hear it anyway," she said quietly. "That's the part you'll never believe. I didn't take the truck to strand you. I took it because if the collectors caught up with you carrying this, they'd bury you with it. I've been trying to get ahead of them since the high camp."

"Liar," Marsh said.

"Probably," Ilsa said. "But not about this."

* * *

Priya jacked the drive into the rig and ran the patch cable to the little speaker she used for field diagnostics. Her hands shook — cold, exhaustion, or dread; with Priya you never could tell which fuel was burning — but her fingers found the controls the way they always did, sure even when the rest of her wasn't.

"Station log, October fourteenth," said a voice, and my whole body went rigid.

I knew that voice. Everyone who'd worked the Kestrel Range knew that voice. Dana Okonkwo, senior meteorologist, dead ten years in the collapse everyone blamed on me.

"Dana," Priya whispered. "That's — Wendell, that's Dana. She recorded herself?"

"She recorded everything," I heard myself say. "She was paranoid about liability. She kept a personal log of every forecast discrepancy. We teased her about it."

The voice on the drive was tired, clipped, professional. *"Fourth correction to the syndicate feed today. Meridian's regional model is showing a stable high over the western approach. It isn't. Barometric data off my own instruments shows a trough building that their feed doesn't reflect at all. Not a calibration error — the error is too consistent, too directional. Someone is feeding us a doctored product."*

My breath stopped working.

*"If the road crew gets the syndicate numbers tomorrow instead of mine, they'll send the convoy up the pass. In this pressure gradient, that pass will slide. I'm filing a formal protest and flagging the discrepancy to every downstream user. If anything happens—"*

Static. Then Dana again, later, her composure fraying at the edges. *"Meridian has rejected the protest. They're instructing the operation to proceed on their forecast. Their forecast. I've called Quintana directly, told her to hold her team off the pass regardless of what the client says. She agreed. Whatever happens next, understand: the call to run that route came from Meridian. Not from the field. Not from Quintana. From the people selling the forecast."*

The speaker hissed. Snow ticked against the tailgate. Nobody moved.

Ten years. Ten years I had carried it — the image of the pass letting go, the trucks going under, the inquiry that found *operator error, failure to heed conditions*, the debt that grew out of the settlement like frost out of a crack. I had built my whole life on top of a lie so heavy I'd mistaken it for bedrock.

"They built the debt on it," Priya said softly. "They falsified the forecasts, caused the accident, then collected on the settlement. They've been collecting ever since. That's the whole business model. That's what the Syndicate *is*."

"Not just the accident," Ilsa said. Her voice had gone flat, stripped of performance. "Keep listening. There's more. There's names."

* * *

There were names. Dana's log ran another forty minutes, and by the end of it the storm felt like the least hostile thing on the mountain. A regional director. Two forecast desk supervisors. A rotating cast of signatures on falsified products stretching back years before the accident and continuing after — because the accident hadn't been a mistake, it had been a *demonstration*. A way to show clients and creditors alike what happened to operations that questioned the feed. And the settlement, the crushing, career-ending settlement levied against the field operator who'd been lied to — that had been the point of the exercise. Wendell Quintana, made an example of, paying interest on a lie for a decade.

When it ended, the silence was enormous.

Tomas sat down in the snow. He didn't seem to decide to; his legs simply finished with him. "All this time," he said. "All this time you thought—"

"I knew what the report said," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's, scraped thin. "I stopped arguing with it around year three."

Ilsa closed the case. "Now you understand the offer I couldn't make you at the high camp. This doesn't just clear you, Wendell. This burns Meridian down to the studs. Which means they will spend any amount of money, do any amount of harm, to keep it from reaching daylight. You don't hand this to lawyers. Lawyers work for people who pay them, and Meridian pays everyone." She looked at me, and beneath the frost of her expression there was something almost like pleading. "You trade it. One buyer, discreet, offshore. Enough to disappear on. Enough for all of you. That's the play. That's the only play where anyone survives this."

"And the names?" Priya asked. "Dana's voice? The truth just — goes in a vault? Sold?"

"The truth doesn't keep anyone warm," Ilsa said.

"No," I said. "It doesn't. But it keeps people *free*."

* * *

I walked to the tailgate and put my hand on the case, and I'll be honest with you, whoever finds this account: I stood there for a long time. The temptation wasn't a devil on my shoulder with a clever pitch. It was quieter than that. It was the image of the crew in a diner somewhere south, eating hot food, unpaid debts gone, no collectors, no running. It was sleep. It was ten years of my life handed back with an apology.

It was everything I'd sworn I didn't want, offered at exactly the moment I was too broken to refuse it.

"Wendell," Ilsa said, gentle now, gently the way a current is gentle. "Take the deal. Be done."

I looked past her, down the trailhead, to where the spruce stood black against the whitening sky. Somewhere down there was the memorial I hadn't visited in ten years. Seven names on a steel plate. Dana's among them. For ten years I'd read those names as an accusation. They weren't. They'd been a message all along, waiting for me to be able to hear it.

"Priya," I said. "How long to boost the rig onto the emergency band and rebroadcast?"

Priya blinked. "The — the whole thing? Unencrypted?"

"All of it. Every second. On every frequency that matters. Weather service, transport authority, the press bands, all of it. Let it rain."

"That's not a negotiation anymore," Ilsa said sharply. "That's a declaration of war. They will come for you with everything—"

"They've been coming for me with everything for ten years, Ilsa. At least now I know why." I turned to her, and I found that I was smiling, and that the smile didn't feel like mine so much as like something older, borrowed from a woman who'd died on a mountain making sure the truth got flagged downstream. "You wanted to sell the truth. I'm going to give it away. Free of charge. To everyone. That's the difference between us, and it's the only thing that was ever going to save any of us."

Ilsa stared at me. And then — God help me — she laughed, short and helpless, and stepped back from the tailgate with her hands raised, like a woman surrendering to weather.

"Do it," she said. "Before I talk myself into stopping you."

Priya was already moving, antenna up, cables flying. The rig whined as it charged. Snow thickened around us, the storm arriving in earnest now, the mountain pulling its white blanket over everything below the summit.

"Signal acquired," Priya said. "Say the word."

I put my hand flat on the archive case, on Dana's voice sleeping inside it.

"Send her home," I said.

The light on the transmitter went green, and the truth went out over the mountains, unstoppable as weather — and forty miles downvalley, in an office tower with a weather vane on its roof, phones began to ring.
