## 10. Provision and Current

The snow came to the valley in the last week of October, and for the first time in ten years, Wendell Quintana was there to watch it.

She stood on the porch of the shop with a mug of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the flakes come down fat and unhurried, filling the air over the lot where the rig sat stripped to its bones. Tomas had the hood up. Priya was underneath, her boots sticking out into the cold, cursing softly at a fuel line that had frozen and thawed and frozen again until it cracked like old bread. The radio on the workbench behind Wendell hissed through static, playing the tail end of the morning news.

"—investigators confirmed today that forecast data sold by Meridian Weather Analytics during the 2015 storm season had been altered before distribution. The company's parent holding group, widely known as the Meridian Syndicate, has filed for receivership. Three executives have been named in federal warrants—"

Wendell turned the dial down until the voice became a murmur, then nothing.

Ten years. That was how long the lie had stood. It had outlasted the station, outlasted the search parties, outlasted the memorial with its granite face and its seven names she'd never once read aloud. And it hadn't fallen to lawyers or confessions or anything so clean. It had fallen because a woman with a broken leg and frostbitten fingers had held a recorder up to a mountain wind and pressed play.

The fragment had gone out over an open amateur band at 4:30 in the morning, from the far trailhead, while Ilsa Voss watched from the cab of the stolen truck with her hands cuffed to the wheel and her breath fogging the glass. Priya had boosted the signal off the repeater at the lower station — the same repeater the syndicate had used to push their falsified forecasts a decade ago. There was a symmetry in that which Wendell still found almost funny, in the way you find things funny when they've stopped being able to hurt you.

The voice on the recording belonged to Dana Okafor. Everyone had thought Dana silenced. The archive had kept her anyway — her voice, her final transmission, her calm, furious insistence that the forecast numbers coming down from Meridian didn't match what the sky was doing. *These gradients are wrong. Somebody check my math against theirs, because one of us is lying.* Nobody had checked. Nobody had been paid to check.

The debt collectors had heard it too, standing in the pass with the avalanche roaring down on them. One of them — Reyes, the lead, the one with the patient eyes — had survived, and it was Reyes who'd testified first. People said afterward that Wendell had bargained like a card shark in the killing cold. She didn't remember it that way. She remembered shouting over the wind, telling a stranger the truth because it was the only thing left in her pockets, and the stranger choosing to believe her because the alternative was dying for a lie.

* * *

Tomas came up onto the porch, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being gray sometime around the second season. He was walking without the limp now, mostly. The leg had set straight. He lowered himself into the chair beside hers with the careful economy of a man who'd learned exactly what his body cost.

"Fuel line's done," he said. "Priya's ordering two. One for the rig, one for when you inevitably do something stupid to the first one."

"That's fair."

"Also," he said, "the bank called again."

Wendell looked at him.

"They wanted to confirm the lien release went through." A pause, and then the grin broke through, the one he'd been carrying around for three weeks like contraband. "It went through. You're clear, Wendell. Paper-clean. First time since I've known you."

She nodded, and found she couldn't say anything for a moment. Thirty days. That was what the collectors had given her in a diner booth that smelled like burnt coffee, back when the hole had seemed bottomless and the crew outside had been laughing about something, unaware. She had walked out of that diner certain she would lose them all — to the debt, to the mountain, to the truth, to something. Instead they were here, in her shop, rebuilding her rig with their own money and their own hands, and the only thing the syndicate had ever actually owned was the lie.

"Clear," she repeated.

"Clear," Tomas said. "So. Season planning. Priya wants the northern plains. I want the plains. Even the truck wants the plains."

"The truck doesn't get a vote."

"The truck has seniority."

She laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising her — and the flakes kept falling, soft as ash, soft as forgiveness.

* * *

Ilsa Voss vanished the way weather vanishes: completely, and leaving everyone arguing about what had caused her.

There were sightings for a while. A woman matching her description boarding a freighter in Anchorage. A woman matching her description winning at cards in a town so small it didn't have a name on some maps. The warrants named three executives but not her; whatever arrangement she'd cut with the syndicate had dissolved along with everything else, and the investigators could prove she'd taken the archive but not, technically, that taking it had been a crime. She'd signed a contract. Contracts were what Ilsa understood.

Wendell got one message from her, six weeks after the trailhead. No return address. Four lines, typed:

*You'll want to hate me and find you can't quite manage it. I know the feeling. For what it's worth, I never thought you made the wrong call on the mountain. I just thought you'd made it for free.*

That was all. Wendell read it twice, then deleted it, then sat for a long time looking at the empty screen, understanding that this was the closest thing to an apology the world was ever going to issue, and deciding it was enough. Not forgiven. Enough. There was a difference, and she was learning to live inside it.

* * *

The memorial stood on a shoulder of land above the valley road, a granite slab angled toward the Kestrel Range like a listener. Seven names cut deep into the stone. Dana Okafor's was third from the top.

Wendell had driven past it maybe four hundred times in ten years. She knew its shape in her peripheral vision better than most people knew their own faces. She had built routes around it, schedules around it, whole seasons around not stopping. Once, early on, she'd pulled over in the dark and gotten as far as the fence line before turning back, hands shaking, telling herself the light was failing. The light had been fine.

Now she parked in the pull-off in full daylight, in falling snow, and sat in the idling truck until the engine ticked itself quiet.

"You don't have to do it today," she said to herself, and then, hearing how it sounded, got out of the truck.

The path was short. The snow was an inch deep and unmarked except by birds. Up close the granite was rougher than it looked from the road, pitted by a decade of weather, the letters still sharp because grief cuts deep and stone holds. She stopped in front of it and read the top name silently, the way you test water with one foot.

Then she said it aloud.

"Marta Ellery-Vance."

Her voice came out rusty. She cleared her throat and kept going.

"Dana Okafor. Samuel Bright. Lena Marsh. Tomás Herrera-Reyes." She paused at that one — no relation to her Tomas, she'd checked years ago, obsessively, needing to know — "Cal Whitfield. June Park."

Seven names. The wind moved through the pines below the shoulder and carried the sound of them off toward the range, and nothing happened. The sky did not open. The mountain did not answer. The world simply received the names the way it receives all weather — without judgment, without memory — and Wendell stood there in the falling snow and felt the strangest thing, which was lightness.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I ran for ten years and it didn't bring any of you back and it nearly took the people who stayed. So I'm done running." She put her bare hand flat against the stone. It was freezing. It was also, she realized, just rock. "Dana — your math was right. Everybody knows now. Your math was right."

She stayed until the cold worked through her coat, and then she walked back down the path, and she did not look away from the memorial as she passed it. She looked at it the way you look at a grave you've finally visited: fully, once, and then forward.

* * *

The rig rolled out of the shop on the fourth of November, painted fresh, radar dome rebuilt, Priya's new antenna array bristling off the roof rack like a ship rigged for weather. They'd argued about the name stenciled on the door for two days. In the end it was Priya who won, the way she usually won, by being right in a way nobody could counter.

**PROVISION AND CURRENT**, it said, in letters tall enough to read from a passing car. Provision — what you carry. Current — what carries you. Dana's phrase, from an old field notebook Priya had recovered from the archive vault, the ink faded but legible: *A forecaster provides nothing. The sky provides. We just read the current and try to be where the people are.*

They loaded the trucks at dawn. Tomas checked the radios twice. Priya ran diagnostics with her headphones on, mouthing numbers. The new hire — a quiet kid named Amara who'd shown up asking about openings the week the news broke, saying she wanted to chase storms for someone whose forecasts matched the sky — secured the cargo straps and asked, for the third time, whether the northern route was really open this early.

"It's open," Wendell said. "Snows are late this year. We'll make the plains before the first front."

"And if we don't?"

Wendell looked at the sky over the eastern ridgeline, reading it the way she read scripture: cumulus building in bands, pressure steady, a clean wind out of the northwest with nothing behind it but distance. Ten years ago she'd have called it a boring day. Now she knew there was no such thing. Every sky was the sky from the accident, waiting to be read honestly.

"Then we drive through it," she said. "Together. That's the whole job."

Tomas climbed into the passenger seat and dropped the tablet of route plans into her lap. Priya gave a thumbs-up from the second truck. Amara pulled the gate shut behind them, and the chain rattled home.

Wendell started the engine. On the dash, taped where she could see it, was a printout of a single line of audio transcript — Dana's voice, the last thing the archive had said before Wendell burned the rest to save her people, the one fragment worth more than every page of debt paper the syndicate had ever held:

*Somebody check my math.*

"We checked," Wendell told the road, and pulled out of the lot, and pointed the convoy east into the wide bright morning, toward the plains, toward the season, toward whatever the current carried next.
