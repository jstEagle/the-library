## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The ledger was a lie the way all honest books are lies. It said Silas Galloway was a provisioner, a man of flour and lamp oil and salt pork, and it said so in a neat, upright hand that took him an unreasonable amount of pride to maintain. What it did not say—because there was no column for it, no line item, no place in the arithmetic—was that every entry in the book was a payment against a debt so old and so large that he had stopped writing it down years ago. Some numbers you keep in the body. They weigh more there anyway.

He sat at the depot desk with the stove ticking behind him and the ledger open under the last good light of the afternoon, and he entered the week's business in his careful figures. *Two sacks flour, Mrs. Ellery, paid. One case kerosene, the Hallorans, half on account. Salt pork, twelve pounds, the fishing camp at Tern Narrows, paid.* Small debts, small credits. A life measured in pounds and sacks, and nothing in it that could crush a man.

Outside, the junction did what the junction always did, which was nothing, beautifully. Two rail lines came together at Ashwater Junction and two rail lines went away from it, and both of the going-away lines had been closed for eleven years, and the rails rusted in their beds like veins in an old man's leg, still there, still carrying nothing. The depot itself had been decommissioned so thoroughly that the company had forgotten to decommission it, and so Silas lived in it the way a hermit crab lives in a shell someone else abandoned: gratefully, and with a constant low hum of anxiety that the original owner might come back.

He rather hoped they wouldn't. He had been the original owner, in a manner of speaking, of a great many things, and he had learned what came of it.

The kettle muttered on the stove. Silas finished the entry, ruled the line, and blotted it, and then he sat for a moment with his hands flat on the page, the way he did every evening, taking inventory of himself the same way he took inventory of the storeroom. One engineer, disgraced. One map, hidden. One debt, outstanding. One promise, kept so far, which was to be a small gray man in a small gray place and never again be the kind of man whose decisions ended up in newspapers.

He was, he reflected, extremely good at it. It was the only thing anyone had ever called him a natural at, and nobody had ever said it to his face.

The kettle screamed. He got up to pour the tea, and that was when he saw the boat coming down the lake toward the depot dock, and the tea went cold in his mind before it ever reached the cup.

* * *

Nobody came to Ashwater Junction by boat in the last week of September unless they wanted something. The steamer *Marten* ran her last scheduled call two weeks prior, and after that it was canoes and stubbornness. This was neither. This was a company launch, painted the gray of official paper, with a man in the bow who sat the way men sit when they have never once been seasick in their lives and consider it a moral achievement.

Silas watched it come from the doorway with his arms folded, and he did the thing he always did when trouble arrived on water: he counted the ways it could still be about something else. Freight, maybe. A survey party lost. A bear into someone's smokehouse, which had happened twice and made for the most exciting correspondence the junction had seen in a decade.

The launch bumped the dock. The man in the bow stepped off without waiting for it to be made fast, which told Silas he was either very confident or very new, and his face said very confident. He was maybe forty, dressed like the city thought the north dressed, and he carried a letter in one hand the way other men carry a pistol.

"Silas Galloway," he said. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking."

"Corver's office." The man held out the letter. "You'll want to read it inside. It's going to rain."

It was, as it happened, going to rain. Silas took the letter and did not read it inside. He read it on the dock, in the wind, because a man should know exactly how bad the news is before he lets it into his house.

The letter was from Corver's office, which meant it was from Corver, which meant it was not really a letter at all but a bill with sentences in it. Silas had owed Corver for nine years—not money, or not only money, but the kind of owing that gets called a favor by people who have never had one called in. In exchange for the owing going quietly to sleep, Silas ran provisions. Quiet ones. Up the Ashwater, up the tributaries, to camps and parties whose names never appeared in his ledger, whose manifests he never saw, whose business he had learned not to wonder about. Three or four runs a year. The interest, Corver called it. The principal, Corver had never once mentioned, which was worse.

This letter was different. This letter had a name in it.

*Re: Verrane expedition,* it began, and Silas felt the name land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He had provisioned the Verrane expedition in the spring. Six men, a mountain of crates, a radio set in a leather case, and a leader named Aldous Verrane who had shaken Silas's hand at the dock with a grip like a man signing something. Survey work, they'd said. The high country north of the range. Six weeks, they'd said, and a radio window every Sunday evening, seven o'clock, so the backers could sleep at night.

Silas had run them upriver himself, in fact. He had stood in the bow of his own boat and shown them where the channel ran and where it lied, and Verrane had stood beside him with his map case under his arm and asked questions—not the polite questions of a passenger, but the sharp ones of a man who intended to come back this way with authority. Silas had answered them all, and hated himself a little for how good it felt, being asked. Being *consulted.* Being, for six days, an engineer again instead of a provisioner.

*The expedition has missed two consecutive radio windows,* the letter said. *Your contract obliges you to conduct a resupply and reconnaissance run. Terms to follow. Be ready to depart within the week.*

Silas read it twice. The wind took the edge of the page and fluttered it, and he held it down with his thumb and read the second paragraph again, because the first time through he had missed the sentence that mattered, the way you miss the one stone in the channel that takes your propeller.

*Your contract.* He had never signed a contract. He had signed a receipt, nine years ago, one page, and Corver had kept it, and Silas had learned since that a receipt in Corver's possession was a contract the way a seed is a tree. Somewhere in that receipt, in language small enough to be honest by being unreadable, was the word *obliged.*

The courier was waiting on the dock with the patience of a man on salary. "You'll confirm receipt," he said.

"I'll confirm nothing on a dock."

"You'll confirm it eventually." The man almost smiled. "Everyone does. Corver says—" and here he consulted nothing, reciting it from memory, which meant Corver had made him memorize it, which meant Corver wanted it said exactly—"Corver says the river's been good to you, Galloway. He says it's time the river asked something back."

The launch pulled away. Silas stood on his dock with the letter in his hand and watched the gray boat shrink up the lake, and behind him the depot waited with its stove and its kettle and its ledger of small debts, all of it suddenly looking less like a life and more like a set of clothes he'd been allowed to borrow.

* * *

That night he did not sleep, which was not unusual, but he did not sleep *productively*, which was. He got the map out.

It lived under the floorboards beneath the stove, wrapped in oilcloth, and he had not unwrapped it in four years. It was his own work, or mostly his own—his father's surveys underneath, his own corrections on top, the closed lines of the northern backcountry drawn in the confident hand of a man who had believed, once, that knowing where the rails went was the same as knowing where he was going. Eleven years closed. Some of them never should have been. Some of them had been closed for reasons that had ended careers, his most thoroughly of all.

He spread it on the floor by stove light and looked at the Ashwater valley the way a man looks at an old scar. There was the junction. There was the gorge. There, north of the range, the blank paper where the map simply gave up, and where Verrane had gone with six men and a radio and a Sunday evening appointment.

Silas had told himself, all spring and summer, that the expedition meant nothing to him. Crates and flour and kerosene, a run upriver, a handshake, done. He had told himself the same thing about the depot, about the ledger, about the whole gray arrangement of his life: that it was a place he had chosen. But a man does not hide this well by accident. A man hides this well because he was once very visible, and being seen had cost him everything, and some part of him had understood all along that the hiding was temporary—that the world keeps a file on men like him, and Corver's office was where the file was kept.

He thought about Verrane's handshake. He thought about the youngest one, a boy who had carried crates like they were made of feathers and asked Silas the name of every rapid. He thought about Sunday evenings, seven o'clock, a radio window opening onto the dark like a door, and nobody on the other side of it now, two weeks running.

The dread that came up in him then was not the dread of danger. He had run enough water to know that the north killed people at a steady, uninterested rate, and that six men missing in the high country was sad and common and none of his business. No. The dread was older and more personal than that. It was the specific, sickening dread of being *remembered*—of the world turning over in its sleep, putting a hand on his shoulder, and saying *there you are, Galloway, we kept your place.*

He had spent nine years teaching the world to forget him. He had been so thorough about it that he had nearly managed to forget himself. And now a letter with Corver's name on it lay on his floor next to a map he had sworn to burn, and both of them said, in their different languages, the same thing: *not yet, Galloway. You're not finished paying.*

He folded the map along its old creases. He wrapped it in the oilcloth. And then, instead of putting it back under the floorboards, he stood holding it for a long time, the stove ticking, the rain starting against the dark windows, the rails outside rusting quietly in the dark like the veins of a thing that was not quite dead.

He put it in his pack. He told himself it was so it wouldn't be found. He was honest enough, at three in the morning, to know that was a lie with good posture.

The compass went in after it—his father's, brass-cased, the needle that had steadied more hands than his own. Then the kettle, the flour, the oilskin coat. He packed the way he had once packed locomotives: everything in its place, nothing carried that wasn't needed, and a man's whole life reduced to what would fit in a boat.

At the window, the rain came down the lake in gray curtains, and somewhere up that lake and up that river and up into the blank paper north of the range, six men had missed their appointment with the world.

Silas banked the stove. He wrote one last entry in the ledger by candlelight—*provisions, one expedition, outstanding*—and ruled the line, and blotted it, and understood as he did it that this was the first entry in nine years he had made in his own hand that told the truth.

Then he sat down to wait for Corver, because Corver did not send letters twice, and the letter had said *within the week,* and Silas had been a railroad man long enough to know that when a schedule says a thing, the thing is already coming, whether you're ready for it or not.

He was not ready for it. He poured the cold tea out, set a second cup on the stove to warm, and got ready anyway.
