## 2. Terms of Passage

The letter had been sitting on my table for three days when Corver's car came up the depot road, and I'd spent those three days pretending it hadn't.

That's a skill, pretending. I'd gotten good at it the way a man gets good at anything he practices daily for eleven years. I pretended the letter wasn't there while I swept the platform. I pretended it wasn't there while I inventoried the last of the winter stores in the freight room — forty pounds of flour, a case of tinned peaches nobody local would buy at any price, and a coil of rope I'd been meaning to sell back to the outfitter in Millbrook since spring. I pretended so thoroughly that when the black car ground to a stop in front of the depot and Corver stepped out into the gray morning, my first honest thought was: *well, there goes that.*

Corver hadn't changed. That was the trouble with men like Corver — they aged the way buildings age, settling a little, gaining a stain here and there, but fundamentally the same structure underneath. Gray at the temples now, going neatly with the gray suit. The same unhurried way of looking at a place as if pricing it. He looked at my depot the way he'd looked at me eleven years ago, across a desk, after the inquiry: *what is this worth, exactly?*

"Galloway." He said it like a man confirming an account number.

"Corver."

"You got my letter."

"I got a letter."

He almost smiled. Almost was as far as Corver ever went with anything; full smiles were bad for leverage. "May I come in?"

"It's your railroad," I said, standing aside. "Or it was. Depends which ledger you're reading."

* * *

He brought two men with him, because of course he did. They stayed by the door the whole visit, hats on, doing their best impression of furniture. One of them was big in the shoulders and small in the imagination; the other one kept looking at my maps on the wall with an interest I didn't care for. I made coffee anyway, because my mother raised me right and then life spent thirty years trying to raise me otherwise, and her training had mostly held.

"Six weeks ago," Corver said, wrapping both hands around his cup without drinking, "the Verrane survey expedition missed its scheduled radio contact. They've missed two more since."

"I heard something about that at the Millbrook dock," I said. "Dock talk. Half of it's weather and the other half's lies about fish."

"The backers are concerned."

"The backers," I said. "Meaning the Northern Concession Company. Meaning you."

"Meaning people who invested a great deal of money in a survey of the upper Ashwater basin, yes." He set the cup down with a small precise click. "There's a resupply mission being mounted. Boats, crew, provisions. Someone who knows the river above the gorge."

"And?"

"And I told them I knew a man."

I looked at him. Eleven years, and not once in all that time had Corver come up the depot road. Our arrangement had been built specifically on the principle that we did not look at each other. He sent instructions by letter. I sent receipts. Money moved through a bank in Millbrook like water through a culvert — necessary, invisible, never discussed. It was the most comfortable relationship of my adult life precisely because it required neither of us to exist.

"You told them you knew a man," I repeated.

"A man who provisioned the expedition in the first place. A man who knows every bend of the Ashwater from the gorge to the headwaters, because he surveyed half of it before—" He stopped himself, which was a courtesy, or a tactic; with Corver those were often the same thing. "Because he knows the river."

"And what does this man get out of it?"

Corver reached into his coat and produced a document. Not an envelope — a document, folded in thirds, heavy paper, the kind of paper that costs more per sheet than I spend on flour in a month. He laid it on the table between us and turned it to face me, squaring the corners to the table edge, because Corver could not leave a thing unsquared.

"Terms," he said.

I read contracts the way some men read scripture: slowly, skeptically, and with an eye toward what's been left out. Eleven years of provisioning runs had taught me that the money is never in the numbers. The numbers are bait. The money is in the clauses around the numbers, the little rooms built off the main hallway where things wait in the dark.

*The party of the first part agrees to guide, pilot, and otherwise convey...*

Standard. Fine.

*...to the last known coordinates of the Verrane survey expedition, and thereafter as directed...*

*As directed.* There it was, the first little room. Directed by whom? The document didn't say. It said *the party of the second part,* which meant Corver, which meant whoever stood behind Corver, which meant men whose names would never appear on any paper because papers could be subpoenaed and men could not.

*...and further agrees that all observations, findings, discoveries, or information of any nature encountered in the course of said engagement shall be reported exclusively to the party of the second part, and shall not be disclosed, transmitted, published, or communicated to any other party whatsoever, under penalty of...*

Under penalty of what, the document did not say either. It just left the sentence hanging there like a rope from a beam. I have found that in contracts, as in barns, a rope hanging from a beam means somebody expects to use it eventually.

I read it twice through. Then I read the compensation clause, which was generous — insultingly generous, the kind of number that isn't payment but purchase. And then I understood the shape of the thing, the whole architecture of it, and I set the paper down and looked out the window at the closed rail line running off north into the trees, rusted and weed-grown and dead these eleven years, and I felt something in my chest that took me a moment to name.

It wasn't fear. Fear I knew well; fear and I were old bunkmates. This was colder and cleaner than fear. It was the understanding of a man reading his own shipping manifest.

"You're not hiring me," I said. "You're spending me."

Corver's face did nothing at all, which from Corver was a great deal of expression. "That's an uncharitable reading."

"It's the only reading. Look at the structure of it. You want a pilot who knows the river, fine — but you've also written in silence terms broad enough to cover a man finding a pretty rock. You've got your two gentlemen at the door. You're paying three times what the job's worth. Nobody pays three times what a job's worth unless the job is expected to cost the man doing it something he can't get back." I tapped the hanging sentence. "And this clause here. *Penalty of.* Penalty of what, Corver? Finish the sentence."

"The penalty clause is standard."

"Nothing about you is standard. That's your entire reputation."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind his eyes, and I thought: *there. There's the man who signed the order that closed my line.* Because that was the debt, if you wanted the truth of it, though the truth of it was complicated the way all debts are complicated. Officially, I owed Corver money. Unofficially, what I owed him was quieter and heavier: he had held the paper over me for eleven years, and I had let him, because the alternative was a hearing and a headline and my father's name dragged through the mud a second time. My father had been dead nine years when the inquiry happened, and still I couldn't bear it. So I'd signed. And Corver had tucked my signature away like a man putting a bill in a drawer, and once a month the bill came due in provisions and silence.

"What did Verrane find?" I asked.

"That's precisely what the mission intends to determine."

"No. Your backers don't mount a rescue over a missed radio window. Rescues are expensive and rescues generate questions. If they wanted Verrane found, they'd have hired rivermen from Millbrook and paid them normal wages. Instead they've hired me — a man with a reason to keep his mouth shut — and written a contract that treats *information* like radioactive ore." I leaned back. "They know what he found. Or they know roughly. And whatever it is, they want it handled quietly, and they want the handling done by someone expendable enough that if it goes wrong upriver, the story writes itself: disgraced engineer leads ill-fated relief party, tragedy, condolences, case closed."

The room was quiet. One of the gentlemen by the door shifted his weight, boards creaking under him.

"You always did read well," Corver said finally. "It was wasted on rails."

"And yet here you are, needing a reader." I picked up the contract again, and I'll be honest with myself now, since I'm being honest about everything else: I looked at that compensation figure a long time. Long enough to be ashamed of. Because there was a version of me — the version that had spent eleven years paying down a debt in tinned peaches and flour, the version that told himself every spring *one more season and I'm clear* — that saw that number and did arithmetic. That number plus what I had saved equaled free. Free of Corver. Free of the depot. Free to go somewhere no one had ever heard the name Galloway spoken in the tone reserved for men who lose bridges.

Corver watched me do the math. He was good at watching men do math; I suspect it was most of his job.

"If I sign," I said, "I want one change."

"The terms are not negotiable."

"Then I don't sign, and you explain to your backers why the resupply mission has no pilot, because there are perhaps four men alive who know the upper Ashwater, and two of them are dead and the third hates you." I set the pen down. It was a cheap pen. I'd bought it for signing receipts, and I remember thinking at the time how strange it felt to own a pen whose only purpose was surrender.

Corver considered. Outside, wind moved through the poplars along the platform, and somewhere down the line a crow said something rude to another crow, and I waited, because waiting was the one skill I had that Corver might actually envy.

"Name your change," he said.

"Silence runs both ways. Whatever happens upriver — whatever I see, whatever I report to you — none of it touches the men I take up there afterward. No penalties attaching to crew. No follow-up visits to anyone who boats with me. Their names stay out of your ledgers entirely." I tapped the paper. "You can spend me. I've made my peace with being spent; a man who lives eleven years on borrowed time knows he's spending it too. But you don't get to spend other people."

Something flickered across his face — not warmth, Corver didn't traffic in warmth, but recognition. The look of a man meeting an opponent's move he'd already predicted and filed under *unlikely.*

"Done," he said. "Initial the amendment."

I initialed it. My hand was steadier than I expected it to be, and I took a small private pride in that.

"One more question," I said, as he rose and squared his coat. "The radio windows Verrane missed. Was the last transmission intact? Did anyone hear anything?"

Corver paused at the door. For a moment I thought he wouldn't answer, and then he did, and the answer was worse than silence.

"The last transmission was forty seconds long," he said. "Most of it was static. But the operator on watch swears that before it cut out, Verrane's man said one clear sentence." He put his hat on. "He said, *'Tell Galloway the map was wrong.'*"

The door closed behind him. The car pulled away. I stood in my depot with the contract on the table and that sentence in my ears, and I will tell you plainly: I sat down on the bench where generations of waiting passengers had worn the wood smooth, and I laughed. Once. It came out of me like something breaking surface.

*The map was wrong.* Which map? Verrane had taken my charts — the working charts, the honest ones, the ones I'd drawn from memory and soundings and eleven years of quiet runs. Those weren't wrong; I'd have staked my license on them, except I'd already lost my license, so I supposed I'd stake my life instead, which was apparently the going rate.

Which meant he meant the other map. The one I kept in the tin box under the floorboards. The one I'd sworn to burn a hundred times and a hundred times had folded shut instead, because burning it felt like burning the last proof that I had ever been good at anything.

* * *

I packed that night, because the boats were staging at Millbrook in two days and I'd learned long ago that dread is best metabolized through labor.

I packed light, the way a man packs who has moved too many times under poor circumstances. Spare shirt, oilskin, the good knife. Coffee, because I have standards even in ruin. And then I knelt on the depot floor and pried up the board by the stove, and took out the tin box, and opened it.

Two things inside. The compass first — brass case gone soft-edged with handling, my father's initials scratched crooked into the lid because he'd done it himself with an awl and more confidence than skill. He'd given it to me at sixteen, on my first survey crew, and said, "Needle doesn't lie, lad. Men lie. When a man tells you north is thataway, check the needle." He'd died believing I'd be chief engineer of the mountain division someday. The needle, faithful to the end, had pointed at everything except that.

And under the compass, wrapped in oilcloth, the map. The closed-lines map. Every decommissioned spur, every abandoned siding, every trestle and portage and ghost road of the northern backcountry, drawn in my own hand over eleven years of walking lines that officially did not exist. I'd sworn to burn it — sworn it aloud, once, drunk on one can of beer, which tells you something about the scale of my vices — because a map like that in the wrong hands was worth more than money and less than nothing. Because it was the record of the only years of my life that had belonged entirely to me.

I sat on the floor of the depot with the map in my lap for a long time. The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, the river I'd have to run in two days went on talking to itself in the dark, the way rivers do, saying the same thing over and over: *come down, come down, come down.*

I should have burned it. Any sensible man would have burned it. But I kept thinking of Verrane's operator, forty seconds of static and one clear sentence, and I kept thinking: *wrong how?* And a man cannot answer that question without references.

So I broke my oath again. I wrapped the map back in its oilcloth, and I slid it into the bottom of my pack beneath the spare shirt, where it lay against my spine like a splint on a broken bone — uncomfortable, possibly useful, definitely a sign something was wrong.

The compass went in my breast pocket, over the heart, the way my father used to carry his. I stood in the doorway of the depot a last time and looked north up the dead line, into the black timber, and I said out loud, to nobody, to the crows, to the cold:

"All right. Let's see what you found."

Then I barred the door, because even a man being spent ought to lock up behind himself, and went to bed, and slept — this surprised me — like a stone.

Two days later, at the Millbrook staging dock, I would meet the crew they'd assembled to accompany me upriver. But that morning, walking to my boat in the dark with a pack on my back and a liar's compass over my heart, I had no idea yet that among them would be a nineteen-year-old girl who'd lied about her age on her radio certificate, a doctor too kind for the company he kept, and a man named Hale whose smile I would learn to count teeth in.

All I knew was the weight against my spine, and the sentence riding in my ears like a frequency I couldn't tune out:

*Tell Galloway the map was wrong.*
