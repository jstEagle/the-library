## 3. Crew of Strangers

The staging dock at Millbrook Bend smelled of creosote, wet rope, and somebody's burnt coffee, and Silas Galloway stood at the end of it with his hands in his coat pockets, counting strangers the way he used to count bridge piers—looking for the one that would fail first.

There were five of them, not counting himself. Two boats, both shallow-draft freighters with outboard engines that looked newer than anything Corver had ever paid for. The rivermen were already aboard the lead boat, coiling lines with the easy economy of men who'd grown up arguing with water and mostly winning. One was big and slow-moving, a man built like a load of gravel; the other was lean and quick, all elbows, with a laugh that kept breaking out of him for no reason Silas could see. Nobody had introduced them yet. Rivermen rarely bothered. They'd introduce themselves when they decided you were worth knowing, or when you did something stupid enough to deserve a name.

The corporate man was easier to read, because corporate men always were. He stood apart from the boats on the dry part of the dock, checking a pocket watch against his wristwatch as though the two instruments might be conspiring against him. Gray suit, gray overcoat, gray expression. His name was Hale—he'd said it once, in a voice like a filing cabinet closing—and he had already asked Silas three questions about the river and listened to none of the answers.

The doctor was the puzzle. Brandt, he'd said. He had a canvas medical bag worn soft at the handles, and he was currently crouched at the dock's edge watching a kingfisher work the shallows with an expression of such uncomplicated delight that Silas found himself suspicious of it. Men on Corver's payroll weren't delighted by kingfishers. Men on Corver's payroll counted things.

And then there was the radio operator.

She couldn't have been more than nineteen, though her papers presumably said otherwise, because Corver's people didn't hire children. She sat cross-legged on a crate beside the second boat, headphones around her neck, running a screwdriver along the seam of a portable transmitter case with the focused tenderness other people reserved for infants. Her kit was military surplus, older than she was, and she'd clearly rebuilt half of it herself. When she caught him looking, she didn't flinch or perform toughness. She just looked back, level and unbothered, the way a lock looks at a key it hasn't decided about yet.

"Okafor," she said. "Junie."

"Galloway."

"I know." She went back to the transmitter. "You're the one who provisioned them. Verrane's lot. Six weeks of flour and lamp oil upriver, no questions."

"Somebody had to carry it."

"Corver says you know the Ashwater better than the river does."

"Corver says a lot of things. It's how he got where he is."

That earned him half a smile, quick and crooked, gone before it finished arriving. She tightened a screw, tested it with her thumb, and moved to the next one. "Radio window's every Tuesday, eleven hundred hours," she said, mostly to the machine. "They missed four."

Silas felt that land somewhere under his ribs, in the old bruised place where debts lived. Four missed windows. Six weeks of silence from people who had eaten his bread and drunk his coffee and told him, one cold evening at the depot, about a valley so beautiful the surveyor had stopped writing mid-sentence. He'd tried to forget their faces. Apparently their faces hadn't gotten a vote.

"Then we're overdue," he said, and walked down the dock to argue with Hale about fuel weights.

* * *

Junie Okafor had lied about her age twice in her life, once to the army recruiter's clerk and once to Corver's hiring man, and both times the lie had worked for the same reason: nobody expects a girl with braids and a soldering iron to be dangerous. She'd learned early that being underestimated was a kind of equipment. You carried it light, and it never ran out of batteries.

The transmitter was her third rebuild. The first two had belonged to her father, who'd run coastal weather reports out of a shed in Tarrow's Landing until the sea took his hearing and then, more slowly, everything else. He'd taught her Morse before he'd taught her cursive. *Dah-dit-dah*, tap tap on the kitchen table while she ate her porridge. *Who is awake out there?* That was the whole religion of it, as far as Junie could tell. Somebody sending into the dark, somebody answering. Everything else was paperwork.

So when the Verrane expedition went quiet, she'd forged a birth certificate, packed her father's spare headphones, and signed on with a company she didn't trust to find six people who might still be alive and tapping into the dark.

The problem was the crew.

She catalogued them the way she catalogued frequencies—by signal strength and interference. The rivermen came through clean. The big one, whose name turned out to be Mott, had carried her crate aboard without being asked and set it exactly where she'd want it, which told her everything. The laughing one, Fenwick, laughed even harder when the work got worse, which she recognized as its own kind of instrument reading. Good men, both. Simple signals.

Hale was pure static. He spent the first hour aboard measuring things—the freeboard, the fuel drums, the distance between Silas's shoulders and everyone else's—and writing the measurements in a little book. When Junie asked if he wanted her radio schedule, he said, "Eleven hundred Tuesdays," without looking up, as if he'd memorized it before she'd been hired. Which meant he had. Which meant someone above Hale cared very much about what the expedition had or hadn't transmitted, and cared enough to send a man who took notes about shoulders.

Brandt confused her most. The doctor spent the morning learning everyone's names and using them, which no one in authority had done to Junie since she was twelve. By noon he knew that Mott had a bad knee that clicked on stairs, that Fenwick was saving his wages for a boat of his own, and that Junie took her coffee with too much sugar, and he produced sugar from his bag as though the bag simply contained whatever kindness was required next. She kept waiting for the bill. There was always a bill. But Brandt only asked questions like *does your head hurt in cold fog* and *have you eaten today*, and wrote nothing down except actual medical things, in a hand so careful it looked like it was apologizing.

And Galloway. Galloway was a weak signal on a frequency she couldn't place yet. He argued with Hale about load distribution until Hale's pen stopped moving, and he won, and then he immediately gave away the victory by helping Fenwick re-rig a cleat he could easily have left broken. He talked to the boats more than to the people. When Mott asked him how long he'd run the Ashwater, he said, "Long enough to owe it money," and walked away, and Fenwick laughed, and Galloway didn't.

Around two o'clock, while the others loaded the last drums, Junie saw him do something strange. He took a folded paper from inside his coat—not a document, something softer than that, handled too often—and stood holding it at the rail, looking north up the gorge where the river went white and narrow between the walls. Then he put it back, deep, the way a man puts away a thing he's promised to destroy and keeps failing to.

She filed it. That was the other thing her father taught her: log everything, decrypt later.

* * *

By late afternoon the boats rode low and true, and Hale called a briefing nobody wanted, standing on the dock with his little book open like scripture.

"Schedule is fixed," he said. "Upriver tomorrow at first light. We locate the expedition, assess condition, secure any materials recovered, and return. Questions of salvage ownership are settled by contract, not sentiment." Here he looked directly at Silas, who examined the sky with the serenity of a man watching weather he intended to ignore. "Radio silence except scheduled windows. Dr. Brandt has final say on medical matters. Mr. Galloway has final say on the river. I have final say on everything else."

"Generous arithmetic," Silas said.

"It's accurate arithmetic."

"Those aren't usually the same thing."

Hale closed the book with a small decisive snap, and Junie saw, in the half-second before his face resettled, something flicker behind the gray—not anger, but a kind of tired recognition, like a man hearing an argument he'd already had with himself and lost. Then it was gone, filed away, and he said, "First light," and went to check his watch again.

Fenwick drifted over to where Junie sat and lowered himself onto the next crate with the gracelessness of a man whose limbs arrived slightly before he did. "So," he said. "You're the voice in the box."

"When the box works."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'm the girl hitting the box."

He grinned. Up close, Junie noticed, his laugh lived in his eyes even when his mouth was resting. "Mott says the old man knows the gorge like his own pockets. Mott also says the old man's lying about something, but everybody's lying about something, so it balances out."

"Mott sounds wise."

"Mott sounds like my grandmother. Same effect." Fenwick stretched until something in his back popped, wincing happily. "You done this before? Upriver in the cold season?"

"No."

"Me neither. Well." He considered. "Once, but the boat was smaller and I was fleeing a marriage proposal, so the circumstances were different." He hopped off the crate, then paused. "Hey—if we're out there and the box dies, and it's just us and the dark—what happens then?"

Junie thought about her father's shed, the lamp, the endless patient tapping. *Who is awake out there.*

"Then somebody answers," she said. "Somebody always does. Eventually."

Fenwick nodded slowly, as if this were the finest piece of navigation he'd heard all day, and went to bother Mott instead.

* * *

Silas slept badly, which was standard, and woke before the lamps, which was habit. In the gray hour he walked the dock alone, checking lashings he'd already checked, because the boats were the only honest conversation available to him.

He found Brandt awake too, sitting on the rail with two tin cups and steam rising off both, and before Silas could arrange his face into unsociability, the doctor handed him one.

"You're up early," Silas said, taking it because refusing seemed like more effort than drinking.

"I find the hour before dawn is when people are most themselves." Brandt sipped. "Minds haven't fully reported for duty yet. What slips out is usually true." He looked at Silas with mild, unhurried interest. "Yours slipped out yesterday, when you argued with Mr. Hale about the weight distribution. You put the plates' safety above the fuel. Interesting priorities, for a man being paid in fuel."

"The fuel's replaceable."

"So are the plates, I'd imagine. Paper burns." Brandt smiled faintly. "But you weren't protecting paper. You were protecting whoever carries it. I've spent thirty years watching what people reach for when the boat tips. It tells you who they are faster than any confession."

Silas drank his coffee and said nothing, and Brandt, to his credit, let the nothing stand, filling it only with the sound of the river working below the dock.

"One question," Brandt said finally, as the east went the color of a healing bruise. "The expedition. Did you know them?"

Silas watched the water. "I provisioned them. Six weeks back. They were—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "They were good people. Tired, underfunded, happy anyway. Verrane talked about the valley like it was a person he was introducing me to." His jaw tightened. "I told myself I'd forget their faces. Seemed safer."

"And now?"

"Now they've missed four Tuesdays." He drained the cup and set it on the rail with the precision of a man setting down something heavier than coffee. "Now I get to find out whether forgetting was ever actually on the table."

Brandt nodded slowly, and there was nothing clinical in it at all. "For what it's worth," he said, standing, "I don't think anyone on this dock believes you'll leave those people up there. Not even Hale. Especially not Hale. It's the first entry in his book."

* * *

They cast off at first light, exactly as decreed, the two boats sliding out from Millbrook Bend into the long green corridor of the lower Ashwater. Mist stood on the water in columns. Somewhere upstream a loon called, that sound like grief practicing.

Junie sat at her transmitter in the stern of the second boat, headphones on, listening to the ordinary morning chatter of the world—weather, freight schedules, a fishing lodge complaining about its generator—and underneath it all, straining for anything on the expedition's frequency. Nothing. Just the dark, and the waiting, and the faith that somebody was still tapping.

Ahead, in the lead boat, Silas Galloway stood at the wheel with his collar up, watching the gorge walls close in around them like a book beginning to shut. Behind him, Hale opened his little notebook and made his first entry of the day.

Junie logged it all, the way her father taught her. *Crew of seven,* she tapped idly against her knee, an old habit, a private prayer. *Who is awake out there?*

Forty miles north, if anybody was, they'd best answer soon. The mist ahead was thickening, and the river was already talking to itself in a colder voice.
