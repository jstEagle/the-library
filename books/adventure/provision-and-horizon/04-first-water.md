## 4. First Water

The Ashwater gorge announced itself the way a debt collector does—first politely, then not at all.

We'd been on the river two days when the walls began to close. The valley narrowed from a mile of spruce and gravel bar to a quarter mile of gray stone, then to less than that, until the water had nowhere to go but forward and fast. I'd run this stretch twice in my provisioning years, both times alone, both times with a light boat and a prayer I didn't put in writing. Now I had two loaded freight canoes, a motor launch riding too heavy in the stern, and five people whose lives were, according to a contract I'd signed without reading past the third clause, my responsibility.

"Is it supposed to sound like that?" Junie Okafor asked.

She was crouched in the bow of the lead canoe with her radio case strapped between her knees like a child she'd been told to protect. Nineteen years old if she was a day, though her papers said twenty-two, and whoever had written those papers had done it in a hurry with a bad pen. She had the particular alertness of someone who listened for a living—a radio operator Corver had thrown into the crew because somebody had to work the sets, and because nineteen-year-olds sign where you tell them.

"That sound," I said, "is the river telling us what it thinks of our plan."

"And what does it think?"

"It hasn't finished the sentence."

Hale stood at the rail of the launch with his arms folded, watching me the way an auditor watches a cashier. He was Verrane's company man—gray suit under a gray slicker, gray eyes that never quite landed on anything so much as assessed it. Three days on the water and he hadn't gotten wet above the wrists, which was its own kind of skill.

"You've run this before," he said. It wasn't a question, but I let him have the fiction of asking anyway.

"Twice."

"In flood or in fall?"

"Fall. Like now."

"And?"

"And the river was younger then." I checked the lashings on the supply bundles a fourth time, mostly so I wouldn't have to look at him. "Water's down maybe eight inches from what I remember. That means rocks that used to be polite are going to be opinionated."

Brandt laughed from the second canoe—a short, surprised sound, like he hadn't authorized it. He was the doctor, a big soft-spoken man with reading glasses he kept losing and hands that looked wrong for medicine until you saw them work. "Opinionated rocks," he repeated. "I'm going to write my sister about this trip. She'll never believe a word."

The two rivermen, Doss and Ferro, said nothing at all. They'd been hired out of the lower-river towns and they communicated exclusively in grunts and rope, which after three days I had come to respect as a form of wisdom.

* * *

The first real water came an hour before noon, where the gorge pinched at a place the old trappers called the Kettle—not on any map I owned, which was most maps, and named, I suspected, by someone who had watched a pot boil and thought, *yes, that's the feeling.*

I ran the canoes through first, talking them through it from the launch with hand signals we'd drilled until Doss stopped looking insulted by them. Then I took the launch myself, because the launch was the problem: too much draft, too much weight aft, and an engine that Hale's employers had bought new and cheap, which are usually the same purchase.

"Keep your weight forward," I told them. "All of you. Yes, even you, Mr. Hale."

"I'd rather observe from—"

"The stern is where boats keep their opinions. Move."

He moved. For a man who clearly resented taking instruction, he did it with remarkable efficiency, which told me something about him I filed away: he obeyed competence even when he hated the source of it.

The Kettle took us the way its name promised. The river dropped four feet in fifty yards through a channel barely wider than the launch, and the whole world became brown water and white noise and the smell of spray. I kept the bow pointed at the one patch of smooth green water—the tongue, my father would have called it, the river showing you the only door in the house—and rode it down with the throttle just enough to steer and no more. A rock the size of a church pew slid past my port side close enough to touch. Behind me, Brandt made a noise like a man being introduced to God.

Then we were through, into the long calm pool below, and the gorge went quiet all at once, the way a room goes quiet after a sneeze.

"Well," Brandt said faintly. "Well."

Doss raised one hand off his paddle—a riverman's ovation, worth more than applause—and Ferro actually spoke, which frightened everyone: "Clean line."

Junie was grinning so hard her face looked in danger of structural failure. "You didn't even look scared."

"I was scared the whole time," I said. "I just did it early, so it was over by the time you needed me scared."

Hale climbed back to his observing station, wrung out one sleeve with dignity, and said nothing. But that evening, when I was checking the engine's fuel line by lantern light, he appeared beside me holding the other end of the lantern without being asked, and held it steady, and left without a word when I was done. From Hale, that was a medal ceremony.

* * *

The near-capsize came the next afternoon, and it came, as these things do, from confidence.

We'd run three rapids clean. The crew had stopped flinching at every rock; Doss and Ferro had started humming while they paddled, which I should have recognized as the exact moment to start worrying. The fourth rapid looked, from the scout I made on foot along the bank, like a friendly version of the ones before it—same shape, same tongue, a little more push. What I couldn't see from shore, because the light lay flat on the water, was the hole waiting at the bottom of the drop: a reversal where the current curled back on itself like a hand closing.

The canoes ran it fine. Junie's bow punched through the trough and up the far side throwing sheets of water, and she whooped—an actual whoop, unguarded, nineteen years old for one full second before she caught herself.

The launch hit the hole broadside.

I felt it happen before I understood it: the stern lifting, the bow digging, the whole heavy animal of the boat beginning the slow stupid rotation that ends with everything upside down and everyone swimming in forty-degree water. Forty degrees is not cold, exactly. It is a decision the river makes about whether you get to keep existing, and it makes that decision quickly.

"Weight LEFT—" I shouted, and hauled the tiller hard against everything the river wanted, and gave the engine everything it had, which wasn't much, which was somehow exactly enough. The launch slewed, shipped half a ton of green water over the gunwale, hesitated on the edge of going over like a cow deciding about a fence—

—and came down right-side up, wallowing, half-sunk, alive.

For a moment nobody moved. Water sloshed around our ankles. Brandt was gripping the thwart with both hands, glasses gone, hair flat, wearing the expression of a man recalculating his entire relationship with rivers. Then Junie's canoe swung alongside, and she looked at the water sloshing in our bilge, and at me, and said:

"So. Was that early fear too?"

"No," I said, bailing with my hat. "That was the other kind."

"Which is?"

"The kind you earn by getting confident." I wrung out the hat and looked each of them in the eye, one at a time, because there was a lesson here and I'd be damned if anyone drowned learning it late instead. "The river doesn't care how good your last rapid was. Every drop is the first drop. We scout everything from here on, everything, and if anyone hums while paddling, Doss, I'm putting you ashore."

Ferro looked at Doss. Doss looked at Ferro. Neither of them hummed again for six days.

We spent two hours bailing and re-lashing, and somewhere in the middle of it, without discussion, the trip changed. I couldn't name the moment exactly. It was in the way Brandt started handing me tools before I asked. In the way Junie stowed her radio case low and centered without my telling her why. In the way Hale, when I said *we* scout everything, didn't correct me to *you*. Small things. Rivets, not beams. But a boat is nothing but rivets, and it holds or it doesn't.

* * *

That night we camped on a gravel shelf below a bend where the gorge opened just enough to show stars. Brandt cooked—rice and salt pork and, from somewhere in his medical stores, dried apples that he distributed like contraband treasure. Doss and Ferro turned in early. Hale sat apart with his notebook, writing in small square handwriting, and I did not wonder what he wrote, because wondering about Hale was a road I'd decided not to walk until walking it was unavoidable.

I waited until the fire burned down to coals. Then I got out the map.

Not the river chart—the other one. The one I'd sworn to burn and packed instead, folded into oilcloth and hidden under my spare shirt like a letter from a mistress. My father's closed-lines map. Every decommissioned spur, every abandoned siding, every trestle and grade and dead-end tunnel of the northern rail network, drawn in his hand thirty years ago when the lines were dying and he was cataloguing them the way a man catalogues the last birds of a species. I'd grown up on those lines. I'd helped him survey three of them. And when the Galloway name went into the mud—when the inquiry board took my license and the newspapers took the rest—I'd come north to live among the corpse of the thing I'd killed, keeping books at a junction depot, paying Corver, staying small.

I spread the map on my knees by the coal-light and read it the way other men read scripture. The old Sable Bend trestle, marked *timber rotten, passable on foot.* The maintenance spur above the Kettle. The portage grade where the rails had been pulled but the bed remained, graded and drained, better than any trail—because engineers build for permanence even when the world only asks for a season.

I don't know how long I'd been reading when I realized someone was standing behind me.

I knew it was Junie before I turned. She moved quietly, but radios teach you to announce yourself in other ways, and besides, Brandt snored like a landslide and she'd learned to step around it in her sleep.

She looked at the map. At the fine ruled lines running up valleys no road served. At the notations in my father's hand—*grade stable*, *trestle suspect*, *tunnel flooded '09.* At the compass lying open beside it, the brass worn bright where my father's thumb had rested for thirty years and mine was starting to wear it now.

She looked for a long time. Long enough that I could have lied, and didn't, and she saw me not lying, and something passed between us in the coal-light that neither of us would have known how to say aloud.

"Why do you carry it?" she asked finally, very quietly. Not accusing. Curious, the way she was curious about frequencies—what signal, what source, how strong.

"Because paper remembers what people are paid to forget."

She nodded slowly, as if that answered something she'd actually been wondering about, which I suspected it had. Then she said, "Your secret's safe. I'm good at those." And she went back to her bedroll, stepping around Brandt's snoring without waking him.

I folded the map away and sat a while longer. The river talked to itself in the dark, patient, unhurried, a creditor willing to wait. Above the gorge the stars were thick and indifferent, and somewhere north of us—six weeks north, past all this water—Verrane's expedition had missed its window, and whatever silence they were keeping, we were carrying toward it in two overloaded canoes and a half-drowned launch.

I slept badly, and dreamed of rails.

In the morning the gorge narrowed again, and the air had a new edge to it, thin and mineral, the first breath of the high country. Junie was at the bow with her set tuned, scanning the expedition's frequency in steady ten-minute sweeps, and on the ninth sweep of the second day she stiffened.

"Silas." Her voice had gone careful, the way voices do around broken things. "I've got something. Or—I had something. Two seconds of carrier wave, then nothing. Bearing due north."

She looked up at me, and the grin from the rapids was nowhere in her face.

"It was a distress tone," she said. "And then it cut off like someone's hand was taken off the key."

Behind us, Hale set down his notebook.
