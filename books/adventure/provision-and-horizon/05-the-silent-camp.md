## 5. The Silent Camp

The Ashwater narrowed on the third morning until it ran between walls of granite so close together that the pines leaning out from either bank seemed to be passing gossip over our heads. I'd run water like this before—different river, different life—and I knew what the gorge was telling us: *hurry now, and mind your manners.* The current had opinions. It did not care that we were six souls in two boats with a corporate minder who'd never poled anything but a golf cart.

"Is it supposed to sound like that?" Junie asked from the bow of my boat, one hand white-knuckled on the gunwale.

"Like what?"

"Like the river's chewing something."

"That's just the rocks," I said. "They're picky eaters."

She gave me a look that said she didn't believe me and had decided to survive anyway, which is the correct attitude for a nineteen-year-old with a forged birth certificate in her kit bag. Behind us, Hale sat in the stern of the second boat with his arms folded and his jaw set at an angle I was beginning to think of as *corporate displeasure*. The rivermen—Osk and Tam, brothers, built like the stumps they'd grown up around—handled the second boat well enough, though Osk kept glancing at me whenever the water got interesting, checking whether the old man up front was worried yet.

I wasn't worried. Not about the water. The water made sense. It was everything past the last known coordinates that had my stomach doing arithmetic.

We made the mouth of Survey Creek by early afternoon—a gravel fan where the tributary spilled out of a side valley, flat enough to beach both boats without ceremony. On the map Verrane's expedition had left behind, someone had penciled a small cross here and written *Base Camp* in a hand so tidy it looked typeset. Six weeks ago, seven men had stood on this gravel and called it home.

Nobody said much as we hauled the boats up. Even Brandt, who narrated everything—the geology, the birdlife, the alarming contents of Tam's lunch—went quiet. Some silences you can feel the weight of.

"Camp's upstream," Hale said, consulting a folder he'd laminated against the weather, because God forbid the weather should touch important paper. "Four hundred meters along the creek. Coordinates match." He looked at me. "You take point, Galloway. You've got the most experience in bear country."

"I've got the most experience in *country*," I said. "Bears are a subset."

He didn't smile. Hale struck me as a man who considered smiling a line item that hadn't been approved.

The trail along the creek was easy walking, and we found the camp exactly where the coordinates promised—which was the first thing that bothered me. Expeditions that come to grief don't stay put. They scatter, or they move toward help, or they dig in somewhere defensible. They do not sit neatly at their last filed position like a receipt waiting to be audited.

The tents were still standing.

All four of them, guy-lines taut, rainflies buttoned down, arranged in the same neat crescent a survey crew uses when they expect to be photographed by head office. The cook tarp was rigged over the fire pit. A kettle hung above a hearth of stacked stones, dry as old bones. Somebody's boots stood side by side outside the largest tent, laces tucked in, the way a careful man leaves them.

"Hello the camp!" I called, because protocol says you announce yourself even when every hair on your arms already knows the answer.

Nothing answered except a raven somewhere up the valley, making its comment on all human affairs.

Hale stepped past me into camp like a man entering a hotel room he'd paid for. "Verrane? Dr. Askeland?" He tried three names, then gave up on names and started on inventory, flipping open tent flaps with a pen he produced from somewhere, as if he intended to write things down rather than touch anything with his hands.

I went into the mess tent. Everything inside was orderly. Ration crates stacked and labeled. Tools racked. A pair of reading spectacles folded on top of a journal, waiting for a face. And there, on the folding table, bolted to the crate beside it—

The radio.

Junie reached it before I did. She'd been drifting behind me through camp with her hands jammed in her pockets, taking it in with those quick dark eyes of hers, and when she saw the set she made a small sound, involuntary, the way you'd react to finding a dog hurt.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, no."

The radio was a Kestrel field transceiver, military surplus, the kind of machine that survives being dropped out of helicopters. I've seen them with bullet holes still transmitting. This one had been killed deliberately and thoroughly—not smashed from outside, not thrown, but opened. The case hinges were undone. Someone had lifted the chassis out, laid it on the table almost tenderly, and then driven a stone through the transformer and the tube cradle, twice, methodically, before setting the whole ruin back inside the case and closing the lid.

"From the inside," Junie said. Her voice had gone very steady, which is how I was learning she sounded when she was frightened. "You'd have to know exactly where to hit it. That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's not rage. Rage throws the radio at a tree. This is a person deciding, calmly, that nobody talks to this camp ever again."

Brandt had come up quietly behind us. He looked at the radio for a long moment, then at the spectacles on the journal, then at the kettle hanging over the dead fire.

"They left," he said. "They weren't taken, and they didn't die here. You don't pack your manners if you're running for your life." He nodded at the boots, the laced fly, the covered woodpile. "And they expected to come back, or they wanted whoever followed to think so."

"Or," Hale said from the tent flap, "they moved on ahead of schedule and this is simply a forward cache. Expeditions leapfrog camps all the time. There's nothing here that requires—" He caught himself, recalibrated. "That requires alarm. We log the site, secure anything valuable, and proceed according to contingency."

"Contingency," Brandt repeated, tasting the word. "Is that what we're calling seven missing men?"

"Six missing men and one confirmed fatality," Hale said, and there it was—the thing he'd been carrying since the staging dock, finally set down on the table between us. "The backers received a coroner's interim report through a separate channel. One death, cause undetermined, prior to the missed radio window. Which is why the contract specifies recovery of remains as a priority deliverable." He said it the way a man reads a shipping manifest, and I watched Brandt's kind face go carefully, dangerously neutral.

"You might have mentioned that," Brandt said.

"It was in the briefing materials."

"The briefing materials were forty pages of liability boilerplate with a map stapled to the back."

"Then you should have read the back."

I let them have it. Arguing with Hale was like arguing with a tariff schedule—you could win points and lose the war. What I wanted was the ground itself. I ducked out of the mess tent and walked the perimeter, slow, letting my eyes do the work my mouth wasn't.

The tracks told it plain, if you knew how to read dirt, and I did—I'd spent enough years walking rail grades to know what ground looks like after men have passed over it. Here at the camp's edge, the story was written in a script anyone could learn: multiple boot prints, mixed sizes, heading north-northwest out of camp in a loose column. Not scattered. Not panicked. A column. And beneath the boot prints, cut deep and clean, the parallel grooves of sledge runners, heavy-laden, the drag marks of something substantial hauled away into the high country.

Seven men don't haul a loaded sledge uphill for fun.

"Hale," I called. "You'll want to see this."

He came, folder under his arm. Brandt came too, and Junie trailed after, and even Osk wandered over with the aimless curiosity of a man who'd finished his chores.

"Sledge tracks," I said, pointing. "Heavy load. Heading north into the pass. Boot prints are organized—look at the spacing, nobody's stumbling, nobody's veering off. Whatever happened here, afterward they moved together, on purpose, carrying weight."

Hale studied it. I could see him trying to fold the evidence into a shape that fit his folder. "Salvage operation. They recovered equipment and relocated to better survey ground. It happens."

"With the radio smashed?"

"A malfunctioning radio is dead weight."

"A radio smashed *from the inside*, with a rock, by somebody who knew where to hit it, is a message," Junie said. She'd come up beside me, and I noticed she positioned herself slightly in front of the tracks, the way you'd stand between a dog and something it wanted to dig up. "You don't smash a radio and then hike off with a sledge unless you don't want to be found. Or unless you want to be found by someone specific and no one else."

Hale looked at her for a moment too long. "Miss Okafor, your remit is communications, not speculation."

"My remit is radios," she said, "and I'm telling you what that radio is saying."

Brandt crouched at the edge of the trackway and touched the soil gently, the way he seemed to touch everything, as if the world were a patient. "These cuts are crisp. No melt-freeze slumping, no rain distortion. Days old. Maybe a week. Not six." He looked up. "Whatever they're doing, they were still doing it recently. They may still be doing it."

"So we follow," I said.

"We *log*," Hale corrected, "and we assess. The contract authorizes search of the last known coordinates. It does not authorize an extended overland pursuit into winter conditions with an unsecured—"

"With what?" Brandt asked mildly. "Unsecured what, Mr. Hale?"

For a heartbeat—one clean, cold heartbeat—Hale had nothing to say. Then the corporate surface closed back over like ice forming. "With an unsecured supply line," he said. "Obviously."

But I'd seen the gap, and I filed it where I was filing everything else: next to Junie's forged papers, next to the fine print in my own contract, next to the growing suspicion that the Verrane expedition hadn't gone silent because of disaster. Silence, sometimes, is a policy.

* * *

We spent the night in the expedition's own tents, which felt a little like wearing a dead man's coat, though nobody said so. Brandt cooked a proper meal—he was the only one of us who believed eating well was a moral obligation—and afterward Junie sat by the fire turning the wrecked radio's chassis over in her lap like a nurse with a broken bird.

"Anything salvageable?" I asked, settling onto a crate beside her.

"Maybe. The power section's intact. If I cannibalize the expedition's spare battery and rewire the audio stage, I might get receive-only working in a day or two." She glanced at me sideways. "Transmit's another matter. Why? Thinking about who they couldn't call?"

"Thinking about who they didn't want to call," I said. "There's a difference."

She turned the chassis in the firelight. "My instructor at the training depot used to say a radio never really breaks quiet. It breaks loud, or it breaks angry, or it breaks scared. This one broke *decided*." She set it down. "Silas. Those tracks going north. The sledge. You've seen this pattern before, haven't you? Don't tell me you haven't. You walk around camp like a man reading a familiar letter."

I fed a stick to the fire instead of answering right away. Across the flames, Brandt was showing Osk how to fold a bandage properly, and Osk was pretending not to be interested and folding it perfectly. Tam snored with the sincerity of a man who trusted the world. Hale's tent was dark, but I'd heard him moving in it late, and earlier I'd seen a red glow through the canvas seam that no lantern makes.

"I've seen men leave a place orderly when they're leaving in a hurry," I said at last. "Rail crews do it. When the company pulls out of a line, they button everything down nice, because the company wants the option of coming back, and because a man who leaves a mess leaves evidence of a decision he doesn't want examined." I looked at her. "Orderly isn't calm, Junie. Orderly is *managed*. Somebody managed this departure."

"And the radio?"

"Somebody decided the world shouldn't hear from this valley." I kept my voice low. "Now ask yourself who benefits from a valley that can't talk."

She looked at me for a long moment, firelight moving in her eyes, and I saw her arrive at the same door I'd been standing in front of since the staging dock. She didn't knock on it out loud. She just nodded once, slowly, and picked the chassis back up.

"I'll have receive working by tomorrow night," she said. "If anybody's transmitting on the expedition frequencies—or near them—we'll hear it."

"Near them," I repeated.

"I listen wide," she said, with the first real smile I'd seen from her in two days. "It's a bad habit. I intend to keep it."

* * *

Morning came gray and cold enough to make the gravel crunch. We broke camp in the expedition's shadow, and I'll admit I took one thing from the mess tent before we left: Verrane's journal, the one under the spectacles. Hale inventoried the scientific cases, the specimen boxes, the sealed document tubes—his hands lingering on the tubes a half-second longer than inventory requires—but he never looked twice at a mud-stained notebook, and I told myself I'd return it to Verrane personally, alive, and that this made it borrowing rather than theft. A man my age gets good at telling himself stories like that. It's how we sleep.

We followed the sledge tracks north.

They climbed along the creek through thinning timber, and the country opened the way mountain country does—gradually, then all at once, the trees giving up and the granite taking over, the peaks ahead stacked blue and white against a sky like unpolished tin. Winter was up there waiting. You could smell it, that mineral emptiness under the pine scent, the season holding its breath.

Two miles up, Brandt stopped walking.

He'd been ranging ahead a little, as doctors do, reading the ground for signs of human trouble, and now he stood very still beside a shelf of stone where the snow lay thin and wind-scoured. "Silas," he said, and his voice had changed—lost its lecture-hall warmth, gone quiet and level. "Come look at this."

The others gathered. Even Hale, drawn despite himself.

A grave.

Not a mound of stones thrown together in panic—those I've seen, and they look like argument. This was a rectangle, edges straight, turves cut and laid with care, a low cairn at the head with a single flat stone set upright. Someone had spent hours here. Someone had grieved here, or had done a very thorough imitation of it.

On the upright stone, scratched deep with a knife point in that same tidy hand I'd seen on the base-camp map, were words:

**E. MARSH — SURVEYOR — HE WAS RIGHT**

Brandt knelt and read the earth the way Junie read radios. "Dug with tools, not hands. Shallow—frozen ground, they did what they could. The cairn stones are placed, not piled. And look—" he pointed, gentle as a man turning a page, "the digging marks start *here*, at the head, and the throw-spoil fans that way. Two diggers, maybe three, working in shifts. Nobody hurrying. They buried him properly, in frozen ground, which costs hours they must have badly needed." He looked up at us, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't apologize for it. "This wasn't concealment. This was a funeral."

"He was right," Junie read aloud. "Right about what?"

Nobody answered, because none of us knew, and because at that moment Osk, who had wandered ten yards upslope the way big quiet men do, said, "Hey," in a voice that emptied the conversation out of the morning.

We climbed to him. Beyond a shoulder of granite, the ground fell away into a shallow basin, and planted in the middle of the basin—driven into bare stone, which is a thing you cannot do, a thing that takes machinery or madness—stood a survey stake.

Iron-banded, painted in the expedition's colors, brass plate gleaming. And stamped into the brass, in characters deep enough to read at thirty feet, was an elevation figure and a notation. I won't pretend I understood the numbers. But Brandt did, a little, and Junie did, more, and Hale—who had gone the color of old porridge—understood it completely, whatever it was, because he took out his laminated folder, checked something against it, and then, for the first time since the staging dock, put the folder away without writing anything down.

"What does it say?" I asked.

Junie read it twice. "It's a benchmark," she said slowly. "A datum mark. But the elevation—it's marking a *grade*. A navigable grade. Through the range." She looked up at the peaks, at the wall of blue-white granite that every map I'd ever owned called impassable, terminal, the end of the line in every sense. "Silas. This stake says there's a way through. A living route. Right here."

The wind came down off the ice fields and moved through the basin, and the stake stood in it like a nail holding the whole impossible claim to the face of the world.

"Log it," Hale said quietly. His voice had gone smooth and careful, a man walking on river ice. "We log the site and turn back. Immediately. That's an instruction, not a discussion."

Behind him, unnoticed by everyone but me, the red glow of his pocket transmitter blinked once through the fabric, and went dark.
