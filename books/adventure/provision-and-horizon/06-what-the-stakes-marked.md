## 6. What the Stakes Marked

Junie Okafor had learned radio the way other girls learned piano: hunched over, alone, at hours that would have worried her mother if her mother had known. The difference was that a piano never lied to you. A radio told you everything and nothing at once—every voice in the world, if you could catch it, and silence when it mattered most.

The expedition's set was smashed. That was the first thing she'd done at the silent camp, before Brandt called her away to look at tracks, before Hale started his clipboard theater about logging coordinates and turning back. She'd knelt in the trampled snow of the tent floor and put her hands on the wreck like a doctor taking a pulse.

Somebody had crushed it from the inside. That was the strange part. The casing was caved in along its own seams, driven down with something heavy and deliberate—a boot heel, maybe, or the flat of a survey hammer. Not thrown. Not dropped in a panic. *Finished.* Whoever did it had wanted the radio dead the way you want a witness dead: thoroughly, and close up.

But radios were like people that way. You could break them and they'd still remember what they'd heard.

She'd pried open the back panel while nobody watched—Hale was busy measuring the distance between tent stakes as though the tents might be graded on it—and found the log buffer intact. Six weeks of transmissions, compressed into a chip no bigger than her thumbnail, sitting in its cradle like a heart that hadn't gotten the memo.

She took it. She didn't even hesitate, which surprised her a little. Back at the staging dock she'd have said she wasn't the kind of girl who stole things. But the kind of girl she was turned out to depend heavily on circumstances, and the circumstance was six missing people and a radio murdered by one of their own.

* * *

The trouble with having a secret chip was finding a place to read it.

The boats were communal space. Hale's eyes were everywhere, and not in a paranoid way—in an *accounting* way, which was worse, because paranoia you could hide from but arithmetic just kept coming around on schedule. He counted the fuel cans twice a day. He counted the food. Junie had a private suspicion he counted the crew when he thought they were sleeping, and she made a point of breathing loudly so he'd get an accurate tally.

So she waited for night watch. That was hers now—nobody had assigned it, but Silas had started handing her the thermos at ten o'clock without a word, and after three nights it was simply true that the middle dark belonged to her. The rivermen slept like felled trees. Brandt slept like a man who'd earned it honestly. Hale slept, she was nearly certain, with one ear open, which meant she couldn't do this aboard.

Which left the bank, and the fire, and the man who sat closest to it without ever quite joining it.

Silas Galloway kept his own watch in a way—she'd noticed it the second night. He sat with his back to a boulder and his boots toward the water and his hands loose around a tin cup he never seemed to drink from, and he listened to the river the way she listened to static: for the thing underneath the thing. Tonight there was frost on his shoulders. He hadn't bothered to brush it off. It made him look older and more permanent, like a fence post winter had decided to keep.

"Your turn," he said, without looking at her, because he always knew.

"I'm not tired."

"Wasn't offering sleep." He nodded at the fire. "Offering company. There's a difference. Company doesn't snore."

She laughed despite herself, and sat, and spent four full minutes working up to it while the river talked to itself in the dark. Then she took out the chip.

She'd expected questions. What she got was silence, and then Silas setting down his cup with the exaggerated care of a man clearing a table for bad news.

"Where'd that come from?"

"Their radio. The one that got smashed." She turned it over in her fingers. "It keeps everything. Every transmission, logged, until you wipe it. Nobody wiped it. They were in too much of a hurry to kill it properly."

"Can you read it?"

"That depends." She pulled her little receiver kit from inside her coat—the one she'd built herself from parts that had officially never existed—and held it up. "This is technically against about four regulations. My instructor at the depot school called it 'a hobby.' I called it insurance. Turns out we were both right."

Silas looked at the kit, then at her, and something moved across his face that she was starting to recognize: the look of a man revising a ledger entry. She got the feeling she'd been entered under *liability* and was being quietly re-entered under something else.

"You knew how to do this," he said slowly, "when Corver hired you."

"I knew how to do lots of things when Corver hired me. He asked if I could operate a radio. I said yes. He didn't ask what else." She shrugged. "I've noticed adults mostly hear the first yes and stop listening."

That earned her a real look—not disapproval, exactly. More like recognition. Like he'd been a nineteen-year-old who'd once said *yes* to something and spent twenty years hearing the echo.

"Play it," he said.

* * *

The fragment came through in pieces. That was the nature of log buffers—they stored what was transmitted, and transmission over mountains was a conversation with a wall. Junie had to reconstruct, filling gaps with context the way you'd fill a torn map with guesswork and hope. She narrated as she worked, partly for Silas, mostly because saying it out loud made it real slower.

"Day nine. Routine position report. Weather. Nothing." Static hissed. "Day eleven. Verrane's voice—he's got this clipped way of talking, like every word costs him money. 'Survey party split. Kessler and I proceeding up the eastern fork on foot. Remainder holding camp.'" She frowned. "Eastern fork isn't on our charts. I checked. Twice."

"Charts get paid to not know things," Silas said. "Keep going."

"Day thirteen." She adjusted the gain. The voice swam up out of the noise, thin and cold, a man talking into weather. "'Passage confirmed. Repeat: passage confirmed. Navigable channel through the range, draft sufficient for loaded barges, gradient favorable. God forgive me, it's real.'"

Junie stopped. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Navigable," Silas said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the way it went when he was doing sums he didn't like. "Through the range."

"A boat route. Through the mountains." She said it out loud to hear how impossible it sounded, and then heard how impossible it *wasn't*, because Verrane's voice didn't sound like a man hallucinating. It sounded like a man standing in front of something enormous and trying to describe it in words small enough to fit through a radio.

She scrolled forward through the fragments. Day fourteen: a longer transmission, half-garbled, Verrane reading measurements—depths, widths, fall rates, numbers that meant nothing to her and clearly everything to Silas, whose jaw had tightened number by number. And then, near the end, a sentence that came through almost clean:

"'—must ask directly whether the concession holders were informed prior to departure. If they knew this route existed and sent us anyway—'"

Static ate the rest.

Junie sat back. The cold felt different suddenly, like it had been waiting outside the circle of firelight all along and had only now been invited in.

"They knew," she said. "Hale's employers knew. Before the expedition ever left. Verrane figured it out up here, six weeks from anywhere, and the first thing he did was ask the question we're asking."

"Or he suspected and wanted it in the log," Silas said. "In writing. In a place somebody might find it." He stared into the fire. "A man who finds a thing worth millions doesn't radio home asking forgiveness unless he thinks he'll need it."

"So why send an expedition at all? If you already know the passage is there—"

"Because knowing isn't proving." Silas turned his cup slowly. "A concession is paper. Paper expires. If the Ashwater Navigation Company's monopoly runs out next spring, and there's a living river route through the range, their whole empire is worth the cost of tearing it up. Unless—" he tapped the chip, gently, like it might bite, "—the route stays a rumor. Or better, a grave story. Expedition lost, tragic, river's clearly impassable, gentlemen, here's your renewal."

Junie felt it land in her chest, cold and clean. "We're not a rescue."

"We're whatever we are after we find them." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Rescue's the shape they gave the job so decent people would say yes."

She thought about the dock at the staging town, about signing her name on Corver's contract with the ink still cold, about Hale counting fuel cans with his neat, patient face. About the word *resupply* and how it had sounded so harmless, so logistical, so boring.

"Hale," she said. "Does he know? Really know?"

"He knows what pays him." Silas's voice was very quiet. "Whether he knows what it costs is a different question. Men like that can hold two ledgers and never notice they don't balance."

* * *

They sat with it awhile. The river ran. Somewhere upstream an ice shelf groaned and settled, a long tectonic complaint, and both of them looked north without meaning to.

Here was the thing Junie had been turning over since the silent camp, and she finally said it, because the fire was warm and Silas was the only person within two hundred miles who wouldn't laugh:

"I keep waiting to feel like part of this crew. And I don't. I feel like part of *you*. And Brandt, a little. And that's it. Four people in two boats pretending the other three aren't there."

"Five," Silas said. "Verrane's lot, if they're alive."

"Five." She hugged her knees. "My mum used to say families aren't the people you're born to, they're the people you'd run a river for. I thought that was just a thing mums said." She glanced sideways at him. "You'd run a river for us. I've watched you do the arithmetic. You hate that you'd do it, but you would."

Silas was quiet long enough that she thought she'd overstepped—that particular border she was always patrolling, the one between *charming* and *too much*, which she'd crossed so many times she'd worn a path. But when he spoke, his voice was rough at the edges.

"My father had a compass," he said. "Never taught me to read a chart with it. Taught me to hold it level. Said a needle doesn't care what you want—it cares where north is. Everything else is you deciding what to do about it." He looked at her. "You found the truth in a dead radio because you couldn't stand not knowing. That's north, girl. Whatever else happens on this river, that part of you points true."

Junie blinked hard and blamed the smoke.

Then practicality reasserted itself, because she was her mother's daughter. "So what do we do? Tell the others? Tell *Hale*?"

"No." The answer came fast and final. "Not Hale. Not yet. Maybe not ever." He fed a stick to the fire. "Brandt, when the time's right—I think Brandt already suspects more than he says. The rivermen don't need carrying; they need feeding. But Hale hears this, and the next transmission from this river is a request for instructions, and instructions will come back downriver faster than we can paddle against them."

"So it's ours. Just ours."

"Just ours." He said it like a weight being accepted, formally, with signatures. "For now. Can you live with that?"

She thought about it honestly, which was the only way she knew how to think. A secret shared with one grumpy river pilot and a stolen chip in her coat. It should have felt lonely. Instead it felt like the opposite of lonely—it felt like the first time in her life she'd been trusted with something that mattered, and had someone to matter *with*.

"Yeah," she said. "I can live with that."

She pocketed the chip. And that was when her receiver, still tuned and idling on the expedition's old frequency out of pure habit, crackled.

Both of them went still.

It wasn't Verrane. It wasn't static either. It was a voice—dry, clipped, corporate-smooth, the voice of a man reading from a prepared document to someone he considered furniture. Junie's hands moved before her brain caught up, steadying the gain, and the words came through in fragments, cold as lake water:

"—intercept team... staged at the lower rapids... confirm the plates are recovered intact... casualties acceptable within..."

The signal dissolved. She twisted the dial. Nothing. Just the river's own endless whisper, and the fire popping, and Silas staring at her across the flames with an expression she'd remember for the rest of her life: not surprise. Confirmation.

"Casualties acceptable," she repeated, barely above a whisper.

Silas rose. He didn't say anything for a moment—just stood there with the frost melting off his shoulders and the firelight making him look carved instead of tired. Then he looked north, upriver, into the black where six missing people and a freshly dug secret were waiting somewhere in the high country.

"Get some sleep," he said quietly. "Tomorrow we follow those tracks. And tomorrow, girl, we'd best find them before whoever sent that voice does."

Junie folded the chip into her coat, over her heart, where secrets apparently lived now.

She was beginning to suspect it was going to get crowded in there.
