## 7. Tracks North

The trail left the river at a place where the Ashwater bent east and the land forgot to be gentle. It climbed through spruce that grew crooked from fighting the wind, and the ground underfoot went soft with needle-fall and then hard with stone, and all the while the tracks of six men walked ahead of us like a sentence we hadn't finished reading.

I counted them every morning. Six sets of boots, one of them dragging a little on the left heel — Verrane's, I'd have bet my compass on it, because surveyors walk like men who trust their instruments more than their ankles. The tracks were eleven days old when we found them, maybe twelve. Old enough that frost had gotten into each print and made a small plaster cast of it, so that we were following not footprints but foot-shaped stones, hundreds of them, laid out across the high country by people who had been in a hurry and then, somewhere along the line, stopped being in a hurry at all.

That was the part I didn't like. A trail tells you things if you let it talk. These tracks started wide-strided and deep-toed, the gait of men pushing hard under heavy loads. Then, day by day, they shortened. They began to wander. And by the time we'd climbed two days into the high country, they had begun to stop — little clusters of prints where somebody had stood still for a long while, facing north, then south, then north again.

"Rest stops," said Hale, behind me.

"Maybe," I said.

"You think they're lost."

"I think," I said, "that a man who's sure of where he's going doesn't stand in one spot long enough to grow roots." I didn't say the rest of it, which was that Verrane had the best survey crew money could buy, and that getting lost was the one thing they weren't built to do.

Junie came up alongside me with her radio slung on her back like a snare drum she'd been drafted to carry. She'd taken to walking point-adjacent, which is to say close enough to me to ask questions and far enough from Hale to not have to answer his. She was nineteen and lied about her age the way some people lie about their weight — cheerfully, and with the assumption that nobody who mattered would check.

"How far up do you figure?" she asked.

"Another day. Maybe less, if the tracks keep honest."

"And if they don't?"

I looked back down the line of us: Hale with his city boots and his ledger face; Brandt with his medical pack riding him like a second spine; the rivermen, Osk and Tam, hauling the lightened boat gear on improvised frames, complaining in the comfortable way of men whose complaints are a form of prayer. Six of us following six of them, minus however many the mountain had already collected.

"Then we read harder," I said.

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow below a ridge the wind had scalped bare, and I'll tell you what I remember about that evening, because it's the kind of thing a man files away without knowing why. Brandt brewed tea over the spirit stove and passed it around without being asked, and he remembered how each of us took it — black for me, honey for Junie, nothing for Hale because Hale took nothing from anybody, which told you more about Hale than any dossier could have. Osk told a story about a moose he'd once insulted, and Tam laughed until he wheezed, and for twenty minutes we were just seven people — no, six, I corrected myself, six of us — sitting in the cold pretending the cold wasn't there.

Hale sat apart, as usual, cleaning a pistol he had no permit to carry and no plausible need for, and checking his watch, which was a fine watch and kept excellent time and never once told him anything worth knowing.

It was after dark when I heard the radio.

Not Junie's. Hers was off — I'd watched her shut it down at dusk, proper procedure, save the battery. This was a different sound: the thin dry click of a keying switch, the almost-nothing hum of a set warming up. It came from the far side of the hollow, past the boats, where Hale had pitched his shelter alone.

I lay in my bag and listened. Three short transmissions, spaced minutes apart. No voice I could catch — too far, too low, wind in the spruce covering everything but the carrier tone itself, a faint electronic breath like something alive and patient exhaling.

I didn't get up. That's the truth of it, and I've had years to make my peace with the truth of it. I lay there and told myself it was probably a scheduled check-in, company protocol, a man doing his job. And underneath that, quieter, the other voice — the one that sounded like my father, dead thirty years and still keeping books in my head — said: *You know exactly what a man transmitting against orders sounds like, Silas Galloway. You used to be one.*

I closed my eyes and did not sleep.

* * *

We found the grave the next afternoon.

The tracks led us up through a saddle of gray rock and into a small hanging valley where the trees gave out and the sky came down close enough to touch, and there, near a stream that ran thin and bright over slate, the trail ended its wandering and became purposeful again. Six sets of tracks converging. Five sets leaving.

The grave was dug into the gravel bar beside the stream, and it was fresh — fresh the way bread is fresh, fresh enough that the spoil heaps on either side still held the shape of shovels, and the stones stacked on top had been chosen rather than gathered, each one placed by hand. Somebody had cut a marker from a piece of birch and burned letters into it with a coal. The writing was careful. It was also wrong, in a way I couldn't name yet, and wouldn't name for another hour.

Brandt knelt first. Of course he did. He knelt in the gravel with his coat hem in the meltwater and he took off his hat, and the rest of us, without anyone saying anything, took ours off too — even Hale, though Hale's hat came off slow, like it was on a hinge he had to pay for.

"His name was Aldous Wrenn," Brandt said quietly. "Expedition geologist. Fifty-one years old. I treated him once, three years ago, for a crushed hand — a coupling accident on the lower line. He played chess by mail with his sister in Verrane City. He was losing, but slowly, on purpose, I always thought."

"You knew him?" Junie asked.

"I knew his file." Brandt touched the birch marker, tracing the burned letters without quite reading them aloud. "And now I know this. Look at the hands, if you can bear it — no. Don't look. Let me tell you instead." He stayed kneeling, and his voice did something I've heard only twice in my life, both times from doctors, that terrible gentleness that is worse than shouting because you understand they are choosing every word the way a man chooses footing on ice. "He was sick before he died. Not injured — sick. There's swelling I can see even now, and the color of him says fever, days of it. His companions dug this grave themselves, which means they had strength left, which means they buried him *after* the worst had passed, or during a lull. And they marked it carefully. You don't carve careful letters into birch when you're running. You carve them when you're grieving."

He stood, finally, knees cracking, and put his hat back on, and looked at the valley the way a man looks at a room where something happened that the room will never mention.

"Fever," Hale said. "Typhoid? Bad water?"

"Could be. Could be a dozen things." Brandt wiped his hands on his coat, an old habit, pointless up here. "What I can tell you is that whatever it was, five men carried it north instead of turning south for help. That's not sickness talking. That's purpose."

Nobody answered that. The stream made its small cold noise. Somewhere above us a ptarmigan complained about our existence and then thought better of it.

It was Junie who solved the thing that had been itching at me since we arrived. She stepped up to the birch marker, tilted her head, and frowned the particular frown of a radio operator hearing a frequency that's almost right.

"The date's wrong," she said.

"What?"

"The date. Burned into the wood. It says the twelfth." She counted on her fingers, lips moving, doing the arithmetic against the age of the tracks we'd followed, the frost-cast prints, the state of the spoil heaps. "But this grave's older than the twelfth. By four days, maybe five. Whoever cut this marker cut it late."

"Or cut it early," I said, "and dated it wrong."

"Or," said Brandt, very softly, "they didn't know what day it was. Fever camps lose track. I've seen it."

All three explanations were true. None of them was comfortable. We stood there a moment longer, six living people around one dead one, and then Hale cleared his throat and said, "We should log the position and move on. Daylight's finite."

And that was true too, damn him, which is the most irritating thing about Hale — that half of what he says is the sensible thing, so that the other half hides inside it like a hook inside bait.

* * *

We made another six miles before camp, following the five remaining trails north and slightly east, toward a wall of peaks that the old maps — my old maps, the closed-lines maps, the ones I swore to burn — called the Serrated Country, on account of nobody had ever bothered surveying it properly and the ridgelines on paper looked like a jaw.

I was checking the boat frames when Junie found me. She didn't announce herself; she just appeared at my elbow the way she did, quiet-footed, holding two cups of tea and handing me the one with honey in it by mistake, then drinking that one herself and giving me the black one, and neither of us commented on it, which is its own kind of conversation.

"Mister Galloway," she said. "Can I ask you something strange?"

"You've asked me stranger."

"Last night. Did you hear the radio?"

I kept my eyes on the lashings. "Whose radio?"

"Don't do that." Not sharp — she was nineteen, she didn't have sharp yet — but steady, the way her voice got when she was reading a signal through static. "Hale keyed three transmissions after midnight. Short ones. Encrypted burst format, or near enough — I heard the rhythm. His set's a commercial unit, but it's been modified; I saw the extra band crystal when he repacked it Tuesday. He transmitted against orders, and you heard it, because you were awake, because you're always awake, because you sleep like a man keeping watch over money that isn't his."

I looked at her. She looked back, unblinking, tea steaming in her cup, snow starting to come down around us in fat unhurried flakes like the sky had all the time in the world.

"When did you decide I was trustworthy?" I asked.

"Chapter and verse? When you read the fine print on Corver's contract and told me what clause nine actually meant instead of letting me sign blind." She shrugged. "Also you take your tea black, which in my experience means either honesty or damage, and I've decided to gamble on the overlap."

I drank the tea. It was terrible. She'd boiled it too long, and there was spruce pitch in it from a careless branch, and it was the best cup I'd had in six years.

"Hale transmitted three times," I said. "No voice. Burst traffic. To whom?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She turned her cup slowly. "There's no relay station on this river. Nothing within two hundred miles except—" She stopped. She didn't finish, and she didn't have to, because we both knew what was within two hundred miles: Corver's staging dock, and whatever Corver kept behind it besides crates.

"If he's reporting our position," I said slowly, "then someone downstream knows exactly where we are and how fast we're moving."

"And if he's reporting something else—"

"Like what?"

"Like asking permission." Junie looked north, toward the Serrated Country, where five sets of tracks walked into a wall of white. "Mister Galloway, I've been thinking about the grave. About the marker being cut late. And about why five healthy men with a working radio would walk deeper into nowhere instead of calling for help." She paused. "Unless the radio they smashed at camp wasn't broken because they needed to break it. Unless it was broken so nobody else could call out either."

The snow thickened. Down the slope, Hale stood alone at the edge of the trees with his back to us, and even at that distance I could see him checking his watch again, that fine watch keeping its excellent time, counting down toward something we hadn't been shown yet.

"Get some sleep, Miss Okafor," I said.

"Yes sir," she said, and didn't move. "One more thing. Tomorrow, when we find them — and we will, the tracks are straightening out, they know where they're going now — tomorrow, when we find them, I'd take it kindly if you stood where I could see you."

"Why's that?"

"Because," said Junie Okafor, nineteen years old and the sharpest instrument on this expedition, "somebody in this crew is sending reports to somebody downstream, and I want to know which side of the story you're standing on before the story gets here."

She handed me her empty cup, picked up mine, and walked back toward the fire, and I stood alone in the falling snow with the compass in my pocket pointing north and my own judgment, for the first time in years, pointing somewhere I couldn't name.
