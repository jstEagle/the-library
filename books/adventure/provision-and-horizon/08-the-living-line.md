## 8. The Living Line

The smoke was what saved us from walking past it.

We'd been following the trail since midmorning—six days of boot prints and drag marks and one abandoned cook pot that Brandt had insisted on carrying the rest of the way like a widow's memento—and by early afternoon I was running on coffee made from grounds I'd already used twice and a stubbornness I didn't fully trust. The high country had gone quiet in that particular way mountains do when they're deciding something about you. Then Junie stopped dead on the switchback ahead of me and said, "Smoke," and pointed with her chin at a gray thread rising off the shoulder of the ridge to the north.

"Campfire," said Hale, coming up behind us. His voice did its usual trick of sounding relieved and disappointed at exactly the same time, which I was beginning to understand was just how his face worked too.

"Or a signal fire," Junie said.

"Or nothing," Hale said. "Let's not get ahead of ourselves."

But we all got ahead of ourselves. That's human nature. Even Hale walked faster after that, though he pretended he was only keeping pace.

The shelter sat in a fold of the mountain where an old maintenance spur once serviced a telegraph line nobody had maintained since before the war—the other war, the one my father's compass had come home from. It was a low stone building with a tin roof gone the color of old pewter, a door hanging open on one hinge, and a woodpile stacked with the kind of neurotic precision that told me an engineer lived inside. Smoke curled from a stovepipe patched with what looked like hammered-flat ration tins.

I put two fingers up and we stopped. Habit from the river: you don't run at a rapid, you read it first.

"Verrane!" I called. "Ashwater expedition! We're coming in!"

Nothing. Then a scrape inside, a shadow crossing the one window, and a voice—hoarse, cracked, but unmistakably alive—said, "How many of you?"

"Five," I said. "Six counting the doctor."

A long pause. Then, wry as a dry creek bed: "Send the doctor first."

* * *

Emory Verrane had lost maybe thirty pounds and most of his hair's dignity, but he was standing when we came through the door, which is more than I'd let myself hope for six days up a frozen trail. He leaned on a survey tripod like it was a cane, and behind him, blinking in the light from the doorway, were the other two—a young chainman named Osgood with a hand wrapped in filthy bandages, and a broad, silent man called Reyes who moved like his joints had been replaced with cold iron.

Brandt went straight past Verrane to Osgood's hand, because that's who Brandt was. She knelt without asking permission, unwound the bandage, and made a small sound—not quite disapproval, more like grief with a medical license.

"Frostbite," she said. "Badly managed. Who wrapped this?"

"I did," Verrane said. "I'm a surveyor, not a surgeon."

"You tied it too tight. You may have cost him two fingers."

"He'd have cost himself the whole arm if I hadn't," Verrane said, and there was no apology in it, just arithmetic, and something in the flatness of it made me look at him twice.

Hale stepped forward with his hand out and his smile deployed. "Mr. Verrane. Hale, representing the consortium. We're very glad to find you alive. The company's been—"

"I know who you are." Verrane didn't take the hand. He looked at it the way you look at weather coming over a ridge. "You're the reason we stopped radioing."

The temperature in that little stone room dropped ten degrees, and I don't mean the draft under the door.

Junie had drifted to the corner where the smashed radio sat—she couldn't help herself, I'd learned; show that girl a broken machine and she'd start grieving for it out loud—and now her head came up sharply. "Stopped radioing? Your window went silent six weeks ago. We were told equipment failure."

"The equipment works fine," Verrane said. "I disabled it."

"Why?" Hale asked, pleasant as a bank teller.

"Because I found something," Verrane said, "and the moment I reported it, every decision about it would be made by men in offices. Men like you, Mr. Hale. So I decided to make sure of it first. All of it. Every reading, every sounding, every plate." He tapped the tripod. "And then Osgood broke his hand, and then the snow came early, and then we were here instead of home, and I'll admit the plan developed certain flaws."

Reyes made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough. With him it was hard to say.

"What did you find?" I asked.

Verrane looked at me for a long moment. Surveyors have a way of looking at things—like they're measuring you for inclusion in the record. Whatever he measured, apparently I passed, because he jerked his chin toward the back wall, where a canvas sheet hung over something.

"Come see," he said. "Then decide whose side you're on. Because there are sides now. I'm sorry to be the one to tell you."

* * *

Under the canvas was a map. Not a sketch—a finished thing, inked and colored, the work of weeks, pinned to boards he'd salvaged from supply crates. And I will confess something here, because this account ought to be honest even if I wasn't always: when I understood what I was looking at, my knees actually loosened. I put a hand on the cold stone wall like a man steadying himself in a swell.

It was a river route. Through the range. The whole range—the wall of mountains that every map in every office in the territory drew as a solid bar of ink, the barrier that justified everything: the concession, the rail monopoly, the freight rates, Corver's contracts, my own small crooked economy of debts. Every map showed the range as impassable, and every map was wrong, because the Ashwater didn't end at the gorge the way the maps said. It turned. It threaded north through a chain of high valleys and came down the far side navigable—navigable, with portages marked and graded in Verrane's neat engineer's hand—for better than two hundred miles.

"A living line," Brandt said softly, reading the title block. "That's what you called it."

"It's what it is," Verrane said. "A route that breathes. No rails needed. A barge could run it eight months of the year. Which means—" 

"—the concession is worth nothing," Hale finished. His voice had gone very quiet and very careful, the way ice sounds before it goes.

"Worth less than nothing," Verrane agreed. "Worth negative. Because whoever holds it would be paying for a monopoly on a road nobody needs to use." He smiled, and it was a terrible tired smile. "So you understand my dilemma, Mr. Hale. The day I transmit these plates south, your employers begin calculating. And men like them, when the arithmetic comes out badly, they don't amend the ledger. They burn the page. They dam the valley, buy the water rights, bury the route under forty feet of reservoir, and the discovery dies protecting the investment. I've watched it done to smaller things."

"That's a serious accusation," Hale said.

"It's a serious map," Verrane said.

Nobody spoke for a while. Outside, wind worried the tin roof. Inside, Brandt kept working on Osgood's hand with a gentleness that seemed almost defiant in that room, and Junie stood in front of the map with her arms crossed, and I watched her eyes move along the route the way a person reads a letter from someone they love.

"So what's the plan, Emory?" I asked finally. "You going to winter here? Guard it like a dragon?"

"The plan was to wait for the relief party," Verrane said, "and then assess whether the relief party was safe to know anything. Which brings me to you." He turned, leaning harder on the tripod. "Who sent you?"

"Corver," I said, because lying to a dying man's face is one sin I still skip.

Something moved through Verrane's expression—recognition, and pity, and I hated both. "Corver," he repeated. "Corver's money is consortium money. Corver's boats are consortium boats. You didn't come up this river, Galloway. You were sent up it. There's a difference, and right now it's the most important fact in this room."

"I know what I am," I said.

"Do you?" he asked, not cruelly. "Because I spent six weeks becoming the custodian of the most dangerous piece of paper in the territory, and I can tell you the job changes a man. Everything you carry starts carrying you."

* * *

We camped that night in the shelter's lee, and the argument started at supper and never really stopped, just lowered its voice when the wind rose.

"We log the site and go back," Hale said. "That's the mission. Find survivors, report condition, return. Nobody said anything about transporting classified materials."

"They're not classified," Junie said. "They're his."

"They're company-funded findings."

"They're *true*," Junie said, with the particular heat of nineteen-year-olds, who still believe truth and ownership are different categories. "You can't fund a mountain, Mr. Hale."

"You'd be amazed what money funds," Hale said mildly, and ate his beans.

Brandt looked up from repacking her kit. "Osgood loses fingers if we delay much longer. Maybe the hand. He needs a hospital, not a debate."

"Then we take the people and leave the plates," Hale said. "Simple."

"No," said Verrane.

Everyone turned. He was sitting against the wall with a blanket around his shoulders like a deposed king, and there was something in his face I recognized from mirrors in bad years: a man who has already traded away things he meant to keep, and has drawn a line at last, too late, and knows it's too late, and holds it anyway.

"I watched Marlowe die carrying half this survey on his back," Verrane said. "He slipped on the scree above the pass and I heard the case hit rock and I knew before I got to him. Sixty pounds of glass and paper, and it broke his fall and it broke him. We buried him with the pieces." His voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "I am not leaving these plates on this mountain. If the route dies, it dies with witnesses who chose it, not in a filing cabinet. Either the plates come down that river, or I do not."

"That's blackmail," Hale said.

"That's a hostage situation," Junie said quietly, "where the hostage is a map," and I saw Hale glance at her—really glance, file her away—and I filed that glance away myself.

I should have slept. Instead I stood outside in the blue dark looking north along the line of Verrane's route, where the valleys ran hidden under starlight, and I thought about maps. My father's compass rode in my coat pocket, needle swinging lazy and true, and in my pack was the other map—the closed lines, the dead spurs, the record of everything I'd helped shut down and everything I'd been paid to forget. Two documents. One showed where the world had stopped going. One showed where it might yet go.

And I understood, standing there with my breath freezing, that Verrane was right about the shape of the thing even if he was wrong about the solution. The discovery was a hostage now. The question was who'd be holding the gun when the ransom came due.

Behind me the shelter door scraped, and Junie came out, wrapping her coat tight. She stood beside me without speaking for a while, which is one of the things I liked best about her—she treated silence like a shared tool, not an empty room.

"He won't give them up," she said finally.

"No."

"And Hale won't leave without them."

"No."

"So somebody's going to have to be smarter than both of them." She looked up at me, and in the starlight her grin was quick and crooked. "Good thing we brought an engineer."

"I'm disgraced," I reminded her.

"You keep saying that like it's a permanent condition." She bumped my shoulder with hers and went back inside, and I stayed out a minute longer, and if I smiled into the dark where nobody official could see it, well. Some debts you pay in money. Some you pay in miles. And some, I was beginning to suspect, you pay by letting a nineteen-year-old radio operator believe in you before you've earned it—so that you have to become the man she thinks she's talking to.

In the morning, Verrane handed me the map tube himself and said, "You carry it. You strike me as a man who knows what it costs to lose something."

He had no idea how right he was.

And none of us—not Verrane guarding his plates, not Hale smiling his bank-teller smile, not me with my father's compass ticking against my ribs—none of us had yet thought to check the fuel drums.
