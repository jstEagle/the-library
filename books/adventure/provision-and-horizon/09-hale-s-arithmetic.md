## 9. Hale's Arithmetic

Hale had always been good at arithmetic. It was, he'd come to believe, the only honest language left. People lied in words, lied in faces, lied even in silences—but numbers held still. A number was a number at midnight and at noon, in a boardroom and in a ditch.

So at four in the morning, while the rest of the camp slept under canvas in the blue cold, Hale sat on an overturned crate with his ledger book on his knee and did the arithmetic that would decide everything.

Item one: the plates. Verrane's survey plates, forty-one exposures documenting a navigable passage through the Serrault Range—a passage that, if it ever reached the light of day, would render the Meridian Concession's rail monopoly worth approximately the paper it was printed on. The concession had nine years left on its charter. Nine years of exclusive haulage rights, valued by the company's own actuaries at eleven million dollars, give or take a depression.

Item two: the witnesses. Six expedition members, four resupply crew, and himself. Eleven souls who had seen the stakes, the shelter, or the plates, in varying degrees of certainty.

Item three: the intercept team. Corver's men, twelve of them by the last count, waiting somewhere below the lower rapids with orders Hale had written himself in a previous life—the life where he'd still believed the company's problems could be solved with paperwork.

He turned the pencil over in his fingers. It was a good pencil, company-issue, hexagonal, the kind that didn't roll off a desk. He found himself admiring it. That was a bad sign. When a man started admiring pencils at four in the morning, he was looking for anything at all to admire that wasn't his own reflection.

*Eleven million,* he wrote, and underlined it twice.

*Eleven lives,* he wrote, and did not underline it at all.

The math was not difficult. That was the terrible thing. He had expected, back when he'd first taken the assignment, that there would be a moment of crisis—a dark night of the soul, a crossroads, some operatic scene where he stood at the edge of himself and chose. He'd read enough novels to know the shape such moments were supposed to take. But the moment had never come. Instead there was only this: a crate, a ledger, a pencil, and the quiet discovery that the sum came out in favor of the company, and that knowing it changed nothing in him that he could feel.

Or almost nothing.

There was the matter of Brandt. Hale turned to a fresh page and wrote the doctor's name, and found himself hesitating over it, which was also a bad sign. Brandt had spent the last week setting the riverman Odell's dislocated shoulder, lancing blisters, feeding broth to Verrane's frostbitten hands with the patience of a man feeding sparrows. Brandt had once, Hale was fairly sure, given his own sleeping bag to Junie Okafor and claimed he ran hot. Nobody ran that hot. It had been a kindness wearing a lie as a coat, and Hale had recognized the construction, because kindness wearing a lie was more or less his own specialty, run in reverse.

Brandt was a complication. Not a large one. Hale wrote *1* beside the name and moved on.

Junie Okafor. Nineteen, though her contract said twenty-two, and Hale had known that within a day because he read contracts the way other men read weather. The girl had a gift for radios and a habit of being awake at hours when awake people saw things. He'd caught her twice now looking at him with that particular expression—the one that wasn't suspicion yet but was saving up for it. He wrote her name, then, after a moment, wrote *watch* beside it.

Galloway. Hale's pencil stopped entirely.

Silas Galloway was not a complication. Silas Galloway was an error in the ledger that had somehow been issued a body. The man had been hired to pilot boats and had instead become—there was no other word for it—*trusted*. Odell trusted him. The girl trusted him. Even Verrane, who trusted nothing on this earth except the tensile strength of his own stubbornness, had begun deferring to him at campfires, in the small unconscious way of men handing over the tiller.

And Galloway owed Corver money. That was the one clean line in all of it. Debts were numbers too, and Galloway's number was the reason any of this would work. Hale wrote the name, wrote *asset* beside it, and then sat looking at the word for a long time, because it was the first entry in three days he hadn't believed.

* * *

The sabotage itself took eleven minutes, and Hale was ashamed of how easy it was, and then he was annoyed at himself for being ashamed, and then he was tired of the whole interior argument and simply got on with it.

The fuel reserve was a pair of steel drums lashed under the tarp in the second boat's stern, a hundred gallons of the good white gas they'd been husbanding since the gorge—enough, at current consumption, to run the engines through the lower river and reach the junction with a margin. Hale had checked the margin three times. Margins were his profession, in a sense. Everything was his profession, if you looked at it correctly.

He waited until Odell's watch ended and Galloway's began, because Galloway on watch looked at the river and Odell on watch looked at his own thoughts, and Hale had learned the difference. He moved along the boats in the gray pre-light with a canvas rag over his hand and a quarter-inch drill bit he'd taken from the survey kit, wrapped in wool so it wouldn't chatter against the steel.

The first drum took two holes, low, on the river side, where any seepage would vanish into bilge water and look like a bad seam. The second drum took one. He'd considered doing it properly—draining them, fouling the lines, some theatrical failure—but theatrical failures were for men who wanted credit for the solution. He wanted the failure to look like the river's own opinion. A slow leak found at dawn, fuel gone, engines useless. And then the only road home would be the one Hale's employers had chosen for them: the winter portage over the divide, twelve days on foot through country where Corver's men could wait with all the patience in the world, because patience was free and the company had bought all of it.

He pressed the rag over each hole until the weeping stopped, lashed the tarp back down, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the river.

The Ashwater talked in its sleep. That was the thing about it—Hale had expected silence from the wilderness and gotten instead a kind of low continuous conversation, water over stone, water under ice, the occasional crack and groan of the cold settling into the banks like a large man finding a chair. He had hated it for the first week. He was no longer certain he hated it. That, too, went in the ledger of things not to examine too closely.

He went back to his crate and added a final line to the page:

*Portage begins tomorrow. Intercept at Miller's Draw, day nine. Casualties: acceptable.*

He looked at the word *acceptable* for a long while.

Here was the thing Hale had learned about arithmetic, the thing they didn't teach you in the company's orientation seminars, with their charts and their motivational lithographs of mountains: the numbers never told you what to value. They only told you the cost of what you'd already decided to value. Eleven million against eleven lives wasn't a calculation. It was a confession. The calculation had happened years ago, in a boardroom he'd never seen, by men he'd never met, when they'd decided that a concession was worth more than the people inside it. Hale was just the decimal point. He moved the sum along and stood very still and called it precision.

He was still telling himself this when Junie Okafor came out of her tent to check the radio batteries, which she did every morning at this hour, because she was nineteen and disciplined in the way only the very young or the very guilty are disciplined. She stopped when she saw him sitting on the crate with his ledger open.

"Couldn't sleep?" she asked.

"Working," Hale said.

"At four in the morning?"

"The numbers don't keep business hours." He smiled the smile he'd practiced in mirrors until it had stopped feeling like a lie and started feeling like a face. "You should be sleeping. The river's long."

"River's frozen," she said, nodding at the skim of ice along the shallows, the first of the season, thin as window-glass. Then she went to her radio box and knelt over it, and did not look at him again, and Hale noted—with the part of his mind that kept the real ledger, the one written in no ink—that she had looked first at the boats. Not at him. At the boats.

He filed it. He filed everything. It was the only thing he was still sure he was good for.

* * *

The leak was discovered at seven, by Odell, with a stream of profanity that woke the camp and would have, Hale reflected, embarrassed a longshoreman. By seven-fifteen the tarp was off and the truth was plain: the reserve was down to a gallon and a half, seeping from seams that Odell swore blind had been sound at the last camp, and Galloway crouched at the stern with his fingers in the wet, smelling it, saying nothing.

"Bad steel," Hale offered, from a respectful distance. "Cold does that. Cracks you can't see until they open."

"Bad steel," Galloway repeated. He said it the way a man reads a sentence he doesn't believe, flat, giving it back. He looked up at Hale then, and his eyes were the color of the water in the gorge, and they held exactly nothing, which was somehow worse than accusation. "Maybe. Cold does a lot of things."

"Fuel's gone either way," Odell said, kicking the drum, and the hollow sound of it went through the camp like a bell. "Engines are done. We walk."

"We walk," Brandt agreed, already repacking his kit, because Brandt met every catastrophe with the same mild competence, as though catastrophe were a patient with a hangnail.

Verrane said nothing at all, but his eyes went to the survey case strapped to his own back, and Hale watched him do it, and understood that the old man had already begun the same arithmetic. Good. Fear made the portage faster.

They broke camp by nine. The snow had started—not the real winter yet, but its advance scout, fine and dry, ticking against the tarpaulins—and the divide ahead was a white line drawn across the gray sky like a rule the whole country was expected to keep.

Hale took the rear position, as was his habit, and as they climbed away from the river he permitted himself one glance back at the boats, sitting empty on the shingle with their dead drums, patient as evidence.

*Day nine, Miller's Draw,* he thought. *Casualties: acceptable.*

The word sat in him like a swallowed stone, cold and smooth and exactly the wrong shape, and he walked on, because the column was moving, and a man who stopped to feel things in the backcountry froze to the spot, and Hale had no intention of freezing. Not yet.

Behind them, unnoticed by anyone but the river, the last of the fuel had already soaked into the gravel, where it would lie all winter, a dark stain under the ice, telling no one anything.
