## 10. The Long Portage

The snow started an hour after we left the water, which felt like the river's way of saying *you're on your own now.*

It came down fine and dry, the kind that doesn't fall so much as hang in the air waiting for you to walk into it. By midmorning it had found every gap in my coat and settled there like a tenant who'd paid rent. My shoulders ached from the first carry — I'd taken one end of the survey crate because nobody asked me to and everybody needed somebody to, and pride is a heavy thing to pack over a mountain.

"Put it down," Silas said, falling into step beside me. He didn't look at me when he said it. He never looked at me when he said things that mattered; he looked at the trail ahead, or his boots, or some middle distance where decisions lived.

"I'm carrying it."

"You're carrying it wrong. Hip into it, not your back. You'll last twice as long." He adjusted the crate's strap on my shoulder with two tugs, brisk as a man fixing a fence, then walked on before I could thank him, because thanking him would have required him to acknowledge that kindness had occurred, and that was apparently against his religion.

Behind us, Hale's voice carried up the line, clipped and reasonable: "Gentlemen, if we maintain pace we make the ridge by dark." The two rivermen — Ossler and Finch, big quiet men who spoke mostly in grunts and weather reports — said nothing, which was their way of saying plenty. Brandt brought up the rear with Verrane, who was limping and pretending not to, which was *his* way of saying plenty.

I counted heads the way I'd been counting them since the silent camp. Seven of us going in. Six coming out of the high country plus one grave. And somewhere behind us, a radio frequency only I knew about, carrying conversations I wasn't supposed to have heard.

* * *

We made camp at the treeline as the light went the color of old tin. The portage trail was an old thing — you could see it in the blazes cut into the trunks decades ago, grown over but still there, a scar of axework leading nowhere anyone remembered. Somebody had hauled boats this way once, long before any of us, and had probably also stood around a fire complaining about it, and there was a strange comfort in that. Misery with ancestors.

Silas got a fire going with the efficiency of a man who'd done it ten thousand times and resented none of them. Ossler and Finch rigged the tarps. Hale sat apart with his notebook, writing things down, and I watched him write them and thought about frequencies and about how a notebook can be a weapon the same as a rifle, just slower.

Brandt crouched by Verrane at the edge of the firelight. I wasn't trying to listen. Honestly. But Brandt has a voice like a warm room — it carries whether it means to or not — and Verrane's boot was off, and even from twenty feet I could see the toes were the wrong color. Waxy. White going gray at the tips.

"When did this start?" Brandt asked.

"A while."

"'A while' meaning?"

Verrane's jaw worked. "Since the shelter. Maybe before."

Brandt didn't scold him. That was the remarkable thing. Anybody else would have read him a lecture on frostbite and foolishness, but Brandt just took the man's foot in both hands like it was something worth being careful with, and began working warmth back into it, and talked the whole time about nothing — the geology of the ridge, a dog he'd owned as a boy, the correct way to brew spruce tea — a steady stream of ordinary words, and I understood eventually that the talking *was* the treatment. You can't flinch if someone keeps your mind busy. He was doing surgery with conversation.

I looked away and found Silas watching the same scene from across the fire, wearing an expression I hadn't seen on him before. Not softness exactly. Recognition.

* * *

He came to find me an hour later, when the camp had gone quiet except for wind and Finch snoring like a landslide rehearsing. I was on watch, sitting on a root with the radio headset half on my ears and half off, listening to static the way you'd listen to rain.

"He told me," Silas said quietly, settling beside me without asking, which was its own kind of progress. "Verrane. About the dead man. Aldous."

My stomach dropped. "What happened?"

"Altitude sickness, mostly. Bad luck, mostly." Silas fed a stick into the fire. "They were crossing a scree field above the shelter. Aldous went down. Verrane went back for him, and the slope let go. Verrane held onto his hand for — he says minutes. It might've been less. It might've been more. People don't measure those things honestly." A pause. "Aldous's pack had the original route plates in it. Verrane went back for the body and couldn't reach it. So what he's been guarding all this time — what he nearly killed himself hauling over that ridge — is the copy set. And the guilt."

"That's why he wouldn't leave without them."

"That's why he wouldn't leave without them." Silas stared into the flames. "Man's not protecting a discovery. He's protecting a headstone."

I thought about that for a long moment. The static hissed gently in one ear. "Does Hale know they're copies?"

"Hale knows there are plates. Hale knows what they're worth. Whether he's stopped to wonder how clean a set a grieving man could produce under those conditions—" Silas shrugged. "Hale trusts paper. Paper's easier than people."

And that was when the idea arrived, the way ideas do — all at once, fully formed, slightly terrifying. "Then let's give him paper," I said.

Silas turned and looked at me properly for maybe the third time since the staging dock.

"Verrane can draw," I said, the words tumbling faster now. "He's a surveyor, it's what he *does*. Give him two hours and a pencil and he'll produce a passage route so convincing it'll make his real ones look sloppy. Wrong elevations. A bend that isn't there. Something beautiful and fatal. We split the real plates three ways — you, me, Brandt — and Hale carries the decoys down the mountain feeling like the smartest man in the concession."

For a long time Silas didn't answer. The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, snow slid off a bough with a soft whump.

"You understand what you're proposing," he said finally. "If it goes wrong — if he finds out —"

"He won't find out. He'll be too busy being right."

"And if it goes *right*, Junie, we're not crew anymore. We're conspirators. There's no walking that back to shore."

I pulled the headset down around my neck and met his eyes. "Silas. I've been a conspirator since the night I lied about my age to get on that dock. I've been listening to Hale's transmissions for four days. I'm not proposing we become something. I'm proposing we stop pretending otherwise."

Something moved through his face then — not a smile, but the place where a smile would live if a man like Silas Galloway ever allowed zoning permits for one. He nodded once, slow, like a man signing something.

"All right," he said. "But we do it clean. Nobody lies to anybody inside this circle. Verrane chooses. Brandt chooses. If either of them says no, we carry the truth ourselves and hang the cost." He stood, brushing snow from his coat. "That's the whole difference between us and Hale. He spends people. We ask."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly the difference."

He paused, then added, with the gravity of a man announcing a change in national policy: "Also your watch ended eleven minutes ago. Go sleep. I'll take it."

"You don't have to—"

"Junie." He said my name the way he fixed straps on crates. "Go."

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, and we gathered at the fire in what Silas announced, with a completely straight face, as a "route conference." Which was technically true. It was also a conspiracy convened over burnt porridge.

He laid it out plainly — no speeches, no drama. The decoy plates, the split, the reasoning. Hale would expect a single secure set; a single secure set was a single point of failure, and any engineer worth his salt distributed his load. Framed that way, it sounded almost sensible. Almost boring. The best lies wear work clothes.

Verrane listened with his bandaged foot propped on a log, and when Silas finished, the man was quiet for a long time.

"You're asking me to forge my own survey," he said at last.

"I'm asking you to protect the true one," Silas said. "There's a difference, and you're the only man on this mountain qualified to know it."

Verrane looked at his hands. They were strong hands, ink-stained at the nails, a draftsman's hands. "Aldous carried the originals," he said quietly. "He always did. Said I was too absent-minded to be trusted with our own work." A rough breath. "He was right. So yes. I'll draw them a valley that doesn't exist. Consider it — " his voice caught, steadied — "consider it the first honest thing I've done since we put in at Ashwater Junction."

Brandt said only, "I'm in. Someone should hold the pieces who owes nothing to anyone." Then he went back to repacking his kit, because Brandt treated moral courage the way he treated everything else: competently, and without making a fuss.

Which left Hale.

Here is what I learned about sending a man decoys: you don't hide the switch. You perform it. So that afternoon, in full view of everyone, Silas and Verrane repacked the survey cases with great ceremony and visible care, and I — nineteen years old, heart hammering like a telegraph key — volunteered to help organize the loads, and slipped the true plates into the bottom of Brandt's medical chest beneath a layer of splints, while Verrane's pencil worked by firelight producing a gorge so gorgeous and so fictional that I almost felt bad for whoever tried to run it.

Almost.

Hale watched the repacking from a distance, arms crossed, nodding occasionally, and I realized with a small cold shock that he *expected* us to try something — and that his expectation was itself part of our plan, because a man who expects deception will find it, and stop looking, satisfied. We weren't hiding the trick from him. We were hiding the trick *inside* his suspicion, like a coin in a magician's other hand.

At dinner, Hale was almost pleasant. He complimented the porridge. He asked Brandt about Verrane's foot. He said, "Once we're down to the lower water, this becomes simple again," and smiled, and everyone smiled back, and the whole camp was a stage play with seven actors and six scripts.

Ossler leaned over to Finch and muttered, loud enough for me to hear, "Porridge tastes like wet rope."

"It's the altitude," Finch said.

"It's the cook," said Ossler.

Gentle humor survives anything, even conspiracy. Especially conspiracy, maybe. You need somewhere to put the fear.

* * *

We broke camp before dawn and made the ridge by noon, and from the top I saw it — the far side of the range falling away in white folds toward a thin silver thread. The Ashwater again. Our river. Except.

Except the thread had edges now. Gray-white margins creeping inward from both banks, like frost on a windowpane seen from the wrong side.

"Silas," I said.

He was already looking. He'd been looking, I think, since we crested. His face did the thing it does instead of expressing alarm, which is to become very still and very technical.

"Early ice," he said. "Week early. Maybe two." He pulled out his father's compass, though there was nothing to navigate — he just held it, the way some men hold a cigarette, and I understood it was not a tool in his hand but a conversation partner. "Every hour we spend on this ridge is an hour the river spends closing."

"How long do we have?"

He didn't answer immediately. Down-slope, Hale called the column forward, cheerful as a ledger. Behind us, the mountains. Ahead of us, a river learning how to become land.

"Less than that," Silas said, and started down.
