## 11. Ice on the Ashwater

The first sign was a sound Silas had spent thirty years learning to hate: a faint, glassy ticking from the eddies along the bank, like someone tapping a spoon against a cup in another room.

He crouched at the water's edge with his morning coffee going cold in his hand and watched a skin of ice form and break, form and break, at the seam where current met still water. Thin as window frost. But it was the ninth of October, and the Ashwater had never frozen before the last week of the month in all the years he'd run her — not even the year the snow came sideways and the mail boat had to be chopped out of the dock.

"Brandt," he called over his shoulder. "Come look at this."

The doctor came down the bank with his collar up and his hands jammed in his pockets, squinted at the eddy, and said, "Is that bad?"

"That depends on how much you like winter."

"I like it fine. From indoors."

Silas dipped two fingers into the river and held them there while he counted to ten. Cold enough to ache, not yet cold enough to burn. The water itself was running warmer than the air — that was the trouble. A clear night coming, no cloud cover, and the whole surface of the river would be giving its heat up to the sky like a man paying out his last coin.

"It's going to freeze early," he said. "Maybe days early. Maybe a week."

Brandt looked at him. "And what does a week mean?"

Silas watched the ice skin reform on the eddy, delicate as the skin on cooling milk. "It means every hour we sit here arguing about it is an hour we don't have."

* * *

They argued about it anyway, because that was what crews did, and because Hale insisted on turning everything into a meeting with himself at the head of it.

"The sensible course," Hale said, standing at the fire with his map case under one arm like a bishop carrying a crook, "is to wait for full daylight. We've got provisions for six more days. The channel through the narrows is uncharted above Sable Bend. Running it blind in the dark is how men die."

"The sensible course," Silas said, "was leaving three days ago." He kept his voice level. He'd learned long ago that the angrier you were, the flatter your voice needed to be; it was the same principle as bridge trusses — take the load and distribute it. "The expedition sat still for six weeks waiting for conditions to improve. You've seen how that went."

Hale's jaw tightened. Around the fire, the others had gone quiet in the way people do when they're pretending not to listen. Junie was sitting cross-legged on a supply crate with the radio's guts spread out beside her like a surgeon's tray, and she wasn't touching any of them, which meant she was listening with her whole body. Brandt was wrapping Verrane's bandaged hand with the exaggerated care of a man who wanted very much to be somewhere else. The rivermen, Osk and Pell, were mending netting and looking at nobody.

"We are not," Hale said, "going to gamble seven lives on one man's—"

"On my what?" Silas asked pleasantly. "Judgment? Experience? The fact that I've run this river in worse conditions than you've ever read about in a report?" He stood. He hadn't meant to stand. "You want to wait for daylight, Mr. Hale, I'll tell you exactly what daylight looks like tomorrow. It looks like a quarter inch of ice on the slack water by dawn, thickening through the day, and by tomorrow night the narrows will be running pancake ice in sheets thick enough to hole a hull. And then we don't die in the rapids. We die here. Slowly, comfortably, with excellent minutes taken at every meeting."

"You can't know that."

"No," Silas agreed. "I can't know it. That's what makes it a judgment call instead of arithmetic." He let that sit for a moment, because he saw it land — he saw the flicker in Hale's eyes, the little twitch of a man who lived entirely inside things he could know. "But I'll tell you what I do know. I know this river the way you know your ledgers. Every bend, every shoal, every place the current piles up when the water drops. And I know that tonight the moon comes up at half past nine over the east ridge, bright as a lamp, and gives us four hours of light on white water. After tonight, we may not get another clear one before the freeze."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere across the river a branch cracked under its own new weight of cold.

Verrane broke the silence. The old surveyor had been sitting apart, wrapped in every blanket he owned, looking like a man who had already died once and found it inconvenient. "I ran the Colorado in '09," he said. "Night runs, low water, no charts worth the paper. We lost a boat and gained our lives." He looked at Hale. "I'm for Galloway."

"Of course you are," Hale said softly. "You've nothing left to lose but your plates."

"Hale." It was Brandt who said it, quiet and terrible. The doctor didn't raise his voice often, and when he did, it came out with the weight of a diagnosis. "The man drowned two days ago. You want to stand at his grave and tell me about risk calculus again, I'll let you. But don't dress waiting around as prudence. Prudence is doing the hard thing early."

Hale looked around the circle. Whatever he was counting behind those eyes, he finished the sum. "Motion carries," he said, in a voice like a door closing. "Against my formal objection, noted for the record."

"There's no record," Junie said, without looking up from the radio. "Except mine."

For just a moment — half a heartbeat — something moved across Hale's face that wasn't anger. It was older than anger, and colder. Then it was gone, filed away, and he nodded once and went to check the boats.

Junie caught Silas's eye across the fire. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. They had developed, over these hard miles, a whole silent language of small looks, and this one said: *did you see it too?*

He gave her the smallest nod. *Yes.* And then: *later.*

* * *

They loaded by lantern light, which is to say they loaded badly and cheerfully, everyone cold-fingered and dropping things, Osk singing some low river song about a girl in Fort Vermilion who could out-drink a moose. Even grief loosens its grip a little when there's work to do; Silas had seen it a hundred times, and he was grateful for it now. Pell had carved a rough cross for the riverman's grave back at the bend, and they'd left it standing with his knife driven into the post, because a man should keep his tools.

Silas walked the lead boat stem to stern twice, checking lashings, checking the fuel drum, checking the bailer, checking the spare oar. Habit. His father had taught him to check a thing twice and trust it once, and then to trust it never quite all the way.

"You always this cheerful before a night run?" Junie asked, hauling the last crate aboard.

"I'm always this cheerful. Ask anyone. It's a burden."

She grinned, then sobered, glancing upstream toward the black throat of the narrows. "How bad is it really?"

Silas considered lying to her, and rejected it — partly because she'd catch him, mostly because she'd earned better. "The channel narrows to maybe forty yards for half a mile, and it bends twice around rock ribs you can't see until you're committed. In daylight, at this water level, I'd rate it a hard hour's work. At night—" He shrugged. "At night it's a conversation with the river, and I intend to do most of the talking."

"And if it doesn't go well?"

"Then it goes badly quickly, and we deal with what's left." He looked at her — nineteen years old, lied-about age, radio operator, keeper of more secrets than any person her size should carry — and felt that strange parental ache he'd been trying to ignore since the staging dock. "Stay behind me in the second boat. Stay low. And whatever happens, whatever you hear me shout — you do exactly what Brandt says. He's steadier than I am."

"He is not."

"He is. I'm only steady in the parts of a river I can see. He's steady everywhere." He picked up his father's compass, checked the needle out of pure ritual, and put it in his breast pocket, where it lay against his heart like a small cold opinion.

* * *

The moon came up at half past nine, right on schedule, and it was everything he'd promised — a great silver lamp wheeling up over the east ridge, laying a road of light straight down the center of the river. Which was, Silas knew, both a gift and a lie. Moonlight on water shows you the shine and hides the shadow; the rocks that would kill you were precisely the ones wearing darkness like a coat.

He took the lead boat with Osk at the oars behind him and Pell handling the sweep. Behind them, forty yards back in the dark, came the second boat: Brandt at the tiller, Junie amidships with the crates lashed down, Verrane bundled in the bow like cargo that complained, and Hale — because Hale had chosen, pointedly, to ride in the boat with the least control over its fate, so that if it went wrong it would be Silas's fault alone. Fine. Silas had carried blame before. It weighed less than fuel drums and floated better.

"Talk to me, Galloway," Osk murmured, leaning into the first strokes.

"Two hundred yards of easy water," Silas said. "Then the banks start talking to each other. When I say *left*, you pull like the devil's holding your ankles. When I say *right*, same. When I say *hold*, you hold, and you hold even if the river tells you different."

"And when you scream?"

"Then we're past the part where words help."

Osk laughed — a short, tight bark, the laugh of a man spending fear to buy courage — and dug in.

The banks began to close. Silas felt it before he saw it, the way you feel a corridor narrowing by the sound of your own footsteps: the current quickened under the hull, the echo off the rock walls flattened and sharpened, and the moon-road of light ahead drew in from a boulevard to a footpath. Cold poured off the stone on either side like water off eaves. Here and there in the slack seams along the walls, the ice had already made its first thin lace, and their bow wave crackled through it with a sound like walking on autumn leaves.

"Left," Silas said.

Osk pulled. The boat swung, bit, held. The first rock rib slid past on the starboard side close enough to touch, black and streaming, its downstream wake boiling in the moonlight like something breathing.

"Right."

They came around the second bend harder than he'd wanted, swept by a cross-current he'd forgotten or misjudged — and there it was, the thing he'd feared, the third hazard that wasn't on any chart because it hadn't existed yesterday: a pan of ice, broad as a barn door, riding down the channel ahead of them, turning slowly in the moonlight like a great pale lily pad.

"Hard right! Pull!"

They pulled. The pan went by to port with ten feet to spare, its edge crusted and ridged, thick enough — he could see it now, moonlight caught in its layers — to stave in planking like a boot through a fence picket. Behind them he heard Brandt's boat swing wide, heard the scrape-silence-scrape of a near miss, heard Junie's voice, high and clear and absurdly calm: "That was a large piece of weather."

A laugh burst out of Osk, ragged with relief. Even Pell grinned into the dark.

"One more bend," Silas said. "Then it opens."

It opened. All at once, the way a fist opens — the walls fell back, the channel widened, the current gentled, and the moon laid its road out ahead of them across five miles of open water, unbroken, silver, and — he made himself look carefully, twice — free of ice. For now.

He let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the staging dock, weeks and a lifetime ago.

Behind them, the second boat came around the bend clean, riding light, Brandt's silhouette steady at the tiller. Hale sat motionless in the stern. As they drew alongside in the calmer water, Silas heard him say something low to Brandt — too low to catch — and saw the doctor's shoulders stiffen and then deliberately ease, the way a man's do when he decides a fight isn't worth having tonight.

Silas logged it, the way he logged everything now: a small debt of suspicion, entered in the ledger he kept behind his eyes.

They made camp two hours later on a gravel bar below the narrows, exhausted and giddy, and Brandt dealt out coffee like a sacrament. Junie sat down next to Silas on the cold stones, shoulder almost touching his, and said quietly, "You talked the whole way. Out loud. I could hear you from our boat."

"Was I saying anything useful?"

"You were saying *come on, come on, come on* in this very patient voice, like you were coaxing a horse over a bridge." She hugged her tin mug. "Did you know you were doing it?"

"No," Silas admitted.

"My mother does that," she said. "When she's scared. She talks softer the scarier it gets." She glanced at him sidelong, and there was something careful in it, something offered. "Anyway. Thought you'd want to know."

He looked out at the river — at the moon-road, at the dark water running fast and free toward a sea it would reach before the ice did — and felt something shift in his chest that had been rigid for twenty years. Discipline was a fine thing. It had built bridges and schedules and a career, and it had buried him alive in a depot at the end of a dead line. But tonight, for four hours in the dark, he had set the rules down and picked up instinct instead, and instinct had carried seven people through.

The price was coming. He could feel it out there in the cold, patient as winter. Nothing ran a river like that for free.

He was almost asleep when Junie's hand closed on his arm in the dark — hard, urgent — and he heard it too: from the direction of the second boat, the thin electric whistle of a transmitter keying up, far past midnight, far past any honest purpose.

Someone was sending a message south.
