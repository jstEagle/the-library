## 12. The Wreck at Sable Bend

The cold came up out of the water like a hand closing around the wrist. Silas had felt it before—on the Kettle River, the winter of the washout, the year everything went wrong the first time—and he had told himself for eleven days that this was not that river, that these were not those boats, that a man did not get to make the same mistake twice.

He was wrong about the second part. He just didn't know it yet.

They ran the narrows at first light, because the ice had given them no choice. All night the river had been talking to itself in a new voice—a clicking, groaning chatter along the banks where the shallows froze first, then sheets of frazil ice riding the current like drowned lace. By dawn the open channel at Sable Bend had narrowed from forty yards to twelve, and every hour they waited, it would give them eight fewer.

"Water's at thirty-two degrees," Brandt said, crouched at the gunwale, thermometer in hand like a man reading a fever. "A person in that water has maybe four minutes of useful consciousness. Five if they're stubborn."

"Then we don't put anybody in the water," Silas said.

It sounded, later, like a promise. He hadn't meant it as one. He'd meant it as arithmetic.

* * *

He laid it out on the gravel bar with a stick in the sand, the way he'd once laid out rail grades for men who trusted him with their lives. Two boats, lightened. Everything nonessential portaged around the bend on the near shore—half a mile of willow and shingle, ugly but walkable. The boats would take the channel with three aboard each: pilot, ballast forward, one hand with a boat hook to fend ice. Everyone else overland to meet them below the bend.

"Silas." Junie stood with her arms crossed, radio strap over her shoulder, and she had the look she got when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed. "The channel's twelve feet wide. The ice shelf on the outside of the bend is—"

"I know how wide it is."

"I know you know. I'm saying it anyway, so that if it goes wrong, it went wrong with everybody's eyes open."

He looked at her. Nineteen years old, lied about her age to get here, had been keeping his secrets since the silent camp, and now she was doing the thing no one had done for him since the washout: she was making him say the danger out loud.

"The current funnels at the head of the bend," he said. "If we hold the boat straight and keep her bow into the ice pans, we thread it. If we broach—" He drew the stick across the sand. "If we broach, we're against the shelf, and the river does what it wants."

"And the plates?"

"The plates go in the dry bag, strapped to the ballast. They float."

Hale, standing apart as always with his coat buttoned to the throat, said nothing. That should have told Silas something. Hale always had something to say about risk. A man who has already decided the outcome doesn't argue about the plan.

"Verrane goes overland," Silas said. "Brandt with him. The doctor doesn't run rapids, and the surveyor doesn't get wet."

Verrane started to object—the man had been objecting on principle since the maintenance shelter, as if complaint were a duty he'd been assigned—and Brandt took his elbow with a gentleness that closed the argument better than force would have.

The two rivermen, Ossler and Tam, drew straws for the second boat, and Tam lost, and grinned about it. Tam was the kind of man who grinned at bad luck the way other men grinned at a joke, on the theory that luck hated a sour face. He was thirty-one years old. Silas would remember that number for the rest of his life, the way you remember the exact weight of something you failed to lift.

* * *

The first boat went through clean.

Silas ran it himself, Junie forward with the hook, Ossler aft. The channel took them the way a hand takes a card—quick, impersonal, a little rough. Ice clattered against the hull like applause from a cheap seat. At the tail of the bend the river opened and there was the gravel bar and the overland party waving, and for one whole minute Silas let himself feel the thing he had trained out of himself years ago: satisfaction.

He waved Tam's boat in.

He should have gone back up and run it again himself. There was a rope. There was time—twenty minutes, maybe less, but time. Instead he stood on the bar and watched Tam take the head of the bend a half-degree wide, and he watched the current find the gap between the boat and the ice shelf, and he watched the river do what it wanted.

It happened the way bad things on water happen, which is to say all at once and then very slowly. The bow caught the shelf edge. The boat slewed. The ice took her amidships with a sound like a door closing in an empty house, and the river folded her over, and three men were in water the color of gunmetal.

Ossler came up fast, grabbed a pan, was hauled out by the overland party with a rope inside of a minute. Verrane came up slower. Brandt had him by the collar before his head fully cleared the surface.

Tam did not come up.

Silas was in the water before he had decided to be. That was the thing he would turn over and over in the nights after, looking for the flaw in it: there was no decision. There was only the four minutes, and his hands.

The cold was not cold. That was the lie of it. The water was so far past cold that it became something else, a pressure, a white noise, a fist. He got a handful of Tam's jacket on the second dive. Tam's eyes were open. That was the detail that stayed. A man can be under thirty seconds and his eyes are still open, still surprised, still asking the river why.

Silas got an arm around him and kicked for the surface and the current took them both, and for eleven seconds—Brandt counted them later, from the bank, counting because counting was all a doctor can do—Silas held a living man in water that kills in four minutes.

Then a pan of ice the size of a barn door, riding high on the current, took them both amidships the way it had taken the boat, and Silas's arm was torn loose, and Tam went under with his eyes still open, and Silas came up alone with his hands full of nothing.

They found Tam two hours later, a quarter mile down, hung in the shallows like a coat on a nail. Brandt worked on him for a long time. Brandt was the kind of man who worked on a man for a long time even when every part of him that knew medicine had already said the word. He worked until his own hands were shaking with the cold he'd ignored, and then he sat back on his heels in the snow and said, very quietly, the time of death, as if writing it in a ledger no one else could see.

* * *

They buried what they could bury. The ground was iron; Brandt read the words over a cairn of river stones, and Junie stood with her hat off and her jaw set in the way she set it when crying would be a luxury, and Ossler wept the way big quiet men weep, without any sound at all, which is worse.

Silas stood apart. He had dried off, changed clothes, done all the competent things. His hands had a tremor in them that would not quit, and he kept looking at them as if they belonged to the inquiry.

He had held the man. He had held him, and the river had simply taken him back, the way a bank takes back a loan. That was the arithmetic he could not make come out: he had done everything right, or nearly everything, and it had not mattered. And underneath that, in smaller letters, the arithmetic he could make come out, and did not want to: there had been a rope. There had been twenty minutes. He had waved the boat in because the first run had gone clean and because some old vanity, some rusted hinge in him, had wanted the second run to prove the first was skill and not luck.

Guilt, he thought, is just ballast you can't jettison. It sits in the hold. It keeps you trimmed. It does not keep you afloat.

* * *

Hale came to him at dusk, at the fire, where Silas sat turning his father's compass over and over in his ruined hands.

"Ossler won't row for you," Hale said. No preamble. Hale did not believe in preambles; they wasted daylight.

"He's in shock."

"He's in grief, and grief needs somewhere to go. Right now it's pointed at you." Hale crouched, warming his hands with the efficiency of a man who had calculated exactly how much warmth he required. "You ran the first boat yourself and sent Tam through on faith. That's what Ossler saw. That's what everyone saw."

"What do you want, Hale?"

"Order." Hale said it without heat, which was the terrible part. There was no cruelty in the man that Silas could find, no gloating. Just an accountant's patience with the fact that accounts must balance. "Six weeks ago you were a hired pilot. Today you're a man the river nearly killed, holding a dead man's weight in your hands, and every decision from here down is going to be second-guessed by people who watched Tam die. You can't lead like that. I can. Nobody expects me to be loved."

"And the plates?"

Hale looked at the fire. "The plates come under my control. For safekeeping. Verrane's judgment is compromised—he's half-frozen and half-mad about his survey. Yours is compromised." He stood, brushed snow from his knee. "It isn't punishment, Galloway. It's just what the situation requires. You of all people should understand that. Situations require things."

Silas looked up at him. *You of all people.* Hale knew. Had probably known since the staging dock—had read the fine print of Silas's life the way Silas had read the fine print of the contract, and found the same clause: *this man can be spent.*

"I'll need Verrane to hand them over," Silas said. "They're his."

"Verrane will hand them over," Hale said, "because Verrane is going to be told, gently, by a doctor he trusts, that carrying them is endangering the party. And it will even be true." He paused at the edge of the firelight. "You're a good pilot, Galloway. That's not nothing. You'll still row. You'll still know the water. But the decisions are mine now, and the first one is that we move at first light, downriver, fast, before the channel closes entirely."

Downriver. Toward Corver's men, though Silas didn't know that yet. Toward whatever Hale's employers had arranged, the way a river arranges itself around a bend you can't see.

* * *

That night Silas did not sleep. He sat with the compass open in his palm and let the needle hunt, and he thought about Tam's eyes, and about the rope on the bank, and about the twenty minutes, and about how a debt is a strange thing, how it can sit quiet for years and then put on new clothes and a new face and sit down across the fire from you wearing a dead man's name.

Junie came and sat beside him without asking. That was her way. She'd been filing things away since the silent camp—his map, his nights, his silences—and she had never once used any of it against him.

"I ran the numbers," she said finally. "On the second boat. Weight, draft, the funnel at the head of the bend. Even balanced, even perfect, it was a coin toss. Maybe worse." She looked into the fire. "You didn't kill him, Silas. The river did. The ice did. The winter did."

"Someone waved the boat in."

"Yes." She didn't flinch from it. That was the thing about Junie; she never flinched from a true thing just because it hurt the person who needed to hear it. "And someone built the boats, and someone made the ice, and someone hired all of us and didn't tell us what we'd find. You want to carry all of it. But some of it isn't yours to carry, and if you load yourself past your draft, you'll swamp the rest of us too."

He almost smiled. Nineteen years old, and she talked like a pilot.

"Hale has the plates," he said.

"I know. I watched him take them." She lowered her voice, and the firelight did something to her face that made her look older and younger at once. "And I watched where he put them. And Silas—" she glanced toward the dark shapes of the boats, toward Hale's tent, toward the river running black and loud in the dark, "—tomorrow, when we launch, I think you should know what I heard on the radio tonight. Because somebody downriver is answering Hale's calls. And they aren't answering like rescuers."

The needle of the compass trembled against its glass, hunting north, and could not settle.
