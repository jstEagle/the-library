## 13. Junie's Frequency

The radio had been smashed from the inside, which was the kind of detail Junie Okafor appreciated the way other people appreciated a good joke. Somebody had wanted it dead—had held it down and put a boot or a rock through its heart with real commitment—and yet here she was at the Silent Camp's collapsed mess table with a screwdriver in her teeth and a soldering iron borrowed from Brandt's medical kit (he used it for cauterizing; she'd promised to clean it), coaxing the thing back toward life.

"You know," she said to nobody, because everybody else was out checking the boats against the ice, "you're not fooling anybody."

The set was a Hallicrafters S-38, older than she was, the kind of radio that came with a personality. Its vacuum tubes were intact—that was the miracle, or maybe just the carelessness of whoever had killed it. The damage was all chassis and wiring: the tuning capacitor bent like a bad opinion, the antenna lead torn free, one transformer hanging by its solder joints like a man off a cliff by his fingernails.

Junie had lied about her age to get her operator's ticket, lied about her experience to get on this expedition, and lied to her mother about all of it in one long letter she'd never sent. Lying, she was learning, was mostly bookkeeping. You had to keep track of what you owed whom. And right now she owed Silas Galloway a truth she'd been sitting on for four days, and the interest was compounding.

Because she hadn't told him about the transmissions.

* * *

It had started the second night after they found Verrane and the two survivors in the maintenance shelter, when everyone else was asleep and the fire had burned down to a red eye. Junie had been fiddling with the portable set—not the smashed expedition radio, that was still back at camp, but her own field unit, the one Hale had signed for and then ignored. She'd been hunting the weather band, because the sky over the high country had gone the color of dishwater and she wanted to know how much trouble it intended.

She found trouble on a different frequency instead.

A voice, clipped and corporate, reading coordinates. Not coordinates of where they were. Coordinates of where they would be—the lower Ashwater, the narrows above Sable Bend, dated three days out. Then a callsign she didn't recognize, and a phrase that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone: *Package confirmation. Interception assets in position.*

She'd written it down. She always wrote things down; it was the only honest habit she had.

And then she hadn't told anyone, because telling meant explaining why she'd been scanning at midnight, and explaining meant admitting she'd been listening on frequencies she wasn't assigned, and admitting that meant confessing that she'd also copied fragments of the Verrane expedition's log weeks ago—fragments about a navigable passage through the range, fragments that made the whole concession look like a dead man walking—and she'd told Silas that secret and no one else, and one secret shared felt like courage while two secrets hoarded felt like something with a colder name.

So she'd carried it. Four days. Through the frostbite and the portage and the splitting of the plates, through Silas teaching her to read water by the way the current combed the surface, through meals where Hale smiled his filing-cabinet smile and asked cheerful questions about their route and their schedule and their fuel, and every time he asked, Junie heard the voice on the frequency saying *interception assets in position*, and she passed the beans and said nothing.

Tonight the beans ran out of time.

* * *

The expedition radio coughed, sparked, and produced a hum like a hive of very tired bees. Junie adjusted the bent capacitor plates with a wooden spoon handle, easing them apart a millimeter at a time, and the hum resolved into static, and the static into the river of voices that lived inside every radio if you knew how to listen.

"There you go," she whispered. "There you go, old man."

Footsteps behind her. She nearly dropped the spoon.

Silas stood in the doorway of the shelter with snow in his gray hair and an expression she was beginning to be able to read: not suspicion exactly, more like a man watching weather. He looked at the resurrected radio, then at her, then at the notebook open on the table beside her—open to a page dense with copied transmissions, dates, times, frequencies.

He didn't say anything. That was the worst part about Silas. He let silences do his talking, and the silences were fluent.

"I can explain," Junie said.

"Probably," he said. "I've got time."

She pushed the notebook across the table before she could lose her nerve. "I've been listening. Not just tonight. Since the shelter." Her voice did something embarrassing in the middle, so she stopped and started again, flatter. "Hale's been transmitting. Short bursts, low power, usually between two and four in the morning when everyone's asleep. I picked up the first one by accident—I was looking for weather—and I should have said something right away, and I didn't, because saying something meant telling you everything, and—" She gestured at the notebook. "It's all there. Read it."

Silas read. He was slow about it, the way he was slow about everything, turning pages with fingers scarred from thirty years of rail work, and Junie stood there feeling like a specimen under glass while the radio murmured static and the wind worried the shelter's loose siding.

"Interception assets," he said finally. "In position."

"Below Sable Bend. Three days out as of four nights ago." She swallowed. "Which means as of tonight, they might already be there."

"And you sat on this."

"Yes." She made herself hold his eyes. His weren't hard, exactly. They were tired, and disappointed, and underneath both of those things, horribly, understanding—which was so much worse than anger that she almost wished he'd shout. "I kept telling myself I'd tell you tomorrow. And then tomorrow there was the portage, and then the ice, and then it got easier not to say it, because every day I didn't say it was another day I didn't have to admit I'd been listening in the first place. I know how that sounds. I'm nineteen, not stupid. It sounds bad."

"It sounds human," Silas said. "It sounds like me, twenty years ago, holding a ledger full of small debts and telling myself I'd settle them next season." He closed the notebook gently, like it was something that could bruise. "Why are you telling me now?"

"Because of this." She turned back to the radio. "Listen."

She'd been copying a transmission when he walked in—one of Hale's, going out on the same frequency, and this one longer than usual, this one with a tremor in the operator's fist even through the code. She tuned it, caught the tail, wrote fast:

*...plates confirmed split. Decoys with my party. True set with Galloway. Recommend intercept at narrows regardless. Confirm: survivors' disposition?*

And the answer had come back before Silas finished reading, crisp and cold, and Junie copied it with a hand that shook and hated itself for shaking:

*Disposition at your discretion. No witnesses beyond required personnel. Concession integrity paramount.*

She read it aloud anyway, because some things needed air. "'No witnesses beyond required personnel.'" Her voice cracked on *witnesses*. "That's us. That's Brandt, that's Verrane, that's the rivermen, that's—" She stopped, because the list had gotten short and personal. "That's me."

Silas stood very still. Outside, the wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale, and somewhere downriver, though she couldn't possibly have heard it, Junie imagined men in a boat, moving upstream in the dark, carrying rifles and a contract.

"He sold us," she said. "All of us. For paper."

"He sold us for arithmetic," Silas said. "It's worse. Paper you can burn." He was quiet a moment, and then he did something she didn't expect: he pulled out the camp's single chair, sat down heavily, and looked at her with those tired eyes. "You should have told me sooner. And I should have noticed sooner that you weren't sleeping. We're both guilty of the same crime—keeping books we don't show anybody." A ghost of a smile. "Your mother know you're out here?"

"She thinks I'm a junior clerk in a survey office in Fort Meridian."

"Good Lord." He rubbed his face. "When this is over, you're writing that letter."

"If there's an 'after,'" Junie said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because his face did something complicated.

"There's an after," he said. "I've decided. I spent twenty years deciding the other way and it got me a depot full of dust. So." He stood. "We wake them up."

"Now? It's past midnight—"

"Junie." He said her name the way he said everything, plain and level, and it landed harder than shouting. "There are armed men on this river who've been told to make us disappear. Every hour we sleep is an hour they gain. Wake them up."

* * *

Waking them up took the form of Silas standing in the middle of the shelter striking a match three times, which brought Brandt out of his bedroll with a physician's instant alertness, Verrane up with a survivor's panic, and Hale emerging last, unhurried, buttoning his coat, wearing the expression of a man who had been expecting this meeting for days and had prepared remarks.

"What's the occasion?" Hale asked pleasantly.

Junie had rehearsed this part on the walk from the radio table—rehearsed it eleven times, each version braver than the last—and now, standing in front of the whole crew with the notebook in her hands, she discovered that rehearsal was useless. Courage, it turned out, wasn't the absence of fear. It was confession with your knees knocking.

"Hale's been transmitting our position," she said. "For days. There's an intercept team on the river below Sable Bend, and their orders are—" her voice wobbled and she planted it, "—their orders are no witnesses. Us. They mean to kill us or disappear us, and Hale called it 'disposition at discretion,' and I know because I've been listening to him since the shelter, and I should have told you all four days ago, and I didn't, and I'm sorry, and I'm telling you now."

The silence afterward had texture. Brandt's face went through surprise into something gentler and sadder. Verrane sat down slowly, like a man whose legs had received news ahead of him. And Hale—

Hale looked at Junie with something that wasn't quite anger. It was almost professional respect, briefly, before the filing cabinet closed again.

"That's a serious accusation," he said.

"I have dates, times, and frequencies." She held up the notebook. "Check my copy against your own log, if you kept one. Operators always do."

"You intercepted company communications without authorization."

"I intercepted *murder arrangements* without authorization," Junie said, and heard her own voice come out steady, and thought: *oh. So that's what it feels like.* "You can court-martial me later. If there's a later."

Brandt spoke quietly. "Is it true?"

And here was the moment Junie watched for, the hinge of the whole night: Hale, given a door marked *deny*, standing in front of five people and a stove. He opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at the fire.

"The company invested considerably in this expedition," he said at last. "The company expects a return. What any of you imagine you're owed—" he spread his hands, reasonable, terrible, "—was never part of the ledger."

"So that's yes," said the elder riverman, Torv, from the doorway, where he'd appeared with the silent inevitability of large men.

"It's arithmetic," Hale said.

"Arithmetic," Verrane repeated. He laughed—a dry, broken sound with no humor in it. "I found a living river route through the range. Do you understand what that means? Boats, Hale. Freight moving through the valley without your rails, without your concession, forever. And your answer is to drown it in blood and bury it in permits."

"My answer," Hale said, "is to keep the company solvent, which keeps two thousand men employed, which keeps towns alive. You found a pretty channel. I found a balance sheet. One of us is being realistic."

"One of us," Brandt said mildly, "is planning to murder a doctor, two boatmen, a teenager, and a disgraced engineer, and calling it realism." He said it without heat, which somehow made it land like a hammer. "I've treated men like you, Mr. Hale. Not morally—physiologically. The body keeps score even when the mind won't. You haven't slept properly in a week. Your hands shake at rest. Whatever you think you serve, it's eating you alive, and it will not thank you."

For one naked instant, Hale's composure slipped, and Junie saw underneath: a tired man in a cold country, far from anyone who'd notice if he vanished, serving masters who had just transmitted the words *no witnesses* on an open frequency. Then it sealed over.

"You're all being hysterical," he said. "Nobody is killing anybody. But I'll thank you to remember who holds the contracts." He gathered his coat and stepped out into the snow, and the door banged behind him.

Silence. Then Torv said, "Somebody should watch him."

"Somebody is," Silas said. "From now on, somebody always is." He looked around the shelter—at Brandt, at Verrane, at Torv and his brother, at Junie—and something shifted in his face, a settling, like a load finding its proper balance. "Here's where we stand. There's a team below Sable Bend. The river's icing early. We can't go down the main channel, can't wait them out, and can't outrun them loaded. Which means we do the one thing nobody sane would do."

"The Gap," Torv said, and spat, ceremonially, at the idea.

"The Gap," Silas agreed. "At first light. And before anyone asks—yes, I know a way through. It's on a map I swore I'd burn, and I find I'm no longer sentimental about oaths taken for the wrong reasons." He paused, and his eyes found Junie's, and stayed there. "One more thing. This girl lied about her age, listened in on a killer for four days without sleeping, and then stood up in front of all of us and confessed it when keeping quiet would've been safer. When we get home—if we get home—somebody put in a word for her license. Because that," and his voice roughened slightly, "is the finest radio operator I've ever traveled with, and I've traveled with a few."

Junie felt her ears go hot. "I cried a little during the confession," she offered.

"Noted," said Brandt. "Still counts."

Outside, the wind rose, and downriver—unseen, unhurried, inevitable—men were moving through the dark toward them with a contract in their pocket and no witnesses in their plans. Junie looked at the ragged, frightened, stubborn crew around the stove, at the doctor and the broken surveyor and the rivermen and the disgraced engineer, and understood that something had finished forming tonight, something none of them had planned and none of them could now undo.

They were a crew. God help them all.

"First light," Silas said. "Get some sleep. All of you. Tomorrow we run the Gap."
