## 14. Corver's Men

The river told me before Junie did.

I'd been reading water since before I could read words—my father's doing, standing me on the bow of a supply scow with his big hand on my shoulder, saying *the river whispers to them that listen, Silas, and shouts at them that don't.* What it was shouting that morning, two days below the portage, was simple: too few birds on the water, too much quiet from the banks where there should have been kingfishers arguing over territory. And the current had a smell to it, faint and wrong, like stove oil.

"Engines," I said, and Brandt looked up from the splint he was rewrapping on Verrane's hand. "Below the bend. More than one."

Junie was already scrambling aft to our radio, which she'd resurrected from the expedition's wreckage with wire, stubbornness, and what I suspected was outright theft from Hale's abandoned kit. She'd been listening for three days now—she'd confessed it the night before, chin up, waiting for us to be angry, and instead Verrane had laughed until his frostbitten ribs made him stop, and said she was the first honest spy he'd ever met. Now her face came up pale over the set.

"It's them," she said. "Corver's frequency. They're asking for confirmation of intercept position." A pause. Her voice went smaller. "Silas. They're using your name."

*Of course they are,* I thought. *A debt always knows where you live.*

We ran the lower rapids because running them was faster than lining them, and lining them meant being caught in the open with boats full of survey plates and one half-frozen surveyor who couldn't grip a line. I took us through the chute at Sable Bend's tail with my hands remembering things my conscience would've rather forgotten—the same water that had taken Ossie Wrenn from us, though a different arm of it, gentler, and I hated myself a little for how easily my body did the work while my mind stood off to the side taking notes. We came through clean. The boats were barely beached on the gravel bar below the bend when they came around it.

Three boats, aluminum, new, the kind of money Corver spent when he wanted a thing done properly. Six men. And standing in the bow of the lead boat, holding a rail like a man holding a lectern, was Emory Corver himself.

He hadn't changed. That was the obscenity of it. Eleven years since the night at Kettle Junction when I signed the paper that put my license in his drawer, and he looked exactly the same—gray at the temples now, but the same neat mustache, the same small eyes that did their arithmetic on you before you finished saying hello. He wore a wool coat worth more than my depot. He stepped onto the gravel bar like a man stepping into his own parlor.

"Silas Galloway," he said, warmly, as if we were old friends meeting at a funeral. Which, in a way, we were.

"Emory." My voice came out flat and steady, and I was grateful to it. "You've come a long way to stand on cold rocks."

"I go where the assets are." His gaze moved past me, cataloguing: the boats, the crates, Verrane sitting wrapped in blankets with his bandaged hand, Brandt standing easy with his arms crossed, Junie planted between the radio and the plates like a gatehouse. "And here they all are. Every loose end in one tidy place. You've saved me considerable expense."

"They're not ends," I said. "They're people."

"Yes," Corver said, with the mild patience of a man correcting a child's arithmetic. "That's the difficulty."

* * *

He walked the bar while he talked, hands clasped behind his back, boots crunching. His men stayed by the boats—not aiming anything, not yet, but standing in the particular loose readiness of men paid to be ready. I counted them without seeming to. Old habit. Six against five, except one of ours was Verrane, whose hands didn't work, and one of theirs was a big quiet man named Dreele whom I remembered from the bad years, who once held a door shut for Corver while Corver did something inside that I still don't let myself think about.

"The way I see it," Corver said, "there are two versions of the next hour. In the first version, Mr. Verrane surrenders his survey materials—which are, whatever else they may be, the property of the concession holders—and everyone present signs a statement confirming the expedition found nothing of consequence. Nothing navigable. Nothing that would embarrass anyone. Then we all go home, and certain debts—" his eyes came back to me, gentle as a trap closing, "—are considered settled. Fully settled, Silas. The Kettle Junction paper, burned. Your license restored. A letter of reference, if you want it."

The words landed in me like coins dropped down a well. I heard them splash. I waited to feel something.

"In the second version," Corver went on, "we take the materials anyway, and take statements anyway, and the difference is that some of you will have become uncooperative, and uncooperative people on remote rivers have historically had accidents. Rivers are dangerous." He spread his hands, a showman offering the weather. "I'd hate for anyone to have an accident."

"You'd write the report yourself," I said.

"I always do."

Behind me, I heard Junie shift her weight. I didn't look at her. Looking at her, I knew, would be the end of me, so I kept my eyes on Corver and did the thing I'd spent eleven years getting good at, which was arithmetic under pressure. Six men, three boats, rifles I could see and probably one or two I couldn't. Two of our boats seaworthy enough to run the gorge below—if the ice hadn't closed it, and every hour the air got colder and the river got quieter in that listening way it does before it locks. If we gave him the plates, Verrane's people would dam this valley inside a year and call it progress. If we fought, somebody bled, and the somebody most likely to bleed was a nineteen-year-old girl who lied about her age to get on a boat.

Eleven years ago I'd done this math and come out the other side a man hiding in a dead town, keeping books of small debts, telling himself that survival was a kind of honor if you kept it quiet enough.

"Surrender the plates," Corver said, "and the survivors' silence, and you walk free. All of you. I give you my word."

"There it is," I said.

"There what is?"

"Your word. Last time I took it, it cost me everything I had that couldn't be repossessed." I finally turned, then, because I couldn't help it, and looked down the bar. Junie, watching me with her jaw set, her whole face saying *don't you dare*. Brandt, calm as Sunday, giving me the smallest nod—a doctor's nod, the kind that says *I'll patch whatever you break.* Verrane, gray-lipped, pulling himself upright against a crate despite his ruined hand, because a surveyor doesn't sit down for the surrender of his life's work.

And past them, the river, running north toward home, carrying its skin of new ice at the edges like lace on a sleeve.

"Here's my trouble, Emory," I said. "I've been paying you back for eleven years. Provision runs. Quiet work. I told myself it was debt, and debt is honest, and a man who pays what he owes gets to keep his self-respect even if he loses everything else." I took a breath. The cold went all the way down. "But I've been reading my own ledger wrong. It was never a debt. A debt has terms. This was just you holding the door shut, year after year, and me thanking you for the draft."

Corver's smile thinned by a degree. "Careful, Silas."

"No," I said. "That's the whole point. I'm done being careful. It's the most expensive habit I ever had."

I looked at Junie. Nineteen years old, three thousand miles from anywhere she'd been born, holding a river valley in a canvas bag because she believed the people in it mattered more than the paper about it. I looked at Brandt, who had treated a drowned man's crewmates with more tenderness than any of us deserved. I looked at Verrane, who had buried one of his own in frozen ground and carried the rest out on his back.

For the first time in my life, I refused.

"No," I said again, and the word came out of me like a stake driven into stone. "No plates. No silence. No deal. Keep your freedom, Emory—I've seen what it buys."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I'll tell it plain because plainness is all I have left that's true.

Corver sighed, the way a man sighs over a spoiled invoice, and nodded at Dreele, and Dreele started forward, and that was when Junie's radio—our radio, her resurrection, her three days of stolen parts and stubborn wire—crackled to life at full volume on the gravel bar.

Every head turned. Corver's men turned, which was the point, because Junie hadn't stumbled onto the frequency by accident; she'd tuned it loud on purpose, and I understood that a half-second after everyone else did, which is becoming a habit with her.

The voice coming out of it was one of Corver's own boat captains, reporting position, and Junie had let it run long enough for every man on that bar to hear his own employer's business broadcast across the rocks like a town crier. It bought us four seconds of confusion. Four seconds is a long time on a river.

"Boats," I said. "Now."

Brandt had Verrane up and moving before I finished the word—he'd been waiting too, all of them had been waiting, and the knowledge of that went through me warmer than whiskey. We shoved off into the current with Corver shouting behind us, his composure cracking at last into something human and ugly, and the first rifle shot cracked off the far bank and skipped its echo away downstream, hitting nothing, hitting everything, ending the world we'd been floating in.

The gorge opened ahead of us, black water between walls of granite, and above it the sky had gone the color of tin, and along the banks the ice was building fast, white teeth growing inward from both shores.

Junie crouched beside me at the stern, one hand on the gunwale, wind whipping her hair sideways.

"That channel," she shouted, pointing at the narrowing throat below, where the two ice shelves reached for each other like hands across a table. "Can we make it?"

I looked at the gap. I looked at the water piling dark and fast through it. Everything I'd ever been trained to say rose up in me—*no, line the boats, wait for light, wait for thaw*—and underneath it, quieter, my father's voice, or maybe just my own at last: *the river shouts at them that don't listen.*

"We're going to find out," I said, and put the bow into the gap.
