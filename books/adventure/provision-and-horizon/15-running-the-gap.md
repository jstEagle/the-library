## 15. Running the Gap

I had always thought dawn was a gentle thing. That was before I learned what it looked like from the bottom of a gorge, arriving like a verdict.

The light came down the canyon walls in layers — gray first, then a thin band of gold that touched the water and made the ice floes glow like broken china. We had maybe forty minutes of usable dark left when Silas shook me awake, and by the time I'd gotten my boots on my feet were already cold enough to file a complaint.

"Up," he said. "All of you. We're leaving now."

Brandt was already up, of course. Brandt slept the way a lighthouse sleeps — technically present, functionally alert. He was crouched over Verrane, checking the man's bandaged hands by the light of a shielded lantern.

"Hale?" Verrane asked, and his voice had the flatness of a man asking about weather he already knew was bad.

"Gone," Silas said. "Sometime in the night. Took the second boat, took the real-looking crate off the portage sled, and took the fuel we didn't have to spare." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "Junie, you want to tell them the rest?"

They all looked at me. I'd been dreading this part since three in the morning, when I'd pulled Hale's transmission out of the static like a splinter.

"He radioed ahead," I said. "There's a launch waiting at the lower rapids. Corver's men. Six of them, maybe more. They're supposed to take custody of the survey plates and—" I stopped. The next part sat in my throat like a fishbone.

"And?" Brandt asked gently.

"And no witnesses," I said. "His words."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The river filled the silence for us, talking its endless gravel-mouthed talk against the shore ice. Okafor — that's me, Junie Okafor, nineteen years old and lying about it so long now that twenty felt like a costume I'd have to grow into — I stood there holding a radio handset like it could defend me.

It was Ovid, one of our two rivermen, who broke it. "So we're what, then? Witnesses?"

"We're evidence," Brandt said dryly. "Best not to be filed."

Silas had the map out. Not his closed-lines map — that one lived folded against his chest like a second heartbeat — but a grease-pencil sketch he'd been working on all night on the back of a supply manifest. He spread it on an overturned crate and weighted the corners with stones, and we crowded around it the way people crowd around a fire.

"The gorge road's dead," he said. "Corver will know that. His men will sit at the lower rapids because the lower rapids are the only way through — by water." His pencil tapped a line above the river, running along the north wall. "But there was a rail spur here. 1911. Served a quarry that failed before the quarry ever shipped anything. The grade is still there. Two miles of it, then an old trestle portage where they used to skid boats across the shoulder of the ridge to a siding on the far side."

"A trestle," Ovid repeated. "How old?"

"Old enough that I'd rather not find out how old."

"That's reassuring," said Pell, the other riverman, who had the particular pessimism of a man who'd been nearly drowned once already this trip and considered himself owed better luck.

"It's not a comfort," Silas agreed. "It's a road. It's the only one." He looked up, and in the lantern light his face had something I hadn't seen on it before — not the careful blankness he wore like a coat, but the thing underneath, which was a kind of fierce, tired love. For the lines. For the dead country he'd memorized. "My father helped survey that spur. He used to say the mountain kept its promises even after the company broke theirs. I intend to test that."

* * *

Loading the boats in the dark is a lesson in humility. Everything weighs twice what it should, everything goes in wrong, and everyone has opinions. We had one boat now instead of two, which meant triage: food, the decoy crates, Brandt's medical kit, the radio — my radio, salvaged and half-resurrected, wrapped in oilcloth and cradled like a baby. Verrane tried to bring a case of survey instruments and Silas looked at him until Verrane put it back.

"You'll get your plates back," Silas said. "What's left of them."

"They're decoys," Verrane muttered. "What's left of them is worth less than the wood."

"Then you won't mind carrying them yourself."

That got the first laugh out of anyone all morning — a small one, startled out of Pell, quickly smothered, but a laugh. I've learned since that this is how Silas worked: he never told jokes, but he arranged circumstances so that jokes became possible, and then pretended he hadn't.

We ran the first stretch of water in the last of the dark, fast and shallow, Ovid and Pell handling the oars while Silas read the channel by memory and moonset. I sat amidships with the radio on my lap and did the only useful thing I'm genuinely good at, which is listen. Below us, faint as a toothache, Corver's launch was idling at the lower rapids. I could hear their engine breathing. Every few minutes a voice crackled through — position checks, bored, confident. Men who believed the trap was already sprung.

"Half a mile," I reported. "They're not moving."

"They won't," Silas said. "Why would they? The river only comes to them."

We put ashore at a scree fan just below a bend, where the old rail grade began — I could see it once Silas pointed, a shelf cut into the hillside, level as a table, running up the gorge wall. Fifty years of alder and slide rock had grown over it, but the grade itself was stubborn. Rail grades are like that. Men build them to hold a hundred tons of locomotive, and then the men leave, and the grade stays, patient as a dog tied to a post.

"Boats on rollers," Silas said. "We brought the skids off the portage sled. It's two miles of grade, then the trestle crossing, then down to the far siding and the water again. By my count we beat the launch around the bend by four hours if we don't stop."

"And if we stop?" Verrane asked.

"Then we stop being a problem Corver solves with money and start being one he solves otherwise. So we don't stop."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hauling a boat uphill on a dead railroad at first light: it is absurd, and it is wonderful, and it will make a family of strangers faster than anything else on earth.

The boat weighed perhaps nine hundred pounds loaded. Six of us on the skids and rollers, Silas calling the rhythm, rocking it onto each roller ahead like a man dealing cards. The grade climbed steadily, the river dropping away below us until it was a silver thread stitched with white. The sun came over the eastern rim and lit the whole gorge gold, and for about ten minutes nobody complained at all, which I noted as a personal record.

Then Pell slipped on frost-slick tie, went down under the gunwale, and came up swearing with a vocabulary that would've embarrassed a mule driver, and morale returned to normal.

"Tell me about your father," I said to Silas somewhere in the second mile, mostly to keep my legs honest. When my mouth works, my legs work. Old trick.

Silas didn't answer right away. He was watching the leading edge of the boat clear a rock, hands flat on the hull like he was taking its pulse.

"He was a locator," he said finally. "Found the lines before the engineers drew them. He had a compass he trusted more than most people, and he was usually right to." A pause. "He died believing the company would do right by the valley. The company sent a form letter. I kept the compass and burned the letter, and I've been arguing with both decisions ever since."

"Which decision?"

"All of them." He almost smiled. "Keep pushing. Roller's drifting left."

But I'd gotten it — the shape of him, anyway. A man built by someone who believed in roads, broken by the people who owned them, still walking the grades out of habit or hope or some mixture he probably couldn't name. I thought about telling him I understood. I decided my legs needed the work more than my heart needed the speech.

* * *

The trestle appeared around the third bend of the grade, and it stopped us all mid-stride.

It crossed a side-canyon — a hanging valley, really, where a creek had sawed its way down to the main river — and it was a hundred and forty feet of blackened timber standing on stone piers, deck planks gone soft and gray, the whole structure leaning just slightly downstream like a tired man against a doorframe. Snow dusted the cross-bracing. Below it, the creek fell in a long white ribbon.

"That," said Pell, "is not a bridge. That is a memorial."

"It held a locomotive," Silas said.

"When?"

"Before the war."

"Which war?"

"The one where men still maintained things." He walked out onto the deck before anyone could stop him, testing each plank with a heel-strike, listening. We watched him go — thirty feet, fifty — the whole structure creaking a low wooden chord under him. He came back looking grim but not defeated, which I was learning to read as good news.

"Deck's sound on the upstream stringers, rotten on the downstream side. We cross single file, boats last, hugging the rail line. Nobody jumps, nobody hurries, nobody looks down." He looked at us. "Especially that last one."

"I wasn't going to look down," said Verrane, looking down.

Brandt went first with the medical kit, easy as crossing a street. Then Verrane, white-knuckled, eyes fixed on the far rim, moving with the stiff dignity of a cat being carried. Then me, with the radio strapped to my chest, and I'll be honest — halfway across, with the creek roaring under the gaps between the ties and the morning wind coming up the canyon smelling of snow, I understood exactly why Silas had banned looking down, and I also understood why he'd chosen this route anyway. Because the alternative was Corver. Because sometimes the rotten bridge is the honest option.

Ovid and Pell brought the boat across last, slow as a funeral, the old trestle groaning its whole history beneath them. When the runners finally touched solid grade on the far side, Pell sat down abruptly on a tie stump and laughed — really laughed, helpless, the laughter of a man whose body had decided fear and joy were the same sensation and picked joy.

Silas let him laugh for a full five seconds. Then: "Siding's a quarter mile. Water by nine. Move."

* * *

The far siding was a clearing and a collapsed shed and a ramp of stacked stone where boats had been lowered to the river sixty years ago. We reloaded in the full sun now, and the river below the gorge narrows ran open and quick, carrying slush pans that clicked against the hull like fingernails.

I had the radio on, scanning low, when I heard it.

"—unit two, confirm visual on the gorge mouth. Nothing moving. Over."

A pause. Then a different voice, and my stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat, because I knew it — I'd been listening to it for days, and hearing it come from *below* us meant—

"—copy. Launch is holding at the rapids. No sign of them topside. If they took the gorge road, they're ours whenever we want them. Over."

Hale. Alive, downstream, and reporting to Corver's men that we were nowhere — which meant either he'd lost us, or he wanted Corver's people relaxed while he did something else. And there was only one thing left to do.

"He knows about the trestle," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "He's not with the launch. He's running ahead. Downriver. To the junction, or to meet them, or—"

Silas was quiet for a moment, looking down the long shine of the river, where the first true ice of the season was forming in the eddies like breath on glass.

"He has the decoy plates," he said at last. "And he thinks they're real. Which means every hour he runs, he's delivering Junie's handiwork straight to Corver with a bow on it." Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, too tired for that, but close. "Let him run."

"And us?" Brandt asked.

"Us," Silas said, stepping aboard and taking the steering oar, "we run too. Straight down the middle, past their launch, before they finish their coffee." He glanced back at me, and there it was again, the thing underneath. "You'll hear them before we see them. You're our eyes, Junie. Call it however you have to."

I put the headset on. The river took us.

Behind us, high on the grade, the old trestle stood in the morning sun, holding nothing, holding everything — a dead line that had turned out, after fifty years, to be the only living road through the gorge. Ahead, around the bend, I could already hear the idle of Corver's launch, patient as a spider, and I keyed the mic and started counting the seconds between their transmissions, because that number, whatever else happened today, was going to save our lives.
