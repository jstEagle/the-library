## 17. The Trestle

The snow came in sideways off the ridge, the kind of dry, hissing snow that doesn't fall so much as arrive, and by the time we reached the cut where the old Ashwater & Northern had once clawed its way through the mountain, it was already filling our footprints behind us like a tide erasing a shore.

I stopped at the lip of the cut and let the others catch up, because I needed a moment I wasn't ready to explain.

There it was. Or there it wasn't.

The trestle.

Forty years ago my father had stood on this span with a transit and a thermos of coffee laced with something medicinal, checking the grade while the river roared two hundred feet below. He'd drawn it into the ledger of lines he kept in his head, and later—after the company folded, after the rails were pulled up for scrap and the ties left to rot—I'd drawn it onto paper. The map I carried against my ribs like a splinter. Closed line, decommissioned 1911, trestle at Mile 44 reported unsafe. Reported unsafe. Not reported gone.

But the middle third of it was gone now. Two hundred feet of timber and iron simply absent, dropped into the gorge sometime in the decades since some inspector's pen had given it a stay of execution. What remained was the near approach running out to a broken stub, and then—impossibly, absurdly—the far approach standing whole on the other side, close enough that you could almost believe you could jump it, if you were nineteen and stupid and Junie Okafor, which she was.

"It's out," Brandt said behind me. Not a question. A doctor delivering a diagnosis.

"The middle's down," I said. "The approaches are sound."

"And how," Verrane said, "do we get from one to the other?"

That was the question, all right. Behind us, maybe four miles back through the falling white, Hale was coming. Hale with the decoy plates in his pack and a boat he'd stolen and whatever was left of the man he'd been before this river started subtracting things from him. Ahead of us, the gorge narrowed toward the lower rapids where Corver's men would be waiting, unless the ice came first and closed the river like a book.

And below us, two hundred feet of air and black water going gray at the edges with new ice.

"We don't jump it," I said. "We walk under it."

* * *

Here is what nobody remembers about dead railroads, because nobody thinks about railroads the way an engineer's son thinks about them: they are not just track. They are bones. Every mile of closed line has a skeleton of maintenance walkways, inspection ledges, anchor points, and—the thing I was praying to God still existed—catwalks. Iron walkways bolted to the downstream trusses so that inspectors could crawl along beneath the deck and tap the timbers with a hammer and write their little reports. My father had crawled this one. He'd told me about it once, when I was small enough to think crawling under a trestle sounded like an adventure instead of a job description. *You can hear the river talking up through the boards,* he'd said. *Like it's telling on you.*

"Under it," Junie repeated. She'd come up beside me, radio case strapped to her back, her face raw and chapped and set in the expression she wore when she'd decided not to argue until she understood what she was arguing against. "Silas. It's been forty years."

"Bolted iron doesn't rot like wood does."

"Iron rusts."

"Some iron rusts." I pulled the compass out of my coat. My father's compass, brass-cased, the needle worn pale at the north tip from a lifetime of pointing. I didn't need it to find the trestle; I needed it because holding it made my hands steady, the way a rosary steadies a certain kind of man. "My father walked this catwalk. If it held him, it'll hold us."

"He also said the trestle was safe," Verrane said quietly.

I looked at him. The expedition leader had come apart over the last week the way wet paper comes apart—slowly, then all at once—and what was left underneath was thinner but somehow more honest. "He reported it safe," I said. "In 1911. Men lie less when they're writing for people who'll check."

Brandt laughed—a short, surprised bark, quickly swallowed. Even now. Especially now.

Junie crouched at the edge of the near approach and peered over the side. Snow swirled down past her into the gorge and vanished. "I can't see anything."

"You won't. You climb down the anchor ladder to the ledge, turn east, and you feel with your feet. Left hand on the upstream cable, right hand on the truss. Twelve inches of walkway, maybe fourteen. You don't look down because looking down is a luxury."

"Fourteen inches," Junie said. "Of iron. Over two hundred feet of ice water. In a blizzard."

"When you put it that way it sounds bad."

She gave me a look that would have curdled milk, and I loved her for it—not in any way that needs saying out loud, but the way you love a person who has become load-bearing. Somewhere between the silent camp and Sable Bend, between the smashed radio and the drowned riverman whose name I would carry to my grave alongside my own debts, this ragged handful of strangers had become the only structure in my life that wasn't collapsed. And now I was asking them to cross a dead railroad's bones in a winter storm because I knew those bones better than anyone alive.

Because of a map I'd sworn to burn.

"Who goes first?" Brandt asked.

"Me," I said. "Then Junie, then Verrane, then you. Doctor goes last so if anyone falls apart on the walkway, they fall apart next to someone who can fix them."

"That's not how medicine works," Brandt said.

"It's how this works."

* * *

The anchor ladder was worse than I remembered it being described, which is to say it existed, which is to say it was a miracle. Rungs of flat bar stock, furred with rust, the top one crusted with ice that cracked under my boot like thin bread. I went down slow, testing each rung the way my father taught me to test everything: weight, then half again, then trust. The river talked up through the boards above me, exactly as promised. It sounded like it was telling on somebody.

The catwalk was there.

Fourteen inches of open-grate iron, bolted to the downstream truss, sagging in places but continuous—a dark seam running along the underside of the great wooden spine of the trestle, disappearing into the snow-hiss ahead. I stood on it with both feet and felt it hold, and something loosened in my chest that had been clenched since the day Corver's letter arrived at the depot.

"Catwalk's good!" I shouted up. "Send Junie!"

She came down the ladder with her teeth gritted and her eyes fixed on mine like I was the last solid fact in the world, and when her boots hit the grating beside me she exhaled a long white plume and said, very calmly, "If I die on this thing, I'm haunting you specifically."

"Noted. Hand on the upstream cable."

We went. Slowly. The storm came down the gorge in gusts that leaned on us like drunk uncles, and twice the catwalk's grating gave that soft, grinding shudder that iron gives when it's thinking about its options, and twice I stopped and pressed my palm flat against the truss and waited until it finished thinking. Below us the river ran black and fast in the channel's throat, already wearing a fringe of ice along the rocks like a collar buttoned one notch too high. Above us the missing middle third announced itself as nothing at all—just sky where deck should be, snow falling through the gap in long white sheets, two hundred feet of weather where forty years ago there had been creosote and iron and men with hammers.

At the gap itself, the catwalk did something I hadn't dared hope for. It didn't end. It swung outward—there had apparently once been an inspection platform cantilevered around the break, a little iron balcony hanging in the gorge—and then swung back in on the far side, where the surviving approach began. The platform's floor was mostly grate and mostly memory, but its frame was there, and its frame was anchored to the far approach's first bent, and the bent was standing.

We crossed that gap one at a time, each of us walking out onto a shelf of rusted iron suspended over a freezing river in a blizzard, each of us pausing at the far end with the particular expression of a person who cannot believe they have just done the thing they have just done. Verrane crossed with his survey satchel hugged to his chest like a child. Brandt crossed last, carrying the medical kit and, without being asked, Junie's spare mittens, which she'd frozen stiff and abandoned halfway.

When all four of us stood on the far approach, on solid—well, on *deck*, anyway—Junie sat down abruptly on a rotted tie and started laughing. Not hysterical laughing. The other kind. The kind that comes out of people who have just discovered they're still alive and find it funny.

"Your father," she said, wiping her face with a mitten, "crawled under here on purpose?"

"Every spring."

"For money."

"For thirty cents an hour."

She laughed harder. Brandt sat down next to her and after a moment Verrane sat too, and for about ninety seconds the four of us just sat on a dead railroad in a snowstorm, laughing at nothing, at everything, at being alive on the wrong side of a two-hundred-foot gap with our enemies on the other side of it.

It was the best ninety seconds of my year.

* * *

Hale caught us at dusk, three miles on, where the old line emerged from the cut and ran level along the bench above the river—the famous grade, the reason the Ashwater & Northern had ever existed, gentle and straight and built by men who believed the future ran on rails.

We heard him before we saw him. Boots on ballast, and then his voice, hoarse from cold, calling out across the falling light.

"Galloway!"

I turned. He stood fifty yards back up the line, a dark shape in the white, one hand raised—not with a weapon, though I had no doubt he carried one, and no doubt he'd carried it the whole way—with the other hand gripping the strap of his pack. Snow had gotten inside his collar and melted and refrozen. He looked, for the first time since the staging dock, like a man rather than a function.

"Don't come closer," I said. "Just say your piece."

"The plates." He lifted the pack slightly. "They're worthless. I know they're worthless. I've known since the portage."

Nobody breathed. Beside me, Junie's hand found my sleeve—not pulling, just anchoring.

"Verrane's real survey," Hale went on, and his voice cracked on the word *real* and he pushed through it, "is in that satchel. I watched him sleep with it. I did the arithmetic a hundred different ways and every sum came out the same: my employers want that route buried, and they want everyone who's seen it buried with it, and I have spent nine days pretending the second part was a rounding error." He stopped. The wind filled the silence. "I'm done pretending. I'm not good at much, Galloway, but I'm good at arithmetic, and the numbers finally changed."

"What numbers?" Brandt called.

"Yours." He started forward, slowly, hands visible. "Six lives against one concession. I used to carry that sum easily. Then a man drowned at Sable Bend and it stopped being six against one. It became seven against one, and seven wouldn't fit." He reached us and stopped, ten feet away, and set the pack down in the snow between us like a man laying down a debt. From inside his coat he drew the plates' oilcloth wrapping—empty, he'd transferred even the decoys—and then, after a pause that seemed to cost him, he shrugged off his outer coat, the heavy wool one, the one that had kept him warm through every mile he'd made the rest of us suffer, and laid it on top of the pack.

"Hale," Verrane said, "you son of a—"

"I know." He said it simply. "Take the coat. I've been warm my whole life. It hasn't suited me."

For a long moment none of us moved. The snow came down and settled on the pack and the coat and on Hale's bare head, and I thought about every man who had ever handed me a contract, and how not one of them had ever handed me anything else.

I picked up the coat and gave it to Brandt, who gave it to Verrane, whose lips had been going the wrong shade of blue for an hour. Some transactions you route through kindness so they can pass.

"The intercept team," Junie said quietly. "Below the rapids. How many?"

"Eight. Two boats. They're expecting me to deliver the plates and a list of names." Hale met her eyes, and something passed between them that I only partly understood—the minder and the girl who'd been listening to his transmissions for days, each knowing the other knew. "They're expecting a lot of things."

"And what are you expecting?" I asked.

He looked down the line, south, toward the junction, toward the depot, toward the deadline and the expiring concession and whatever tribunal waited at the end of all this. Then he looked at the four of us, standing together on a dead railroad in the gathering dark, and I watched him do the arithmetic one more time.

"A head start," he said. "And maybe a place by your fire, if there's room."

Junie answered before I could. "There's room."

I took out the compass. The needle swung, wobbled, and settled—north, steady, true, the way it always did, the way my father's hands had always been steady on the things he built. Forty years dead, the line was carrying us yet. The map I'd sworn to burn had become, against every plan I'd ever made, the road home.

"One condition," I said to Hale. "When we reach the junction—you testify. All of it. Names, orders, dates. Whatever it costs you."

He nodded once, no ceremony. "I was going to anyway. It's in the numbers."

We shouldered the packs and turned south together, five of us now, and behind us the storm moved in over the trestle like a door closing on a room we'd never see again. Downriver, somewhere in the dark, eight men in two boats were waiting for a delivery that was never coming.

By morning, the concession expired.
