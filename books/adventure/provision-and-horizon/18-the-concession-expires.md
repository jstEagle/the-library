## 18. The Concession Expires

The junction came up out of the fog the way old things do—gradually, and then all at once.

Junie saw it first from the bow of the lead boat: the water tower leaning like a tired man against a fence post, then the depot roof, then the long gray spine of the closed line running off into the trees toward nowhere in particular. She'd spent nineteen years thinking of places like this as the end of the world. Now it looked more like a front porch. Somebody's front porch, anyway. Silas's, probably, though he'd deny it with his whole chest if she said so out loud.

"Radio mast," Brandt said behind her, pointing. "Still standing."

"Still standing isn't still working," Junie said, but her hands were already checking the set in its oilcloth wrap, the way she'd checked it every hour since Sable Bend. The expedition's radio—smashed from the inside, Verrane said, by a man who'd rather be deaf than heard—had come apart under her soldering iron like a confession. She'd put it back together on the portage, in the snow, with numb fingers and one wire borrowed from a bootlace. It worked. That was the trouble. Working radios told you things.

The clock in her head said four minutes past noon. Corver's deadline had been noon. The concession expired at midnight. Somewhere between those two numbers, Silas Galloway was going to have to decide what a river was worth, and Junie had been doing arithmetic of her own for three days straight trying to guess which way he'd fall, and she kept getting two different answers depending on whether she looked at his hands or his eyes.

His hands said: sell it. His hands had been steady all week, the way they got when he was carrying something heavy and didn't want anyone to notice. A man who'd already lost everything once knew exactly how much everything cost.

His eyes said something else entirely.

* * *

They tied off at the depot dock, which was really just a suggestion of a dock, planks gone soft as bread. Hale was gone—gone with a boat and what he thought were the plates—and nobody talked about him much, the way you don't talk about a tooth that's coming out tomorrow. The rivermen had gone quiet after Sable Bend, the way rivermen do, keeping their grief ballasted low where it wouldn't roll the boat. That left Silas, Brandt, Verrane, and Junie herself, standing in the mud with a secret that could buy a valley or bury one.

Verrane sat down on a crate like a man whose legs had filed a formal complaint. He'd aged ten years on the portage. Frostbite had taken the tips of two fingers, and Brandt had wrapped them in gauze so neat it looked almost ceremonial.

"You should eat," Brandt said.

"I should do a great many things." Verrane looked at the depot. "Is that where you lived?"

"It's where I kept my books," Silas said.

"That's not the same thing?"

Silas didn't answer, which was an answer. Junie watched him go inside, and she watched the way he stopped in the doorway for half a second—just half a second, like a man pausing at the door of a house he'd been evicted from—and then went in and lit the stove because that was the kind of man he was. Cold outside, cold inside, so light the stove. Provision first. Everything else after.

She followed him in. So did the others, because apparently they were a family now, though nobody had signed anything.

* * *

The depot smelled like kerosene and old paper and, faintly, like the ghost of coffee made badly for many years. Junie knew the smell. She'd grown up around her uncle's freight office in Kettle Falls, and every dead rail line in the country smelled the same: like paperwork that outlived its purpose.

Silas set the map case on the desk. Then he set the plates beside it—the real ones, the ones that had ridden in Brandt's medical kit the whole way downriver while Hale ran off with Junie's careful fakes. She'd drawn those fakes herself, hunched under a tarp in the snow, copying Verrane's route onto blank survey sheets with deliberate little errors. A passage through the range that narrowed to nothing. A river that bent east where it actually bent west. She'd even aged the paper with tea. Her uncle would have been proud. Her uncle had also once said she'd end up in prison or government work, and she was starting to think he hadn't meant it as a compliment either way.

"So," she said. "Noon came and went."

"I noticed," Silas said.

"Corver's men are maybe an hour behind us. Maybe less. They know these lines better than we do."

"They don't know them better than I do."

"No," Junie agreed. "But they have guns, and you have a stove."

Brandt laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly swallowed. Even Verrane's mouth twitched. Silas gave Junie a look that had no heat in it at all, which from Silas was practically applause.

He opened the map case.

Junie had seen the closed-lines map before—caught him reading it by firelight back in the gorge, night three, and said nothing, and filed it away the way she filed everything. But seeing it flat on the desk in daylight was different. It was beautiful, honestly. Every decommissioned siding, every dead spur, every trestle marked with dates and load ratings in a cramped engineer's hand. Some of the annotations weren't Silas's. The older ink was finer, surer. His father's, she guessed. A whole family's knowledge of how to get somewhere the world had decided not to go.

And folded inside it, the page that mattered: the living route. The passage north through the range that Verrane's expedition had found and Hale's employers had paid to erase.

"Here's the shape of it," Silas said, and his voice was the voice he used for river reports—flat, complete, no drama wasted. "Corver wants the plates and our silence. In exchange, my debt clears, and everyone walks. That's the offer. That's the good version. The bad version is they take the plates anyway and we're a problem that needs solving."

"So we give him the decoys again," Brandt said. "Junie's fakes."

"He'll check them this time."

"Then we run."

"On what? One boat, winter ice, and a crew that hasn't slept—" Silas stopped himself. Rubbed his face. "I'm not saying no. I'm saying count first. Counting first is the only reason any of us are alive right now."

Junie leaned against the desk. "Count for me, then. What does selling it actually buy?"

"Everything," Silas said simply. "Corver keeps his word about debts—that much I'll swear to. Everyone goes home. Verrane gets his name back. You get your license without questions about your age. Brandt keeps practicing. I disappear the way I've been disappearing for six years, except clean this time. No one hunting us. No one burning anything." He looked at the map. "One valley stays secret, and one company keeps a monopoly it was going to lose anyway, and everybody lives."

"That's a very tidy speech," Junie said.

"I've had six years to practice tidy speeches."

"And the untidy version?"

Silas didn't answer. It was Verrane who answered, from the crate by the window, in a voice like something dragged a long way over gravel.

"The untidy version is that it doesn't matter what you sell or don't sell."

Everyone turned. Verrane had his bandaged hand resting on his knee, and he was looking at the stove, and when he spoke again it was slow, like a man reading his own confession off a telegraph tape.

"You should know what I've known since before we launched. Since before I recruited any of you." He took a breath. "Hale's employers commissioned my survey as a formality. A box to tick. The concession expires at midnight tonight—they've known the date for years, and they've known the river route exists for nearly as long. There were rumors. Old fur-trade journals. A missionary's letter in an archive in the capital. They sent me north not to find the passage but to *disprove* it. To produce a report saying the range is impassable, so the concession lapses quietly and the government grants them the dam rights instead."

"Dammed," Brandt said quietly. Not a question.

"Within a year," Verrane said. "Either way. Whether the route is public or buried. If the route is real, they dam it to kill it. If the route is fake, they dam it for power. The valley drowns either way, gentlemen. The only variable is whether my survey plates exist to prove there was ever something worth drowning." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and honest, finally, completely honest, the way men get when they've run out of lies and found the truth fits worse. "I let a man die protecting papers that change nothing. I smashed my own radio. I told myself it was for science. It was for a contract. And now you're sitting here weighing whether to trade that same nothing for your lives, and I want you to understand the ledger before you sign it. Corver's price buys your safety and buries a river that's going to be buried regardless. That's the deal. That's all the deal has ever been."

The stove ticked. Outside, the fog was lifting off the Ashwater, and the water itself had that particular late-autumn look, dark and slow and thinking about ice.

Junie found she was angry—not at Verrane, oddly, or not only. At the arithmetic. At the whole genre of arithmetic. Men with ledgers deciding what valleys were for.

"So it's worth nothing," she said. "The route."

"It's worth everything," Verrane said. "That's the horror of it. It's worth everything to everyone except the people holding the money. To them it's a liability with a deadline."

Silas stood very still. Junie had learned to read his stillnesses the way sailors read water. This one wasn't the stillness of a man deciding. It was the stillness of a man who had already decided and was waiting for the rest of himself to catch up.

"My father surveyed some of these closed lines," he said, to no one in particular. "Spurs into timber country that never turned a profit. Trestles to mines that played out. He used to say a line isn't dead just because the company stopped running it. It's only abandoned. Abandoned means somebody could pick it up again." He touched the edge of the map, the fine old ink. "I burned six years hiding behind that idea. Told myself the lines would matter again someday. Told myself keeping the map was stewardship." A short breath that might have been a laugh. "It was a security blanket with coordinates."

"Silas," Brandt started gently.

"Corver will be here within the hour," Silas said. "So let's use the hour."

* * *

What they did with the hour was argue, warmly, the way families argue—which is to say everyone talked over everyone and somehow all the important things got said twice.

Brandt wanted to negotiate. Meet Corver at the dock, present the decoys openly, dare him to call the bluff in front of witnesses. "Men like that operate in the dark," she said. "Light is free. We should use more of it."

Verrane wanted to surrender the real plates and keep only copies, a scholar's compromise, saving the data while sacrificing the original. Junie pointed out that Corver couldn't tell an original from a copy, which meant neither could history, which meant the compromise saved nothing but Verrane's conscience, and his conscience had done enough damage for one season.

Junie herself argued for the radio. Broadcast the route. Put it on every frequency, plain language, before Corver's men cut the mast. Make it public property; you can't bury what the whole province has already copied down. It was a good plan and she knew it was a good plan and it still made her stomach hurt, because broadcasting meant admitting she'd been listening for days, meant handing the tribunal a whole new list of questions, meant—she realized, hearing her own voice pitch faster—meant becoming the kind of person whose story ended up in newspapers, and she'd joined this expedition specifically to avoid being perceived.

"You'd be doing it to save a valley," Brandt said. "People forgive that."

"People *print* that," Junie said. "Forgiveness is optional."

It was Silas who finally held up a hand, and the room went quiet the way it always did, not because he demanded it but because everyone had privately agreed he'd earned it.

"Here's my count," he said. "If we broadcast, Corver's men are standing right here when it happens, and Brandt is correct that light helps, but I've watched men like Corver work in daylight too. They don't need darkness. They need deniability, and a radio tower falling over in a storm is very deniable." He looked at each of them in turn. "If we fight, someone dies, and we've paid enough. If we run, the ice runs us instead. If we sell—" he laid his palm flat on the closed-lines map, gently, like touching a sleeping animal, "—we live, and the valley dies on schedule, and I spend whatever years I've got left knowing I priced it correctly."

"And if we burn it?" Junie asked.

The question landed in the middle of the room and sat there.

"Burn what?" Brandt asked carefully. "The plates? The map?"

"All of it," Junie said. She hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way true things do, without permission. "All of it. The plates, the map, the decoys, everything. Corver shows up and finds ash. No route to buy, no evidence to steal, no concession to defend. His employers' problem solves itself at midnight when the concession lapses, and their dam rights are just—dam rights. Ordinary greed. Nothing to burn a valley over, because there's no proof there was ever anything special here." She spread her hands. "Verrane said it himself. The valley drowns either way. The only thing the papers decide is whether men like Corver get to pretend otherwise. Take the papers away and all that's left is the truth, and the truth doesn't fit in a safe."

"It also doesn't save the valley," Verrane said hoarsely.

"Nothing saves the valley," Junie said. "You told us. But there's a difference between losing a thing and having it stolen, and I'm not giving Corver the receipt."

Silas was looking at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere. Something between grief and relief, like a man watching a bridge he'd been afraid to cross finally being tested by someone lighter than him.

"The stove's hot," he said at last.

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Brandt nodded, slowly, the way doctors nod when a treatment is terrible and correct. Verrane closed his eyes and let out a breath six weeks long.

Outside, far down the dead line, there came a sound none of them needed a radio to identify: engines. More than one. Coming on at a pace that said the deadline had been a courtesy and courtesies were over.

Junie looked at Silas. Silas looked at the stove.

"Well," he said, picking up the map case in one hand and the survey plates in the other, holding six years of hiding and one broken man's life's work together in the same grip, "they're early. Someone help me feed the fire."

He carried both toward the stove—and stopped, because through the depot's grimy window, at the tree line where the old spur met the river road, the first of Corver's men stepped out of the fog with a rifle slung easy across his back, and behind him came more, and behind them, unmistakable even at distance, came the broad gray shape of Corver himself, walking like a man arriving to collect on a note that had finally come due.
