## 20. The Hearing

The tribunal was held in the freight office at Corver Junction because it was the only room in the district with enough chairs and no opinion of its own. Brandt arrived early, as he always did for anything that frightened him, and found the benches already filling: rivermen in their good coats, a scattering of company men who had come to watch the ledger balance itself, and half the valley's homesteaders, who had come because when something happens on the Ashwater, the valley comes to look at it.

He counted chairs out of habit. Doctor's habit — you learn how many people a room can hold before it becomes a waiting room.

The three commissioners sat behind a table draped in green felt that had been green sometime during the previous administration. The middle one, a Mr. Aldous from the Territorial Water Board, had the particular stillness of a man who had read every document twice and believed none of them. To his left sat a woman named Kettering, who represented the families of the lost expedition, and who had shaken Brandt's hand that morning with a grip like a mooring line. To his right, taking notes in a hand so small it looked like weather, was the board's own counsel.

On the wall behind them hung a map of the Ashwater drainage. Someone had drawn a red X where Sable Bend was. It was not to scale. It was, Brandt thought, exactly as accurate as everything else about this proceeding would be.

Junie Okafor sat two rows back with her hands folded, wearing a dress she clearly owned under protest. She caught his eye and mouthed something. He decided it was *you'll be fine* rather than what it actually looked like, which was *I hid the radio parts under the floorboards.*

Silas Galloway came in last, alone, and sat where there was room, which happened to be beside Junie. Neither of them acknowledged the other. It was the most fluent conversation Brandt had ever watched two people have without speaking.

* * *

They began, as such things do, with the dead.

Aldous read the names into the record: Tomas Verrane's surveyor, Pell; the two expedition members never recovered above the high country; and then, last, because the alphabet is indifferent to grief, Rennick Cole, riverman, drowned at Sable Bend on the night of November the ninth.

Rennick's mother sat in the third row. She was a small woman with a face like a closed door, and she did not cry, and Brandt understood that she had done her crying somewhere private and brought only the finished product here. When her son's name was read, she looked straight ahead at the map, at the red X, as if daring it to mean something.

Brandt had pulled Rennick out of the water himself. He had worked on him for eleven minutes in the bottom of a boat while ice formed on the gunwales, and he could tell the tribunal, truthfully, that nothing anyone had done or failed to do in those eleven minutes had mattered. But tribunals are not built for truths like that. Tribunals want causes. Causes can be assigned.

"Dr. Brandt." Aldous looked up. "You were the medical officer on the resupply mission. You will describe the circumstances of Mr. Cole's death."

Brandt stood. His knees announced themselves, as they had since he was forty.

"I'll describe what I saw," he said. "I wasn't in a position to see much else."

"We'd prefer the whole of it."

"The whole of it," Brandt said, "is that we ran a channel at night in freezing water because the alternative was being frozen in until spring with a dying man's frostbite and no fuel. The channel narrowed. The boat hit an ice shelf. Rennick went over the side. I put my hands in the water after him and the water declined my offer." He paused. "That's the whole of it. Rivers don't take blame, gentlemen. They just take."

There was a sound from the benches — not quite a laugh, more the exhale of people who had been holding something. Kettering wrote one word in her notebook.

"You're saying no one is responsible."

"I'm saying responsibility is a thing people carry, not a thing rivers issue. If you want to know who carried it, ask the man at the tiller. He's been carrying it since before any of us met him."

Aldous's eyes flicked down the table toward Silas, and Brandt felt the room tilt its attention the way water tilts toward a cut bank.

* * *

They called Silas Galloway in the afternoon, after the company's man — a pale, precise person named Wexler who spoke as though reading from a document even when he wasn't — had spent two hours establishing that the resupply mission had been authorized, insured, and conducted by persons whose employment status insulated certain other persons from consequence.

Brandt listened to Wexler the way you listen to a heart murmur: noting the rhythm, waiting for the skip.

Then Silas took the stand.

He stood the way he did everything — economically, as if standing were a task with a correct method. He had shaved. He wore a coat that had been brushed but not pressed, and Brandt recognized it as the coat Hale had handed over at the trestle, and wondered whether Silas knew he was wearing a dead man's arithmetic, or whether he knew exactly.

"Mr. Galloway," Aldous said. "You hold — held — an engineer's license, suspended eleven years ago following the Meridian Line collapse."

"Suspended," Silas said. "Yes."

"For cause."

"For cause."

"You understand the board may consider revocation permanent."

"I've understood that for eleven years. It's why I moved somewhere with no trains."

Someone in the back made a noise that might have been sympathy or might have been a cough. Brandt decided to believe it was sympathy.

"Describe your role in the mission."

"I piloted the boats. I chose the routes. On the night of the ninth, I chose to run Sable Bend in the dark instead of laying over."

"Why?"

Silas was quiet for a moment. Brandt had learned, on the river, to read the lengths of Silas's silences the way sailors read water — short silence meant he was choosing words, long silence meant he was choosing whether to say the true thing. This was a long one.

"Because the river was closing," he said finally. "Every hour cost us margin we didn't have. Because a man on my boat had blackening toes and needed a hospital, not a snowbank. And because I believed — wrongly — that I knew that channel well enough to buy us the time." He looked at Rennick's mother. "I was wrong. Rennick Cole died because I made that call. Not the river. Not the ice. Me."

"That's very tidy," Wexler said, rising. "Almost rehearsed. Isn't it true, Mr. Galloway, that you were under pressure from your employer—"

"I wasn't employed. I was spent. There's a difference, and the contract makes it clear."

"—that the decision to run the bend at night was influenced by the presence aboard of Mr. Hale, acting for the concession holders, who had—"

"Hale sabotaged our fuel," Silas said. Flatly, without heat, the way you report a gauge reading. "He wanted us on the winter portage so his people could intercept the survey plates. I didn't know it then. I know it now. It doesn't change my call. I'd make the same call again tomorrow, and Rennick would still be dead, and I'd still be the reason."

The room went very still. Brandt watched Aldous set down his pen — actually set it down, deliberately, the gesture of a man clearing his desk for something heavier than paperwork.

"You're confessing to negligence."

"I'm confessing to being the engineer. That's what the word means. When the bridge holds, everyone shares the credit. When it falls, it's yours alone. I've been living on the wrong side of that equation for eleven years. I came back to the right side." Silas's voice didn't rise. It never rose. "Take the license. It stopped meaning anything a long time ago anyway. But write down whose call it was, because the Cole family deserves a name attached to their grief, and 'the river' isn't a name."

Rennick's mother stood up.

She didn't ask permission. She simply stood, small and upright in the third row, and the whole room turned to her like iron filings to a magnet.

"My boy signed on with Galloway because the dockmen said he was the only pilot who'd never lost a boat on the Ashwater," she said. Her voice was dry and level, a voice that had been wrung out long ago. "They said he was careful past the point of stubbornness. Well. My Rennick was careless past the point of foolishness, and I told him so every Sunday for twenty-six years." She looked at Silas. "You want to carry it all. That's pride, Mr. Galloway, dressed up as penance. My son went over that rail because he was leaning out to clear ice off a line, same as he leaned out of everything his whole life. You can hold the blame if you need it. But don't you dare hold all of it, because some of it belongs to him, and I won't have him erased from his own death."

She sat down. The silence afterward had texture, like snowfall.

Aldous cleared his throat twice before it worked.

* * *

They corroborated each other the way stones corroborate a wall.

Junie Okafor testified next, nineteen years old and lying about her age with the serene confidence of someone who had already lied her way onto a riverboat and figured the tribunal was a smaller body of water. She described the smashed radio, the intercepts, the sabotage of the fuel reserve — producing, to Wexler's visible horror, a fragment of the tampered valve assembly wrapped in a handkerchief like a saint's relic.

"I documented everything," she said. "Dates, times, frequencies. I keep records. It's the whole job."

"You intercepted communications not addressed to you," Wexler said.

"Yes."

"You understand that's—"

"It's the only reason anybody on that river is alive," Junie said, "including, I'd note, several people whose companies are paying your fees. So yes, I understand what it is. I'd do it again before you finish the sentence."

Kettering, of the families, made a small mark in her notebook. Brandt suspected it was a good mark.

Verrane testified from a chair, his frostbitten hands bandaged to the fingertips, and gave the tribunal the thing nobody had asked for: the truth about the passage through the range, the navigable route that would have emptied the concession's monopoly into the sea. He gave it plainly, brokenly, a man setting down a weight he'd carried up a mountain. Wexler objected four times. Aldous overruled him four times, and the fourth time he said, "Sit down, Mr. Wexler," in a tone that suggested the fifth objection would be entered into the record as a confession.

Brandt himself was recalled to speak to Verrane's condition, and found himself, to his own surprise, telling the story of the grave in the high country — how they had buried Pell, how Verrane had stood at the head of it and said the man had died of a fall but also of being led, and how Brandt had written in his log that night: *cause of death, gravity; contributing factor, ambition.* Aldous asked him to repeat the phrase for the record. He did, slowly, so the small-handed counsel could get it all down.

Hale's absence was noted, discussed, and filed. A boat found adrift. No body recovered. The company expressed regret. The company, Brandt observed, expressed regret the way a stove expresses heat — constantly, mechanically, and only when fed.

* * *

The deliberation took one day, which everyone agreed was either efficiency or an insult, depending on which bench you occupied.

On the second morning, Aldous read the findings aloud in a voice sanded flat by thirty years of findings. The loss of the expedition: attributed to terrain, weather, and decisions of the original party, no action against surviving members. The death of Rennick Cole: attributed to navigation under conditions of extreme hazard, decision of the pilot, Silas Galloway.

"And regarding Mr. Galloway's license," Aldous said, "this board finds a pattern going back eleven years that cannot be ignored. The suspension stands. Revocation is permanent. Mr. Galloway will not pilot, engineer, or certify on any licensed line or waterway in this territory."

Brandt watched Silas receive it the way he'd watched him take a wave broadside — braced, unmoved, already calculating what came next.

"However," Aldous continued, and here he paused, and looked over his glasses at the room, "this board notes for the record the testimony of Mrs. Cole, and concurs with its substance. Responsibility is apportioned, not transferred entire. This board further notes that the same knowledge and judgment which produced the tragedy at Sable Bend also produced the survival of nine persons through conditions this board's own hydrologist describes, quote, 'as statistically unsurvivable.' We revoke the license. We do not revoke the man. Mr. Galloway's name stands cleared of fraud, of concealment, and of the charges brought by the concession holders, which this board dismisses with prejudice and, frankly, distaste."

He set down the paper.

"This hearing is concluded. The Ashwater remains open to all parties." A beat. "All parties," he repeated, in a way that made several company men suddenly find their boots fascinating.

* * *

Afterward, the freight office emptied slowly, the way a church does. Rennick's mother stopped in front of Silas on her way out. Brandt, gathering his bag, pretended not to listen and listened completely.

"He was leaning out to clear the ice," she said. "Wasn't he."

"He was," Silas said. "It was working, too. He'd nearly got it."

"Of course he had." She nodded once, sharply, sealing something. "Thank you for not lying to me in there. Everyone else lies so kindly. It's a relief to be talked to like I can stand up."

She left. Silas stood there a moment with his hands in Hale's coat pockets, looking like a man who had set down a hundred pounds and discovered his arms remembered how to swing.

Junie appeared at his elbow. "So," she said. "No license."

"No license."

"They know you can't stay off the river, right? The whole valley knows. Half of them were taking notes."

"I imagine," Silas said, "the valley will manage to find work for an unlicensed man with a boat. The valley has never cared much for paperwork."

"The valley," Junie said, "has excellent taste and terrible filing systems." She grinned, and it was the first grin Brandt had seen on her since Sable Bend, and it did something to the room's remaining air that all the testimony hadn't managed.

Brandt slung his bag and joined them. Outside, the light had gone long and gold, and the river below the junction was doing what it always did — moving, indifferent, carrying whatever was given to it toward the sea.

"Well, Doctor," Silas said. "Eleven minutes of chest compressions and eleven years of penance, and the board gives me a piece of paper saying what I already knew. How's that for medicine?"

"Ineffective dosage," Brandt said. "But the prognosis—" He looked at Junie, at the river, at the thin smoke rising from the depot stove where, though none of them said it, a certain page of ash had settled a debt. "The prognosis is better than the chart suggests."

They walked down toward the water together, three survivors of a transaction that had become something else, and behind them the freight office stood empty, its chairs stacked, its green felt table bare — having held, for two days, the closest thing to a family any of them had sworn to.

At the dock, a supply boat waited with spring painted all over its intentions, and Junie Okafor was already untying the line.
