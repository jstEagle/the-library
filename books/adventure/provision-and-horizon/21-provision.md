## 21. Provision

The ice went out of the Ashwater on the fourteenth of April, with a sound like a door being opened in a house everyone had thought was empty.

Junie Okafor was on the depot porch when it happened, a mug of coffee going cold in her hands, and she heard the river groan once, deep down in its chest, and then the whole gray sheet of it shifted and cracked and began to move. She'd been listening for that sound since February. Everyone in the valley had. But hearing it was different from waiting for it, the way reading a letter was different from being handed one.

She ran down to the bank with her coffee slopping over her wrist and stood there grinning like a fool while the river shrugged off winter, ice plates tilting and sliding and grinding themselves into slush, the water underneath running brown and fast and gloriously, unmistakably alive.

Behind her, the depot door banged open. "Well?" said Brandt.

"It's going."

"Thank God." He came out onto the porch in his shirtsleeves, which by valley standards in April was practically nudity, and stood beside her. "I've had three patients this month ask me if the river would ever open. As if I'd know. As if I could prescribe it."

"You could write it on a little slip of paper. One dose, open water, take as needed."

"I have considered it." He sipped from his own mug. "Silas is already down at the boathouse. He's been down there since five. I heard him moving around and decided not to ask, because some kinds of happiness you shouldn't interrupt."

Junie looked downriver toward the old boathouse, its windows lit yellow against the blue morning, and felt the thing in her chest that she had stopped being embarrassed about sometime around January — the feeling of being exactly where she was supposed to be, which was a feeling she had never once had before the winter she'd spent in this valley.

Nineteen years old when she'd lied her way onto that resupply boat. Twenty now, and licensed, and the holder of a folded page that lived inside her coat lining like a second heartbeat.

The river ran on, breaking itself apart, going wherever rivers go in the spring.

* * *

The license had come in February, in an envelope with a government stamp on it, and Junie had carried it around unopened for two days because she was afraid of what it might say. When she finally opened it — sitting on her bunk in the depot's back room, with the stove ticking and Silas pretending to read a catalog across from her — it said she was licensed to operate powered vessels on inland waterways, Class Two, and that her name was spelled correctly, which was more than the last three official documents concerning Junie Okafor had managed.

Silas had looked at it for a long time.

"Class Two," he said.

"Class Two."

"That's a real license. That's the kind you frame."

"I'm going to frame it. I'm going to put it right over the radio."

"You should put it over the radio." He handed it back with a care that struck her as funny and touching at once, the way he handled anything official, as if it might go off. "Congratulations, Operator Okafor."

"Thank you, Mr. Galloway."

"Don't call me that. It makes me feel like I owe you money."

"You do owe me money. You owe me for the winter's worth of coffee you've drunk out of my tin."

"That was communal coffee."

"It was *my* tin."

He'd smiled then — the real one, the rare one, the one that changed his whole face from something shut and weathered into something almost young — and Junie had understood, not for the first time, that she would have walked back up that river in winter, plates and all, just to keep this.

The hearing had gone the way hearings go, which is to say badly for the truth and tolerably for the people in it. Silas had stood up in front of the tribunal in his one good coat and said, in that flat careful voice he used for facts, that the loss of the boat at Sable Bend was his decision and his alone, and Junie had watched him take the blame like a man paying a bill he'd been expecting. What he hadn't said — what none of them had let him say alone — was that every one of them had voted to run the channel, and that the vote had been unanimous, and that unanimity meant the blame was a communal coffee too.

The tribunal had taken his license. They had not taken his name, which was what Junie had been afraid of, back when she'd understood what the hearing might actually cost. He walked out of that room with no papers and no standing and the whole valley watching him, and within a month the whole valley was bringing him their boats.

It started small. A fisherman with an engine that wouldn't idle. A widow whose supply scow needed caulking. Then the Kettle Creek people, whose crossing was a wreck of a thing, asked him — not the licensed man two towns over, not the company agent, but Silas, unlicensed Silas — whether the spring crossing would hold, and when he said it would, they crossed, and it held, and that was that. You could take a man's license away. You could not take away the fact that he had run a narrowing channel at night, in first ice, and brought four of five home, and that the one he lost he had gone into the water for, hands out, until his arms stopped working.

The valley did its own arithmetic. The valley had never needed a tribunal to know what a man was.

* * *

By May the provision run was a going concern, which was a phrase Silas used with deep suspicion, as if the concern might turn on him.

Junie ran the boats. That was the arrangement, and it had been formalized in a conversation that had lasted approximately eleven seconds on the depot porch in March:

"I can't pilot," Silas had said. "You know that."

"I know that."

"But I can load a boat, read water, fix an engine, and tell you where the river's lying to you. And you can pilot. And you're licensed, so if the inspector comes, you're the captain and I'm crew."

"Am I the captain?"

"You're the captain."

"Say it again."

"You're insufferable is what you are."

"Captain Junie Okafor," she'd said, to the river, to the sky, to the whole cold spring morning. "It has a ring."

It did have a ring. She'd painted it on the first boat, small, on the stern, where only the fish could read it: *J. OKAFOR, PROVISION & PASSAGE.* Silas had looked at the name for a long moment and said nothing, but she'd seen his thumb move over the letters once, the way he touched things he was memorizing.

The route ran from the junction downriver to the settlements along the lower Ashwater — flour and salt and lamp oil going out, furs and letters and news coming back — twice a month when the water allowed, which in spring was nearly always, and in summer was usually, and in winter was a matter Junie preferred not to think about until she had to.

The crew, such as it was, assembled itself the way the best things do: without anyone deciding.

Brandt kept his doctoring, but he rode the boat on the long runs because, as he put it, "half the settlements downriver haven't seen a doctor since the war, and the other half have seen one they didn't like." He had a way with the downriver people that Junie envied — a patience, a gentleness that never curdled into pity. He'd sit on a porch with an old man's ruined knee between his hands and explain it like the knee was a colleague he was consulting with, and the old man would walk away straighter and stand taller, and Junie had watched Silas observe this more than once with an expression she could only describe as *taking notes.*

Verrane taught surveying.

That one had surprised everyone, including Verrane. He'd come back from the hearing a smaller man than the one who'd refused to leave the maintenance shelter without his plates — humbled, hollowed, honest in the way of men who have been broken open and found the crack lets light in both directions. The company had washed him out quietly; the concession was dead; his name was a footnote in a file somewhere in a city he never spoke of. He'd drifted up to the junction in June of the first summer with a single case of instruments and nowhere to be, and Silas had looked at him across the depot office and said, "There's a bunk in the back. It's not warm."

"I've slept colder," Verrane said.

"You'll sleep colder here."

And that had been that. By autumn he was teaching the mill boys and the Kettle Creek kids how to run a level line and read a contour, out on the flats with stakes and string, his voice carrying in the cold air — *no, again, again, the river doesn't forgive a lazy measurement and neither do I* — and the strange thing, Junie thought, watching him from the boat one October afternoon, was that he'd gotten *good* at it. Not the teaching. The caring. The man who had once guarded survey plates like a prisoner of his own discovery now gave the knowledge away freely, to anyone who'd stand in the cold and listen, and seemed to grow younger by the lesson.

"They should have let him do this from the start," she'd said to Silas.

"Companies don't hire men to care," Silas said. "They hire men to count. He was a good counter. Turns out he's a better teacher." He'd paused. "Turns out most people are better at almost anything than what they were hired for."

* * *

The second provision run of the season went out on a bright hard morning in late May, the river high and brown and full of snowmelt opinion, and Junie stood at the wheel of the *Okafor* — the second boat, the bigger one, bought over the winter with money that had mostly come from the valley itself, subscribed in sums of two dollars and five dollars and one memorable twenty from a widow who said Silas Galloway had pulled her husband's scow off a snag in '09 and it was past time somebody paid it forward — and felt the old familiar lift in her chest when the engine took and the bank began to slide past.

Silas was forward, checking the lashings on the flour sacks for the fourth time. Brandt was amidships with his case and a book he wasn't reading. And in the stern, uninvited, unpaid, and utterly immovable, sat a passenger: a Kettle Creek girl of maybe fourteen named Ada Soren, who had shown up at the dock at dawn with a bundle and an announcement.

"I'm going downriver to my aunt's," she'd said. "I paid."

"You paid," Silas had said, looking at the coins in his palm, "a fare that would have been reasonable in 1904."

"Is that a no?"

"That's a *yes*," Junie had called from the wheel, "and don't let him bully you, he does it to everyone."

Now Ada sat wrapped in a blanket, watching the banks go by with the particular hunger of a person seeing her own river from the middle of it for the first time, and Junie caught Silas glancing back at her every few minutes, not checking on her exactly — Silas didn't check on people, he *tracked* them, there was a difference — and she thought: there it is. There's the family, doing what it does. Gathering strays without a by-your-leave.

The morning went easy. The river was high but honest, running straight in the channel it had cut, and Junie took the bends wide and the current on the hip the way Silas had taught her — *the river's always trying to throw you to the outside of the turn, so you lean into the argument, not away from it* — and by noon they were through the Narrows and into the long slow water above Miller's Landing, and Brandt had given up pretending to read and was telling Ada about the time he'd stitched up a riverman using fishing line and a sailmaker's needle, and the girl was listening with her mouth open, and Silas was dozing against the flour sacks with his hat over his face, and Junie thought: *this. This is what I lied about my age for. Not the adventure. This.*

She'd told him once, in the winter, in the depot with the stove ticking. Told him the truth she'd never told the hiring men — that she hadn't come upriver for the wage, or the war-surplus romance of it, or even to get away from her uncle's house, though all of those were true. She'd come because she'd heard the expedition's radio operator had died of fever and they needed a replacement, and she'd thought: *someone has to be the voice on that river. Someone has to be the one who hears.*

"And now you're the one who's heard everything," Silas had said.

"And now I'm the one who's heard everything."

"Does it sit heavy?"

She'd thought about it honestly, because he was a man you thought honestly for. "Some of it. The drowned man's name sits heavy. But most of it—" she'd touched her coat lining, where the folded page lived, "—most of it sits *warm.* Like a coal you carry in your pocket. You have to keep moving so it doesn't burn through, but you wouldn't give it up."

Silas had nodded slowly, and she'd known he understood, because he carried a coal of his own — several, actually: a drowned riverman, a burned map, a debt paid in ash. But his pockets had gotten lighter all winter. She'd watched it happen.

* * *

Miller's Landing took its flour, took its lamp oil, took Brandt for two days of doctoring, and gave back a crate of hens, a sack of mail, and news, which traveled upriver faster than any boat.

The news was this: there were men in the lower towns asking questions. Not company men — the company was finished, its concession expired, its name a joke now in three counties — but *other* men. Well-dressed, well-funded, polite in the way of people reading from a script. Asking about the Ashwater. About the upper gorge. About whether anyone local knew the old survey routes through the range.

Junie heard it first from the Miller's Landing postmaster, who told Brandt, who told her and Silas that evening on the dock with the hens complaining in their crate between them.

"Developers?" Brandt guessed.

"Speculators," Silas said. He'd gone very still, the way he did, the stillness of a man reading fine print. "Same animal, different collar. The concession's dead, so they think the valley's unclaimed. They think nobody knows the water."

"Do they know about—" Junie didn't finish it. She didn't have to. *The passage. The living line. The thing we burned the map to keep.*

"They know something's up there," Silas said. "They don't know what. And they can't find out from paper, because there isn't any paper anymore." His eyes came up and met hers, and there was something in them she hadn't seen in months — not fear exactly, but its cousin. "There isn't any paper," he said again, "except one page."

The dock boards creaked. Downriver the water ran high and brown and full of spring, going somewhere it had always gone, through a country it had always watered, and Junie put her hand flat against her coat, against the folded page that lived there, and felt the coal sit warm.

"They'll come upriver eventually," she said. "People always do. The river's too good to stay secret forever."

"Everything's too good to stay secret forever," Silas said. "That's not the question." He looked down at the water a long moment, and when he spoke again his voice had the flat careful tone he used for facts — but underneath it, faint as bedrock under topsoil, was something else. Something that sounded almost like hope.

"The question is who tells it, and how, and whether the valley gets a say. And that—" he picked up his hat, settled it, and Junie saw the ghost of the real smile cross his face, "—that's a conversation for the first open-water run. All hands. Verrane too. Because if men are coming to look at *our* river, then it's past time somebody showed them what it's worth — and I know exactly who's been carrying the only honest map."

Junie's hand tightened against her coat lining.

The page. The route. The thing he'd folded into her hand in the firelight a year and a lifetime ago, because she was the one who'd use it for people and not permits.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "First light."

"First light," Silas agreed, and turned to see to the hens, and the river ran on below them, high and brown and going somewhere, and Junie Okafor — licensed, twenty, captain of her own boat and keeper of the only map in the world — stood on the dock of Miller's Landing and felt the horizon open like ice going out.
