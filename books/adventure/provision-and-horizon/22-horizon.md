## 22. Horizon

**Silas**

The ice went out of the Ashwater on a Tuesday, with a sound like a door being opened very slowly in a very large house.

I was at the depot when it happened, which is where I spent most of my time now, though nobody paid me to be there and nobody had asked me to leave. The river had been groaning for three days, working at its seams, and then sometime before dawn the whole sheet let go at once and moved downstream in plates the size of barn roofs, grinding and tilting, and I stood on the platform with a cup of coffee going cold in my hands and watched a winter end.

A year ago I would have told you that a man could not come back from where I'd been. Not the disgrace — disgrace, as it turns out, is survivable; there are men at every junction between here and the coast who've lost a license, a fortune, or a wife, and they all still eat breakfast. No, what I'd have said you couldn't come back from was the arithmetic. The kind of bookkeeping where people go in one column and debt goes in the other, and you learn to keep them separate so you can sleep. I had kept those books for eleven years. Corver taught me how. I'd gotten good enough at it that I nearly taught it to myself again on the river last winter, standing over the hole where Tom Ellery went down, doing the cold math of one boat against six lives.

The difference was Junie. And Brandt. And Verrane, God help him. And even Hale, in the end, handing over his coat at the trestle like a man paying off a note he hadn't known he'd signed.

The family I never meant to gather. I'd have laughed at the phrase a year ago. Now I just kept the stove lit and waited for them to show up, which they did, irregularly and without apology, the way family does.

Junie arrived first, as she always did, an hour before she said she would.

* * *

**Junie**

The thing about Silas Galloway that nobody who'd only read about the hearing would ever understand was that he fussed.

The tribunal had made him sound like granite. Plain truth, plain speech, a man taking blame like a load of gravel dumped square on his back. The newspapers upriver ran two paragraphs on him under the headline ENGINEER ACCEPTS RESPONSIBILITY, and both paragraphs made him sound like a monument. What the papers didn't mention was that the same man, on the morning of the first open-water run of the new season, had re-tied the same cleat knot four times because he "didn't like the look of it," and had asked me twice whether I'd checked the fuel line, and had stood at the rail of the *Provision* holding his father's compass and staring north like the needle owed him money.

"You checked the fuel line," I told him, coming up the dock with the last crate. "You watched me check the fuel line."

"I watched you say you checked it."

"I checked it while saying it. It's called multitasking. Brandt taught me."

That got the ghost of a smile out of him, which was the most I could usually hope for before ten o'clock. He took the crate from me — he always took the crates, no matter how many times I told him my arms worked fine — and stowed it amidships with the particular care he gave to everything that wasn't himself.

The *Provision* sat low in the water with her first real cargo: flour and lamp oil for the valley settlements, seed potatoes for Brandt's clinic garden, a crate of books Verrane had ordered for the surveying class he ran out of the schoolhouse on Thursdays. Ordinary freight. The kind of run I'd been making since the ice left, and would keep making until the leaves came, and after that too, because somebody had to, and because it turned out that "somebody has to" was the whole secret of a life. Nobody tells you that at nineteen. You think a life is supposed to be a discovery. Mostly it's a delivery schedule.

But there was one item aboard that wasn't flour or potatoes or books.

It rode in the inside pocket of my coat, where it had ridden since the night in the depot when Silas fed his closed-lines map into the stove page by page and kept exactly one sheet back. The passage route. The living line through the range that Verrane's expedition had found and Hale's employers had meant to bury — the water road that would have made their expiring concession worthless, and that they would have dammed and drowned within the year either way, broken valley or honest one. One page, folded small, soft as cloth now from a winter of carrying.

All through the hearing I'd kept it. All through the spring floods. I'd taken it out maybe six times, always alone, always at night, looking at the penciled contours and Verrane's careful annotations and the little notation in Silas's hand at the margin — *depths unverified below the narrows* — and each time I put it away again thinking: not yet. Because it wasn't mine to open. It had never been mine. Silas had given it to me, but giving isn't the same as spending, and some things you hold the way you hold a struck match.

Today, I decided, somewhere past the second bend, with the gorge walls going gold in the morning light and the water running high and clean and loud — today I'd ask him.

* * *

**Silas**

She asked me at the narrows, which figured. Junie never chose easy ground for anything.

We'd tied off at the old mooring ledge above the white water to let the worst of the spring surge drop — standard practice, the kind of thing I'd have done by reflex in any year — and while we waited she climbed up onto the gunwale beside me, unwrapped her sandwich, ate half of it, and then said, with her mouth full, in the tone she used for radio checks:

"So. What do you want to do with it?"

I didn't have to ask what she meant. I'd known this conversation was coming since November, the way you know a storm front is coming off the lake — not the hour of it, but the fact.

"The page," she said, when I didn't answer fast enough. She finished the other half of the sandwich and brushed the crumbs from her knees. "You gave it to me. I've carried it all winter. And I keep thinking — it's not really a keepsake, is it? It's a route. Routes want to be used." She looked out at the water. "So I'm asking. What do you want done with it?"

Here is what I had expected to feel, in the year I'd imagined this moment: the old pull. The ledger instinct. The voice that says a navigable passage through the range is worth money, worth a pardon, worth a new license with a stamp on it, worth trading to Corver or the company or the government or whoever bids highest. That voice had been my constant companion for eleven years and it knew all my pockets.

What I actually felt was tired of it. That was all. A clean, settled tiredness, like a knot finally worked loose.

"Verrane told me something at the junction," I said. "The night before the hearing. He'd had time to think, up in that shelter, guarding his own discovery like a prisoner. He said the valley gets dammed within ten years no matter what anybody does. Growth comes up the coast, power demand follows, and some engineer sits down with these same contour lines and finds the same narrows." I nodded at the water, running white and indifferent below us. "The only question is whether it happens to a valley full of strangers or a valley full of neighbors."

"So the route's worth more as a map than as a secret," Junie said.

"It's worth more as a *community's* map than as a *company's* secret. There's a difference." I turned the compass over in my hands. My father's brass compass, dented, the needle slow to settle, the kind of instrument you keep not because it's good but because it's yours. "Corver offered me my freedom for it once. Did I ever tell you that?"

"Twice. Three times if you count the version where you were drunk on Brandt's elderberry wine."

"Four times, counting the true version." I almost smiled. "He thought he understood the price of things. Most men who keep ledgers do. But he never once asked what the valley wanted. That was the flaw in all his arithmetic. He priced the route and forgot the people already living along it."

Junie was quiet a moment. Then she reached into her coat and brought out the folded page, and held it between us, and the wind off the water tried to take it and she held on.

"Then give it to the valley," she said. "Not sell it. Not trade it. Give it. Copy it out a hundred times if you have to. Nail it to the schoolhouse door if that's what it takes. Let Verrane teach it, let the rivermen run it, let Brandt's patients argue about it in the waiting room. Make it so common that no company can ever buy it back." She looked at me, and there was nothing nineteen about her eyes just then. "Make it worthless, Silas. Worthless to them. Priceless to everybody else."

And there it was. The answer I'd been carrying toward all winter without knowing the road.

"Yes," I said. "That. Exactly that."

She unfolded the page carefully against her knee, smoothing the creases, and we looked at it together — the passage through the range, the living line, drawn in pencil and ink by dead men and frightened ones, annotated by a surveyor who'd learned honesty the hard way. The river ran through it like a sentence finally finished.

"My father used to say a compass doesn't point anywhere," I said. "People think it points north. It doesn't. It just settles. Everything else — the reading, the bearing, the course — that's the person holding it." I opened the case. The needle swung, wobbled, fought the wind and the iron ore in the gorge walls the way it always did.

And then, as if the valley itself had been waiting for the word, it steadied.

North. Straight and quiet and sure, pointing up the Ashwater, into the gorge, along the route on the page, toward everything we hadn't seen yet.

"Huh," said Junie softly.

"What?"

"Nothing. It's just — I've watched that needle fight you for two years. Thought it was broken." She folded the page again, deliberately, and tucked it back inside her coat — but differently now, loosely, like something ready to be handed over rather than hidden. "Turns out it was just waiting for you to make up your mind."

I looked at her. She grinned at me, nineteen again, insufferable, indispensable.

"Well?" she said. "The surge's dropping. We've got flour to deliver and a valley to inform." She stood, balanced easily on the gunwale, and put out her hand to haul me up. "Come on, old man. The horizon's not getting any closer sitting here."

I took her hand, and let her pull me to my feet, and cast off the line I'd tied four times, because today I liked the look of it fine.

The *Provision* swung out into the current, and the current, as it always does, knew the way.
