## 1. The Wreck They Deny

The navy says there's no wreck off Punta Ceniza. I know this because they told me so, in writing, on the same day they paid me to go look at it.

That's the kind of arithmetic I've learned to live with. Seven years of it. You take the contract, you don't ask why a government that denies a ship exists also wants its hull stripped to the waterline before anyone with a camera gets curious. You take the money, you dive, you come up, you leave. Belonging is just another word for being somewhere when the trouble starts.

The *San Cristóbal* — if that was ever her name, and the brass plate I pried off her wheelhouse said something close — sat in forty meters of green murk, keel-down in the silt like a drunk sleeping off a bender in a field. A transport, by her bones. Naval lines, naval rivets, and not one document aboard saying so. Somebody had gone through her before me with a torch and a grudge. What was left for my crew and me was ballast, plating, and the slow work of making a liar out of an entire ministry.

I went down at first light, because morning water lies less than afternoon water. My lamp cut a cone through the particulate, and the wreck exhaled around me — that particular sigh a big dead ship gives when the current moves through her ribs. Fish had colonized the mess hall. A grouper the size of a door had claimed the captain's chair, and I respected him for it.

Ballast was my job that day. The manifest — the one that didn't exist — listed stone, but stone doesn't rust, and what I found packed into the number three hold was machinery, crates stenciled with numbers instead of names, and things I didn't have clearance to describe even to myself. I tagged them, photographed them, and kept my mouth shut, which after seven years is less a virtue than a posture.

It was on my third descent, working the aft ballast where the hull had split like a struck bell, that I found him.

He was wedged in the gap between two granite blocks, and at first I took him for more cargo. Divers learn to see bodies as shapes before we let ourselves see them as people; it's how you keep eating breakfast. But the shape resolved, the way they always do, into a man. Older. Leather jacket gone to leather-colored rot. Boots still laced, which told me he hadn't run from anything. One arm curled against his chest like he'd been holding something there when the water took him, and the sea, in its indifferent way, had let him keep holding it.

A canvas pouch, tarred and waxed until it shone like a beetle's back. Waterproofed by somebody who'd expected to get wet.

I should have logged it as salvage. That was the job. Instead I worked it free of his arms, and the motion disturbed the silt, and his face came clear under my lamp — and I'll tell you something I don't tell many people: I said sorry. Out loud, into my regulator, to a dead man forty meters down. Because his eyes were open, and they weren't frightened. They were expectant. There's a difference, and every diver knows it. Frightened means he died surprised. Expectant means he died waiting for someone.

The pouch held a map. Oilcloth over oilskin over oiled paper, wrapped around a brass tube, and inside the tube a river drawn in a surveyor's careful hand — except the river kept going past where every published chart stops, up into a valley marked with a single word in faded ink: *Provision*. And beneath the valley, in a different hand, shakier, pressed hard enough to tear the paper: coordinates, depth soundings, and three words that made my skin prickle through the neoprene.

*They flooded us.*

I'm not a sentimental woman. Sentimentality underwater gets you killed; you start thinking about the surface instead of your gauges. But I hung there in the cold green dark with a dead man's map in my gloves, and I heard — clearly, absurdly, the way you hear things in deep water — the last thing he would ever have said, if the water had let him say it. Not to me. To whoever found him.

*Take it to Ines.*

No surname. No address. Just a name spoken the way you speak the name of the only door left open in a burning house.

My gauge said eleven minutes of bottom time. I spent four of them arguing with myself, which is poor diving and worse economics. Then I put the map inside my suit, against my chest, where it lay like a second heartbeat all the way up.

* * *

Commander Hale arrived at dusk, which told me everything about him before he opened his mouth. Men who want to intimidate you come at dawn. Men who want to *own* you come at dusk, when you're tired and the light makes everyone look reasonable.

His launch was gray and clean and flew no flag, because flags are for ships that exist. He came up my ladder without asking, which takes either rank or rudeness, and he had both. Silver at the temples, jaw like a mooring cleat, and eyes that did the thing I hate most in a man: they inventoried me. Boat, crew, winch, crane. Everything priced, everything provisional.

"Ms. Reyes." He knew my name. I made a note of that. "Commander Hale, Naval Salvage Directorate. I'm told you've been working a site in restricted waters."

"I've been working a rock pile," I said. "Rocks aren't restricted."

"The vessel you're stripping is naval property."

"Then you'll want to file a claim," I said. "With whatever office handles property that doesn't exist. I hear their queue is short."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Appraisal. He decided I was clever rather than difficult, which meant he hadn't been briefed properly, which meant whoever sent him thought a salvage diver was weather. Something you wait out.

"You recovered material from the site," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Scrap metal. Some crates. They're tagged and logged." I handed him my manifest, the real one, the boring one. He read it the way men read things they already know the ending to.

"And personal effects? Remains?"

"There's nobody down there but fish."

He looked at me for a long moment. Dusk was going purple behind him, and my crew had drifted to the far rail with the studied deafness of men who owed me money. "This is a matter of national security, Ms. Reyes. Whatever you think you found or didn't find — the wisest course is to finish your contract, take your pay, and forget the coordinates."

"Commander," I said, "I've forgotten better coordinates than yours. It's practically a hobby."

He almost smiled. That was the worst part. He *almost* smiled, and I understood that he'd met women like me before, and that some of them had taken his advice, and that at least one of them hadn't, and that he could tell the difference faster than I could lie.

"Good evening," he said, and went down my ladder the way he'd come up it, owning it.

I stood at the rail and watched his launch cut a white seam toward the navy lights ashore, and I did the inventory he'd taught me to do. Map against my ribs. Dead man's last word in my head. *Ines.* A daughter, presumably. Somewhere, presumably. Presumably alive, though men who seal maps in wax don't do it for heirs they're sure will outlive them.

Seven years I'd been water. Moving through places, taking nothing with me, leaving nothing behind, owing nobody. It's a clean way to live. Nobody drowns holding onto you.

But I stood there on my own deck, in the country where I didn't live, holding a promise I hadn't made, and felt it pull. Not hard. Just steady. The way a current pulls at your fins when you're trying to hold position — ignorable, right up until it isn't.

In the morning I'd log the rest of the salvage, collect my pay, and point my boat downriver toward the towns where old prospectors' daughters end up when their fathers stop writing.

I told myself it was about the map. Evidence, leverage, a bargaining chip against a commander who inventories people.

I told myself a lot of things that night. The map stayed warm against my chest anyway, and by dawn I'd stopped pretending I couldn't feel it.
