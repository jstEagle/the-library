## 2. A Debt in Dry Season

The river town was called Puerto Ceniza, which meant ash port, which told you everything about its optimism. I came down out of the hills on a bus with a broken fan and a chicken that judged me for six hours, and by the time I stepped onto the mud street my shirt had dried into a map of its own — salt lines where the sea had been, sweat lines where it was going.

Seven years, I'd kept a rule: deliver nothing to nobody. No addresses memorized, no favors banked, no names held past the job. Drifting isn't loneliness if you do it right. It's just motion. But a dead man's hands had closed around mine forty fathoms down and made me a courier against my will, and now here I was, walking a street that smelled of diesel and frying plantains, asking after a woman named Ines like I had any business knowing her name.

I found her at the far end of town, where the river widened and the houses gave up pretending. She ran a boat-repair shed — or it ran itself and she stood inside it arguing with engines. She was maybe thirty, dark-haired, forearms scarred from propeller work, and when she looked up at me she did it the way people do who've already decided not to be impressed.

"You're not a customer," she said.

"No."

"Then you're selling something, or you're trouble."

"Both, probably." I set the waterproof tube on her workbench between a dead carburetor and a coffee can of bolts. "I pulled this off a wreck off Cabo Verde Shoals. Naval transport, unmarked, officially doesn't exist. There was a man aboard. Drowned a long time ago, but he was holding this like he'd been ordered to hold it." I paused. "He said your name. Ines. He said *tell my daughter.*"

She didn't touch the tube for a long moment. Her jaw worked once, twice, like she was chewing on twenty years at once.

"My father went downriver for salvage work when I was nine," she said finally. "Navy contract. He never came back. They sent a letter saying the vessel was lost with all hands, and then they sent a second letter saying there had never been such a vessel, and would we please stop writing." She picked up the tube, turned it over. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "What was his name? When you found him?"

"He didn't give one. Just Ines."

"That's him." She laughed, or something near it. "Stubborn to the end. Wouldn't even haunt me properly."

She cracked the seal and unrolled the map across the bench, weighting the corners with bolts. Ink on oilcloth, hand-drawn, precise as a confession. A valley. A river's source. Markings along the banks — X's, numbers, a cluster of small crosses that could have been graves or could have been something worse. And along the bottom edge, in a different, shakier hand: *They flooded it over us. The gold is still down there. So are they.*

Ines read it three times. Then she looked up at me with an expression I recognized, because I'd been wearing a version of it since the wreck: the look of someone recalculating their whole life around one new fact.

"They didn't lose his ship," she said. "They sank it. He saw something upriver, before the dam closed. Something they needed drowned." She tapped the cluster of crosses. "This is San Esteban. My grandmother's village. They told everyone it was evacuated for the reservoir project. Evacuated." She said the word like it tasted of copper. "There was no evacuation. There was a massacre, and then there was water, and then there were letters telling us to stop asking."

* * *

Hale's men reached Puerto Ceniza two days later, which told me two things: the Commander was faster than I'd credited, and somebody on my old crew had a mouth. They came in civvies, but civvies don't teach a man to stand with his weight even on both feet and his eyes doing arithmetic on every exit. There were four of them working the waterfront, buying drinks, asking about divers. Asking about a woman diver, specifically, recent arrival, possibly carrying documents recovered from government property.

Government property. That was the phrase Hale had used on the deck of his launch, standing over me with the sun behind him while I lied about what I'd found. *Everything below the waterline belongs to the state, Ms. Reyes.* I'd watched his face while I lied and seen nothing in it, which frightened me more than anger would have. Anger means a man is still deciding. Hale had decided years ago.

By the third evening I'd stopped sleeping at my room and started sleeping on Ines's shed floor, which she pretended not to notice and provisioned anyway, leaving a blanket and a thermos within reach without comment. On the fourth morning she woke me before dawn by dropping a duffel bag on my legs.

"We're going," she said.

"Going where?"

"Upstream. To the source." She was already packing: rope, flares, a first-aid kit older than both of us. "Dry season closes the channels in three weeks, maybe less. After that, nothing bigger than a canoe gets past the Narrows until the rains. If we wait, we wait eight months, and Hale will have found us by Tuesday." She cinched the bag. "You found him. You brought me the map. That makes you half-owner of this, whether you like it or not."

"I don't like it."

"Noted."

"Ines." I sat up. "Whatever's under that water, it isn't gold. Not really. It's bodies and paper and a truth the navy killed a shipload of men to keep down. People who go after that don't come back rich. Mostly they don't come back."

"Then we'll be in good company. My father didn't come back either." She shouldered the bag and looked at me, and there was nothing of the girl whose father left when she was nine anywhere in her face. There was only the woman the leaving had built. "I spent twenty years thinking he abandoned us. Turns out the state murdered him and mailed me a form letter. I'm not going upriver for treasure, Rosalind. I'm going for the receipt."

* * *

The boat was called the *Mariposa*, which was generous, because the only thing butterfly-like about her was the way she fluttered at idle. Forty feet of patched hull and a diesel engine that sounded like a man clearing his throat forever. Her owner was a barrel-chested ex-ferryman named Tomás who owed me from a bad year in the delta when I'd raised his brother's boat — and his brother with it — off a channel bottom everyone else had written off as a grave.

Tomás owed me. His engineer, a wiry kid called Pico who'd once taken a police baton meant for my spine during a dock strike, owed me. The cook, Marisol, who'd fed me through a winter when I had nothing but a duffel and a grudge, claimed she didn't owe me anything and signed on anyway, which is its own kind of debt. By noon we were five, loading fuel drums and diving gear under the eyes of two of Hale's men drinking beer at the cantina across the quay, watching us with the patience of cats outside a mousehole.

"They know," Ines said quietly, coiling line beside me.

"They know we're going. They don't know why yet, and they can't stop us on open water without explaining to someone why a nonexistent wreck matters." I tied off the coil. "That's our whole margin, Ines. Their silence. We move faster than their paperwork."

"And when the paperwork catches up?"

"Then we're upstream of the law, and the law floats downstream."

We cast off at midafternoon, the *Mariposa* wallowing out into the brown current with all the grace of a filing cabinet, and Puerto Ceniza slid away behind us — the ash port, the cantina, the two watchers lifting their glasses in a small ironic toast. Ines stood at the bow with the map in her oilskin pocket and her father's stubbornness in her spine, and I stood at the wheel and felt the strange, alarming sensation of having chosen a direction.

Seven years of drift, and it turns out all it takes to anchor a woman is a dead man's promise and a living one's fury.

We made the first bend by dusk. Behind us, far down the shining water, a white hull with no name on it cleared the headland and began, unhurried, to follow.
