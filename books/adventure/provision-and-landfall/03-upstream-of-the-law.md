## 3. Upstream of the Law

*Rosalind*

The white hull held its distance for two days, which told me everything I needed to know about Commander Hale. A man who wanted you dead closed the gap. A man who wanted you *led* somewhere let you swim.

"He's herding us," I said on the second evening, watching the patrol boat's running lights hang in the dusk like a patient wasp. "He could have boarded us at the delta. He wants us to find the valley for him."

"Then we lose him," Ines said.

"You don't lose a navy boat on open water. You lose it where the water stops being navy water." I pointed upstream, where the channel narrowed between walls of drowned forest — dead trees standing to their shoulders in brown current, their crowns bleached like bones. "Above the old logging camp the river braids out into maybe nine channels, and half of them are knee-deep by now and getting shallower every day. His draft is four meters. Ours is one and a half, if we're lucky and light."

"We're not light," said Okonkwo from the wheel, without turning around. He had that gift for stating facts like weather.

We weren't. The *Marta B* carried fuel, food, dive gear, rope, and six people who all had reasons not to be asked questions anywhere south of here: Okonkwo, who'd run cargo with me through two blockades; the Ferreira twins, who could fix an engine with a spoon and a grudge; Marisol, who cooked and kept a shotgun under the rice pot; and a boy called Teo who claimed to be nineteen and claimed it too often.

And now, apparently, a traitor among them. But I didn't know that yet. On the second evening I only knew the river was dropping an inch a day and the white hull was still there, and I stood beside Ines at the rail feeling something I hadn't felt since I was twenty-three and stupid enough to love a town: the specific weight of other people's lives resting on my decisions.

"Your father," I said. "Tell me about him. Not the map part. Him."

Ines was quiet long enough that a heron crossed the whole width of the river before she answered.

"He wrote letters he never sent. I found them after — forty-one of them, bundled with string. Every year on my birthday he wrote one. 'Dear Ines, today you are nine. I am still not coming home.'" Her voice didn't waver; it went flat instead, which is its own kind of wavering. "He thought staying away was protecting me. He thought the same thing about the map, probably. That if he kept it far from me, I'd be safe." She looked at me. "I'm going to find out what kind of protection that is."

* * *

*Ines*

I did not tell Rosalind everything. This is what I have learned about honesty: it comes in installments, and you pay each one hoping the collector doesn't notice the balance.

What I didn't tell her, on the third night, while she slept sitting up against the wheelhouse with her hand near the map tube even unconscious, was this: I had done the arithmetic years ago. Forty-one unsent letters, one drowned father, one flooded valley — and underneath the flood, according to those letters, gold enough that the government had killed a village to keep it quiet. My father's dying promise bought my grief. The gold bought my passage. I needed both, and I wasn't proud of the ratio.

The crew noticed things. Teo noticed most of all, because Teo was the kind of boy who watched everyone to learn how to be someone. On the fourth morning he came to me at the bow, quiet as a cat burglar.

"The twins were talking last night," he said. "Rui took the radio key off the board. Put it back wrong. I checked — the spare's been used."

I looked aft. Rui Ferreira was coiling line with the serenity of a man with no secrets, and his brother was laughing at something Marisol said, and the white hull sat on our wake like a debt.

"Say nothing," I told Teo. "Watch. And if Rosalind asks, you learned it from me."

Because here was the installment I couldn't pay yet: I wanted to know what Rui was selling before I decided whether to forgive it. Everyone on this boat owed Rosalind something. I was starting to understand that I might owe her more than any of them, and I hated debts I couldn't price.

* * *

*Rosalind*

The betrayal came at the narrows, as betrayals do — at the place where there was no room to maneuver.

We'd run the braided channels at first light, Okonkwo reading the water like scripture, taking the *Marta B* up a throat of current so narrow the dead trees brushed both rails. The white hull fell behind at the mouth of the braid and did not reappear, and I made the mistake of feeling good about it.

At dusk we anchored in a cut behind a gravel bar, engines cold, no lights. And at moonrise the ambush came down both banks — two boats, black, running silent on electric motors, men with rifles and lights they didn't switch on until the last hundred meters. Hale hadn't followed us up the river. He'd been *carried*, in pieces, ahead of us, and Rui's radio calls had been the delivery schedule.

"Contact port!" Marisol's shotgun spoke first and the night tore open.

I had the map tube in its harness across my chest, because I always had it there, and I had Ines beside me at the rail, because she refused to sleep below anymore. The lead boat swung in to board us amidships and a man grabbed the rail three feet from me, and I had a choice that lasted about half a heartbeat.

The map tube. Or the hand closing on Ines's arm, hauling her over the rail into the black water.

Seven years ago I'd have calculated. Seven years ago I'd have kept the map, because the map was the mission and the mission was the only thing I'd ever let myself carry. Instead I ripped the harness off, felt the tube go over the rail into the dark on the far side — gone, gone, my father-figure's promise sinking in silt — and put both hands on Ines and *pulled*, and we went down together onto the deck plating with a rifle round stitching the rail above us.

The fight lasted four minutes and cost us Teo's arm — grazed, ugly, not mortal — and cost them two men overboard and their nerve, because when Marisol's shotgun found their engine and the lead boat started burning diesel on the water, the rest decided drowning was worse than disobeying. They backed off into the dark.

I surfaced from the scuffle already knowing what I'd see. Rui stood at the stern rail, hands raised, his brother's pistol pointed at his ribs, and his face was the face of a man doing arithmetic he'd lost.

"They had my daughter," he said. "In Manaus. They showed me photographs."

Nobody moved for a long moment. Then Rosalind — bleeding from the scalp, soaked, empty-handed where the map should be — walked over and looked at him the way you look at a depth gauge.

"Your daughter," she said, "is why we're all still breathing, because you fed them just enough to make them lazy. Get below. We'll talk about Manaus when there's a tomorrow."

Later, in the wrecked quiet of the wheelhouse, Ines found me staring at black water.

"I threw away your father's map," I said. "First real thing anyone's trusted me with in seven years, and I traded it for you inside one heartbeat. I don't know whether that makes me loyal or an idiot."

"It makes you someone who chose," Ines said. And then, because trust forged under fire apparently includes confessions under fire, she said the rest of it flat and fast, like pulling a splinter: "I want the gold, Rosalind. Not just the truth. The truth won't buy back a village or raise my father. I've been telling myself it's justice, but some of it is money, and I need you to know that before you decide what I'm worth."

I looked at her — at the fury and the arithmetic and the terrible honesty — and I thought of my father-figure clutching his waterproof bundle in the dark of a ballast tank, betting everything on a stranger.

"Good," I said. "Now we're both liars who came clean. That's the strongest kind of crew."

She almost smiled. Then Okonkwo's voice came down from the bow, very calm, the way it got when the news was worst:

"Rosie. You need to see what the tide left on the gravel bar."

The map tube lay wedged in the shallows, thirty meters downstream, snagged on a root — and beside it, caught in the same root, glinting under the moon, was a navy marker buoy of the kind they drop to claim salvage. Fresh. Painted with a number.

Hale hadn't lost us. He'd marked the channel. Somewhere ahead, in the dark, he knew exactly where the river would deliver us — and he'd left the buoy so we'd know it too.
