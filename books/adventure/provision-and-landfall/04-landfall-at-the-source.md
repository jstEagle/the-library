## 4. Landfall at the Source

The river gave us one more morning, and it made us pay for it.

We came up the last reach at first light with the *Magpie* drawing water she had no business drawing, her hull groaning over bars that had been six feet under a week ago. Mateo stood at the wheel with his jaw set like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like, and every few minutes the propeller came up shallow enough to churn white, and every few minutes none of us breathed.

"There." Ines pointed, and her voice cracked on the word.

I'd seen the valley only on the map, inked in a dead man's hand, the contours of a place that officially did not exist. But the map hadn't lied. The hills folded in on either side, and between them lay the water — wide, brown-green, unnaturally still, a lake where no lake had any right to be. The tops of drowned trees stood out of it in gray rows, dead forty years and still standing, like a congregation waiting for a sermon.

The church tower broke the surface a half mile in, just the belfry and the cross, tilted like a drunk leaning on a fence.

"That's San Sebastián," Ines said. "My father was baptized there."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that the water wasn't already saying.

Mateo brought us in against the old orchard — the trees were tall enough that their crowns made a natural dock, black branches just breaking the surface — and we tied off while the *Magpie* settled with a sigh, as if she knew this was as far as she'd ever go. Which it was. The channels behind us were closing by the hour. Whatever happened next, we were walking out or we weren't walking out at all.

"Air's good for maybe forty minutes at that depth," I said, checking my gauges. "The village center should be off the church. Records, if they kept them, would be in the municipal building or the church itself."

"And the gold?" Ines asked.

"Your father's map marks a strongroom under the customs house." I looked at her. "You still want it?"

She met my eyes without flinching. "I want them to *pay* for it. All of it. The gold is the price tag."

I could respect that. It was more honest than most grief.

* * *

The water closed over my head and the world went green and quiet, and I was seven years younger and a hundred miles from any shore I'd ever pretended to love.

Visibility was maybe ten feet, the water thick with forty years of settled silt waiting to be kicked up by anyone careless. I went down slow along the orchard slope, Ines beside me — she'd insisted on diving, and I'd decided not to waste our last hours arguing — with Mateo and the twins topside running the lines.

The village rose out of the murk like something from a fever dream. A street. Actual cobblestones, furred with algae. Doorways standing open. A cart in the road, its wooden bed rotted to lace, and beside it — I turned my light on it and then wished I hadn't — the shape of a man, bones settled into the driver's seat, still holding the rotted remnant of a rein.

He'd been running, or trying to. The whole town had been running when the water came. I'd known that in my head since Ines told me the story in the river town, but knowing a thing and floating above its evidence are different animals entirely.

The church door was oak and it had held. I put my shoulder to it and it swung inward with a groan of bubbles, and inside, the silt lay deep and undisturbed, a gray snowfall over pews and an altar and the small bones of people who had run to the one place they'd been taught God would protect them.

I found the records chest behind the altar, iron-banded, and the lock gave up without a fight after forty years of drowning. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job: ledgers. Parish registers. And a bundle of letters in a military hand, stamped and countersigned, orders and reports and one document that made my blood go cold even through the chill of the water — a list. Names, ages, and a column of checkmarks. Two hundred and eleven names. Every one of them checked.

Ines had come up beside me, her light trembling. I felt her go rigid when she saw the list. Through the regulator I couldn't hear her, but I didn't need to. Her hands closed on the oilcloth bundle and she held it against her chest like a child, and for a long moment neither of us moved, two women kneeling in a drowned church with the dead all around us.

Then I tapped my gauge. Twenty minutes. We still had a strongroom to crack.

* * *

The customs house was three streets over and the swim between them was the worst of my life. We kicked up silt in clouds, went half-blind, and came out the other side into clearer water and the sight of the strongroom door — steel, naval-issue, which told its own story about who had built this valley's coffin.

The cutting charge was small, shaped, the kind of thing I'd sworn off using since a job in the Coral Sea went wrong and took a good man's eardrums with it. But we were past finesse. I set it, we backed off behind the customs house wall, and the muffled thump rolled through the water like a fist on a coffin lid.

Inside: crates. Military crates, stenciled, sealed. And gold — not the romantic kind, not coins and bars in neat rows, but smelted ingots, crude, the product of the valley's mines, packed in the same crates as the rifles that had been used to clear the valley of the people who dug it out. The state had flooded the town on top of its own theft and called the lake a reservoir.

We got the ledgers and the list into the waterproof case. The gold we could only mark — I hammered a piton into the strongroom frame and wired a buoy line to it, because gold that has to be drowned twice is evidence too, and evidence is heavier when the navy has to explain why it's down there.

I was coming up the line when Mateo's voice crackled in my earpiece, tight as wire.

"Ros. Boat coming up the channel. Navy gray. It's him."

* * *

Hale's patrol boat came around the drowned orchard at noon like it owned the water, which, officially, it did.

We had maybe ten minutes. The *Magpie* sat low and slow, no hope of running. Mateo looked at me, and I looked at the spillway.

I'd seen it on the approach — the old dam at the valley's mouth, concrete, government-stamped, with a spillway gate on the east flank and, more to the point, a maintenance winch house perched on the abutment. The dry season had dropped the reservoir. The spillway, if it opened, wouldn't drain the valley — forty years of engineering wouldn't allow it — but it would drop the level fast, and fast-moving water carries things. Silt. Debris. Whatever the current decided to take.

And downstream, at the delta, waiting on a press boat that Ines had spent three weeks arranging through a journalist who owed her father a debt older than mine — waiting, with cameras, because the dry season meant the delta channels were shallow and a navy patrol drawing naval draft couldn't follow them in — waiting for whatever the river brought them.

"Open the spillway," I said.

"The gate's forty years seized," Mateo said.

"Then we've got work to do."

We went at the winch house with crowbars and the twins' bad tempers, and it took eleven minutes I will remember for the rest of my life, because for eight of them the patrol boat was closing and for three of them Hale's launch put men ashore on the dam crest and I could hear boots on concrete.

The gate moved two inches, then a foot, then with a shriek of rusted steel it ran free, and the reservoir began to pour itself through the gap in a brown roaring column, and the level started down.

Hale came down the abutment stairs with four men and a sidearm, and he was shouting something about naval property and sabotage and treason, and I stood at the top of the spillway stairs with the waterproof case under my arm and let him shout.

"Commander," I said, when he'd run out of statute. "There's a church down there with two hundred and eleven names in a chest behind the altar. There's a customs house full of your navy's gold packed in the same crates as the rifles used on the people who mined it. And there's a man in the road who died holding a rein, trying to outrun the water you people sent." I held up the case. "The list is going downstream to the delta, where there are reporters, and by tonight every one of those names is going to be a headline. You can arrest me. Or you can read it."

He reached for the case.

And his men didn't move.

That was the thing I'll carry longest — not the gold, not the gate, not even the church. It was the youngest of the four, a sergeant with river mud to his knees, who looked at Hale and said, quietly, "Sir. My grandmother was born in that valley."

And Hale stood there on his own dam with his own men not moving, and the water roared through the gate carrying forty years of silt and silence down toward the sea, and he understood — I watched him understand — that the flood had finally changed direction.

He stepped aside.

* * *

We rode the outflow down in the *Magpie*'s tender, the case between us, Ines holding it the whole way like she'd never let go, and at the delta the press boat was exactly where she'd promised, and the journalist — gray, sharp-eyed, older than grief — took the list in both hands and read the first three names and had to stop and start again.

By nightfall the wire services had the story. By morning, the strongroom buoy line had a navy salvage crew on it — Hale's crew, under new orders, hauling the crates into the light with the whole world watching.

I stood on the delta dock that evening with river mud on my boots and didn't wipe it off. Seven years I'd measured my life in miles — distance from the last job, distance to the next one, always a horizon between me and anything that could hold me. I'd thought drifting was freedom. It was just fear with better scenery.

Ines came and stood beside me, and after a while she said, "The church is going to be raised. The government can't stop it now. They're talking about a memorial."

"Someone should be there," I said. "For the dive surveys. Structural work. It'll take years."

"Years," she said, and there was a question in it.

"Years," I said. "I'm not going anywhere."

And the strange thing, the thing I'd have laughed at a month ago, is that I meant it. The river had taken everything it was going to take. What it left behind — a town to raise, a name to say, a woman beside me who wanted justice and had gotten it and was still standing — was mine to keep.

The dead man in the ballast had made me promise a map. He never said the promise would come with a place at the end of it.

I planted my feet in the mud of that dock, and for the first time in seven years, the ground held.
