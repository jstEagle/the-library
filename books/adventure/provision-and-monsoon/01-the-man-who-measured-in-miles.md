## 1. The Man Who Measured in Miles

The flats taught you patience or they killed you, and Juniper Vane had learned which kind of man he was a long time ago.

He lay on his belly at the crest of a dune that had no name, watching a town called Perdition's Rest cook in the late afternoon heat. Ten years of this. Ten years of reading tracks like scripture—boot prints, hoof prints, the drag of a stolen trunk, the telltale spacing of a man running scared—and for what? Petty thieves, mostly. A man who stole a crate of nails. A woman who lifted a purse in a saloon and got three counties of pursuit for her trouble. The river towns called him the best tracker on the water, and he supposed it was true, though being the best at something so small felt like being the tallest man in a room with a low ceiling.

He measured his life in miles now. That was the honest arithmetic of it. Four hundred miles to the territorial line. Sixty miles back to the last waystation where anyone remembered his name. Zero miles, most nights, to anything resembling a reason.

Below him, a figure moved along the back wall of the dry goods store, quick and low, hugging the adobe shadow like it had been born there.

Juniper smiled without meaning to. He'd been watching Perdition's Rest for two days, not because he expected anything but because the town sat on the only road between here and the ferry crossing, and roads were where thieves became predictable. He'd told himself he was resting. He'd told himself a lot of things over ten years. But when the figure slipped through the gap between the store and the stable—moving with that particular economy, that refusal to waste a single motion—he felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since morning.

Work. Thank God. Work.

He slid down the back side of the dune, leading his mule by the rein, and cut across the hardpan toward town. The sun leaned on him the whole way, the way it always did, patient and impersonal as a debt collector. His badge hung inside his coat where nobody could see it. He'd stopped wearing it outside years ago. Badges invited conversation, and conversations invited questions, and questions had answers, and his answers were all the same shape: *nobody, nowhere, nothing worth knowing.*

The dry goods store belonged to a widower named Ochs who watered his stock down and cheated travelers and still somehow managed to be beloved, because in the flats even a cheat was company. Juniper came around the alley behind it and stopped.

She was already out the window.

Not fleeing—she hadn't run yet. She stood in the alley with her back against the wall, catching her breath, and she was younger than he'd expected. Mid-twenties, maybe. Dust-colored coat, dark hair cut short and practical, a face built for forgetting: nothing remarkable except the eyes, which were doing rapid arithmetic, cataloguing the alley, the exits, him. She had a folded paper in one hand, small enough to be a letter, and she was tucking it into her coat with the care of someone handling something that could go off.

"Evening," Juniper said.

She didn't startle, which he noted and respected. She just turned her head, took him in—the dust-caked coat, the rifle slung casual, the mule standing behind him like a bored witness—and said, "Evening," like they were neighbors discussing weather.

"You left your window open," he said. "Back there. On the second floor."

"Careless of me."

"It was."

"And yet here I am, closing it." She pushed off the wall, unhurried, angling slightly—not toward the street, he noticed, but toward the far end of the alley, where a drainage cut ran under the fence line. She'd mapped the alley before she ever went in. He knew the type. He'd spent a decade learning the type from the inside of their footprints.

"I'm going to need you to stand still," Juniper said.

"Are you the law?"

"That depends."

"On what?"

"On whether you make me earn the word."

Something flickered across her face then—not fear. Amusement, maybe, or its close cousin, contempt. "You don't look like the law," she said. "The law looks like it sleeps indoors."

"The law looks however the flats want it to look."

She laughed. It was a short, real laugh, gone as fast as it came, and it did something unwelcome to the space behind his ribs. When had he last heard anyone laugh within arm's reach of him? He couldn't place it. That was the answer. He couldn't place it, and the not-placing rang in him like a struck bell.

"Stand still," he said again, quieter.

"No," she said, pleasantly, and moved.

She went for the drainage cut exactly as he'd known she would, because everyone went for the drainage cut, because people believed the ground wanted them to escape. The ground didn't want anything. The ground just kept records. Juniper stepped sideways, put himself between her and the cut, and caught her wrist as she tried to slip past—not hard, just enough to stop her momentum, the way you'd stop a door.

She twisted free of it in half a heartbeat, dropped low, and came up with a knife.

Of course she did.

"Let's not," Juniper said.

"You're pointing a rifle at a woman holding a letter knife," she said. "Who's being reasonable here?"

"It's not pointed at you. It's pointed at the fence behind you, which is where you'll be spending the next several minutes while we talk."

"We're talking now."

"We are."

"Then talk." But she didn't move, and neither did he, and the alley held them both in its narrow shade while somewhere down the street a dog barked itself hoarse at nothing.

* * *

Her name, she eventually admitted, was Wren Calloway, though he suspected that was one name among several, the way a river has many names along its length and is none of them truly. He got it out of her the slow way, sitting on an overturned crate in the alley while she sat on the ground where he told her to sit, knife surrendered after a negotiation longer than some marriages.

"Calloway," Juniper repeated. "There's a warrant out of Salt Fork. Horse theft."

"Horse *borrowing*."

"There's a difference?"

"There's a return."

"Not if the horse comes back lame."

"That horse came back fat," Wren said. "I fed him better than his owner did. Ask anyone."

"I'll ask the magistrate."

"You do that." She squinted up at him. "You've been following me. Since Cane Bend."

"Since before Cane Bend."

"Three weeks." She sounded almost flattered. "You're the one who swept my camp at Dry Wash. I thought that was a ranger. You don't camp like a ranger. Rangers leave their coffee grounds in a neat little pile like they're apologizing to the ground. You burned yours."

"You looked at my fire pit."

"I look at everything. It's how I stay uncaught." She paused. "Was. How I *stayed* uncaught."

Juniper looked at her for a long moment. The light was going amber in the alley, and the heat had finally begun to loosen its grip, and he was aware—uncomfortably, specifically aware—that this was the longest conversation he'd had with another human being in five days, and that he was enjoying it, and that enjoying it was a problem.

"What did you take?" he asked. "From Ochs's store."

"Nothing worth a warrant."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest answer I have."

"Wren." He said her name flatly, testing its weight. "I've chased men across four hundred miles of nothing for stealing a crate of nails. Don't insult me with small."

She held his gaze, and he watched her decide something—watched the arithmetic move behind her eyes, the same arithmetic she'd done when she first saw him, except this time the numbers came out different. She reached into her coat slowly, two fingers, and drew out the folded paper.

"This," she said. "That's all. A letter."

"A letter."

"Unsent. Addressed to nobody I know. I read it in the window before I climbed down, and I'll tell you right now, I wish I hadn't."

"Why take it, then?"

"Because it wasn't mine to leave lying there." Her jaw tightened, and for the first time since the knife came out, she looked young. "Because whoever wrote it wanted it destroyed, and instead it was sitting in a strongbox in a cheating dry goods store in a town called Perdition's Rest, and that seemed like a wrong thing. The kind of wrong that gets people killed."

Juniper held out his hand. "Give it to me."

"No."

"That wasn't a request."

"Everything's a request until somebody makes it otherwise, and you haven't." She stood up, unfolding from the ground with that same economy, and faced him, and the amber light caught the paper in her fingers. "You want it? Take it. But hear me first, because I've been carrying this thing for six hours and it weighs more than my saddle."

He should have taken it. That was the job. That was ten years of training distilled to a single motion: relieve the suspect, secure the evidence, ride to the nearest authority, file the report, disappear again into the miles. He had done it a hundred times. He could do it in his sleep, and often felt that he had.

Instead he said, "Hear what?"

Wren glanced past him, up the alley, toward the street—an old habit, checking sightlines—and when she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone careful, the voice of a person walking on ice and listening for the crack.

"The letter names a man. Magistrate Corven Hale. It says—" She stopped. Started again. "It says he's been buying up water rights along the upper river through men who don't exist. And it says the towns that wouldn't sell—" Her throat worked. "Dry Hollow sold last spring. Two months later, their well went bad. Forty-one people sick. Seven dead, three of them children. And the letter says that wasn't sickness. It says Hale's men poisoned the water to make the next town easier to buy."

The dog down the street had finally given up barking. The silence it left was enormous.

"That's a serious accusation," Juniper said, and heard how thin his own voice sounded, how much it wanted to dismiss her.

"It's a dying man's accusation. The handwriting shakes so bad halfway through he switches hands. Left to right. You can see it." She held the letter out—not quite offering it, just showing it, the way you'd show a wound. "I stole a dead man's last words out of a strongbox, Ranger, and now I can't give them back and I can't deliver them, because the man they accuse owns every law between here and the capital. Including, I'd guess, whatever badge you keep hidden inside your coat."

Juniper didn't touch the badge. Didn't confirm it. But she saw the answer in his face anyway, and nodded, unsurprised.

"So," she said. "Now you know what I stole. A letter. Just a letter." Her mouth twisted. "Funny how heavy paper gets."

He should arrest her. The thought arrived on schedule, dutiful and dull: *arrest her, take the letter, ride downstream, hand it to the magistrate's office, file the report.* The machinery of his decade turned over in him, smooth as a well-oiled wheel, asking only that he keep turning.

But he was looking at the letter. At the paper trembling very slightly in her fingers, and he understood suddenly that it wasn't fear making it tremble. It was the weight she'd described. She'd carried it six hours and it had already gotten into her blood, and Juniper Vane, who had measured his life in miles because miles asked nothing of him, felt the first mile-marker of something else go into the ground beneath his feet.

"Elias Marsh," he said quietly.

Wren blinked. "What?"

"The handwriting. Shaking, switching hands. There's a prospector named Elias Marsh who writes with both hands—had a stroke two winters back, up in the high country. Half the river towns have seen his assay marks." He paused. "Is the letter signed?"

She looked down at it. Looked back up. And whatever she saw in his face made her finally, fully extend the paper toward him.

"You know him," she said.

"I bought water off him at a waystation three days ago," Juniper said. "He was coughing blood and paying in gold dust and asking strange questions about the ferry schedule. I gave him directions and rode on." The words came out of him like stones pulled from a wound. "I gave him directions and rode on."

The alley had gone blue with dusk. Somewhere in the store above them, Ochs was lighting a lamp, and its glow spilled yellow across the window ledge, ordinary as anything.

"Ranger," Wren Calloway said, and for the first time she used the word like it meant something, like it might mean something still, "if that letter's from who you think it's from—then you didn't ride past a sick man three days ago. You rode past the only witness to a massacre. And I don't think either of us is going to sleep tonight until we know which way he went."

Juniper took the letter from her hand.

It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. He unfolded it under Ochs's lamplight, and the shaking script swam up at him out of the page, and the first crack opened in ten years of competent loneliness, wide enough to see the stars through.

"He went downstream," Juniper said. "Toward the waystation at Miller's Ford."
