## 2. A Dying Man's Arithmetic

The waystation at Mile Marker 112 had been abandoned for six years, which made it the most honest building on the river. No one lied in a place nobody wanted. The roof sagged like a tired shoulder, the corral fence had gone to silver-gray splinters, and the wind came through the walls with the frankness of a man who had nothing left to lose. Juniper Vane liked it here. He stopped whenever his circuit brought him past, boiled coffee on the old iron stove, and let the silence sit on him like a coat that almost fit.

Tonight the silence was broken.

He smelled it before he saw anything—the sweetish rot of a body cooking from the inside out. Ten years of tracking men through the flats taught a ranger to read smells the way a priest reads scripture. This one said poison, and it said poison done slow, the kind you sip without knowing.

His hand went to the rifle before his mind finished the thought. He eased through the doorway with the moon at his back.

A man lay on the bunk against the far wall, propped half upright by a saddle rolled under his shoulders. Old, or old the way hard country makes men old—sixty years worn like eighty. His beard was gray shot through with red dust, and his hands, resting on his chest, were the hands of a prospector: knuckles swollen, nails blackened, a mapwork of scars that told of twenty years of digging where the river didn't want him. His eyes were open. They found Juniper in the dark the way eyes do when they've been waiting.

"Took you long enough," the man rasped.

"I'm not who you're waiting for."

"You're the only kind there is." The man coughed, and the cough went on too long, wet and deep, and when it ended he smiled with bloodless lips. "Ranger. I can see the badge even in the dark. You people shine it so the river can find you."

Juniper kept the rifle low but ready. "Who are you?"

"Elias Marsh." He said it like a man laying down a card he'd been holding all night. "You'll have heard the name."

Juniper hadn't. That was the truth, and he'd learned long ago that lying to dying men bought nothing but bad dreams. "Can't say I have."

"Good." Marsh laughed, and the laugh turned into another cough. "Good. Means I did it right. Ten years keeping my name small." His eyes glittered. "You know something about that, I'd wager. A ranger riding the empty miles alone, no partner, no town, no name worth saying twice. I've watched your kind pass this station for a decade. You're all running from something. Most of you just call it duty."

Juniper didn't answer. He crossed to the stove instead, struck a match, and set water heating, because that was what you did—that was the whole liturgy of the flats: you put water on, you gave a man a moment to be a man instead of a problem. The match flared and showed him more of the room. A pack against the wall, slit open and rifled through. A mule outside, he guessed, grazing the dead weeds by the corral. And on the floor beside the bunk, a leather tube, cracked with age, clutched in the man's left hand even now, fingers curled around it like roots around stone.

"Poisoned," Marsh said, watching him. "Three days ago. Slow stuff. Arsenic in the whiskey, maybe, or the flour. I've been tasting metal since the crossing at Dry Hollow, and now everything south of my ribs is turning to soup." He said it flatly, the way a man reports weather. "I've got until morning. Maybe noon if I'm stubborn, and I am stubborn, but not that stubborn."

"Who poisoned you?"

Marsh's smile widened a fraction. "There it is. First question a lawman asks. Who. Not *what*." He lifted the leather tube an inch off the floor, just enough that Juniper could see it was heavy with paper. "You're asking the wrong question, Ranger. Ask me what I have, and then ask me why a man would kill to keep it buried."

The water began to murmur toward a boil. Juniper took down the tin cup, poured, and carried it over. Marsh drank in small sips, his throat working, and Juniper saw how thin the man had become—not sick-thin but starved-thin, as though whatever was killing him had been eating him for weeks before it started to finish the job.

"There's a town called Dry Hollow," Marsh said. "Downstream, forty miles. You know it?"

"I know it. Small place. Ferry landing, some farms."

"Farms." Marsh spat the word. "They had farms. Now they've got dust and children with blue gums and bellies full of ache. You know what arsenic does to a water table, Ranger? It doesn't leave. It sits in the silt like a debt. Every crop they pull up is a crop that's killing them, and every cup their babies drink is a little more of the same." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I rode through there two weeks back. I counted eleven graves with fresh dirt. Eleven. In a town of ninety souls."

Juniper stood very still. He'd ridden through Dry Hollow in the spring. He remembered the town being quiet, the ferryman surly, a woman selling jerky who wouldn't meet his eye. He remembered thinking: poor year. He remembered thinking nothing else at all.

"That's not my jurisdiction," he heard himself say, and hated the words even as they left his mouth, because they sounded exactly like what they were—a door closing.

"No," Marsh agreed softly. "It isn't. Nothing on this river is anybody's jurisdiction. That's rather the point."

* * *

The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, the mule snorted once and went quiet. Marsh talked, and Juniper listened, and somewhere in the second hour the coffee went cold in his cup untouched.

The story came out in pieces, the way dying men tell things—out of order, circling back, impatient with details that mattered and obsessive about ones that didn't. Elias Marsh had prospected the high gorge country above the river's source for nineteen years. Nineteen years in country every map marked with a blank space and the words *closed by order of the territorial magistrate*. Everyone knew the source was forbidden. Everyone had a theory—sacred ground, unstable rock, a quarantine from some old fever. Marsh had a different theory, because Marsh had spent nineteen years walking where the signs said don't, and what he'd found was neither sacred nor unstable nor sick.

It was a dam.

"A dam," Juniper repeated.

"Biggest thing a man ever built out here, and nobody knows it's there. Stone and timber and steel shipped up in pieces over three seasons, hauled by crews who never came home." Marsh's hand tightened on the tube. "Magistrate Corven Hale sealed the headwaters six years ago. Diverted the flow into his own channels, sold the water rights downstream to the rail companies and the mining concerns at prices that would make a bishop weep. And the towns?" He shrugged, a terrible small motion. "The towns that signed his papers got water. The towns that balked—" He tapped his own chest. "Got what I got. Got what Dry Hollow got. Arsenic doesn't need a dam to travel. It needs a wagon, a night, and a man willing to pour."

"Why are you telling me this? Why not take it to a judge? A newspaper?"

Marsh looked at him for a long time. In the dimness his eyes had the flat shine of river stones.

"I tried a judge. Judge Aldous, in Harrow Bend. Good man, I thought. He listened to me for an hour, thanked me kindly, and two days later Hale's riders were combing the gorges for a prospector with a gray beard. I only kept my scalp because I know country they don't." He coughed again, shorter this time. "I tried a newspaper man in Candelaria. He printed one paragraph—a paragraph, four lines about irregularities in the water rights—and by the end of the week his press was burned and he was on a boat east, and he wrote me a letter apologizing. An apology, Ranger. That's what the truth buys on this river. An apology and ash."

"So you ran."

"So I dug." Marsh lifted the tube at last, fully, holding it across his knees like a child. "Nineteen years, I didn't just walk the gorges. I copied. Survey marks, supply manifests, pay ledgers from the diversion camps—I pulled them out of company offices and dead men's saddlebags. Names, dates, amounts. Judges paid. Deputies paid. Editors paid. It's all in here, written in my own hand where I couldn't steal the original, and the originals are cached where I cached them, and the map to both is in this tube." He turned it slowly. "And none of it is worth a dried bean, because I'm dying in a waystation and the only man within fifty miles is a ranger who polices horse thieves while the biggest thief on the river wears a magistrate's chain."

The words landed harder than Marsh could have known. Juniper thought of ten years of mile markers, of warrants served for stolen saddles and rustled cattle, of the careful small machinery of his life. He thought of Dry Hollow's quiet streets and the woman who wouldn't look at him, and felt something shift in his chest like a rockslide starting.

"What do you want from me?"

"Now that," Marsh said, "is the right question."

* * *

They negotiated the way men negotiate in the desert: plainly, and with death as the third party at the table.

Marsh laid it out in arithmetic. "Here's what I have. A map to the source—the true source, behind Hale's dam—and a map to my caches, three of them, with the ledgers and testimony enough to hang a magistrate twice over. Here's what I want. I want it delivered. Not to a judge—judges are bought. Not to a newspaper—newspapers burn. To the people. Downstream. Dry Hollow first, then Candelaria, then every landing on this river until the story is in so many mouths that killing it means killing everyone." His breath rattled. "I want the water freed and the truth carried down like the spring flood. Can you do that, Ranger?"

"And if I say no?"

"Then I die tonight with my secrets, and in ten years some other prospector finds the graves and starts over, and more towns die in the meantime." Marsh's voice dropped. "I'd rather it was you. But it doesn't have to be you. That's the arithmetic."

Juniper walked to the door and stood looking out at the flats. The moon had risen fully now, laying the desert out in silver and black, and somewhere out there Wren Calloway was presumably laughing at him from a fresh hide, and somewhere beyond her was Sera, and beyond her the whole patient machinery of a law that worked fine for horses and not at all for people. Ten years he had kept himself small. Ten years, and the river had been dying upstream of him the entire time, and he had measured his worth in miles ridden and thieves caught and had called it peace.

"You're asking me to make war on a magistrate," he said without turning around.

"I'm asking you to deliver mail," Marsh said. "What Hale makes of it is his own affair."

"It won't stop with mail. You know that. Men like Hale don't surrender ledgers because a ranger asks nicely. If I carry this, it ends with guns, or it ends with me in a cell, or it ends with me dead in a canyon next to you."

"Yes," Marsh said simply. "Probably. I won't lie to you; lying's what killed me." A pause, and then, quieter: "I'll tell you what I told myself nineteen years ago, the day I first saw the dam and understood what I'd found. I told myself: a man can live invisible, or he can matter. He can't do both. I chose late, and it cost me everything, and I'd choose it again before the whiskey was cold." He shifted on the bunk, and the sound he made was not quite a laugh. "You've been invisible a long time, Ranger. I can see it from here. It looks comfortable. It looks like a badge and an empty road and nobody remembering your name. And underneath it, it looks like Dry Hollow's eleven graves, because invisibility has a price too—it's just paid in other people's coin."

Juniper stood in the doorway a long moment. The wind moved through the sage with a sound like distant water, which was a lie the desert told, and he had heard it ten thousand times and never once believed it, and never once stopped listening for it.

He came back and sat on the stool beside the bunk.

"Show me the map," he said.

Marsh's face did something complicated—relief and grief and grim satisfaction all at once, the expression of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body. He unrolled the tube with shaking hands. Inside were three sheets: the top one oilcloth-backed, drawn in ink gone brown with age, showing the gorge country above the source in lines so fine and confident they could only have come from a man who had walked every inch of them. A route was traced in red. Waypoints marked. Distances noted in a cramped, precise hand.

"The caches," Marsh said, tapping. "Canyon of Names—you'll know it when you see it, the walls are carved with every fool who ever prospected there, mine's the third mark past the split rock, a circle with a cross. Second cache under the old diversion camp, in the cistern. Third—" he hesitated, and his eyes came up, and for the first time all night he looked afraid, "—third is at the source itself. Behind the dam. There's a testimony cut into the stone there. Mine. I cut it eight years ago, when I still thought I'd live to read it aloud myself."

"You carved your testimony into a canyon wall?"

"Stone keeps better than paper, and paper keeps better than men." Marsh closed his eyes briefly. "Promise me, Ranger. Not the map—the map's yours either way now, I haven't the strength to take it back. Promise me the rest. That it gets downstream. That the towns hear it from a living mouth, not read it off a grave marker. Swear it on whatever you've got left to swear on."

Juniper looked at the map. He looked at the dying man. He understood, with the clarity that comes only at night in empty places, that he was standing at a fork from which there was no road back—that some promises, once spoken, changed the shape of the man who spoke them, the way a river changes the land it crosses whether or not the land consents.

"On the badge," Juniper said. "On the ten years. On everything I've got left."

Marsh exhaled, long and shuddering, and something in his frame seemed to settle, the tension of nineteen years releasing all at once into the arms of the dark.

"Good," he whispered. "Good. Then listen close, because I won't say it twice, and the passes seal with the dry season, and after that even the map won't save you—"

* * *

Elias Marsh died an hour before dawn, mid-sentence, between the name of a bribed judge and the depth of the second cache. He went quietly, the way he had lived loudly in secret—one breath, then none, his hand still resting on the empty tube as though he might yet think of one more thing worth hiding in it.

Juniper sat with him until the sky went gray, then pink, then the hard clean gold of another day on the flats. He covered the body with the tarp from the mule's pack and stacked stones over it, working methodically, the way he did everything, and when he finished he stood at the head of the grave with his hat off and realized he could not remember the last time he had promised anyone anything.

That was the trouble with promises. They fit so easily in the mouth. It was only later, carrying them, that you learned their weight—and Elias Marsh's promise weighed like a dam.

Juniper saddled up, tied the mule's lead to his own rig, and took out the oilcloth map one more time. The red line climbed away north and west, up into the blank spaces, toward the source, toward the caches, toward a canyon full of dead men's names. Forty miles downstream, behind him, Dry Hollow slept beside its poisoned fields, its eleven fresh graves, its children with blue gums.

He turned the mule north.

He was three miles out, the waystation already sunk behind him into the heat shimmer, when he saw the rider tracking him along the ridge—a small figure, moving light and fast, keeping just at the edge of rifle range with the easy insolence of someone who had robbed him once already and meant to make a habit of it.

Wren Calloway. The thief from the letter.

Juniper's hand drifted toward his rifle, then away. He looked down at the map case strapped to his thigh, and at the ghost of an old man riding behind him in the dust, and he did not reach for the gun at all.

She was following him. And for reasons he would examine later, in colder hours, he let her.
