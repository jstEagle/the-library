## 3. What Wren Stole

The letter was still warm from her body when Juniper Vane held out his hand for it.

Wren Calloway had hidden plenty of things in her time—coins in her boot heel, lockpicks in her braid, a whole silver candlestick once strapped flat against her spine under a coat two sizes too big. She knew how to make a thing disappear. But a letter was different. A letter had to be carried close, against the skin, where the paper took the heat of you and gave it back, so that by the third day you forgot it was there and then remembered all at once, like a coal sewn into your shirt.

She'd carried this one for nine days. From Corven Hale's own study in the magistrate's house at Fort Meridian, down through the garrison town where every second man wore a badge and the other half wished they did, along the river road past the toll bridge, past the ferry crossing, into the flats where the law thinned out like broth. Nine days of sleeping sitting up with her back against walls. Nine days of checking every shadow twice.

And now a ranger with dust in his seams and a decade of empty miles in his eyes had her by the wrist, and she had maybe a minute before he decided she was just another thief and dragged her to whatever jail served this end of nowhere.

So she did the only thing left to do. She told the truth.

"You want to know what I stole?" she said. "Read it. Go on. I'd rather you read it than arrest me for it."

His hand didn't loosen. His eyes didn't move from her face, either—gray eyes, patient as erosion, the kind that made you feel like ground being measured. She'd heard about Juniper Vane even in Fort Meridian, in the way people say things quietly in garrison towns. *The ranger who catches everything. The ranger who never talks. Ten years and he's never lost a man he set out after.*

Well. He'd never set out after her before.

"It's stolen property," he said. His voice was low and even, a voice that had gotten used to not being used. "Whose?"

"Read it first. Then I'll tell you whose."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind working at the loose tin on the waystation roof, a slow metallic worry, worry, worry. Somewhere behind the building a mule shifted and sighed. Juniper Vane looked at her the way he'd probably looked at a hundred thieves before her, and Wren understood, with a thief's instinct for the shape of a thing, that he was not deciding whether to believe her. He was deciding whether he wanted to know.

"Read it," she said again, softer. "Please."

He took the letter. He broke the seal—her seal, a careful forgery, pressed back down with a heated knife blade—and unfolded the page, and Wren watched his face while he read, because she had learned that faces told you more than documents did.

She watched the stillness come into him.

It started at the eyes. A man reading a bill of sale reads with his mouth, lips moving faintly, eyes skimming. A man reading something that changes the ground under his feet reads with a stillness that spreads. Juniper Vane read the first lines and his jaw set. He read the middle and his breathing slowed, deliberate, like a man rationing air. And when he reached the end—the signature, the date, the cold little arithmetic at the bottom—his hand had gone white around the paper's edge, and the paper trembled, just barely, just once.

"You forged this seal," he said.

"I forged it back on. The seal was already broken when I took it. Hale had already read it."

"Whose letter is it?"

"His own. Written in his own hand, to a man named Dree in the capital. A surveyor. Hale was asking him to falsify the headwater charts." She watched him now, watched for the flinch. "The river doesn't start where the maps say it starts, Ranger. It starts higher. It starts at a spring in the gorge country that Hale's crews dammed six years ago, and everything since—the water rights, the tolls, the new settlements he planted on the diverted channel—it's all built on a lie with a wall around it. And the towns that wouldn't sell their rights to him—"

She stopped. Not because she didn't know the rest. Because she wanted him to say it.

Juniper Vane looked down at the letter. "Dry Hollow," he said quietly.

"You've been there."

"Last month." His thumb moved along the edge of the paper. "Children sick. The well water tasting of metal. I wrote it up as bad ground."

"It's not bad ground. It's copper sulfate and arsenic, ordered from a supplier in Meridian under a false name, paid from an account that doesn't exist. It's in the letter. The second page." She nodded at the fold. "Hale itemizes it, Ranger. He *itemizes* it. He writes like a man who's never once imagined anyone would read his mail."

The ranger stood very still. Out on the flats the light was going long and amber, and the wind had picked up the smell of the waystation's cold stove ash, and Wren Calloway—wanted in three counties, caught red-handed at last by the one lawman who never lost anybody—stood in front of him and felt, absurdly, like the honest one.

"You could hang for this," he said. "Stealing from a magistrate. Forging his seal."

"I could hang for a lot of things. That's not one of them." She lifted her chin. "You want to know why I took it? It wasn't for the reward. There isn't one. It wasn't to sell. Nobody would buy it—Hale owns everyone who could afford it. I took it because I was in his house to steal a candlestick and I read a page of it instead, and I couldn't put it down. I read the whole thing standing in a dead man's study with a candle going, and by the end I was crying, and I don't cry, Ranger, and I thought: somebody has to know this besides the man who wrote it. Somebody outside the wall. So I took the one thing in that house worth stealing, and I've been running for nine days, and you're the first person who's caught me, and I am *begging* you—" and here her voice did something she hated, cracked in the middle like green wood, "—I am begging you to read it all the way through before you decide what I am."

He read it all the way through. She knew because he stood there in the failing light with the pages in his hands and did not move for what felt like the length of a psalm, and when he finished he folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, and did not give it back.

"Where did you get the names," he said. "Dree. The supplier. The account."

"From the letter. Where else?"

"And Elias Marsh?"

The name landed between them like a dropped tool.

Wren blinked. "The prospector? What about him?"

For a moment she thought the ranger wouldn't answer. Then he reached into his coat—slowly, the way a man moves when he's showing you he's not going for a weapon—and drew out a folded square of oilskin, and inside it a map, hand-drawn, annotated in a cramped and failing script, and a scrap of paper with a dead man's arithmetic on it.

"He gave me this yesterday," Juniper Vane said. "He was dying. Poisoned, same as Dry Hollow's well. He'd been to the source. He drew it from memory because he didn't trust paper." His voice stayed level but something under it had begun, very quietly, to burn. "He made me promise to carry the truth downstream."

Wren looked at the map. She looked at the letter in his other hand. And she did what she was best at in all the world, better than locks, better than lies: she saw the shape of a situation in three seconds flat.

Two pieces of proof. One dying man's map and one magistrate's own hand. Neither one enough alone—a map is a madman's scribble to a bought court, and a letter is a forgery claim waiting to happen. But together. Together they were a case. Together they were a hammer.

And the only man who had both of them was standing in front of her with handcuffs on his belt.

"All right," she said. "Here's my offer."

"I'm not making deals with thieves."

"You're going to want to hear this one." She stepped closer, and she kept her hands where he could see them, open, empty, the oldest gesture of good faith there is. "You can take me in. It's a two-day ride to the nearest lockup and you'll have to write a report, and the report goes to the district office, and the district office reports to the magistrate's court. To Hale. The man whose letter you're holding. The man who poisoned Dry Hollow's well, and the waystation, and Elias Marsh, and who knows how many towns that didn't make it into the itemized list. You'll deliver me, and the letter, and the map, into the hands of the one man in this territory with a reason to bury all three. And he will. He'll bury them deep, and he'll bury you with them, and he'll do it smiling, because that's what he does. I've seen him do it."

Juniper Vane said nothing. But he didn't move toward the handcuffs, and in Wren's arithmetic, that was a signature on a contract.

"Or," she said, "you let me go, and then you do something smarter. You let me *come with you.*"

That got a reaction—a flicker, quickly flattened, but she caught it. Surprise. Maybe something under the surprise.

"You know the gorge country?" she went on. "I do. Not the maps of it—the real one. I ran cargo through the low canyons for two years before I got honest-adjacent. I know which routes flood, which routes are watched, which ferry crossings Hale's riders work, which toll bridges will wave a ranger through and which will search his saddlebags. You've got a map drawn by a dying man and a letter that proves a magistrate is a murderer, and what you don't have is anybody watching your back while you carry both of them through country that wants you dead. I'm the best thief in three counties. That means I'm the best at not being caught, and right now that is the single most valuable skill in this territory."

"You'd run," he said. "First chance."

"Of course I'd run." She said it plainly, because she'd learned long ago that a lie offered early costs double later. "First chance, if the chance is good. But here's the thing, Ranger—I've had nine days of chances, and I haven't taken a single one, because every road out of this territory runs past a man wearing Hale's colors, and the only direction that doesn't is *upstream.* Toward the thing he's hiding. So my freedom and your truth are pointed the same way for once. That's not trust. That's geometry."

The wind gusted, and the loose tin on the roof clattered like applause from an empty house. Juniper Vane looked down at the map in his hand, at Elias Marsh's cramped annotations, at the little drawn symbol at the top of the gorge country that the prospector had marked with three words in failing ink: *they sealed it.*

"Ten years," he said, mostly to himself. "Ten years I've been chasing people like you across the flats. Petty theft. Stolen horses. A man who took a coat off a clothesline." He said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "And the whole time, this."

"You didn't know."

"I policed it." His eyes came up and fixed on her, and for the first time she saw past the patience to the thing the patience was holding down. "I caught hungry people stealing bread from towns that were being poisoned, and I called it law."

Wren had rehearsed a dozen versions of this conversation on nine nights of bad sleep. In none of them had the ranger said anything like that. In all of them she'd been the one talking, selling, spinning. Now she found she had nothing to sell. Just the truth, which was a strange, naked feeling, like going out without a coat.

"So," she said. "Do we have a bargain? You don't arrest me. I don't rob you in your sleep. We go upstream together, we find whatever Elias Marsh found, and we put Corven Hale's own signature in front of enough people that even a bought territory has to look at it. And when it's done—" she shrugged, and made her voice light, because light was easier, "—you can arrest me for the candlestick. Fair's fair. I'll even confess to the candlestick."

"The candlestick?"

"It was a very nice candlestick."

Something happened to his face then. It took her a moment to recognize it because it was so faint and so long-unused, and when she did recognize it she felt oddly like she'd found water in a place with no wells.

Juniper Vane almost smiled.

He didn't, quite. But the almost was there, and then it was gone, folded away with the letter into his coat, and he was all business again, turning toward the waystation, toward the mule, toward the dark coming down over the flats.

"We leave before first light," he said. "There's a man I need to tell you about—Elias Marsh. Everything he said. And you're going to start talking, and you're not going to stop, and I'm going to remember everything, because if we're doing this, we do it like a case. Evidence, witnesses, chain of custody. I've spent ten years building cases against men who stole chickens. I can build one against a magistrate."

"And if the case needs a little theft along the way?"

He paused at the waystation door. The lantern inside had gone out, and the wind was rising, and somewhere far off to the north, over the gorge country, thunder was muttering—the first word of a conversation the dry season would finish.

"Then I suppose," Juniper Vane said, "I'll have to look the other way. Just once."

Wren grinned, and went to see about the mule.

It was only later, in the blue hour before dawn, with her bedroll laid out by a cold stove and the ranger sitting up across the room with the letter, reading it again by the light of a shielded match, that she let herself think about the part she hadn't told him. The part of the letter she'd read in Hale's study and carried for nine days and not said out loud, because saying it out loud would make it a plan instead of a fear.

There'd been a postscript. Four lines in Hale's neat, small hand, added after the main body, the ink slightly different, written later, the way a man adds a thought before sealing a thing.

*Regarding the prospector Marsh—if he surfaces, do not take him alive. He has seen the spring. A dead witness is a closed account. And find out who else he has spoken to. I want names.*

*I want names.*

Wren lay in the dark and listened to Juniper Vane breathing across the room, slow and even, a man who had no idea he was already on a list. She thought about telling him. She thought about the way he'd looked reading the letter, the stillness spreading through him, the ten years of empty miles suddenly meaning something—and she thought, *he'll still go. Knowing won't stop him. It'll only make him careful, and careful men are slower, and slow men die in the gorge country.*

She'd tell him. Tomorrow. On the trail. When there was distance behind them and the flats were wide enough to run in.

Outside, the thunder over the gorges rolled closer, and the first fat drops of a false rain began ticking on the tin roof, and Wren Calloway closed her eyes and did the arithmetic she was best at: two days to the river road, four to the ferry crossing, and somewhere behind them, already moving, riders with a list of names.

One of the names was hers.
