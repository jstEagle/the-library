## 4. Downstream Towns

The river came back to them on the third morning, thin as a rope dragged through sand.

Juniper had been following it since before dawn, Wren a half-step behind him with her hands bound at the front—a concession he'd made somewhere around the second day and hadn't examined too closely. She walked like a woman out for a stroll, which annoyed him, because he knew she was cataloguing everything: the lie of the land, the depth of the arroyos, the distance between his hip and his holster.

"You keep looking at my gun," he said.

"I keep looking at the horizon," she said. "Your gun just happens to be in it."

The water in the channel was wrong. He'd known rivers all his life—had grown up on one, before the drought years took his family's farm and gave him to the territory instead—and a river had a sound when it was healthy, a low continuous conversation between current and stone. This one whispered. It moved shallow over its own gravel bed, and where it pooled against the cutbanks it left a residue he didn't like: not silt, but something finer, faintly iridescent, like the sheen on a dead fish's eye.

He knelt at the bank and didn't touch it.

"Problem?" Wren asked.

"Maybe." He stood, wiped his hands on his thighs even though they were clean. "How far to Dry Hollow?"

"Day and a half, if you trust the maps."

"I don't trust anything anymore."

"That's very dramatic," she said. "For a lawman."

They made the town by late afternoon of the second day, when the light had gone the color of old brass and the heat was finally losing its argument with the earth. Dry Hollow sat in a bend of the river like something that had tried to climb out and given up. Juniper had passed through it twice in ten years—once chasing a horse thief, once escorting a prisoner—and both times it had been a hard, plain, working settlement: adobe and timber, windmills turning, laundry strung between posts, the particular noise a town makes when everyone in it is busy surviving.

This time he heard the quiet first.

No children shouting. That was the thing that stopped him at the edge of town, hand drifting to his badge out of habit though there was nothing to show it to. A river town without children's noise was like a body without breath. The windmills turned. A dog watched them from under a wagon with the flat patience of an animal that has learned not to expect much.

Wren felt it too. Her swagger had gone somewhere; she walked close to him now, and her voice, when it came, was low. "Plague?"

"No," Juniper said, because he could already smell it—the water smell, that fine mineral sweetness, concentrated here the way it hadn't been upstream. "Something worse than plague. Plague doesn't come from anywhere. This does."

* * *

The town's only doctor was a woman named Adah Reyes who worked out of a room behind the dry goods store, and when Juniper pushed open her door she looked up from a cot with the eyes of someone who has been awake for days and has stopped pretending otherwise.

"Ranger," she said, reading the badge before he said a word. "If you've come about the well poisoning, arrest whoever did it. If you've come about anything else, get out."

"The river," Juniper said. "It's poisoned upstream."

She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the creek beds. "Upstream. Yes. Everything's upstream. I told the territorial office that eight months ago. I wrote them letters. I sent samples wrapped in wax and salt. You know what came back?" She held up a slip of paper. "A form. Acknowledgment of receipt. Signed by the magistrate's office in Corium. Magistrate Hale sends his regards and assures me the matter is under investigation."

Her name landed in Juniper's chest like a dropped stone. *Corven Hale.* The man who had signed his pardon ten years ago, whose signature sat in Juniper's file like a brand. The man Elias Marsh had died cursing.

"Tell me what you're seeing," Juniper said quietly.

Adah Reyes led him down a corridor of cots.

There were nineteen of them, and most of them held children. Small bodies laid out straight as felled timber, some shivering under blankets in the heat, some burning, some gone gray and still in the way that meant the fight had mostly left them. A girl of maybe seven lay nearest the door, and as Juniper passed she opened her eyes—huge, dark, unnaturally clear, the clarity of fever—and looked at his badge for a long moment.

"Mister," she said, "are you here to take somebody's horse?"

He couldn't answer. She closed her eyes again.

"They drink the water," Adah said, walking beside him, her voice flat with the effort of keeping it flat. "That's all they did wrong. They drank the water their fathers dug wells for and their mothers boiled and prayed over. It comes down from the headwaters carrying tailings—arsenic compounds, I think, something from mining work, though I'm no assayer. It builds up slow. First the stomachs. Then the skin. Then the nerves. The little ones go fastest because they're small and the poison isn't." She stopped at the end of the row. "I've buried eleven this year. Eleven. In a town of two hundred people. Do you understand what that arithmetic does to a place? People have started leaving. The ones who stay are the ones too poor or too stubborn, and every week I do the math again and the math gets worse."

Juniper stood among the cots and felt something shift in him that would never shift back.

Ten years. Ten years he had ridden these flats measuring himself in miles, proud of his clean record, proud of the warrant book in his saddlebag with its neat columns of names—horse thieves, claim jumpers, a man who stole a church bell. He had tracked Wren Calloway across forty miles of hardpan for the crime of lifting a letter. He had been, by every measure the territory used, a good ranger. Diligent. Relentless. Forgotten, which was how he'd wanted it.

And the whole time, murder had been flowing past him in the water. Not one killing but thousands of small ones, distributed so evenly across so many towns that no single death ever looked like a crime. Just sickness. Just bad luck. Just the desert being the desert. Somebody upstream had understood that perfectly—that if you poison a river slowly enough, the territory itself becomes your accomplice, and the deaths get filed under weather.

Elias Marsh hadn't stumbled into poison. He'd been silenced with it, the same weapon, the same hand. And Juniper had knelt beside the dying man and taken his map and made his promise, and part of him—some cold professional part—had still been thinking of it as a case. Evidence. A thread to pull.

Looking at the girl on the cot, he understood that it had never been a case. It was a debt, and the interest was running.

"Who owns the claims upstream?" he asked. "Above the gorge. Who's got rights up there?"

Adah's mouth twisted. "Nobody, officially. The headwater country was closed off years ago—territorial reserve, protected watershed, all very official. Nobody's allowed up there." She paused, and something flickered behind her exhaustion. "Which is why it's strange that a survey crew came through last spring buying supplies. Twenty men, mule train, drilling equipment. Headed up into the reserve like it was Sunday picnic. When I asked who authorized it, they showed me papers with a seal on them." Her eyes found his. "Magistrate's seal."

* * *

They camped that night in the dry wash outside town because Dry Hollow had no rooms to spare and no food to sell strangers, and Juniper sat up long after Wren slept, feeding the fire stick by stick, doing his own arithmetic.

A decade of service, and what had it purchased? He had returned stolen horses to men who beat them. He had dragged a boy to jail for lifting flour while the flour merchant watered his grain and cheated widows openly. He had worn a badge that meant, in practice, that the small crimes got punished and the large ones got paperwork. Somewhere in Corium, a man with a magistrate's seal had figured out that a ranger like Juniper Vane wasn't a threat—he was furniture. Part of the scenery of order. Useful for keeping the desperate in line while the powerful did the real stealing, upriver, out of sight, behind a wall of official silence.

And Sera. God help him, Sera, who believed in the law with her whole scarred heart, who would come for him when word spread that he'd gone rogue with a thief and a dead man's map—and she wouldn't know, couldn't know, that the law she served had been hollowed out from the top like a rotten cottonwood. When she caught him, she'd be doing her duty. That was the cruelest part. Hale had built a machine where duty itself pointed the wrong way.

The fire popped. Across the coals, Wren stirred, and he realized she'd been awake longer than he thought.

"You've got a face," she said sleepily, "like a man redoing ten years of books."

"Go back to sleep."

"Can't. Thinking." She rolled onto her side, wrists still bound, and looked at him across the flames. "You saw the kids."

"Yes."

"And now you're wondering what else you didn't see. All those years. All those miles." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "I'll tell you something about thieves, Ranger. We're honest about what we are. We take things, we know we took them, we run. It's the respectable ones you want to watch. Nobody ever stole a river and called it theft. They call it development. They call it water rights. They call it progress, and they get a seal on the paper, and everybody tips their hat." She settled back down. "Your dying prospector knew that. That's why he picked you. Not because you're fast. Because you're the kind of man who counts."

Juniper fed another stick to the fire and watched it catch.

In the morning he unbound her wrists. He didn't announce it, didn't make a speech; he simply cut the cord while she was saddling up, and she flexed her hands and looked at him with one eyebrow raised, waiting.

"Don't make me regret it," was all he said.

"No promises," she said, and grinned, and for the first time since the waystation the grin reached her eyes.

They rode out as the sun cleared the rimrock, heading upstream, toward the gorge country, toward whatever waited at the source of the poisoned water. Behind them Dry Hollow's windmills kept turning, patient as ever, grinding at air.

They had made perhaps three miles when Juniper reined in.

Dust on the back trail. A single rider, moving fast, coming down out of the north with the easy seat and relentless pace of someone who had ridden territory police routes for years and knew exactly how much ground a horse could give in a day.

Juniper knew that seat. He'd ridden behind it for six years, shared campfires with it, trusted it with his life more than once.

"Sera," he said quietly.

Wren shaded her eyes. "Friend of yours?"

"Worse," Juniper said, and turned his horse off the trail into the rocks. "My partner."
