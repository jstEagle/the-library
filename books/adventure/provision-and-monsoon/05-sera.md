## 5. Sera

The warrant had Juniper Vane's name on it, and Sera Quill read it four times before the ink changed.

She sat in the magistrate's office in Corven Hale's courthouse at Riverbend, the paper flat under her palm, the ceiling fan turning its slow useless circles above her. Outside, the town went about its morning—water carts rattling, a woman beating dust from a rug, children running the gutter where the last of the spring runoff still trickled brown and thin. A river town pretending it still had a river.

"Read it again if you like," said the clerk, a soft man named Pell with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that never quite met hers. "It's straightforward. Theft of correspondence, obstruction, and now flight from questioning. He was seen at Dry Hollow two days past. With a woman. The thief, most likely—the Calloway girl."

Sera looked at the signature at the bottom. Magistrate Corven Hale's hand was elegant, unhurried, the letters leaning forward as if eager to be done with the business. She had seen that handwriting on commendations, on land grants, on the pardon she'd helped arrange ten years ago for a young ranger who'd made one mistake and paid for it with half his life.

"He didn't strike me as a thief," she said.

Pell smiled the way men smile when they've been told to smile. "People change, Deputy Quill. Ten years is ten years." He slid a second paper across the desk. "The Magistrate asks that you handle this personally. You know his habits. His routes. Where he'd hide."

*Where he'd hide.* As if Juniper Vane were a rabbit and she knew every warren.

"I'll need provisions," she said. "And a fast horse."

"You have both by noon."

She stood, folded the warrant once along its crease, and felt it against her ribs all the way down the courthouse steps, through the saddle room, out past the water tower where the town's whole future hung in a tin tank that caught rain when rain bothered to come. She told herself the tightness in her chest was just the heat. She told herself a lot of things that day.

* * *

She picked up his trail at Dry Hollow because of course he'd gone to Dry Hollow. Juniper had always been drawn to the places that hurt worst, like a tongue to a broken tooth.

The settlement sat in a shallow bowl of cracked earth forty miles off the river road, and even from the ridge above it Sera could see something wrong. Too few people moving. Too many doors shut against the sun. She rode down anyway, warrant or no warrant, because a deputy who wouldn't look at a sick town wasn't worth her badge.

The well at the center of Dry Hollow had a fence around it now, and a sign: **DO NOT DRINK**. The letters were fresh. The fence was not. Children had been drinking from it for weeks before anyone thought to mark it.

An old woman named Ada Tull—Sera remembered her from a land dispute three summers back, sharp-tongued and generous—sat on her porch shelling beans that looked gray as river stones.

"Sera Quill," Ada said. "Come to count us."

"Come to ask questions. There's sickness here?"

"There's dying here," Ada said, without drama, which made it worse. "The little ones first. Fevers, then the gut cramps, then they stop wanting to eat, and after that—" She snapped a bean pod with her thumb. "Doctor came up from Bendwater. Said it's in the water. Said somebody put something in the water."

"Who?"

Ada looked at her for a long moment, and Sera saw the calculation happen behind her eyes—the same calculation Sera herself was making, the slow arithmetic of how much truth a person could afford.

"You're wearing the Magistrate's badge," Ada said.

"I'm wearing my own badge."

"They're the same thing now. Haven't you noticed?" But she softened, because she was Ada Tull, and kindness was a habit she couldn't break. "There was a ranger through here two days ago. Tall, quiet, looks at everything like he's memorizing it for later. And a girl with him, quick as a whip. They asked about the well. They asked about Elias Marsh—old prospector, passed through here poisoned near to death about three weeks back. Then they went north. Toward the high country."

"The source," Sera said, before she could stop herself.

Ada's eyebrows rose. "Nobody goes to the source. That's Magistrate land. Closed by order, what is it now, six years?" She leaned forward. "You want my advice, Deputy—and you don't, but I'm giving it—ask yourself why a man would close the headwaters of a public river. Ask yourself what needs hiding up there so badly it's worth posting riders around it. And ask yourself why your Magistrate sent *you*, of all people, after *him*."

Sera didn't answer. She bought water from the good cistern, filled her skins, and rode out of Dry Hollow with Ada's words riding behind her like a second passenger.

By nightfall she'd found the campfire remains—two bedrolls, one small, coffee grounds, horse sign heading northwest into the broken country below the salt flats. She could take them tomorrow. She was a day behind and gaining; Juniper traveled careful but not fast, not with a stranger along.

She made her own fire small and sat beside it working the whetstone along her knife, and she let herself remember, because there was no one to see her do it.

Ten years ago, Juniper Vane had been the best partner she ever had or ever expected to have. Twenty-six years old, already famous in the territory for tracking a horse thief across ninety miles of alkali flat with no water and coming back alive. He'd been funny in a dry, sideways way. He'd shared his tobacco and never his doubts. And then the Kestrel job went wrong—a payroll robbery, a shot fired in panic, a clerk dead on the floor of his own counting house—and Juniper, who had been half a mile away buying nails, took the blame for a crime committed by a man whose name Sera never learned, because the case closed too fast and too clean.

He'd confessed. That was the part she'd never understood, the splinter under her skin for a decade. Juniper Vane did not confess to things he didn't do. Except he had. And she'd believed it—or she'd believed the paperwork, which amounted to the same thing—and she'd stood in the courtroom while they stripped his rank, and afterward she'd been the one who petitioned for leniency, who got the sentence commuted to service, who arranged the pardon that let him keep a badge of a lesser kind and spend ten years chasing chicken thieves in the empty miles.

She'd thought she was saving him. Standing in the magistrate's office this morning, watching Hale's clerk smile his soft smile, she'd felt for the first time the other possibility: that the pardon hadn't been mercy at all. That it had been a leash. That someone had wanted Juniper Vane exactly where he was—far away, harmless, grateful, invisible—chasing petty thieves across the flats while real crimes walked free upstream.

She turned the knife over in her hands and did not sleep much.

* * *

She caught them on the third night, at the edge of the canyon country where the map of the territory went vague and the stars did most of the navigating.

She'd done it deliberately—waited until full dark, approached downwind, dismounted a quarter mile out and walked the last stretch in her socks with her boots slung around her neck, an old trick Juniper himself had taught her. Their fire was low, banked for concealment, and the two of them sat close together over it, heads bent toward something between them. Not touching. But close the way people get when they've been saving each other's lives for a while.

Juniper heard her at thirty feet. Of course he did. He didn't reach for his rifle; he simply lifted his head, the way a man lifts his face to rain, and said quietly, "That's far enough, Sera."

She stepped into the firelight with her hands visible and her sidearm holstered and the warrant in her breast pocket like a coal.

"Hello, Juniper."

Wren Calloway came up off her bedroll like a cat, a knife appearing in her hand from somewhere impossible, and Juniper said, "Easy," and the girl froze, glaring at Sera with a hatred that was almost impressive in its completeness.

"She's the law," Wren said.

"She's my former partner," Juniper said. "Which is different."

"Is it?"

Neither of them answered that, and the silence answered for them.

Sera lowered herself onto a rock across the fire, boots back on, and laid her rifle on the ground beside her—not surrendered, but set aside, which in their old language meant *let's talk first*. "I have a warrant," she said. "Theft of correspondence. Obstruction. Flight. Signed by Hale himself. He wants you brought to Riverbend personally, by me."

"And you came all this way to do it."

"I came all this way to look at you first." She fed a stick into the fire. "Dry Hollow, Juniper. The children. Did you see them?"

Something moved behind his eyes, some door opening onto a room she suspected he kept locked. "I saw them."

"Elias Marsh. Poisoned. The well at Dry Hollow fouled on purpose. And the man who signed my warrant wants me to bring you in for stealing a letter." She watched him carefully. "What was in the letter?"

Juniper glanced at Wren. It was a small glance, quick, but Sera had spent six years reading that face across campfires and stakeouts and long bad roads, and she saw everything in it: the question, the trust, the decision.

"Hale's name," Juniper said. "In Marsh's hand. Hale's been damming the headwaters. Buying the water rights through judges he owns. And poisoning the towns that won't sell." He said it flatly, factually, the way he'd report weather. "Marsh traded me a map to the source for a promise. I carry the truth downstream or die trying."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a coyote laughed at all of them.

"That's a hell of a story," Sera said.

"It's not a story. It's arithmetic. Same as always." He leaned forward. "You've seen Dry Hollow. You've seen the well. Tell me the numbers don't add up."

They added up. God help her, they added up perfectly, and she'd known it since Ada Tull's porch, since the fresh paint on the warning sign over a fence that should have been built months earlier, since the elegant forward-leaning signature on a warrant that charged a man with theft while murder went uninvestigated.

"If it's true," Sera said slowly, "then the right move is to come back with me. Make the case properly. Testimony, evidence, a hearing—"

"A hearing before whose judges?" Wren said sharply. "Hers? The ones he bought? Wake up, Deputy. The law is the weapon. That's the whole point."

"Wren." Juniper's voice was quiet but it landed like a hand on a shoulder. Then, to Sera: "She's not wrong. And you know she's not wrong, or you'd have arrested me at thirty feet instead of walking into our fire with your rifle on the ground."

Sera looked at the rifle. Her own doing. Her own body deciding before her mind caught up, the way it had decided a hundred times in their old partnership—*cover me, watch my left, I trust you with my life*—and she hated it, hated that ten years and a ruined career and a warrant in her pocket hadn't burned that instinct out of her.

"I can't just let you go," she said.

"I know."

"There are riders. Hale's men. At least two patrols on the roads north of here—I crossed their sign yesterday. If I report your position, you're dead or caged before noon."

"If you report my position."

"Yes," she said. "If."

The word sat in the fire between them, smoking.

Juniper studied her across the flames, and she let him, because she deserved to be studied. "Why did you really take this assignment, Sera?"

Because Hale asked for me by name. Because he knows what I am to you and thinks that makes me useful. Because maybe he thinks I'll bring you in, and maybe he thinks I'll bury you in a canyon and call it escape, and either way he wins. Because I need to see with my own eyes whether the boy I knew is still in there somewhere, or whether I spent ten years polishing the legend of a coward.

What she said was: "Duty."

"Liar," said Wren Calloway, and for once Sera didn't disagree with her.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Neither did they, quite—all three of them lying in the dark around dead coals, listening to each other breathe, three people pretending the night wasn't a decision.

Near dawn, Juniper rose and came and sat beside her at the edge of the camp, where the canyon country opened below them, black ridgelines running north like frozen waves.

"You could ride back and tell them you lost me," he said.

"I don't lie in reports."

"You lied in one ten years ago. Or signed one. Whichever." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow crueler. "Somebody closed the Kestrel job awful fast, Sera. Somebody wanted it closed. I've had ten years of empty miles to think about who that might have been, and lately—" He stopped. Started again. "Lately I've been wondering if the pardon that saved me was the same hand that buried me."

The sky in the east had gone the color of an old scar. Below them, the world waited to be lit.

"I followed you to bring you in," Sera said.

"I know."

"So don't make me regret what I'm about to do instead."

He looked at her then, fully, and it was the old look, the one from before, the one that said *I know what this costs you*. "Thank you," he said, and she turned her face away because gratitude from Juniper Vane had always been the hardest thing in the world to bear.

She gave them until full light. Then she mounted, turned her horse south, and rode a mile before she stopped on a rise and looked back. Two figures were small against the northern ridgeline, moving fast, a dark thread stitching itself toward the forbidden country. Toward the source. Toward whatever Hale had sealed up there with riders and silence and six years of closed orders.

She should follow. She should report. Every hour she didn't was another hour she was spending her oath like stolen money, and she could feel the debt accumulating, interest compounding, duty tightening around her chest like a rope paying out one coil at a time until one day it would pull taut and there would be no slack left anywhere.

Instead she took out the warrant, unfolded it, and read Juniper Vane's name one more time in the growing light.

Then she turned her horse north and followed the thread.

Behind her, far to the south, she imagined she could hear the courthouse clock in Riverbend striking seven, and beneath it, fainter, the sound of Magistrate Corven Hale learning that his deputy had chosen—and the particular quality of silence a dangerous man makes when he begins, very quietly, to plan.
