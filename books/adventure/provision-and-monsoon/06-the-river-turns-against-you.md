## 6. The River Turns Against You

The map did not lie, but it did not tell the whole truth either. Elias Marsh had drawn his lines in a prospector's hand—confident, economical, indifferent to anything that wasn't ore or water—and where he wrote *follow the dry wash north to the split rock*, he neglected to mention that the wash was only dry because it was waiting.

Juniper learned that on the third day upstream, in the hour before dawn, when the air went strange and heavy and the birds stopped singing all at once, like someone had shut a door on them.

He was on first watch. He'd taken every first watch since Dry Hollow, partly because he slept badly anyway and partly because he didn't trust himself to dream about the children with the gray lips and the swollen bellies and wake up able to look at Wren Calloway sleeping ten feet away without something in his chest turning over. So he sat with his back against a boulder the color of old rust, the map open on his knee even though he'd memorized it twice over, and he watched the sky do nothing at all, and then he watched it do everything.

The storm came out of the western mesas like something released rather than arrived. No wind first—that was the wrong part, the part that made the hair on his arms lift. In the flats, storms announced themselves for an hour, dust running ahead of the rain like outriders. This one came silent, a wall of bruised purple stacked against the stars, and beneath it he heard—not thunder, not yet—a sound like a long exhalation from the throat of the mountains.

"Get up," he said.

Wren was awake before he finished the sentence. Ten years of thieving had given her the same animal reflexes his decade of tracking had given him; she came off her bedroll with her knife already in her hand, blinking at the sky, and he saw the moment she understood, because her face went the particular pale it went when she was frightened and deciding not to show it.

"That's no rainstorm," she said.

"No."

Sera Quill was slower to wake but faster to move once she understood—she'd been a ranger too, before the warrant and the badge and whatever else lived between them now. She was rolling the canvas within thirty seconds, working in the dark without wasted motion, and when she spoke her voice had the flat calm he remembered from bad nights on patrol, the voice she'd used on flooded crossings and downed horses and once, memorably, on him.

"How high does this wash run?"

"Higher than us." He was already scanning the walls of the arroyo they'd camped in—the map's *dry wash* was forty feet deep here, walled in slickrock, beautiful in the evening light and now a stone throat waiting to swallow. "We need the rim. Now."

They climbed. That was the whole of the next four minutes: climbing, in the dark, with gear strapped wrong and Sera's lantern guttering, up a slope of broken talus that shifted under their boots like it resented them. Juniper went last, because that was the arithmetic he'd settled into somewhere between Dry Hollow and here—himself as the rearguard, the man who took the risk coming from behind. He'd spent ten years walking behind people, watching their backs, telling himself it was caution. He was starting to suspect it was the only way he knew how to be near anyone.

He was twenty feet below the rim when the river arrived.

There was no gradual rising. There was a sound like the world tearing lengthwise, and then the wash below him turned white and vertical, a wall of water and rock and torn-up mesquite moving faster than any horse he'd ever ridden, filling the arroyo floor to its brim in what couldn't have been a minute. His bedroll was gone. The spare water skins were gone. The mule—they'd bought the mule in Dry Hollow, a mean little dun with a talent for standing still—was gone, and Juniper heard its cry cut short and made himself not think about it.

Above him, hands reached down. Two hands. He took both.

They hauled him over the lip and the three of them lay on the slickrock gasping while the flood roared past below, carrying boulders the size of wagons with a grinding boom that came through the stone itself, up through Juniper's ribs, into his teeth. Lightning now, finally, sheeting the canyon in white so that for half a second at a time he could see the river that hadn't existed an hour ago, brown and violent and enormous, eating the banks in bites.

"The supplies," Sera said.

"Gone."

"The food?"

"Gone with it."

Wren said nothing. She was lying on her stomach at the rim's edge, watching the water with an expression Juniper couldn't read in the strobing light, and after a while he realized it wasn't fear. It was recognition. Somewhere downstream, in the towns along the river, this same water would arrive tomorrow or the next day, and it would arrive carrying silt and sediment and whatever else the mountains gave it, and children would drink it, and—

"We're lucky," Wren said quietly. "Dry Hollow got three days' warning. These towns up-canyon get none."

Juniper looked at her. She didn't look back. The lightning came again and showed him her jaw set hard, and he thought: *she stole a letter, and she has been carrying it the way I've been carrying the map.* He didn't say anything. There was a time for saying things, and the edge of a flood canyon at midnight was not it.

* * *

Dawn found them inventorying ruin.

It was worse in the light, the way most things are. The flood had taken the bulk of their food, both spare canteens, the tent canvas, Sera's rifle ammunition (soaked, though she was drying it page by page like a woman nursing a sick child), and—Juniper felt this loss in his stomach more than the others—the map's oilcloth case had been on his hip, but the tin of matches had been in the panniers, and the panniers were somewhere downstream doing violence to the canyon.

What remained: one rifle, two pistols, Wren's knives, a bag of jerky that had been lashed high, a flint and steel in Juniper's pocket because he'd never trusted matches anyway, and the map itself, which Elias Marsh had drawn in ink that had clearly been chosen by a man who expected his documents to be rained on.

"He was careful," Juniper said, turning the map over for the fourth time. "Everything important, he drew twice. Look—here, and again here. Same lines."

"Paranoid, you mean," Sera said. But she was looking at the duplicate markings too, and her mouth did the thing it did when she was revising an opinion downward into respect.

"It's not paranoia if someone's actually trying to kill you." Wren said it lightly, around a strip of jerky, but nobody laughed, because it wasn't a joke. It was the premise of all their lives now.

They walked. The map said the wash became a gorge became a slot canyon became, eventually, the terraced country below the source, and the map was right about the shape of things if nothing else. But the land had changed overnight. Trails Juniper had noted on the way up were erased—scoured down to raw rock, cairns swept away, a stretch of petroglyph-covered wall that Wren had admired two days ago now tumbled into the channel in slabs. The desert had shown them, in one night, how temporary all their marks were. How temporary *they* were.

By midday the sun came out with the vicious clarity the high desert kept in reserve after storms, and the heat rose off the wet stone in visible waves, and their water situation went from bad to arithmetic. One full skin each, half a skin spare. At a ranger's pace, two days to the next seep marked on the map. At their pace, wounded by lost gear and hauling themselves over flood wreckage, three. Maybe four.

"We ration," Sera said. It wasn't a suggestion. She'd gone back into ranger cadence somewhere in the night and hadn't come out of it, and Juniper recognized the armor for what it was. Duty tightening like a rope. She'd let them go once, back on the flats—one night, one lapse, one look across a fire that had lasted too long—and she'd regretted it every mile since, and now she was going to be the most lawful fugitive in the territory until she decided otherwise.

"We also move faster," Wren said. "I know these canyons. Not this exact one, but this kind. There are shortcuts the flood just opened for us—new breaks in the walls, scoured chutes. If we take them we make the seep by tomorrow night."

"If we take them we die in one of them," Sera said. "A chute that runs water once runs it again. You want to be inside the mountain when it decides to?"

"I want to not be dead of thirst on Thursday," Wren said. "Different priorities."

They both looked at Juniper. He hated that. He hated more that some tired, buried part of him had gotten used to it in six days—the way two people who should have been enemies had somehow arranged themselves on either side of him like the two pans of a scale, waiting for him to tip.

He looked at the canyon. He looked at the map. He thought about Elias Marsh dying in a waystation bunk with poison in his blood and a lifetime of prospecting in his eyes, trading the only thing he had left for a stranger's promise, and he thought: *the land is not against us. The land doesn't know we're here. The land is just being what Hale's money made it—a thing diverted, dammed, poisoned, pushed out of its own course. We're walking through the wound.*

"Shortcuts," he said. "But we scout each one before we commit. Nobody enters a chute blind. And we tie together through the narrows."

Wren grinned—the quick, crooked grin that had disarmed half the merchants in the territory and infuriated the other half. "Look at him. Ten years alone and he still remembers how teams work."

"I never forgot," Juniper said. "I just stopped having one."

He said it without meaning to say it aloud, and the silence afterward had a texture he could feel, and Sera looked away first, and Wren's grin faded into something quieter and more dangerous, and they walked on.

* * *

The second chute nearly killed her.

It was near dusk, a slot no wider than a wagon, polished walls funnelling down toward a lower terrace, and it looked exactly like what it was—a shortcut the flood had carved and abandoned. Juniper went first, testing footholds, calling back the turns. Sera followed. Wren came last, and she was halfway down when the sky, which had been innocently blue for eighteen hours, remembered itself.

No rain this time. Just the sound—that long exhalation from the mountains, translated through forty miles of stone into a vibration in the rock under Juniper's palms. He'd have missed it a week ago. He'd never miss it again.

"Water!" he shouted. "Climb! Up, not down!"

The problem with slots is there is no up. The walls were sheer, slick with flood polish, offering handholds the size of coins. Below Juniper, Sera pressed herself into a notch. Above him, Wren froze—he could see it happen, the thief's instinct to go still, to become invisible, colliding head-on with the fact that invisibility was about to be fatal.

"Wren." He made his voice the voice he used on spooked horses and green deputies. Flat. Certain. "Look at me. There's a ledge behind you, left side, two moves up. Don't think about the water. Think about the ledge."

"There is no ledge—" But she'd turned, and there was, a flake of sandstone jutting like a shelf, and she moved toward it because he'd told her to, and that fact lodged in him somewhere permanent.

The water came as a sound first, then a brown fist around the bend below, punching upward through the slot, and the whole canyon became a pipe with a heartbeat. Juniper got one boot onto the ledge beside Wren and got an arm around her and pressed them both into a hollow above it as the surge hit their legs to the thigh and tried, with the patient idiot strength of moving water, to peel them off the wall.

She screamed. He didn't blame her. The cold was total, the force was total, and the noise was the noise of the world ending in a stone corridor. He got his free hand into a crack and locked his wrist and held, and held, and felt her grip on his jacket tear fabric, and put his other arm across her chest and pinned her to him and to the rock, and shouted into her ear the only useful thing left:

"I've got you. I've got you. Hold."

The surge passed. Water in a slot doesn't pass so much as diminish—it fell from thigh to knee to ankle over the course of a hundred heartbeats, each one individually counted, and then it was roaring away below them and the slot was draining and the sun was setting gold and ordinary on the rim far above, as if none of it had happened.

Wren was shaking. So was he; he could feel it in the arm still locked across her, and he didn't let go right away, and she didn't pull away right away either, and for maybe ten seconds the two of them stood in a drained chute plastered in mud with their hearts hammering against each other through soaked clothes, and it was the least alone Juniper Vane had felt in ten years.

"You let go when you're ready," he said.

"I'm ready," she said, and didn't move, and then laughed—a ragged, real laugh—and moved.

Below, Sera's voice came up the chute, tight with a fear she'd deny to her grave: "Report. Both of you. Now."

"Alive," Wren called back. "Soaked. Your ranger's got strong arms."

"He's not my ranger," Sera said, and the words came out wrong, too fast, with history in them, and Juniper closed his eyes briefly and climbed down through the dripping dark toward a conversation none of them were ready to have.

* * *

They made the seep after full dark, wrung out and half-frozen and laughing at intervals in the unhinged way of people who have recently almost died. The seep was a damp streak on a shaded wall feeding a pool the size of a dinner plate, and they filled every container they had left and drank like animals and built a fire from Wren's emergency kit—which she produced from a hidden pocket with theatrical smugness—and ate the last of the jerky and did not talk about tomorrow.

Later, when Sera had taken second watch and Wren lay wrapped in the single blanket they'd saved, drying by degrees, Juniper sat apart with the map and did sums he didn't like. Three days' food gone. Four days to the terraces, if the land allowed. After that, the high country, where the map's notes grew sparse and strange—Elias's handwriting shrinking, crowding, as if the man had been writing faster the closer he got to the truth.

Wren's voice came softly out of the dark. "You counted us out today. Back in the chute. I saw your face."

"I count everything. It's how I stay alive."

"What was the number?"

He was quiet a while. The fire ticked. Somewhere above, water dripped from the day's storm, keeping time.

"Fifty-fifty," he admitted. "Before the chute. Better now."

"Why better? We lost everything."

Juniper looked at her—at the thief he'd arrested, who had bargained her freedom into a partnership; at the outline of her against the firelight, mud-streaked and shivering and utterly unbroken—and heard himself say the true thing, the thing ten years of miles had taught him and ten years of silence had buried:

"Because the land keeps trying to kill us," he said, "and we keep refusing. That's the first team I've been on in a decade that I'd bet on."

In the morning, they broke camp and climbed toward the terraces, and before noon they found the first thing that hadn't come from the flood or the map or the mountains at all: hoofprints, fresh, shod, four riders' worth, cutting upstream ahead of them toward the source.

Riders who had not been slowed by any storm.

Juniper knelt over the sign a long time, and when he stood, his face had gone ranger-flat, and Wren, watching him, asked the question he was already answering in his head.

"How far ahead?"

"Half a day," he said. "And they're not hurrying. They know exactly where they're going."
