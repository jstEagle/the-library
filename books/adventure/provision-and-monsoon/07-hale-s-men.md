## 7. Hale's Men

The river at the crossing was the color of old rope.

Wren Calloway had seen a hundred fords in her years of taking what wasn't nailed down, and she'd learned to read water the way other people read faces. This one lied. It moved slow and brown and patient between banks of cracked clay, looking like something you could wade, but the midstream current ran deep and cold off the mountains, and it would take a horse's legs out from under it before the animal knew what happened. That was why the ferry existed. That was why Old Tam existed, sitting on his mooring post with a pipe going cold in his fist, watching three riders come down the east bank like he'd been expecting them since the last monsoon.

"You'll want the flat-bottom," he said, before any of them spoke. "Not for you." He nodded at the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against Juniper's saddle. "For those."

Juniper didn't answer right away. He'd been quiet all morning—quiet in a way Wren had come to distrust, because she knew quiet. Quiet was what people did when they were counting something. She'd spent two weeks on the trail with the ranger who'd caught her with a letter in her hand, and she'd mapped him the way she mapped a house before a job: the long silences, the way he checked the horizon not like a man looking for weather but like a man looking for witnesses, the mile markers he carved at every crossroads with a knife worn thin as a promise. He measured the world in miles because miles couldn't disappoint you. People could. She understood that better than he'd ever guess.

"How much?" Juniper asked now.

Tam looked at him over the cold pipe. "For the crossing? Two bits apiece. For carrying what you're carrying—" he spat into the water, "—nothing. Some things you don't charge for. You just do them, and then you're done."

Sera Quill sat her horse apart from them, the way she'd done everything since Dry Hollow. The warrant was still folded in her breast pocket; Wren had watched her touch it four times that morning, a small unconscious gesture, like checking a wound to see if it had started bleeding again. She hadn't tried to arrest anyone since the night she'd let them ride out of her camp. But she hadn't burned the paper either. Wren figured Sera Quill was a woman standing on a bridge of her own making, and every hour that passed, more planks fell away behind her.

"Riders," Sera said.

She said it the way you'd say *rain*. No urgency. Just fact, delivered to the air, and then everyone else's stomach dropped while hers stayed level. Wren turned in the saddle and looked back along the east bank, upstream, where the trail came down through a stand of dead cottonwoods stripped white by the wind.

Dust. A lot of it. Not the lazy dust of travelers. The hard, disciplined dust of men riding in formation, holding their horses back to something just short of a gallop because they weren't chasing anything—they were arriving.

"Six," Sera said. "Maybe eight behind the rise. Gray coats. Hale's colors don't have a crest, so they wear gray and let the gray do the talking."

"How far?" Juniper asked.

"Ten minutes if they push. They won't push. They want us to see them coming." She swung down and loosened her rifle in its scabbard without drawing it. "That's the message. Run, or stand, or drown—it's your choice, but they're here either way."

Old Tam hadn't moved. He sat on his mooring post like a piece of the landscape, and when Wren looked at him she saw something in his face she recognized from her father, from every old man she'd ever known who lived alone by water: the particular calm of someone who has already decided how he will die and finds the question boring.

"They came for the ledgers," she said. "Not us. We give them the oilcloth and they'll wave us across like honored guests."

"No," Juniper said.

It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. Wren had heard him use that voice exactly once before—at Dry Hollow, kneeling beside a child with gray lips, promising a mother something he had no right to promise. When Juniper Vane said *no* in that voice, it wasn't refusal. It was arithmetic. Elias Marsh had traded him a map for a promise, and Juniper did his sums the way he did everything: slowly, completely, and only once.

"Hale poisoned three towns," Juniper said. "He killed Elias. He'll kill whoever holds these pages next. There's no version of this where I hand them over and sleep again."

"There's no version of this where we keep them and live," Wren shot back, and heard her own voice crack on *live*, and hated it.

"That's the toll," Juniper said. "Elias told me. Everything true costs."

The riders came down through the cottonwoods.

* * *

There were seven of them, and they rode like men who had never once been in a hurry, which was worse than riding like killers. Killers hurry. These men spread out along the bank in a loose crescent with the ease of long practice, cutting off the downstream trail first, then the upstream one, leaving open only the way they wanted their quarry to look: at the water.

Their captain was a lean man with a sun-scalded face and a voice like gravel poured slow. "Morning," he said. "Magistrate's business. Names and goods."

"Ferryman's crossing," Old Tam said pleasantly, "and none of yours."

The captain looked at him the way you look at furniture. "Old man. Walk your boat to the far bank and wait there. Nobody wants your blood on the ledger today."

"Everybody wants somebody's blood on somebody's ledger," Tam said. "Question is whose, and when, and whether the ink dries." He stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and put two fingers in his mouth, and whistled.

It took Wren a moment to understand what the whistle was for, because nothing answered it. Then she saw the rope. The ferry was tied to a winch on the near bank, but there was a second line—a release line—running up into the rocks above the crossing, anchored somewhere out of sight, and Tam had spent forty years keeping that line clear of silt and sand because a boatman who can't cut his own mooring is just cargo.

"He means to scuttle the ferry," Sera said quietly. "With himself on it, if it comes to that."

"It won't come to that," Juniper said, and Wren heard the lie in it, plain as a counterfeit coin. He was already moving, though—dismounting, pulling the oilcloth bundle from his saddle, splitting the load. Ledgers into his coat. Map into Wren's shirt, against her skin, where sweat would try to eat the ink and fail. "If they take me, ride for the canyon country north. Elias marked a shelter. Don't come back for me."

"Don't be stupid," Wren said.

"I'm being honest. It feels similar."

"It doesn't."

The captain's patience ran out the way patience does with such men—all at once, and loudly. "Take the ranger alive," he called. "The girl and the deputy, whatever's left of them is fine. And burn the boat."

The nearest rider put a torch to the ferry's tarred hull, and Old Tam laughed.

It was a terrible sound, Wren thought later. Not bitter, not afraid. Delighted. Forty years of slow brown water and two-bit crossings, and at the end of it the old man got a moment of pure, undiluted spite, and he savored every drop of it. He hit the release line with an axe he'd kept under the mooring post, and the winch drum spun free, and the ferry began to drift from the bank trailing fire like a comet made of pitch and rope.

And Tam, instead of stepping back, stepped onto it.

"What is he *doing*—" Wren started forward and Juniper's arm caught her—not gently, not cruelly, the way a beam catches a falling roof.

"Buying us the water," Juniper said. His face had gone very still. "Look."

Because Tam wasn't drifting aimlessly. He was poling the burning ferry straight into the deep channel, into the fast cold midstream current, and as it went he was paying out rope—cutting lengths, dropping them, fouling the crossing. Within a minute the ford was strung with floating, burning, tangled line, and the first of Hale's riders who tried to swim his horse across got a length of smoldering hemp wrapped around its forelegs and went down thrashing into the current, screaming at his horse to swim, swim, until the river took the argument out of both of them and handed them, coughing, to the far bank a half-mile down.

The rest of the riders pulled up. Horses won't cross fire on water. Everyone knows this except desperate men, and Hale's men were many things but not yet desperate.

Old Tam stood on his burning deck in the middle of the river, and he looked back at the three of them on the west bank, and he raised his cold pipe to them like a toast.

"The far landing's a mile down!" he shouted over the water. "Follow the clay bluffs! And boy—" this to Juniper, and his voice carried impossibly, the way voices do at the end of things, "—the truth you're hauling. It's heavy. Carry it like it's heavy!"

Then the fire reached the pitch seams, and the deck went up in a single soft roar, and Wren turned away because some things are private even in front of witnesses, and when she turned back the ferry was a black shape sinking in brown water and Old Tam was nowhere on it, and the river moved on, slow and patient, keeping what it kept.

* * *

They walked the horses a mile down the bluffs and crossed at the far landing in silence, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't have been a eulogy, and none of them were ready to write one.

On the west bank, finally, Wren stopped and made herself say it.

"He knew them. He knew who they were the moment he saw the gray coats, and he knew what they'd do to a witness, and he got on the boat anyway." Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was a skill she'd learned young and never been grateful for. "That's the second person dead for Elias Marsh's map. How many is it going to be? Because I did the arithmetic too, Juniper, back in Dry Hollow, and my sum said *one letter, one chance, get paid, get gone.* Your sum keeps adding bodies."

Juniper stood facing the river. The water that had taken Tam slid past his boots, brown and indifferent, already halfway to Dry Hollow, already carrying smoke and story to towns that drank it without knowing what it cost.

"You're right," he said.

Wren blinked. In two weeks she'd heard Juniper Vane argue, deflect, go silent, and once—memorably—deliver a twenty-minute lecture on the ethics of mile markers. She had never heard him simply agree.

"You're right, and I've been lying to both of us," he went on, still facing the water. "I've been calling this a job. A delivery. Truth, downstream, like freight. Freight doesn't get people killed. Freight doesn't make old men light their own boats." He turned, and Wren saw that whatever had been sealed behind his face since the day she met him—the ghost-with-a-badge calm, the competence-as-armor—had cracked, and what showed through wasn't weakness. It was fury, banked and patient, the kind that lasts. "Elias died holding my hand and calling me an honest man. Tam died buying us an hour. Every mile from here, someone pays for this promise, and I'm done pretending the bill isn't mine."

"So what changes?" Sera asked. She'd dismounted and stood with her reins loose in her hand, and Wren noticed—for the first time—that the warrant was no longer in her breast pocket. It was in her hand. Unfolded. Creased soft from handling.

"Speed changes," Juniper said. "We stop camping. We stop being findable. We ride the high route through the canyon country, day and night, and we reach the source before Hale's next crew reaches us." He looked at Sera, and his voice gentled by a degree. "And you decide what that paper is. Leash or warrant. Today, before the canyons. Because once we're in the gorge country, there's no ferry back."

Sera looked down at the paper. The wind off the river lifted its corner, and she pressed it flat with her thumb, and for a long moment she was perfectly still, a woman weighing two kinds of drowning.

Behind them, upstream, the gray riders had found the far landing and were wheeling their horses, and the dust of them rose against the afternoon sun like a wall coming on.

"We ride now," Sera said, folding the warrant away unburned—but into her saddlebag, Wren noted, not her pocket. "Argue on horseback."

They swung up. The clay bluffs waited, and beyond them the canyon country, and beyond that the passes that the dry season would close like a door.

Wren touched the map against her skin, felt the ink through the fabric, and thought about an old man's last words carried on a burning deck. *Carry it like it's heavy.*

For the first time since she'd stolen that letter, she understood that she was.
