## 10. Dry Season Rising

The salt flats began where the river's memory ended.

Juniper had crossed them twice in ten years of ranging—once chasing a horse thief who'd thought the white expanse a shortcut, once carrying a dead man's body back to his widow. Both times he'd sworn never again. The flats didn't forgive. They didn't even remember you were there. A man could walk all day and leave no more trace than the wind, and the wind erased that by morning.

Three weeks. That was what the map said, measured against the season. Three weeks before the dry season closed the high passes like a fist, and every day they spent on the salt was a day the fist tightened.

He stood at the flats' edge at first light, watching the sun come up over a country so flat it seemed the sky had been laid down and forgotten. Behind him, the last green thread of the river country. Ahead, forty miles of nothing that glittered like broken glass.

"We should have gone around," Sera said beside him. She'd been quiet since the canyon, quieter since she'd burned her warrant, as if the act had taken something out of her she hadn't budgeted for. She looked at the flats the way a woman looks at a debt she can't pay. "South route adds six days but there's water."

"Six days we don't have." Juniper checked the water skins lined up on the pack mule. Eleven. He counted them again, hoping to be wrong. Eleven skins, three people, forty miles, and a spring marked on Elias Marsh's map halfway across that might or might not still run. "We go straight through. Night marches, camp in whatever shade we find."

"And if the spring's dry?"

"Then we learn how thirsty we really are."

Sera didn't laugh. Neither did he. Behind them, Wren was cinching the mule's pack with quick, competent hands, and Juniper watched her without meaning to—the way her braid had come half undone, the salt-crusted hem of her coat, the way she moved like someone who'd decided the world was worth moving through quickly before it changed its mind about her.

She caught him looking. "What?"

"Nothing. Check your boots. The salt gets into everything. Blisters you don't feel until they're infected."

"Yes, mother." But she checked them.

They walked into the white.

* * *

The flats did not care about their schedule.

By the second night the heat had taken its tax from everyone. They marched in the dark when the salt gave back the stars so completely it felt like walking on the sky, and slept through the worst hours wedged under the mule and a scrap of canvas that made shade the way a coin makes wealth—technically, insufficiently. Juniper rationed the water with a ranger's arithmetic: two swallows each at dawn, two at dusk, one at midnight if anyone woke gasping. The mule got what was left, which was never enough.

Wren stopped joking on the third day.

That was how he knew. Wren Calloway joked through everything—through ambushes, through floods, through the boatman's grave they'd dug with their hands at the ferry crossing. Jokes were her way of standing upright. When she went silent, something underneath had already given way.

She stumbled at dusk, caught herself, kept walking. An hour later she stumbled again and this time Juniper caught her arm and held it.

"How long have you been hot?"

"I'm fine."

"You're burning." Her skin under his hand was dry furnace heat, no sweat at all, which was worse than sweat. Heat exhaustion he could treat. This was past that. "When did it start?"

"Last night. Maybe." She tried to pull free and couldn't, and the trying cost her; she sagged against him, and for a moment her forehead rested on his shoulder, and he felt the weight of her whole body trust him before her mind agreed to. "It's just the flats. Everyone's hot."

"Everyone's hot. You're sick." He lowered her to sit on the pack. Her eyes were too bright, pupils wrong, tracking something that wasn't there. "Wren. Look at me. What did you drink?"

"The same as you. The same—" She coughed, and the cough had a wet rattle in it that hadn't been there yesterday. "Maybe I drank from the seep. Back at the canyon mouth. It tasted off, I thought it was the minerals—"

The seep. Juniper closed his eyes for half a second and saw it: brown water welling slow through cracked stone, and himself waving her off from filling his own skin because he'd wanted to push on. His own caution, and her thirst, and now this.

"Sera," he called. "Camp here. Now."

Sera came back down the line fast. One look at Wren and her face did the thing it always did—went hard and professional, feeling pushed down deep where it wouldn't interfere. "Fever's high. How high?"

"Don't know. No thermometer out here. High enough that she's seeing things."

"I'm not seeing things," Wren said with tremendous dignity, and then, to the empty air to her left, "stop laughing."

They made camp in the lee of a salt ridge no higher than a man's knee. Juniper got water into her, small sips, the way you'd coax a fire. He used one of the feverfew packets from the medical kit Elias's cache had included, steeped in the last of the warm water, and held the cup while she drank because her hands shook too badly to hold it herself.

Her hands. He'd watched those hands pick a lock in the dark, throw a knife true at thirty feet, hold steady on the oars during the flood while the river tried to peel her off the seat. Now they trembled around a tin cup like an old woman's, and something in Juniper's chest tore along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"Sleep," he told her. "We'll be here when you wake."

"You can't stop." Her voice came slurred but stubborn. "The passes. Three weeks. You said—"

"The passes will wait for one day."

"They won't." She gripped his wrist with both hands, fever-hot, grip still stronger than it had any right to be. "Juniper. Listen to me. If I slow us down, you put me on the mule and you keep walking. Promise me. That's the deal. That's always been the deal between us—we don't die for each other, we finish the thing."

"That is not the deal."

"It is. It has to be." Her eyes were wet now, and he couldn't tell anymore what was fever and what wasn't. "Because if it isn't, then I'm just another person you'd trade the truth for. And I can't be that. I won't."

He held her gaze and lied to her, which was the second hardest thing he'd done that day. "Fine. Deal. Sleep."

She slept. He sat with his hand on her forehead through the worst of the night, feeling the heat climb and crest and refuse to break, and Sera sat across the tiny fire watching him watch Wren, and said nothing, and the nothing she said was very loud.

* * *

Dawn brought the choice, and the choice was arithmetic, and Juniper Vane had spent ten years being good at arithmetic.

Eleven skins minus what they'd drunk: seven and a half. Distance to the marked spring: twenty-two miles. With Wren riding the mule, feverish, needing water they could barely spare: four days, maybe five, if the spring ran. Without stopping: three days, tight but doable, if the spring ran.

And if they stopped—if they stayed here, or turned back to the canyon seep, or did any of the merciful things—the dry season would close the passes while they were still on the salt, and everything Elias had died carrying, everything the boatman had died covering, everything Sera had burned her name to the ground for would sit sealed behind a wall of winter rock until spring. Hale would win by calendar alone. The towns downstream would drink poison through another year, and the children of Dry Hollow would—

He thought of the children. He thought of Wren's hand gripping his wrist. He thought of Elias Marsh dying by inches in a waystation bunk, trading the only thing he had left for a promise, and the promise had never said anything about this. About choosing. About the price being paid in installments, and the currency being people.

"Ranger." Sera's voice, low, behind him. She'd come up beside him at the salt ridge while Wren slept, and she spoke the way she used to speak over case files at midnight, flat and precise, because precision was how she loved things. "You're doing the math."

"I'm always doing the math."

"What's the answer?"

He looked out at the white. Twenty-two miles of glare. Somewhere out there, a spring that might be a lie drawn by a dying man. Somewhere behind them, a magistrate with riders and patience. Somewhere under the canvas, a thief with a knife-thrower's hands shaking around a cup.

"The answer is I can't carry her and the ledgers and the map and the promise. Not all of it. Something gets left."

"So leave the ledgers. Bury them. Come back for them after."

"After the passes close? Then they're Hale's by default, or the salt's." He shook his head. "No. The evidence travels or nothing does."

"Then leave her."

He turned. Sera met his eyes without flinching, because she was Sera, and she'd earned the right to say the unsayable by being the one who'd say it. "Leave her with water and the mule and my rifle. Ride ahead, reach the source, break the dam, bring help back. She's strong. She's survived worse than a fever."

"She nearly drowned in the flood."

"She didn't drown."

"She died at the ferry crossing and we both know the old man bought her those extra miles." Juniper heard his own voice go rough. "I'm not spending her. Not for speed. Not for the mission. Not even for the towns."

"Even for the children?"

"Even for them." And he meant it, and meaning it frightened him, because it meant the promise had stopped being the largest thing inside him somewhere in the night, without his noticing, and something else had grown larger in its shadow. "That's the difference between me and Hale, Sera. He spends people. I don't. The day I start, I'm just him with a better map."

Sera studied him for a long moment. The wind moved the salt dust between them in low white ghosts.

"You love her," she said. Not a question. Not an accusation either, which was somehow worse.

"I don't know what I—"

"You love her," she said again, patient, terrible, "and you've known it since the flood, and you're only now letting yourself do the math on it. Because loving someone means they can be spent, and you've built ten years on never letting anyone close enough to spend." She glanced toward the canvas shelter. "Well. Congratulations, ranger. You're spendable now. We both are. So decide fast, because she's getting worse, and the sun doesn't care what you feel."

* * *

They carried her.

There was no other answer, once he let himself see it—not speed, not mercy, but the third thing, the hard thing: they would carry her and the ledgers and the promise, all of it, and they would simply hurt more. Juniper rigged a litter from the canvas and the mule's spare pack frame, padded with his own bedroll, and they took turns—two hours hauling, two hours walking guard, swapping at the top of every hour while the salt glared and the horizon refused to come any closer.

Wren surfaced from the fever in fragments. Once she woke and asked for her brother, dead eight years, and Juniper told her he'd be along directly. Once she woke clear-eyed and furious and demanded to walk, and Juniper lied to her again, gently, and hated how good he'd gotten at it. Once, near dusk of the second day, she woke and found him hauling the litter shafts with blood blisters split across both palms, and she went very quiet, and reached up and touched his wrist where she'd gripped it nights ago.

"You didn't leave me," she said.

"No."

"You should have. By your own math."

"My math was wrong." He kept walking. The litter shafts creaked. "I've been measuring in miles for ten years. Turns out miles are the wrong unit."

"For what?"

"For most things."

She laughed—a small, cracked sound, half fever—and slid back under, and her hand stayed on his wrist until gravity took it, and he walked the next two hours with the ghost of that touch burning cooler than the fever ever had.

* * *

The spring was real.

Near noon on the fourth day, when the water was down to one skin and a half and Sera had stopped suggesting they turn back because turning back had stopped being possible, Juniper crested a rise of crusted salt and saw it: a dark stain on the white, a scatter of greasewood, and beneath the greasewood, a pool no bigger than a wagon bed, ringed with old prospectors' stones.

Someone had carved a mark on the largest stone. He knew the mark. He'd seen it cut into a canyon wall full of names, a hundred miles and a lifetime ago.

Elias had known about this place. Had maybe kept it alive, clearing silt, stacking stones, a dead man tending a door for travelers he'd never meet. Juniper knelt at the pool's edge and filled the first skin with hands that shook, and brought it to the litter, and trickled it into Wren's mouth between her cracked lips, drop by drop, patient as rain.

Her eyes opened. Focused. Found his face.

"There you are," she whispered.

"Here I am."

"Told you," she murmured, already sliding back toward sleep, but a different sleep now, a healing sleep, the fever breaking like a fever finally allowed to lose. "Told you the spring was real. Elias never drew a lie."

"Elias never drew a lie," Juniper agreed, and sat back on his heels in the shade of the greasewood, and let himself shake, just for a minute, where no one could see.

Sera appeared at his shoulder with a filled skin and handed it down to him without a word. He drank. The water tasted of stone and iron and, faintly, impossibly, of the river far below and far ahead, running dark and poisoned toward the towns, waiting for someone to set it free.

He looked south, toward the passes. From here he could see them on the horizon—a blue-gray wall of rock with snow still holding in the high shadows, and above the lowest notch, a thin plume of dust rising in the afternoon light.

Not cloud. Dust. Moving. The kind of dust that riders make.

Sera saw it too. She went still beside him, and her hand drifted to the rifle sling she no longer legally carried.

"How many?" Juniper asked.

"Hard to say. Six. Maybe eight." She paused. "They're not coming from behind us, Juniper. They're coming from ahead. From the pass itself."

Which meant Hale hadn't been waiting for them to reach the source.

Hale had been guarding it all along.
