## 11. The Toll Bridge

The river showed us the bridge before we saw it—a long brown ribbon of water sliding out of the gorge country, and strung across it like a stitch, the toll bridge at Miller's Crossing. From a mile off you could read it in the way the light came back to you: not the dull glare of raw timber but the polished shine of new planking, fresh-cut and freighted in, which meant money, which meant Hale.

We lay in the shade of a leaning sandstone slab while Juniper studied the crossing through Elias's cracked spyglass. He was good at going still. Ten years of tracking had taught him how to be furniture, how to be geology. I'd learned a different kind of stillness—the thief's stillness, the kind that keeps your hands busy and your eyes moving—but watching him I understood we were two dialects of the same language.

"Twelve men," he said finally, passing me the glass. "Maybe fourteen. Two on the near tower, one on the far, the rest in the garrison house. They've got a chain across the span for anything bigger than a mule."

I found the towers, the low stone garrison with its cookfire smoke standing straight up in the windless heat, and then I found something else and my stomach went cold before my head knew why. A rider coming down the east road. Alone. Riding like she had somewhere to be and no doubt about what it would cost when she arrived.

The glass steadied on her and there she was: dust-caked duster, lawman's hat pulled low, a rifle scabbarded at her knee and a warrant folded somewhere against her heart like a second lung. Sera Quill. The woman who'd spent three weeks on our trail and lost us twice in the salt country, and who Juniper talked about in his sleep sometimes—not by name, never by name, just a tension that entered his breathing and stayed.

"She'll give us up," I said. "She has to. That's the whole shape of her."

Juniper took the glass back and looked a long time. "Maybe," he said, which from him was a paragraph.

* * *

We watched her ride down onto the bridge deck and dismount, and we watched the garrison sergeant come out to meet her, and whatever passed between them made the man laugh once, short and ugly, and gesture her inside. An hour crawled past. The sun moved the width of a hand. Juniper didn't speak and neither did I, because there was nothing to say that wasn't fear wearing a different coat.

It was Wren logic—my logic—that finally broke the silence. "If she's told them everything," I said, "they're not searching for us yet because they don't believe her. Or they do believe her and they're setting the net wide, letting us come to the water." I turned a pebble over in my fingers. "Either way, the bridge is the only crossing for forty miles, and behind us is the salt, and Wren Calloway does not walk back into the salt."

"You nearly died in the salt."

"I did die in the salt. What's walking around now is the part of me that refused." I said it lightly, because that was how I carried things, but his eyes came around to me and stayed, and under all that ranger flatness there was something that had been growing since the flood, since the night I coughed half the river out of my lungs and woke to find him sitting up beside me, keeping watch over nothing, keeping watch over me. "So we go through," I said. "Not around. Through."

"The codes," he said quietly.

"The codes." Because that was the other thing we'd learned, the thing that had turned this from flight into plan: every garrison on the high route ran on signal flags and pass-phrases, and whoever held those could move through Hale's country like a rumor. And who better to get them than the one person Hale's men trusted enough to laugh with?

I hated the plan the moment I finished building it. I built it anyway.

* * *

We came down to the bridge at dusk, when the light goes copper and everyone's eyes are tired. Juniper walked in front with his hands visible and his badge—his old badge, the one he was supposed to have surrendered years ago—pinned where the last of the sun could catch it. I walked behind him with my wrists bound in front of me with a length of soft rope, tied loose enough to slip and tight enough to look true. A ranger delivering his prisoner. It was almost too neat. It was exactly neat enough to work on tired men.

"Hold it there." The near-tower guard stepped out, carbine levelled across his forearm. Behind him the chain across the span caught the last light and threw it back in a bright line, like the bridge itself was baring its teeth.

"Ranger Vane," Juniper said, and let the name sit there. Names were currency out here; his was worth more than most, and worth less every year, and he spent it anyway. "I've got a prisoner for the magistrate's circuit. Wren Calloway. There'll be papers ahead of me."

"There's papers ahead of nobody." But the guard's eyes flicked past Juniper to me, and I gave him what I've given a hundred marks: the wide frightened stare, the tremble in the bound hands, the whole theater of a small criminal in big trouble. Men love a woman who looks breakable. It makes them feel structural.

"Sergeant Oakes will want to see that badge," the guard said, and shouted up the span, and we waited while the day drained out of the sky, and then Sergeant Oakes came down off the garrison porch wiping grease from his fingers, a heavy man with a sergeant's way of looking at people like inventory.

He looked at Juniper a long time. Too long. My heart ticked up toward trouble.

"Heard you were dead," Oakes said.

"Took longer than expected," Juniper said.

Oakes laughed—one bark, humorless—and then the porch door opened again and she came out, and the world narrowed to a needle's point.

Sera Quill crossed the yard with the unhurried stride of someone whose authority doesn't need hurrying, and stopped an arm's length from Juniper, and the two of them stood in the copper light like figures carved on opposite ends of the same monument. Up close she was thinner than the glass had shown, and there was a healing scrape along her jaw, and her eyes did something complicated when they touched his face—something I recognized despite myself, because I'd been doing it to him for weeks.

"Vane," she said.

"Quill."

"You're under arrest." She said it loud, pitched for the yard, for the guards drifting closer, for the record. "For theft of official documents, obstruction, and the murder of the boatman at Kettle Ferry."

That last one landed like a slap. I saw it hit Juniper physically, a flinch he killed halfway through. The old boatman who'd died covering our escape—we hadn't even learned his name until a passing trader gave it up, Tomas, just Tomas, and now here it was again, hung around Juniper's neck by a man who'd never met him.

"That's a lie," I said, and Sera's eyes cut to me, cold as well-water.

"So is your name, probably. Get her in the lockup." Two guards took my arms. The rope slipped free of my wrists so easily it felt like an insult, and they retied it hard, and I decided, looking at the backs of their necks, to remember both of them.

They marched Juniper past me and for one stride we were close enough to touch, and he didn't look at me, and I understood why: whatever was about to happen, it would only work if every eye believed it. So I played my part. I cursed him. I called him a liar and a badge-wearing jackal and worse, and the guards chuckled, and somewhere in the middle of it Juniper said, quiet, without turning his head, words meant for me alone:

"Don't do anything brave tonight."

And they took him into the garrison, and the door closed, and I stood in the yard with the rope biting my wrists and the first stars coming out over the gorge country, and I realized I had gotten exactly what I'd planned for and it felt like swallowing a stone.

* * *

The lockup was a root cellar with bars. They put me in it because Oakes liked tidy paperwork and a prisoner was a prisoner, and besides, the magistrate's circuit would want her delivered intact. I sat in the dark listening to the garrison settle into its evening rhythms—boots, dishes, the change of watch—and I built and discarded six escape plans, and rejected all of them, because every one ended with Juniper in chains or dead, and because of the other thing, the thing I kept turning over like a coin I couldn't read.

Sera Quill had looked at Juniper the way you look at a wound you gave yourself.

An hour into the dark, boots came down the cellar stairs. Not the guard's shuffle—these steps were measured, deliberate, a woman who'd decided something on the way down and wasn't going to renegotiate with herself. Lamplight spilled between the bars and there she was, warrant folder under one arm, rifle slung, face lit from below like something out of a bad dream.

"Wren Calloway," she said.

"Sera Quill." I stood up slowly. "Come to gloat?"

"Come to ask what Elias Marsh promised you."

The name hit the dark like a dropped lantern. I kept my face still, mostly. "You know that name."

"I buried him." Her voice didn't move at all. "Or I watched him get buried, at the waystation outside Dry Hollow, after he crawled thirty miles with poison working through him to put a map in your ranger's hands. I was two days behind him the whole way. I've been two days behind everything the whole way." She set the lamp on a barrel, and the light steadied, and so did she. "So I'm going to ask you once, and you're going to decide whether lying to me is worth what it costs."

So I told her. Not all of it—I kept back the ledgers' hiding place and the canyon of names, some habits survive anything—but I gave her the shape of it: the letter, the diversion camps, the poisoned towns, the magistrate who'd bought the river and was drowning anyone who mentioned the receipt. I watched her take it the way stone takes rain. Nothing moved. Everything soaked in.

When I finished she was quiet a long moment. Then: "Hale posted a reward for Vane. Alive. He wants him delivered to the circuit court at Redstone personally." Her mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "Which means every garrison on the high route has orders, and every garrison reports to me now, because I'm the one carrying the warrant."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because ten years ago I helped arrange a pardon for that man, and I have spent ten years wondering what it cost him, and tonight I watched Hale's sergeant call him a murderer and I watched him take it, and Juniper Vane has never once in his life taken a thing he didn't earn." She picked up the lamp. "Stay here. Stay quiet. When the watch changes, stay quieter."

"What are you going to do?"

She paused at the bottom step, lamplight throwing her shadow huge up the stairwell, and for a moment she looked less like a lawwoman than like the land itself, which gives and takes and explains nothing.

"My job," she said, and climbed.

* * *

What happened next, I heard rather than saw, piecing it together later from the sounds that drifted down the cellar stairs and from what Juniper told me after, in the low flat voice he used for things that hurt.

Sera Quill arrested him properly. That was the performance, and she performed it flawlessly: the formal reading of charges by lamplight in the garrison common room, the confiscation of his effects itemized aloud—spyglass, map case, the false-bottomed tin where the ledgers' copies rode—the signing of the transfer order that would move him to Redstone under escort. Oakes relaxed visibly through all of it, a man watching his problem get handed to someone else. She even ate supper with the garrison, asked sharp professional questions about the route to Redstone, complimented the coffee insincerely, and left the table with the entire room convinced that Ranger Quill was the most dangerous honest woman in the territory.

Then, near midnight, she went to move her prisoner to the cell adjoining mine for the night. Standard procedure. Nobody questioned it. Nobody followed.

There was maybe four minutes when that corridor held only the three of them and the lamp. Four minutes. Later, Juniper would tell me what happened in them, and I would hear in his voice the same thing I'd heard in hers in the cellar: people deciding, alone, in the dark, whom they actually served.

She gave him the codes.

All of them. The garrison's flag sequences, the pass-phrases rotating on the high route, the schedule of riders, the location of Hale's supply caches—written out in her own hand on the back of the transfer order, folded small, pressed into his palm during the one moment the lamp was trimmed and the corridor unwatched. And with it, the thing that mattered more: the knowledge that the escort to Redstone would travel the river road, not the gorge trail, and that the gorge trail was open, and that the gorge trail was the way to the source, and that the dry season would seal it in nineteen days.

"You understand what you're doing," Juniper said to her. I know because he told me, word for word, the way you memorize a thing you can't afford to lose.

"I'm arresting you," Sera said. "Publicly. Loudly. In front of twelve witnesses and a paper trail a mile wide. Tomorrow I report that my prisoner escaped during the night—overpowered me, took my horse, humiliated me thoroughly. I'll carry that the rest of my career. Maybe the rest of my life." A pause. "You always were easy to underestimate, Vane. Let's keep it that way."

"Why?" Just that. One syllable holding up the whole sky.

And she answered him, and I have thought about her answer many times since, usually late at night, usually next to him, usually wondering how many other people in this story were braver than I ever gave them credit for. She said: "Because I checked the ledgers you think I don't know about. Because I rode through Dry Hollow and saw the children. Because the law I serve stopped being the law somewhere upstream of here, and somebody has to find where it went wrong and say it out loud." Then, quieter, the truth under the truth: "Because I pardoned you once for the wrong reasons, and this is me doing it for the right ones."

Then she opened the cell doors—both of them, mine and his—and stood aside in the lamplight with her warrant folder under her arm, and said the last thing she said to us that night:

"Go. And don't you dare get caught. If Hale breaks either of you, everything I just burned was for nothing."

* * *

We went up the cellar stairs together into the sleeping garrison, past the snoring watch, out through the tack room where Sera's spare horse waited saddled—she'd seen to that too, hours ago, threading the animal's care through the ordinary machinery of an honest evening. The bridge stretched silver-black under the stars, the chain across it hanging slack because the night watch hadn't bothered to raise it for a woman they trusted.

Halfway across, Juniper stopped. He stood in the middle of the span with the river running dark beneath him, and he looked back at the garrison, at the single lit window where a lamp still burned, and I watched him carry something up out of himself and set it down and leave it there. Whatever he'd been to Sera Quill, whatever she'd been to him—it went into that look, and stayed on the bridge, and we crossed the rest of the way in silence.

At the far end I touched his arm. "She'll hang for this if it goes wrong."

"No," he said. "She won't. Because it isn't going wrong." He swung up into the saddle, and for the first time since the ferry, since the old man died, there was something in his face that wasn't grief or guilt. It was direction. "Nineteen days, Wren. The dry season seals the passes in nineteen days, and tomorrow morning Sera reports her prisoner escaped on the gorge trail, and every rider Hale owns rides this way chasing us—" he almost smiled, and it transformed him, "—straight into the country where the ledgers say he buried his dead."

We rode north along the black thread of the river, two fugitives on a borrowed horse, the garrison's lamps shrinking behind us, and above the gorge country the stars came out so thick they looked like snow falling upward.

Behind us, faint on the night air, we heard the garrison bell begin to ring.

Someone had found the empty cells early.
