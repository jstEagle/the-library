## 13. Breakout at First Light

The garrison at Toll Bridge was built to be seen from a mile off, and it was. A square of pale limestone squatting on the riverbank, walls thick as a man is tall, a single gate facing the bridge, and a watchtower at each corner where lanterns burned yellow through the night like the eyes of something that never slept. Wren had spent her whole life studying buildings for the ways into them, and this one offended her. It had no soft places. No cellar doors forgotten, no drainage grates a slim girl could slip through, no drunk guard with a loose keyring.

It had Sera Quill, though.

Wren crouched in the dead reeds downstream of the bridge, close enough to the water to smell the mud and the faint sweet rot of the season turning, and watched the garrison through a gap in the stalks. Sera had walked in through the front gate an hour before sundown, warrant in hand, in her dusty ranger's coat with the badge still pinned to it, and the gate guards had straightened like boys called before a schoolmistress. That was the thing about Sera Quill that Wren had been slow to understand and was now, in the dark, betting her life on: the woman wore the law so naturally that no one inside the law ever thought to check her.

"She'll do it," Wren whispered. "She'll burn it. I know she will."

Beside her, in the reeds, Juniper's horse shifted and blew warm air through its nostrils. Wren put a hand on its neck. The horse was the only witness to what she'd said, and she was glad of that, because saying it out loud had been less a prediction than a prayer, and she didn't pray where people could hear.

The plan was simple, which was the only kind of plan that worked at first light. Sera would take the overnight watch rotation as her authority, reassign the gate guards to the tower, walk Juniper out of his cell under pretense of transfer to the magistrate's court at Candler's Ford. If anyone asked for paperwork—and someone would—she had a transfer order written in the garrison's own hand, lifted from the commander's desk while he ate his supper. Wren's job was smaller and worse: she was to be the thing that went wrong. Because plans that depended on everything going right were plans made by optimists, and neither of them was an optimist anymore.

If the gate held, Wren opened it. If Sera was discovered, Wren made noise on the far side of the compound until the garrison looked the wrong way. And if it all came apart—if Sera turned them in at the last, if the whole thing was a trap dressed in a ranger's coat—then Wren was to take the horses and the ledgers and ride north alone, and not look back, and not die of it.

She had agreed to all of it. She had agreed in the flat voice of a person who has decided that if the worst comes, she will simply disobey, and let the argument happen after.

The reeds dripped. Somewhere upstream, a night bird made its one note and quit. Wren counted the lanterns in the towers—four, then three, when the eastern watchman's flame guttered and he did not relight it—and she counted her own heartbeats, which were faster than she liked, and she thought about Juniper in a cell below the commander's quarters, and about the last time she'd seen him, at the toll bridge, when Sera had put irons on him in front of forty witnesses and he had looked at Wren with an expression she was still learning to read. Not fear. Not blame. It had been the look of a man handing something over. *Keep it safe,* the look had said. *Keep going.*

She had kept going. She had ridden out, doubled back through the salt scrub, and found the place where the reeds grew thick, and waited, and hated waiting, because waiting was the part of any job where your mind did its worst work. Her mind now kept returning to the garrison's cookfire, to the smell of bread that had drifted across the water at dusk, to the ordinary human sounds of forty men living their small lives inside walls built to protect a lie. She had stolen from better people than Magistrate Corven Hale. That was the joke she couldn't stop telling herself. Somewhere in her career she had crossed a line where the people she stole from were cleaner than the people who hunted her, and she had never noticed the crossing, and now here she was, crouched in the mud, about to commit a crime that felt—for the first time in her life—like the honest thing to do.

The eastern sky went the color of a bruise healing. First light was close. First light was when the watch changed, when the tower men came down stiff and half-asleep and the new men came up yawning, when the gate stood open longest for the supply cart that came up from the river landing every dawn with flour and salt meat.

The cart was already on the bridge. She could hear the mule's shoes on the planks.

*Now,* she thought. *Come on, Sera. Now.*

* * *

Inside, Sera Quill stood in the corridor below the commander's quarters with a lamp in one hand and a lie in the other, and the lie was heavier.

The transfer order was in her coat pocket, warm from her body. The cell key was in her fist, warm from the ring she'd lifted it from. Between her and the cell door stood Corporal Vess, a young man with a cowlick and a conscientious face, who had been on duty all night and had spent a portion of it telling her, unprompted, about his mother's orchards outside Candler's Ford, and how the trees were coming in thin this year because the river ran low, and did the ranger know why the river ran low.

"I've heard theories," Sera had said.

Now Vess looked at the key in her hand, and then at her, and the conscientious face did its work.

"Transfer's not till the wagon comes, ma'am. Commander said noon wagon."

"Commander's orders changed at midnight." She kept her voice level, bored, the voice of a woman reciting facts she'd recited a hundred times. "Magistrate Hale wants the prisoner at Candler's Ford ahead of the circuit judge. I'm taking him myself. You can note it in the log."

Vess hesitated. She watched him hesitate, and she watched something move behind his eyes—the small, fatal arithmetic of a junior man deciding whether a ranger's word outweighed a commander's, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I used to be this boy. I used to be the one who believed the coat meant something.*

"Ma'am," Vess said, "can I ask—is it true what they're saying? About the prisoner? That he broke a man out of the Dry Hollow jail and shot Deputy Arliss?"

"Arliss is alive and lying about it," Sera said, which was true, and she watched the boy relax by degrees, and she hated herself for how easily it worked, and hated Hale more, because Hale had built a world where a boy like this would take her word over his own commander's simply because of the badge—and the badge, she now knew, had been Hale's coin for ten years. It had bought her silence without her ever signing anything. It had bought Juniper's pardon and turned it into a leash. Every honest thing she had ever done in the coat had been used to make the next lie heavier.

She opened the cell.

Juniper sat on the bunk with his back to the wall, hands cuffed in front of him, and he looked up at her with the lamp-light in his eyes, and he did not look surprised. That was the worst part. He had known. He had let her put the irons on him at the bridge, had let the garrison take him, and he had trusted her to come back, and she had spent the whole night not knowing whether that trust was faith or foolishness, and now she saw it was faith, and it made her throat hurt.

"You're late," he said.

"Traffic on the bridge." She stepped inside, turned her back to the corridor, and worked the key into the cuffs. "Vess is at the door. He'll walk us as far as the gate. After that it's the cart and the change of watch."

"And if it goes wrong?"

"Then it goes wrong loudly." She freed his wrists. He rubbed them, stood, rolled his shoulders, and looked at her in the lamplight, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because there was a decade between them and only a few seconds to stand in it.

"You don't have to do this," he said quietly. "There's still the other way. You walk out of here at noon with your warrant and your coat, and you're clean. I get to Candler's Ford, I get a trial I might even survive, and you—"

"Burn it," she said.

He blinked. "What?"

She reached into her coat and took out the warrant. It was a good piece of paper, heavy stock, the magistrate's seal in green wax, her own signature on the line where the law required it, and beneath the seal the words she had read so many times in the last ten days that she could recite them in the dark: *for the apprehension of one Juniper Vane, wanted for obstruction, theft of evidence, and the unlawful release of a prisoner.* She had read it by firelight, by lamplight, by the gray light of the salt flats. She had read it the way you read a scar.

She held it up between them.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "you told me the law wasn't the men who wrote it down. You said it was what you did when the men who wrote it down were wrong. I thought that was just something you said to make the miles go faster." Her hand was steady. She was surprised by that. "It took me ten years and one dying prospector's map to hear it."

"Sera—"

"I'm not doing this for you." She said it fast, before he could make it about him, before he could put that look on his face, the one that carried other people's burdens like they were his own. "I'm doing it because there are children in Dry Hollow drinking poison, and the man who put it there signs my orders, and I have spent ten years calling that justice because it was easier than calling it what it is." She turned toward the corridor, toward the lamp and the waiting boy with the cowlick. "Burn it. That's what I'm doing. Come on."

They walked out past Vess, who touched his cap and asked no more questions, and the corridor ran gray and cold toward the yard, and the yard was waking—the cookfire stoked, the supply cart creaking through the gate with its mule and its flour, the tower watchmen coming down the stairs rubbing their eyes. Sera walked with the warrant in her hand and Juniper half a step behind her, and it was almost working, it was *working*, the gate was ten yards off and the cart was in it and the driver was arguing with the sergeant about the weight of the sacks—

And then the commander came out of his quarters in his shirtsleeves, pulling on his coat, and saw them, and stopped.

"Ranger Quill." His voice carried across the yard the way voices do in early morning, when the air is still and every sound arrives whole. "Where are you taking my prisoner?"

"Candler's Ford. Magistrate's orders." She did not stop walking. Stopping was death. "Transfer order's in my coat, Commander, if you want to read it."

"I do," he said, "as it happens."

And the yard went quiet, the particular quiet of forty men discovering a spectacle, and Sera Quill stood in the middle of it with ten yards of open ground between her and the gate, and she did the thing she had decided to do in the dark hours, alone, when she had finally understood that there was no version of this where she kept the coat and kept her soul.

She took out the warrant. And she took out the garrison commander's own matches, lifted from the same desk as the transfer order, and she struck one, and she held it to the corner of the paper until the green wax seal caught and flared, and she held it up burning so the whole yard could see the magistrate's name curl and blacken and go to ash on the morning wind.

"Corven Hale poisoned the water towns," she said, in a voice built for courtrooms and canyons, a voice that reached the tower men and the cart driver and the cook and the sergeant and every man who would repeat it by supper. "He bought the judges, he dammed the source, and he killed the men who dug it. I was sent to bring in the one man trying to prove it. I'm not going to do that. I'm going to help him. And every one of you is going to stand there and let me, because every one of you has drunk that river water too."

The burning paper came apart in her hand. She let the last of it fall.

The commander's hand went to his sidearm. Juniper's hand went to Sera's shoulder, turning her, and they ran.

* * *

Wren heard the shouting before she saw them—the yard erupting like a kicked anthill—and then the gate was full of running men and she was moving, out of the reeds and up the bank with the horses, and she saw them come through the supply cart's confusion, Juniper first with Sera's wrist in his grip, the sergeant grabbing for Sera's coat and Juniper's elbow finding his jaw with a sound like a door closing. The commander was shouting *arrest them, arrest them all*, and two men started forward and then stopped, because the warrant was ash and the word *poisoned* was in the air now, and words like that move through a garrison faster than orders do.

"Mount!" Wren screamed, and Juniper threw Sera up onto the second horse and swung up behind her, and Wren wheeled her own horse as the first crossbow bolt from the tower went wide and splashed into the river, and then they were through the reeds and into the salt scrub with the garrison shouting behind them and the sun coming up red over the bridge like a coin dropped in blood.

They rode hard for an hour. Then another. When the garrison was a smudge of dust behind them and the horses were blowing hard, Wren called the halt in the shade of a leaning rock, and they stood there in the growing heat, three people on two horses, breathing.

Sera slid down first. She stood with her back to the rock, and her hand went to her collar, to the place where the badge was pinned, and Wren watched her take it off. Sera held it in her palm for a long moment—the tin star that had defined her for ten years, dulled at the edges, warm from her coat—and then she did not throw it away, and did not keep it. She knelt and set it on a flat stone, face up, in the middle of the trail, where anyone following them would find it and know exactly what it meant.

"You could have ridden out clean," Juniper said. His voice was rough. "Noon wagon. I told you."

"You told me a lot of things over the years." Sera looked up at him, and there was no softness in it and no hardness either, just the flat clear light of a woman done pretending. "You were wrong about most of them. So was I." She took the canteen from the saddle and drank, and passed it to Wren without ceremony, and Wren drank, and passed it on, and something settled among the three of them in that small transaction that none of them named, because naming it would have made it smaller.

"So," Wren said, swinging back into the saddle. "Fugitives. All three. I've been a fugitive for years, so I'll be leading. There's a route through the gorge country the map shows—Elias marked it in the margin, the part Juniper wouldn't let me look at until we were past the last town." She dug the map from her shirt and spread it against the rock, and the three of them bent over it together, and the ink lines of the gorge country ran up the page like cracks in glass, and at the top, where the paper was worn soft from handling, Elias Marsh's dying hand had written two words in pencil gone gray: *the source.*

"Hale will have riders in the gorges by tomorrow," Sera said. "He won't trust a garrison again. He'll send his captain."

"Then we ride faster than his captain," Wren said.

She looked at Juniper over the map. He was watching the lines of the gorge with an expression she'd learned on the salt flats—the look of a man measuring distance not in miles anymore but in what waited at the end of them. Somewhere up those cracks in the glass was a spring behind a dam, and a dead man's testimony, and the truth that had already cost a boatman his life and a ranger her badge and a thief her last clean escape route.

"First light," Wren said. "We ride at first light. It's a good hour for breaking things."

Juniper almost smiled. Sera swung up behind him and put her hand on his shoulder, and the horses turned north into the gorge country, and behind them, small in the distance on its flat stone, the badge caught the sun and held it.

Ahead, the first walls of the canyon rose out of the earth like the ribs of something buried, and the map's lines grew strange, and the wind coming down from the high country smelled, for the first time in Wren's life, of water that had never been drunk.
