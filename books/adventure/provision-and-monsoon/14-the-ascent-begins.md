## 14. The Ascent Begins

The gorge country announced itself the way a debt collector does—not with a knock but with the whole wall of the world leaning over you.

We'd climbed since dawn out of the salt flats, and by midmorning the land had changed its mind about being flat. Red stone rose around us in fins and buttresses, then in walls, then in walls that leaned together overhead until the sky was a blue river running far above our heads. The heat that had been trying to kill us for three days suddenly couldn't reach the canyon floor. Instead there was a cool, mineral-smelling shade, and the sound of our own footsteps coming back at us from every direction, so that it seemed we were six people walking instead of three.

Wren counted them aloud, because Wren counted everything. "Four. Five. Six." She turned a slow circle, arms out. "Hear that? We're an army."

"We're three tired people echoing," Sera said.

"An army of echoes," Wren said. "That's how most armies are made anyway. Somebody shouts and everybody repeats it."

Juniper didn't answer. He was looking at the map.

Elias Marsh had drawn it in the last hours of his life, his hand shaking so badly that some lines looked less like cartography than like a man trying to hold onto something while the earth pulled it away. Through the flats and the lowlands it had been precise—mile markers, water holes, the names of dead towns written small and careful. But here, in the gorge country, the ink went strange. The line of their route thinned to a thread and then broke apart into fragments: *where the two sisters lean, sleep under the third eye. Do not trust the green. The river remembers its old bed. Follow the old bed.*

Below that, in letters pressed so hard they'd nearly torn the paper: *There is no way back from here. I am sorry. I did not know how else to say it.*

Juniper had read those words a dozen times since the waystation. He'd thought they were a dying man's poetry. Standing now on the threshold of the gorges, with the light going thin above him and the walls rising on either side like the pages of a closing book, he understood they were instructions.

"He knew," Wren said quietly. She'd come up beside him without her usual noise, which was how he knew she'd read it too, or guessed it. "Elias. He knew this was a one-way door and he sent us through it anyway."

"He sent us through it because the truth was on the other side," Juniper said.

"People say that kind of thing when they're not the ones walking through."

"Then we'll be the ones who prove them wrong."

She looked at him sidelong, and in the strange canyon light her eyes were the color of the stone itself, red-gold, ancient. "You've changed," she said. "The man who arrested me in Dry Hollow wouldn't have said that. That man would have said something about procedure."

"The man who arrested you in Dry Hollow was mostly asleep," Juniper said. "I've been waking up in stages. It's undignified."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if it decided to settle there permanently. She shouldered her pack, what remained of it after the flood, and walked on. Sera fell in beside Juniper, and for a while none of them spoke, and the gorge carried their silence up to the ribbon of sky and gave nothing back.

* * *

They made camp that first night where the map told them to: under the third eye.

It took an hour to find it. The "two sisters" were twin pillars of sandstone leaning toward each other across the canyon, close enough at the top that a strong man might jump between them, and between and behind them, set into the north wall, was a hollow in the rock—a dark oval like a socket where a giant's gaze had been pried out. Beneath it the floor was smooth sand, sheltered, and there was even a seep of water threading down the wall, cold and faintly sweet, tasting of iron.

"Do not trust the green," Sera quoted, nodding at a patch of moss near the seep. "What do you suppose that means?"

"It means Elias found poison in pretty places before," Juniper said. "He learned to be suspicious of anything that looked easy."

They ate cold food, because firelight in a canyon travels farther than a scream. Wren laid out the map again by the last grey light and traced the route with one finger, frowning.

"It gets worse tomorrow," she said. "Look. The line stops following the canyon floor. It goes up." Her finger climbed the page, over hatched marks that might have been switchbacks or might have been a child's drawing of lightning. "And here—" a cluster of symbols, circles within circles, "—he's stopped writing words entirely. It's just marks. Like he assumed whoever followed him would already know things."

"Or like he was running out of time to explain them," Sera said.

"Both can be true," Juniper said. "Most true things are two things at once."

Wren glanced up at him. "That's the kind of sentence a man says when he's avoiding a different sentence."

"I'm aware."

"Good. Just checking." She rolled the map away and lay back with her arms behind her head, studying the strip of stars visible between the walls. A few minutes later she said, more softly, "Juniper. If the way back closes—if this really is a one-way door—are you glad? About who you're becoming? The awake version?"

He thought about it honestly, because she would know if he lied, and worse, he would know.

"I spent ten years measuring my life in miles," he said. "Miles walked, miles between me and other people, miles of empty country where nobody needed anything from me except that I stay gone. It was a good arrangement. Quiet. And then a dying man handed me a promise, and the promise got heavier every mile, and somewhere back on the salt flats I realized the weight was the first thing I'd carried in ten years that wasn't just myself." He paused. "So yes. I'm glad. Glad and terrified, which I'm told is what glad feels like when you mean it."

In the dark, Wren's hand found his wrist—just briefly, just enough pressure to say *heard*—and let go. Sera, lying with her back to them both, said nothing at all, but her breathing changed in a way Juniper recognized from a hundred nights on watch together years ago. It was the sound of a woman deciding not to speak.

* * *

The second day, the map abandoned words altogether.

The canyon forked and forked again, and each fork looked identical to the last—red walls, blue sky-ribbon, rubble underfoot—and only the marks guided them: a circle scratched into stone at a junction, matching a circle on the page; a cairn of three stones; once, impossibly, an arrow carved high on a wall where no man could reach, which meant Elias had stood on someone's shoulders, or lowered himself on a rope, or been younger than the man Juniper had met dying. The gorge kept its own history written all over it, older than Elias—ladder rungs cut into stone by hands long gone, petroglyphs of bighorn and floodwater, a ledge littered with potsherds bleached white as bone. People had been climbing toward this river's source for longer than anyone remembered. The river had always been worth climbing for.

Toward noon the walls began to narrow, and the sound of water reached them for the first time in days—not the river itself, which ran hidden somewhere below in the rock, but a side stream tumbling down a slot canyon, loud as applause.

"There," Wren said, pointing. "The old bed. See it?"

Juniper saw it. Along the eastern wall, forty feet above the current canyon floor, a smooth channel was cut into the stone—the scar of where the river had once run, ages ago, before some ancient rockfall had turned it aside. Elias's note came back to him. *The river remembers its old bed. Follow the old bed.* The raised channel offered a path above the boulder-choked floor, dry and level, winding up into the narrowing heart of the gorge.

It also offered no cover, no escape route, and no way down if trouble came. It was a road built for salmon and dead rivers and men with nothing left to lose.

They climbed to it anyway.

For two hours it was the best traveling they'd had since the flats—smooth stone underfoot, the side stream keeping them company, the walls opening just enough to give them back a wider slice of sky. Wren walked the center of the channel with her arms spread along its banks like a woman wading, narrating the river's biography as she imagined it. "And then one day the mountain dropped a house on me," she said, patting a boulder wedged in the ancient channel, "and I picked a new bedroom, and I've sulked down here ever since."

"You'd have made a fine geologist," Sera said.

"I'd have made a fine criminal geologist. Stealing strata. Fencing fossils." She grinned, and for a moment the three of them were just three travelers on a bright impossible road, and Juniper caught himself laughing—an unpracticed sound, rusty, real.

Then the mountain answered her.

It started as a sound below hearing, felt through boot soles rather than ears—a deep grinding groan, like the gorge clearing its throat. Juniper stopped. The laughter died in the air. Above them, at the top of the western wall, a seam of shadow that had been a seam all morning became a crack, and dust breathed out of it in a long grey sigh.

"Run," Juniper said. "Down-channel. Now."

They ran. The old riverbed gave them two hundred yards of level sprinting and then the world came apart behind them. Not one rockfall but a conversation of them—the wall sloughing off in sheets, boulders the size of wagons leaping free and striking the canyon floor with impacts Juniper felt in his teeth, dust boiling up in a brown tide that swallowed the light. Something enormous struck the rim of the old channel ahead of them and shattered, and shrapnel sang past Wren's ear, and she ducked without breaking stride, and Sera grabbed Juniper's arm and hauled him left, into a shallow alcove, as a slab the size of a barn door thundered down and buried the channel where they'd been standing.

Then it was over, the way earthquakes are over—suddenly, insultingly, leaving only ringing silence and the slow rain of settling dust.

Juniper coughed. The world reassembled itself in shades of brown. When the air cleared enough to see twenty feet, he stood and walked back to the edge of the alcove and looked.

The old riverbed was gone. Where it had wound upward into the gorge there was now a wall of fallen stone, fifty feet high, fused and interlocked, the kind of rubble that would take a crew of miners a month to shift. Behind them, the way they'd come was buried too—he could see it in the fresh scar on the wall, the whole length of the channel choked. The gorge had closed its hand around them like a fist.

Sera came up beside him. She didn't say anything for a long moment. Then, in the flat voice she used for delivering hard arithmetic: "No way forward through that. No way back through that. Maybe climbing routes, maybe. Days of exposure, with what gear the flood left us."

"Maybe," Juniper agreed.

Wren appeared out of the dust, pale under the grime, one cheek scraped and bleeding thinly. She looked at the rubble, and at the scarred wall, and at the two of them, and Juniper watched her arrive at the same sum they had. He braced for fear. What he saw instead was something fiercer—she straightened, wiped her face with a dirty sleeve, leaving the blood smeared wider, and laughed once, short and sharp.

"Well," she said. "Elias warned us. *There is no way back from here.* I thought that was poetry." She kicked a pebble into the void where the path had been. "It was a contract. We signed it by walking."

Juniper turned and looked back the way they'd come, down the long throat of the gorge, at the dust still hanging in the air like a curtain drawn across the past. Ten years ago he would have felt panic. Five years ago, resignation. What he felt now was quieter and stranger: relief. The choice had been taken out of his hands by the oldest law there was, and some traitorous part of him was grateful. There was no invisible life waiting behind him anymore. No badge, no empty miles, no ghost-with-a-badge arrangement to creep back to. The door was shut. All that remained was the source, and the truth, and whatever price the mountain intended to charge for them.

"Sera," he said. "Your warrant. Your career. Everything you had is on the other side of that rock."

She considered it. Dust settled in her hair, turning it grey at the temples, aging her a decade in a moment. "My deputies are behind that rock too," she said. "Half of them are Hale's men. I've been a fugitive since the toll bridge. This just makes it official." She flexed her burned hand—the one she'd wrecked holding the pass against her own former deputies—and winced, and smiled grimly. "Besides. I've read your map-maker's notes. Forward is the only direction he left anyone."

"Forward it is," Wren said. She pulled out the map, unrolled it against the clean stone of the alcove wall, and weighted the corners with rocks. Beyond the buried channel, the inked line resumed—climbing, always climbing, up through hatch-marks and broken symbols toward a final mark near the top of the page: a circle within a circle within a circle, and beside it, in Elias's last legible hand, two words.

*The spring.*

"And there's our road," Wren said, tapping the line where it climbed off the edge of the drawing. "Up. Whatever's up there, we go see it."

Juniper folded the map with care, the way you fold a letter from someone dead. As he tucked it inside his shirt, his fingers brushed the ledgers from the Canyon of Names, wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs—Hale's bribes, Hale's bought judges, the paper skeleton of the crime. Proof. But proof of money, only. Elias had sworn there was more at the source. Testimony. Names. The why of it, carved in stone.

Above them, past the fresh scar of the rockfall, the gorge narrowed to a slot of violet shadow, and from somewhere deep in that shadow came a sound none of them had heard in weeks: running water. Real water, moving with purpose, cold and certain, going where it had always meant to go.

The river remembered its old bed. Now, so would they.

Juniper tightened his pack straps, tested the first handhold in the cliff above the rubble, and began to climb.
