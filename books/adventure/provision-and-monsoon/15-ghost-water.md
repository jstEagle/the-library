## 15. Ghost Water

The map lied, or Elias Marsh had lied with it, or the gorge itself had shifted in the years since a dying man traced its lines with a shaking hand. Wren couldn't tell which, and by the third day of climbing she'd stopped caring. Her boots were ruined. Her hands were raw from the rock. And Juniper Vane walked ahead of her like a man reading scripture, following a line of ink that kept promising water and delivering nothing but stone.

"It should be here," he said for the fourth time that morning, standing at the mouth of a side canyon so narrow the sky above it was a blade of blue.

"Things that should be here," Wren said, "have a poor record on this trip."

Sera said nothing. She hadn't said much since the pass—since she'd held it alone and come back to them with a bandage around her ribs and blood dried black down her side, walking like a woman who had decided pain was just another kind of weather. She climbed last now, always last, where she could watch their backs, and when Wren looked at her she saw something new in Sera's face: not the law anymore, not exactly. Something older and hungrier that had been living underneath the law all along.

"The map says the source feeds a narrows," Juniper said. "Elias marked it *sealed*. I thought he meant hidden."

Wren looked past him into the canyon. The light came down strange there, greenish, filtered through something. She'd spent her whole life learning what light meant off different surfaces—the glint of a purse chain under a market awning, the shine of a lock worth picking, the particular dull gleam of a strongbox that wasn't locked at all because it was bait. She knew light the way Juniper knew miles.

And this light was wrong.

"There," she said quietly. "Look at the walls."

Juniper turned. High up on both sides of the canyon, well above any flood line, ran a stain—a dark horizontal seam in the rock, like a tide mark left by a sea that had once stood ten feet higher and been taken away.

"That's mineral," Juniper said slowly. "That's where the water used to reach."

"Water doesn't get shy," Wren said. "It doesn't climb up a canyon wall for a hundred years and then decide to leave."

She started walking. The canyon floor was sand, but wrong sand—packed, layered, streaked with pale silt that crunched underfoot like old bone. The greenish light thickened. And then the canyon opened, without warning, into a bowl walled in red stone, and Wren stopped so fast Juniper nearly walked into her back.

There had been a town here.

Not a town—a camp. But big. Bigger than Dry Hollow, bigger than anything this far upstream had any right to be. Terraces cut into the canyon walls held the foundations of bunkhouses, a cookhouse with its chimney still standing, a stable whose roof had fallen in, a machine shed housing the rusted skeleton of something enormous—a crane, or a dredge, its arm reaching out over the canyon floor like the neck of a dead bird. Cable lay everywhere, coiled and uncoiled, furred with rust. And at the far end of the bowl, filling it wall to wall, stood the dam.

It was not the graceful masonry of a bridge or an aqueduct. It was brutal—a mass of poured concrete and mortared stone, scarred with scaffolding holes, stained dark with decades of seep, rising two hundred feet to plug the gorge completely. Behind it, somewhere above, the river's source sat held like a hostage. Below it, the canyon floor stretched out dry as a kiln.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The wind moved through the cables and made them sing, low and mournful, dozens of dead notes at once.

"Hale didn't divert the headwaters," Sera said at last. Her voice came out flat. "He amputated them."

* * *

They made camp in the cookhouse, because the walls still stood and the wind couldn't get at them fully, and because none of them wanted to sleep in the open under that dam. Wren told herself it was practicality. She told herself a lot of things that evening.

The cookhouse still held its tables. Long ones, scarred with knife marks, pushed against the walls as if the men who'd eaten at them had only stepped out for a moment. Tin plates were stacked in a corner, fused together by rust into a single solid block. A ledger-sized book lay open on the serving counter, swollen with damp, its words long dissolved into gray bloom. On one wall, someone had nailed up a calendar, years out of date, its picture faded to a brown ghost of a woman holding a jug.

"They didn't leave," Wren said. She was standing at the tables, touching the knife marks. "People who leave take their plates. They burn their trash. They—" She stopped. Started again. "This is a place where everyone put down their tools at the same moment and never picked them up."

"Maybe they finished the job and moved on," Juniper said, but his voice didn't believe itself. He was standing in the doorway looking out at the dam, and the last light was coming off the concrete the color of an old bruise.

"Then where's the pay ledger? Where's the payroll chest? Where's the—" She gestured at the room. "You don't close a camp this size without wagons, without a week of loading. There's no wagon ruts leaving this canyon, Juniper. I looked. There's ruts coming in, plenty of them, heavy loads—and nothing going out."

Sera had gone quiet in the way Wren was learning meant danger. She sat on a bench with her forearms on her knees, staring at the floor, and when she finally spoke her voice was very controlled. "In the garrison, Hale's captain talked about the source project like it was history. 'The old works,' he called them. Finished works." She looked up. "You don't finish a work like this and leave the tools."

Night came down fast in the canyon, the way it does in deep country, pouring into the bowl like water into a cup. They lit no fire. They ate cold food and listened to the cables sing, and Wren lay on a table with her coat over her and stared at the rafters, where swallows' nests clung like dried lungs, empty now, abandoned season after season by birds that had better sense than the men who'd built this place.

She dreamed of water that night. In the dream it was everywhere, warm, rising, and it sounded like voices.

* * *

In the morning they found the graves.

Or rather, Juniper found them—he had the ranger's eye for disturbed ground, for earth that had been turned and settled again, and he crouched at the edge of the bowl near the talus slope below the dam and went still in the way he did when the world confirmed something he'd already feared.

"Wren," he said. Just that. Just her name, in a voice she'd never heard him use.

She came over. The ground in front of him was a field of low mounds, maybe forty of them, fifty, laid out in rows too straight for grief and too careless for ceremony. No markers. No names. Just stones rolled onto each mound to keep the coyotes honest, and even that done grudgingly, a single rock apiece, as if whoever buried these men had resented them the weight.

Sera knelt and brushed dirt from the nearest mound with two fingers, gentle, the way you'd touch a sleeping stranger. Under the stone, the soil was stained. Everything under the stones was stained.

"They were shot," Sera said. It wasn't a question. She'd been a deputy too long; she knew what violence looked like after the flesh was gone. "Most of them. Some of these—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Some of these mounds are small."

Wren felt the world tilt slightly, the way it did the first time she'd ever been caught, the first time a hand closed on her wrist and the whole bright architecture of her freedom came down. She sat down hard on the rocks and looked at the rows and the rows and the rows.

Workers. Hale's workers. The men who'd built the dam that strangled the river's source—who'd known, every one of them, where the water actually came from, what had actually been sealed away behind two hundred feet of concrete. Men who could talk. Men who, when the job was done, became not employees but witnesses.

And witnesses, in Magistrate Corven Hale's arithmetic, were liabilities.

"Some of them are children," Wren said. Her voice came out strange, distant, like someone else's. "Boys. Camps like this hire boys for the powder work, the small spaces. Twelve, thirteen years old. My brother would have—" She stopped. She never talked about her brother. "He'd have taken this work. We'd have both taken it. Anybody hungry takes this work."

Juniper crouched beside her. He didn't touch her—that wasn't how either of them operated—but he stayed, a solid presence at her shoulder, and after a while he said, quietly: "Elias worked this camp."

She looked at him.

"He knew the source was sealed. He drew us a map to it. He carried this—" Juniper's hand opened, indicating the graves, the dam, the silent cookhouse, the singing cables. "For years. Whatever happened here, he saw it. Maybe he dug some of these graves himself. Maybe that's what killed him slower than the poison did."

"And he traded the truth for a promise," Wren said. "To a stranger. To a man with a badge." She laughed, and it came out broken. "He gambled everything on the honesty of the law, and the law sent him you."

"The law sent me Sera Quill with a warrant," Juniper said. "Elias got lucky."

It was almost funny. Neither of them laughed.

* * *

They found the shaft entrance that afternoon, and with it, the reason the camp had died.

It was behind the machine shed, a tunnel driven into the canyon wall, timbered at the mouth, half-collapsed. Blasted shut—not collapsed, Wren realized as they pulled away the rubble with their hands and a pry bar from the shed; *blasted*, the charge set deliberately, tamping packed over it, the signature of professionals. Someone had wanted this hole closed forever. But concrete and powder are patient enemies of each other, and thirty years of seep had undermined the plug until a gap yawned at the top, wide enough for a person willing to crawl.

Wren was smallest. Of course she was. She went first, with Juniper's lantern and Sera's knife, worming up through the gap into air that tasted of dust and old iron and something else—something sweetish and rotten underneath, the smell of a place where time had stopped being allowed to pass.

The tunnel ran back into the mountain perhaps sixty feet and ended at a chamber where the lantern light found things that made her stop breathing.

Tools, racked. Powder crates, stacked. A shift board on the wall with chalk names still ghosting the slate—*MERCER, HOLLIS, T. BRAM, JUAN R., E.M.*—and next to the names, tallies, days worked, wages owed. And on a table in the center of the chamber, weighted down with a piece of ore, a stack of papers that the dry tunnel air had preserved the way desert air preserves everything it decides to keep.

Pay records. Correspondence. And letters—dozens of them—in a fine, careful hand that Wren recognized from the letter she'd stolen in another life, three weeks and a hundred miles ago. Hale's hand.

She read them by lantern light while Juniper and Sera crawled in behind her, and she read them aloud because she couldn't not, because the words needed air, needed voices, needed to be *said* in this room where the men they concerned had last drawn breath.

"*Regarding the matter of severance*"—her voice shook on the word—"'*the magistrate directs that no man who labored on the upper works be released to the settlements below. Provision has been made. See that it is applied humanely, and see that the record reflects a closure due to flooding.'*"

Provision had been made. Wren looked at the tallies. At the chalk names. At the grave rows outside, laid out too straight, one stone apiece.

"*Humane,*" she repeated, and her voice cracked clean in half. "*He wrote humane. He signed it humane.*"

She was crying before she knew it, furious tears, the kind that scald, and she didn't wipe them because wiping them would mean admitting them and she had spent her whole life being light-fingered and light-footed and light-hearted and she refused, she *refused*, to be light about this. Juniper put his hand on her shoulder then, finally, and she let it stay.

"Elias's mark," Sera said quietly. She was holding the shift board. Her finger rested on the chalk ghost: *E.M.* Beside it, unlike every other name, the tally didn't end mid-number. It ended neatly, at a round figure, with a final entry beneath it in different chalk, older handwriting, hurried: *paid in full — took papers.*

He'd been paid. Not in coin. In papers. In proof. The dying prospector downstream, poisoned and desperate, trading maps for promises—he'd walked out of this canyon as the sole survivor of a massacre, carrying the evidence in a waterproof roll against his spine, and he'd spent thirty years staying alive long enough to give it away.

"He didn't survive," Wren said, understanding arriving like cold water. "He was *spared*. Left alive because he took the records and vanished, and killing a man who's already buried your crime is wasteful. Hale let him go because a dead man can't testify but a frightened one won't." Her fists clenched. "And then Elias got old, and dying, and suddenly not frightened anymore."

The lantern flame bent in a draft from deeper in the tunnel—from beyond the chamber, where rubble blocked the way but air still moved, cool and wet, smelling of living water. The source was close. Somewhere above and behind that fall of rock, the river waited, imprisoned, and the proof of its imprisonment sat in Wren's arms in a fine careful hand.

"We take everything," Juniper said. His voice had changed. Wren had heard it hard before, and quiet before, and once—only once, at the ferry, over the boatman's body—she'd heard it break. This was none of those. This was something forged new, out of all of them. "Every page. The shift board. We carry it out if we have to carry it a pound at a time."

"And then?" Sera asked, though Wren could hear in her voice that she already knew.

Juniper looked up at the tunnel ceiling, toward the mountain's heart, toward the sound of water that no one had been permitted to hear for thirty years.

"Then we open the mountain," he said, "and we drown every lie he ever built."

They worked until dark, wrapping the records in oilcloth, and it was Wren, crawling out last, who paused at the tunnel mouth and looked back at the chalk names one final time. *Mercer. Hollis. T. Bram. Juan R.* Boys and men who provisioned a magistrate's ambition and were paid in graves.

"I'm coming back for you," she whispered to them. "All of you. The whole river's coming back for you."

Behind the dam, faint through two hundred feet of stone, the water sang.
