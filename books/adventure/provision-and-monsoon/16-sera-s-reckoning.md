## 16. Sera's Reckoning

The pass had a name once. Sera had read it on a survey map years ago, back when she still believed maps were promises: *Cutthroat Notch*, some dead prospector's joke, though nothing up here had ever been cut but wind. Now the name was gone from her mind entirely, and what remained was the shape of the place—a throat of stone, narrow enough that three riders abreast became one, high enough that the air came thin and tasted of old snow.

She had climbed ahead of the others all morning, and she told herself it was because she knew the route, because her legs were longest, because someone had to break trail through the scree. All true. None of it the truth. The truth was that she had heard the birds go quiet an hour back, and she had spent the last sixty minutes listening to that silence the way a gambler listens to a deck being shuffled.

Juniper would have heard it too. She'd seen him register it—his head tilting, that old habit from the academy, a hound's motion on a man's body—and then he'd looked at her and looked away, and neither of them had said a word, because saying it would make it real, and not saying it let them keep climbing.

Wren Calloway climbed between them like a woman being dragged uphill by her own stubbornness. The fever had broken in the salt flats, but it had left her hollowed, moving on credit. Twice she'd stumbled and caught herself on the rock and said nothing, and twice Sera had watched Juniper watch her and not offer his hand, and understood that this was its own kind of tenderness—that some people would rather fall than be carried by the one they were falling for.

Sera knew something about that. She'd spent ten years not being carried.

* * *

The ambush came at the worst possible place, which meant it came exactly where she would have set it herself.

The trail bent around a shoulder of granite and narrowed to the width of a wagon, with a drop on the left that went down four hundred feet into a gorge full of morning shadow, and a wall on the right that went up twice that. Perfect ground for a choke. Perfect ground, if you had six men and a grudge, to spend two lives and buy the rest.

The first shot came off the wall above them and took her hat instead of her head. She heard the crack roll down the gorge, heard the echo come back wrong, doubled, and knew there were at least two rifles up there, probably three, spaced wide.

"Down!" she was already shouting, already moving, because the old drills lived deeper in her than thought did. She hit the scree on her knees and slid toward the wall, and Juniper had Wren by the collar and was dragging her into the lee of a boulder the size of a house, and the second shot spanged off the granite where Wren's spine had been.

"Sera Quill!" The voice came from above, amplified by the stone, and she knew it before the last word finished—knew it the way you know a voice in a dark house. Deputy Marshal Ansel Roark. She'd trained him. She'd taught him to read sign, to hold his fire until he had the shot, to never threaten a man he wasn't prepared to kill. He had learned all of it well. "You're standing with fugitives! Warrant's dead, Quill. You burned it. You know what that makes you."

She knew exactly what it made her. She'd known since the garrison, since she'd pressed the route codes into Juniper's palm inside a handcuff grip, since she'd walked out of that bridge with her career folded up small enough to fit in her breast pocket and burning a hole there anyway.

"It makes me somebody who read the ledgers, Ansel!" she called back. "You ought to try it. Hale bought the judges the way you'd buy boots. You're not wearing the law. You're wearing his name on the inside of the collar."

Silence from above. Then, quieter, and somehow worse: "Come up alone and talk. Man to— " he corrected himself, and she heard the shame in it, heard him wish the words back. "Come up and talk. Nobody else has to die today."

Nobody else. Which meant somebody did, in his arithmetic, if she didn't come up.

She looked at Juniper. He was crouched against the boulder with Wren pressed behind him, and his revolver was out and his face was the face she'd first seen across a training yard ten years ago—young, hard, already carrying something heavy he wouldn't name. He shook his head, one small motion. *Don't.*

She almost laughed. Ten years, and he still thought he could spend other people's courage on her behalf.

"He wants me," she said, low. "He's always wanted me. I'm the one who went wrong, in their ledger. You two are just the interest."

"Sera—"

"Listen to me." She grabbed his sleeve, and the contact was the first they'd allowed each other since the garrison, since she'd turned him in with one hand and freed him with the other, and she felt him go still under her grip like a horse hearing a snake. "The trail bends around that shoulder and there's nowhere to run for half a mile, and they've got the high ground and all the time in the world. But there's a chimney—" she pointed, up and left, to a crack in the wall where the granite had split and weathered into a ragged staircase, "—a chimney, maybe two hundred feet, and it comes out above the shoulder. I saw it on the way up. You take Wren up that, and by the time they figure out where you went, you're past them."

"And you?"

"I hold the throat."

"You can't hold a throat against six rifles."

"I can hold it against six rifles that don't want to shoot me," she said. "Not yet. Roark will spend an hour negotiating before he spends a bullet, because I trained him to, and every hour is an hour you're climbing. That's the whole plan. It's not a good plan. It's the plan we have."

Juniper looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him do the arithmetic—watched him arrive at the same sum she had, and hate it, and know it was right. That was the thing about him she'd never managed to hate. He was honest with himself even when it cost.

"Wren can't climb a chimney," he said.

"Wren can climb anything if you tell her there's something at the top worth stealing." She almost smiled. "Go."

* * *

They went.

She watched them do it—watched Juniper put Wren's hand on the first hold and then go up ahead of her, calling the footholds down in a voice pitched low, and Wren following with her fever-thin arms shaking and her jaw set like poured concrete. They moved slow. They moved like people who intended to see the top. And the whole time, Sera stood in the throat of the pass with her back against the granite and her rifle across her knees, and made herself the loudest thing on the mountain so no one would look up.

She fired first. It wasn't a decision so much as a policy—she put a round into the rock above Roark's position, a warning shot with a message stitched into it: *I'm here, I'm armed, I'm not running.* Then she stood up into the open, deliberately, where the shooters above could see all of her, and she shouted up at the wall.

"Roark! You want to talk? Talk!"

He came down partway, into view—a lean shape against the sky, rifle slung, hands loose. Cautious. She'd taught him that too, and felt the strange pride of it, the way a parent feels watching a child do something admirable and terrible.

"You burned your warrant," he said. "That's a hanging offense if Hale wants it to be."

"Hale wants a lot of things. He wanted a river, and he stole it. He wanted towns quiet, and he poisoned them. Children, Ansel. I've seen them. You haven't, and that's the only mercy in this whole story."

"You don't know that. You've got a thief's letter and a dead prospector's map and a fugitive's word—"

"I've got ledgers." She let that land. "In Hale's own hand. Bribes, dates, names. Some of the names are men you eat lunch with."

The shape above her was still for a long time. Wind moved through the pass and carried grit against her cheek, and she stood in it and did not flinch, because flinching was the first thing to go, and everything after that went faster.

"You were the best of us," Roark said finally, and his voice had lost its marshal's flatness. "That's what nobody can figure. You had everything."

"That's what Hale counted on. That I'd have too much to lose." She shifted her weight, casual, and glanced—just a flicker, a nothing—up at the chimney. *Look there. Look at me instead.* "I found out what I had was on loan. That's all a career is, Roark. It's Hale's coat around your shoulders, and one day he asks for it back, and you're standing in the cold in your shirtsleeves wondering whose man you've been."

"Come down, Sera. Come down and testify through channels. There are ways—"

"There were ways. He poisoned them. There's a dam on the source and dead men under it, and the ways closed a long time ago." She raised her voice, let it fill the throat of the pass. "You want to shoot me, Ansel, you'll have to do it standing in the open, and my rifle's already on you, and I've been shooting longer than you've been shaving. Or you can let me walk up this pass and be there when the truth comes down the river like a flood, and be standing on the right bank when it does. I'm offering you the only deal anybody's offered you since you pinned that badge on. Take it."

He didn't answer. That was the answer's first half.

The second half came a minute later, from far up the wall, from a position Roark didn't control—a voice she didn't know, young and scared and eager, the voice of a man about to make his reputation: "Movement on the chimney! They're on the chimney!"

* * *

Everything after that happened fast, the way bad things do.

The shot came off the wall and she was already moving, already firing back, not to hit—she couldn't see him—but to spoil his aim, to make the rock sing around his position and put fear in the space between his eye and his target. Juniper was halfway up the chimney with Wren below him, exposed as a target for the length of a breath, and the bullet meant for him cracked past and struck sparks off the stone a hand's width from Wren's fingers, and she heard Wren cry out—not hurt, Sera thought, not hurt, just human—and then Juniper's body folded over Wren's like a shield, hiding her with his own back, and kept climbing.

Sera stood in the open and fired and fired and fired, emptying her rifle with the calm of a woman at practice, and every shot was a shout: *Here. Here. Me. Look at me.* And they did. Roark was shouting something she couldn't hear over the gunfire—*hold, hold, damn you, hold*—but the young one was past listening, and there were others with him, and the pass filled with the thunder of it, echoes stacking on echoes until the whole gorge was a drum.

The bullet took her high in the left shoulder, through the meat above the collarbone, and spun her half around, and the pain arrived a full second later, enormous and impersonal, like weather. She sat down hard against the granite. Her rifle clattered. She reached for it and her left arm answered with a white scream and she used the right hand only, dragged the rifle into her lap, and began reloading one-handed, because that was the drill, that was the oldest drill, *you reload until you can't, and after that you reload anyway.*

They stopped shooting.

She understood why a moment later, when she got her eyes to focus: Juniper and Wren had topped out. She could see them against the sky above the shoulder, small, moving, alive—Juniper half-carrying Wren now, both of them stumbling up the trail on the far side, into the country beyond the pass, into the high ground where the map grew strange.

They were clear. They were clear, and she had bought it, and the price was written into her shoulder in lead.

Roark came down to her. He came alone, and he came slow, and he knelt in the scree in front of her with his rifle slung and his hands open, and up close she could see he was crying without any of the noise of it, just the shine on his face and the wreck in his eyes.

"You dumb, brave, stupid woman," he said.

"Watch your mouth. I outrank you."

"You don't outrank anybody anymore. That's the whole point." He looked at the wound, and she watched him assess it the way she'd taught him—through and through, high, no bone that he could see, but a lot of blood, and blood up here was a budget that spent fast. "I can get you down to the ferry. There's a surgeon at the garrison who owes me."

"The garrison is Hale's."

"The surgeon isn't. Not all the way." He tore a strip from his shirt sleeve, and she let him, because pride was a luxury and she'd spent her last of it years ago. "Why, Sera? Ten years. You had everything. Why?"

She thought about the children in Dry Hollow. She thought about the boatman at the ferry crossing, dead in the water with his pole still in his hands. She thought about a young ranger named Juniper Vane, ten years ago, standing in her office while she arranged a pardon he never asked for, both of them believing they were doing mercy, and neither of them knowing they were tying a leash.

"Because I finally found something worth having," she said. "And it wasn't anything Hale could give me, and it wasn't anything he could take. Go find out, Ansel. When this is over—when the water comes down and the truth comes with it—go look at what he did, with your own eyes. Then decide whose man you've been."

* * *

They left her at the pass mouth with a canvas shelter, water, her rifle, and a signal lamp, and Roark posted two men at a distance who were there to help her or arrest her, and hadn't decided which, and she suspected they'd decide based on the news from downriver over the next few days. Fair enough. So would everyone.

She could have gone down. That was the honest arithmetic of it—she was wounded, the season was turning, and no one would have thought less of her. No one would have thought of her at all, which after ten years of being thought of constantly, in the wrong ways, sounded almost like peace.

But the map was cryptic past the shoulder. Juniper had said so, tracing the dead prospector's lines in the dirt: *the gorge country, where the marks stop meaning miles and start meaning warnings.* Two hundred feet of chimney behind them, and beyond it country that ate the unprepared, and Hale's riders regrouping, and Wren running on a fever's borrowed money. They'd need to know the garrison's movements. They'd need to know whether Roark held or broke. They'd need to know someone was behind them, alive, paying attention.

So she did the thing she was actually best at, the thing ten years of service had built her for, the thing no one had ever thanked her for because no one ever saw it: she became the station.

She built the signal lamp up on the high rocks where it could be seen for miles along the back trail—Juniper would know to look; she'd taught him that code too, in another life, on another range. She worked the shutter with her right hand, her left arm strapped tight and singing its one long note of pain, and she sent the message in the old academy flashes, the ones they'd used on night patrol before the telegraph came:

*ALIVE. WOUNDED. ROARK WAVERING. GARRISON MOVES AT DAWN. ROAD CODE UNCHANGED. CLIMB.*

She sent it three times, to be sure. Then she sat back against the stone and watched the sun go down the far side of the gorge, and the cold came on like a slow animal, and she did not think about dying, because she had decided, somewhere in the thunder of the pass, that she was not going to. Redemption wasn't a thing you purchased all at once. It was a thing you paid for in installments, and this—blood, cold, a lamp burning on a mountain for people who might never look back—was just the first payment, and she intended to live long enough to make the rest.

Far below and behind her, down the long stair of the river country, she could imagine the towns: Dry Hollow with its sick children, the ferry with its dead boatman, the whole wounded watershed waiting on a flood of truth. And above her, past the shoulder, in the dark, two people she had spent ten years loving in the wrong directions were climbing toward the source with everything she'd given them.

The lamp flashed into the dark one more time, though no one was left to see it, and it wasn't a code at all, just light, just her, saying the only true thing left:

*Go on. I'm behind you. I'm still in this.*

And high on the shoulder, small against the last of the light, a light answered—two flashes, three, then a pause. Juniper. He'd seen. He'd understood. *Received. Keep burning.*

Sera Quill leaned her head back against the granite and laughed until her shoulder screamed, and then she laughed a little more, because it hurt so good to be, finally, on the right side of a fight.
