## 17. The Source

They climbed through the dark hours because the map said to.

*Above the ghost water, where the gorge narrows to a throat, walk the dry channel until the stars close over it like a hand. The spring keeps its own hours. Come before dawn or not at all.*

Elias Marsh had written that in a hand gone shaky with poison, and Juniper had read it a hundred times since Dry Hollow, tracing the letters like braille until he could have recited them in his sleep. Now he recited them walking, one boot in front of the other on stone still warm from yesterday's sun, Wren a pale shape ahead of him moving on stubbornness alone. Her fever had broken two days past on the salt flats, but it had taken something out of her she hadn't gotten back yet—a certain quickness, a lightness of step. She climbed like a woman paying off a debt, each motion deliberate, nothing wasted.

"You could tell me to rest," she said, without turning around. "I'd probably even listen."

"No, you wouldn't."

"No," she agreed. "But it's polite of you to offer."

The gorge narrowed as Elias had promised, walls leaning in until the sky was a black river overhead crowded with stars, more stars than seemed lawful, so many that Juniper's eyes kept trying to resolve them into shapes and failing. He had spent ten years under this same sky and never once looked up with anything but irritation—*clouds mean rain, rain means tracks wash out*—and now he looked up and felt small in a way that wasn't bad. Small the way a man is small standing in a cathedral. Small the way Elias must have felt, an old prospector alone in the high country, deciding that some truths were worth dying on a mountainside for.

"Juniper." Wren had stopped. "Look at the walls."

He raised the lamp.

The marks started low, near the modern flood line—chisel-cut survey numbers, Hale's engineers' tidy Roman numerals—and went up from there, back through time, back past the diversion camp they'd found two days ago with its rusted cable and its graves nobody had marked. Higher, older: names cut into granite by men who had come looking for gold and found water instead. *R. TULLY, 1849.* *GOD GAVE US ROCK AND RIVER, WE CHOSE WRONG.* A handprint pressed in ochre, centuries old, faded to the color of dried blood. And among them, fresh enough that the dust still sat in the grooves, in a prospector's cramped and patient lettering:

*ELIAS MARSH CAME THIS FAR AND NO FARTHER WAS NEEDED.*

"He knew we'd see it," Wren whispered. "He carved it facing the trail. You couldn't miss it if you tried."

"He wanted whoever came after to know they were close." Juniper touched the letters. The stone was cold here, colder than the night justified, and damp. He felt the dampness before he understood it—a fine cool breath rising out of the rock, the gorge exhaling. "Listen."

They stood still. Beneath the wind there was another sound, low and constant, so deep it was less heard than felt through their boots: water. A great deal of water, moving behind stone.

Wren's hand found his in the dark. She didn't say anything about it and neither did he, but her grip was cold and her pulse was fast, and when she spoke her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was frightened and refusing to admit it. "It sounds like it's under us."

"It is." He lifted the lamp higher. Ahead, the dry channel ended at a wall of fitted masonry that no natural force had built—dressed blocks mortared with something pale, rising out of the canyon floor and up into darkness beyond the lamplight, curving with the gorge's bend like the wall of a fortress. Moss grew in the seams. Water wept from a dozen places between the stones and ran down the face in silver threads that caught the lamplight and threw it back broken.

The dam. Hale's dam. The thing that had turned three towns' rivers into trickles and their wells into poison, the thing Elias Marsh had crawled half a territory to testify against, the thing Juniper had promised a dying man he would see with his own eyes.

It was bigger than he had imagined. That was the thing nobody told you about crimes of this size—they weren't sly, they weren't hidden in shadows. They were enormous. They stood in plain sight and dared you to comprehend them.

"There," Wren said.

Halfway up the dam's face, where a ledge of living rock jutted out beside the masonry, the lamplight found a rectangle of paler stone—a slab set flush into the granite, framed with a chiseled border, and covered edge to edge with writing.

* * *

Getting up to it took an hour. The route was Elias's own doing—handholds cut into the rock in a ladder pattern, spaced for a man with short arms and long patience, each one worn smooth at the grip by exactly one pair of hands. Juniper went first with the lamp slung across his back, testing every hold twice, and Wren followed, and neither of them spoke, because the sound of the water behind the wall filled everything, and because some silences are not empty but full.

The ledge was wide enough for two to stand if they stood close. The slab was taller than Juniper, wider than both of them arm to arm, and the writing on it was Elias Marsh's lettering grown monumental—each character an inch deep, cut with a chisel by a poisoned man who had known he was working against his own failing strength, and who had solved the problem the way he'd solved every problem on the flats: by starting early and not stopping.

Juniper held the lamp and read aloud, because Wren asked him to, because she said her eyes were still fever-blurred, though he suspected she wanted to hear it in a voice that wasn't her own. So he read, slowly, while the hidden river thundered behind the stone:

*I, Elias Marsh, being of sound mind and unsound body, set down here what I know, in the place where it can't be burned or buried or bought.*

*I came to these hills in '61 chasing color and found instead the true source of the River Vane—not the mouth the maps show, not the springs the magistrate's surveys name, but this: a mother-spring rising cold and endless under the gorge, enough water for every town from here to the delta and twice over. There has never been a shortage. Understand that first. There has never been a shortage. There is only a man who profits from one.*

*In the year of the great drought Magistrate Corven Hale sealed this spring behind his dam and called it flood control. It is not flood control. There has been no flood here in forty years; the gorge tells you so if you read its banks. The dam exists to hold the water hostage. Towns that sign over their rights to Hale's Provision Company drink. Towns that refuse get a season of his "rationing"—and when rationing doesn't break them, they get his other medicine. I watched the well-diggers of Dry Hollow haul barrels upriver in the dark. I followed. I saw what was in the barrels. Arsenic and alum, mixed to taste of iron, easy to blame on old mines. I carried samples out. I am carrying them still. If you are reading this, look in the cache at Canyon of Names, third marker from the left hand. The ledgers will tell you what the barrels cannot: eleven payments, eleven towns, eleven lists of the dead that Hale signed with his own initials, C.H., in the margin, like a man initialing a grocery order.*

*I did not come down. I should have. I told myself the evidence wasn't safe below, that Hale's riders watched every road, and that much was true. But underneath the truth was a smaller one: I was afraid, and I was tired, and I told myself fear was prudence. Do not do as I did. Take what I leave you and go down fast.*

*One thing more. Whoever you are—ranger, thief, pilgrim, fool—you did not find this by accident. Nothing in these hills is found by accident. Someone sent you, or someone died sending you, or both. Honor them. Not with grief. Grief is cheap out here; the wind takes it. Honor them by finishing.*

*The water behind this wall belongs to no one and everyone, which is the same as saying it belongs to the law, and the law has been sold. So I say this plainly, chiseled where no judge can reach it: breach the dam. Let the river testify. Stone can be argued with. Water cannot.*

*Elias Marsh, prospector, who measured his life in ounces and found at the end it weighed more than that.*

Juniper's voice gave out on the last line. He stood with the lamp raised and let the silence come back, the deep water-silence, and felt Wren's shoulder against his arm, and neither of them moved for a long time.

*Honor them by finishing.*

"Sera," Wren said quietly. "The boatman. Old Tomas at the ferry. Everyone we lost getting here—it was for this. For a chisel and a dying man's stubbornness."

"For the truth," Juniper said. "That's what it cost. That's what it always costs. I used to think the price of truth was high because liars made it expensive." He shook his head. "No. It's expensive because it weighs something. Because somebody has to carry it up a mountain on their back."

Wren stepped closer to the slab and laid her palm flat against the letters, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, careful, respectful. "He wrote *breach the dam*."

"I know."

"That's not testimony anymore, Juniper. That's instructions."

"I know that too."

She turned to look at him, and in the lamplight her face was thin and fever-hollowed and absolutely clear. "Then say it. Say what we're doing. Because I've been following your lead since Dry Hollow, hunter, and I'll follow it up this wall and over the top of it—but I want to hear you say it out loud first. I want it in the air between us. No taking it back later. No pretending afterward that the ranger didn't know what the thief was up to."

Below them the gorge breathed its cold breath. Behind the wall the imprisoned river turned over in its sleep, patient as only water is patient, waiting the way it had waited for six years behind a corrupt man's masonry.

Juniper thought about the promise. He had made it in a waystation that smelled of kerosene and death, holding a dying man's hand, and he had thought then that he understood what he was agreeing to—carry the truth downstream, deliver the message, be the courier. Simple. A job. He had been telling himself it was a job ever since, right up through the flash flood and the ferry and Sera's light flashing *keep burning* off the mountainside, telling himself that promises were like warrants, paper things, dischargeable.

But standing here, with Elias's whole life cut an inch deep into stone, he finally understood the shape of what he'd accepted. Elias hadn't asked for a courier. He had asked for a witness who wouldn't stop at witnessing. He had known—he had counted on—that whoever climbed to this ledge would stand where Juniper stood, feel what Juniper felt, and understand that some words, once spoken, make the speaking of them a debt.

"The ledgers prove the crime," Juniper said. "This proves the motive. And the dam—" he put his hand on the masonry, cold and weeping and alive with the pressure of everything it held back, "—the dam is the crime. Still happening. Every day it stands, Dry Hollow drinks poison and Hale collects rent on thirst. Testimony isn't enough. Elias knew it. That's why he wrote it in stone instead of ink. Ink waits for a court. Stone gives orders."

"So we're going to break it."

"We're going to break it." He heard himself say it and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched for ten years—the thing that had made him a ghost with a badge, the thing that had let him count miles instead of counting what mattered. "And when it goes, the water goes downstream carrying everything—the ledgers' copies, the testimony rubbings, the samples, all of it, wrapped and waxed, straight into the towns. Hale can burn a courthouse. He can't burn a flood."

Wren laughed—a short, disbelieving sound, half exhaustion and half delight. "You've been planning this since the cache."

"I've been planning it since a man put a map in my hand and made me look at him while he died." He turned back to the slab one last time. "I just needed to hear him say it was allowed."

"And was it?"

Juniper read the last lines again, silently, the chiseled words standing out of the stone like scars: *Honor them by finishing.*

"Yeah," he said. "It's allowed."

* * *

They came down the ladder holds slower than they'd gone up, and at the bottom Juniper did something he'd been turning over in his mind since the ledge. He took out his field notebook—the one he'd kept for ten years, mile markers and track readings and the petty arithmetic of petty thefts—and on the last blank page he wrote, in a hand steadier than he expected:

*Day 214. Found the source. Found the truth. Both were behind the same wall.*

Then he tore the page out, folded it around a rubbing of Elias's signature from the slab—charcoal on paper, done by lamplight, every stroke captured—and sealed it into the oilcloth packet with the ledger copies and the arsenic vials. The packet went into the waxed canvas satchel, and the satchel would go into the flood, and the flood would carry it to Dry Hollow, to the towns, to whatever remained of the law after the law finished drowning in its own corruption.

Wren watched him work. "You realize what happens after," she said. "When the water comes down and the truth comes with it. Hale doesn't surrender. Men like him dig in. There'll be riders on every road, and warrants with our names, and Sera—" She stopped. Started again, gentler. "There'll be a reckoning. This isn't the end of it. It's the beginning of the hard part."

"I know."

"And you're smiling."

He was. He could feel it, unfamiliar muscles doing unfamiliar work. "Ten years I patrolled the flats catching kids who stole flour," he said, "and told myself that was the law, that was the job, that was enough. Tonight I'm standing on the biggest crime in the territory with the proof of it in a bag on my back, and tomorrow I'm going to drown it downstream into the daylight." He shouldered the satchel. "Yeah. I'm smiling."

They made camp in the lee of the dam's abutment, a hollow in the rock where Elias himself had clearly slept once—an old fire ring, a flattened bedroll space, a tin cup left behind with a hole rusted through its bottom. Juniper laid the lamp low and divided the last of the food, and Wren ate sitting hip to hip against him for the warmth, her head tipping gradually toward his shoulder as exhaustion won its long argument with her pride.

"Juniper." Half-asleep, her voice blurred. "When this is done. After. What happens to people like us? People who broke the law to fix it?"

"I don't know," he said honestly. "Maybe we find out together."

Her breathing evened out. He sat awake a while longer, listening to the river behind the wall, and near midnight he noticed the sound had changed—or he had changed, and could finally hear what had been there all along. Under the deep turn of the impounded water there was a thinner note, a whisper of seepage, water finding the flaws in Hale's masonry the way truth finds the flaws in any lie. Given time, the dam would fail on its own. Everything built on rot does.

But the dry season was coming, and the passes would close, and time was the one thing Hale had left to steal.

He was still awake, watching the stars wheel over the gorge rim, when Wren's hand closed suddenly on his wrist—awake now, fully, her whole body gone rigid.

"Listen," she whispered. "Downstream. Below the dam."

He listened. At first: wind, water, the ordinary night. Then, faint but unmistakable, floating up the throat of the gorge from the direction they had come—

Hooves. Many horses, moving fast, and with them the thin metallic carry of tack and chain, and beneath it all a sound Juniper knew from a decade of patrols and had hoped never to hear aimed at him: the high, carrying note of a bugle sounding the *close and search*, Hale's garrison signal, the call that meant the net was drawn.

They're not behind us, Juniper thought, already moving, killing the lamp, pulling Wren up into the dark. They're below us. Between us and the way down.

Which meant Hale's captain had guessed where they were headed—and meant to seal the source with the two of them inside it, forever, just like the water.
