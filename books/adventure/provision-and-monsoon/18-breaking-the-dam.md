## 18. Breaking the Dam

The dam was bigger than anything Wren had imagined, and she had spent her whole life imagining things bigger than they were.

It rose out of the gorge like a wall built by giants with something to hide—rough-hewn stone and packed earth, forty feet of it at least, spanning the canyon from wall to wall, furred over the years with saltbush and creeping yellow grass that had no business growing in a place this dry. Behind it, hidden from every eye in the territory, lay the source: a lake where no lake should be, dark and deep and patient, holding back enough water to drown every town along the river twice over.

Wren stood on the canyon rim with Juniper and stared down at it, and for a long moment neither of them said anything.

"That's it," Juniper said finally. Not a question.

"That's it." She could hear it now, faintly, beneath the wind—a sound like breathing, like the whole mountain exhaling through stone. Water moving under pressure against the far side of the dam, looking for a way out that had been denied it for years. "Elias's map didn't lie."

"Elias's map never lied," Juniper said. "It just made you work for the truth."

She glanced at him. He was thinner than when she'd met him—that lifetime ago, in a dusty waystation, when he'd had a badge and she'd had a letter and between them they'd had nothing but suspicion. Now his face was burned brown and cut with new scars, and there was something in his eyes she'd only seen once before, in Dry Hollow, looking at the sick children: a grief so large it had stopped being personal.

"Down there," she said quietly. "That's ten thousand people's worth of water. Maybe more. Sitting behind a wall while towns died of thirst and poison downstream."

"Hale didn't build it to hoard the water." Juniper's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man who had learned to say terrible things without letting them shake him. "He built it to control it. Sell it back to the people it belonged to. And kill anyone who found out."

They had found the workers' camp two days before—Ghost Water, Sera would have called it, if Sera were here. Rusted tools. Collapsed tents. A shallow trench lined with stones that Wren had recognized for what it was before her mind finished refusing to see it. Forty men, maybe fifty, who had built this wall and then been erased like chalk marks. Elias Marsh had been the surveyor. He had walked away because he couldn't stay silent, and Hale had poisoned him slow rather than quick, and Elias had spent his last months turning his dying into testimony—carving names and dates and figures into the stone of the springhouse with a prospector's chisel, hour by hour, while the poison worked its way through him.

Wren had read every word of it by lantern light. She had copied what she could onto paper, but paper was fragile, and paper could burn, and paper could be dismissed as forgery. The carvings could not. But carvings forty miles up a sealed gorge might as well not exist unless someone saw them who mattered.

Unless everyone saw them.

* * *

Juniper laid out the plan on a flat rock with bits of gravel and a strip of jerky for markers, the way he did everything—precise, unhurried, as if the world would wait for him to finish thinking. Wren had learned that about him. He wasn't slow. He was thorough, and thoroughness looked like slowness until the moment it looked like genius.

"The dam has a spillway channel on the east side," he said, tapping gravel into place. "Cut into the rock, meant to bleed off floodwater in wet years. It's been plugged—Hale's engineers filled it with rubble and timber so the level would keep rising. That's our weak point. We don't have to break the dam. We just have to unplug the drain and let the water do the rest."

"And the powder?" Wren asked.

"One cache left from the diversion crews. Elias marked it on the map—blasting powder the workers used to cut the spillway in the first place. Ironic enough to be almost funny." His mouth didn't smile. "We set charges at the plug, blow it clear, and the lake starts eating its own way out. Once it starts, nothing stops it. The flood goes down the gorge, through the old diversion channels, and rejoins the river proper above Dry Hollow."

"And carries everything with it." Wren pulled the oilcloth packet from inside her shirt—the one thing she'd carried since the Canyon of Names. Pages torn from Hale's own ledgers, wrapped around a rubbing she'd taken of Elias's carved confession, tied with wire and waxed against water. "Every scrap we can spare. It all goes in the flood."

"It'll be scattered. Ruined, some of it."

"Some of it will reach the towns," Wren said. "And the towns will know it came from upriver, from the source, and they'll come up here and see the rest with their own eyes. Carved in stone. You can't forge a canyon, Juniper."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw something pass across his face that she'd been watching grow for weeks and still didn't have a name for. Something that lived in the space between trust and the other thing, the bigger thing, the thing they'd both been too busy surviving to say out loud.

"When this is done," he started.

"Don't," she said. "Don't make promises at a dam. Bad luck."

"You believe in luck?"

"I believe in not tempting it." She forced a grin. "Ask me after the flood."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the work was brutal.

The powder cache was where Elias's map promised, half-buried under a rockfall near the old works, the crates swollen and cracked but intact, the powder inside still dry—the desert kept its secrets well. They hauled it down the switchback trail to the spillway in loads that made Wren's shoulders scream, while Juniper scouted the rim with a spyglass every hour, watching the southern approach where the riders would come from, if they came.

"They'll come," he said, on the fourth trip. "Sera's message reached the valley towns two days ago. Word travels fast when it's about water. Hale will know we're here before nightfall."

"Then we'd better be fast."

"We'd better be lucky," Juniper corrected. "Fast is optional."

They set the charges as the sun went down, tamping powder into the plugged spillway channel by lantern light, running fuse line back up the slope to a shelf of rock sixty yards out. Wren's hands shook—not from fear, or not only from fear, but from the sheer size of what they were about to do. This was the kind of act you couldn't take back. Everything before this moment had been running, hiding, gathering proof. This was the moment the proof became a weapon.

"Juniper." She crouched beside him at the firing point, fuse coil in her hands. "If this goes wrong—if the dam doesn't break clean, if it just cracks—we've flooded the gorge and told Hale exactly where we are."

"Then we'd better hope Elias knew his engineering better than Hale's men did." He struck the match. "He surveyed this canyon for thirty years. He told me once the river always wins. You just have to give it somewhere to go."

Wren took a breath. "For Elias," she said.

"For the towns," Juniper said.

"For the forty men in the trench at Ghost Water," she said, and struck her own match, and together they touched flame to fuse.

* * *

The fuse hissed away into the dark like a snake going home, and they counted, and the count got long, and Wren's heart climbed into her throat—and then the night turned white.

The explosion was less a sound than a fact. The air punched her flat against the rock shelf; grit rained down; the whole canyon rang like a struck bell. When she could see again, the spillway plug was gone—simply gone, blown into vapor and rubble—and behind it, in the wound they'd opened, the lake was already moving.

At first it was almost gentle. A tongue of black water sliding through the breach, spilling down the old channel, finding its feet. Then the pressure behind it did what pressure does. The breach widened with a groan that Wren felt in her teeth, in her sternum, in the bedrock under her knees. Chunks of dam face the size of wagons tore loose and vanished. The roar built and built until it swallowed thought, and the lake—the forbidden source, the stolen heart of the river—came out of the mountain like something that had been held prisoner for years and remembered everything.

"Go!" Juniper hauled her up. "High ground! Move!"

They ran. Behind them the dam was dying in stages, each collapse louder than the last, and the flood front went down the gorge ahead of them like an avalanche that had decided to become a river instead. Wren risked one look back and what she saw she knew she'd see every time she closed her eyes for the rest of her life: the wall breaking open top to bottom, moonlight caught in tons of falling water, the whole structure folding into the torrent it had imprisoned.

The river was free. The river was coming for everything below.

* * *

They reached the high ledge above the old ferry crossing as the first grey light came up, and that was where Wren saw the riders.

Six of them, maybe eight, small with distance—coming up the southern trail at a hard gallop, dust pluming behind them. Hale's men. Too late by an hour, maybe less. They reined up at the sight of the gorge, and even at this distance Wren could read their body language: the frozen stillness of men watching a catastrophe they couldn't stop.

Below them, the flood hit the first bend of the old diversion channel and spread out, slowing, spreading, doing what rivers do—finding every low place, filling every scar the diversion crews had cut, pouring itself back into the path it was born to follow. Miles downstream, invisible but certain, it was flowing toward Dry Hollow. Toward the sick children. Toward the towns that had forgotten what it was to drink without fear.

And riding on the water, scattered across miles of current, was the evidence. Ledger pages and wax-sealed packets, Elias's words in Wren's hand, Hale's own figures in Hale's own ink, tumbling and soaking and drifting toward the people they belonged to.

"They can't stop it," Juniper said. He stood at the edge of the ledge with the wind tearing at him, and he wasn't watching the riders anymore. He was watching the water. "There's nothing left for them to stop. It's done. It's—" His voice caught, and Wren, who had heard him lie smoothly to magistrates and calmly to flash floods, heard him fail at a single sentence, and understood that this failure was the truest thing he'd ever said.

"It's done," she agreed softly.

One of the riders detached from the group and started up the trail toward them alone. Even at distance, Wren knew the horse, and then the set of the shoulders, and then the way the rider sat like the saddle was a judgment seat.

"Sera," Juniper said.

"She's early," Wren said. "Or we're late."

"She's exactly on time. She always was." Juniper's hand moved toward his side, then stopped. Whatever he'd expected to find there—a gun, a warrant, a badge—he didn't seem to find it, or didn't want it. "Stay here. Let me talk to her."

"Not a chance," Wren said, and stepped up beside him, shoulder to shoulder, where the rising sun put them both in silhouette against the broken dam. "You don't get to have the hard conversations alone anymore. That was the old rule. The new rule is different."

Behind them, the last of the dam settled into ruin with a sound like a mountain sighing, and the source ran bright and unguarded in the morning light for the first time in years, and Sera Quill rode up the switchbacks toward them with her warrant in her hand and something else, something folded and official-looking, tucked in her belt—and Wren understood, with the particular clarity of a thief reading a mark, that whatever Sera had come to do, it wasn't arrest anyone.

The rider drew rein twenty feet away. Dust settled. The river roared below, carrying the truth to town.

"Juniper," Sera said, and her voice carried strangely in the thin air, steady and cracking at once. "You need to hear what happened at the toll bridge. And then you need to decide, right now, whether you're going to run from Hale's captain—or stand and meet him. Because he's thirty minutes behind me, and this time there's nowhere left to run."
