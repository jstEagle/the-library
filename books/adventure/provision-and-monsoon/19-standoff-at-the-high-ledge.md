## 19. Standoff at the High Ledge

The water came down like a verdict.

Juniper heard it before he saw it—a low, grinding roar that rose through the gorge floor and climbed the canyon walls, shaking grit loose from the ledges above. Then the flood arrived, a brown wall of the river's stolen life, bursting through the breach Wren and Sera's powder had torn in the dam, carrying splinters of timber, coils of rope, and the whole rotten architecture of Hale's lie down toward the towns that had forgotten what a full river looked like.

He stood on the high ledge with the evidence strapped against his chest—Elias's carved testimony copied onto oilcloth, the ledger pages wrapped in canvas—and watched ten years of silence drown.

Below, the gorge was becoming a lake. The water climbed fast, swallowing the trail they'd climbed two days ago, erasing the switchbacks, erasing the diversion camp with its shallow graves and its rusted tools, erasing everything except the truth, which floated.

"You should have kept running."

The voice came from behind him, and Juniper didn't turn. He knew it. Captain Dree Voss of the Magistrate's Riders had a voice like a boot heel on gravel, and she'd been three hours behind them since the ferry crossing, since the old boatman died, since the cost of the promise started coming due with interest.

Juniper turned slowly. Voss stood at the mouth of the ledge with six riders fanned out behind her, carbines leveled, their horses stamping and blowing at the smell of the flood. She was a broad, weathered woman with a face like sun-cracked clay, and her carbine was aimed at the center of his chest with the unhurried steadiness of someone who had done this a hundred times and expected to do it a hundred more.

"Captain," Juniper said.

"Ranger." She said his old title like an accusation. "Warrant says Juniper Vane, fugitive, armed, dangerous. Says the same about the thief and the sheriff who burned her own paper. That's three nooses I'm owed."

"The warrant's worthless now." He nodded past her, down at the water. "So is the dam. So is the magistrate who signed your pay. That's his river coming down. Every town on it is about to learn what he did."

"Then they can learn it at a trial. My job ends at your arrest." Voss's carbine didn't waver. "Hand over the papers. Hand over the woman. You can keep your sermon."

"They're not papers," Juniper said. "They're names. Forty-one names of men Hale's crews killed at the diversion camp and buried in the mud you're standing over. Elias Marsh's name is carved in the stone behind me, along with everything he saw before they poisoned him. You can shoot me, Captain, but the water's already carrying it downstream. You'd be killing a man for a story everyone's about to hear anyway."

Something moved in Voss's face—small, quickly buried, the way a crack moves through ice. He'd seen that look before, on his own face, in waystation mirrors, in the ten years he'd spent pretending a badge made him invisible.

"Behind you," Voss said. "The stone."

"Elias Marsh. Prospector. He spent his last month of life dying of Hale's poison and drawing me a map so the truth would outlive him." Juniper's voice stayed level, but he could feel the ledge under his boots, could feel the whole gorge trembling with the river's return. "I made him a promise. I intend to keep it."

"Sentiment," Voss said. "I've hanged men for less defensible things."

"I know. So have I." He let that sit. "Not today."

* * *

Wren was at his shoulder before he registered her movement—of course she was; ten years of hunting her had taught him she moved like water finding a crack, and apparently being allies hadn't changed it. Her left arm was still bound from the fever, her face still hollowed from the salt flats, but her eyes were bright and furious and fixed on Voss's carbine.

"Juniper," she said quietly. "The ledge is narrow. The water's rising. There are seven of them."

"I can count."

"Can you? Because from where I'm standing it looks like you're about to do something noble and stupid, and I've only just gotten used to you."

"Both of you," Voss said, "on your knees."

Juniper didn't kneel. Instead he did the thing he'd spent the whole climb deciding not to do, the thing every instinct of his decade as a hunter screamed against: he stepped forward, out onto the open rock, away from cover, and spread his empty hands.

"I'm not going to draw," he said. "I want you to look past me. Look down."

"Last warning—"

"Look *down*, Captain."

And because she was a professional, because curiosity is the one thing no soldier ever fully drills out of themselves, Voss glanced past him at the gorge.

The water had reached the base of the cliff. And on the far bank—impossible, absurd, arriving out of the very country that had spent three weeks trying to kill them—came people. First a dozen, then dozens, then a straggling river of them: townsfolk from Dry Hollow and the lower settlements, miners and mule drivers and women with children on their hips, following the flood up the old drover trails because the flood was a message and messages travel fast in a country that has been dying of thirst. Juniper could see them stopping at the drowned remains of the diversion camp, could see them wading into the shallows where the water was uncovering what the mud had kept—picks, boots, bones arranged in rows.

He watched a woman in the crowd below lift something small and white out of the water and go to her knees with it.

"That's forty-one men," Juniper said. "That's Dry Hollow's children, who drank what Hale poisoned. That's the towns that are going to want someone to answer for it. You can deliver me to Hale and be the captain who handed the towns' only witness to their murderer. Or you can lower that carbine and be the captain who stood aside when the truth came down the river." He took a breath. "I won't shoot you. I never was going to shoot you. But I'm not going to run anymore either. I'm done being invisible. Arrest me if you have to. But do it in front of them."

The gorge roared. The water climbed another foot.

Voss looked at the crowd. She looked at the bones. She looked, last and longest, at Juniper's empty hands, and he understood that she was doing the same arithmetic he'd done at a waystation with a dying man—weighing a promise against its price, and finding that some debts don't come due in coin.

"Riders," she said finally, and her voice had changed, gone quieter, rougher. "Carbines down."

Nobody moved.

"Carbines *down*. That's an order." And then, to Juniper, with something that might have been disgust and might have been respect: "You're still under arrest, Vane. All three of you. You'll stand where the towns can see you do it, and you'll testify with chains on if that's what it takes. I'm not letting you turn this into a legend. I'm making it a trial."

"Captain," Juniper said, "that's the first honest law I've seen on this river in ten years."

"Don't make me regret it." She swung down from her horse. "Where's the sheriff?"

"Sera's below," Wren said. "Wounded. Holding a pass she didn't have to hold, so we could get here. She'll want to be at that trial."

"Then she'll stand trial with the rest of you." Voss paused, and something human flickered through the clay of her face. "And I'll testify that she held it *well*."

* * *

They came down as the water came down, side by side—fugitives and riders, the hunted and the hunters, roped loosely together in a line that looked, from a distance, like nothing so much as a funeral procession walking into a resurrection. The townspeople parted for them in silence. Then a man Juniper recognized from Dry Hollow—old Tomasin, who'd sold him rope at a crossing years ago—stepped forward and looked at the carbines, at the ropes, at Juniper's face.

"You found it," Tomasin said. "You found where our river went."

"We found it," Juniper said. "And it found its way back."

The old man turned to the crowd and raised the small white thing he'd pulled from the water. A child's skull, no bigger than a fist, mud still in its hollows. He held it up the way you'd hold up a lamp, and the silence that fell over the gorge was deeper than the flood's roar.

"No more miles," Tomasin said. "No more running. We do this here. We do this *now*."

Juniper looked at Wren. She was pale, swaying on her feet, held up as much by stubbornness as by his arm around her. She met his eyes, and for the first time since the waystation, since the letter, since the whole long chase began, she smiled.

"Trial's in Dry Hollow," she said. "Hope you like testifying."

"I've had practice," Juniper said. "Ten years of it. Just never told the truth before."

Above them, the dam groaned, cracked, and gave way entirely, and the last of the river's stolen water came home.
