## 20. The Long Way Down

The river ran brown and honest beneath us, and for the first time in ten years I was following it the right direction.

I carried Wren on my back, her arms looped loose around my neck, her fever burning through the blanket I'd wrapped her in like a second sun. She weighed less than she should have. Three days of fever had burned through her like salt through ice, and what remained felt breakable, bird-boned, a thing the wind could take if I wasn't careful. Her breath came hot against my ear, shallow and quick, and every hundred steps I stopped to shift her weight and listen to it.

Still there. Still going.

"You're counting again," she murmured once, near dawn on the first day, half-delirious. "I can feel you counting."

"Counting what?"

"Miles." A ghost of a smile against my shoulder. "You count everything. It's how you pray."

I didn't answer that. The trail down from the gorge country was a goat path stitched into the canyon wall, switchbacking through talus and greasewood, and it took everything I had to keep my feet under me with her on my back. But she was right. I was counting. I'd spent a decade measuring the world in miles—miles between towns, miles between water holes, miles between me and whatever I didn't want to look at—and now every mile had a name at the end of it. Dry Hollow. Salt Lick. The ferry crossing where old Tomás had died so we could live. Provision. Monsoon Crossing. Towns that drank from this river. Towns that had been drinking poison for years because one man wanted their water rights cheap and their objections buried.

The flood we'd loosed at the dam was carrying the proof downstream ahead of us. Elias's carved testimony, the ledgers' copies sealed in oilcloth, the diversion camp records—all of it riding the brown water toward the towns like a letter that had taken ten years to arrive. All we had to do was follow it. All I had to do was keep walking, and keep her breathing, and not fall.

Simple arithmetic. Elias would have appreciated it.

* * *

We hit the first settlement on the evening of the second day—a cluster of adobe walls and cottonwood shade called Salt Lick, no more than forty people, a place so small it hadn't even made Hale's ledgers except as a line item. *Minor resistance. Resolved.*

I meant to walk straight through. Wren needed a real bed, a doctor if there was one, and Dry Hollow was still two days off. But an old woman drawing water at the river's edge saw us coming and dropped her bucket, and then there were people all around us before I could decide whether to run.

"It's them," someone said. "From the gorge. The ones the riders were asking after."

My hand went to the rifle sling out of pure habit, and then I saw their faces and let it drop. Nobody looked afraid of me. They looked afraid of something else—the thing they'd seen in the water that morning, maybe, or the thing they hadn't seen in it for years.

"The river," the old woman said. She gripped my sleeve with fingers like dried chilis. "Three days ago it ran black-bellied, foul, the way it has since I was a girl. Then the flood came down and—" She stopped. Started again. "It runs clean. Do you understand? It runs clean for the first time in thirty years. My husband drank from it last night and didn't wake with his gut knotted. My granddaughter—"

She couldn't finish. Another voice did, a lean farmer with dust in the creases of his face: "The children are better. Not well. But better. Whatever was killing them slow—it's gone from the water."

They looked at me like I'd done it. Like I'd personally reached into the mountains and torn the sickness out by the root. And I stood there with a dying thief on my back and a dead prospector's promise in my chest and said the only true thing I had:

"Don't thank me. Thank a man named Elias Marsh. He paid for this. So did others." I thought of Tomás on the ferry deck, of Sera holding the pass alone somewhere above us, bleeding, sending word ahead instead of following. "A lot of others."

They gave us a room anyway. A real bed, a pallet really, in the back of the meeting house, and a woman named Ada Reyes who had doctored both animals and men and saw no difference in the difficulty. She fed Wren willow bark and broth and cold cloths, and she looked at me over the patient with eyes that missed nothing.

"Fever's been three days?"

"Four."

"She'll live," Ada said, "if the fever breaks tonight. If it doesn't—" She shrugged, the shrug of a woman who had delivered bad news too many times to dress it up. "Sit with her. Talking helps sometimes. Even when they can't hear you. Especially then."

So I sat. And in the lamplight, with the sound of the clean river running past outside the window, I talked.

I told her about the waystation and Elias Marsh coughing blood into his bandana while he drew me a map with a shaking hand. I told her about the letter Wren stole, and how I'd caught her mid-theft and hesitated—one hesitation, ten seconds long, that had unraveled a decade of my life and rebuilt it into something else. I told her about the flash flood, the ambush at the ferry, the canyon wall covered in prospectors' marks, Elias's among them, cut deep enough to outlast him. I told her things I hadn't told anyone, some of which I hadn't admitted to myself until the words came out: that I'd known, somewhere in me, for years, that the towns along the lower river were sick in a way theft couldn't explain. That I'd policed stolen chickens and lifted purses while murder wore a magistrate's seal and walked around in daylight. That the pardon Sera arranged for me ten years ago hadn't been mercy. It had been a leash, and I'd worn it so long I'd mistaken it for freedom.

Wren slept through most of it. Near midnight her fever peaked—she shivered hard enough to rattle the cot frame, mumbling names I didn't know, places I'd never seen, a childhood in the green country far east of here that she'd never once mentioned on the trail. And then, sometime before dawn, the shivering slowed. Her skin cooled under my hand. Her breathing deepened, evened, found a rhythm I could count without fear.

Ada checked her at first light and nodded once. "She'll live."

I went outside and sat on the meeting house steps and watched the sun come up over the river, and I found that my eyes were wet, and I didn't much care who saw.

* * *

Wren woke properly on the third morning, demanding food with a voice like gravel, and by noon she insisted on walking. Insisted being a generous word; she managed twenty paces leaning on my arm before her knees quit, and I carried her back to the cot while she cursed me with impressive creativity.

"I'm not luggage," she said.

"No. Luggage is useful."

That earned me a weak laugh, which earned her another bowl of broth, which she drank while interrogating Ada Reyes about everything that had happened since the dam. When she got to the part about the flood carrying the evidence downstream, she went quiet, turning the bowl in her hands.

"They'll have heard by now," she said. "Dry Hollow. Provision. All of them. The water changed, and the papers came with it. They'll know what Hale did." Her eyes came up to mine. "And they'll know we're coming. Juniper—the riders won't stop. Hale's finished if we reach those towns, and he knows it. His captain will throw everything left at us before we get there."

"I know."

"And Sera. Have you heard anything? Any word from the pass?"

"Nothing." I'd asked at every homestead. No rider, no note, no deputy. Either Sera was still holding her ground somewhere above us, or—

I didn't finish the thought. Wren watched me not finish it, and reached over and put her hand on mine, and neither of us said anything else for a while. Some silences are heavier than speech. That one held both our weights without complaint.

We stayed two more days, because Wren could barely stand and because the people of Salt Lick wouldn't hear of us leaving sooner. On the second evening the whole settlement turned out at the riverbank—forty-odd souls, most of them thin and weathered and marked by years of drinking poisoned water—and they sang. Old songs, river songs, songs I didn't know, sung off-key and out of time while the clean water caught the sunset and turned it to hammered copper. A girl of maybe eight, the granddaughter of the woman with the bucket, danced in the shallows barefoot, splashing like the water was new. To her, I suppose it was.

Wren leaned into my shoulder and watched. "Look at that," she said quietly. "Ten days ago I was a thief running from a ranger. Now I'm—" She gestured at the singing, the dancing child, the whole improbable evening. "What am I?"

"Alive," I said. "Start there. The rest is negotiable."

* * *

We left Salt Lick on the fifth day, Wren riding a borrowed mule and hating it, me walking beside her with the rifle across my back and the map—Elias's map, soft as cloth now from handling—in my shirt pocket. We followed the river down through country I knew the way other men know their own houses: the red bluffs above the Narrows, the dead orchard at Miller's Bend, the long gravel shoals where I'd once tracked a horse thief for six days and brought him back to a justice that, I understood now, only worked for the people it chose to work for.

Every mile, the river told its story. At the Narrows we found the high-water line of our own flood, a brown stain on the stone six feet up, snagged with debris—and wedged in the rocks, half-buried, a piece of timber stamped with the Hale Consolidated Water mark. Wren fished it out and turned it in her hands and smiled a hard little smile. "Even the river testifies," she said. We left it standing upright in the mud at the trail junction, a marker any traveler could read.

At Miller's Bend, a farm family flagged us down—not to warn us away but to give us bread and eggs and ask, shyly, if the stories were true. That the magistrate had dammed the source. That the workers who built the dam had been killed to keep it quiet. That a ranger and a thief and a sheriff had broken it open. I told them what I could prove and pointed at the river for the rest. The father listened with his hat in his hands, and when I finished he said, "My boy was sick two years. Doctors from Provision, doctors from the coast, nobody could say why." He looked at the water running clean past his fields. "Now I know why. Now everybody knows why." He pressed the eggs on us like they were gold and wouldn't take payment.

Word traveled faster than we did. By the time we crossed into the Dry Hollow valley on the eighth day, everyone we met already knew who we were, and nobody looked at us the way people used to look at a ranger and his prisoner. They looked at us like we were neighbors. I kept waiting for that to feel strange. It never quite did.

But we also saw, twice, riders on the far bluffs—four horses, moving parallel, keeping their distance. Hale's men, or what remained of them, shadowing us down the valley. Watching. Waiting for orders that hadn't come, or weighing whether their paychecks were worth dying for now that the whole river knew their employer's name. I didn't reach for the rifle. I just noted them, the way I'd once noted tracks, and kept walking. Fear needs an audience. Alone up there on the rocks, they were starting to learn what I'd learned on the flats: that the empty miles don't care whose side you're on.

* * *

Dry Hollow at dusk was unrecognizable.

Not the buildings—they were the same wind-scoured adobe and tin I remembered, the same crooked bell tower, the same street that turned to soup in rain. What was different was the sound. When I'd ridden through eight months ago, chasing a petty thief and feeling nothing, the town had been quiet in the wrong way: the silence of a place conserving its strength, doors shut early, children kept indoors, a community holding its breath. Now the street was full. Lanterns everywhere. People on stoops and in doorways, talking, arguing, laughing—the rough, disbelieving laughter of people testing whether joy is allowed again. Someone had strung bunting between the feed store and the well house, faded cloth that had probably hung there for a festival years ago and never come down until now.

And the children were outside. Dozens of them, running through the dusk, playing some game involving chalk lines and shouting, and I stopped in the middle of the street and watched them because I couldn't help it. I thought of the sickroom I'd stood in on my first visit, the gray-faced boy named Mateo whose mother had asked me—not the doctor, not the magistrate, me, because I was the only arm of authority that ever came—to find out what was killing them. I'd told her I'd look into it. I'd ridden out the next morning and looked into a horse theft instead, because that was the job, because the job was the shape of my hiding.

Mateo was in the street now. Chalk in his hand, thinner than a boy his age should be, but running. Running flat-out, laughing, alive.

He saw me before I decided whether to approach. He skidded to a stop, stared, and then shouted something to the other kids, and suddenly I had a dozen children orbiting me, asking questions faster than I could answer—was it true, did he really blow up a dam, was there really a treasure map, had I fought the magistrate's men—and behind them came the mothers and fathers, and behind them the town, and I stood there in the lantern light with a thief on a mule beside me and realized I was, for the first time in my adult life, exactly where I was supposed to be.

The boy's mother found me near the well. She didn't say anything at first. She just took my hand in both of hers and held it, and her eyes said the rest, and I let her hold it as long as she wanted.

"He asks about you," she said finally. "Since the water changed. He says the ranger will come back and make it right." She laughed, wet-eyed. "I told him rangers don't come back. I owe you an apology."

"No," I said. "You owe me nothing. I'm the one who took ten years to answer."

Wren appeared at my elbow, moving under her own power now, pale but upright, and surveyed the lantern-lit street with a thief's appraisal. "Nice town," she said. "Terrible security. You could steal anything here."

"You would know."

"I would know," she agreed, and bumped her shoulder against mine, and left it there.

An elder of the town council found us an hour later, a spare gray woman named Odile Ferran who had signed the original petition to Hale asking for clean water and gotten, in return, a visit from his riders. She spread papers on the well house table: copies of the ledgers' pages that had come down with the flood, already circulating from town to town, each settlement adding its own testimony—dates of sickness, deaths, the names of the diverted springs, the amounts Hale's judges had been paid. A case, assembling itself the way water finds its level.

"The trial is set," she said. "Here, in Dry Hollow, in nine days. Every town on the river is sending witnesses. The territorial governor, may he be embarrassed, has been petitioned to allow it locally rather than drag everyone to the capital—and given what's in these pages, half the capital's judges need watching too." She looked at me over the lamplight. "Your testimony will matter, Ranger Vane. More than anyone's. You saw the source. You saw the camp. You can put Elias Marsh's dying words on the record."

"I'll testify," I said. It wasn't even a decision. It was just the last mile of a road I'd been walking since a waystation cot.

"There's one more thing." Odile's voice gentled. "The magistrate has been taken. His own captain surrendered him this morning at Provision—said the man's money had stopped meaning anything. He's being held in the grain warehouse under guard of volunteers, because half the deputies in the territory won't guard him and the other half can't be trusted to." She paused. "There's also a warrant outstanding. For you. For the breakout at the toll bridge, the destruction of the garrison lockup, and the assault on a territorial officer. The governor hasn't rescinded it. Whoever testifies against Hale will be testifying as a criminal himself, unless—" She let it hang.

Unless I ran. Unless I did what I'd always done, folded myself back into the miles, let the towns tell the story without me, stayed a ghost. The old life was right there, offered up on a plate: testify by letter, stay anonymous, vanish downriver before the trial, keep the badge and the invisibility both.

I looked at Wren. She was watching me steadily, arms crossed, and she didn't say a word—didn't argue, didn't plead. She'd learned on the trail that some choices a man has to make out loud, in front of witnesses, or they don't take.

"I'll be here," I said. "Nine days. I'll surrender the warrant myself, before I testify. My name goes on the record next to Elias Marsh's. Both of them. That's the deal."

Odile studied me for a long moment, then nodded slowly, the nod of a woman crossing a name off one list and writing it on another. "Then rest, Ranger. You've got nine days, and a town that owes you its children."

Outside, the monsoon clouds were building in the west—huge, blue-black thunderheads stacked over the gorge country, the first of the season, still hours away but coming on like something promised. The air had that charged, waiting taste. Down the street, the children had lit a bonfire in the plaza and were feeding it scraps of bunting, and the sparks went up into the darkening sky like the river's evidence going down to the sea, everything moving at last, everything headed somewhere.

Wren stood beside me watching the storm build. "Nine days," she said.

"Nine days."

"And after?" She didn't look at me when she asked it. She didn't have to.

"After," I said, "I was thinking of staying. Measuring something other than miles." The first drops hit the dust between our boots, fat and warm, smelling of wet stone and distance. "If the company suits you."

The rain came down harder, and the whole town came out into it—singing, shouting, faces turned up—because it was the first monsoon in years that fell on a river that ran clean, and nobody in Dry Hollow was willing to waste a single drop of it indoors.
