## 22. Provision and Monsoon

### Juniper

The judge in Dry Hollow gave him two hundred hours of labor and a sentence that was no sentence at all: *stay where we can see you.*

Juniper had expected worse. He had stood before the bench half-hoping for worse, the way a man hopes for a clean break instead of a slow one. But Judge Amara Osei had looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment, and then at Wren Calloway sitting in the gallery with her arm still bound from the gorge, and said, "This town drank poison for three years because men like you were too busy counting stolen chickens to look upstream. You'll fix what's broken here. That's your term."

So he stayed.

The first weeks were shovels and lime. He helped dig new cisterns along Dry Hollow's eastern rim, hauling stone in a cart whose axle he greased every morning out of habit, because a ranger learns that everything fails without maintenance—bridges, wagons, promises. The children who had been sick came out to watch him work. At first they kept their distance, the way you keep distance from anything that carries a badge, even a broken one. Then one of them—a girl named Tally with the gray pallor of arsenic still fading from her lips—asked him if the river would stay clean.

"It will," he said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I watched it come down off the mountain," he told her. "And I know what it cost."

She considered this with the gravity only children and judges possess, then handed him a dried apricot, which was, as far as Juniper could reckon, the first payment anyone had ever offered him for telling the truth. He ate it slowly. It tasted better than any pardon ever had.

His mile markers went into the ground in the third week.

He'd carried them the whole length of the journey—the iron stakes he'd hammered into earth across ten years of empty country, each one a small declaration: *a man passed here, measured, moved on.* He'd kept them bundled on his pack even after the flood took nearly everything else, even after the ferryman died and the canyon closed behind them and there was nothing left to carry but the ledgers and the dead man's map. He'd told himself it was habit. He'd told himself a lot of things for a decade.

Now he planted them in a line outside the schoolhouse, all eleven of them, driven deep with the maul until they stood straight as fence posts. The children asked what they marked. Nothing, he said. Or everything. He hadn't decided yet.

Wren found him there at dusk, wiping rust from the last stake.

"You're building something," she said. It wasn't quite an accusation. It wasn't quite not one.

"I'm putting them down," he said. "There's a difference."

She sat on the schoolhouse step and watched him work, and the light went long and amber across the flats, and neither of them said the thing that had been riding unspoken between them since the salt flats, since her fever, since the night he'd carried her three miles through rising water with her head against his chest and her pulse hammering against his palm like something trying to get free.

### Wren

Wren Calloway had stolen many things in her life: watches, horses, a bishop's ring, once—and this was her favorite story—an entire afternoon from a tax collector by convincing him his own shadow was following him. She was good at taking things. She was terrible at being given them.

Which was the problem with Juniper Vane.

He gave without ceremony. He gave her water when hers ran out on the salt flats and never mentioned it after. He gave her the dry side of the bedroll at the garrison and the wet side of the tarp in the gorge and never once made it a ledger entry. In Dry Hollow he'd given her a room in the boarding house two doors from his own, paid for out of wages he earned hauling stone, and when she'd tried to argue he'd said, "You dug the ledgers out of a canyon wall with your bare hands. Consider it back pay."

She didn't know how to steal from a man who kept giving things away. It ruined her whole method.

So she worked. The town gave her community service too—two hundred hours, same as him, though hers came with the judge's dry remark that "reformed thieves make excellent auditors." She reorganized the general store's books and found the owner had been shorted by a supplier for two years. She taught Tally and three other children to pick a lock, then spent four times as long teaching them why they shouldn't. She mended fences. She learned the names of things that grew here: mesquite, saltbush, a stubborn yellow flower the locals called soldier's delight that bloomed only after rain.

It hadn't rained in fourteen months.

Every evening she walked past the schoolhouse and looked at the iron stakes standing in their row, and every evening Juniper was there or wasn't, and either way she felt the same tightness in her chest that she used to feel standing outside a vault door—except there was no vault, no lock to pick, no angle to run. Just a man planting markers in ground he finally meant to keep, and a thief with no plan except the terrifying possibility of staying.

On the forty-first evening, she stopped walking past.

"You know what those are?" she said, nodding at the stakes.

"Mile markers."

"They're grave markers," she said. "For the man you used to be. Eleven of them. That's a lot of funerals."

He looked up at her, and the last light caught the lines around his eyes, deeper than they'd been a year ago, and easier somehow. "I buried him shallow," he said. "In case I need to check that he's staying down."

"He's staying down." She sat beside him on the step, close enough that their shoulders touched. "I checked. I'm very thorough."

"I know," Juniper said. "You stole my letter."

"Your dying prospector's letter."

"My whole life, then." He turned his hand over on the step between them, palm up, an offer with no pressure behind it—the way he did everything now. "What are you going to do with it?"

Wren looked at that open hand for a long time. Then she did the bravest thing she'd ever done, braver than the dam, braver than the gorge, braver than walking into Hale's garrison with a smile and a lie: she put her hand in his and didn't take anything.

### Juniper

The monsoon came on a Tuesday.

He knew it before he saw it. Ten years of reading country teaches a man the sky's handwriting, and the sky had been writing for three days—clouds stacking blue-black over the western mesa, air gone thick and sweet, the soldier's delight flowers trembling on their stems like they knew. The whole town felt it. Doors opened. People stood in the streets looking west with the naked hope of people who have been thirsty so long they've forgotten hoping is allowed.

Sera Quill arrived the same morning, riding down from the north with dust on her coat and a scar on her jaw that hadn't been there at the toll bridge.

Juniper met her at the town's edge. For a moment neither spoke. The last time they'd stood face to face, she'd been burning her warrant in front of thirty armed men, choosing ruin over obedience, and he'd been running for the high country with her covering his escape. Before that, she'd been the law and he'd been its hound, and between those two versions of themselves lay a decade of miles and one corrupt magistrate's leash.

"You look terrible," Sera said.

"You look employed."

"Suspended. Pending review." She shrugged. "Turns out burning a warrant is complicated paperwork. Turns out testifying against the magistrate's captain is more complicated." She dismounted and stood beside him, both of them watching the storm build. "Osei wrote me a letter. Says Dry Hollow needs a sheriff who knows which laws matter. Says she heard I might be between positions."

"She hears a lot for a town this size."

"Towns that almost died talk about everything." Sera glanced sideways at him. "She also says there's a man here doing two hundred hours of community service who used to be the best tracker in the territory. Says when his hours are done, maybe he'd want steady work. Honest work. Work where nobody hands him pardons."

Juniper was quiet for a while. The wind had come up, carrying the smell of wet dust from fifty miles away—the smell of provision, the land keeping its promise at last.

"I don't want a badge," he said finally.

"Good. I'm not offering one. I'm offering a job. There's a difference." She smiled, thin and real. "You taught me that. Or Wren did. Hard to say who corrupted whom."

Thunder rolled down off the mesa, long and low, like the mountain clearing its throat.

* * *

### Wren

The rain hit at sundown and it hit like a verdict.

Not a polite rain, not a soaking-in rain—a monsoon, the sky splitting open all at once, water coming down in gray ropes that turned the street to river inside a minute. The town poured out of its houses screaming and laughing. Children ran naked through the flood channels. Old Marta Reyes stood in the middle of the street with her arms spread and her face turned up, sobbing without shame, and around her the whole settlement of Dry Hollow did the same—every soul who'd drunk poison for three years standing under clean water falling straight out of heaven, unowned by any magistrate, unbought, undiverted, free.

Wren stood in the doorway of the boarding house and let it soak her through.

She thought about the diversion camp in the gorge, the bones they'd found under the collapsed timbers, the workers Hale had killed to keep his secret. She thought about the old boatman at the ferry crossing, whose name she'd never learned, who'd died so strangers could escape. She thought about Elias Marsh, the dying prospector whose arithmetic had started everything—one map, one promise, one truth traded for a life already spent. The rain fell on all of them equally now. That was the whole point of rain. That was the whole point, maybe, of everything worth building.

Juniper came to stand beside her in the doorway, dripping, and she saw that he was holding something: one of the mile markers, the iron stake pulled fresh from the ground, mud still on it.

"You're taking them back?" she asked.

"No." He held it out to her. "I'm giving you one. First thing I've ever given away that mattered."

Wren took it. It was heavier than it looked, cold and wet, ordinary as a fence post. "What does it mark?"

"Nothing yet." His voice was rough. "That's the idea. Every marker I ever planted said *a man passed here.* This one says *he stopped.*"

She looked at the stake, and at the man, and out at the drowned street where Tally was dancing in the flood channel with her arms around two other children, all of them shrieking, all of them alive, their color coming back day by day like spring returning to a burned hillside.

Home, Wren had always believed, was a word for people with nothing better to steal. Standing there, soaked and holding an iron post in the rain, she understood at last that she'd had it backward. Home was the one thing you couldn't take. You could only be given it, and hold it, and plant it deep enough to last.

"Well," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because some cracks are just doors opening. "We'd better find somewhere to put it."

They planted it together in the mud outside the schoolhouse, hammering it down while the rain hammered down on them, while the town sang and shouted, while the first monsoon of the rest of their lives turned the desert green as far as the eye could see. When it was set, Juniper put his hand flat on top of the wet iron, and Wren put hers over his, and the marker stood straight in the ground with no number on it and no name—just a place, and two people, and nowhere else either of them needed to be.

Behind them, lightning lit the western mesa white, and the river, far off in the gorge country, ran loud and full toward the sea, carrying everything downstream at last.

*The end.*
