## 1. The Weight of Moving

The supercell came up out of the southwest like a debt called in, and Elodie Galloway drove toward it because that was the only direction she knew how to afford.

She'd been running the flats for six hours, the chase rig's tires humming a low complaint against the cracked asphalt of the old haul road. On the dash, her instruments painted the storm in greens and yellows and one ugly smear of magenta where the rotation was tightening. She watched it the way other people watched bank statements—with the flat attention of someone who already knew the number.

Fuel: three-quarters of a tank, which meant two hundred miles of mercy and then nothing. Debt: four hundred and twelve days since she'd last paid the Vane brothers anything but interest, and the interest had a way of growing teeth. Distance to the Corren Range: still too far, always too far, the mountains sitting on the horizon like creditors who hadn't yet learned her address.

The arithmetic never changed. That was the thing nobody told you about chasing—that it wasn't freedom at all, it was just motion with a price per mile. Every storm she sold footage from bought her another week of fuel and another week of not stopping, because stopping was where the numbers caught you. She had learned to sleep in the rig, eat from cans, wash in depot sinks. She had learned to calculate her life in gallons.

The cell was a beauty, though. She'd give it that. It stood on the flats like a cathedral built by something with no use for cathedrals, the anvil top spreading gray and vast, the wall cloud underneath turning with a slow, patient hunger. Lightning walked inside it like a man pacing a room. Elodie pulled off onto a gravel shoulder, killed the engine, and let the rig rock in the inflow wind while she shot forty minutes of footage she might sell for enough to cover the next fill-up and half a payment, if the buyer in Meridian was feeling generous, which he usually wasn't.

Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady. It was everything behind them that shook.

* * *

The fuel depot at Kessel's Crossing was a single pump, a single light, and a proprietor who'd stopped asking questions around the same time Elodie stopped having answers. She was screwing the cap back on the tank when the headlights swung in off the road—big, unhurried, the kind of lights that didn't need to hurry—and she knew before the door opened who it would be.

Rill Vane's collector was named Rill, which was either a family name or an irony; Elodie had never decided. He was a narrow man with a narrow face and the terrible patience of weather. He stepped out of his sedan holding two paper cups of coffee and offered her one, and that was the worst part, that courtesy. The Vane brothers were not violent men. They were optimistic men—they genuinely believed everyone could pay—and their optimism was a machine that ground slower than any fist and finer.

"Galloway." He nodded at the sky, where the storm was walking away east, trailing its lightning. "Good cell tonight."

"Good cell," she agreed.

"Meridian buyer took your footage?"

"He will."

"Then you'll want to hear the good news." Rill sipped his coffee. Behind him, the depot light buzzed and moths threw themselves against it with small, devoted violence. "My brothers ran your numbers again. They're optimists, you know. They figure there's a version of this where you come out whole." He turned the cup in his thin fingers. "Contract money from the range job. That's real money, if the company pays. They'll take it—all of it—against what you owe. And they'll give you until first snowfall to bring it to them personally."

Elodie looked at him. "And if the snow comes first?"

Rill smiled the way frost forms—not unkindly, inevitably. "Then they stop being optimistic. You don't want them pessimistic, Galloway. Pessimistic men collect differently." He set the second coffee on her hood, untouched, a small white flag or a small white threat. "First snowfall. The passes close after that anyway. Even if we wanted to find you, we couldn't."

He got back in his sedan and drove away without hurry, and Elodie stood at the pump with her hand flat on the warm hood and did the only math left to her: contract pay, minus debt, minus fuel, minus food, equals zero. Equals exactly zero, if nothing went wrong, and things went wrong the way storms went wrong—suddenly, and all at once.

First snowfall. She'd been given a season, which was more than the storm gave anyone.

* * *

She found Hask eleven miles north of the crossing, where the haul road bent toward the foothills and the dark came down early this time of year.

He was lying at the road's edge like something the wind had set down carefully, an old man in a prospector's canvas coat, and at first she took him for dead. Then he coughed—a wet, grinding sound, like gravel turning over in a sluice—and she was out of the rig and kneeling beside him before she'd decided to be.

"Easy. Easy, I've got you." She got an arm under his shoulders. His breath came in shallow pulls, and when he exhaled she smelled it—metallic, faintly sweet, the smell of ore dust worked deep into lungs until the lungs themselves became part of the mine. Silver-dust. She'd heard of it. Miners out of the Corren camps used to call it *the bright death*, because it glittered.

"Hauling water," Hask rasped, as if explaining himself. "Got to keep the garden going." There was no garden. There was nothing within twenty miles but road and dark and the first cold stars coming out over the range.

"You're not hauling anything tonight." She got him into the passenger seat, wrapped him in the blanket she kept for stakeouts, and sat with the heater running while he fought for air. His eyes, when they focused on her, were the pale washed-out blue of old ice, and they were terribly clear.

"You're a chaser," he said. "Storm rig. I seen you work the flats two seasons now."

"You've seen me lose money two seasons."

"I seen you *keep moving*." A cough took him, long and ruinous, and when it passed he lay back against the seat with his eyes closed. "That's rarer than you'd think."

He reached inside his coat with fingers that moved like they were remembering how, and brought out an oilcloth packet, tied with wire. He held it on his knees between them.

"There's a claim up in the Correns," he said. "Silver. Good silver—the kind they wrote songs about and then stole. I was there. I dug it. And the company that owned our wages—" another cough, "—owned the sheriff too, so nobody was ever paid, and the ones who complained are buried above the tree line, and the ones who didn't complain are mostly dead of the bright death like me, and the widows are still down in the valley settlement pretending they don't know why their men never came home rich."

Elodie said nothing. The heater ticked. Somewhere far off, the dying storm grumbled over the eastern flats.

"This is the map to it." He patted the packet once, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Every marker, every bearing. Worth more than everything I ever dug out of that mountain, because it's the only way back to it."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I'm done moving, girl, and you're not." He turned those ice-pale eyes on her. "Two things. One: the map's yours. Sell it, spend it, I don't care—it's worth more than any debt you owe, and I can see a debt on a person the way I see weather coming. Two:" and here his voice dropped, and the clarity in it frightened her more than the coughing had, "the ledger goes to the widows. Names, dates, every wage they stole. It's in my coat. You carry it down the mountain and you read it out loud where they can hear it, before the passes close. That's the price. Map for promise. Say it."

Elodie looked at the packet. Four hundred and twelve days of arithmetic sat in her chest, and here was a number that erased them all—a claim, a fortune, a door out of the moving. All she had to do was say yes and mean only half of it.

Hask watched her do the math. He'd watched men do worse math for less.

"Say it," he said again, softer.

Outside, high over the black line of the Corren Range, the first clouds of the next system were already gathering, patient as creditors, white as snow.

"I promise," Elodie Galloway said, and felt the words settle into her like ballast—like weight, finally, worth carrying.
