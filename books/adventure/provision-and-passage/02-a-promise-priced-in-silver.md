## 2. A Promise Priced in Silver

Elodie spread the map across the folded-down tailgate of her rig, weighting the corners with four stones because the wind off the Corren foothills wanted it more than she did. Dawn came up gray and thin, the kind of light that made everything look like it was already leaving.

The map was drawn on oilcloth, soft as cloth and tough as hide, inked in a hand that shook only at the ends of strokes—Hask's hands had been bad by then, silver-dust tremor working its way up from his lungs into his fingers. The claim itself sat in a hanging valley above the old Kestrel workings, marked with an X so crude it was almost insulting. But around it, Hask had annotated everything. Springs. Timber stands. A shelf of rock where he swore the ore ran in veins thick as a man's wrist. Elevations. Distances measured not in miles but in hours of walking, which told her he'd expected whoever carried this map to be on foot.

She did the arithmetic the way she always did, because arithmetic was the one thing that never lied to her.

A verified silver claim, even a played-out one, even a rumor of one, sold to any of a dozen speculators in Corren Crossing for enough to erase what she owed the Vane brothers twice over. Enough to buy back the rig's title outright. Enough to put fuel in the tanks for five years of chasing without doing the math at every depot, without lying awake listening to the interest compound like weather building on the flats. Rill would know she had it—the moment she'd taken anything from Hask, she'd become interesting to men like him—and the Vane brothers' optimism, that terrible sunny faith that every debt could be collected if you just believed hard enough, would come down the mountain after her like a front line.

One map. One promise. And between them, the whole weight of what she owed.

She'd promised a dying man. That was the trouble with promises—they were easy to make when the man making the ask was already half gone, his breath rattling like gravel in a pan, his eyes fixed on her with the desperate clarity of someone spending his last coin. *The widows,* he'd said. *Kestrel camp, and the two families that went down to Miller's Bend. They worked those years and never saw a wage. My ledger tells them what they're owed. You tell them I tried.*

She hadn't asked why he couldn't deliver it himself. She'd known. Dying men don't climb passes.

* * *

She broke camp before full light, and the ascent began gently, which was the mountain's way of lying to her.

Hask's route climbed the eastern shoulder of the range through country that had been worked and abandoned so many times the land itself seemed tired of it. By midmorning she passed the first ghost camp—a scatter of collapsed cabins sinking back into the scree, their roofs caved in under decades of snowload, a rusted head-frame leaning against the slope like a drunk against a wall. A sign still hung by one nail over what had been a mess hall: KESTREL CONSOLIDATED, and beneath it, smaller, someone had painted in fading letters: WAGES PAID WEEKLY.

Someone else had crossed it out.

She stopped the rig and walked through the camp because Hask's map said to—there was a spring behind the third cabin, still running cold and sweet, and she filled her bottles there while the wind moved through the broken windows making sounds like low conversation. In one cabin she found a child's boot, small enough to fit in her palm, left on a shelf as though someone meant to come back for it. Nobody had come back. The company had gone under—or hadn't gone under, had simply stopped paying, which amounted to the same thing for the people waiting on payday—and the mountain had kept the difference.

She thought about the ledger wrapped in oilskin in her pack, next to the map. Names. Dates. Amounts. Hask had kept it for thirty years, she realized, carrying it from camp to camp, through the boom years when everyone got paid and it didn't matter, through the bust years when it mattered enormously and no one could collect. He'd been a thief of wages himself once—that much he'd admitted, coughing, ashamed—and the ledger was his penance, itemized.

And she was going to carry it up a mountain in winter for people she'd never met, while the thing that could actually save her sat folded in the same pack, burning a hole through the canvas.

"You could sell it," she said aloud, to the empty cabin, and the wind took the words and gave nothing back.

* * *

The second day the road ended, and Hask's route became a track, and the track became a suggestion.

She drove the rig as far as it would go—a switchback cut into the mountainside above a valley full of dead mining town, tailings fans spreading below like gray glaciers—and then the switchback itself gave out into rubble, and she stood on the brake with her heart hammering, looking at a wall of rock ahead and a drop behind, and understood that this was as far as four wheels went.

She should have turned around. Every instinct she'd built in ten years of chasing storms said turn around, live to run another day, keep your options open, keep your escape route clear. Options were oxygen. Debt was drowning, and you didn't drown yourself further on principle.

But the map showed a foot trail cutting up through the talus, marked in Hask's shaking hand: *CUT ABOVE THE SLIDE FIELD — GO EARLY, ROCK LOOSENS WITH SUN.* And beyond it, three days' walk to the pass, and beyond the pass, the valley settlement where the widows waited, if they still waited, if they were still alive, if thirty years hadn't scattered them like chaff.

She packed light. Water, the ledger, the map, the contract papers proving she'd been paid honest money for the chase season—money she'd been planning to hand to Rill, money that wouldn't even cover the interest now that she thought about it honestly, money that suddenly looked very small. Rope she didn't have. Cold-weather gear adequate for autumn, laughable for winter. She locked the rig, which felt absurd—locking a truck on a dead switchback above a ghost town—but habit was habit.

The slide field caught her at noon.

She heard it before she saw it—a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, deep and grinding—and she was forty meters onto the talus when the slope above the trail let go. Not a big slide. A medium one. The kind that doesn't kill you but takes everything you were standing on.

She ran. The rock moved under her feet like water deciding to flow, and she made the far side, made the solid ground beyond the slide field, and turned around with her lungs full of dust to look at the route she'd climbed.

It wasn't there anymore. Forty meters of trail, gone under fresh scree, and worse—below, at the base of the switchback, half-buried under a fan of new rubble, sat her rig. Her home for ten years. Her radar mast bent double, her instrument rack crushed, her fuel, her shelter, her entire life reduced to a shape under the rocks that used to be a truck.

She stood there a long time. Long enough for the dust to settle. Long enough for the sun to move and the shadows to change and the wind to pick up again, carrying the first real bite of winter in it.

She could go back down. Down through the ghost camps, down to the crossing, find Rill, hand over the map, take whatever mercy the Vane brothers' optimism allowed. It was the sensible play. It was the play the arithmetic demanded.

Instead she found herself looking up. Three days to the pass. The sky above it was the color of a bruise coming in, a front building over the high country right on schedule, right on the edge of the season. Somewhere past that pass, women who'd been robbed thirty years ago were about to learn they'd been remembered.

Elodie pulled the map from her pack, checked Hask's shaky annotations one more time, and started climbing.

Behind her, far below on the valley road, a vehicle appeared—moving fast, raising dust, heading for the switchback where her crushed rig sat like a marker saying *she came this way.*

She didn't need binoculars to know whose it was.
