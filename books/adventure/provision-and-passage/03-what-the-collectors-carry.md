## 3. What the Collectors Carry

*Elodie*

The weather line came down off the Corren Range like a curtain being drawn by a careless hand—gray at the top, bruise-purple beneath, and ahead of it a hard wind that smelled of iron and coming snow. Elodie walked with her collar up and Hask's map folded against her sternum, inside her coat, where her heartbeat could keep an eye on it.

Her rig was a day behind her now, buried under rock on the switchbacks above the ghost camp of Alder Notch. Everything she owned that mattered fit in one pack: rope, a spirit stove, three days of food she'd stretched to five, and the ledger wrapped in oilcloth. The map rode separately. She'd told herself that was practicality. It wasn't. The ledger was Hask's. The map was hers—the only thing left in the world that was hers—and some stubborn animal part of her refused to let them share a grave if she went down into a crevasse.

The waystation appeared out of the wind like something remembered rather than seen: a low stone building with a sod roof, a stable smelling of old hay, and smoke rising from a stovepipe in a thin, defeated line. A sign over the door read KETTLE GAP — PROVISIONS, PASSAGE, and someone had scratched out *passage* years ago.

She should have walked past. Every instinct she'd built in ten years of chasing storms said don't stop where you can be found. But the light was failing, the temperature had dropped six degrees since noon, and above the pass the clouds had organized themselves into something with intent.

She went in.

The keeper was an old woman with a face like a dried riverbed who took Elodie's last honest coin for stew and a corner of floor near the stove. Elodie ate slowly, watching the window, and told herself the shape moving against the dusk outside was just drift snow.

It wasn't drift snow. Drift snow didn't knock.

*Rill*

Rill had chased debt across four territories and learned this much: people running from money always stopped eventually. They stopped because bodies were honest things. They needed heat, food, sleep. Debt could go without any of those; flesh couldn't.

He'd tracked her from the rockslide—easy, once he found the rig. He'd stood over the crushed chassis of her chase truck for a long moment, reading the story in the wreckage: which way she'd climbed, how fast, whether she'd looked back. She hadn't looked back. That told him more than tire tracks ever could.

Now he stood in the doorway of the waystation at Kettle Gap, snow in his beard, and watched Elodie Galloway set down her spoon with the deliberate calm of a woman deciding whether to reach for a weapon.

"Evening," he said. "Mind if I thaw?"

The keeper looked between them and wisely began scrubbing a pot that was already clean.

"You're alone?" Elodie said.

"I'm always alone. It's cheaper." He hung his coat by the stove and sat across from her, uninvited, close enough to talk low and far enough to be polite about it. Up close she looked worse than the reports suggested—hollowed, wind-scoured, hands cracked from cold—but her eyes hadn't changed. Those eyes did arithmetic even while they blinked. He'd always liked that about her, in the way you liked a storm you were standing too close to.

"I'm not going to fight you," she said.

"I know."

"That doesn't mean I'll hand it over."

"I know that too." He warmed his hands. Outside, the wind found a loose board somewhere and worried it, rhythmic as a pulse. "Galloway, I'm going to tell you something the Vane brothers would skin me for telling you, and then we're both going to drink whatever's in that kettle, because I've been walking since dawn and I'm too old and too tired to pretend otherwise."

Her spoon stayed down. "Talk."

"The claim." He kept his voice under the wind. "Hask's silver claim. You're carrying the map to it—I've known that since the fuel depot, I can smell paper that matters. And here's what you don't know: Vane sold those rights eighteen months ago. To a syndicate out of Marrow City. Then, when the first buyers started asking why no survey crews ever showed up, he sold them again. To a mining concern with offices in two provinces and lawyers in three." He turned his palms up. "There is no claim, Galloway. There are two sets of documents saying there's a claim, and neither of them belongs to Hask or his dead crew, and the ground itself has been argued over so long that whoever digs first will spend twenty years in court before they see ore. The map isn't worth anything."

"Then why are you chasing me through a mountain range for it?"

"Because it's worth everything." He leaned forward. "Not as a deed. As leverage. Your map proves the Vanes sold paper twice. It's evidence. It's the kind of thing that ends brothers like Silas and Dree Vane—not in court, courts they own, but in front of every creditor and partner they have. You aren't carrying treasure, Galloway. You're carrying a bomb with a surveyor's seal on it."

The stove ticked. The old keeper had gone very still over her pot.

Elodie looked at him for a long time, and Rill watched the arithmetic move behind her eyes—watched her price the map, price her debt, price the promise she'd made to a dying man on a frozen road—and then watched her arrive somewhere he hadn't expected.

"If it's worthless," she said quietly, "then I can't sell it to clear my debt. Which means the choice I've been torturing myself with for two hundred miles doesn't exist." Something in her face settled, like sediment finding bottom. "All I have left is the promise."

"That's not all you have."

"No?"

Rill rubbed his jaw. Here was the part he'd rehearsed on the trail and abandoned eleven times. "The ledger. Hask's wage book. Paper proving what the company owed and never paid—owed to men whose widows are sitting right now in a valley below a closing pass, believing their husbands died owing money instead of being owed it." He met her eyes. "A ledger delivered quiet changes nothing. Vane buys the settlement's silence for less than he spends on whiskey. But a ledger read aloud—in front of everyone, with the map burned so there's nothing left to bargain over—that's not a document anymore. That's testimony. You can't buy back words a whole valley has heard."

"Why are you telling me this? What do you get?"

He almost smiled. "I get to stop working for men who sell the same grave twice." He stood, took the kettle himself, and poured them both a cup. "And I get passage over that pass tonight, because in about two hours the weather line comes down and doesn't lift until spring, and I am not spending winter in this building with your suspicious stare."

*Elodie*

They crossed in the dark, which was either courage or stupidity, and Elodie had stopped believing there was a difference.

The storm caught them at the saddle—a wall of white driven sideways, the wind screaming through the notch with a sound like the whole range clearing its throat. Rill roped them together without asking, his hands quick and practiced, and when she raised an eyebrow he shouted over the gale: "Dead clients pay nothing!"

After that there was only the climb. Boots on ice, breath torn away, the world reduced to the three feet of slope visible in the lantern's shudder and the pull of the rope. Twice Elodie went down and was hauled up before panic could take root. Once Rill fell and she held him, arms burning, teeth gritted around words she didn't remember choosing—*not yet, not like this, hold*—and understood, somewhere below thought, that she meant them.

At the top of the saddle they crouched behind a spine of granite, and for one lull between gusts the clouds tore open and showed them the valley below: small lights strung along a river flat, woodsmoke standing straight up in the sheltered air, a settlement holding its breath against winter.

"They're down there," Elodie said. Her voice came out scraped raw. "Every name in the book."

Rill said nothing. He was looking at her, not the valley, and his expression in the lantern light was strange—something like respect arriving too late to be useful, or exactly in time.

Then the clouds closed, the wind returned with its full voice, and they went down toward the lights together, two people bound by a rope and a dead man's paper, racing the snow that would seal the pass behind them.

Below, in the nearest house, a lamp moved past a window—and Elodie thought she saw, brief as lightning, a figure pause at the glass and look up at the ridge.

Someone was awake. Someone was waiting.
