## 4. Provision and Passage

The valley opened below her like a held breath.

Elodie came down the last switchback with the storm at her back, driving her forward the way it had driven her for three days—wind shouldering her between steps, snow filling her boot prints before she could lift her foot again. Her hands had stopped hurting somewhere above the tree line. That was bad. She knew enough about cold to know that pain was a currency, and when you ran out of it, you were spending something else.

The settlement sat in the fold of the valley like a coin dropped in a pocket: forty roofs, maybe fifty, smoke standing straight up from the chimneys in the windless cold beneath the cloud deck. A church steeple without a bell. A long low building with a sign she couldn't read from this distance, its paint scoured to gray wood.

She counted her steps because counting was all she had left. Four hundred to the first fence. Three hundred to the well. Two hundred to the door of the long building, where a woman was already coming out, drawn by some instinct that people in high country develop—the sense of a figure on a ridge who has been walking too long to be walking for pleasure.

"You're off the pass," the woman said. Not a question. Her eyes went past Elodie's shoulder to the white wall swallowing the heights. "Last one through this year, I'd guess."

"I have to be." Elodie's voice came out cracked, a stranger's voice. She fumbled inside her coat, inside the oilcloth, and pressed the ledger against the woman's mittened hand. It was warm from her body, the only warm thing left in her. "Hask Corren's ledger. His wages book. He died on the flats below the range and he made me promise."

The woman looked down at it. Then up. Something moved through her face like weather.

"Get inside," she said. "Then we'll see what he sent."

* * *

They put her by the stove in the long hall—the community house, someone called it, though half the town seemed to live in it come evening—and wrapped her in blankets that smelled of wool and cedar smoke while feeling crawled back into her fingers like pins and needles turned merciful. She drank broth she didn't remember being given. Around her, chairs scraped and settled. Word traveled fast in a valley; word always did.

By full dark there were perhaps sixty people in the hall. She saw them clearly now: women mostly, and children, and old men whose lungs had the same rattle Hask's had. The widows. She understood it without being told. This was what a played-out mine left behind—not ghost camps on the mountain but ghost marriages down here, households with one chair empty at the table and no body ever shipped home because the company hadn't paid for shipping either.

An older woman with iron-gray hair braided tight to her skull stood at the front. Marta, they called her. She held the ledger now, and she looked at Elodie across the room.

"Hask Corren kept our men's hours," she said. "Kept them in his own hand when the company books went... creative." Her voice didn't break. It narrowed. "He told us once, years back, that someday the paper would speak. We thought the cold got his mind before it got his lungs."

"It got his lungs," Elodie said. "The paper outlived him."

Marta opened the ledger. And then, quietly, she closed it again and held it out to Elodie.

"You carried it over the pass. You read it."

Elodie looked at the book. Sixty faces looked at her. Every instinct she owned said *stay small, stay moving, don't stop anywhere long enough to owe anyone anything*—the arithmetic she'd lived by since the first Vane note. But Hask had traded her a map for a promise, and promises were the only currency she'd ever respected more than money.

She stood. Her legs shook. She let them.

She read names. That was most of it, in the end—names, and beside each name the wages owed and the dates they'd been withheld, in Hask's cramped, patient hand, entry after entry going back eleven years. *Tomas Reyes, four months' pay, spring flood year. Aldous Crane, six weeks, plus hazard rate never honored. Peter Grieve.* At Peter Grieve's name a woman in the third row made a sound like a hinge and her daughter took her hand, and Elodie kept reading, because stopping would have been a mercy to herself and not to anyone else in the room.

She read until her voice gave out and Marta took the book and finished the page, and then another woman took it, and another, passing it hand to hand like a coal, each voice adding its section to the record until the whole room had heard every name spoken aloud in a warm place among living people. Paper into testimony. Debt into witness.

When it was done, nobody cheered. They did something better. They wrote the names down again, fresh copies, three of them, and agreed where each copy would live—one in the church, one in the settlement box, one to go out with the spring mail run to the magistrate at Corren Forks. Eleven years of theft, multiplied by sixty memories. Rill had told her the truth on the pass: the map was worthless as paper. But a claim could be sold twice; a name, spoken publicly, could not be unsaid.

* * *

The Vane men came nine days later, riding down out of the pass behind the weather line, three of them with Rill leading—a man who had crossed a mountain at her side and still, somehow, remained the enemy.

They filled the doorway of the community house with snowmelt and menace, and the tall one, Broch Vane himself, held out his gloved hand like a man collecting at a counter.

"The map, Galloway. You know the terms. Collateral against your note."

Elodie was sitting by the stove. She had been sitting by the stove for nine days, drinking tea, watching the sky, learning the valley's weather the way she'd learned the flats'—by living under it. She looked at Broch's open hand for a long moment.

Then she reached into her coat and drew out the map, oilcloth and all, and everyone in the hall watched her do it, and Broch Vane smiled with the optimism that was worse than his violence.

She fed it into the stove.

The oilcloth caught first, blue-flamed, and then the paper underneath, Hask's careful inked ridges curling black, the silver claim that wasn't worth anything except as leverage going up in heat and ash while sixty witnesses watched her do it. Broch lunged a step and stopped, because there was nothing to take, and taking her instead would mean explaining to a roomful of people why a dead prospector's map had mattered so much to two respectable lenders.

"That was collateral," he said.

"That was a lie with lines drawn on it," Elodie said. "Here's what settles my note." She set the contract money on the table between them—her chase fee, earned honestly, counted twice, every bill of it—and beside it the arithmetic she'd done in her head for nine days. Principal. Interest. The penalty clause Rill had shown her how to void. "Count it. Then get off this mountain before the passes close on you too."

Rill counted it. He was thorough; she'd give him that. When he finished, he glanced at the stove, at the last corner of map curling to nothing, and something passed over his face that might have been respect or might have been relief.

"Debt's cleared," he said.

Broch stared at her a long moment, recalculating, the way he must have recalculated everything his whole life, looking for the angle. There wasn't one. That was the point. He took his men and his optimism out into the cold, and the door shut behind them, and the hall was quiet except for the fire eating the last of the map.

Marta sat down across from her and pushed the teapot closer.

"Spring mail run goes to Corren Forks in April," she said. "Magistrate will want statements. From everyone. It'll take all winter to write them properly."

Elodie looked out the frost-rimed window at the valley—the roofs, the smoke, the mountains already erasing the pass she'd come through. Somewhere south, the supercells would be stacking up over the flats right now, green-black and gorgeous, and some other chaser with good tires and bad debts would be running them down. She found she didn't miss it. She found, to her surprise, that she'd spent years chasing storms because they were the only things that ever came *to* her, and here was a valley full of people who stayed.

"I'll need a board," she said. "And chalk. And someone to climb to the ridge line twice a day."

Marta raised an eyebrow.

"If you're going to live under this sky," Elodie said, "you're going to learn to read it. Cloud base, wind shear, the smell before a whiteout. Come autumn, when the passes close, I want every soul in this valley able to tell me what's coming over those peaks a day before it arrives."

Marta almost smiled. "And what do we call you? Storm chaser?"

Elodie looked at the stove, where the last fleck of silver-gray ash was settling among the coals. Hask's map, gone. Hask's ledger, copied three times and alive. Her debt, paid. Her promise, kept.

"Neighbor," she said. "Call me neighbor."

Outside, the first real snow of winter began to fall, fat and slow and unhurried, and for the first time in longer than she could reckon, Elodie Galloway watched weather come and did not run—not toward it, not away from it—but simply stood at the window of a warm room, among her own people, and named the clouds as they gathered.
