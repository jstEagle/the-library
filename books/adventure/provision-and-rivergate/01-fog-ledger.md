## 1. Fog Ledger

The fog came up off the Rivergate before dawn the way it always did in the wet season, rising off the water like breath off a sleeping animal, and by the time I had the engine turning over at the strip outside Provision it had swallowed everything past the third fence post. I sat in the cockpit of the old De Havilland with my hands on the yoke and let the propeller wash push against the white, and I did what I always did: I counted.

Three crates of penicillin. Two of quinine. One crate of sutures and one of the little glass ampoules the mission doctor downriver swore by and nobody else could pronounce. A parcel of letters wrapped in oilcloth, because paper drowned faster than people out here. And a tin of boiled sweets for the children at Two Sisters, which was not cargo, officially, but which I carried anyway, because a man who carries nothing but cargo eventually forgets there was ever anything else worth carrying.

I counted twice. Then I pulled out onto the grass and flew into the white.

* * *

There are three rules I fly by, and I made them myself, one at a time, each one paid for. I don't talk about them much because talking about rules makes them sound like philosophy, and they aren't philosophy. They're scar tissue.

Rule one: never land twice in the same place. Not in the same week, not if you can help it, not even when the landing is good and the coffee is hot and somebody at the mission asks, casually, when you'll be back. You come back on a different day, or you don't come back at all. The river towns are small and memory out here is long, and a pilot who becomes predictable becomes a schedule, and a schedule is a thing people can set their watches by, and a thing people can set their watches by is a thing people can wait for. I learned that from a pilot named Halloway, who isn't alive to have learned it himself. But that's later. That's rule three's story, mostly.

Rule two: never learn names too well. You learn the names of places, because places don't mind. Provision, Rivergate, Two Sisters, the flooded channel they call the Widow's Elbow because the current bends there like a woman looking back over her shoulder. Place names are safe. They're geography. But the people — you learn them by function, not by name. The doctor at the mission is *the doctor*. The trader with the crooked scales at Brannoch's old dock is *the trader*, though everyone else calls him something worse. The woman who runs the radio at Two Sisters and has a voice like warm gravel is *the radio woman*, and she has told me her name four times now, and I have let it slide off me four times, politely, the way water slides off waxed canvas. She thinks I'm forgetful. I'm not forgetful. Forgetting is the only way I know to leave without leaving.

Rule three: never carry anything but cargo. No promises. No debts of feeling. No messages that begin with *tell her* or end with *if I don't come back*. Cargo weighs what it weighs. You can put it on a manifest and be done with it. But a promise rides in the chest, next to the heart, and it doesn't come off at altitude, and it doesn't come off ever, really, until either you keep it or it keeps you.

I broke rule three once. Once is enough. That's how rules get made.

* * *

The fog held for forty minutes, which was long enough for me to do nothing but listen to the engine and feel the river underneath me through the soles of my boots, the way you feel a horse under a saddle. There's no instrument for the Rivergate. The maps are forty years out of date in places, drawn before Vessany came off them, and the river doesn't care about maps anyway; it moves its channels every flood season the way a snake moves its skin. So you fly it by eye and by ear and by the color of the water, brown where it's shallow and running fast over sand, green-black where it's deep and slow and holding its secrets down. In fog you fly it by nothing at all, and you climb, and you climb, and you say the names of the bends to yourself like prayers, and you hope the prayers are still accurate this season.

The Widow's Elbow passed beneath me somewhere around minute twenty-five. I felt it more than saw it — a cold spot in the air, a slight tug on the tail as the channel's breath met the main current's — and I corrected two degrees right and kept climbing, because the Elbow's far bank has a stand of dead snags that reach up sixty feet and have killed better pilots than me, pilots with better planes and worse luck, or maybe just worse mornings.

At minute forty the fog tore open along the eastern bank like cloth giving way at a seam, and the sun came through sideways and turned the whole river to hammered pewter, and I could see again. I could see the mission at Two Sisters sitting on its bluff with its tin roof catching light, and I could see the smoke of breakfast fires standing straight up in the windless morning, six or seven columns of it, gray against gray-green, like the town was sending up signals it didn't know it was sending.

Two Sisters wasn't my stop. My stop was further down, at the mission on the lower bend, the one the doctor ran who'd sent word upriver two weeks ago that his quinine was gone and his fever cases weren't. But I dropped low over Two Sisters anyway, the way I do, just to look, just to count the roofs, just to confirm the world was still where I'd left it. The radio woman stood out on her platform beside the antenna mast and waved both arms above her head, big slow arcs, the signal we'd agreed on years ago that meant *all well, nothing urgent, keep flying.* She waved the way she did everything, thoroughly, like the message might not land unless she put her whole body into it.

I rocked my wings once. All well. Keep flying.

Then I climbed away from her, and I watched her shrink in the mirror until she was a speck on a speck on a river, and I thought, not for the first time, that this was the whole shape of my life: people waving from places I was leaving.

* * *

The lower bend mission sat on a shelf of red clay above the water, and the strip was mowed grass that ended abruptly at a drop-off to the river, which meant you landed uphill and took off downhill and prayed about both. The fog had burned off clean by the time I came around, and the doctor was already at the strip with a handcart, waiting, because he'd heard me coming ten minutes out — sound carries strange over water, and everyone on the Rivergate knows an engine the way city people know a voice on the telephone.

He was a thin man, sunburnt to the color of the clay he lived on, with a stethoscope worn around his neck like a priest wears a cross, and hands that shook slightly unless they were doing something medical, in which case they were the steadiest hands I've ever seen. He waved me down with both arms, taxied alongside as I rolled out, and had the first crate off the plane before the propeller stopped.

"Quinine," he said, not asking, just confirming, the way a man confirms a delivery of ammunition.

"Quinine. Penicillin. Sutures. The ampoules." I handed him the manifest. "And the letters."

He took the oilcloth bundle and weighed it in his palm, and I watched him do the thing everyone does with mail out here, which is hold it a moment too long, as if the weight of it might tell him whose handwriting was inside. "Big bundle."

"Rivergate's been busy."

"Rivergate's always busy. Busy isn't the same as writing." He stacked the crates on the handcart and looked up at me, squinting against the sun that was now fully committed to the morning. "You'll take tea?"

"I've got a schedule."

"You always have a schedule." He said it without rancor, almost fondly, the way you'd describe weather. "One cup. It's boiling already. I heard your engine and put the kettle on, same as every time. You could break rule whatever-it-is just this once."

I hadn't told him my rules. Nobody knew my rules. But he'd flown with me twice, years ago, back when I still rode shotgun for other men, and he'd watched me refuse things enough times to reverse-engineer the pattern. Doctors notice patterns. It's most of the job.

"One cup," I said, and immediately regretted it, and drank it anyway.

We sat on his porch, which was really just a roofed platform with two chairs and a rail polished smooth by elbows, and looked out at the river while the tea scalded our fingers. Below us, women were washing clothes at the shallows and beating them flat against stones, and the sound carried up — *whap, whap, whap* — patient as a heartbeat. A dugout crossed the bend with two men poling it, standing tall, leaning into the current like it owed them money.

"Fever cases are down," the doctor said. "Since the quinine came last month. Down but not gone. It's the flooded channels — the water sits in the low ground after the storms and breeds them. Mosquitoes. You'd know, you fly over it."

"The Widow's Elbow's been high all week."

"It's been high all year. Whole season's been wrong. Rain in the wrong months, dry in the wrong ones. Old Brannoch says the river's changing its mind about where it wants to go." He sipped. "Brannoch says a lot of things."

Brannoch. One-eyed trader, half legend, half liar, owner of the worst boats on the river and possibly the best information. I'd bought fuel from him twice and neither time had I stayed longer than it took to pour it, and both times he'd tried to make me stay, telling stories with his one eye glinting like he was letting you in on something enormous. I liked him the way you like a dog that bites strangers — from a distance, with respect.

"He says the river remembers," the doctor went on. "Says it gave things up this flood that went down forty years ago. Says a barge chain surfaced near the old Vessany works, and a door, and God knows what else. Forty years. Things go down in rivers and rivers keep them, and then one year the water's wrong and it hands them back."

"Vessany," I said, mostly to hear how the word sounded in my own mouth.

It sounded like a closed door.

"You remember Vessany?"

"I remember the maps changed." Which was true, and was also the smallest possible true answer, which was how I preferred my answers. I was nine, maybe ten, when the atlases in the schoolroom at Provision got inked over — a whole nation, gone between one page and the next, its name replaced with a blank stretch labeled only TERRITORY. Nobody explained it. Nobody explained anything, that year. The famine did the explaining. The Provision fund did the burying. Half the river towns got built by that fund — schools, clinics, this very mission, probably — and the other half got graves, and the difference between the halves was decided in rooms I'd never seen, by names I'd never learn, because learning names was dangerous and I'd known that even at ten, even before I'd made it a rule.

"They say the ledgers burned," the doctor said. "When Vessany went under. All the records of the Provision — where the money came from, where it went. Burned, scattered, sunk. Forty years and nobody's ever produced a page of it." He set down his cup. "Funny thing to think about. All that paper. Somewhere."

"Paper sinks," I said.

"So do people." He smiled, tired. "Forgive me. It's the season. Fever season makes a man morbid."

* * *

I finished the tea because I'd said I would, and breaking a promise to a doctor seemed like bad arithmetic, and then I loaded the return freight — fever blood samples packed in straw, a broken autoclave he wanted repaired in Rivergate, and one envelope addressed in a hand I recognized without wanting to.

Magistrate Corven.

I knew the hand before I read the name. Corven wrote the way he spoke, in long confident strokes that leaned forward like a man walking into a room he owned. I'd seen it on my first flying license, countersigned, fourteen years ago. I'd seen it on the letter that gave me the medicine run, back when the run was new and so was I. I'd seen it on the pension notice that arrived every quarter for Halloway's mother, signed by a magistrate who had never met her and never missed a payment.

Corven had pulled me out of the wreck on the Sandbar Reach when I was nineteen and Halloway was dead, and he'd stood on the bank in the rain with his coat off, wrapping it around me, saying nothing, absolutely nothing, for a long time, and then he'd said: *You'll want work. Work keeps a man from thinking.* And he'd been right, and he'd given it to me, and I'd been running from thinking ever since, and some part of me understood that the running and the saving were the same transaction, though I'd never once said so out loud.

The envelope was heavy. More than paper weight. I held it up to the light out of habit — a smuggler's habit, a pilot's habit, checking cargo — and saw the shadow of something stiff inside. Cardboard? Photographs?

"From the Magistrate himself," the doctor said. "Came up on the packet boat Tuesday. Asked specifically for you. Well — asked for 'the pilot on the medicine run,' which is you, which means he knows your route, which means—" He shrugged. "Which means a magistrate knows things. That's what magistrates are for."

I put the envelope in the map case behind my seat, face down, and did not open it on the porch, and did not open it during preflight, and told myself I'd open it over Rivergate, tomorrow, in daylight, in a public place, with witnesses, because that was the kind of letter it was shaped like. Heavy. Stiff. Deliberate.

The doctor walked me to the plane and helped strap the samples in, and at the cabin door he did the thing he always did, which was look at me a moment too long with those clinical eyes that catalogued everything — the hollows under my cheekbones, the way my left hand hovered near the doorframe instead of resting on it, the gray coming in early at my temples.

"Eat something today," he said.

"I ate."

"Something that didn't come out of a tin."

"I'll eat in Rivergate."

"You'll eat in Rivergate," he agreed, in the tone doctors use when they're writing something down in a chart you'll never see. Then: "Come back Thursday. I'll need the autoclave."

"Thursday's four days."

"Then Wednesday. Or Friday. Whenever you come back." He grinned. "Whenever the fog allows."

That was the joke everyone made about me, on the river. *Whenever the fog allows.* As if the fog were my employer. As if I were less a man than a weather condition with a cargo manifest. I laughed at it the first hundred times. Somewhere past the hundredth I stopped laughing and started listening, because jokes that persist are usually diagnoses.

I cranked the engine. The doctor stepped back, handcart rattling behind him, and raised one arm — not a wave, exactly, more a salute, or a farewell, or a *next time*, and I lifted off downhill into the bright empty morning and banked north up the river, and watched him shrink, and watched him vanish, another person waving from a place I was leaving.

* * *

I flew the afternoon leg alone, upriver toward Provision, and the light went long and gold across the water, and the river did its pewter trick again, and I thought about nothing, which is a skill, which takes practice, which I have practiced for fifteen years until I can hold a whole day in my head like a held breath.

But thoughts are like the river. Push them down and they find another channel.

Somewhere over the flooded country below the Elbow, where the storm-water sat in the timber like spilled tea, I found myself thinking about what the doctor had said. *Rivers keep things. Then one year the water's wrong and it hands them back.*

Forty years since Vessany. Forty years since the ledgers burned, supposedly. Forty years since the maps got inked over and the blank space got a new name and everybody agreed, in the way crowds agree, silently and completely, to stop asking.

And the season was wrong. The doctor said so. Brannoch said so. The rain in the wrong months, the dry in the wrong ones, the floods reaching channels that hadn't flooded in living memory. The Widow's Elbow running high all year. Water sitting where water had never sat, at least not in my fifteen years of flying over it, and I had flown over it in every month, in every mood, in fog and fire-light and the blue hour after the storms.

A wrong season uncovers things. Every salvage man on the river knows it. Every smuggler. Every kid who beaches-combs after a flood and comes home with a rifle or a wedding ring or a skull.

I checked my watch. Two hours of light left. Provision by dark, if I pushed. The strip there was lit by lanterns on poles, which was to say it wasn't lit at all, which was to say I'd land by the sound of the frogs going quiet ahead of me, which was a method, and it worked, mostly.

I pushed.

The storm showed itself twenty minutes out — a wall of it, building over the western hills, purple-black and flickering inside like a lantern with a moth trapped in it. The monsoon wasn't due for weeks yet. This was early. This was wrong. Everything this season was wrong, and I filed that thought away in the place where I file inconvenient thoughts, which is nowhere, which is a filing system with excellent capacity and terrible retrieval.

I beat the storm into Provision by eleven minutes. Eleven minutes is a margin. Eleven minutes is the width of a life, sometimes. I came in low over the town with the first fat drops hitting the windshield like thrown gravel, and the lanterns were already swinging in the wind, and the frogs had gone silent in a moving line ahead of my approach like a carpet being rolled up, and I put the plane down on the wet grass hard enough to wake the dead and soft enough to keep them asleep, and rolled out to the fence, and shut down, and sat there in the sudden hush while the rain came down all around me like applause.

The station keeper waded out with a tarp, shouting cheerfully over the noise, and together we tied the plane down double, and I carried the mail sack and the heavy envelope into the station house, and I was dry, and fed, and safe, and I should have slept like a stone.

Instead I lay on the bunk in the back room with the rain hammering the tin roof and the heavy envelope on the table beside me, face down, and I listened to the storm lean on the building, and I thought about rivers keeping things, and handing them back.

Toward midnight the storm moved off downstream, grumbling, taking its rain to drown some other channel, and the silence afterward was enormous. In that silence I heard the river — you always hear the river out here, if you listen, a vast low breathing under everything, the sound of the biggest animal in the world turning over in its sleep.

And I thought: it's awake tonight.

In the morning I'd fly the mail down to Rivergate. In the morning I'd open Corven's letter in daylight, in public, like a careful man. In the morning the fog would rise off the water the way it always did, and I'd climb into it, and count my cargo, and follow my rules, all three of them, the way I had for fifteen unbroken years.

But that night, lying in the dark with the river breathing under everything, I had no way of knowing that the storm that had just passed over me had spent its fury on the flooded channels below the Widow's Elbow — that it had shoved the water where water didn't belong, into the dead timber, into the sunken places, into the forty-year dark.

I had no way of knowing that the river, awake, restless, in its wrong season, had its hands on something.

And was getting ready to give it back.
