## 3. What Vessany Was

The rain had followed me for three days, the way bad news follows a name.

I came down out of the hill country on foot, then by ferry, then by standing in the bow of a fisherman's punt while he poled me through channels that weren't on any map worth trusting, and all that time the rain kept its patient company, drumming on my hood, filling the ditches, turning the world the color of pewter. I had learned to read weather like other people read faces, and this weather was telling me: *turn back*. But I had spent six years learning to ignore that particular voice, so I stepped off at Provision Landing with wet boots and a dry smile and asked the first person I saw where a pilot might be found.

Everyone knew. That was the trouble with river towns—they had nothing to trade but talk, and they traded it freely.

"Yarrow," said the woman mending nets outside the fuel shed, not looking up. "The one who lands twice nowhere. He'll be at the Kettle until the fog lifts, if he's not already gone."

"He carries passengers?"

She looked up then, and her eyes did a slow inventory of me—boots, coat, the satchel I kept my hand resting on the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping dog—and she laughed without much humor in it. "Yarrow doesn't carry passengers. Yarrow carries cargo. If you want to be a passenger, be cargo."

I thanked her and paid for it with a coin, which she took the way people take coins from strangers on rivers: quickly, and without gratitude.

* * *

The Kettle was the kind of place that smelled of kerosene and cardamom and forty years of spilled beer, and Oscar Yarrow sat at the far end of the bar with a cup of something black and a map spread out beside it, weighted at the corners with salt cellars. I knew him before anyone said his name. There is a look that people who live alone in the sky get—the stillness of them, the way they hold their bodies like they're always braced against turbulence even sitting down. My grandmother had that look too, in the last years, after she stopped talking about Vessany and started only listening.

I sat down two stools away, because closer would have been a question and farther an insult, and ordered what he was having.

He didn't look at me. "Whatever you're selling," he said, "the answer's no."

"I'm buying."

"Even worse." He turned a page of the map. Up close I could see it was hand-annotated, water depths and sandbars marked in pencil, the pencil refreshed so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth. A man who loved a river enough to keep rewriting it. That was useful information. Useful men are predictable men.

"I need to go downriver," I said. "Past Two Sisters. As far as you're going."

"I'm not going past Two Sisters."

"Then as far as Two Sisters."

"Fog's lifting in an hour." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were gray as the weather and just as uncommitted. "And I don't carry passengers."

"So I'm told." I set the coin on the bar between us—not a large coin, but the right kind, old, worn smooth, the kind that says *I know what things cost here*. "But I hear you carry cargo. I'm cargo. Weigh me, load me, deliver me. I won't talk, I won't touch anything, and I'll pay for the weight."

Something moved behind his face—not a smile, more the ghost of where a smile might have lived once, before it was evicted. "Cargo doesn't buy its own drinks."

"Cargo also doesn't drink." I pushed the cup he'd bought me an inch toward him. "Consider it ballast."

He studied me for a long moment, and I let him. This is the part I am good at, if I am honest about my talents, and I try to be honest about them in private if nowhere else: letting people look at me and see exactly what they expect. A traveler. A trader's daughter, maybe. Someone unremarkable, going somewhere unremarkable, carrying nothing worth the asking.

"You'll sit behind the cargo net," he said at last. "You'll keep your hands off the controls, the radio, and the manifest. And if I tell you to get out, you get out, wherever we are, even if it's water."

"Agreed."

"And your name?"

Here it was, the small hinge everything turns on. I gave him the name I'd been wearing since I left the coast—a plain name, a river name, one that belonged to no records I couldn't account for. "Maren," I said. "Ilse Maren."

He wrote it on the manifest in block letters, and I watched him do it, and I thought: *he writes everyone down, this man who never learns names too well. He keeps them at arm's length in ink.* It told me something about him. People who refuse intimacy often keep meticulous lists of the people they've refused.

We flew within the hour.

* * *

I will say this for Oscar Yarrow: he flew the way my grandmother used to fold letters—carefully, completely, with an economy that looked like carelessness until you understood it was the opposite. The Rivergate unspooled beneath us, brown and braided, the flooded channels spreading wide after the storm like veins under thin skin. Villages appeared and vanished. Smoke rose straight up from cooking fires because there was no wind, only heat and water and the enormous green silence of the interior.

He didn't talk. Good. Neither did I. The engine made conversation pointless anyway, which is why I had chosen a plane over every slower option: engines forgive silence. On a boat, silence is suspicious. In the air, it's just physics.

We put down twice—at a mission strip where he traded a crate of antibiotics for fuel, and at a sandbar settlement where children ran out to meet us before the propeller stopped, which told me he was known here, and known kindly, whatever the net-mender had implied. He never landed twice in the same place, the woman had said. But the children knew him. Children always know the ones who bring medicine and leave before they can be thanked properly. Being known by children and strangers and no one else—that is a specific loneliness, and I recognized it the way you recognize a scar pattern.

It was on the second day, at the third stop, that I saw the chest.

* * *

His plane had a cargo bay behind the two seats, and he kept it organized with a rigor that bordered on devotion: crates lashed, tools shadowed on a board, everything tied against the day the river decided to shake us. I wasn't supposed to touch anything. I touched nothing. But when he climbed out at the fuel stop at Corben's Reach to argue with a man about a fuel pump, I was alone in the plane for eleven minutes, and I used nine of them.

The chest sat forward, under the netting, wrapped in a tarp that had been tied and retied. I didn't open it. Opening it would have been madness—I could hear the fuel argument through the fuselage, voices rising and falling. I only lifted the tarp at one corner, the way you check whether a wound is still bleeding.

The seal was brass, round as a communion wafer, stamped into a lead disc that had gone white with river-scum. Even crusted, even half-eaten by forty years of water, I knew it. You cannot grow up in my family and not know it. The twin herons, necks crossed, standing in reeds above a broken crown. The arms of the Republic of Vessany, struck in 1911, abolished in everything but memory sometime around the year I wasn't yet born, when the maps were redrawn and the schoolbooks rewritten and a nation the size of a long afternoon simply stopped being spoken of.

My hands were shaking. I noticed them shaking the way you notice someone else's hands, from a distance, with mild academic interest, and then the shaking climbed my arms and got into my chest and I had to put my palm flat against the cold metal and breathe.

Forty years. Forty years of my grandmother's locked drawer, of her whispering ledger numbers to herself like rosary beads, of her dying with a word in her mouth that she wouldn't say because saying it had already cost her everything once. *Vessany.* And here it was, in a bush pilot's cargo bay, wrapped in a tarp like a dead man's boots.

I heard the fuselage door handle turn and had the tarp back in place before the hinge finished moving, but I hadn't counted on Yarrow's eyes. Pilots' eyes are calibrated instruments. They measure drift.

He stood in the doorway with rain dripping off his collar and looked at me, and then at the chest, and then at me again, and I watched him decide—visibly, almost mechanically—whether to ask. I watched him decide not to. His rules were written all over him: *never learn names too well, never carry anything but cargo.* Curiosity was a weight he'd jettisoned years ago to stay airborne.

"Pump's fixed," he said. "Two hours to Two Sisters."

"Fine," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, because inside I was still kneeling at my grandmother's drawer.

* * *

I lasted four hours. I want that on the record somewhere, in whatever ledger the universe keeps of my failures: I lasted four hours, through a squall line and a low pass over the flooded timber, through his careful silence and mine, before I broke.

We had set down on a shingle bank to wait out the worst of the wind—he didn't like flying the narrows in gusts, another point in his favor—and we sat in the plane with the engine ticking as it cooled, sharing a thermos he'd offered with the reluctance of a man donating blood. Rain needled the windows. The chest lay three feet from my knee.

"It's Vessany," I said.

I hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way things come out when you've held them too long under water—they come up still expanding, still trying to become the size they were meant to be.

Yarrow didn't move. The rain ticked on the glass. Somewhere down the shingle, a heron unfolded itself and considered the water.

"Don't know what you mean," he said.

"The chest. Under the netting. The seal—it's the herons of Vessany. Crossed necks, broken crown." I was doing it now, the thing I do badly, the thing my grandmother warned me about with her eyes if never with words: I was talking too much, filling silence with specifics, and every specific was a confession. "I saw it when you were arguing about the fuel pump. I only lifted the corner of the tarp. I didn't open it."

"Uh-huh." He poured more tea into the thermos cap and handed it to me, unhurried, and I understood that the slowness was itself a technique. Give the liar time and watch her hang herself with rope she brought. "And how does a passenger named Maren know what a Vessany seal looks like? They don't teach that in schools. They don't teach anything about it anywhere. That was rather the point of erasing it."

"Coins," I said. "Old coins turn up. Markets, river dredges. Collectors pay for them."

"That's true," he agreed pleasantly. "They do. Except the coins show the herons facing forward. The crossed-neck version with the broken crown was only ever used on state documents and fund seals. Provision seals." He drank his tea. "So either you're a very serious collector of documents that officially never existed, or you lied to me about who you are, and I'd like to know which, because I have a rule about cargo that lies."

The rain filled the pause. I looked at the chest instead of at him, because looking at him was costing me accuracy.

"My grandmother was from there," I said. Which was true. "From Vessany. Before." Also true. Everything else I added to it was varnish, and varnish is where liars live. "She talked about it sometimes. The seal was on everything—flour sacks, clinic doors, the deed to their house. When I saw it under the tarp I—" I stopped. Started again, worse. "It startled me. That's all. It's been forty years. Nobody's seen that mark in forty years."

"Except you recognized it in four seconds, through river scum, at an angle, in bad light."

"I told you. She talked about it."

"She must have talked about it constantly." He set the thermos down with a soft click that sounded, in the closed cabin, like a lock engaging. "Because you didn't just recognize it, Miss Maren. You went pale. Your hands shook. And then you spent four hours not looking at it, which is the most expensive kind of looking there is." He turned in his seat to face me fully, and up close his gray eyes were less uncommitted than they'd appeared in the Kettle; they were the eyes of a man reading instruments in weather. "So here's what happens now. You can tell me the truth, some of the truth, or none of it. All three options end with me flying you to Two Sisters, because I gave my word and I keep it even when it turns out to be stupid. But only one of those options ends with me ever carrying you again."

"And if I choose none of it?"

"Then at Two Sisters you get out, and I wish you luck, and I mean it, because anyone hunting that chest is going to find you eventually, and luck is the only thing left that travels free on this river."

There it was. Not a threat—an arithmetic. He was showing me the equation and letting me solve it myself, which was either respect or a trap, and I genuinely could not tell which, and that uncertainty frightened me more than any enforcer could have.

I should have chosen none of it. Six years of discipline, of cover stories rehearsed in ferry queues and border sheds, and I should have folded the varnish back over the truth and smiled my market smile and said *just an old woman's stories, pilot, just homesickness.* That was the play. Any professional would have made it.

Instead I heard my grandmother's voice saying the ledger numbers like beads, and I heard myself say:

"There are people who paid a great deal of money to make sure that chest stayed lost. And there's a price on it now—I'd bet the fuel bill on it—and the people who posted it aren't the ones who stole what's inside. They're the ones who stole it *from*." I made myself look at him. "Your magistrate. Corven. Ask yourself why a man who runs half the interior needs a sealed chest fished out of a drowned channel badly enough to send men upriver in the rain."

Something happened in his face then—small, fast, quickly shuttered, but I was watching for it the way he'd watched my hands. The name cost him something. I filed that away with the pencil-refreshed map and the children on the sandbar: *Oscar Yarrow owes Corven something, or Corven owns him, and either way the lever exists.*

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said, but he said it the way I had said *coins*, and we both heard it, and something passed between us that was almost, in its way, honest: the mutual recognition of two people whose professions were lying, caught in the same bad light.

The squall eased. He checked the wind sock, the sky, the manifest—as if the manifest might have changed while we weren't looking—and began the preflight ritual of a man who trusted procedure more than people.

"Two Sisters," he said. "Get in or get out, Miss Maren. But decide in the next minute, because I don't wait on the ground longer than I have to. Ground is where things catch up with you."

I got in. Of course I got in. The chest was getting in, and where the chest went, I went; that had been decided for me six years ago in a locked room in my grandmother's house, with a dying woman's hand pressing a brass rubbing of crossed herons into my palm.

As the wheels left the shingle and the river swung away beneath us, Yarrow spoke once, without turning his head, his voice flattened by the engine into something almost gentle:

"For what it's worth—the fuel man at Corben's Reach asked me this morning if I'd pulled anything unusual out of the flood channels lately. Asked twice. Offered to buy my whole manifest." A beat. "Somebody's already paying for word of it. So whatever your grandmother talked about, Miss Maren, I'd start talking faster."

Below us the Rivergate ran high and brown and patient, carrying everything it had swallowed toward the sea, keeping what it liked, giving up the rest slowly—one chest, one secret, one drowned nation at a time.
