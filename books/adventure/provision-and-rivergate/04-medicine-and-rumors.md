## 4. Medicine and Rumors

The town was called Sallow Bend, though the sign at the landing had lost its *w* to weather sometime before Oscar was born, so that it read *SALLO BEND*, and the river had done the rest—gnawing the bank back a foot every year, taking the road with it, taking the ferry slip, taking, in the end, the whole reason anyone had named the place a bend at all. Now it was just a shelf of mud and rotting pilings where the Rivergate's tributary slowed and thought about things.

Oscar came in low over the water, the way the rules demanded. Never land twice in the same place—but Sallow Bend was a medicine stop, and medicine stopped for no one's rules. The compromise was this: land at a different point on the shelf each time. Last trip it had been the north pilings, near the fish-drying racks. This time Oscar set the plane down south of the landing, where the water was the color of strong tea and the reeds stood thick enough to hide the floats from anyone who wasn't already looking.

Fog had followed them all the way down from the high channels, thinning as the river widened, hanging in the cypress like gauze someone had forgotten to take down. The chest sat in the back where the second crate of penicillin should have been, wrapped in a tarp, lashed under a coil of rope that had never in its life been used for anything but looking like rope. Oscar had spent the whole flight not thinking about it, which was a discipline like any other—harder than instrument work, harder than dead-stick landings, because the mind wanted to circle back to the thing the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

A seal. A nation that wasn't. A stamp pressed into lead the way a thumbprint is pressed into wax, and forty years of river water hadn't blurred it.

Oscar killed the engine and let the plane drift the last fifty yards, listening. That was another rule, unspoken, learned from the fog ledger of years: the river told you who was waiting. Oars said fishermen. Chains said loggers. Boots on a dock, many boots, in step—said trouble. What the river said now was nothing much. A dog somewhere up the bank, complaining. The creak of the drying racks. A woman's voice, singing low, in a language Oscar half-knew and didn't ask about.

They tied off, hauled the medicine crates onto the mud shelf, and carried them up to the dispensary, which had been a granary once, and before that—who knew. The Provision had built it. Everyone knew that much, the way everyone knew the river flowed down. There was a plaque by the door, bronze gone green: *ERECTED BY THE PROVISION FUND FOR THE HEALTH OF THE INTERIOR PEOPLES, YEAR 12 OF THE CONCORD.* Year 12 of a Concord that had ended in fire and erasure, and here was the plaque still, green as pond scum, feeding half the bend its medicine.

Old Marta ran the dispensary. She was seventy if she was a day, small and brown and quick as a wren, with hands that had set more bones than the magistrate's whole office had filed papers. She met Oscar at the door with the usual scowl that meant welcome.

"You're late."

"The fog was late. I just followed it."

"You're always following something." She had the first crate open before Oscar set it down, counting vials by touch, her eyes going soft at the penicillin the way other women's eyes went soft at babies. "The fever's in the north channel villages. Three children buried last week in Tarn's Landing. You'll carry more up on the return?"

"Already stowed."

"You're a good pilot, Oscar Yarrow. It's a pity about the rest of you."

"It's a pity about everyone, Marta."

She snorted and went on counting, and Oscar stood in the doorway a moment, in the smell of carbolic and dried herbs and river damp, and let the town's sounds come in. That was when the wrongness arrived—not as a sound but as an absence. The dog had stopped complaining. The singing had stopped. And under the creak of the racks, under everything, there was a new sound: a boat engine, idling low and easy, holding its position in the channel like a man holding his breath.

Oscar had grown up on engines the way other children grew up on lullabies. This one was a patrol skiff, forty horsepower, maybe forty-five, and it was not going anywhere. It was watching the landing.

* * *

The trading post at Sallow Bend was a long low building on stilts, its floor worn into a shallow bowl by forty years of boots, its shelves stocked with everything from fishhooks to wedding cloth to a single dusty bottle of Rivergate gin that had been priced, Oscar suspected, as a joke or a dare. The trader was a man named Pell, round as a rain barrel, with a face like a dropped pie and a voice that could carry across three channels when the wind was wrong.

Oscar went in for lamp oil and salt, which is to say Oscar went in to listen.

Pell was holding forth to a half-circle of dock men when Oscar came through the door, and he did not lower his voice, which was itself information. Whatever he was selling, he wasn't selling it quiet.

"—paid in hard scrip, not river money. River money's what you buy fish with. This was Provision scrip, the old kind, the kind with the seal on it, and the man carrying it had boots that never touched mud in his life. You could tell by the way he walked. Mud walks. That man *clacked*."

"Clacked," said one of the dock men, delighted.

"Clacked. Like a heron on a tin roof. And what he wanted was simple. Word of a chest. Pulled from the flooded channels above the Narrows, after the storm, by a pilot." Pell's little eyes went around the room like two fish in a barrel, and Oscar kept his face busy with a tin of lamp oil, reading the label as though it were scripture. "A bush pilot. Flies medicine. Lands wherever the fog allows."

The room did the thing rooms do when everyone in them knows exactly one person who fits a description: it went quiet in a spreading circle, the way ice goes, from the edges in. And then, because dock men are dock men and discretion is a fish that dies fast out of water, six faces turned toward Oscar with the smooth, terrible unanimity of a shoal changing direction.

"Morning, Yarrow," said Pell, who had known Oscar for eleven years and had never once used the name in front of a crowd before.

"Pell."

"Heard you had some weather up in the channels."

"Everyone had weather."

"Storm took a lot of things loose off the bottom, they're saying. Old wrecks coming up. Boats. Cargo." Pell leaned on his counter with both hands, and the counter, an old boat plank, groaned in sympathy. "Chests."

"River gives up what it likes," Oscar said. "It doesn't take requests." Oscar set the lamp oil on the counter, then the salt, then, after a moment of theatrical memory, a coil of the good rope that had been lashed over a certain tarp in the back of the plane—bought here last month, and returning it now for no reason that would bear examination, except that a man who had nothing to hide doesn't think about which rope he's carrying. "And it doesn't leave receipts."

Pell laughed, and the dock men laughed, and the laugh had a listening quality to it, the way a dog's bark has when the dog isn't sure yet. "That's the truth. No receipts. Though a man might wonder—" and here Pell dropped his voice into a register meant to be confidential and audible to everyone, "—what a chest full of paper's worth. Paper's heavy, they say. Lead-banded paper. Sealed paper."

"Paper rots," Oscar said. "Ask any fisherman."

"Paper with a seal on it doesn't rot," Pell said. "Paper with a seal on it *waits*."

Oscar paid for the oil and salt with river money, counted out slow, and did not hurry toward the door, because hurrying was a confession. At the door Oscar stopped, as if struck by an afterthought, and said, "Who's paying for the word?"

Pell's smile was a crack in the dropped pie. "Man didn't leave a name. Left a way to reach him, though. Left it with the ferryman at Two Sisters, and the ferryman's not talking, and the ferryman talks to *everyone*." He tapped the counter twice. "That's what hard scrip does, Yarrow. It buys silence. River money only buys fish."

* * *

Oscar walked back down to the landing the long way, along the bank, through the reeds, taking the time to look at everything and be looked at by nothing in particular. The patrol skiff was still holding its station in the channel, but it had moved—drifted a quarter mile down, to where it could see both the landing and the mouth of the back channel. Someone competent was aboard. Someone who knew that a man who lands wherever the fog allows will leave wherever the fog allows, and that the fog was due back by evening.

The chest was where Oscar had left it, lashed under the rope that was now, technically, sold. Oscar untied the plane, hauled it around to face upriver, and did the preflight checks with the slow, deliberate care of a man who has all the time in the world and is using every second of it to think.

Here was the arithmetic, done cold, the way fuel is done. One: someone knew about the chest. Not guessed—*knew*. Knew the channel, knew the storm, knew the salvage, knew the pilot. Which meant the chest had been missed, or lost, or let go, by people who wanted it found and were now sorry. Two: the payment was in old Provision scrip. Not gold, not bank notes, not cartel chits. Provision scrip, the kind that hadn't been printed since the Concord fell. Which meant whoever was hunting had been holding that scrip for forty years, keeping it like a relic, or like a weapon. Three: the hunt had come to Sallow Bend within a day of the salvage, which meant the hunters had not been far away when the chest surfaced. They had been waiting near the Narrows for a long time. Waiting for a chest to come up was not the act of a thief. It was the act of a *creditor*.

Oscar looked at the tarp. The tarp looked back, the way tarps do.

"You're not cargo," Oscar told it, quietly, in the empty air between the reeds. "Cargo doesn't have people waiting for it. Cargo has a bill of lading. You have a congregation."

The dog up the bank started complaining again. The woman started singing again. The world resumed, and Oscar finished the checks, and did not fly.

Because here was the last item in the arithmetic, the one that sat at the bottom of the column like a stone: the fog was due by evening, and a man who lifts off into evening fog with a patrol skiff watching the channel is a man who gets followed—and a man who gets followed leads the followers wherever he goes. Marta's fever villages were up the north channel. The north channel was narrow, and fog-bound, and full of places to land and places to *not* land, and a skiff could not follow a plane into fog.

But a skiff could follow a plane to wherever the fog let it down.

Oscar stood a long time at the water's edge, watching the skiff hold its station, and did the thing that eleven years of rules said never to do: thought about staying. Sallow Bend had a dispensary and a trader and a wren of a woman who set bones. It had mud, which was honest. A man could bury a chest in mud. A man could bury himself in mud, and the river would keep the secret the way it kept everything, and in forty years some other fool would pull up the tarp and the rope and the bones and wonder.

Then Oscar thought of Marta counting vials by touch, and the three children in Tarn's Landing, and the penicillin stowed for the return run, and understood that the rules had never actually been about staying alive. They had been about staying *uninvolved*, and that particular ship, whatever its name had been—Vessany, maybe, or something older—had already gone down.

The fog came in at dusk exactly as promised, rolling off the water in slow white shelves, erasing the landing, the skiff, the world. Oscar lifted into it on instruments alone, climbing through white nothing with the chest lashed in the back and the rope sold and the compass the only honest thing left in the sky, and the last thing heard from Sallow Bend was Pell's voice in memory, cheerful and terrible:

*Paper with a seal on it waits.*

Below, unseen, the skiff turned upriver. Above, unseen, the fog closed over the plane like a door.
