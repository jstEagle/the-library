## 5. The Magistrate's Debt

The fog came off the Rivergate that morning the way it always did in the weeks before monsoon—not rolling, but rising, as if the river itself were exhaling after a long night of holding its breath. Oscar Yarrow stood on the dock at Little Ferrow with a crate of penicillin under one arm and watched the water swallow the far bank whole, and thought, not for the first time, that a man could live his entire life inside a country he never saw.

The plane sat behind them on the grass strip, dew beaded along the wing struts like the river had tried to claim it overnight and only half succeeded. Old girl. Eleven years of medicine runs and mail sacks and the occasional coffin, and she still started on the second try if you asked her nicely.

Oscar did not ask nicely. Oscar never asked anything nicely. That was one of the rules.

*Never land twice in the same place. Never learn names too well. Never carry anything but cargo.*

Three rules, learned young, kept absolutely, and broken exactly once—though Oscar had spent eleven years pretending the chest in the hold was cargo, so perhaps the third rule was only bent, not broken. Cargo didn't make your hands sweat when you thought about it at three in the morning. Cargo didn't have a seal stamped into its lid that belonged to a country that no longer existed.

But that was later. That morning there was only fog, and penicillin, and the long gray run downriver to Saltmarsh Bend, where a clinic nurse with a face like a closed door would sign for the crate without saying thank you, because she never said thank you, and Oscar preferred it that way. Names too well. Faces too long. The river towns were full of people who wanted to be remembered, and remembering was how a bush pilot got heavy.

Oscar loaded the crate, ran the preflight by feel in the half-dark, and took off into a sky the color of dishwater, climbing until the fog became a floor beneath them and the dawn broke red over the interior hills—the hills where Vessany used to be, before the maps were redrawn and the name scraped away like paint.

From up here you couldn't tell a nation had ever drowned. From up here it was all just green.

* * *

Eleven years earlier, the wreck had been in almost exactly this kind of weather.

Oscar had been nineteen, flying for a man named Doss who paid in food and lies, ferrying engine parts to a mining camp that turned out not to exist anymore. The fog had come down off the escarpment faster than any forecast promised, and the valley had closed like a book, and Oscar had put the old Cub down in a flooded channel because the alternative was the escarpment itself, and the channel at least offered the dignity of choice.

The plane went in nose-first. The channel went in over the wings. And Oscar, who could swim well enough, discovered very quickly that swimming well enough meant nothing when your jacket snagged on the radio mount and the cold reached through your ribs and squeezed like a fist, and the surface became a rumor somewhere above you—a gray light getting grayer.

That was the last thing: gray light, going out.

Then hands. Big hands, scarred across the knuckles, that found the jacket collar and tore it clean off Oscar's body rather than waste time with the buckle, and hauled, and the world came back as air and rain and the face of a man in a magistrate's boat leaning over the gunwale with his sleeves rolled up.

"Breathing," the man said, to someone behind him. "Barely. Get the blanket."

Oscar coughed half the channel back into the river and looked up at him. He was older then than Oscar is now—fifty maybe, silver coming in at the temples, a face built for courtrooms but currently wearing an expression courts never saw. Not pity. Something more practical than pity. The look of a man assessing salvage.

"You're the pilot," he said. "Doss's boy."

"Not his boy."

"No." A dry almost-smile. "I can see why you'd say that." He wrapped the blanket around Oscar's shoulders himself, which magistrates did not do, and sat down on the thwart across from Oscar as though they had all the time in the world, while his two boatmen wrestled the wreck's tail out of the reeds. "Corven," he said. "Magistrate Corven. Interior circuit."

"I know who you are." Everyone knew who Corven was. Half the river towns had been built with Provision money back before the war, and Corven was the man who'd spent twenty years making sure the money kept arriving even after the fund's founders were gone, even after Vessany itself was gone. Schools. Clinics. The levee at Two Sisters. There were mothers on the upper river who'd named sons after him, which Oscar had always privately considered the surest form of idolatry and the most dangerous.

"Then you know I should fine you for flying freight in fog with no instruments worth the name."

"Yes, sir."

"But the plane's dead and Doss is a thief, so the fine would be performance." Corven studied him. Rain ran off the brim of his hat and dripped between them. "How old are you?"

"Nineteen."

"Nineteen." He said it the way a carpenter says *soft wood*. "You pulled up in that channel instead of pushing on to the camp. That was the right call. Most pilots your age would have trusted the fog to open."

"It didn't open."

"No. It usually doesn't. But knowing that and acting on it are different animals." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and now the courtroom face was fully on, and Oscar understood suddenly that everything else—the blanket, the rescue, the mildness—had been prologue. "Here's what happens next, boy. Doss will tell everyone you died. It saves him your wages and explains the loss of the plane. In a month nobody will remember there was a pilot named Yarrow at all. That's a gift, if you're smart enough to take it."

"A gift."

"A blank page." Corven's eyes were pale gray, river-colored, and they did not blink enough. "I need a pilot who doesn't exist. Someone to carry things up the small channels—medicine, mostly, letters sometimes, nothing you'll want to ask about. Someone with no debts, no family, no history worth digging up. You'll live at my expense. You'll answer to me alone. And in exchange, when you've earned it, I'll get you a license that says whatever we agree it should say." He paused. "You nearly drowned just now. Ask yourself whether you'd rather owe the river or owe me. The river collects forever. I collect once."

And Oscar—nineteen, blue-lipped, wrapped in a stranger's blanket, with a dead plane and a dead future and a name about to be erased—had heard the offer for exactly what it was and taken it anyway. Because the arithmetic was honest. Because the alternative was Doss, or the dredge crews, or the long slow drowning of being nobody with no way to become somebody.

Because Corven had pulled them out of the water. That counted. In Oscar's private ledger, that counted more than everything else combined, and it still counted now, eleven years later, which was precisely the problem.

"I don't ask about the cargo," Oscar had said.

"No," Corven agreed. "You don't."

They shook on it in the rain, in the bottom of a magistrate's boat, with the wreck of Oscar's first life settling slowly into the mud beside them.

* * *

Saltmarsh Bend smelled the way it always smelled—brine and creosote and the sweet rot of the reed beds—and the nurse signed for the penicillin without a word, and Oscar bought fuel and bad coffee and told themselves the tightness in their chest was the coffee.

It wasn't. It was the chest.

Four days since the flood channel gave it up. Four days of telling no one, which in the river towns was four days of shouting it from every rooftop, because silence traveled faster than speech out here. Oscar had wired it shut again, tarped it in the hold under spare parts, and flown two more runs with it riding behind them like a passenger who knew too much. At night, moored or tied down wherever the light failed, Oscar caught themselves lying awake doing sums: how far to Rivergate, how much fuel, how many checkpoints, how fast a rumor flies against a plane.

And now, in the Saltmarsh Bend post office—a room the size of a closet, smelling of ink and mildew—the postmaster handed over the usual drift of circulars and a single envelope of heavy cream paper, sealed with wax, addressed in a hand so controlled it might have been typeset.

*O. Yarrow, Pilot. To be delivered into no hand but theirs.*

Oscar stood in the doorway a long moment before breaking the seal. Around them the town went about its business: fish carts, a dog fight, two children racing a paper boat down the gutter toward the flood drains. Ordinary sounds. The sound of a world that did not know yet.

The letter was short. Magistrates do not waste paper.

*Yarrow,*

*I am informed that certain property of historical interest has come out of the north channel following the spring floods. I will not insult us both by asking how it came to you; salvage is salvage, and the river has always paid its pilots strangely.*

*I ask instead that you bring it to me. Unopened, if it is unopened. Opened, if it is not. Either way, to me, and to no one else—you know better than most what the river does with things that pass through too many hands.*

*Come to Graywater House within the fortnight. Come as my guest. Come as the person I pulled from the water, if that helps; it helps me.*

*There are matters in that chest that concern the peace of the whole interior, and I would sooner they were settled between friends than otherwise. You understand me. You have always understood me.*

*C.*

Oscar read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the doorway while the paper boat children shrieked past, and the third time was the worst, because by then the words had stopped being words and become the thing underneath them, which was: *he knows.* Four days of silence, and the silence had crossed two hundred miles of river faster than Oscar's own plane, which meant it hadn't traveled as rumor at all. Rumor wanders. This had been carried. Deliberately. By someone who had seen the chest come out of the water—or seen Oscar hauling it—and reported straight up the chain to the one man in the interior with both the authority and the appetite to care.

Corven wanted it quiet. Of course he did. Quiet was how the interior worked; loud was how men ended up in channels.

And the letter was kind. That was the trap of it, the part that sat in Oscar's stomach like swallowed ballast. It was kind the way Corven had been kind in the boat—practical kindness, precise kindness, kindness with the courtroom face on. *Come as the person I pulled from the water.* As if that person were a debt still outstanding. As if eleven years of flights and silences and never-asked questions hadn't already paid it down to almost nothing.

Almost nothing. Not nothing.

Oscar folded the letter along its original creases and put it inside their jacket, against the chest, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

The trouble—the whole rotten trouble of it—was that Oscar could not pretend the debt was imaginary. Somewhere in the flooded dark, with the cold closing its fist, Corven's hands had been real. The blanket had been real. The license, the hangar rent forgiven twice, the winter Oscar spent feverish at Graywater House with Corven himself reading aloud in the evenings because the housekeeper had quit—all of it real, all of it weight, and weight doesn't stop weighing just because you discover the scale belongs to someone else.

Loyalty, Oscar thought, walking back toward the strip with the coffee going cold in their hand, was gravity. It didn't argue. It didn't explain. It just pulled, constantly, in one direction, and the only question was whether you were strong enough to fly against it—and pilots, Oscar knew better than anyone, were not strong enough to fly against anything forever. Every pilot came down eventually. The art was choosing the ground.

The chest rode in the hold where Oscar had left it, tarp over the seal, patient as ballast. Oscar untied the tarp and looked at the stamp a long moment—that erased nation's crest, the double river and the broken crown—and heard Ilse's voice from two nights ago, low across the fire at the fuel dock: *You don't want to know what that seal means? Then you're the only person on this river who doesn't.*

She was still aboard. Still lying badly about her grandmother, still watching the horizon south with an expression Oscar recognized from mirrors. Two liars, one chest, and now a summons from the most dangerous man on the interior, offering friendship with one hand and something else with the other, folded flat between the lines where polite words go to drown.

Oscar retied the tarp.

Then they walked to the post office counter, bought a telegram form, and stood over it for a long time with the pencil hovering—because there was one reply possible that wasn't surrender and wasn't suicide, and it required exactly six words, and six words had never been harder to write.

Finally Oscar bent and wrote:

*Received. Traveling. Will arrive when able.*

Not yes. Not no. A pilot's answer—true on its face, promising nothing, buying days. Oscar paid the clerk, walked out into the briny light, and found Ilse waiting by the plane with her arms crossed and her eyes on the envelope-shaped shadow still pressed against Oscar's jacket.

"Well?" she said.

Oscar looked at her, and at the fog beginning to rise off the far bank, and understood that whatever came next, the days of carrying this alone were over.

"That was Corven," they said. "He wants his chest back."
