## 6. Ilse's Half of the Story

Ilse had learned to count lies the way other people counted coins—by weight, by wear, by whose hand had spent them last. The pilot lying across the boat's thwart from her was carrying at least three: one about not caring what was in the chest, one about not knowing what Vessany meant, and one about being the kind of person who ferried strangers out of kindness rather than because the river gave them no choice.

She could respect that. She was carrying four herself.

The rain had thinned to a fine grey sift, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air and wait for you to walk through it. They were moored under a stand of water-figs on a bend where the channel widened into something almost lake-like, the current slack enough that the boat didn't strain at its line. Oscar had said they'd wait out the worst of the weather here, and then had gone silent in the particular way of people who wanted you to believe their silence was indifference.

The chest sat between them like a third passenger. Sealed. Patient. Smelling faintly of river mud and older things beneath it—oilcloth, ink, the ghost of a room that no longer existed anywhere but in paper.

"You keep looking at it," Oscar said, without opening their eyes.

"I keep looking at the rain."

"You keep looking at it the way a dog looks at a door." Oscar opened one eye. "I've hauled crates of vaccines, crates of rifles, once a crate of wedding dresses for a bride who'd changed her mind about everything except the dress. Cargo doesn't look back at people. That one's looking at you."

Ilse turned her collar up against the sift. She had rehearsed this conversation for eleven years—in ferry cabins, in border queues, in the blue hour before dawn in rented rooms—and every version of it had ended differently, and none of them had ended with her telling the truth. Truth was a currency she'd never been able to afford. But the plane was dead somewhere behind them, the monsoon was coming down the valley like a debt collector, and this stranger with careful hands and careful silences was the only thing standing between her and forty years of walking.

So. One coin, spent.

"My grandmother's name was Mirela Voss," she said. "Before that, it was Mirela Kestrel-Vossany. She changed it in the winter of the erasure, when having that second name could get you a visit at night and no morning after."

Oscar sat up slowly. The boat rocked with the small adjustment of their weight, and the chest shifted an inch toward Ilse, as if it had been listening too.

"Vessany," Oscar said. Not a question. Just the word, set down gently on the deck between them, like something that might go off if dropped.

"The country," Ilse said. "Except it isn't a country anymore. It's a word your maps don't have and my family can't stop saying."

* * *

Her grandmother had been a clerk. That was the whole of it, and the immensity of it, and Ilse made herself say it plainly because plainness was the only armor she trusted.

"When the Provision was founded—the famine fund, the one that built the granaries and the clinics and half the river towns your gossip routes touch—someone had to keep the ledgers. Actual ledgers. Ink and linen paper and wax seals, because the men who ran the Provision trusted paper more than people, which is the most honest thing I know about them. My grandmother kept the disbursement records for the upper basin. Every sack of grain, every bolt of canvas, every salary paid to every overseer. She had beautiful handwriting. I still have a letter she wrote me when I was six, and it looks like it was printed by a machine that loved its work."

The rain thickened briefly against the fig leaves, then relented. Somewhere across the water a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"For nine years she watched the numbers come in and the numbers go out, and for six of those years the numbers matched. And then they stopped matching. Not obviously—that was the genius of it. A percentage shaved here. A shipment recorded as spoiled that arrived intact somewhere else. Grain sold twice, paid for once. Contracts awarded to companies that turned out to be owned by the brothers-in-law of the men who signed them. The Provision wasn't looted all at once, the way a bank is robbed. It was bled, the way a body is bled. Slowly enough that the patient stayed standing. Long enough that by the time anyone thought to check, the people who would have done the checking had been promoted into silence or buried under new names."

Oscar hadn't moved. But Ilse had spent years reading stillness in people who made their living at borders, and she knew the difference between someone who had stopped listening and someone who had stopped breathing evenly. This was the second kind.

"She started copying," Ilse went on. "Nights, mostly. She couldn't take the originals—the vaults were sealed, the counts were audited, and a missing page is a shout. But a copy in the same hand, on the same stock, with the same seals pressed from the same matrices—" She touched the chest lid without quite meaning to. The metal was cold and beaded with damp. "She copied for two years. Ledgers. Correspondence. A list she called the Family Tree, though it wasn't families the way you'd think—it was the network. Who married whom, who owed whom, who signed which authorization and whose account the money actually reached. Forty pages of names. Some of them are dead now. Most of them aren't. Their grandchildren run the interior districts. Their grandchildren sit on the boards of the shipping concerns. Their grandchildren—" she paused, and decided to spend the second coin— "hold magistracies."

She watched that land. Watched Oscar's face do the small, controlled thing that people did when a fact they'd been circling for days finally stood up and introduced itself.

"And when the erasure came," Oscar said quietly. "When Vessany stopped being on the maps."

"That was the masterstroke, if you want to call it that. You can't audit a country that doesn't exist. The Provision was international—chartered across four governments, funded by five—but its heart, its archives, its accounting seat, was Vessany. When the border wars gutted it and the treaties redrew everything, the Provision's records became, officially, the property of a dissolved state. Unowned. Unownable. The successor administrations each claimed the territory and none claimed the debts. The money that had been stolen simply... stopped having been stolen. There was no victim nation left to press the charge. There were no books left to balance." Ilse smiled, and knew it was the bad smile, the one that made strangers move their bags closer. "It's the cleanest theft in the history of the interior. They didn't even need to burn anything. Geography did it for them."

"Except your grandmother's copies."

"Except those." She drew a breath that hurt a little on the way in. "She smuggled them out over three trips, sewn into grain sacks, in the false bottom of a coffin—she liked telling that part; she said the funniest hiding places are the ones grief makes people afraid to open. The chest itself was a Provision transport case. Standard issue, thousands of them, except hers carried the seal because it came out of the archive proper, on the last night, with help from a man whose name she took to her grave and I've spent eleven years trying to dig up. The chest was supposed to reach the Provision's oversight commission in Rivergate. There was going to be a hearing. There was going to be justice, the slow ugly kind, but real."

"What happened?"

"Rain happened. War happened. The courier drowned at the Two Sisters crossing—or that's the story—and the chest went into the river, and the river kept it for forty years, and everyone who might have testified died or scattered or learned to pronounce their own names differently." Ilse looked out at the grey water. "My grandmother lived to be eighty-one. She spent the last thirty of those years waiting for news that never came, and teaching me the ledger numbers the way other grandmothers teach hymns. She died with the first page of the disbursement records memorized, in case the copies were ever destroyed. In case *I* ever needed to rebuild them from memory. She made me recite them at funerals. Under my breath, during the eulogies. So they'd be anchored to something solemn enough to stick."

The silence afterward was long and full of rain-sound. Oscar looked at the chest, then at her, and Ilse braced for the questions that always came next—the reasonable ones, the cruel ones: *why you, why now, what's your share.*

What Oscar asked instead was: "The seal. It's the Provision's mark, not Vessany's national arms?"

"Both. The Provision used the double seal—fund and state—because the charter required it. That's how I knew, the day you pulled it up, before you even opened the crate. There are maybe sixty genuine double-seal cases documented. Collectors pay fortunes for empty ones." She hesitated. Third coin. "I've handled two fakes in my life. Held them. The weight is wrong. The wax matrix is wrong by a millimeter. Yours is real. I knew it from the water, from twenty meters, before your rope even reached it."

"So you weren't stranded at that landing by accident."

"I was stranded at that landing because I've followed this chest down every rumor of it for eleven years, and the flood brought it to the one channel I was already walking." She let that sit. "Whether that's luck or something else, I haven't decided. Neither should you."

* * *

Oscar got up, eventually, and dug coffee out of the cargo box—a dented tin pot, grounds that tasted of char and altitude—and poured two cups without asking, which Ilse chose to read as a good sign. Pilots, in her experience, were either generous with small things or generous with nothing, and the ones who shared bad coffee were usually the ones worth trusting with large things.

"Why now?" Oscar said, settling back onto the thwart. "Forty years. Your grandmother's dead. The names in that chest are old men or their heirs. Why spend your life hauling a dead nation's paperwork around the interior?"

Ilse wrapped both hands around the cup. The tin was hot enough to sting, and she was grateful for it. Pain made honesty easier; that was another thing her grandmother taught her, though probably not on purpose.

"Because three years ago I found the granddaughter of one of the names," she said. "A woman in a river town your routes probably cross twice a month. Her grandfather's signature is on page nineteen of the Family Tree—an authorization diverting famine grain to a private buyer while his district lost four hundred people to the hunger winter. She didn't know. She had grown up on the pension his theft paid for, gone to the school his theft built, and she was proud of him. When I showed her the transcription—just one page, just one name—she looked at it for a long time and then she said, *'That can't be right, because if it were right, everything would be different.'*" Ilse drank. "And then she asked me to leave her town, and I did, because she was right. If it's right, everything is different. Everything. The towns, the pensions, the schools, the graves, the histories carved on the stones. Some people can't live inside that much different. I understand it. I've never forgiven it."

"And you can?"

"I don't live inside anything. That's the trick of it." She heard her own voice go flat and didn't correct it. "No fixed address. No surname I use twice in the same district. No debts, no lovers, no dog. I'm the last clerk of a country that doesn't exist, and my entire job is to carry one filing cabinet across a river and put it on the right desk. After that—" she shrugged— "after that I'll find out what I am when I'm not carrying it. Or I won't survive to find out. Both outcomes have a certain tidiness."

Oscar was quiet for a while. The light was failing; the channel had gone the color of pewter, and the fig trees stood black against it like a crowd waiting for something to start.

"There's a magistrate," Oscar said at last, carefully, the way a person steps onto ice. "Corven. Out of Harrow Bend. He holds the interior circuits."

"I know the name." She said it lightly, and watched Oscar flinch at the lightness, and understood—suddenly, completely—what she'd been circling since the salvage. "He pulled you out of something once. Wreck, fire, river—I don't know which, and I'm not asking. He gave you your first work. And he's on the list, isn't he? That's why you've been flying like a man with a stone in his boot since we found the chest. Not because of what's in it. Because of whose hands you're afraid it lands in."

Oscar stared at her. The rain had stopped entirely now, and in the last grey light their face was open in a way it hadn't been in five days of shared cargo, and what was in it wasn't anger. It was the look of someone checking whether a wound had started bleeding again.

"I don't know what's on any list," Oscar said. "I haven't seen a list."

"No," Ilse agreed softly. "But you're afraid of one."

The chest sat between them, and the dark came down over the water, and neither of them moved to light a lamp. Ilse finished her coffee cold. She thought about her grandmother reciting numbers into the ground of other people's graves, keeping faith with a country that had been erased so thoroughly that its own survivors apologized for pronouncing it. She thought about the woman in the river town, and the word *different*, and how heavy a single syllable could get.

Then, far down the channel—so far it might have been imagination—a motor coughed. Once. Twice. Then nothing.

Oscar's head came up. Ilse was already reaching to kill the small spirit lamp she'd forgotten she'd lit, cupping the flame out with her palm, and in the sudden total dark they listened together, two liars with one secret, while the river carried the silence toward them like a message it expected them to sign for.
