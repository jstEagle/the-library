## 7. Grounded

The fuel stop at Kettering Bend was the kind of place Oscar had always trusted precisely because it was too dull to hide anything in.

A gravel strip mowed crooked into the riverbank grass. A fuel shed with a hand-painted sign—*PETROL, KEROSENE, NO CREDIT*—faded to the color of old bone. A dog that belonged to no one and everyone, sleeping in the shade of the pump trolley as if it had been assigned there by some municipal committee of dogs. The kind of stop where the most dangerous thing that ever happened was a wasp in the fuel cap.

Oscar had been landing at Kettering Bend for eleven years. Never twice in the same week, never long enough for the dog to learn their stride, but often enough that the man who ran the shed—a wide, slow-moving fellow called Doss—would raise two fingers off his coffee tin by way of greeting and ask nothing more than "the usual?" And the usual was forty gallons, a bottle of warm ginger beer, and no conversation.

That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement.

But this morning Doss wasn't at the shed.

The Yarrow's engine ticked itself cool on the apron while Oscar stood in the wet heat of early monsoon season and looked at the empty coffee tin sitting on an upturned crate, still steaming. Abandoned mid-sip. Doss did not abandon coffee. Doss had once finished a cup during a minor flood because, as he put it, *water'll wait, coffee won't.*

"Hello the shed," Oscar called, because you called hello even when you suspected nobody wanted to answer.

Nothing. The dog lifted its head, considered Oscar with one brown eye, and put its chin back down—but it didn't come over. That was the detail that sat wrong in Oscar's chest, small and cold as a swallowed stone. The dog always came over. The dog had opinions about everyone who landed here, and it shared them freely, and it had never once declined to share them with Oscar.

Ilse came around the wing with her bag over her shoulder, squinting at the shed, then at Oscar. She'd learned to read Oscar's silences in the three days since the chest had come aboard—not well, not completely, but well enough to know which ones meant *stay back* and which meant *come look at this.*

"Doss?" she asked.

"Gone somewhere." Oscar kept their voice level. "Coffee's still hot."

She glanced at the tin. Oscar watched her do the same arithmetic—the steam, the empty apron, the dog lying flat and deliberate as a rug—and saw her arrive at the same sum. Her hand drifted toward her jacket pocket, where she kept a short knife she pretended was for cutting rope.

"Maybe he heard the plane and went to the privy," she offered, without conviction.

"Doss has a privy behind the shed. It's nine steps away. He'd have taken the coffee."

They stood there a moment longer, two people listening to a river town pretend to be asleep. Somewhere across the water a kingfisher rattled. The monsoon clouds were stacking themselves up over the western hills like furniture being moved into a room nobody used, gray and patient and enormous. Rain would come by evening. Rain would come whether they were ready or not; rain always did.

"Well," Oscar said, and made themself walk to the shed.

Inside, everything was where it should be. Fuel ledger open on the desk, last entry Doss's own crabbed hand from yesterday evening. Spare spark plugs in their greased paper. The smell of kerosene and rubber and mouse. Nothing ransacked, nothing missing, nothing wrong—except that the whole room had the quality of a stage set viewed from too close, the paint a little too fresh, the props arranged by someone who knew what a fuel shed should contain but not what Doss kept in his pockets.

On the desk, weighted under the ledger stone, was an envelope.

Oscar looked at it for a long time before touching it. Heavy cream paper. Sealed with wax—the old way, the expensive way—and pressed into the wax, a crest: a bridge over three wavy lines, crowned.

Oscar knew that crest the way you know a scar. Not because you study it. Because it's yours.

Magistrate Corven's seal.

* * *

They opened it standing in the doorway, where the light was honest, and Ilse read it over Oscar's shoulder without asking permission, which told Oscar something about how far trust had come and how far it hadn't.

*Yarrow,*

*I'm told you've been pulling things out of the river that don't belong to you. I'm sure that's a misunderstanding. You were always better at finding than at keeping.*

*Come to Corven Hall. Bring what you found. We'll talk about it the way we talked about your first wreck—you remember how that conversation went. Nobody shouted. Nobody lost anything they couldn't afford to lose.*

*You owe me a life. I've never once called it in. Consider whether this is the moment I should have to.*

*—A.C.*

The handwriting was beautiful. That was the worst part. Forty years of signing death warrants and land seizures and famine requisitions, and the man's hand still looked like something out of a calligraphy primer, each letter placed with the care of a gardener setting out bulbs.

"He knows," Ilse said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"He knows *something.*" Oscar folded the letter along its original crease, which was already there—it had been folded and unfolded before it reached the desk, read by someone else, or several someone elses. "He doesn't know what. If he knew what, this wouldn't be an invitation. It'd be a warrant."

"You're sure?"

"I've seen his warrants." Oscar heard their own voice go flat, the voice they used for weather reports and cargo manifests. "This is worse."

"Why worse?"

Because a warrant was the law, and the law could be fought, delayed, appealed, survived. An invitation from Aldous Corven was a different instrument entirely. It was the sound of a door closing so gently you almost thanked him for it.

Oscar didn't say any of that. Instead they said, "Fuel first. Then we decide."

And that was when they walked the preflight, and found what Doss's absence had really been about.

* * *

The sabotage was good work. That was the second worst part.

Oscar had learned engines from a woman named Ferro who used to say that every machine tells you when it's been touched, you just have to know its dialect. The Yarrow spoke to Oscar in a language of torque values and wire wear and the particular shine that metal gets when a wrench has loved it properly. And walking the wing, running fingers along the cowling seams, Oscar felt the dialect change.

Someone had been inside the port-side magneto housing. The screws were back in their holes, seated flush—but flush isn't right. Factory screws sit a half-turn proud and wear a bright ring of contact. These sat deep, and the slots were clean, unscarred, turned by a driver that fit perfectly. A professional. Someone who owned the correct tools and had used them many times.

Oscar popped the housing and looked for a long minute without touching anything.

The points had been gapped wrong—not grossly wrong, not *won't start* wrong, but wrong in the sly way that kills you forty minutes out over water when the engine heats and the metal grows and the gap closes to nothing. Intermittent ignition failure. The kind of fault that reads as bad luck. The kind of fault that grounds you at whatever field you manage to limp onto, or doesn't ground you at all, just drops you into the river between one heartbeat and the next.

There was more. In the fuel strainer, a fine mesh of fibers—cotton, maybe, or hemp—that would swell with petrol and choke the flow slowly, gradually, a starvation rather than a seizure. And under the tailplane, a hairline score in the elevator pushrod that Oscar only found because they lay down in the gravel and looked up along the line of it the way Ferro had taught them, cheek to the earth, eye following the rod like a surveyor sighting a horizon.

Somebody had prepared this plane to fail beautifully. To fail in a manner indistinguishable from age, from weather, from Oscar's own famously casual maintenance. To fail in a way that would leave no accusation possible, because there would be nobody left to make it.

Or—one survivor, maybe. One witness, limping home with a story about engine trouble, about the river giving out beneath them, about how the chest must have gone down with everything else.

Oscar sat back on their heels in the gravel and let that thought finish itself.

Not murder. Erasure.

"They want it quiet," they said aloud.

Ilse crouched beside them. She'd followed the inspection in silence, watching Oscar's hands, learning the plane's dialect a little herself. Now she looked at the opened magneto housing, then at Oscar's face.

"The magistrate's men did this."

"Not his men." Oscar closed the housing gently, reverently, as if the screws might apologize. "His men are clerks and constables. This is contract work. Somebody he pays through a third hand, somebody who's done it before." They wiped their fingers on their trousers and left four clean stripes in the grease. "Corven wants the chest. But he can't send soldiers to seize it—there'd be paperwork, witnesses, a record. And he can't ask for it legally, because legally it's salvage and salvage is mine until someone proves otherwise, and proving otherwise means opening questions about Vessany that nobody in the interior wants opened."

"So he makes the problem disappear."

"He makes *me* disappear, and lets the river keep the rest." Oscar stood. Their knees cracked. Eleven years of climbing in and out of cockpits, written into the joints. "Crash at sea, salvage lost, sad story about a pilot who flew careless in monsoon season. Everyone who ever warned me I flew too much on luck gets to be right posthumously. Very tidy. He'd hate the mess of anything less."

Ilse was quiet. When Oscar glanced at her, she was looking at the letter in their hand with an expression Oscar couldn't file—something between fury and recognition, as if she'd seen this exact machinery before, in another country, aimed at other people.

"My grandmother's village got a letter like that once," she said. "Official stationery. Asking them, very courteously, to surrender their ledgers for review. The ones who surrendered them were fine afterward. The ones who didn't—" She stopped. Started again. "The polite letters are the dangerous ones. The rude ones mean they think you're weak. The polite ones mean they're afraid of what you're holding."

Oscar looked down at the cream paper, at the crowned bridge, at the careful beautiful hand. *You owe me a life.*

And there it was. The gravity of it. Thirty years ago, a boy pulled half-drowned from a wrecked mail plane, coughing river water into the chest of a magistrate who happened to be inspecting the levee that day. Corven had wrapped him in his own coat. Had sat with him by the fire. Had said, *you've got pilot's hands, boy, wasted on drowning—come see me when you're grown,* and had meant it, and had made it true. First license. First route. First hangar key, pressed into Oscar's palm with the words *don't lose it, and don't lose yourself.*

Every rule Oscar lived by—never land twice, never learn names, never carry anything but cargo—had been built on top of that debt like a house on a foundation nobody inspected.

And now the foundation was writing letters.

"We're not going to Corven Hall," Oscar said.

"No," Ilse agreed. "We're not."

"But we can't fly either." Oscar turned and looked at the Yarrow, gleaming dully under the bruise-colored sky, poisoned in three places by hands they'd never see. "I could fix the magneto in an hour. The strainer in twenty minutes. The pushrod—" They shook their head. "The pushrod needs a replacement I don't carry, and Doss doesn't stock, and the nearest airfield that does is two days' drive through country where somebody is clearly paying attention to who moves and how."

"So we're grounded."

"We're grounded." The word tasted like gravel and river water. Oscar had spent thirty years making sure that word could never apply to them. Grounded pilots got caught. Grounded pilots became names instead of rumors. Grounded pilots answered doors.

Across the strip, the dog finally rose, stretched with theatrical slowness, and trotted over to lean against Oscar's leg—which meant the coast was clear, which meant whoever had worked on the plane was truly gone, which meant Doss would come shuffling back within the hour with a story about visiting his sister and eyes that wouldn't quite meet theirs. Paid to be absent. Paid to forget. The whole bend bought for the price of a few envelopes.

Oscar scratched the dog's ears and felt the trap close around them with the softness of a lid, not the slam of a cage.

Rain began—not the monsoon yet, just the overture, fat drops hitting the gravel like thrown coins. The clouds over the western hills had finished moving their furniture. By nightfall the river would be up. By morning the low fields would be lakes.

"There's a trader moored below the bend," Ilse said, watching the water darken. "Old fellow. One eye. Boat looks like it lost an argument with the entire river and negotiated a settlement."

"Brannoch," Oscar said, before they could stop themself, and then cursed inwardly—because knowing the name broke a rule, and breaking rules was how you ended up owing people, and owing people was how you ended up reading letters with crowns on them.

"You know him?"

"I know *of* him. Everybody on the river knows of Brannoch. He'll sell us a boat." Oscar looked at the Yarrow one last time—at the wings that had carried medicine and gossip and silence up and down this water for eleven years, grounded now by a screwdriver and a length of cotton thread—and felt something shift in their chest that they refused to name, because naming it would make it true, and true things had to be dealt with.

The truth was simple: Corven had taken the sky away. Which meant the river was all that was left.

"Get the chest," Oscar said. "Wrap it in the tarp. We're going to go buy the ugliest boat on the Rivergate."

Thunder answered from the hills, low and patient, like something clearing its throat before it spoke.

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, at the tree line past the fuel shed, a man in a rain cape lowered a pair of binoculars and walked away unhurried through the wet grass, leaving footprints that the rising water would take by morning—along with everything else.
