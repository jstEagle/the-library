## 8. The Leaking Boat

The rain came the way it always did at the start of monsoon—not all at once, but in a kind of negotiation. A few fat drops on the tarmac of the fuel dock, then a pause, as if the sky were reconsidering. Then more, heavier, drumming on the dead plane's wing like fingers tapping out a question Oscar didn't want to answer.

Oscar stood under the corrugated awning of Brannoch's Salvage & Sundries and watched the water bead up and run off the Cessna's cowling. The engine sat open like a patient on a table, its magneto housing cracked clean through, its wiring chewed to copper confetti by someone who'd known exactly which three wires mattered. Sabotage was a precise trade. Whoever Corven had sent had done tidy work.

"You can stare at it all week," Ilse said beside him. She'd wedged herself into the one dry corner with her back against a stack of crab traps, arms crossed, watching the river instead of the sky. "It won't fix itself."

"I know how engines work."

"And I know how long parts take to come upriver this time of year. Six weeks if you're lucky. Eight if the ferryman at Kessel's Landing is still drinking." She turned her head, just enough. "Monsoon starts for real in four days. Maybe five."

Oscar said nothing, because there was nothing true to say that wasn't also hopeless. The chest sat between them on the concrete, wrapped in a tarpaulin, looking smaller than it had any right to look—just a box, forty years old, salt-stained and sealed with the crest of a country that no longer existed. It had ruined a perfectly good airplane. It was about to ruin everything else.

Across the lot, half-hidden behind a leaning wall of outboard motors and net floats, a hand-painted sign read: BOATS. BUY. SELL. REPAIR (SOMETIMES). Underneath, in smaller letters: NO REFUNDS, NO QUESTIONS, NO EXCEPTIONS.

"That sign," Ilse said, "is either a warning or an invitation."

"Both," said the man who owned it.

He came around the corner of his own inventory like something the tide had left—a big man gone soft at the edges, one eye clouded white as river fog, the other sharp and brown and amused. He wore an oilskin coat that had been patched so many times it was less a garment than an archive, and he moved with the rolling gait of someone who'd spent fifty years compensating for decks that wouldn't hold still.

"Brannoch," he said, and stuck out a hand like a boat cleat. "Salvage, sundries, and occasional honesty. You're the pilot whose plane died at my dock."

"Word travels fast."

"Word travels *wet* this time of year. Everything comes downriver eventually—driftwood, rumors, dead men's hats." He looked at the tarpaulin bundle without appearing to look at it, which was a skill. "You'll be wanting a boat."

"We're wanting a miracle," Oscar said. "A boat will do."

Brannoch grinned, and the grin did something complicated with his face, rearranging the scars and the stubble into something almost boyish. "Then follow me, and remember: no refunds, no questions, no exceptions."

* * *

His inventory lived in a flooded slip behind the shop, reachable by a plank walkway that bounced underfoot like a bad rope bridge. Boats lay there in every state of dignity and decay—canoes split along their seams, a cabin cruiser with a tree growing through its deck, punts stacked like fallen dominoes. Rain ticked off the water around them, filling the slip with a sound like slow applause.

Brannoch led them past all of it to the far end, where a riverboat sat low in the water with the resigned air of an old dog that knows the vet is coming.

She was maybe thirty feet, clinker-built, once painted a green so faded she now looked like something the forest had grown rather than built. Her cabin was a low wooden box with two small windows fogged from the inside. Her name—*Marta B*, by the ghost of gold lettering on her bow—was flaking away like old skin.

"She's got good bones," Brannoch said.

"She's got a list," Oscar said, already walking her length. "See that? She's sitting two inches lower on the port side than starboard. Either she's carrying water or she's carrying regret."

"Regret," Brannoch said comfortably. "Water you could pump."

Oscar crouched at the stern and rapped the hull with a knuckle. The wood answered with a dull, wet thud—the sound of timber that had given up arguing. Farther forward, near the waterline, a patch of caulking had been renewed recently and badly, with the wrong pitch, the way a liar overexplains.

"When did you last sail her?"

"Sailed? Never. Floated her, mostly. From here to the fuel dock and back, when I needed to feel young." Brannoch leaned against a piling, entirely at ease, watching Oscar inspect his merchandise with the expression of a man enjoying theater. "Before me she belonged to a fisherman up at Two Sisters. Before him, a tobacco trader. Before him—" he waved a hand, "—history. She's carried everything. Fish, tobacco, contraband, brides. One time a piano. Don't ask about the piano."

"What happened to the fisherman?"

"He retired."

"To where?"

"Retired," Brannoch repeated, with a weight that closed the subject like a hatch.

Ilse had climbed aboard and was standing in the cabin, testing the deck with her feet, listening to it the way Oscar listened to engines—with the whole body. "Bilge pump?" she called out.

"Hand pump. Brass. Works fine."

"Anchor?"

"Rust, mostly. But it's shaped right."

"Radio?"

Brannoch laughed, a great bark that scattered birds from the pilings. "Radio! On *her*? Girl, a radio would be cruel. Like giving glasses to a blind man and telling him to drive."

Oscar straightened up and looked at the boat the way you look at a horse you're about to buy from a man who loves horses: honestly, and with dread. "How much?"

"For you? Because I like your face, and because your friend there has the look of somebody who'd burn my shop down if I cheated her—" he held up a finger before Ilse could speak, "—which she should know I respect. Four hundred."

"Four hundred." Oscar looked at the boat. The boat looked back, listing gently, dripping rain. "I've seen coffins cost less."

"Coffins don't take you anywhere. That's their whole problem." Brannoch spat thoughtfully over the side. "Look, pilot. I'll tell you what she is, since you asked honest and I'm feeling the season. She leaks. Not fast—slow, dignified, like an old man weeping at a funeral. You'll pump her twice an hour in calm water, four times in weather. Her engine is a twenty-year-old single-screw diesel that runs rough but runs, because I rebuilt her myself and I don't rebuild things twice. She has no radio, no lights worth trusting, and a hull that would embarrass a raft. And she will get you downriver to Rivergate, because spite is the strongest material God ever made and this boat is full of it."

There was a silence filled only by rain.

"That," Ilse said from the cabin doorway, "is the most honest sales pitch I've ever heard."

"It's why I put NO QUESTIONS on the sign. Saves time."

Oscar looked at the money in their head—what remained after fuel, after bribes at the last two landings, after the quiet tax everyone on the river paid to everyone else. Three hundred and change. "Two seventy-five."

Brannoch's good eye narrowed with genuine pleasure, the pleasure of a craftsman meeting another craftsman. "Three-fifty, and I throw in the pump handle spare, two coils of line, and a tarp that's only been rained through once."

"Three hundred, and you throw in whatever's in that crate behind your shop marked LAMP OIL that I'm fairly sure isn't lamp oil."

For a moment the one good eye went very still. Then Brannoch threw back his head and roared, and somewhere in the slip a heron took offense and left. "Three hundred, the oil, the line, the tarp, and—" he pointed a thick finger at each of them in turn, "—you never mention the crate again."

"Done."

They shook on it, and his grip ground Oscar's knuckles together like a mortar and pestle.

* * *

Loading took the rest of the gray afternoon. They carried the chest aboard wrapped in its tarpaulin, and Oscar noticed that Brannoch watched the operation with his whole body very carefully not moving—watched the way they stowed it low and central, under the bunk frame, watched the weight distribution, watched the care—and said nothing at all. No questions. He was a man who honored his own signage.

The rain thickened toward evening, settling in for the night the way monsoon rain does, without hurry, like it owned the lease. By the time they cast off from Brannoch's private dock—it turned out he lived in a shack behind the shop with a stove pipe and a wind chime made of spoon handles—the river had gone the color of gunmetal and the current had picked up its monsoon pace, sliding past the banks with a long, muscular hiss.

Oscar took the tiller. Ilse stood at the bow with a lantern, calling depth against the dark the way she seemed to call everything—flatly, precisely, as though emotion were a currency she'd stopped carrying years ago.

"Sandbar, port side. Big one."

"I see it."

"You're drifting toward it."

"I'm *avoiding* it. There's a difference."

"There really isn't, from the sandbar's point of view."

The boat, true to her seller's word, ran rough and ran. The diesel knocked and coughed below like a consumptive uncle, but it kept its rhythm, and the *Marta B* shouldered into the current and held her heading, and after the first ten minutes Oscar began—reluctantly, professionally—to respect her. She was heavy in the turns and light in the hands, and when a log swept past close on the starboard side she rolled and recovered with a kind of grumpy grace, like a dancer who'd quit decades ago but remembered the steps.

At the top of the hour, water gurgled in the bilge, exactly as advertised. Oscar found the brass pump, seated the spare handle, and began to work it. The bilge water came up black and smelling of forty years—fish, tobacco, tar, and something faintly floral that Oscar decided not to think about. Twice an hour. Dignified, like an old man weeping.

Ilse came aft and sat across from them on the engine cover, lantern swinging from her hand, throwing their shadows huge against the cabin wall. For a while neither spoke. The river talked enough for both of them—water on wood, rain on water, the diesel's tubercular knocking.

"Your plane," she said finally. "You loved it."

It wasn't a question, but Oscar treated it like one anyway. "It's a machine."

"You checked the cowling four times before we left the dock. You touched it like people touch graves."

Oscar worked the pump. Up, down. Black water, silver rain. "Corven gave me that plane. First job after the wreck. Said a person who'd survived a crash that bad ought to have something to fly that wouldn't." Up, down. "Eleven years."

"So we get it back."

"With what? Parts don't exist this time of year. Money doesn't either, much of it." Oscar set the pump handle down and looked at her across the lantern light. "You knew that when you asked me to ferry you. Back at the landing. You saw the chest before I ever pulled it out of the channel, didn't you? You weren't surprised by that seal. You were *afraid* of it."

Ilse didn't flinch. She never flinched; it was one of the things about her that was starting to feel less like coldness and more like armor worn so long it had grown into the skin. "Yes."

"You could have told me what I was picking up."

"If I'd told you, you'd have said no."

"Would I have?"

"Yes," she said simply. "You have rules. Never land twice in the same place. Never learn names too well. Never carry anything but cargo." She recited them without mockery, which somehow made it worse. "A chest full of proof that half the interior's ruling families looted a famine fund—that's not cargo. That's a funeral. You'd have flown away from it, and I'd have let you, and then I'd have hired someone worse than you and they'd have gotten us both killed."

The diesel knocked. The rain fell. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat.

"And now?" Oscar asked.

"Now you already know too much, and so do I, and the boat leaks, and the monsoon's here, and there are men paid to find us who are better funded than we are." She lifted the lantern slightly, and in its light her face was younger than her voice. "So now we're the same kind of stranded. That's all trust is, out here. Being stranded in the same direction."

Oscar wanted to argue. It was an old reflex, arguing with the river, with the weather, with the truth—arguing with anything that couldn't be argued with. Instead they picked the pump back up and worked it, up and down, and the black water came, and the boat held.

* * *

They anchored past midnight in the lee of a drowned orchard—dead trees standing hip-deep in the floodwater, branches bare, like a congregation waiting for a sermon that had been canceled forty years ago. The rain had softened to a steady whisper. The diesel was off. In the silence, the river sounded enormous.

Oscar pumped the bilge one last time and found Ilse awake in the cabin, sitting with her back against the chest itself, knees drawn up, using it the way you'd use a wall. She wasn't sleeping. She was listening—to the hull, to the water, to the dark.

"It's holding," Oscar said.

"For now."

"That's the only tense we've got."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and was gone. "Brannoch," she said. "The fisherman at Two Sisters who 'retired.' You believe him?"

"No."

"Neither do I." She pulled the blanket tighter. "But he sold us the boat cheap and lied to us free, and there's a difference between a man who lies for profit and one who lies for practice. He liked us. Did you notice? He liked us and it embarrassed him."

Oscar thought about the handshake that had ground their bones, the eye that missed nothing, the way he'd watched them load the chest and asked nothing—no questions, no exceptions. A man who'd spent a lifetime learning exactly which silences kept you alive.

"Yeah," Oscar said. "I noticed."

Outside, the river slid past the drowned orchard, and the dead trees stood in the water with their arms out, and far downstream—two days, maybe three, if the *Marta B* held, if the pump held, if the rain held off long enough to let them run the narrows—the lights of Rivergate were burning behind curfew, and the hearings were coming, and somewhere between here and there the river would decide what it meant to carry the truth in a leaking boat.

Oscar settled onto the bunk above the chest, one hand resting on the tarpaulin the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you didn't fully trust, and listened to the water rise, and fall, and rise again.

Twice an hour. Dignified.

They'd make it, or they wouldn't. But for the first time since the fog that morning—since the chest, since the seal, since the name Vessany had crawled out of a drowned country and into their life—Oscar slept without dreaming of flying.

The river had them now. All that was left was to see where it set them down.
