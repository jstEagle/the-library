## 9. Monsoon Rules

The monsoon did not arrive so much as it was announced—by the birds first, then by Brannoch's knee.

I had known weather in the interior that came like a thief, quiet and total, but this river country broadcast its intentions. For two days the herons had flown low and stubborn, hugging the water as if pinned there. The air went thick enough to chew, sweet with rot and river mud and the green exhalation of drowned forests. And Brannoch, who claimed his body was a better barometer than anything the survey office ever built, spent those two days limping around the deck of the *Marta Kay*—his name for the rotten boat he'd sold us at three times its worth—pressing his palm to his bad knee and muttering figures under his breath like a man counting down to something.

"Twelve hours," he said on the second evening, spitting over the rail. "Maybe ten if the wind swings south."

Oscar stood at the tiller with their collar up, watching the sky go the color of old pewter. They didn't answer right away. They never answered right away; I had learned that much about them in the week since we'd bought this floating coffin and pointed it downriver. Oscar Yarrow answered questions the way a river answers a stone—eventually, and having worn something away in the process.

"Then we make for the cut at Serren Bend," they said finally. "There's an oxbow behind the levee. Old channel. It'll break the wind."

"It'll break the wind," Brannoch agreed, "and give every leech from here to Two Sisters a warm place to breed. But yes. It'll do."

I sat on the cargo hatch with my back against the cabin wall and my hands wrapped around the chest, because I had not let go of the chest in six days, and because sitting with it against my body was the only way I slept. It was smaller than people imagined, when they imagined it at all. A meter long, iron-banded, water-stained, stamped with a seal that no longer existed anywhere except inside it: the crowned reed of Vessany, pressed deep into the lead plate, patient as a scar.

My grandmother had carried papers out of a burning country sewn into the lining of a coat. She had crossed three borders with them and buried them twice and dug them up once, and she had died without telling anyone where the rest of it was—without telling me, though she told me everything else, all those winters in the cold flat above the bakery, her voice dropping whenever the neighbors' children were near. *The fund was not charity, Ilse. The fund was a promise. And promises are the first thing powerful men eat.*

The chest had found me before I found it. That was how I chose to tell it. The river gave it up to a bush pilot with salvage habits and no politics, and I walked onto Oscar Yarrow's dock with a forged ferry permit and a lie about needing passage to Rivergate, and within an hour we were both lying to each other so fluently it was almost a kind of honesty.

Now the sky was going pewter to slate to black, and the lie we were both living inside—a boat, a plan, a shared destination—was about to be tested by twelve hours of rain.

* * *

We made the oxbow with perhaps an hour to spare, and even that felt like theft.

The cut behind the levee at Serren Bend was exactly what Oscar promised: a dead arm of the river, curled away from the main current like a finger withdrawn from a hand, choked with willow and standing water and the pale ghosts of trees that had drowned decades ago and never been allowed to fall. Brannoch backed us in under the willows until the branches scraped the deckhouse roof, and then he dropped both anchors—one ahead, one astern—with the economy of a man who had ridden out a hundred storms in worse holes than this, which he made sure to mention.

"Rules," he said, once the lines were set and the lamp was lit in the cabin. He held up fingers. "One: nothing loose stays loose. Tie it or lose it. Two: nobody on deck after dark, not for any reason, because the deck will be underwater and so will you. Three—" he looked at me, then at Oscar, and his one eye had a wet gleam of genuine malice in it, "—nobody touches my brandy."

"You don't have any brandy," Oscar said.

"Rule four," said Brannoch. "Nobody accuses me of things before the storm makes me do them."

It should have been funny. Some part of it was—I caught the corner of Oscar's mouth doing something it almost never did, a twitch toward a smile, quickly suppressed, as if smiling were another form of landing twice in the same place. But underneath the humor the air in the cabin was taut as wire. We were three strangers in a box seven feet wide, about to be sealed into it by a storm, and none of us had chosen the other two.

Oscar checked the bilge pump—a hand-cranked thing bolted to the keel, older than Brannoch by the look of it—and cranked it twice to hear it suck. The sound was wet and reluctant. I watched Oscar listen to it the way a doctor listens to a heart, and I watched the small frown that followed, and I filed it away. I had gotten good at filing things away about Oscar Yarrow. It was the closest thing to intimacy either of us permitted.

The first rain came at dusk, fat drops the size of grapes that hit the deck like thrown stones. Then the wind arrived behind it, swinging south just as Brannoch's knee had sworn it would, and the whole world outside the willows turned to sound.

* * *

I have been in storms. I want to say that plainly, because what happened that night was not simply a storm. It was the monsoon proper, the front that the river towns name and remember, the one that comes off the high plateau carrying half a mountain's worth of water and spends it all in a night. Inside the cabin of the *Marta Kay*, it sounded like the sky had come down to argue with the river personally, and we were the subject of the dispute.

The boat leaked. Of course it leaked. Brannoch had sold it to us with a straight face and a speech about honest wear, and now the honest wear announced itself in a dozen thin silver threads of water finding their way through seams in the planking, tracing paths down the ribs of the hull, pooling in the low places with a patience that felt personal. By midnight there was an inch of water sloshing across the deck boards inside the cabin, and the hand pump needed cranking every twenty minutes, and the crank needed two hands, and the two hands available belonged to whoever wasn't holding the chest or bailing the corner where the worst seam wept.

So we learned the boat. That was the first lesson of the monsoon: you learn a vessel the way you learn a wound, by putting your fingers in it. Oscar mapped the leaks by lamplight, calling them out in a flat pilot's voice—*seam, port side, third rib; seep, sternpost, slow*—as if naming them would tame them. Brannoch showed me how to wedge oakum into a working seam with the back of a knife, hammering it home while the boat rolled and the river tried to shake my hands off. My knuckles split. The blood washed away pink in the bilge water and I kept hammering, because the alternative was thinking about the water rising, and I had decided somewhere in the last week that I was not a person who thought about water rising.

Around two in the morning, the anchors began to drag.

I felt it before anyone said it—a change in the rhythm of the rolling, a new note in the scrape of the willow branches along the roof, as if the boat had shifted its weight from one foot to the other. Brannoch felt it too. He was up off his bunk without a word, moving through the dark cabin with the certainty of a man navigating his own skull, and when he threw open the door the storm came in with him—a fist of rain and wind that put the lamp out and left us in absolute blackness except for the lightning, which was coming now in long silent sheets that turned the world white for a heartbeat at a time.

In one of those heartbeats I saw the view through the door: the oxbow gone brown and violent, the drowned trees waving, the far levee invisible, and our stern swung a full boat-length out of the position we'd anchored in.

"We're walking," Brannoch shouted, and disappeared onto the deck.

Oscar went after him. I heard myself say their name—it was the first time I'd said it aloud, actually, rather than merely knowing it—and then I did the thing I am apparently constitutionally incapable of not doing: I picked up the chest, braced it against my chest, and followed.

What happened on deck took four minutes and lasted approximately a year. The rain hit sideways with enough force to bruise. The deck pitched underfoot, slick as ice, running with water already shin-deep because the scuppers couldn't keep pace. Brannoch was at the stern anchor line, hauling, his ruined knee buckling with each pull, cursing with a creativity that the wind tore apart before it could be properly appreciated. Oscar got the bow line and leaned back against it with their whole body, boots braced against the rail, and I saw—in the strobing light—that they had tied themselves to the boat. A length of line around the waist, clipped to the mast step. They had done it hours ago, quietly, without announcing it, without making it a lesson or a rebuke. They had simply assumed the storm, the way they seemed to assume everything: alone.

I wedged the chest under the lip of the deckhouse where the wind couldn't take it, and I went to the stern line, and I hauled.

I am not strong. I have never been strong; I was a clerk's granddaughter and a baker's daughter and I have spent my life being underestimated in ways I encouraged. But fear is a kind of strength, and the fear I felt that night was very specific: not of drowning, oddly, but of losing the boat, the chest, these two impossible people, all of it together into the brown water, and being left on the bank of my own purpose with nothing in my arms. So I hauled. Brannoch roared something that might have been *take the slack* or might have been a prayer, and I took the slack, and the line burned through my palms, and between the three of us we walked the *Marta Kay* back into the lee of the willows inch by inch, and Brannoch set the second anchor deeper, and it held.

When it was done we stood in the deckhouse doorway, three shapes streaming water, breathing like landed fish. Lightning sheeted again. In the white instant I saw Brannoch look at me—at my hands, which were bleeding freely now, at the chest jammed under the deckhouse lip, at me—and I saw him decide something. I didn't know what yet. I know now. That was the night Brannoch stopped calculating whether we were worth the trouble and started calculating what the trouble would cost him personally, which is, in his language, the same thing as love.

"Below," he said. "All of you. Pump rotation. Four hours on, eight off, and I get the first sleep because I'm old and useful."

* * *

The eight hours off were the problem.

You cannot sleep in a storm like that—not really—but you can collapse, and we did, in shifts, on the bunks and the bench, while the boat groaned around us like a large animal in pain. When my turn came to relieve Oscar at the pump, near dawn, I found them cranking with a steadiness that was somehow worse than panic, water sheeting off their elbows, eyes fixed on the middle distance.

"Go sleep," I said.

"I'm fine."

"That's not what I asked."

They stopped cranking. In the guttering lamplight they looked at me, and for a moment—the first moment since the dock at Brannoch's yard—they looked young. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with the night. "The pump's dying," they said. "The diaphragm's cracked. It's pumping maybe half of what comes in. If the storm holds past noon, we bail by hand or we swim."

"Then we bail by hand."

"You say that like it's simple."

"It is simple. It's just terrible." I took the crank from them. Their hands were shaking—I saw it now, the fine tremor of someone who has been afraid for a long time and has run out of ways to hide it. "Yarrow. Sit down. You've been upright for nine hours."

"And you've been holding that chest like it's a child for six days."

"It is a child." I said it before I could stop myself, and the words came out with more truth in them than I had planned, more than I had ever intended to spend in this company. "It's the only thing my grandmother ever managed to save, and everyone else who touched it is dead, and I am not going to watch it go to the bottom of an oxbow because its pilot is too proud to sleep. Sit. Down."

The rain hammered. The lamp guttered. And Oscar Yarrow—bush pilot, keeper of rules, the most self-contained person I have ever met—sat down on the bench beside the bilge, dripping, and within ninety seconds was asleep. Truly asleep, the boneless sleep of the completely spent, head tipped back against the cabin wall, mouth slightly open, hands still curled around the ghost of the crank.

I pumped. I watched them sleep. And I understood, with a clarity the storm had wrung out of me like water from cloth, that I was in danger—not from Sable, not from Corven's men, not from the cartel whose hunters I knew were somewhere behind us on the river, but from this. From the specific gravity of exhausted people in a small space. From the way survival strips you down to the parts that can be loved.

I had spent eleven years making sure no one could be lost to me. My grandmother taught me that too, though she never said it aloud: *love no one you cannot carry across a border.* And here I was, pumping bilge in a leaking coffin, guarding the sleep of a stranger who pulled wrecks out of rivers for pocket money and trusted no one, and I could feel the border dissolving.

The storm peaked an hour after dawn—or I assume it did; time had gone soft by then—and began, grudgingly, to spend itself. By midday the rain had thinned to a steady gray fall, the wind dropped to a moan, and the oxbow settled from violence into sullen chop. We bailed anyway. We bailed all afternoon, bucket and pot and, at one point, a boot, passing them in a rhythm that required no discussion: fill, pass, throw, return. Brannoch bailed with his knee propped on a crate and narrated the whole operation like a man commenting on a horse race he was also running. Oscar worked beside me, close enough that our elbows knocked on the backswing, and neither of us moved away.

That was the intimacy the monsoon imposed. Not conversation—we barely spoke—but rhythm. Three bodies learning to occupy the same small disaster without colliding. I learned that Oscar counted under their breath when they were frightened, numbers with no pattern I could find. I learned that Brannoch hummed when he hammered, some tuneless hymn from whatever coast had made him. And they learned, I suppose, whatever the storm showed them of me: that I don't flinch at thunder, that I can work eighteen hours without complaint, that I talk to the chest when I think no one is listening. All of which is true, and none of which is the truth.

* * *

Evening came down clean and strange, the sky scrubbed to a hard violet, the river running high and fast and glittering. The willows dripped. Somewhere across the oxbow a bird resumed its business as if the world hadn't nearly ended. The *Marta Kay* rode at her anchors, swollen with water, alive despite everything, and Brannoch declared her seaworthy in the way that a man declares a mule loyal: with affection and no illusions.

We ate heated ration tins on the deckhouse roof, because the cabin still stank of bilge, and for a while nobody talked. Then Brannoch, chewing, jerked his chin at the chest, which sat between us on the planks, drying, its iron bands rusting fresh blooms of orange.

"Soon as we're moving again," he said, "that thing gets opened. Not because I'm nosy. Because I'm practical. Whatever's in there has cost us a plane, a patrol boat's worth of attention, and my best knee. Before I risk any more of myself, I want to see the goods."

Oscar looked at me. Not asking permission—Oscar didn't ask, as a rule—but measuring, the way they measured weather and fuel and the load-bearing capacity of rotten decks.

"The seals are lead," I said. "They're meant to be broken. That's what seals are for—to prove whether they've been broken." I turned the chest so the Vessany stamp faced the last of the light. The crowned reed gleamed dully, forty years drowned and forty years hidden, and beneath my palms I could feel the faint tick of the metal cooling in the evening air, like something alive settling down to wait. "Tomorrow," I said. "At anchor, in daylight, with the hatch battened. There are things in there that shouldn't be read on a moving river."

Brannoch snorted. "Everything's read on a moving river. That's the whole point of a river."

But he let it stand. And when the light failed and we went below, I lay on my bunk with my hand flat on the chest lid, feeling the cool iron breathe, and I thought about what was waiting inside: my grandmother's ledgers, the correspondence, the list of names—the architects of the Provision and, folded among them somewhere like a blade in bread, the thieves. Corven's signature. I had never seen it. Tomorrow I would.

Above me, through the deckhouse roof, I heard Oscar climb out into the clearing night—breaking their own rule about decks after dark, the first rule I'd ever known them to break—and stand at the rail for a long time, looking downriver, toward Rivergate, toward whatever they were still deciding about all of us.

The chest ticked softly under my hand, cooling, keeping its counsel.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow we open it, and none of us will be able to un-know what's inside—and none of us, I already understood, would want to.
