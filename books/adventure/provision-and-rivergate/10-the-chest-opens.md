## 10. The Chest Opens

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, and in its absence the river made a sound I had never heard it make before—a low, patient breathing, as if the whole waterway were asleep with one eye open. I sat on the deck of Brannoch's rotten boat with my back against the cabin wall and the chest between my knees, and I did not touch it.

I had not touched it for three days.

That was a kind of discipline, or a kind of cowardice, and by the gray light seeping through the monsoon clouds I could no longer tell which. The chest had ridden with me from the flooded channel through checkpoints and sabotage and the first hard edge of the storm season, and all that time it had been a thing of weight and seal—iron banded, cedar beneath, stamped on its lid with the coiled serpent-and-sheaf of Vessany. A nation's ghost. As long as it stayed shut, it was cargo. The moment it opened, it became something else.

"You're going to stare at it until it opens itself?" Ilse stood at the rail, hair tied back with a strip of torn tarpaulin, watching the brown water slide past. She hadn't slept much either. I could tell by the way she held her shoulders, too carefully, like a woman carrying water across ice.

"I'm considering it," I said.

"Consider faster." She turned. "We're two days from the fuel dock at Marrow Bend, and after that there's nothing between us and Rivergate but river and people who want this box. I'd rather know what we're carrying than guess."

"We know what we're carrying."

"We know what we *hope* we're carrying." She crouched down beside me, close enough that I could smell woodsmoke and river damp on her. "Hope isn't ballast, Yarrow. It doesn't keep a boat steady."

She was right, and I hated that she was right, because the truth was simpler and worse: I didn't want to open it because part of me still believed there might be nothing inside worth killing for. Salvage habit had put the chest in my hands. If it turned out to hold forty-year-old grain receipts and the correspondence of dead clerks, then everything since—the burned plane, Sable at the checkpoint, the price on our heads—had been theater, and we'd been fools to play our parts.

But I'd seen Corven's men work. Men don't burn a pilot's engine block over grain receipts.

"Brannoch," I called.

A clatter from below, then the old trader hauled himself up the companionway with a pry bar in one fist and a mug of something steaming in the other. He looked at the chest, then at me, then at Ilse, and grinned with the half of his face the river hadn't taken.

"About time," he said. "Three days of you two circling that box like vultures with manners. Give us the bar."

* * *

The lock was Vessany work, which meant it was older than either of us and better made than anything sold on the river now. Brannoch attacked it anyway, muttering to it like an animal he meant to gentle, working the pry bar into the seam where lid met body while Ilse braced the chest against the deck with both boots. The first groan of iron was loud enough to startle a heron off the far bank. The second bent the hasp. On the third, the whole lock sheared away with a crack that ran up my arms even though I wasn't touching it, and the lid came free.

For a moment none of us moved. The rain had started again somewhere above us, fine as sifted flour, ticking on the canopy. Inside the chest, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay paper. Reams of it. Bundles tied with waxed cord, ledgers bound in government blue, folders fat with letters, and on top of everything, sealed in a leather folio cracked with age, a single sheet bearing the crest of the Provision Commission itself—the sheaf of wheat crossed with the physician's staff, the emblem that hung, painted and faded, over half the clinics and schoolhouses from here to the delta.

Ilse reached in first. Of course she did. Her hands were shaking, and she pretended they weren't.

"Oilcloth kept the water out," she said, and her voice had gone strange, flat, careful. "Whoever packed this knew what they were doing. Knew it would end up in the wet."

"Or hoped it wouldn't," Brannoch said.

She unwrapped the top bundle and spread it across a hatch cover, weighting the corners with the mugs and the pry bar and a coil of rope. Ledgers. Columns of figures in ink faded to the color of dried blood, but legible, all of it legible, and the headings were printed, official, the work of a proper chancellery: *Provision Fund — Regional Disbursement — Year Twelve.*

I read the first page standing up. I read the second sitting down, because my legs decided the question for me.

Here is what the numbers said, laid out in a clerk's patient hand: the Provision had been funded at a scale I had never imagined. Not the trickle of medicine and seed-grain that reached the towns—I'd flown that trickle myself for years, crate by crate, and knew its weight to the ounce—but a river of money, wide enough to pave every road, roof every school, stock every clinic from the highlands to the sea. The disbursements listed were enormous. And beside each entry, in the column where a receiving officer's countersignature belonged, there were names.

I knew some of them. Everyone on the river knew some of them. They were the names on the magistrate's rolls, the names on the trading-house letterheads, the families whose sons went into the interior service and whose daughters married into the provincial courts. The Harrowgates. The Vell family. The Odum brothers, who owned every fuel dock between Two Sisters and the falls.

And under the largest single disbursement—Year Fourteen, capital works, the sum of it enough to build a city—countersigned in a hand I recognized before I finished reading it:

**M. Corven, Provision Commissioner, Interior Districts.**

"That's not possible," I said.

Nobody answered me, which was answer enough.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened inside me in that hour, because precision is the only honesty left to a man holding a dead friend's signature in his hands. It wasn't rage. Rage came later, and coldly. What came first was arithmetic—the mind's ugly instinct to balance a ledger even when the ledger is your own life.

Corven had pulled me out of the wreck of the *Kestrel* when I was nineteen, out of black water and burning avgas, and carried me four miles through swamp to a mission clinic, and sat with me through the fever, and when I woke he had been reading a newspaper in the corner as if he'd never left. He gave me my first ferry contract when no one else would insure a pilot that young. He taught me the routes, the fog rules, the way a river changes its mind in autumn. Every good thing in my flying life had his fingerprints on it, and I had built the whole architecture of my loyalty on those fingerprints.

Now here was the same hand, younger, surer, signing away a famine fund. Signing it away in Year Fourteen—and I did the arithmetic again, slower, crueler—Year Fourteen was the year the granaries failed in the upper districts. Year Fourteen was the year the mission at Callow's Ford buried forty-one people in a mass grave because the relief barges came empty. I had grown up hearing about Callow's Ford. Half the pilots I knew had grown up hearing about it. It was the story we told to explain why we flew medicine through weather that should have grounded us.

The money that should have fed Callow's Ford had a signature on it, and the signature belonged to the man who taught me how to fly through fog.

"Yarrow." Ilse's voice, quiet. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You've stopped moving. You do that when—" She stopped herself. She didn't know me well enough yet to finish sentences like that, and to her credit she seemed to realize it. She just knelt there among the papers, sorting them into stacks with a clerk's own economy, and let me come back to the world on my own schedule.

Brannoch had retreated to the rail with his mug, giving us room, watching the banks with the professional paranoia of a man who had spent fifty years learning that the worst things on a river arrive when you're looking down. But even he glanced over once, and whatever he saw in my face made him say, gruffly, "Paper's paper, lad. It can't take back what it already did."

No, I thought. But it can take back everything I thought I knew.

* * *

We worked through the morning, and the rain worked with us, soft and constant, sealing us into the boat's small world. The chest held more than ledgers. Beneath them, wrapped separately, was correspondence—years of it, letters between the Provision's regional commissioners and the central office in Vessany itself, before the erasure. Some were routine, requisitions and inventories. Others were not.

One, dated eight months before Vessany vanished from the maps, was written in a hand I was beginning to know too well. Corven again, writing to a commissioner whose name meant nothing to me. The language was careful, bureaucratic, the language of a man building deniability into every sentence, but the meaning came through like a bone through skin: the writer proposed that certain disbursements be routed through "interior intermediaries" to avoid "the delays of audit." That the intermediaries' fees be "generous enough to ensure discretion." That the arrangement continue "for as long as the Fund's purpose is served"—and everyone reading it, then and now, understood that the Fund's purpose had already changed hands.

"They didn't steal it all at once," Ilse said. She had gone pale, and her voice had the tightness of someone holding a door shut against wind. "They siphoned it. For years. Through legitimate channels, with legitimate signatures, and every auditor who looked saw a functioning system because the system was designed to look like one. My grandmother—" She stopped. Started again. "My grandmother was a junior clerk in the Vessany chancellery. She spent eleven years copying these pages by hand, hiding them, passing them along, and she died believing someone would eventually read them. Do you understand? This is why the country was erased. Not politics. Not war. Someone needed the ledger to disappear, so the country disappeared with it."

"And Corven survived the erasure," I said. "He's the only commissioner who did."

"He didn't survive it." Ilse's eyes came up to mine, and there was something terrible in them—not accusation, just clarity. "He *managed* it. Look at the dates. Every man who countersigned alongside him is dead, exiled, or nameless. He's the one who helped draw the new maps."

At the bottom of the chest, beneath everything, wrapped in a final layer of oilcloth, was the list. A single sheet, longer than the others, written in the same clerk's hand as my grandmother's ghost might have used—no, Ilse's grandmother, I corrected myself; the hands of the dead are not mine to claim. Names in columns. Beside each name, a figure. Not amounts this time. Something else. I read the column heading twice before I understood it.

*Households unprovisioned — cumulative, by district.*

It was a count of the starved. Not the dead—the dead were elsewhere, in mission registers and mass graves—but the households that had received nothing, year over year, while the money flowed uphill. Thousands of entries. Whole villages reduced to a line of figures. And pinned to the list, keeping it company in the dark of the chest all these years, was a note in a different hand, smaller, hurried, human:

*So that no one may say we did not know.*

* * *

I walked to the stern after that. I told myself it was to check the bilge, and I did check the bilge, and the bilge was fine, and I stayed at the stern anyway with my hands on the rail because if I had stayed at the hatch cover one more minute I was going to put the papers back in the chest, seal it, and drop the whole thing into the river, and I knew it, the way you know the exact depth of water you're standing in.

Because here was the arithmetic that mattered, the one underneath all the others: if I dropped it, the past stayed buried. Corven stayed my savior. The wreck, the rescue, the first contract, the whole scaffolding of my life stayed standing, and I went on being a man who carried cargo and learned no names and landed nowhere twice. There was a version of me that could do it. I had met him before. He lived in the part of me that had never once, in twenty years of flying, asked a passenger why they were really going where they were going.

But the list was in my head now. *So that no one may say we did not know.* I knew. Knowing is a one-way door.

Behind me, pages turned. Brannoch had come back from the rail and was reading over Ilse's shoulder, and I heard him say, quietly, without any of his usual gravel-humor, "My sister's village is on this page. Third column." A pause. "She always said the barges came empty. Nobody believed her. We thought she was bitter."

The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat.

When I finally came back to the hatch, Ilse had set aside one document apart from the rest—a thin folder, newer than the others, its paper less foxed. She held it out to me without a word, and I took it, and the first thing I saw was a letterhead I knew: the Office of the Magistrate, Interior Districts. Corven's current station. The letter was recent. Within the year.

It was addressed to a fuel broker at Marrow Bend—the same dock we were two days from—and it authorized payment for "the recovery and quiet disposal of salvaged materials, per prior arrangement." No description of the materials. No signature line filled in.

But the handwriting was Corven's, and the seal was his, and the word *disposal* sat on the page like a bullet sits in a chamber.

"He's known where it was," Ilse said softly, "or suspected, for a long time. This isn't panic. This is housekeeping."

I folded the letter along its original creases and put it in my jacket, against my chest, where a man keeps the things that have stopped being documents and become verdicts. Then I looked downriver, toward Marrow Bend, toward the fuel dock where our plane's corpse waited and where Corven's arrangements were waiting too, and I felt the last of my old loyalty go out of me—not with a crash, but the way a tide goes out, so gradually you only notice it when the keel starts to grind.

"Two days," I said. "Then we stop running from this and start aiming it."

Brannoch spat over the rail, unhurried, ceremonial. "Aiming it at what, exactly?"

"At the hearings in Rivergate," Ilse said. "Where the whole world will be watching."

And that was when Brannoch laughed—not his market laugh, but something lower, aimed at the water. "Then you'd best hope," he said, "that the whole world is still watching by the time we get there. Because I've run this river forty years, and I'll tell you what I know: things that float toward Rivergate carrying trouble have a way of arriving lighter than they left."

He nodded at the chest, open and gutted between us, its ghost-nation staring up out of the ledgers.

"And I've never yet seen anything arrive heavier than this."
