## 11. Brannoch's Price

The one eye was the good one, mostly, though Brannoch had never been entirely sure which of the two had failed him first. The left had gone dark the winter the ice took his father's barge at Kell's Reach; the right had been going soft ever since, the world at its edge dissolving into a brown fog that no amount of squinting could burn off. What remained was enough. Enough to read water, which was the only reading that mattered on the Rivergate. Enough to count coin by feel. Enough to see, across the smoke of his own stove, exactly what those two fools from upriver were carrying in their leaking tub of a boat.

He'd known since the night he sold it to them.

* * *

Brannoch kept his trading post moored where the Marrow Channel split around a drowned island nobody bothered to name, because naming things on this river was how you got attached to them, and attachment was how you drowned. His post was three barges lashed together and sagging at the seams like an old man in a wet coat: fuel drums forward, dry goods amidships, and aft, under a tarp that flapped like a wounded bird, the things people paid him not to ask about. He'd run the Rivergate forty years. He'd sold rope to smugglers and salt to missionaries and once, memorably, a wedding dress to a woman who turned out to be marrying two men in different towns on consecutive Sundays. He asked nothing. That was the whole of his religion, and he practiced it devoutly.

But a man didn't need to ask questions when the answers walked up his gangplank wearing their fear like cheap coats.

The pilot—Yarrow, though Brannoch hadn't learned that name until later, and even now held it loosely, the way you hold a fish you intend to throw back—had come aboard first, all sharp angles and watchfulness, counting his coins out with hands that wanted to be flying somewhere else. Behind him the woman, Ilse, quiet as ballast, her eyes doing the pilot's watching for him while he did the paying. They'd bought the boat off him—the *Charity*, God help them, named by a previous owner with a sense of humor as black as the river bottom—and they'd bought fuel and tar and a bilge pump that Brannoch had personally assured them was nearly new, which was true if you counted the year it spent half-submerged in his hold as nearly new.

And when the pilot had counted out the last coin, Brannoch had seen it. Not in their faces—they were both better liars than most—but in the way they stood relative to each other. Two people guarding the same thing will always stand a certain way, angled inward, shoulders making a wall between the thing and the world. Forty years on the river teaches you geometry like that. People think traders sell goods. Traders sell nothing of the kind. Traders sell the reading of other people, and Brannoch had always been the best reader between Two Sisters and the sea.

They were carrying something worth killing for. And they were carrying it badly.

* * *

Now, six days later, they came back.

Brannoch heard the *Charity* before he saw her, which was fitting, since she announced herself to the whole river the same way—a cough in the engine, a list to port that no amount of tar seemed to shame into hiding, and a wake that spread behind her like a confession. She came around the drowned island at half speed, riding low, and Brannoch set down the net he was mending and watched her come with his good eye and felt the old arithmetic start turning in him, the one that weighed every kindness against every coin.

They tied up without asking. That told him something too. A man with nothing to hide asks permission to tie up; a man running asks forgiveness after.

"Your pump died," Yarrow said, before hello, before anything. He came up the gangplank with riverwater dried white on his boots and a look on his face like a man who'd been arguing with God and losing on points. "Second time. I've been bailing since Two Sisters."

"I sold you a boat," Brannoch said, "not a miracle."

"You sold me a coffin with delusions."

Brannoch grinned, or made the shape of one; his face had stiffened years ago into expressions that were approximations at best. "The *Charity* has carried worse men than you to worse places. Usually floating. Come in, sit, drink something that'll put hair on whatever you've got. You look like a drowned cat that lost the argument with the drowning."

Ilse came up last, and Brannoch watched her the way he watched weather. She moved differently than she had six days ago. Something had happened between here and Two Sisters—something that had taken the suspicion out of her spine and put a kind of grim partnership there instead. She and the pilot stood close now. Not lovers-close. Shipwreck-close. The closeness of two people who have bailed the same water through the same night and come out the other side knowing each other's hands.

She sat. She didn't drink. She looked at him with those pale eyes and said, "You knew what we were carrying."

Not a question. Brannoch poured himself a finger of the plum brandy he saved for occasions and lies, and drank it slow, and let the silence do his talking for him, because silence was the cheapest commodity on the river and he'd cornered the market.

"The chest," she said.

"There it is," Brannoch said. He set the glass down. "Six days ago you sat where she's sitting, and you bought my worst boat with money you counted twice, and you never once asked what I knew about the channel below Two Sisters, which any fool buying a boat should ask, because the channel below Two Sisters eats boats the way I eat breakfast. You weren't shopping. You were fleeing. And people fleeing don't carry luggage worth counting twice over unless the luggage is the cargo." He tapped the deck with one boot. "And you stood like I said. Angled in. Guarding."

Yarrow's jaw worked. "If you knew, why sell us the boat at all?"

"Because you paid," Brannoch said, "and because I don't ask." He leaned back against the rail and let the river fill the pause—the slap of water against hull, the creak of the lashed barges, far off the cry of some bird that lived out in the reeds and sounded perpetually offended. "But I'll tell you what I know anyway, since you've come all this way to hear it. Three days after you left, a man came up my gangplank wearing city boots and a river face, which is to say he dressed poor but stood rich. Offered me gold for word of a chest pulled from the flooded channels above the Marrow. Gold, mind—not silver, not script. Gold. For *word*. Not the chest itself. Just word of where it was." He picked up the brandy again, considered it, put it down untouched. "I told him I hadn't seen a chest. Which was true. I'd seen two liars and a bad boat. But I knew what he was asking about, and so did he, and we both knew I knew, and he left anyway, and I've been sitting here six days doing sums."

"What kind of sums?" Yarrow asked.

"The kind where I add up what happens to an old man who knows too much and keeps it in a head attached to a body anchored in one place." Brannoch let that sit. "You can't run, friend. That's your problem. I can't stay. That's mine. You're carrying the most dangerous cargo on this river and you're carrying it in a boat that takes on water when it rains, and the rain—" he nodded upward, at the sky that had been bruising purple all afternoon, the monsoon gathering itself out beyond the delta like a debt coming due, "—the rain is coming early this year, and heavy, and every soul who wants what you've got knows that when the monsoon comes, the checkpoints thin out and the river gets fast and stupid, and everything moves downriver at once."

"So you want to sell us another boat," Ilse said flatly.

"No," Brannoch said. "Boats are the problem. Boats are just holes you pour money into. I'm offering to sell you the hole *and* the man who knows where all the other holes are."

* * *

He laid it out for them the way he laid out goods, item by item, because he'd learned long ago that honesty delivered in installments went down easier than honesty delivered all at once.

Item: he knew the Rivergate below Two Sisters the way a man knows his own mouth—every sandbar that moved, every channel that lied, every stretch of water where the cartel boys ran their patrols and every gap in the patrols that opened and closed like a fish's gills. Item: he had papers, real ones, old ones, trader's writs signed by officials whose grandchildren now held office, papers that would float him past a checkpoint where a leaky boat full of fugitives would sink on sight. Item: he had a name that opened doors, or at least didn't slam them—the name of a neutral old trader whom everyone owed and no one trusted, which was the safest reputation a man could hold on this river. Item: he had a post, these three barges, which could be cut loose and abandoned in an hour, because everything he truly owned he'd owned for forty years and it all fit in a sea chest and a bad attitude.

"And in exchange," Yarrow said slowly, "you want—"

"A share," Brannoch said. "When your chest does whatever chests like that do—when the hearings happen, when the names come out, when somebody finally pays for forty years of stolen bread—I want a share. Small. One part in ten. Paid however it gets paid, coin or credit or a pardon, I'm not particular. I'm sixty-one years old and I've spent my whole life selling other people's cargoes at a markup. I'd like, once, before the river takes me, to own a piece of something that matters." He heard himself say it and was surprised by it, the way you're surprised by your own reflection in a shop window—you knew the face was there, but seeing it is another matter. He covered it with a shrug. "Also I want out. Downriver is south. South is away from the man with the gold and the city boots. A share and an exit. That's my price."

"That's not all," Ilse said.

Brannoch looked at her. Her eyes hadn't moved from his face the whole time. She had the stillness of a heron, that woman, the terrible patience of something that stands in cold water all day waiting for one moment of truth.

"No," he admitted. "That's not all."

"Say it," she said. "All of it. We've been lied to enough lately. By Corven's people, by the river, by this boat. I won't take a partner who rations his honesty."

The wind was rising. The tarp overhead snapped and settled, snapped and settled, like something trying to get free. Out past the drowned island the water had gone the color of gunmetal, and the first fat drops were coming down sparse and deliberate, each one landing with its own small report, scouts ahead of the army.

"All right," Brannoch said. "Here's the rest of it, then, and you can weigh it however you like. I know what's in that chest. Not page by page—I'm no clerk—but I know the shape of it. Vessany seal. Provision ledgers. I was a young man when Vessany was erased. I ran cargo up there. I saw the schools go up, the clinics, the granaries. I saw what that fund built, and I saw who got rich standing next to it, and when the maps got redrawn I understood that the redrawing wasn't geography, it was bookkeeping." He turned the brandy glass in his fingers, watching the lamplight swim in it. "My father drowned at Kell's Reach hauling grain for the Provision routes. Officially he was compensated. Officially we received a settlement. I was nine years old. My mother used that settlement money to buy flour during the famine year, at famine prices, from a factor whose name is probably written in your chest. So." He looked up, and let them see, for one unguarded second, the whole ruined landscape of the thing. "One part in ten, and an exit, and a promise. That's the price."

"What promise?" Yarrow asked quietly.

"That when it's done—when the names are read out loud in Rivergate, in daylight, on the record—that somebody says my father's name. Aldous Brannoch. Deckhand, Kell's Reach route, drowned in the service of a fund that was already being robbed while it buried him." Brannoch's voice came out level, which cost him more than shouting would have. "I don't need justice. Justice is for people with lawyers. I need it *said*. Once. In a room where saying it costs something."

The rain found its rhythm then, coming down proper, drumming on the tarp, silvering the river, erasing the far bank entirely. The three of them sat in the lamplight inside the sound of it, and Brannoch watched the two of them do their silent arithmetic—he was getting good at reading these two—and waited, because patience was the one thing the river had taught him that couldn't be bought or stolen.

It was Yarrow who spoke first, which surprised him. The pilot had struck him as a man who kept his yes locked up tight.

"One part in ten," Yarrow said. "Paid after the hearings, however it comes. Your exit south. And the name gets said—I'll say it myself, on the record, if it comes to that." He paused, and Brannoch saw the cost of the next words arrive in his face before they left his mouth. "But I'm not promising you a happy ending, old man. I'm promising you a seat in the boat. There's a difference, and you've been on this river long enough to know it."

"Oh, I know it," Brannoch said. "I've been selling seats in boats for forty years. Nobody's ever come back to complain, which either means they were satisfied or the river ate the complaints." He stuck out his hand, and Yarrow took it, and the grip was dry and hard and honest, and Brannoch held it a half-second longer than a bargain strictly required, because some contracts you seal with spit and some you seal with time.

Ilse shook too, last, and her hand was cold from the river air, and her eyes said *I'm watching you*, and Brannoch nodded slightly, because that was fair, and correct, and the only sane way to enter into anything on the Rivergate.

"Now," he said, standing, joints cracking like green firewood, "we've got work. The *Charity* wouldn't survive the monsoon crossing if Jesus himself bailed her, so tonight we strip her, re-caulk her, and transfer what matters to my barge—which, before you ask, leaks less and runs uglier, which is the trade I make everywhere in life. And you—" he pointed a thick finger at Yarrow, "—you tell me everything that's happened since Two Sisters, every checkpoint, every boat that passed you, every face you didn't like. Because if the man with the gold found me, others found you, and I want to know what's following us downriver before it introduces itself."

Yarrow and Ilse exchanged a look—the shipwreck-close look, the one that passed whole paragraphs without a word—and then Yarrow said, "There was a patrol boat at the Two Sisters checkpoint. It let us through too easily."

Brannoch stopped halfway to the hatch.

"Too easily," he repeated.

"Let us through, waved us on, barely looked at the papers. It felt wrong. It's been bothering me for two days."

Outside, the rain thickened until the world beyond the rail was nothing but sound and gray motion, and Brannoch stood a moment in the lamplight feeling the old cold walk up his spine, the one that had kept him alive through forty years of cargoes he never asked about. Being let through easily was the river's oldest trick. The net doesn't close when you see it coming. The net closes when you're grateful for the open water.

"Well then," Brannoch said, reaching for the lamp to turn it down. "Best we get moving before whoever's being so polite about it changes their manners."
