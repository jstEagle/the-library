## 12. Enforcer

The checkpoint announced itself the way trouble always does on the Rivergate: quietly, and then all at once.

We'd been on the water four days since Two Sisters, since the monsoon had taught us the boat's limits and ours, and I'd begun to believe — foolishly, the way a man begins to believe anything after four days of dry socks — that we might actually slip downriver unnoticed. The *Maren's Grace*, as Brannoch insisted on calling the rotten tub we'd bought, was making a grudging six knots against the current's mood. The rain had thinned to a gray drizzle that softened the banks into watercolor. Ilse was below with the ledgers, transcribing by lamplight. Brannoch stood at the wheel with his one eye narrowed at the channel ahead, chewing on a stem of something bitter he'd pulled from the reeds.

Then he spat the stem out and said, without any particular urgency, "Checkpoint. Two boats. That's not a toll stop."

I saw it a moment later. The river bent east around a bluff thick with bamboo, and where the channel narrowed, two patrol boats sat anchored across the current like a gate — dark-hulled, government-gray, each with a machine gun under a tarp that fooled no one. A signal lamp blinked at us in the drizzle. Three flashes, pause, three flashes.

*Heave to.*

"Could be customs," I said, knowing it wasn't.

"Could be," Brannoch agreed, in the tone of a man humoring a child. "Could be the weather's dry in the interior, too."

He brought us alongside the nearer patrol boat with a skill that surprised me — the *Maren's Grace* handled like a drunk ox, but Brannoch coaxed her in as gently as a ferryman. A ladder came down. Two soldiers in the mottled green of the interior constabulary stood at the rail, rifles slung, faces bored in the way that meant they were anything but. Behind them, at the rail's end, stood a woman in a long oilskin coat, hood back, hair plastered dark to her skull by the rain.

I knew her before I knew her name. Some people carry their profession in their posture, and she carried hers the way a blade carries its edge — not displayed, just there, in the stillness of her hands and the way her eyes went from me to Brannoch to the hatch and back again, cataloguing.

"You Yarrow?" she said. Her voice was low and even, pitched to carry over water without effort. "The pilot."

"I'm the pilot," I said, which was true, and not an answer.

"Sable." She didn't offer a hand. "Magistrate Corven's office. You'll come aboard the *Vigilant*. Your boat will be inspected."

"Inspected for what? I'm carrying rice, fuel drums, and a woman who snores."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, filed away for later use. "You're carrying a chest stamped with a seal that hasn't been legal in forty years. Come aboard, Yarrow. The soldiers can stay here and keep your crew company. Nobody needs to get wet who doesn't have to."

I looked at Brannoch. He gave me the smallest shrug, which from Brannoch was a full paragraph: *We're not outrunning two gunboats in this tub, so go learn what they know.*

I went up the ladder. The rain ran down my collar the whole way.

* * *

The cabin of the *Vigilant* smelled of diesel, wet wool, and gun oil. Sable poured tea from a dented thermos into two tin cups and pushed one across the chart table at me, and I noticed she poured it before I could refuse, which is a technique, and noticed that I noticed, which is a problem. She was perhaps forty, with a face that would have been ordinary except for the eyes — pale gray, nearly colorless, set deep, the eyes of someone who spent her working life watching other people's faces for the thing they didn't say.

"Drink," she said. "It's not poisoned. If Corven wanted you dead, you'd have been dead at Two Sisters, and I wouldn't be drinking bad tea in the rain talking to a bush pilot about cargo."

"Then what does he want?"

She sat across from me, cup in both hands, and studied me for a long moment. "Do you know how many people work for the Magistracy of the Interior?" she asked. "Counting clerks, constables, pilots, informants, and men like me?"

"I couldn't guess."

"Neither can I. That's the answer. Nobody can. It's the point of it." She drank. "Corven built it that way. Forty years ago the Provision built the towns — schools, clinics, grain stores, the whole spine of the river. Then Vessany fell, and the money kept flowing, and somebody had to administer it. Somebody had to be the hand that reached into every town and touched every storehouse and knew every ledger. The Magistracy is that hand. It's the largest organization on this river, and it exists to do one thing: make sure the money goes where it goes."

"And where it goes is into the pockets of the families who run it."

I said it to see what she'd do. She did nothing. That frightened me more than anger would have.

"I'm not here to argue history," she said. "I'm here because a chest came out of the flooded channel at Kessel's Bend three weeks ago, hauled up by a pilot who didn't report it, and because the Magistrate has spent forty years making sure that nothing from Vessany ever surfaces again — not because he's ashamed, Yarrow. Because it's *complicated*. Because half the river's stability sits on top of that chest, and if you pull it out, everything built on it shifts. Towns. Families. The peace."

"The peace." I tasted the tea. It was terrible. "You mean the silence."

"I mean the peace." She set the cup down. "Here's what happens now. The Magistrate requests — requests — that the chest be surrendered at Rivergate, to his office, through proper channels. In exchange, certain matters are set aside. There are questions about your license. There's the matter of the fuel you've been buying on credit from a trader in Marrow's Landing who has since discovered irregularities in his books. There's the question of your passenger, who entered the interior on papers that were expired before she was born."

The cabin felt smaller. "You know about Ilse."

"I know about everyone. That's the job." She said it without pride, the way a man says *I dig ditches*. "The Magistrate is offering you a clean road, Yarrow. No charges. No questions. The chest comes to Rivergate, it goes into the annex, it gets catalogued and sealed, and in twenty years some archivist opens it and finds the interesting pages already gone, and no one living remembers why. That's not a threat. That's how it's done. That's how it's *always* been done. The alternative—" she paused, and I watched her decide how much to give me, "—the alternative is that you keep going downriver with it, and other people who want that chest, people who are *not* the Magistrate, keep coming for it. And they are not me. I follow rules. I have rules. They have a budget."

"Is that the offer, then? Surrender the chest at Rivergate?"

"That's the request." She rose, and so did I, my knees stiff from the wet cold. "You have until the hearings convene. The Magistrate is a patient man. He pulled you out of a wreck once, didn't he?"

The question landed like a hand on the back of my neck. "He told you that."

"He tells me everything that matters." She walked me to the cabin door, and in the gray light her colorless eyes held something I couldn't name — not kindness, but its distant cousin, the thing that lives in people who've done hard work for a hard master and haven't yet stopped asking themselves why. "Yarrow. One thing, off the record, because you flew medicine into Ferrow during the fever year and my mother was a nurse there and I don't forget that kind of thing."

"Off the record," I said.

"When I let you go — and I am going to let you go — you'll want to believe it was a mistake. A lapse. That you got lucky." She opened the door onto the rain. "Don't. Nothing I do is a mistake. Think about what it means when the man hunting you lets you keep running."

* * *

They let us through.

That was the whole of it. The soldiers waved the *Maren's Grace* past the second patrol boat without so much as stepping aboard. The machine guns stayed under their tarps. The signal lamp blinked twice — *proceed* — and the gate of gray hulls swung open like a held breath released, and we were through, and the river took us back.

For an hour, nobody on the boat said much. Brannoch steered. Ilse came up from below, saw our faces, and didn't ask. The drizzle thickened, then thinned. The bluff fell away behind us and the channel widened into drowned grassland where egrets stood in the shallows like patient clerks.

It was Ilse who finally broke it. "They searched nothing," she said. "They didn't even look in the hold."

"They didn't need to," Brannoch said. "They knew what's in the hold before we bought the boat."

"They knew the chest came out of Kessel's Bend," I said. "They knew about the fuel debts. They knew your papers, Ilse. Your *expired* papers." I turned the tin cup Sable had sent back with me — a small courtesy, or a small message — around in my hands. "There are maybe six people on this river who knew I pulled that chest up. Me. You. Brannoch. The trader who sold us the boat. Whoever paid for word of it at the last port. And Corven."

"And everyone those people told," Ilse said.

"Which is the point." I set the cup down. "Don't you see it? If Corven wanted the chest, that checkpoint was the moment. Two gunboats against a leaking scow. He could have taken it, sunk us, called it banditry on the water, and no one would ever have asked a question. Instead his enforcer pours me tea and gives me until the hearings. She *warned* me — told me there are others coming, told me they're worse than her. Why would you do that? Why would you show your quarry the teeth behind you?"

"Because fear moves people faster than force," Ilse said quietly. "Force makes them hide. Fear makes them run — and running things go where you can see them."

Brannoch grunted from the wheel. "Or," he said, "because the Magistrate doesn't want the chest. He wants to know where it goes. A chest in a hole is a chest in a hole. A chest that keeps moving downriver leads him to everyone who wants it. Every buyer. Every hand it passes through. Every name on every list that's been waiting forty years for that paper to surface." His one eye found me in the gray light. "You're not carrying cargo anymore, pilot. You're carrying bait. And you're swimming exactly the way the hook wants you to."

The rain picked up again, soft and steady, needling the river into a million small rings. I went to the stern and stood there a while, watching our wake unspool behind us — a long white seam stitching shut behind the boat, closing over everything, so that the water ahead and the water behind looked identical, and there was no way to tell, from the surface, which direction we'd actually come from.

That was what Sable had done. That was what Corven had done, forty years ago, with the Provision itself: let the evidence move, let the current carry it, and stitch the surface shut behind it. Nothing stopped. Nothing surfaced. Everything continued.

Except that Sable had broken the pattern, hadn't she? She'd *told* me. Off the record. Because of a fever year and a nurse and a medicine run — a debt I never knew I'd paid. An enforcer who kept a private ledger of kindnesses, hidden inside the Magistracy's great sealed book. People were not their offices. Corven had taught me that once, pulling me out of the wreck. Maybe Sable was teaching me the other half of it now: that the hand of a hard master can still be a human hand, and that a human hand can open.

Or maybe that was exactly what I was meant to believe. Maybe the mercy was the message. Maybe somewhere behind us, in the gray rain, the *Vigilant* was making a slow, patient six knots of its own, and Sable was logging our heading in a book I'd never see.

I went below. Ilse had the ledgers spread across the bunk, her transcription pages weighted down with a tin plate, the lamp turned low. She glanced up when I came in, and I saw her hand move — just slightly — to cover the topmost page. Not hiding it from me. Hiding it from the habit of hiding. I'd seen the gesture before, in people who'd grown up in houses where drawers were searched.

"They'll come again," I said. "Not Sable. The others she mentioned. The ones with a budget."

"I know."

"And when they come, they won't pour tea."

"I know that too." She looked at the ledgers, at the forty years of names and figures that her grandmother had carried out of a drowning country page by page. "Oscar. If it comes to it — if we have to choose what to save and what to burn — the chest isn't the thing. The pages are the thing. Remember that."

"I remember."

She went back to her transcription. I climbed up on deck, took the wheel from Brannoch, and pointed the *Maren's Grace* downriver into the rain, toward a bend I couldn't see yet, feeling the whole gray weight of the river behind us — following, patient, letting us run.

It was Brannoch, an hour later, who spotted it first: a thread of black smoke rising off the water ahead, past the next bend, where no chimney should be. He lowered his glass slowly, and his face had gone the color of the sky.

"That's not a checkpoint," he said. "That's a wreck. And it's not one of ours."
