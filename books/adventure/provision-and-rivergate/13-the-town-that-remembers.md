## 13. The Town That Remembers

The village announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke and wet clay, the green rot of river reeds, and beneath it all something sweeter, like sugarcane pressed too long in the sun. Ilse stood at the rail while Brannoch eased the boat toward a landing of blackened pilings, and she thought: *I know this place. I have never been here, but I know this place.*

It was called Serrano's Bend on Brannoch's water-stained chart, though the man himself admitted he hadn't stopped there in years. "Nothing to trade but fish and memories," he'd said, spitting over the side. But the bilge pump needed a day to dry out, the boat needed caulking they couldn't buy anywhere bigger, and Oscar had argued for the stop with that particular flatness that meant it wasn't an argument at all.

So they tied up under a sky the color of unpolished tin, and Ilse climbed the muddy stair cut into the bank with the chest's weight riding against her ribs—not the chest itself, which stayed hidden below deck beneath a false floor of fish crates, but its contents, or her memory of them. She had transcribed pages every night since the fire, hunched over a candle stub with the boat rocking under her, copying columns of names and figures into a school exercise book until her eyes burned. The numbers lived in her now. She could recite them the way other people recited prayers.

*Provision disbursement, District Nine, Year Twelve: 40,000 marks. Received by: Halloway, Voss, Adeyemi, Corven.*

She had thought those were just names. Ink on paper, signatures on theft. She had not expected to walk into a town where the names still had faces.

* * *

Serrano's Bend was built the way Provision towns were built—that much was obvious even to someone who'd grown up far from any river. The buildings stood in neat rows along a raised levee street, their foundations poured concrete instead of pilings, their roofs pitched to shed monsoon rain. There was a clinic with a faded red cross painted on its wall, the paint gone chalky with salt. A schoolhouse with tall windows, half its glass replaced by boards. A grain store whose great doors still bore stenciled letters, worn almost to ghosts:

**PROVISION OF THE RIVER PEOPLES — BUILT TO LAST**

Built to last. Ilse ran her fingers along the stencil and felt the irony settle into her bones like damp. It *had* lasted. Forty years, and the walls still stood. Only the promise inside them had rotted.

The money that raised these buildings—the forty thousand marks, year after year, district after district—had come from a fund meant to feed people through the famine years. And then the fund's own architects had begun to skim, and skim, and skim, diverting grain money into private accounts, into land holdings, into the families who now governed the interior from behind respectable desks. Her grandmother had copied the ledgers by lamplight in Vessany's last winter, smuggling them out page by page sewn into a coat lining, and had spent the rest of her life telling anyone who would listen—and mostly no one listened—that the miracle on the river was built on a theft.

Ilse had inherited the obsession the way other children inherited eye color.

A woman was watching her from the clinic steps. Old, small, wrapped in a shawl the color of dried chilies, with hands folded over a cane as if the cane might bolt if left unsupervised. She had the stillness of someone who had learned that moving too fast invited trouble.

"You're not traders," the woman said. Not a question. Her voice was dry as August.

"We're travelers," Ilse said. "Our boat needs work."

"Boats always need work." The woman's eyes went past her, down the levee, to where Oscar stood talking to a knot of children near the landing, showing them something—a coin trick, probably, or a knot. Keeping them charmed and at a distance, the way Oscar did everything. "That one moves like a pilot. We used to get pilots here, before."

"Before what?"

The woman looked at her for a long moment. "Before the clinic stopped being a clinic." She unfolded herself from the step with the care of someone negotiating with her own joints. "Come inside. If you're going to lie to me, you can do it sitting down, and I'll have tea while you do it."

* * *

The clinic's interior smelled of carbolic soap and old paper. Rows of benches, a dispensary cabinet with two vials left in it, a child's height chart penciled on a doorframe with dates going back decades—*Marta, 1971; Marta, 1972; Marta, 1973*—and then nothing. The last entry stopped the same year the entries in the chest's ledgers turned fraudulent.

The woman made tea on a spirit stove, moving with the economy of long practice, and introduced herself as Odile Ferran—"no relation to anyone you've heard of"—before settling onto a bench across from Ilse with her cane laid across her knees like a bar.

"I was the clerk here," she said. "Thirty-one years. I took the census, kept the vaccination rolls, recorded births and deaths, and once a year I signed the Provision receipt confirming the district received its full allocation." She sipped her tea. "I signed it every year for thirty-one years. Do you know what I actually received?"

Ilse's mouth had gone dry. "Less than the receipt said."

"A third," the old woman said. "Sometimes a quarter. Grain shorted, medicine substituted with chalk and dyed water, building materials 'delayed' until the monsoon ate the budget and everyone shrugged. And every year I wrote to the Provision office in Rivergate asking why, and every year a letter came back saying the matter had been reviewed and found satisfactory, signed by some undersecretary whose name changed every few years but whose handwriting never did." She set down her cup with a small, precise click. "You learn things, keeping records. You learn that the same hand writes many names."

Ilse's heart was hammering. Slowly, carefully, she drew the exercise book from her bag—the transcription, not the original, never the original—and laid it on the bench between them.

"I have some documents," she said. "Copies of ledgers. From the Provision itself. From before."

Odile Ferran did not reach for the book. She looked at it the way one looks at a snake one has suspected in the grass for thirty years and finally sees coiled in plain view.

"May I?" she asked, and her voice had changed—smaller now, younger somehow, the voice of the clerk she had been before the clerk became the survivor.

Ilse opened it to the disbursement pages. "District Nine. Years ten through fourteen."

The old woman put on spectacles that hung from a ribbon around her neck, and read.

Ilse watched her face do something terrible and quiet. It wasn't shock—shock is for surprises, and this woman had suspected for three decades. It was *confirmation*. It was the moment a person who has carried a suspicion alone so long it has fused to their spine finally sets it down and finds it real. Her lips moved over the names. Her thumb trembled against the page.

"Halloway," she whispered. "Voss. Adeyemi." A pause, a breath drawn through teeth. "Corven."

She said the last name differently. Softer. With something underneath it that Ilse couldn't name yet.

"He came here," Odile said. "Early on. Before my time as clerk—I was a girl. He was young then, the magistrate's protégé, up from Rivergate with his ledgers and his fine boots. He walked this levee street when the concrete was still curing and he put his hand on the clinic wall and he told us—" her breath caught, steadied— "he told us the river peoples would never be hungry again. He said the Provision was a covenant. His words. *A covenant.*" She laughed, and it was the worst sound Ilse had heard in years, dry and cracked as the floodplain in drought. "And every year after, I signed his receipts for a third of what he promised, and I told myself the shortfall was clerical error, distance, weather, anything but what it was. Because he had *said*. Because we had *believed*."

Tears came then—not dramatically, not sobbing, but steadily, the way rain comes to the monsoon coast, without announcement and without end. They ran down the deep lines of her face and dropped onto the exercise book, onto the columns of figures, and Ilse did not move to save the pages because some things are worth more than ink.

"They starved," Odile said. "Not all at once—starving is slow, it's patient, it picks off the weak first. My brother. Two of the Adeyemi children down the street—no relation to *that* Adeyemi, God forbid, the ones who got fat on our grain. The Osei family lost four in the bad winter. And every year the receipts said *received in full*, and every year I signed, because what else does a clerk do? What else does anyone do?" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears, at the years, at the arithmetic of it. "Forty thousand marks a year. For thirty-one years. Do you know how many lives that is? How many children's fevers broken, how many granaries filled? It's not money. It's *people*. It's my brother, multiplied by every district on this river, and they spent it on—" 

She stopped. Looked at the book again. At the name.

"Corven," she said again, and now Ilse heard what lay beneath it. Not hatred. Something worse. Grief. "He saved my mother's life once, during the fever year. Carried her himself to this clinic, six miles, when the roads were flooded. Everyone loved him here. Everyone *still* loves him—they hang his portrait in the schoolhouse, they toast him at festivals. Magistrate Corven, father of the river towns." Her jaw tightened. "If you take this to Rivergate, they will burn you for it before they burn him. Not because it isn't true. Because it *is* true, and the truth about him is the truth about everyone who believed in him, and no one forgives a mirror."

* * *

Oscar found them there an hour later, when the light through the tall windows had gone amber and the tea had gone cold. Ilse heard the footsteps on the clinic's concrete floor—Oscar walked quietly for a big person, but concrete gave them away—and looked up to see that careful face taking in the scene: the old woman, the open book, the salt tracks on both their cheeks.

Oscar didn't ask. That was the thing about Oscar; Ilse was learning it day by day, the way you learn a river by traveling it. Oscar read a room the way a pilot reads weather, and whatever was read there made them simply sit down on the bench beside Odile Ferran and say nothing at all, which was, Ilse suspected, exactly right.

It was Odile who broke the silence.

"You're the pilot," she said. "Yarrow. The one who flies the medicine runs. We used to watch your plane go over—my neighbor's boy would stand on the levee and shout your registration number like a prayer." She studied Oscar's face. "You knew him. Corven. Everyone says the magistrate pulled you out of a wreck yourself."

Something crossed Oscar's expression—quick, shuttered, gone. "He did."

"And you're carrying those papers anyway." It wasn't a question either. This woman had spent thirty-one years reading documents and the people attached to them.

"We're deciding," Ilse said, before Oscar could answer. It was more honest than anything she'd planned to say. "We're still deciding."

Odile Ferran closed the exercise book with both hands, gently, the way one closes a coffin lid, and pushed it back across the bench.

"Then let me tell you what deciding costs," she said. "In the fever year, I had a chance to report the shortfalls. A river inspector came through, honest man, asked questions. All I had to do was show him my receipts next to my actual stores. One afternoon's courage." Her hands folded over the cane again. "I didn't. My brother died the following winter. I have done that arithmetic every night for thirty years, and I will tell you what I've learned: the cost of silence doesn't come due all at once. It comes due forever, in installments, and the interest is *everything you might have saved*."

She rose, joints cracking, and stood over them both—an old woman in a dying clinic in a town built on a broken covenant, straight-backed as the concrete pillars that held up its roof.

"Take it to Rivergate," she said. "Take it even if it kills you. Take it especially if it kills you. Because I am tired, and my town is tired, and we cannot carry the knowing alone anymore."

Outside, the first drops of evening rain began to strike the clinic's tin roof, soft as fingertips, then harder, drumming, insistent. Through the tall windows Ilse watched Oscar help the old woman down the steps, offering an arm she accepted with the dignity of a queen, and beyond them, down the levee street, the lamps of Serrano's Bend were coming on one by one in windows that stolen money had framed and hunger had hollowed.

Brannoch appeared at the clinic door, shaking rain from his shoulders, and jerked his thumb toward the landing. "Weather's turning ugly," he said. "We should push off before dark."

But it was Oscar who hesitated, looking back up the street—at the schoolhouse, the grain store, the portrait of Corven that Ilse knew must hang inside it—and when Oscar spoke, the voice was low, meant only for her.

"There's a checkpoint at Two Sisters," Oscar said. "Brannoch mentioned it. Cartel water. If we're running those papers to Rivergate, we run them through it." A pause, rain loud on the roof between them. "And Ilse—someone paid for word of this chest back at Marlow's Landing. Word travels faster than boats. Whatever's waiting at Two Sisters, it already knows we're coming."

Ilse tucked the exercise book inside her coat, against her heart, where her grandmother's coat-lining secrets had once ridden, and nodded once.

"Then we'd better be worth the wait," she said.
