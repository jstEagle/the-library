## 14. Corven's Letter

The rain had settled into its long argument with the river by the time we tied up at Ferrow's Landing, and I was arguing back—mostly with the boat.

She was called the *Merry Adela* in some former life, before rot took her nameplate and Brannoch took her off a debtor's hands for less than the price of a good dog. Her planks wept at every seam. Her bilge pump worked on a philosophy of intermittent mercy. Every hour on her was an hour spent listening to the water come in and deciding, again, not to panic about it.

"You talk to her like she can hear you," Ilse said from under the tarp we'd rigged over the chest. She'd taken to sitting with it the way other people sat with sleeping children—one hand resting on the lid, not guarding it so much as keeping it company.

"She can hear me. That's the problem. Boats always hear exactly what you're afraid of."

Brannoch snorted from the stern, where he was doing something violent and inexplicable to the outboard with a wrench and a length of rubber hose. "That one hears nothing but her own complaints. Like most women of her age." He ducked the rag Ilse threw at him without looking up, which suggested he'd been dodging thrown objects his whole life.

We had been three days out from the village where the old woman had wept over the ledger names, and the river had gone quiet around us in that way rivers do when they've decided to mind their own business. The monsoon hadn't broken yet, but you could feel it gathering somewhere north like a debt collector who knows where you live. We were moving downriver in daylight only, anchoring in the reeds at dusk, burning no lights, buying fuel in small amounts from small traders who asked no questions because Brannoch paid them in a currency older than money—recognition, a shared squint, a name dropped just loudly enough.

I should have felt safer. Instead I kept catching myself counting the boats behind us.

* * *

Ferrow's Landing wasn't much—a fuel dock, a drying shed, a cantina built from three wrecks and a prayer, run by a woman named Odji who had hands like tree roots and a memory for faces that made her either the best or worst person to know, depending on what your face was doing that week. Pilots came through. Traders came through. Once, years ago, I'd come through with a cargo of vaccines sweating through their ice and a cracked windscreen and no idea how I was going to pay for the repair, and Odji had fed me anyway and never mentioned it since.

I was checking the tie-offs when I heard the engine.

You know engines the way you know voices. This one was a Lycoming, tired but honest, running slightly rich on the left cylinder—the sound of a man who'd been told about the cylinder and had decided the river would decide. I turned and watched a battered high-wing floatplane drop out of the low cloud, sideslip over the drying shed with more confidence than skill, and settle onto the brown water in a long, flat smear of spray.

Denny Achebe climbed out onto the dock like a man dismounting a horse he didn't entirely trust. He was sixty if he was a day, built like a rain barrel, wearing a flight jacket older than both of us put together, and he had been flying the Rivergate longer than I'd been alive. He'd taught me my first crosswind landing. He'd also taught me, without ever saying it outright, that a pilot's word is the only cargo that has to arrive undamaged.

"Yarrow," he said.

"Denny."

He looked past me at the boat, at the tarp, at Ilse standing very still beside it, at Brannoch pretending total absorption in his engine. Then he looked back at me, and everything he wasn't saying filled the space between us like fog filling a valley.

"Came out of my way," he said. "Had freight for Two Sisters, but there's something I was asked to carry. Personally." He reached inside his jacket. "Man said you'd know whether to take it or burn it. His words."

My mouth had gone dry. "Who?"

"You know who." Denny held out the envelope. It was heavy paper, cream-colored, sealed with wax—which alone would have identified its sender to anyone who'd ever stood in a government office in the interior. Nobody else used wax. Wax was a statement. Wax said *I have time, and I have power, and I am not afraid of either.*

The seal was the magistrate's office. But the hand that had written my name across the front—*Oscar Yarrow, c/o any pilot bound downriver*—was one I knew better than my own signature. I'd seen it on my first flight license. I'd seen it on the chit that paid for my mentor's funeral. I'd seen it every year after, on the renewal papers that arrived without me ever applying, as if the interior's bureaucracy simply understood that Oscar Yarrow flew, the way it understood that the river ran downhill.

Corven's handwriting. Corven's wax. Corven's reach, stretching two hundred miles of flooded country to find a leaking boat and the woman standing guard over its cargo.

"You read it?" I asked Denny.

"I can't read," Denny said, which was a lie so old it had become a kind of courtesy between pilots. "And I wouldn't. Whatever's in there, it's between you and him." He shifted his weight. "Oscar. He pulled you out of the water once. Everybody knows the story. Just remember the river's pulled *me* out twice, and I still don't take orders from it."

He clapped my shoulder hard enough to mean something, walked back to his plane, and left the way he'd come—loudly, richly, and slightly sideways into the wind.

* * *

I didn't open the letter right away. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the river keeps: I carried it around for half a day first, because some part of me understood that once I broke that seal, I would be having a conversation with Magistrate Corven again—and conversations with Corven had a way of ending with him holding all the cards and you feeling grateful he'd let you play at all.

I opened it at dusk, in the reeds, alone. Ilse watched me go and didn't follow, which was its own kind of grace.

The letter was four paragraphs long. It was written in the same steady hand that had signed my license, and it began, of all things, with weather.

*Oscar.*

*I'm told the rains will be early this year. I'm told you're in a boat that leaks. Both of these things trouble me more than you might expect, given our circumstances.*

*You know what I did for you. You know what I gave up to do it—you were there; you saw the wreck; you know what the channel took from me that day along with you. I have never asked you for anything since. Not loyalty, not silence, not even thanks. I wanted you free of debts, so I made sure you had none. That was the point of everything I did for you afterward. Every license, every route, every year the paperwork renewed itself without your asking—that was me, paying down a debt so that you would owe me nothing and could therefore choose freely.*

*Now I am asking.*

*Return the chest to me, unopened in substance, and three things happen. First: every record of your past stays buried. You know which records I mean. The ones from before you were a pilot. The ones that would make interesting reading at certain hearings, in certain rooms, in front of certain people who believe they are owed an accounting of everyone's life but their own. Second: your debts vanish—not the ones I already erased, but the new ones. The ones you've acquired in the last month, traveling with people whose enemies have long memories and short fuses. Third: you go back to the air. No boat, no bilge, no monsoon. Your plane will be repaired within the week—I know what happened at Kessel's field; I am sorry for it; it was meant to delay you, not to strand you—and the Rivergate will be yours again, all of it, forever.*

*You have spent your whole life leaving places before they could become home. I am offering you the one thing you've never had: permission to stay.*

*Burn this letter if you refuse. No one will ever know it existed. That too is a promise.*

*C.*

I read it twice. Then I sat in the dark with the reeds talking their dry gossip around me and the chest twenty feet away under its tarp, breathing its slow wooden breath, and I let myself feel the full weight of what he was offering.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't come dressed as evil. It comes dressed as *relief*. It comes in a familiar voice, in a familiar hand, speaking the exact sentence you've been saying to yourself in the dark for twenty years. *Permission to stay.* As if staying were a thing that required permission—as if I hadn't built my entire life on the theory that staying was simply a failure of nerve, a softness, a way of letting a place get its hooks into you before you understood what it would cost to leave.

Corven knew that about me. Of course he did. He'd pulled me out of the water; he'd sat with me through the fever afterward; he'd heard me say things in delirium that I've never said sober. He knew that underneath the rules—never land twice, never learn names, never carry anything but cargo—there was just a kid from a drowned farmstead who'd learned young that attachment was the first thing the river takes.

And he was offering to make it all legitimate. To turn my exile into a home, retroactively, with paperwork. That was Corven's genius. He didn't threaten. He *arranged*. He made the world reorganize itself around your best interests until refusing him felt like ingratitude.

I thought about the records. My records. There weren't many—there never are, for people like me—but there were enough. Enough that if someone went looking with official authority and patient malice, they'd find the shape of a life I'd spent thirty years sanding smooth. And enough that if those pages surfaced during the hearings—if they surfaced next to Ilse's ledgers, next to testimony, next to my name on a witness list—they wouldn't just embarrass me. They'd discredit me. They'd make the chest look like a fugitive's fantasy instead of what it was.

He knew that too. The offer wasn't charity. It was a hook baited with my own history.

But God help me, sitting there in the reeds with the letter in my lap, I wanted it. Not the safety—I'd never cared much about safety. I wanted the *staying*. I wanted a hangar with my name on it. I wanted a town that expected me back. I wanted to stop being a rumor with a pilot's license.

I folded the letter along its original creases, very carefully, and walked back to the boat.

* * *

Ilse was awake. Of course she was. She sat cross-legged by the cold stove with the lantern turned low, and she read my face the way she read water—looking for the current underneath.

"He offered you something," she said.

"How do you know?"

"Because you look like a woman who's been handed a gift she can't unwrap in public." She nodded at the letter in my hand. "What was it?"

I could have lied. Two liars circling each other—we'd been doing it since the day she boarded, and we'd gotten reasonably good at it. But somewhere between the weeping village and the leaking bilge, the lying had started to feel like ballast we no longer needed.

"He offered to erase my past," I said. "All of it. The parts that would ruin me if they came out at the hearings. In exchange for the chest."

"And?"

"And a repaired plane. And permission to stop running."

Ilse was quiet for a long moment. The lantern flame leaned and straightened, leaned and straightened, keeping its own small time.

"My grandmother got a letter like that once," she said finally. "From a different magistrate, in a different year. Offering to let her family keep their shop if the ledgers stayed lost." She looked at me. "She sent the ledgers anyway. It cost her the shop, and then it cost her the country, because Vessany stopped existing forty years ago and everyone agrees it was nobody's fault." Her voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the frightening thing about Ilse—everything important she said came out level as still water. "I used to think she was brave. Now I think she just couldn't stand the arithmetic. Some things cost more to keep than to give away, and some things cost more to give away than to keep, and you don't get to know which is which until afterward."

"I'm not your grandmother."

"No. You're the woman Corven saved, being asked to save him back." She tilted her head. "Is that what it feels like? Because from out here it looks simpler. It looks like a man who stole from starving towns offering to buy the only witness who's seen the proof."

"It feels like both," I said. "That's the problem. It feels exactly like both."

She studied me for a while, and whatever she found seemed to satisfy her, or at least to be filed for later. "So what does the pilot do?"

I looked at the letter. Corven's handwriting, steady as a horizon line. *No one will ever know it existed.* Even his mercies came with confidentiality clauses.

"The pilot burns it," I said, "and keeps flying."

I fed it to the stove and lit it, and we watched Corven's careful sentences curl and blacken—the records, the plane, the permission to stay—all of it going up in a breath of orange light that smelled like cedar and power. The wax seal melted last, a slow silver bead, like something bleeding.

When it was ash, Ilse said, "Thank you," in a voice smaller than any I'd heard her use.

"Don't thank me yet. Refusing him doesn't end anything. It just tells him we can't be bought—which means the next thing he sends won't be a letter."

* * *

Brannoch appeared at the hatchway sometime in the gray hour before dawn, drawn by the smell of burnt paper or by the instinct that wakes old traders when the cargo starts making decisions. He looked at the stove, at the ash, at the two of us sitting silent in the lantern light, and asked no questions, which was his highest form of communication.

Instead he jerked his thumb toward the stern. "Come see what the river brought us."

Tangled in the reeds off our port side, bumping gently against the hull in the pre-dawn murk, was a patrol boat. Government hull, grey paint, the interior's crest stenciled on the bow. Empty. Drifting. Its windshield starred with cracks, its deck scorched in a way that fire on water shouldn't produce, and its radio antenna snapped off clean.

There was no one aboard. There was no body either. There was only a single boot caught on the tow cleat, and a scorch mark shaped like a hand on the rail, and downstream, far off in the dark, the sound of an engine that wasn't ours and wasn't Denny's, running without lights, going away.

Ilse stood beside me at the rail, and I felt her go rigid.

"That's a patrol boat," she said.

"That's Sable's patrol boat," I said, because I'd know that hull anywhere—it had shadowed us through two checkpoints, patient as a heron. "And Sable doesn't swim."

Brannoch spat thoughtfully over the side. "Then somebody out there," he said, "just sank the magistrate's own hound. And I'll tell you what, friends—Corven's men don't sink Corven's men."

The engine sound faded downriver. The reeds whispered. And the chest sat under its tarp between us, suddenly worth more than evidence—worth more than truth—worth, possibly, to whoever had done this, more than any of our lives.

"We're not being hunted anymore," Ilse said quietly. "We're being *herded*."

I looked at the empty patrol boat rocking against our hull, and for the first time since the chest came out of the water, I wished Corven's letter were still intact—because whatever was coming for us now, at least his offer had come with a return address.
