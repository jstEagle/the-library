## 15. Sable's Wreck

The river changed its mind about the weather twice before noon.

We had come down out of the hills overnight, riding the last of the storm-swollen current with Brannoch at the tiller and Ilse asleep sitting up, her back against the chest as though she could guard it even unconscious. I took the dawn watch because I no longer trusted myself to sleep. Sleep was where Corven lived now—Corven in his good coat, Corven holding out his hand over a table twenty years gone, saying *you'll do fine work for me, boy*, and meaning it then, and maybe meaning it still, and it not mattering either way.

The channel narrowed below the old ferry crossing. On both banks the jungle leaned out over the water like men reading over your shoulder.

"Smoke," Brannoch said.

I smelled it before I saw it—diesel and something sweeter underneath, varnish or paint, burning slow and wet. He cut the engine and we drifted, the boat wallowing in its own bad manners, and around the next bend we found what was left of Sable's patrol boat.

* * *

It lay on its side in the shallows where the channel split around a gravel bar, half sunk, black along the waterline, its cabin roof charred down to bare ribs. The name on the stern had burned away entirely, but I knew the hull. You spend enough years watching boats on this river and you learn them the way you learn faces—the rake of the bow, the set of the wheelhouse, the particular arrogance of a government vessel running without lights. This one had chased me once, two years ago, when I'd landed on a sandbar that turned out to be a customs staging ground. It had come out of the fog with its searchlight swinging and I had thought, *that is the most efficient machine I have ever seen.* That efficiency lay on its side now, breathing smoke through a hole in its flank you could have put a barrel through.

"That's not grounding damage," Brannoch said. He had not moved from the tiller. His one eye went over the wreck the way other men read scripture—slowly, and finding everything damning. "That's not fire from a fuel leak, neither. Look where it started."

I looked. The burn pattern told its story plainly enough: the fire had begun aft, near the engine housing, but the hole in the hull was forward of that, low, punched inward. Something had been driven into the boat below the waterline, and then something else had been used to make sure the job finished.

"Ilse," I said.

She was already awake. She came forward without a word and stood at the rail beside me, and I watched her face do the arithmetic. "Sable's boat," she said.

"You know it?"

"I know the class. Government interdiction hull, Rivergate yard." Her voice was flat, careful. "I don't know hers."

But she said *hers* the way I would have said it, and we both heard it.

Brannoch brought us in against the gravel bar with more gentleness than the old tub deserved, and we stepped off into water shin-deep and cold despite the season. Up close the wreck smelled worse—burnt oil, burnt paint, and under it the river's own smell of silt and drowned things. Gulls had already found it. They sat along the exposed keel like a jury that had reached its verdict and was only waiting for the sentencing.

The patrol boat carried a crew of four when it ran full. We found no bodies. That was the first thing I counted, standing in the shallows with the water pulling at my legs: no bodies, no blood in the scum line, no gear scattered the way it scatters when men go into the water fast. A life ring hung on the rail, scorched but intact. A pair of boots stood upright on the cabin deck, side by side, as if their owner had stepped out of them to wade ashore and simply never come back.

"Clean," Ilse said quietly. "Whoever did this let them go."

"Or took them."

She shook her head. "Took them, there'd be signs of a fight. There's nothing here but the fire and the hole." She crouched at the waterline, studying the breach, and touched the torn metal with two fingers. "Driven in. Not shot—a ram, or a pole with a weight on it. Someone came alongside in the dark and opened her up low, where the pumps couldn't keep pace. Then they burned her so she couldn't be traced." She looked up at me. "This wasn't rage. This was bookkeeping."

I didn't like how well she understood it. I didn't say so.

* * *

We searched the wreck because searching wrecks is a habit you can't break once the river has taught it to you. Brannoch took the engine compartment, whistling tunelessly through his teeth, cataloguing what the fire had left. Ilse worked the cabin. I took the wheelhouse, which was mostly a shell—glass gone soft and crazed, instruments melted into abstract sculpture—but the chart drawer beneath the console had been built of hardwood by someone who believed government property should outlast governments, and it had survived.

Inside were charts of the middle river, marked in pencil with patrol routes. And beneath them, wedged flat, a leather folder gone stiff with heat at one corner.

I should have left it. I want to be honest about that—I knew, standing there with the gulls screaming and the smoke still rising, that everything touching Sable's boat was going to be a door I couldn't close again. But I had pulled a sealed chest out of a flooded channel on the same instinct, and look where that had gotten me: hunted by the man who saved my life, grounded, monsoon-bound, afloat on a rotten hull with two people I was beginning, dangerously, to think of as mine.

I opened the folder.

Patrol logs. Dates, positions, vessels boarded, cargo inspected. Sable's handwriting—tight, slanted, economical, every letter doing exactly the work required of it and no more. I had seen that handwriting once before, on a citation pinned to my hangar door after the customs incident, informing me that charges were declined. *Declined,* not dismissed. Even her mercy had been filed correctly.

Most of the log was ordinary. Rice barges inspected. Fuel permits checked. One entry about net poachers above Two Sisters, resolved with a fine. Then, eleven days ago, an entry that stopped my breath:

*Boarded wreck site, flooded channel, grid 44-K. Recovered salvage marker buoy, cut loose. No vessel present. Reported to Magistrate's office per protocol. Response received: stand down, area closed pending survey.*

Stand down. Area closed. I had pulled the chest out of that channel nine days ago—which meant Sable had found evidence of my salvage before I'd even gotten clear of the first bend, reported it up the chain as her protocol demanded, and been told to forget it. Not *investigate*. Not *apprehend*. *Forget.*

And then she hadn't forgotten. Because here was her boat, burned on a stretch of river nowhere near any route she needed to patrol, unless the route she was patrolling was mine.

"She disobeyed him," I said aloud, without deciding to.

Ilse appeared in the wheelhouse doorway, a tin dispatch box under her arm, water dripping from her sleeves. "What?"

"Sable. Corven told her to stand down from the channel and she didn't. She kept looking." I held up the log. "He sent her after us anyway—or she made him think he did—and somewhere between there and here, somebody else decided she was too close."

Ilse set the dispatch box on the console and opened it right there among the melted gauges. Inside: ammunition, dry and boxed. A signal flare pistol. And a sheaf of papers in a rubber sleeve—copies, not originals, typed on a machine with a failing *e*.

She read them the way she read everything, fast and expressionless, and then she handed them to me, and I understood why her face had gone still.

They were transfer records. Provision funds—our funds, the famine money, the money that had raised half the towns on this river—moved through a chain of accounts I didn't recognize, landing finally in a name I did recognize from the chest's ledgers, but attached now to an entity that had never appeared there: the Meridian Combine. Shipping, warehousing, river freight. The cartel's public face, if a cartel bothers with a face.

"They weren't just laundering the stolen money," Ilse said. "They're still moving it. Every year, on schedule. The theft isn't history—it's income." She tapped the page. "Corven looted the fund forty years ago and got away clean. These people inherited the arrangement. To them, the chest isn't evidence. It's a threat to cash flow."

"And Sable?"

"Sable was Corven's problem. Then she became theirs." Ilse closed the dispatch box. "A magistrate can be bought, stalled, managed. An honest enforcer with a patrol boat and a conscience—that's a variable. Variables get removed."

The gulls screamed. The wreck ticked and settled, giving up another few inches to the river, and I thought about Sable stepping out of her boots on the cabin deck—orderly, methodical, even in surrender—and wondered where the river had taken her, and whether the river had bothered to give her back.

"We should go," Brannoch called from aft. "Fire drew eyes. Eyes draw boats."

* * *

We pushed off downstream and let the current take us past the wreck, and I watched it slide astern until the bend swallowed it. Ilse stood at the stern rail for a long time. When she came forward she didn't sit; she stood over the chest instead, arms folded, staring down at it as if it might explain itself.

"Three players now," she said. "Corven, who wants it buried quietly. Us, who want it read. And Meridian, who wants it gone so thoroughly that no one ever learns it existed." She glanced at me. "You understand what that means for the hearings? If they reach Rivergate before we do—"

"The witnesses stop testifying. The clerks stop remembering. The records develop gaps." I had spent my whole life learning how things vanished on this river: planes into fog, names into silence, nations off the map. Vessany hadn't died of neglect. Somebody had erased it, carefully, and Meridian's fingerprints were probably on the eraser.

Brannoch spat over the side. "Meridian runs the checkpoints below Two Sisters," he said. "Been running them three years, whatever letterhead they fly that week. If they've turned from counting cargo to killing crews, there's no papers in the world getting us past." He worked his jaw. "There's a back channel. Old logging cut, joins the main stem below the narrows. Shallow, snaggly, hard on a hull." He patted the rail affectionately, which told me what he thought of our chances for the hull. "But nobody counts cargo on it, because nothing worth stealing goes that way."

"How long since you ran it?" Ilse asked.

"Since the last time I owed those people money." He grinned, and it wasn't a happy grin. "Which is also why they'll be looking for us on the main stem. So. Back channel, and we pray the boat holds together better than my credit."

I went aft and took the tiller from him for the midday watch, and he went forward and slept the way old rivermen sleep—instantly, totally, one hand still hooked on the gunwale. Ilse sat across from me with the copied transfers spread on her knees, memorizing them, because that was what she did: she copied things, she committed things, she made sure that paper could burn and the truth survive it. I watched the banks slide past. Somewhere behind us, Sable was dead or drowned or walking out of the jungle with her boots in her hand, and the thought sat in my chest like a stone I'd swallowed.

Because here was the thing I couldn't say out loud yet: part of me had been counting on her. All this way, hunted, I had held a shape in my mind—Sable boarding us at some gray checkpoint, Sable's level voice reading regulations, Sable taking the chest with a receipt signed in that tight slanted hand. Terrible, but *knowable*. Corven's reach, at least, had rules. His enforcer was the rule itself.

Now her boat was a burned shell and her rules were ash with it, and the people who'd done it hadn't left a note, a demand, or a body. They had left nothing at all, which was worse than anything they could have left. Men who leave messages want something. Men who leave nothing just want you gone.

Toward evening the sky went the color of pewter and the wind backed around into the southeast, and Brannoch woke, sniffed the air, and said the word we'd all been not-saying: "Monsoon's early."

We rigged the canvas and doubled the lashings on the chest, and I stood my watch in the first spits of rain with the copy of Sable's log inside my coat, against my heart, where I kept everything I was afraid to lose.

And that was when I saw it—far upstream, back the way we'd come, a light where no light should be. Not a navigation light. A searchlight, sweeping the water in slow, patient arcs, the way you sweep when you're not looking for a boat.

You're looking for a burn.

I killed our own lamp and eased the tiller over, and we slid into the dark with the rain coming harder, and behind us the light swept on, patient as arithmetic, marking the place where Sable's boat had died—and learning, stroke by stroke, exactly which way the current runs.
