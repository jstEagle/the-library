## 16. The Third Party

The river had a way of telling you things before people did.

Ilse had learned that from her grandmother, who claimed the Vessany clerks read water the way other men read ledgers — by what it carried, and what it refused to carry. A river that ran brown carried topsoil and honest work. A river that ran green and still carried a smell of oil meant money moving where money shouldn't. And a river that went quiet — that was the worst. A quiet river meant someone upstream had paid for the silence.

For two days after they found Sable's patrol boat broken on the shallows, the river had been quiet.

Not empty. Quiet. Barges still passed with their tarps lashed down, dugout canoes slipped through the reeds, a paddle-steamer groaned past at dusk trailing woodsmoke like a bad memory. But the *talk* had gone out of the water. At the last three landings, the traders who usually shouted prices at Brannoch had looked at their boat — at the patched hull, at Oscar's lean silhouette at the tiller, at Ilse herself — and had found urgent business elsewhere. One old woman selling smoked fish wouldn't take coin from Brannoch's hand. She set the fish on the gunwale and stepped back as if the planks were hot.

"They know," Ilse said, on the second evening, watching the fish seller's wake dissolve downstream. "Word travels faster than we do."

"Word always does," Brannoch said around his pipe. "Cheaper than cargo. No duty on it."

Oscar said nothing. Oscar had been saying nothing for two days, which for Oscar was a kind of shouting. They stood at the stern with their arms folded, watching the channel markers, and Ilse recognized the posture because she'd worn it herself for years: the body braced for a blow it couldn't yet name.

She could name it. She'd been naming it since the wreck of Sable's boat, since she'd crouched on those torn planks and seen what the breakwater hadn't done to them.

*Someone else wants the chest.* She had known it in her bones before her mind caught up, the way you know a storm before the first drop lands. And now, moored in the lee of a drowned orchard with the monsoon clouds stacking up like debt, she was going to have to say it out loud.

* * *

They ate under the boat's rotten canopy: rice gone soft, river fish Brannoch swore was catfish and Ilse suspected was carp, tea brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in. The lantern hung from its nail and swung with the current, throwing shadows that moved even when nothing else did.

Ilse turned her cup in her hands. She had rehearsed this speech for years — in ferry terminals, in border towns, in the rented rooms where she spread her grandmother's transcriptions across the floor like a fortune-teller's cards. She had never once delivered it. Telling people made them targets; not telling them made her alone. She had chosen alone so long that the word had grown comfortable, like an old coat.

But these two had bailed river water out of a dying hull beside her. These two had earned the truth, or at least the shape of it.

"The men who broke Sable's boat," she began, "weren't Corven's."

Brannoch's pipe stopped mid-draw. Oscar's head came up slowly, the lantern light catching the flat wariness in their eyes.

"I've been thinking about it since the shallows," Ilse went on. "Corven doesn't destroy his own enforcer. Sable is his hands. You don't cut off your hands because they're reaching for something you want." She set the cup down. "And whoever did it didn't board. There were no boarding lines, no hand-weapon damage. It was done from a distance, with something heavy and fast. That's not a magistrate's method. That's commerce."

"Commerce," Oscar repeated. The word came out dry as August.

"There's a family of names I've been following longer than Corven's," Ilse said. "They don't appear in the ledgers as thieves. They appear as *bankers*. When the Provision was looted — when the grain money and the medicine money and the road-and-school money drained out of Vessany forty years ago — it didn't vanish. Money never vanishes. It gets washed. Someone took that dirty river of stolen relief and ran it clean through shipping firms, through timber concessions, through river trade itself." She gestured, taking in the dark water, the barges, the whole breathing length of the Rivergate basin. "Through all of this. Half the legitimate traffic on this river floats on money that was meant to feed starving towns. And the families who did the washing are still doing it. Still here. Still respectable."

The lantern swung. Somewhere across the channel a night bird called once and thought better of it.

"You're saying," Brannoch said carefully, "that the chest isn't just proof against Corven."

"I'm saying Corven is the small part of it." Ilse heard her own voice go quiet, the way it did when she spoke of her grandmother. "He signed the thefts. He deserves the rope or the docket, whichever comes. But he spent the money through people whose names aren't on any page I ever copied — because they made sure their names would never be on a page. They built the laundering into the river's bones. And if the Provision hearings in Rivergate open those ledgers publicly, it doesn't just end Corven. It ends *them*. Their concessions, their charters, their grandchildren's inheritances." She looked at each of them in turn. "So they've hired their own hunters. Not soldiers. Contractors. Quiet men with fast boats and no uniforms. The kind who make accidents."

Oscar was very still. "Sable's wreck wasn't an accident."

"Sable was hunting us with official authority. To the cartel, that made her either competition or a loose thread — someone who might find the chest and hand it to Corven instead of destroying it. Either way, she was in the channel ahead of us, so they removed her." Ilse swallowed. "We weren't the target that day. We were simply standing in the blast radius. Next time we won't be."

* * *

She told them the rest while the rain started — the first fat, indifferent drops ticking on the canopy, the river's smell rising up green and mineral.

Her grandmother, Mirela, had been a junior clerk in the Vessany fund's final year, one of dozens who watched the numbers stop making sense and understood what the nonsense meant. Most clerks looked away. Looking away was survival; looking closely was treason. Mirela had looked closely, and then she had done the only thing a clerk could do: she had copied. Night after night, in a hand so small it could hide inside a hymnbook, she transcribed the true ledgers — the real disbursements, the false vendors, the signatures — until the country itself dissolved and the copying became smuggling and the smuggling became exile.

"She carried the originals out in a false bottom of a sewing chest," Ilse said. "The copies she hid. She always said the originals were the message but the copies were the *insurance*. She died believing the insurance would be cashed someday." Ilse turned her cup again. "The sealed chest you pulled from the flood channel — the fund's own archive, the master documents — that's the message. My grandmother's pages are the confirmation. Together they can't be argued with. Apart, they're just stories."

"And the cartel knows the chest exists," Oscar said. It wasn't a question.

"The price on it at the river towns — that wasn't Corven. Corven sends summons and enforcers. This was money passed hand to hand through traders, deniable, generous. That's how the cartel works. They buy information the way other people buy salt." She paused. "They've probably known about the chest since it surfaced. Probably knew about my grandmother's route decades ago. Why do you think every archive that held Vessany records burned conveniently? Why do you think the maps stopped printing the name?"

The rain thickened. Brannoch got up without a word and wedged a plank over the worst leak in the roof, and the drumming changed pitch, and Ilse felt the strange domesticity of it — three fugitives under a tarpaulin, passing truth back and forth like a dish too hot to hold alone.

"So we're hunted twice over," Oscar said at last. "By the law, which wants it quietly. By the money, which wants it gone entirely."

"Yes."

"And you waited until now to tell us this."

Ilse met their eyes. She didn't flinch, though everything in her wanted to. "I waited until I was sure. I've been wrong before about who was behind me. I've accused innocent bargemen and run from harmless customs boats. I wasn't going to say *cartel* until I saw their work." She nodded toward the darkness where Sable's boat lay broken. "Now I've seen it."

Oscar held her gaze for a long moment, and whatever moved behind their eyes, they kept it there. Then they reached for the teapot and refilled her cup, which in Oscar's language was a verdict of some kind. Possibly forgiveness. Possibly just thirst.

* * *

Later, when Brannoch had gone forward to sleep sitting up against the mast as he always did, Ilse sat with Oscar under the dripping canopy and listened to the rain work the river into a fury of small sounds.

"My grandmother used to say the river keeps everyone's secrets and charges interest on all of them," Ilse said. "I used to think it was just an old woman's proverb."

"It's a ledger entry," Oscar said. "Everything on this water is a ledger entry."

Ilse almost smiled. Then she said the thing she had been carrying since the shallows, the thing heavier than the chest itself.

"Oscar. If we run this downriver — if we actually reach Rivergate with these documents — the hearings won't just put Corven on trial. They'll touch every timber concession, every shipping charter, every bank between here and the delta. Men who have never once been named will suddenly be nameable. Those men don't send letters and offers like Corven does. They don't sabotage engines and hope you'll turn back." She wrapped her hands around the warm cup. "They sink boats. They sink boats full of strangers and call it weather. And they are very, very good at calling it weather."

The rain hammered. The lantern flame bent and recovered.

"I need you to understand what you're tied to," she finished. "Not the chest. Me. My grandmother chose this and it cost her everything except her life, and eventually it cost that too, in a smaller way — she died in a rented room with her life's work hidden under floorboards, waiting. I chose it at nineteen and it has cost me every name I ever learned too well, every place I stayed more than a season. You still have a choice. Your plane is dead but planes get repaired. There are channels out of this. If you want to take the chest and bury it in some swamp and fly west and forget the name Vessany—" Her voice caught, and she hated it. "I would understand. I want you to know that I would understand."

Oscar was silent for so long that the silence itself seemed to fill with rain.

Then they said, quietly, "You bailed water with us. You navigated blind through the squall while my arms gave out. You didn't sleep for thirty hours and you still caught the sandbar before I did." They looked at her, and in the lantern light their face was older and younger at once. "Nobody gets to carry this alone. That's not how ledgers balance."

It wasn't agreement. It was something better and worse than agreement. It was company.

Ilse looked away first, out at the black water, and let herself feel, for exactly one breath, what it would be like not to be alone in this. Then she counted the breath away, because hope was a currency she'd learned not to spend.

* * *

Near dawn the rain slackened and the river rose, brown and muscular, dragging debris past their mooring — branches, a fence rail, half a door painted river-green. Brannoch stood at the bow reading the flotsam like scripture.

"High water early this year," he muttered. "Monsoon's coming mean."

Ilse came forward to stand beside him, and that was when she saw it: far upstream, where the channel bent around the drowned orchard, a boat running without lights.

Not a barge. Not a fishing skiff — no lamp, no nets, no sound of an engine worked gently by a man who loved it. This engine ran smooth and throttled low, the way a hunter walks. It came around the bend, held the deep channel for a moment, and then — deliberately, unmistakably — slowed. Held position. A dark shape against darker water, a mile off, doing nothing.

Waiting.

"Brannoch," Ilse said softly.

"I see it." He spat over the side, unhurried, and knocked out his pipe against the rail. "Been holding there near an hour, off and on. Since before first light."

Behind them, Oscar emerged from the cabin, hair wet, eyes immediately finding the distant shape as though they'd heard it in their sleep. "How many aboard?"

"Can't tell. Two, maybe three. Running dark means they don't care whether we see them." Brannoch's voice was gravel and calm. "That's the message. *We see you. We're patient.*"

Ilse's mouth had gone dry. Everything she'd said in the night was suddenly no longer a warning but a weather report. The noose wasn't tightening — it had already dropped over them, and what remained was only the question of how much line the hunters would give before they drew it home.

"Weigh anchor?" Oscar asked.

"Not yet." Brannoch squinted upstream. "If we run, we run blind into high water, and they know this river better than we do in flood. If we stay, we stay visible." He turned, and in the grey pre-dawn his single eye was bright and hard as a coin. "There's a third option. There's a cutoff channel, maybe six miles down — narrow, shallow, ugly, the kind of water no hired boat will follow a fool into. My kind of water. But once we're in it, there's no turning back for a day and a night, and the bilge pump—" he patted the deck beneath his feet, that faithful, failing heart, "—isn't long for this world."

As if the boat had been listening, the pump coughed, stuttered, and fell silent.

The three of them stood in the dripping grey, listening to the water rise in the bilge — a soft, patient sloshing, the river collecting its interest — while a mile upstream the dark boat held its station, and the monsoon stacked its black battlements along the whole southern sky.

"Well," said Brannoch, reaching for the hand bailer. "Best start practicing."
