## 17. Bailout

The bilge pump died at half past two in the morning, and it died the way everything on Brannoch's boat died: without ceremony, without apology, with a small wet cough and then nothing.

I heard it stop before I understood what I was hearing. That's the thing about constant noise—you stop listening to it. The pump had been chattering away in the stern for three days, a grumbling mechanical heartbeat we'd all stopped noticing, and when it quit, the silence it left behind was so complete that it woke me out of a shallow, aching sleep on the bench beneath the wheelhouse awning. For a moment I lay there listening to the river, thinking: *quiet. Too quiet.* Then my boots were in my hands and I was moving.

The water in the bilge was already over my ankles. That was the first bad fact. The second was the pump itself—Brannoch's ancient diaphragm pump, its rubber diaphragm split clean through like a mouth that had finally said everything it had to say. I held it up to the light of the storm lantern and turned it over twice, as if a second look might change the diagnosis. It didn't.

"Brannoch," I called, and my voice came out flatter than I wanted. "Brannoch, wake up."

He came up the companionway already half-dressed, which told me he'd been sleeping in his clothes the way old rivermen do, one ear always open for the sound of their own boat dying. He looked at the pump in my hands, then at the water around our shins, and his face did something complicated—not fear exactly, but the weary recognition of a man greeting an enemy he'd been expecting for years.

"Aye," he said. "Well."

*Well.* That was all. That was the eulogy for the pump.

Ilse appeared at the wheelhouse door with the chart case hugged to her chest like a child, her hair plastered to her forehead with river damp. "How bad?"

"Bad," I said. "But we're not sinking yet. We're just—losing the argument slowly."

The rain had stopped an hour before, which should have been mercy but wasn't. The monsoon doesn't stop; it only changes its mind about how it will kill you. What we had now was the between-storm dark, a sky like a wet wool blanket pulled down to the treeline, and the river running high and fast and black, carrying whole islands of torn vegetation past us in the dark like a crowd of drowned things hurrying somewhere. Out here, past the last of the channel markers, we were in open water—the wide flooded reach where the Rivergate forgot it was a river and became a moving sea. No banks to feel for. No lights. Nothing to steer by but Ilse's dead reckoning and the compass, and the compass swinging lazy and drunk in the storm's leftover swell.

Brannoch found the hand bailer in the locker under the net shelf—a galvanized scoop on a short handle, the kind of tool that outlives every machine around it because it has no parts to fail. He also found a dented cooking pot and, after a moment's thought, one of his own boots.

"Three of us," he said. "Two bail, one steers. Rotate. Nobody's a hero."

"I'm always a hero," I said, and took the scoop.

* * *

Here is what bailing a boat is, for those who've never done it: it is the most honest work in the world. You put the scoop in the water and you bring it out and you throw the water over the side, and then you do it again, and the boat is either winning or losing, and there is no way to lie about which. There's no cleverness in it. No negotiation. Every scoop is a small argument with the river, and the river never gets tired, and you do.

We set up the rotation with Brannoch at the tiller first, because he knew the boat's moods best and the boat was sulking—listing a little to port, the engine laboring against the flood current, the hull groaning in that low register that old wooden boats use when they want you to know they're thinking about giving up. Ilse took the pot, I took the scoop, and we fell into a rhythm that was almost, almost music: *scoop, throw, scoop, throw,* the water going over the side in silver arcs that the lantern caught for a half-second each time, like the river giving us back small pieces of the moon.

"How much water are we taking on?" Ilse asked, between throws.

"Enough," Brannoch said from the tiller. "Don't count. Counting's for people with time."

But of course we counted. Everyone counts. You count the scoops, you count the minutes, you count the inches on the bilge boards—Brannoch had notched a stick with marks like a prisoner counting days, and every hour or so one of us would dip it in and hold it up to the lantern and read the river's intentions off it like tea leaves. For the first hour we held even. The boat took water at the rate three people could throw it out, and the needle on that stick didn't move, and I told myself that was victory.

It wasn't victory. It was a truce, and truces end.

* * *

The second hour, the rain came back.

It didn't build. It didn't warn. One moment the dark was merely dark, and the next the sky opened like a wound and the rain came down in ropes, in columns, in a sound so total it erased thought. The river surface vanished under the hammering of it. The lantern's circle of light shrank to the size of a held breath. And every square inch of that boat began collecting water—deck seams weeping, the canvas awning sagging and dumping its load in cold gallons down the back of my collar, the open companionway drinking from the wash that came over the gunwale in brown fists.

The needle on the stick moved.

I don't remember deciding to work harder. The body decides these things on its own. What I remember is the moment the work stopped being a rhythm and became a fight—scoop, throw, scoop, throw, my shoulders burning, my hands going from cold to numb to a strange hot ache, the water in the bilge climbing past my ankles toward my calves. Brannoch bellowed something about the awning and I tore it down and heaved it into the locker, and the rain hit the bare deck like applause.

"Ilse!" I shouted into the roar. "How far to the channel?"

She was lashed to the wheel now, both hands on it, the compass light painting her face green. She had the chart case open under an oilcloth and she was navigating the way I imagine people navigate in dreams—by memory, by feel, by the ghost of a river she'd only ever seen in fair weather. "Six hours," she shouted back. "Maybe eight. If the flood's pushed us east we're wide of it."

"Eight hours of this?"

"Eight hours of worse." And then, because she was Ilse, and because lying badly was the one skill she'd never mastered: "The boat can't do eight hours of this."

She was right. I knew it in my arms and my back and the water rising around my knees. The boat couldn't do eight hours. Which meant we had to find something the boat could do, or find something else to do with the water, or become the kind of people who could do eight hours of impossible work, and there was no time to choose between those options, so we did all three at once.

* * *

Brannoch solved the awning problem by sacrificing the sail cover, cutting it into a funnel that channeled the deck water into a barrel, which we then bailed with the pot—water that had already been thrown at us, thrown back at the river, a closed loop of futility that somehow helped. I solved the pump problem the way bush pilots solve everything, which is badly and with whatever's at hand: I found his spares locker, cut a patch from a rubber float, and stitched the diaphragm back together with waxed twine from the net shelf, working by lantern light with my hands shaking from cold and effort. It would never pump the way it had. But it coughed, and it swallowed, and it moved water—maybe half what it used to, maybe less, a wounded heart still beating.

When it caught—that first ragged *chug-chug-chug* from the stern—Brannoch laughed. I'd never heard him laugh. It was a shocking sound, a rusted gate swinging open, and for a moment, over the roar of the rain, the three of us stood in the flooded bilge of a dying boat and laughed with him, all of us, at nothing, at everything, at the sheer stupid comedy of three people at war with a river at three in the morning.

Then the pump seized, and the laughing stopped, and we went back to the scoops.

* * *

Somewhere in the fifth hour I stopped being a person and became a machine for moving water. I want to be honest about that, because it's the part nobody tells you. Exhaustion past a certain point isn't pain anymore; it's a kind of erasure. My name went somewhere else. The chest in the hold, Corven, Sable, the hearings, the whole drowned country of everything we were running from and toward—all of it went somewhere else. There was only the scoop and the water and the next scoop. *Scoop, throw. Scoop, throw.* My hands had split along old calluses and the bilge water stung in the cuts, and I remember being grateful for the sting because it was the only proof I was still in my body.

And here is the thing I'll carry out of that night, the thing I didn't expect: I was never alone in it. Every time I came up out of that erasure—surfacing like a diver, gasping, remembering I was Oscar Yarrow and this was my life—someone was there. Brannoch taking the scoop from my hands without a word, his own palms bleeding in the same places mine were, because we'd been gripping the same handle. Ilse climbing down from the wheel to spell one of us, navigating by shouting course corrections over her shoulder to Brannoch while she bailed, refusing to sit, refusing to rest, her lips gray with cold. When I tried to take the wheel from her once, she shook her head.

"You fly," she said. "I'll steer. It's the same thing. You've been doing it all night."

"I've been bailing all night."

"No." She pushed the scoop back into my hands. "You've been keeping us up. All three of us. That's what you do. I've watched you do it for a thousand miles." She said it without softness, without ceremony, the way she said everything—like a fact she'd checked twice. Then she went back to the wheel, and I stood in the black water to my thighs, and I bailed.

* * *

The river nearly had us twice.

The first time was a log—a whole tree, roots and all, riding the flood like it had somewhere to be. Brannoch saw it a half-second before any of us, a paler darkness in the dark, and put the tiller over hard, and the boat wallowed and shipped a wave over the stern that added an inch to the bilge in one insult, and the tree went by close enough to touch, its branches combing the water with a sound like fingers through hair.

The second time was worse. The engine coughed—once, twice—and died.

The silence after it was the worst silence of my life. Worse than the pump. A boat without power in open flood water isn't a boat; it's a leaf. I felt us start to swing, stern to the current, and I knew with a pilot's cold certainty what came next: we'd broach, we'd ship water, we'd go over, and the river would take the chest and the boat and the three of us and grind the whole story into the delta mud where no one would ever find it.

Brannoch was already at the engine, tearing the housing off. Ilse had the wheel and her whole body braced against the swing. And I—this is the part I keep returning to—I did the only thing that made sense. I kept bailing. Because that was my job, and the boat was still taking water, and if Brannoch fixed the engine in thirty seconds or three minutes, either way the boat had to still be a boat when he did. There's a strange peace in that, in a crisis. In knowing exactly what your one job is and doing it and nothing else. I bailed like the world was a scoop and a throw and nothing more.

"Fuel line," Brannoch snarled, elbow-deep in the engine. "Water in the fuel. Give me—give me—" His hand came out and I put a rag in it, then a wrench, then my pocketknife, without looking, without thinking, the two of us working like we'd done it together for years instead of weeks.

The engine caught on the fourth try. It ran rough, it ran angry, it ran like a mule with a grievance, but it ran, and the boat came around, and the river that had been reaching for us with all its hands closed on nothing.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The rain kept falling. I kept bailing.

* * *

Dawn, when it finally came, was not a sunrise so much as a slow confession—the black sky turning the gray of old dishwater, the rain thinning to a drifting veil, and out of that veil, materializing like something remembered rather than seen, the far shore. A treeline. The ghost of hills beyond it. Ilse had brought us through the night on dead reckoning and stubbornness, and she had missed the channel mouth by less than two miles, which in that water, in that dark, was a miracle wearing work clothes.

The channel mouth itself was a seam of calmer water, a brown-green ribbon threading into the drowned forest, and when we crossed its threshold the river's grip on us loosened all at once, like a fist remembering it was a hand. The swell died. The boat stopped groaning. And in the sudden quiet, the three of us looked at each other across a bilge still six inches deep in river, at three sets of ruined hands and three faces gray with exhaustion, and Brannoch sat down heavily on the engine housing and said, to no one, to the boat, to the morning:

"Right. Tea, then."

We drank tea at seven in the morning in the flooded bilge of a rotten boat, sitting on upturned crates, our backs against a mast that had seen a century of worse. The pump—my stitched, scarred, half-blind pump—chattered away in the stern, doing its wounded best. The stick showed the bilge falling. Falling. For the first time in six hours, falling.

Ilse fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, the chart case still in her lap. Brannoch covered her with the ruined awning canvas without comment. I sat with my hands wrapped around the warm tin cup and watched my palms—the splits, the blisters, the bilge water still stinging in every cut—and I thought about what she'd said. *You've been keeping us up. That's what you do.*

I had spent my whole life believing that leaving was my one talent. That I was built for departure the way other men were built for staying—that the fog and the solo runs and the never-learning-names were not wounds but a nature, the way a bird has a nature. But sitting there in the wreckage of the night, watching two people sleep who had bled beside me, I felt something shift in the deep hold of myself, some cargo sliding and settling into a new configuration.

We'd held even against the river. Not won—held. Together.

Brannoch opened one eye. "You're thinking loud," he said.

"Go back to sleep."

"Aye." He closed the eye. Then, from under the canvas, half-gone already: "There's a village up this channel. Kessin's Landing. Old woman there owes me a debt, and she has a bilge pump off a patrol boat that'd fit our hull like it was born to it." A pause. "She also has a radio."

I looked at him. "Whose frequency?"

"That," said Brannoch, "is the question, isn't it." And he rolled over and slept, and I sat alone with the tea and the falling bilge and the thought I'd been outrunning all night: that somewhere behind us, on this same river, someone had been listening for word of a chest, and every radio in every village was a door we couldn't know was locked until we knocked.

The channel narrowed ahead into the green. Somewhere up it, Kessin's Landing. Somewhere up it, a pump, a radio, and whatever came next.
