## 18. Names We Don't Say

The river gave us a sandbar that night, a pale crescent of silt and driftwood heaped like bones, and Brannoch declared it as good a harbor as any. "Better than most," he said, spitting over the gunwale. "No one's ever drowned on a sandbar."

"Give it time," Oscar said, but they were already untying the tarp, and there was something in the set of their shoulders I had learned to read by then—not ease, exactly, but the loosening that comes when the water finally lets go of you and lets you stand.

We had been four days out from the checkpoint where Sable had boarded us and let us go, four days of looking over our shoulders at every wake on the water, and the boat had developed a new leak that Brannoch swore was only a seep and Oscar swore was the river coming in to collect us early. We were all of us tired in the way that strips a person down to the studs. Maybe that's why the fire happened. Maybe tiredness is the only honest country left, after enough days on the water.

Brannoch built the fire the way he did everything—economically, with an old man's economy that was really a young man's speed slowed down. He stacked the driftwood in a pattern I didn't understand until it caught, some trick of airflow that turned damp wood into a low, steady burn with almost no smoke. "Forty years of running from people," he said, catching my look. "You learn to make a fire that doesn't advertise."

The light did strange things to the three of us. It took the river-dark around the sandbar and pushed it back, and inside that small orange country we became, briefly, people instead of passengers. I watched Oscar's face across the flames and saw lines I hadn't noticed—the fatigue carved deep around the mouth, the way their eyes kept going to the chest lashed under the tarp even now, even here, even with the river empty in every direction.

I had been transcribing again, in my head, the way I did every night now. Page forty-one. The Vessany Agricultural Relief disbursement, third quarter of the last year the nation existed. I could recite it the way other people recite prayers. I didn't say any of this out loud. There were things we didn't say, and we had all agreed on them without agreeing.

But the fire was warm, and the brandy was Brannoch's, and terrible brandy has a way of dissolving the agreements.

* * *

It was Brannoch who broke first, which surprised me. He was the oldest of us and the most closed, a man whose silences had load-bearing walls.

"You know what I was doing," he said, to the fire more than to us, "the night the Provision hearings failed? First time. Forty years back." He turned the bottle in his hands. "I was in Rivergate. Dockside. I watched the news on a shopkeeper's television through the window, and I remember thinking, well, that's that, then. The money's gone. And then I went and bought a boat with money I shouldn't have had."

Oscar looked up. "You knew?"

"I knew nothing." He said it flat, but not defensive. "That's the whole point. Nobody knew nothing. That was the design. A fund that big, spread across that many towns, administered by that many respectable families—there's no crime scene. There's just a river that keeps flowing, and everyone drinking from it, and no one asking where the water comes from." He drank. "I didn't steal from the Provision. I just stopped asking questions in the same decade everyone else did. There's a difference. It's a small difference. But it's mine."

The fire popped. Out on the water, something large turned over and sank again—a log, a fish, the river breathing in its sleep.

"My mother," Brannoch said, and stopped. He almost never spoke of his mother. He had mentioned her exactly once before, to tell us she was dead, in the tone of a man reporting weather. "My mother cleaned houses in Rivergate. For the Vessany families. The ones who stayed after the erasure, who changed their names and kept their houses." He turned the bottle again. "She used to say they were kind to her. Kind. That was the word. She never once said the word *stolen*, because you can't say that word about the people who feed you. That's the trap. That's the whole beautiful trap of it. They build a school with your grandmother's famine money and then they're kind to you forever, and kindness is a debt you can't refuse."

I felt Oscar go still beside me. Not visibly. But I had spent enough nights in a leaking boat with them to feel it, the way you feel a storm front come over the water—pressure before weather.

"Your turn," Brannoch said to me, and I understood that he had told his story first on purpose, the way you throw a rope to someone in the water. He was giving me something to hold.

* * *

What could I say that wasn't already in the chest?

I told them about my grandmother, but I told it properly this time, not the version I'd given Oscar on the second day, the bad lie, the partial truth. I told them that Mirela Voss had been a junior clerk in the Provision's final office in Vessany's capital, that she had been twenty-six years old when the maps were redrawn, that she had understood before almost anyone what the erasure would mean—not just a country gone, but a ledger gone, and a ledger is the only country the dead can live in.

"She smuggled out the copies over two years," I said. "In a false bottom of a bread box. She said later it was the most boring smuggling in history. She just walked out every Friday with bread for her sister, and the bread box was heavier than bread should be, and nobody ever stopped a tired young woman carrying bread to family." I turned my hands over the fire. "She spent the rest of her life trying to get the ledgers to someone who would listen. She wrote to newspapers. To lawyers. To the Provision's successor charities. She got one reply, a form letter thanking her for her interest. She died with the papers in a bank box under a false name, and the false name was the only inheritance she left me, because a name on a key is worth more than money if the name is the right name."

"And the chest?" Oscar asked quietly. "The chest isn't hers."

"No. The chest is what she was looking for." I fed a stick to the flames. "Her copies were the clerks' records. Disbursements, requisitions, the paper trail of the fund itself. But the fund's money didn't stay in the fund. It moved. It moved through accounts, through land purchases, through a shipping concern, through—other hands. The clerks could prove the Provision had money. They couldn't prove where it went. Mirela spent thirty years trying to find the second half of the proof, the movement of the money out of the light. She had a list of names she suspected. She had no documents." I looked up. "Then forty years after the erasure, a chest with the Vessany seal surfaces in a flooded channel, and a bush pilot who doesn't learn names pulls it out of the river like it's a crate of engine parts."

"You followed the salvage report," Oscar said.

"I've followed every salvage report on that stretch of river for six years. Every storm season. Every flooded channel." I smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile; there wasn't much warmth in it to have. "Do you know what it's like, waiting for a river to give you back your grandmother? I do. It's like this. It's cold, and it takes forever, and it doesn't care about you."

Brannoch nodded slowly, the way old men nod when a story confirms something they'd already paid for. "And the cartel?"

"The cartel is where the money went. Some of it. The shipping concern Mirela suspected became, over forty years, a dozen concerns, and some of those became the river families that run cargo nobody asks about. The Provision's stolen money is the seed capital of half the gray economy on this water. They don't just want the chest gone. They *need* it gone, the way a body needs a wound closed. Because if the money is traced—if the first theft is proven—then everything built on it after is theft too. Every dock. Every route. Every quiet shipment." I looked at them both. "We aren't carrying paper. We're carrying the root."

The fire burned down a little. Brannoch fed it without being asked. And then we both looked at Oscar, because there were two of us who had told our histories and one who had not, and the fire had a way of making the arithmetic obvious.

Oscar looked back at us for a long moment. I watched them weigh it—I could see the weighing, the scales behind the eyes—and I thought they would deflect. They were good at deflection. They had a whole architecture of it, built over years, load-tested.

Instead Oscar said, "All right. But I'm only telling half. And you don't get to ask which half."

* * *

"You know I don't talk about my family," Oscar said. "You've both noticed. Brannoch, you've been on this boat with me for weeks and you don't know if I have a sister. Ilse, you know more about my carburetor than my childhood." They turned the brandy bottle without drinking. "That's not an accident. That's the design. My design."

The river made its long sound against the sandbar, that hush and drag, hush and drag.

"I grew up in a Provision town," Oscar said. "One of the ones that actually got the money, or some of it. There was a clinic. A school with a real roof. I went to that school. I had a teacher who told me I could be anything, and I believed her, and the reason I could believe her was that the roof didn't leak and the books weren't rationed and the clinic was eleven minutes' walk from my house." A pause. "My mother worked at the clinic. My father ran barges. We were—God, we were *grateful*. That's the word I keep coming back to. Grateful. The way Brannoch's mother was grateful. You don't audit a gift. You don't ask a gift where it came from. Gratitude is the lock on the door, and the Provision's families knew that, and they built half the interior on it, and every one of those towns said thank you for forty years."

"Your family," Brannoch said. Not a question. A door held open.

Oscar was quiet long enough that the fire had to be fed again. I did it this time.

"My father's barge ran Provision cargo," Oscar said finally. "Relief freight. And one season the freight changed. Heavier crates, different manifests, routes that didn't match the paperwork. He noticed—he was a barge man, he knew what a crate weighed by the sound it made landing. And he did the thing you're not supposed to do. He asked." Oscar's voice had gone very even, the way water goes even right before it goes over the edge. "He asked a question at a dock in a Provision town, to a Provision man, about a Provision crate. And six weeks later his barge 'took water' in a channel that barge men had run for thirty years without incident. And the inquest said weather. And the Provision sent a condolence payment. And my mother took it, because there were two children and no barge, and what else do you do."

The fire cracked. I found I had stopped breathing somewhere in the middle of it and made myself start again.

"I was fourteen," Oscar said. "I decided two things that year. One: I was going to fly, because flying is the only way to move on this river where nobody owns your route. Two: I was never going to ask another question about anything, ever, for the rest of my life, because questions are how they get you, and the dead don't get to testify anyway." They looked at the chest under the tarp, at the shape of it in the dark. "I kept both promises for twenty years. Never learned names too well. Never carried anything but cargo. Never asked where anything came from or where anything went. I built a whole life out of not-knowing, and I want you to understand that it was a *good* life. It was warm. It was safe. Not-knowing kept me alive through every checkpoint and every regime and every season of this river."

"And then," I said.

"And then the river gave me a chest with my father's killer's signature in it." Oscar said it without drama, which was the worst way to say it. "I didn't know that yet, that night I hauled it aboard. I just knew the seal. But some part of me—the fourteen-year-old part, the part I thought was dead—some part of me knew before I opened it. And when I read the ledgers, and I saw the shipping routes, and I saw the channel where my father drowned listed as a *transfer point*—not an accident, a *transfer point*, a place where freight changed hands—" Oscar stopped. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes for a moment, then took them away, and their face was dry and terrible and calm. "That's the half I'm telling. The other half stays in the chest."

Nobody spoke. Brannoch passed the bottle to Oscar, and Oscar drank, which they almost never did, and the fire ate another piece of driftwood, and the river went on saying its one word over and over.

* * *

It was late when the shape of it settled over us—the shape of three people who had been running alone and were, without any of us deciding it, running together. I felt it happen the way you feel a boat take a current: no one commands it, and yet you are moving, and the moving is the decision.

"Found family," Brannoch said, out of nowhere, half to the fire. "My mother used the phrase. Said the river's full of orphans and the orphans find each other. I thought it was sentimental nonsense when I was young." He looked at us across the flames, one eye catching the light. "I've had crews. Thirty years of crews. You feed them, you pay them, you bury the ones the river takes. That's not family. That's payroll." He tapped the sand with his boot. "This is different. I don't like it. I like it very much. Both things are true."

Oscar laughed—a short, cracked sound, the first laugh I'd heard from them since the checkpoint. "You're a sentimental old wreck, Brannoch."

"I'm a trader. I know value when it's sitting in front of me." He said it gruffly, but his hand, when he reached out and gripped Oscar's shoulder, was not gruff at all. And then, to my astonishment, he did the same to me, and his hand was heavy and warm and it smelled of rope and engine oil and forty years of river, and I did not pull away, because my grandmother had been dead eleven years and no one had put a hand on my shoulder like that since the funeral, and the body keeps its own ledgers, and that one was owed.

We sat there a while, the three of us, in the orange country the fire made, and the dark beyond it, and the chest between us like a fourth passenger who never slept.

It was Oscar who broke the quiet, and when they spoke, their voice had changed—lower, and aimed at the fire, and I understood that we had crossed some line and were now on the far side of it where the other half of the story lived.

"There's something you should both know," Oscar said. "Since we're doing this. Since we're—" they gestured vaguely at the fire, the sandbar, the whole improbable arrangement of us. "Tomorrow, or the day after, depending on the current, we pass a channel. The locals call it the Kestrel Run. Narrow, fast water, overhanging trees. There's no reason to stop. There's no reason to even slow down." They turned the bottle in their hands, once, twice. "But when we pass it, I'm going to want to look at it. And I'd rather tell you now than have you see my face and wonder."

"Someone's buried there," I said. It wasn't a guess. Everything about them had said it, all these weeks—the never stopping, the never landing twice, the life built on departures.

Oscar nodded, once, and looked at the dark water as if they could already see the channel waiting for us downriver, patient as everything the river keeps.

"My mentor," they said. "The man who taught me to fly. And the reason I never stop moving." They fed the fire the last of the driftwood, and the flames stood up tall, and their face in that light was the face of someone standing at the edge of a thing they'd circled for twenty years. "His name was Aldous Wren. And if we're going to pass his channel tomorrow, then I suppose tonight's the night I finally tell you how he died—and why it was my fault."

The fire burned. The river said its one word. And downriver, in the dark, the Kestrel Run waited.
