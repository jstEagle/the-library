## 19. The Pilot's Grave

The river narrowed in the afternoon the way it always did before it remembered something.

Oscar felt it before they saw it—a change in the water's voice, a tightening, as if the current had drawn a breath and was holding it. They were three days out from the checkpoint at Two Sisters, running south with the monsoon swelling behind them like a tide that meant to swallow everything it touched. The boat rode low. The chest rode lower still, lashed amidships under oilcloth, and every hour Oscar found their eyes going to it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

Brannoch stood at the tiller with his one eye on the channel ahead and the other—his missing one, the one he claimed could see weather coming from behind—squinted against the rain that hadn't started yet but was thinking about it. Ilse sat forward with the transcriptions wrapped in waxed paper against her chest, reading nothing, watching the banks slide past: cane gone feral, then mangrove, then the pale shoulders of limestone rising out of the green like the bones of something old.

"Kestrel Channel," Brannoch said, and said it the way you'd say a name at a funeral. Quiet. Just loud enough to be heard by whoever needed to hear it.

Oscar's hands went still on the fuel line they'd been checking for the hundredth time.

"I know," they said.

"You want to pass wide or pass through?"

Through. That was the answer that lived in Oscar's body, older than thought. Through, because Wren Ashby had flown through it forty-one times without incident and died on the forty-second, and because avoiding a place had never once made it smaller. But what came out of Oscar's mouth was: "Doesn't matter."

Brannoch looked back at them. One eye, and it saw plenty. "Everything matters on this river. That's its whole personality."

Ilse turned around too, and there was something in her face—not pity, worse than pity, *attention*—that made Oscar want to be anywhere else, anywhere with an engine and altitude between them and other people's eyes.

"Pass through," Oscar said finally. "It saves us an hour."

Nobody believed the hour. Nobody said so. Brannoch eased the tiller over and the boat swung toward the gap in the bluffs where the water ran fast and green and cold even in monsoon season, fed by springs that never learned about summer.

* * *

Wren Ashby had taught Oscar to fly in a plane held together mostly by prayer and baling wire, out of a strip carved into a hillside above a town whose name Oscar had spent years deliberately forgetting. Wren had been small and wiry and laughed like a door hinge, and she'd had three rules, which she called the only cargo worth carrying:

One. The river is always right.

Two. When you don't know, land. Pride has killed more pilots than weather ever did.

Three. Never love anything you can't take off from.

Oscar had memorized all three before they were sixteen, and had spent the twenty years since breaking the third one over and over again in small ways—in towns, in faces, in a hangar cat named Governor who had belonged to no one and therefore, briefly, to everyone—and each time, when the leaving came, Wren's voice had been there like ballast: *never love anything you can't take off from.* As if grief were a weight-and-balance problem. As if you could just calculate your way clear of it.

She died eleven years ago, in Kestrel Channel, flying medicine upriver in weather she should have waited out. Everyone agreed on that last part afterward, the way people agree on things that cost them nothing. She'd been carrying vaccines and a letter, and the fog had come down the channel like a lid closing on a pot, and she'd gone in low looking for the water, because rule two said land, and there was nowhere to land except the river itself, and the river is always right except when it kills you.

They never found the plane. They found one wheel, washed up two miles downstream, and a crate of vaccine vials floating intact in their packing straw, which the mission clinic used anyway, and which saved, by the sister's own count, some dozens of lives. Oscar had done the arithmetic on that for years. It never balanced. Nothing ever balanced. That was the whole secret nobody told you about loss: it didn't weigh anything you could put on the other side of the scale.

Oscar had scattered nothing, marked nothing, mourned nothing. There was no grave. The channel was the grave. And Oscar had simply stopped flying it—rerouted, added hours, invented fuel stops—until this boat, this leaky, cursed, magnificent boat, carried them straight down its throat without asking permission.

* * *

The channel walls rose on either side, close enough that the echo of the engine came back at them doubled, tripled, a crowd of engines. The water here moved in long slow coils, deep water, patient water. Rain began—not the monsoon's full fury yet, just a fine grey sifting, the sky clearing its throat.

"There," Ilse said suddenly, pointing.

On the left bank, where the limestone stepped down to the waterline, someone had built a cairn. Small, human-sized, made of flat river stones stacked with more care than skill, and on top of it, sheltered under a sliver of tin nailed to a stick, a wooden marker gone silver-grey with weather. Letters cut into it, filled once with white paint that the rains had eaten down to shadows:

**W.A. — SHE FLEW HOME**

Oscar stared. The boat drifted closer, engine idling, and the cairn grew larger and more impossible, stone by stone.

"Who—" Ilse started.

"I don't know." Oscar's voice came out strange, flattened, like sound heard underwater. "I never built it. I never came."

But someone had. Someone had carried stones down to this bank, year after year maybe, or once, or a dozen times, and stacked them against the current's appetite, and cut those four words into wood, and nailed up tin to keep the rain off the letters. *She flew home.* Not *died*. Not *lost*. Home.

Oscar thought of every departure they'd ever made—the rope thrown off, the prop wash flattening the grass, the faces on the dock getting smaller—and how each one had felt less like freedom than like falling and calling it flight. Twenty years of never landing twice, never learning names too well, never carrying anything but cargo. Twenty years of mistaking motion for safety, as if grief were a place you could outrun instead of a passenger who boarded at your first takeoff and never got off.

Wren had known. That was the joke of it, the terrible hinge-laugh joke of it. Rule three wasn't advice against loving. It was a diagnosis. *Never love anything you can't take off from*—because you can't take off from anything, not really, not once it's in you. Wren had loved the river towns enough to fly into fog for them, and the fog had kept her, and here was her grave built by hands Oscar would probably never learn the name of, and Oscar had spent eleven years flying loops around a hole in the map.

"Bring us in," Oscar heard themselves say.

Brannoch didn't argue, didn't ask. He brought the nose against the bank gentle as a mother setting down a sleeping child, and killed the engine, and the silence that followed was enormous, filled with rain and water-voice and the tick of cooling metal.

* * *

Oscar climbed out onto the stones. The cairn was taller than it had looked from the water—waist-high, maybe forty stones, each one chosen. Some had smaller marks scratched into them: initials, dates, a crude bird. Offerings sat in the crevices, weathered to anonymity—a rusted compass housing, a propeller blade worn smooth, a glass bottle with a rolled paper inside gone soft as cloth.

Oscar knelt. Their hands, which could rebuild a carburetor blind in the dark, shook doing the simple work of lifting a stone from the pile's base and holding it.

"She taught me everything," Oscar said. They hadn't meant to speak aloud. The words came anyway, pulled out by the channel the way the channel pulled everything. "How to read weather. How to land on water without tearing the floats off. How to talk to magistrates and customs men and lie about our weight. She pulled me out of—" A breath. "I was nobody. Dock rat, orphan of the flood year, stealing parts off wrecks. She caught me stripping a magneto off her plane and instead of turning me in she made me earn it back bolt by bolt. Took two years. Best two years anybody ever gave me."

Ilse had come ashore without being asked. She stood a few feet off, rain darkening her shoulders, saying nothing, giving the story room the way you give a fire room.

"After she died I told myself it was the routes. Too many ghosts on these runs, better to keep moving, keep the manifest clean. But that's not true." Oscar set the stone on the cairn, fitting it into a gap near the top, and pressed until it held. "I keep moving because if I stop, I have to be a person somewhere. A person who lost her. A person who owes her. You can't owe a dead woman anything if you never stay anywhere long enough to count your debts." A laugh came out of them, cracked and ugly. "Twenty years of departures and I never once took off. I've been on the ground this whole time. Dragging."

The rain thickened. Downstream, thunder cleared its throat in the hills.

"She used to say the river is always right. I hated that. Because if the river is right, then the fog was right, and the crash was right, and none of it was a mistake I could've fixed if I'd just been smarter. It was just the world being the world." Oscar wiped their face, or the rain, indistinguishable. "And now there's this chest full of proof that the world being the world means families starving while thieves build clinics with the money. And Corven—Corven who pulled me out of my second wreck, who gave me my first contract, who I'd have followed into a burning hangar—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Everyone who ever saved me turns out to be standing on somebody. And I flew for them. All those years, I ferried their medicine and their gossip and I never asked where the money came from, because asking questions means staying, and staying means—"

"Grief," Ilse said softly.

"Grief," Oscar agreed. "Grief and arithmetic."

Ilse crouched beside the cairn then, and from the waxed-paper bundle she drew nothing—just rested her hand flat on the wet stones, a moment, a stranger honoring a stranger. "My grandmother never got a cairn," she said. "Vessany doesn't get cairns. Vessany gets erased. Maybe that's why I can't stop either. Same disease." She looked up, rain streaming off her jaw. "You don't outrun it. You just carry it somewhere it can do some good."

Oscar looked at the marker. *She flew home.*

"Who built this?" they asked the channel, quietly. "Who comes here?"

No answer but the water. But at the base of the cairn, half-sunk in mud, Oscar's eye caught something new—something that hadn't weathered. A strip of oiled canvas, military fold, tucked beneath the lowest stones. And pinned to it, bright as the day it was placed, a small brass badge: the winged propeller of the Rivergate Pilots' Guild, and stamped beneath it, a number.

Oscar knew that number. Every pilot knew that number. It was the guild registration of a plane that had gone missing two seasons ago, piloted by a young ferry-runner named Tam Voss, out of Two Sisters, last heard requesting weather over the upper channels.

Tam Voss hadn't crashed. Tam Voss had been here. Recently. Leaving offerings at Wren Ashby's grave.

And Tam Voss, Oscar remembered with a cold that had nothing to do with the rain, had been on Sable's list—the enforcer's list of names to watch, glimpsed once at a checkpoint, folded away, half-seen. Names to watch. Or names already collected.

Oscar stood slowly, the badge heavy in their palm, the rain coming down harder now, and far downstream, through the channel mouth, a shape was moving against the current. A boat. Running without lights.

Brannoch saw it the same moment. His hand found the tiller.

"That'll be why the fog's coming early," he said. "Get aboard, pilot. Whatever this river remembers, it looks like it wants to introduce us properly."
