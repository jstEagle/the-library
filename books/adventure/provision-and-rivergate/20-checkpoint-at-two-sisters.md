## 20. Checkpoint at Two Sisters

Brannoch had run the Two Sisters checkpoint eleven times in his life, and nine of those times he'd been carrying something he shouldn't have been. The other two times he'd been carrying something worse: nothing at all, which made a man look guilty in a way that cargo never could.

The Two Sisters were two rock spires that rose out the river like the shoulders of a drowned giantess, close enough together that a boat had to thread between them with no more than an oar's width to spare on either side. The cartel had built their checkpoint on the eastern sister because it was the only high ground for twenty miles and because there was something about watching boats come at you through that narrow throat of water that made men feel like gods. Brannoch knew the feeling. He'd felt it himself once, long ago, standing on a different piece of high ground with a different kind of power in his hands. He'd given it up. The river had taken the payment in installments ever since, and the first installment had been his left eye.

He stood at the wheel now with his good eye fixed on the gap and his bad one doing what it always did—aching dully, remembering things the rest of him preferred to forget.

Behind him, under the tarpaulin where the salt barrels were stacked, sat the chest.

"Tell me again," Yarrow said quietly, coming to stand beside him. The pilot had a way of talking that never rose above the sound of the engine, as if loudness were a debt they couldn't afford. "The papers."

"Manifest says we're running palm oil and dried fish down to Ferrow's Landing." Brannoch didn't look away from the gap. "Consignee is a fish-packing house called Merle & Sons, which exists, which pays its taxes, and whose owner owes me money from a card game in 'ninety-three, which is why the signature looks right. Because it *is* right. I've been practicing it since before you were flying."

"And the brandy?"

"The brandy," Brannoch said, "is the most important part."

It sat in a crate by his feet: six bottles of Vellacourt brandy, vintage year whatever-they-claimed, with labels so ornate they looked embarrassed to be attached to what was inside. Brannoch had bought the lot off a wrecked barge three seasons back for less than the price of the crate. He'd tasted one bottle, once, and had spent the following hour convinced he was going to die, not from poison but from sheer disappointment. It was brandy the way a mudflat was a beach. Technically accurate. Spiritually devastating.

But the man who ran the Two Sisters checkpoint—a soft-bellied ex-soldier named Doss with a gold tooth and a memory like a ledger—had a weakness for Vellacourt the way some men had a weakness for women or cards or other people's money. Brannoch had learned this four years ago, on run number seven, when Doss had held him for six hours over a paperwork discrepancy and then, at the end of it, accepted a bottle of the stuff with the tenderness of a man accepting a newborn child.

"You're giving him the whole case?" Ilse asked from the hatchway. She had the chest key on a cord around her neck, tucked under her shirt, and she touched it now without seeming to know she was doing it. She'd been touching it all morning. Brannoch had decided not to mention it. A woman guarding a thing was calmer if she thought nobody noticed her guarding it.

"I'm giving him five bottles," Brannoch said. "The sixth stays aboard. You don't hand a man everything. You hand him almost everything, and then the almost becomes a relationship."

Yarrow made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "You've done this before."

"I've done everything before, pilot. That's the entire value of me." He adjusted the wheel a half-degree. The eastern Sister slid closer, wet stone streaked white with bird filth, and the water between the rocks went suddenly green and fast, the current funneling through the gap like it had somewhere important to be. "Now listen. Both of you. When we tie up—and we will tie up, Doss doesn't inspect moving boats, he likes a man trapped—you stay in the cabin. Not on deck. In the cabin. If he asks for the pilot, I'll say my pilot's drunk below, which will be believable, because everyone believes pilots are drunks, and because Yarrow can act sober better than anyone I've met, which is the same skill turned inside out."

"And if he wants to search?" Ilse asked.

"He won't want to search. He'll want to drink." Brannoch let the boat drift into the throat of the gap, felt the current take the stern and swing it, corrected with a touch of throttle that was more prayer than mechanics. "But if he does want to search, then we are past bluffing and into begging, and I'll do the begging, because I'm better at it. I've had more practice being beneath people's contempt than either of you."

The gap took them. For ten seconds the world was green water and black stone on both sides, close enough to touch, the roar of the funnel-current filling the hull like a held breath—and then they were through, into the wide slack water beyond, and the checkpoint was ahead of them: a floating dock of lashed logs, a corrugated shack, a flagpole flying the cartel's plain gray banner, and four men with rifles who were, at this distance, just silhouettes against the rain.

Brannoch cut the engine and let the boat glide the last hundred yards, silent except for the slap of water on wood.

"Nerve over firepower," he murmured, mostly to himself. "That's all this is. They've got the rifles. We've got the nerve. And nerve's worth more, because rifles only work if somebody's willing to use them, and Doss isn't willing. Doss is a fat man in the rain who wants to go inside and drink. Remember that. Every man at every checkpoint in the world is a fat man in the rain who wants to go inside and drink. Find what he wants to go inside *for*, and give it to him, and he'll wave you through faster than any gun could force him to."

The hull kissed the dock tires. A rope came down, and Brannoch caught it and made fast, and then he climbed out onto the planks with his hat pulled low and his one eye already smiling.

* * *

Doss had gotten fatter. That was the first thing. The second thing was that he wasn't alone.

There was a second man standing behind him on the dock, out of the rain under the shack's awning, and he was wrong in every particular that mattered. He was lean where Doss was soft. His rifle was slung properly instead of carried carelessly. And he was watching the boat—not Brannoch, not the manifest, but the boat itself, with the flat patient attention of a man counting things.

"That's new," Brannoch said, keeping his voice easy, climbing the last step onto the dock and extending his hand. "Doss. You old river rat. You've put on weight. Good weight. Prosperity weight."

"Brannoch." Doss took the hand with both of his own, which was how fat comfortable men shook hands, and Brannoch felt the gold tooth flash in the smile. "Still one eye, I see."

"Still the one that counts."

"And still running—" Doss squinted past him at the boat, and here it came, the pause, the little hitch where the real question lived underneath the small talk. "What is it this time? Fish? Oil? Something interesting?"

"Palm oil and dried fish for Merle & Sons at Ferrow's Landing." Brannoch produced the manifest folder from inside his coat with the smoothness of long practice—not too fast, not too slow, the gesture of a man who had nothing in the world to hide and slightly resented having to prove it. "Boring as Sunday. I'd trade the whole load for a dry pair of boots."

Doss took the folder. Behind him, the lean man shifted his weight, and Brannoch saw his eyes move—just once, quick, professional—from the tarpaulin over the salt barrels to the cabin hatch where Ilse had, thank every god that still answered, withdrawn herself from view.

"This is Kettering," Doss said, with the slight awkwardness of a man introducing someone he hadn't been told to introduce. "New security. From downriver. The organization's tightening things up."

"Organization's welcome to tighten," Brannoch said heartily. "Tight is safe. Safe is good for trade." He stuck out his hand toward the lean man. "Brannoch. Independent haulage. Thirty years on this river, and I've paid toll at this dock since before your mother was deciding whether to keep you."

Kettering didn't take the hand. He looked at it, then at Brannoch's face, then at the boat, in that order, and Brannoch felt the assessment land on him like a hand pressing down on a scale.

"Merle & Sons," Kettering said. His voice was quiet and had no accent Brannoch could place, which meant it had been trained out of him, which cost money. "You've run for them before."

"Fourteen times. Fifteenth today. They pay late but they pay."

"Your consignee seal." Kettering nodded at the folder in Doss's hands. "Show me the wax."

And there it was. The moment. Brannoch had known it would come—he'd built the whole performance around it, around this exact hinge—and knowing didn't make his stomach any quieter. Because the seal on the manifest was genuine. Merle's actual seal, pressed by Merle's actual clerk, on documents Brannoch had forged around it the way a forger builds a cathedral around one true relic. But a man like Kettering wouldn't know that. A man like Kettering would take the paper, hold it to the gray light, and start asking questions that had answers, and questions with answers were the dangerous kind.

"Doss," Brannoch said, turning away from Kettering entirely, dismissing him with the oldest trick in the social book, "you look like a man who hasn't had a decent drink since the monsoon started. Am I right? Don't answer. I'm always right about thirsty men."

He was already moving, back down the dock steps, across the wet deck, lifting the crate of Vellacourt with a grunt that was half effort and half theater. He brought it up and set it on the dock boards between them with a thump, and peeled back the lid, and watched Doss's face do what faces did when they saw the label—the softening, the brightening, the whole small resurrection of a man's day.

"Vellacourt," Doss breathed.

"Salvaged. Long story involving a barge, a widow, and a bet I regret. Six bottles. I'm keeping one—I'm not a saint—but the rest are yours, old friend, for the trouble of stamping a tired man's papers in the rain."

For a moment—one full, sweet, suspended moment—it worked. Brannoch watched it work. Doss's hand came out and touched the nearest bottle's shoulder the way you'd touch a horse you were thinking of buying, and his eyes had gone somewhere warm and interior, and the rain drummed on the awning, and the whole machinery of the checkpoint ground gently to a halt around a fat man's happiness.

Then Kettering stepped forward and picked up the manifest folder off the dock rail where Doss had set it, and began, unhurriedly, to read.

* * *

Brannoch had a theory about moments like this. He'd developed it over thirty years and eleven checkpoints and one lost eye, and the theory was this: panic is a smell, and men like Kettering are bred to smell it, and the only countermeasure is to be genuinely, sincerely interested in something else. So while Kettering read, Brannoch talked to Doss about the monsoon, about the fishing, about a woman in Ferrow's Landing whom they had both known in different decades, and he kept his voice at the pitch of a man sharing gossip, and he kept his one eye on Kettering's hands.

The hands were careful. Page by page. Thumb along the edge of each sheet, checking thickness, checking count. This was not a man reading a manifest. This was a man weighing paper.

"Three pages short," Kettering said.

The rain filled the silence that followed.

"Short?" Brannoch laughed, and made the laugh big and baffled and slightly offended. "Son, I don't write the manifests, I carry them. If Merle's clerk can't count, that's a conversation for Merle's clerk, and I'll gladly carry your complaint downriver free of charge—"

"Standard consignment for Merle & Sons runs seventeen pages." Kettering hadn't raised his voice. That was the worst part. He read numbers off the top sheet like a man reciting scripture. "Fourteen pages plus cover. Three missing. Either your clerk miscounted, or you removed pages, or—" and here he finally looked up, and his eyes were the color of river water in flood, brown and total, "—this manifest was built around a seal rather than sealed around a manifest."

Somewhere behind Brannoch, in the cabin, he heard the smallest movement—Ilse shifting, or maybe just the boat working against the tires—and he prayed with a fluency he hadn't managed since boyhood that it was the boat.

"Doss," Brannoch said, and now he changed registers entirely, dropped the bluster, let something older and flatter come into his voice. "Who is this young man, exactly, and who does he think he's talking to?"

"He's—" Doss began, uncomfortably.

"He's right," Brannoch said, before Doss could commit to anything. And he watched both men blink. "He's right, and I'll tell you why, because I'm too old to lie badly and too tired to lie well." He sat down heavily on the dock rail, rain running off his hat brim, and rubbed his face with one hand. "I pulled three pages. Tariff pages. The transit duties on the middle stretch—they doubled them this season, and Merle asked me to run the goods light-papered and settle the difference privately, because his margin's gone to nothing and mine's not much better. It's not honest. It's also not treason. It's the arithmetic of a poor man in a bad year, and every man on this dock has done some version of it, including, I'd wager, the man paying that gold tooth's upkeep."

Silence. Rain. Doss looking anywhere but at anyone. Kettering looking at Brannoch with that flat brown patience, and Brannoch meeting it with everything he had, which was thirty years of being underestimated and a face that had learned, the hard way, how to be exactly as honest as a situation required.

Because here was the beauty of it, the thing he'd gamboled the whole run on: the confession was true-shaped. Men believed confessions. A confession explained the missing pages, explained the nervousness, explained everything except the tarpaulin over the salt barrels—and a confession, once accepted, made a man stop looking, because a man who's been handed a sin doesn't go hunting for a bigger one. Nobody wants the bigger one. The bigger one makes work.

"Tariff evasion," Kettering said at last.

"On forty barrels of palm oil." Brannoch spread his hands. "Steal me something worth stealing, son."

And Kettering smiled. It was a small smile, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes, and it told Brannoch more than the man probably intended: it said *you're good*, and underneath that, *but I'm going to check anyway.*

"Open the barrels," Kettering said.

* * *

Later—much later, when the fear had drained out of him and left behind that hollow ringing quiet that followed every time he'd bet his life on a story and won—Brannoch would try to reconstruct exactly how the next twenty minutes had gone, and he never quite could. Memory refused to file it in order. What remained were fragments:

Doss protesting, weakly, that the oil was fine, that Brannoch was a fixture, that they could settle the tariff matter with a fee—

Kettering, unmoving: *open the barrels*—

Himself, hauling the first barrel upright, hammering the tap loose with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, thinking: *palm oil, please God, let it be palm oil, let the universe honor the paperwork for once*—

And the oil coming out. Thick, amber, reeking faintly of nuts and heat. Real. Barrel after barrel after barrel, fourteen of them, Kettering standing over each one with a dip-stick and a small brass hydrometer he produced from a canvas roll like a surgeon producing instruments, testing, nodding, moving on. The rain easing. The light going from gray to silver. Doss drifting toward the brandy crate with the gravity of a large moon toward a smaller one, and Brannoch, mid-performance, gesturing magnanimously: *take the case, Doss, take the whole case, call it tariff compensation*—

And Doss taking it. All six bottles. Even the seventh, the one Brannoch had hidden under the wheel seat, which Doss found himself, and held up, and Brannoch laughing and saying *that one's medicinal, that one's for the eye,* and Doss saying *which eye,* and both of them laughing, and Kettering not laughing, Kettering sealing the last barrel and writing something in a small notebook in handwriting so small it might have been code.

In the end it was Doss who saved them, though Doss would never know it. Because Doss stamped the manifest—tariff surcharge added, fee collected, the whole transaction dissolving into the great soft corruption that greased every mile of this river—and because Doss, pocketing his brandy, clapped Kettering on the shoulder and said, loudly, in front of his own men: "Thirty years I've known this one-eyed pirate. He's a thief, a cheat, and a liar, and he's never once carried anything worth the trouble of shooting him. Wave him through."

Kettering waved them through.

But as Brannoch cast off the line and backed the boat away from the dock, he watched the lean man standing in the rain with his notebook, and Kettering watched him back, and neither of them smiled, and Brannoch understood—with the certainty of a man who has survived by reading weather in water—that the notebook entry had not said *cleared.* It had said something else. It had said *again.* As in: *watch for him again.* As in: *someone downriver told me to look at boats like this one, and I intend to find out why.*

He turned the boat downriver and gave her the throttle, and the Two Sisters fell away astern, and the rain closed behind them like a door.

Yarrow appeared at his shoulder. So did Ilse. Neither of them spoke for a long while. The river ran wide and brown and endless ahead of them, and the chest sat under its tarpaulin, and the brandy was gone, and the fear was gone, and none of it felt like winning.

"Well," Yarrow said finally. "That was the worst brandy in the world."

"Bought us fifteen miles," Brannoch said. "Cheapest drink I ever bought."

"No." Ilse was looking astern, back the way they'd come, her hand at her throat on the key. Her voice was very quiet. "Look."

Brannoch looked. Far back, at the limit of sight, where the gray water met the gray sky near the mouth of the channel below the Sisters: a patrol boat, running dark, running fast, and gaining.

"Not Doss's," Brannoch said. He'd know Doss's boat by its smoke. This one burned clean fuel. "Not the cartel's either. Wrong lines."

"Whose, then?"

Brannoch fed the engine everything it had and didn't answer, because he didn't know, and because the only thing worse than being chased by a man whose name you know is being chased by one whose name you don't.
