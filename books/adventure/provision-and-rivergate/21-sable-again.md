## 21. Sable Again

The river gave her back to us at dawn, the way it gives back everything it doesn't want to keep.

I saw her before I knew what I was seeing—a pale shape snagged against a fallen kapok tree, half a mile below the wreck of the patrol boat we'd found two days past. Brannoch spotted it too, and his one eye went narrow in that way he had, like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"That's no log," he said.

"No," I said. "It's not."

Ilse was already at the rail with the boathook. "Oscar. If it's who you think it is—"

"Then we're already having this argument," I said, "and we haven't even started it. Bring us alongside."

Brannoch grumbled something about rivers and their habit of washing trouble onto honest decks, but he swung the bow around all the same. That was the thing about Brannoch I'd learned by then: he complained the way other men prayed. It wasn't meant to change anything. It was just how he talked to whatever was listening.

* * *

She came aboard heavier than a drowned woman should have—waterlogged coat, boots full of the Rivergate's whole gray memory—and when we laid her out on the deck planks she coughed up half the channel and looked at me with eyes that were furious to be open.

Sable. Corven's enforcer. The woman who had boarded us at the checkpoint weeks ago, turned our papers over twice, looked me dead in the face, and let us go. Too easily. I'd spent every night since wondering what that ease had cost, and here was the answer, blue-lipped and shivering on my deck: it had cost her everything else.

"She needs fire," Ilse said. She was already peeling off Sable's coat, working with the brisk hands of someone who'd done triage before, somewhere, for reasons she still hadn't told me. "And dry clothes. And if she's swallowed as much river as I think she has, she needs to be watched tonight."

"We could watch her from a distance," Brannoch offered. "Off the stern. Rivers are good at taking things back."

"Brannoch."

"I'm only saying the option exists."

But he was already dragging the brazier out from under the tarp, and I was already wringing river out of Sable's hair, and none of us said the true thing, which was that we were saving the life of the woman sent to end ours, and that we all knew it, and that knowing it changed nothing.

Some debts don't ask your permission.

* * *

She came around properly near midday, wrapped in Brannoch's spare blanket—the ugly orange one, the one he claimed had been won in a card game against a customs officer, which explained its ugliness—and sat propped against the cabin wall with a tin mug of tea she hadn't touched. Her hands were still shaking. She hid it well, the way she hid everything, but I'd watched this woman search a boat once with the patience of a surgeon, and I knew the difference between her stillness and anyone else's.

"You pulled me out," she said. Her voice was gravel dragged through water.

"The river did most of the work," I said. "We just objected."

"Why?"

It wasn't an accusation. It was genuine curiosity, the flat professional kind, as though I were a mechanism she needed to understand before she could predict it. I crouched down across from her, keeping the brazier between us—not because I feared her, exactly, but because courtesy seemed wise.

"Because I've pulled enough people out of that river to know how it feels to leave one in," I said. "Because Corven saved my life once and I've spent forty years trying to decide what that means, and I'm not going to answer it by letting his enforcer drown while I watch." I paused. "And because you're the third person to try to take this chest from us, and the first one the river beat to it. I wanted to know why."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not warmth. Recognition, maybe—the look of someone finding an unexpected door in a wall they'd memorized.

"You found the wreck," she said.

"We found the wreck." Two days ago, wedged in the shallows where the channel bent wrong: her patrol boat, holed below the waterline, burned from the inside. Not an accident. Not weather. Someone had wanted her dead, or wanted what she carried, or both, and had gone about it with the thoroughness of professionals. "We also found the scorch marks. Whoever put you in the water didn't do it gently."

"No," she said. "They didn't."

She drank the tea then, all of it, in three swallows, and held the mug out for more, and Ilse refilled it without being asked, and I watched the strangest transaction of my life happen over a dented tin mug: an enemy accepting hospitality from the people she'd been hunting, because the alternative was admitting she needed it, and pride, apparently, drowns slower than people.

"They weren't Corven's men," Sable said finally.

"I know."

"You don't know. You suspect. There's a difference, and the difference is that you're still alive." She shifted against the wall, wincing—I saw the bruising along her ribs when the blanket slipped, purple-black, the color of deep water. "Corven sent me downriver to intercept you. Quietly. He wanted the chest brought to him before the hearings convened, no witnesses, no paperwork. I told him I'd do it. I told him a lot of things." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I've been telling him things for eleven years. It turns out that's a skill, and skills get noticed, and the people who laundered his stolen money through the interior have better ears than he thinks they do."

"The cartel," Ilse said quietly.

"The cartel." Sable looked at her, and for a moment the two women regarded each other across the brazier smoke like chess players acknowledging a game neither had expected to be playing. "You know about them."

"I know enough."

"Then you know they don't send warnings." Sable set the mug down. Her hands had steadied, mostly. "There were six of us on that patrol boat. Four are still in the river. I got clear because I was awake, because I don't sleep well anymore, and because I've been on boats that burn before." She didn't elaborate on that. I didn't ask. Some biographies you can read in the way a person says *before*. "They took the dispatch case. They know Corven's timetable. And they know about you."

* * *

We anchored that night in a side slough, behind a curtain of willow, with the monsoon clouds stacking up along the western horizon like furniture someone was moving upstairs. Brannoch cooked rice with dried fish and too much pepper, the way he always did when he was nervous, and we ate in a silence that had shapes to it.

Sable ate sitting up now, propped in the cockpit corner, watching the sky the way pilots watch it—which surprised me until I remembered that enforcers on a river country learn to read weather the way we read instruments. Survival is survival, whichever direction you come at it from.

"You should turn back," she said, into the dark.

"No," I said.

"You should hear the rest of the sentence first."

"Fine. Say it."

"Corven isn't going to stop. The cartel isn't going to stop. And the hearings—" She stopped. In the brazier light her face was all planes and shadow, a face built for interrogation rooms, and yet there was something underneath it now, some load-bearing exhaustion, that made her look almost young. "The hearings convene in nineteen days. And Corven means them to be theater. A ritual. An empty room where old men apologize for nothing and go home richer."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've been cleaning up after him for eleven years, and I know how he finishes things." She turned to look at me directly, and I felt it like a hand on the sternum. "He's going to burn the records, Yarrow. All of them. The annex in Rivergate holds the master register—the original Provision disbursements, the land grants, the account books, forty years of paper. As long as that register exists, the hearings have something to check his testimony against. So it won't exist."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the slough a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining.

"When?" Ilse asked. Her voice was very controlled. I'd learned by then that Ilse's control was a lid on something enormous, and that the lid had rivets, and each rivet was a year.

"Soon. Before the hearings open. He'll call it an accident—an electrical fault, a carelessly kept lamp, the monsoon. Rivergate's annex is old timber. It will burn beautifully." Sable picked up her tea again, found it empty, and stared into the mug as if the answer might be poured into it. "I know because I was asked to arrange it. Three weeks ago. I said yes. I always say yes."

"And now?" I asked.

"Now I'm half-drowned on a leaking boat owned by a man who cheats at cards, drinking terrible tea, telling the people I was sent to destroy how to save the evidence that will hang my employer." She looked up. "Now I'm asking myself the question you've apparently been asking yourself for forty years, Yarrow. What a man's rescue of you actually buys. What you owe. Whether debt is the same thing as loyalty, or whether I just spent eleven years confusing the two because confusing them was easier than counting."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been a lie, and lying to her felt suddenly, strangely expensive.

Because here is the truth I hadn't said aloud to anyone, not even to myself, not in all these weeks of running the river with a sealed chest in my hold: Magistrate Corven had pulled me out of a wreck when I was nineteen years old and nobody, and he had given me my first job, and my first plane, and my first reason to keep moving, and I had spent my whole life since paying him back in the only currency I had, which was silence. Every year I didn't ask questions. Every cargo I carried without looking inside. Every rumor I heard in the river towns and chose not to repeat. I had thought of it as gratitude. Sitting there in the brazier light, watching Sable hold an empty mug like a rosary, I understood it had been something closer to rent. And that Corven had known that all along, and had priced it accordingly.

"You look like a man seeing his own face in bad light," Sable said.

"I feel like one."

"It passes." She said it flatly, and then, quieter, almost against her will: "It doesn't pass. But you get used to carrying it."

* * *

Brannoch relieved me at the anchor watch around midnight, and I lay in the cabin's heat listening to the rain start—first the scattered fat drops on the deck above, then the long steady roar of the monsoon arriving in earnest, the river rising under us like a living thing changing its mind.

I couldn't sleep. I went forward instead, into the covered well-deck, where Ilse sat with the lantern hooded and the chest open at her feet, transcribing again by the light of a single stub of candle. Her pen scratched. The rain drummed. She didn't look up when I sat down across from her, but she moved the candle an inch toward me, which was her way of making room.

"Sable's warning," I said. "Do you believe her?"

"Yes."

"Just like that?"

"Oscar." She set the pen down. In the candlelight her face was tired and sharp and certain. "I've spent nine years learning to tell when people lie about Vessany. Everyone lies about Vessany—it's practically the national language of the interior. She didn't lie. She told us the worst possible thing in the flattest voice she had, and then she told us it was her own fault." Ilse's jaw tightened. "Liars decorate. She didn't decorate anything."

Outside, lightning stitched the horizon white, and for a heartbeat the whole river stood revealed—rain like wire, banks like the edges of the world—and then dark again.

"Nineteen days," I said.

"Eighteen, now. It's past midnight."

I laughed, or something near it. "Of course it is."

Ilse closed the ledger she was copying, squared it with the others—a growing stack now, her grandmother's smuggled pages reborn line by line in Ilse's careful hand—and looked at me with an expression I was beginning to know the way I knew weather fronts. It was the expression she wore when she was about to say something that would rearrange the furniture of my life.

"If the annex burns," she said slowly, "and the master register goes with it, then everything we're carrying becomes the only proof left in the world. Not corroboration. Not support. Everything." She touched the stack of transcriptions. "Which means everyone who wants this history to stay buried stops hunting the chest and starts hunting us. All three of us. And the woman in our cockpit who used to be one of them."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I keep waiting for you to say something practical, Oscar, and instead you keep saying *I know* in that pilot voice, like the problem is weather you can fly around." There was no anger in it. There was something worse: worry. "This isn't fog. This isn't a fuel stop. We are four hundred miles from Rivergate, in a boat that takes water for a hobby, with a wounded enforcer, a traitor's brandy, and eighteen days."

"Sixteen, if we cut the inside channel at Two Sisters."

"That's not the point."

"It's entirely the point," I said. "It's the only point I'm any good at. Give me a distance and a deadline and I can find a line between them. Everything else—" I gestured vaguely at the chest, the rain, the whole sodden catastrophe of our lives. "Everything else is cargo. You taught me that. Cargo gets delivered. You just have to keep it dry and keep moving."

Ilse studied me for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a small, tired, real smile, the first I'd seen from her since the wreck.

"You're going to get us killed," she said, "in the most competent way imaginable."

"That's the plan."

* * *

I went aft before dawn to check the anchor, and found Sable awake, standing at the rail despite her ribs, watching the eastern sky go the color of old pewter. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist. The river ran high and brown and patient beneath us.

"Your friend Brannoch offered me a knife last night," she said, without turning. "So I'd have a fair chance, he said. When I decided to go back to being what I am."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I'd had a fair chance my whole life, and it's overrated." She turned then. In the gray light her face had lost some of its armor—or maybe the water had gotten into it, the way water gets into everything on this river, swelling the grain, loosening the joints. "Yarrow. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to spend the rest of this voyage pretending I didn't. Corven keeps a schedule. Boats, couriers, the annex guard rotations—all of it's written down, because he trusts paper more than people, and he's right to. If you're going to reach Rivergate before the fire, you need that schedule. Guard changes, courier runs, the day the annex detail rotates off."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you pulled me out of the river." She said it simply, without ceremony, and somehow that was worse than any speech. "Because I've spent eleven years collecting debts for a man who burns things, and I would like, before the end, to pay one back to somebody who doesn't." She looked away, downriver, toward the city none of us could see yet and all of us could feel. "The schedule's in my head. All of it. Ask me the right questions, Yarrow. And ask them fast. Because the cartel knows you're coming now—and they don't leave loose ends floating."

Behind us, forward, I heard Ilse's pen stop scratching.

Ahead of us, downriver, the mist was lifting off the water in long slow banners, and somewhere past all of it—past the checkpoints and the rapids and the burning thing waiting in Rivergate—was an annex full of paper, and a match, and eighteen days.

"All right," I said to Sable, and to the river, and to whoever was keeping the ledger of all this. "Then let's talk fast."

I woke Brannoch. We weighed anchor. The current took us.
