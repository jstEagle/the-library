## 22. The Copy

Ilse had learned to write in the dark before she ever learned to do it well.

Her grandmother's kitchen had one window, and the curtain over it was grey felt, and the rule was that no lamp would be lit past sundown because lamps could be seen from the road. So Ilse had copied her letters by feel—chalk on slate first, then pencil on paper, her fingers learning the shape of each word the way other children learned prayers. *Write it twice,* her grandmother said. *Once for them to find and once for you to keep.* It was not a lesson about penmanship. It was a lesson about survival dressed up as one.

She thought of that kitchen now, twenty years and half a river away, sitting cross-legged on the deck of Brannoch's rotten boat with an oil lamp hooded under her coat and Oscar's borrowed fountain pen scratching across the last clean page of a school exercise book she'd bought in Two Sisters for three coins.

The ledgers were open on her knees.

Not the real ones. The real ones were locked in the chest below, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping their heavy sleep of paper and proof. What lay open now was the third copy—the third night's work—and her wrist ached from the wrist bone up, and the ink had gotten into the crease of her thumb where it would stay for days, a small dark stain like a bruise shaped like a word.

*Provision disbursement, seventh year. Vessany District Office. Received by:*

And then the names. Always the names. She wrote them slowly, checking each letter against the original twice, because a transcription with one wrong name was worse than no transcription at all—it was a gift to whoever wanted to call the whole thing forgery. Her grandmother had taught her that too, though she hadn't known she was teaching it. *If you copy a thing, copy it true, or don't bother. A lie in the margin poisons the page.*

The boat creaked around her. Somewhere aft, Brannoch snored like a landslide deciding whether to happen. The river moved beneath the hull with that low continuous mutter it made at night, as if it were reading aloud from a book only it could see.

Ilse turned the page of the ledger and stopped breathing.

She knew this signature. Not Corven's—that one she'd memorized weeks ago, the elaborate looping C like a hook set in flesh. This was smaller, cramped, pressed hard enough into the paper that she could feel the groove of it with her fingertip even through the copying. A clerk's signature. A clerk who had signed off on a transfer of four hundred thousand crowns out of the famine fund and into an account whose name had been scraped from the register but whose number was still there, patient as a scar.

*E. Voss.*

Her grandmother's name had been Edith Voss.

Ilse sat very still. The lamp under her coat threw a small orange world across her knees, the ledger, the pen, the trembling line of her own shadow. Downriver somewhere a bird cried once and quit.

She had known, of course. She had known since the chest opened, since she'd seen the district office stamps and the clerk initials in the margins—she'd known the way you know a face in a crowd, instantly and then with a horrible slowness, detail by detail confirming what the first glance already told you. Her grandmother had been one of them. One of the clerks. The hands that moved the money.

But knowing was not the same as seeing the signature. Knowing was a story you told yourself on long roads: *Grandmother worked for Vessany, yes, but she was small, she was a filing clerk, she was nothing, whatever she did she did because she had to.* Seeing was different. Seeing was the ink itself, forty years old, still black, still certain, authorizing the emptying of granaries while the villages her grandmother came from boiled bark for soup.

Ilse closed her eyes. Breathed. Opened them.

Then she did what her grandmother had trained her to do without either of them naming it. She copied the page. All of it. Every figure, every initial, the signature included, in her careful schoolroom hand, true and complete, no lie in the margin.

Because that was the whole point. That had always been the whole point.

* * *

She'd started the copies the second night after the chest opened, while Oscar slept badly on the bench by the wheel and the boat swung at its mooring in the reeds. She had told no one. Not because she didn't trust Oscar—though trust, in her experience, was a currency that devalued the moment you spent it—but because a secret shared is a secret shaped by two hands, and two hands leave two sets of prints.

The logic had come to her whole, the way the best and worst ideas do, sometime between midnight and the hour when the mosquitoes gave up. Everyone wanted the chest. Corven wanted it back. The cartel wanted it destroyed or owned. Sable wanted it delivered. Every plan every one of them had made began and ended with the chest—as if the chest were the truth itself, rather than a box that happened to be holding it.

But paper burns. Paper sinks. Paper can be taken at a checkpoint by men with rifles who read only the first page before throwing the rest in the river. Her grandmother had understood this in her bones; it was why the woman had smuggled out fragments instead of the full registry, why the fragments had gone to three different hiding places in three different towns, why Ilse had spent nine years chasing them down like a woman gathering spilled beads on stairs.

So: let them want the chest. Let them watch the chest, follow the chest, bleed for the chest. The chest was bait now. The copies were the cargo.

She had sewn the first finished transcription into the lining of her jacket, flat against her spine, where a blade pressed between the shoulders would find it before any searcher's hand would. The second went into the false bottom of her satchel, under the maps. The third—the one drying on her knees tonight—would go into the boat itself, taped inside the bilge cavity behind a board so warped and filthy that no one alive would voluntarily put a hand near it. Three copies, three hiding places, three chances. If they took the chest tomorrow, burned it, sank it, fed it page by page to a stove in Corven's annex, the truth would still be moving downriver in three directions at once, quiet as water finding its level.

It was, she reflected, the most Vessany thing she had ever done. Take the valuable thing away and leave a convincing shell. Erase the map and keep the road.

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick with a fingernail and kept writing.

* * *

Oscar found her at dawn.

Not because she'd hidden it poorly—she'd hidden it perfectly, the exercise book back in the satchel, the jacket buttoned, the bilge board replaced and smeared with a fresh thumbprint of bilge scum for authenticity. Oscar found her because Oscar found everything, eventually, the way fog finds windows. They lowered themselves onto the deck beside her with two tin cups of chicory and handed her one without asking, which was, Ilse had learned, how Oscar said most things.

They sat looking at the river. It was doing its morning trick, wearing mist like a coat it hadn't decided to keep.

"You were up late," Oscar said.

"I sleep badly on this boat. Your pilot friend's mattress has opinions."

"Mm." Oscar drank. Watched the water. "Your jacket's been hanging wrong for six days."

Ilse kept her face still. Six days. She'd have bet the boat it was five.

"There's a seam at the left shoulder that sits differently when there's paper behind it," Oscar went on, conversational, terrible. "Also you smell of ink after midnight, and you write right-handed but your left hand has ink on the heel of it, which means you brace the page. Also—" they took another sip, savoring it, "—Brannoch says someone's been into his bilge, and Brannoch blames ghosts, and Brannoch doesn't believe in ghosts."

Ilse looked at them. Fog-grey eyes, wind-cracked face, that expression of a person who has spent a lifetime noticing things professionally and has given up pretending otherwise.

"How long have you known?"

"Since the fourth night. I checked the chest the morning after we opened it—counted the ledgers, the way you count anything you're afraid of losing. Nothing was missing. But the ribbon marker in the seventh-year volume had been moved." Oscar shrugged. "You don't move a ribbon by accident. And you're the only one aboard who reads the old district shorthand."

The river muttered. Brannoch's snoring had stopped, which meant he was listening, which meant he'd probably known too, which meant she was the last person on her own boat to know her own secret, and something about that was so absurd that Ilse laughed—a short, unguarded sound that startled a heron off the pilings.

"Three copies," she said. "Jacket, satchel, bilge. The chest stays bait. If they take it, they take a box. If they burn it, they burn a box. Everything that matters is already distributed."

Oscar was quiet for a long moment. Then they nodded, slow, the way they acknowledged weather.

"That's the smartest thing anyone's done on this boat," they said, "and I include my own flying in that assessment." A pause. "Why didn't you tell me?"

The question underneath the question. Ilse heard it, the way she was trained to hear things: *why am I still outside it.*

"Because telling you made it yours," she said. "And things that are mine, I keep alive alone until they can survive being held by someone else. My grandmother taught me that too." She looked down at her ink-stained thumb. "I'm telling you now. That means I think it can survive you."

Oscar turned that over. She watched them do it—the flicker behind the eyes, the old arithmetic of a person who had spent decades keeping their cargo light and their names shallow, weighing a new weight against all the old rules.

"The chest," Oscar said finally. "Corven will offer for it again. Or Sable will come for it. When that happens—"

"When that happens, we give them exactly what they expect. Panic. Resistance. Maybe we lose it on purpose, if it comes to that." Ilse met their eyes. "The box is worth more to us lost than saved, as long as the pages live. You understand what I'm saying? We've been guarding the wrong thing since the channel. There's nothing in that chest we don't already carry."

Oscar looked at her for a long time. Then, quietly, with something that might have been relief or grief or both wearing the same coat:

"Forty years, and nobody thought to just copy it."

"Somebody thought of it," Ilse said. "My grandmother. She just never got the chance to finish."

* * *

Brannoch emerged mid-morning, drawn by the smell of chicory and the absence of conspiracy, and when Ilse showed him the bilge cache—he insisted on seeing it, on putting his one eye close to the warped board and grunting like a man appraising livestock—he surprised her.

"So the chest's a decoy," he said.

"Yes."

"And everyone hunting us is hunting a decoy."

"Yes."

He scratched his beard, considering. "Best cargo I ever carried," he declared, "and it weighs nothing." Then he spat over the side, ceremonially, and went to check his pump, and that was the whole of it. No questions about her grandmother, no questions about the years. Just a man adjusting his understanding of the load and finding it lighter.

She caught herself smiling at his back. Caught herself, a day later, saving him the larger share of the salt fish without deciding to. Caught herself, on the fourth evening, teaching him the district shorthand for *disbursed* because he asked, idly, what the squiggles meant, and because some part of her had apparently decided that a man who bailed river water all night deserved to know what the water was for.

This was how it happened, she thought. This was how it had happened to her grandmother too, probably—not betrayal, not grand sacrifice, just the slow accumulation of other people in the shape of your life until the shape didn't close anymore. Her grandmother had smuggled ledgers alone and died alone with the fragments scattered, and Ilse had sworn she'd do it better, and here she was doing it better precisely because she wasn't doing it alone.

She hated how much she didn't hate it.

That night she started the fourth copy. Not for hiding—for Oscar. Because Oscar had stood at the rail that morning and said *nobody thought to just copy it* with a voice like a door opening, and because when the hearings came, whenever they came, Oscar would need the pages in their own hand, testimony flowing from their own pen, undeniable. She wrote the heading carefully:

*For O.Y., who counts what they're afraid of losing.*

And under the lamplight, wrist aching, ink climbing her thumb, she began again at the beginning: *Provision disbursement, first year. Vessany District Office. Received by—*

The river carried them down through the dark toward Rivergate, three copies deep in silence, carrying a box full of bait and a boat full of the real thing.

Two days later, at the fuel dock at Marrow Bend, Brannoch went ashore to settle an old debt—and stayed too long.
