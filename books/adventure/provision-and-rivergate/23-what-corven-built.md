## 23. What Corven Built

The river widened below Kessel's Landing the way a held breath widens a chest—slowly, and with a sense that something was about to be given up. Brannoch took us in on the last of the flood current, easing the patched boat past the pilings of an old timber wharf with the tenderness of a man steering a wounded animal, and I stood at the bow and watched the town assemble itself out of the morning haze and felt something I had not expected to feel on this stretch of water.

I recognized it.

Not the town itself—I had never landed here, had never once in nineteen years of flying the Rivergate put down at Kessel's Landing, because Corven had asked me not to, early on, in that mild voice he used for things he meant absolutely. *Skip my district, boy. I don't want my people learning to expect favors from the sky.* I had obeyed, the way I obeyed everything Corven said in those years, the way a man obeys the hand that pulled him out of a burning wreck and then hands him a paycheck and a reason to keep breathing. But I knew it the way you know a face you've only seen in photographs: the schoolhouse with its green shutters, the water tower painted with the district's crest, the long low clinic with its white walls catching the light.

Corven's district. Corven's town. Built, the ledgers in the chest beneath my feet now made brutally clear, with money meant for mouths that had starved waiting for it.

"Pretty place," Brannoch said behind me, and there was nothing pretty in the way he said it. He'd read the ledgers too. He'd read them twice, the night after we opened the chest, by lantern light, his one eye tracking down the columns of figures like a man following a blood trail.

"It is pretty," I said. "That's the whole damn problem."

Ilse came up beside me with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth under her coat, and for a while none of us said anything. The boat bumped the wharf. Somewhere up the street a bell was ringing—school bell, I realized. Children. There were children here, hundreds of them, and every roof over their heads and every chalk stick in their hands had been bought with a fund that had been meant, forty years ago, to keep an entire nation from starving, and had instead been carved up like a feast by the families who now governed the interior, and Corven's name was signed on the worst page in a hand I had once admired the way you admire calligraphy, clean and confident and beautiful, before you knew what the words said.

"Rules," Brannoch said, tying us off. "We're traders running brandy and engine parts. We're nobody. We stay till the boat's patched proper and we leave. Nobody asks about magistrates."

"Nobody was going to," I said.

"You were going to," he said. "I can see it sitting on your face like a bird."

* * *

Kessel's Landing in the morning was a town going about the business of being loved. That was the only way I could describe it, and I hated that it was the only way. The market stalls were full—not heaped the way river towns heap things when they're showing off, but full in the steady, unremarkable way of a place that has never once worried about winter. The school let out its morning recess and the yard flooded with children in uniforms that were actually uniforms, matched, mended, clean. A woman sold fried dough at the gate for a coin apiece and every child had the coin.

The clinic had a queue outside it, orderly, shaded by an awning, with a bench for the old ones. Through the open windows I could see a dentist's chair. A dentist's chair, on the Rivergate, four hundred miles from anywhere that had ever appeared on a map I respected.

I stood in the street with my hands in my pockets and felt the chest's weight in my memory and could not make the two things cancel out. That was the trap of it. I had spent the whole run downriver wanting the ledgers to be wrong, or wanting them to be right in a way that would be simple—wanting Corven to be a monster, because monsters were easy to testify against. But the ledgers were not wrong and Corven was not a monster, or not only, and what he had built with the stolen money was standing right in front of me with green shutters and clean uniforms and a dentist's chair, and children were laughing in it.

"You're staring," Ilse said.

"So are you."

"I'm staring at the roof tiles. My grandmother's village had thatch until the year they erased it. Thatch, and then nothing." She said it flatly, the way she said the worst things, as if flatness were a kind of armor she'd had fitted years ago and never taken off. "He built all this with money that was supposed to go to people like her. And it worked. That's what I can't get past. It *worked.* You can steal a fund and starve a nation and the schools still open in the morning and the bell still rings."

"The bell rings," I said, "because somebody paid for the bell."

"Everything rings because somebody paid," Ilse said. "The question the hearings will have to answer is whether the ringing counts for anything against the silence." She looked at the schoolyard a moment longer. "It doesn't. But it will be argued. That's what men like Corven buy with other people's money—not just buildings. Ambiguity. A town like this is a defense exhibit that breathes."

An old man selling river plums waved us over, and because refusing would have been more conspicuous than buying, we bought, and the plums were sweet, of course they were sweet, everything here was sweet, the whole district was watered with money that should have kept Vessany's children alive and had ripened into this instead.

* * *

We got the boat hauled onto the slip by noon. Brannoch knew a shipwright here—knew him the way Brannoch knew everyone, through debts and favors and thirty years of river traffic—and while the two of them argued cheerfully about planking, Ilse and I walked, because walking was what you did in a town where you couldn't afford to be seen asking questions, and because I think we were both drawn toward the thing we dreaded, the way a tongue goes back to a broken tooth.

The schoolhouse had a dedication plaque. I made myself read it.

*This school is dedicated to the memory of the Provision, and to the belief that no child of the Rivergate should go hungry, unlettered, or unhealed. — E. Corven, Magistrate.*

I stood in front of it for a long time. Ilse stood beside me. Down the street, the bell rang the children back inside, and the sound went through me like a hook.

"He believes it," I said. "That's the thing I keep coming back to. I've been telling myself he's a thief with good manners, and he might be, but look at this plaque. He *believes* it. He thinks the Provision was a promise and he's the one who kept it, and the fact that he kept it with money he stole from the dead—" I stopped. My throat had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with the ledgers.

"Your magistrate," Ilse said quietly. "The one who pulled you out."

"His district. His town. He'd fly me over it sometimes when I was learning the routes—not landing, never landing, but circling—and he'd point out the school and the clinic and the water tower and he'd say, *that's what governing is, boy, not speeches, roofs.*" I could hear the voice as clearly as if he stood behind me, that mild, patient voice, the voice that had talked me out of the wreck of my first plane and out of the wreck of everything after. "And I believed him. I built my whole life on the belief that the man was what he built. And now I've got a ledger that says the roofs were bought with famine money and I'm standing under one of them and it's *raining children*—"

"Oscar."

"—and the worst part, the part I can't get out from under, is that if he'd never touched the fund, none of this would be here. The money would have gone to Vessany, and Vessany would still be on the maps, and these children would—" I made myself finish it. "These children wouldn't exist. Their grandparents would have starved in the famine years and these exact children would never have been born. That's what the theft bought. That's what's in the ledger, if you follow the columns far enough. Not just theft. *Substitution.* He stole a nation's survival and spent it on a different set of people, and the different set of people is alive, and the nation is not, and I don't know which column the hearings are supposed to weigh."

Ilse was silent. Then she said, in the flattest voice I'd ever heard her use, flatter than the one about the thatch: "My grandmother kept the ledgers because she thought the truth would matter. She smuggled them out in a false-bottomed flour chest and she watched her country get erased anyway, and she died in a rented room in a river town that wasn't hers, and she told me, the last time I saw her, *Ilse, the worst thing they did wasn't steal the money. It was making the theft work. Because now everyone who benefits has a reason not to look.*"

She looked up at the schoolhouse, at the green shutters, at the plaque with its beautiful confident lettering.

"I'm looking," she said. "I've looked my whole life. But I'll tell you something I've never said out loud, pilot. Some nights, on this river, I've hated her for it. For keeping the ledgers. Because before I found them, I could just miss my grandmother. Now every time I miss her I have to decide all over again whether the truth was worth what it cost her, and what it's costing me, and what it's going to cost that bell."

* * *

The trouble found us at the clinic, the way trouble does—not loudly, but with a small wrongness you feel before you understand it. I'd gone in for tape and antiseptic for Brannoch's shoulder, which was healing ugly, and the nurse who served me was competent and kind and asked no questions, and it was only as I was leaving that I saw it: on the wall by the door, a framed photograph, old, sepia going to brown, of a groundbreaking ceremony. Men in suits with shovels. And at the center of them, forty years younger, straight-backed and already gray at the temples, a face I knew.

Corven. Young Corven, before the magistracy, before the wreck on the Kettle Narrows, before me. Standing on raw earth with a shovel in his hands and the whole future of the town in his face, and he looked—there was no other word for it—*happy.* Not the happiness of a man about to steal. The happiness of a man about to build.

The nurse followed my eyes. "The Founder," she said, with the easy reverence of someone who has never had cause to question a word. "Magistrate Corven laid the first stone himself. Came back every year for the festival, until the roads got bad. He paid for the clinic roof out of his own purse when the fund ran short, you know. Twice."

"The fund ran short," I said.

"Years ago, before I was born." She smiled. "He fixed it. He always fixes it. That's what people say about him up here. Whatever goes wrong on the river, the Magistrate fixes it."

I carried my tape and antiseptic out into the sunlight and stood on the clinic steps and understood, with a clarity that felt like a physical blow, exactly what I was walking into at the hearings. Not a courtroom against a monster. A courtroom against a founder. Against the man whose face was on the wall of every building in this district, whose name was in the dedication plaques, whose purse had fixed the roof, who had—God help me—pulled a half-drowned pilot out of the Kettle Narrows and given him a life. Every child in that schoolyard would grow up to be his witness without ever being called. Every roof was an exhibit. Every bell was testimony.

And the ledgers in the oilcloth under Ilse's coat were still true.

Both things. Both things at once, forever, and no verdict was ever going to separate them cleanly, and I finally understood why Corven had asked me, in that mild voice, never to land here. Not because he was hiding the town. Because he couldn't bear to have the two truths stand in the same place at the same time. Because he'd known, all along, exactly what I was feeling now, standing on his clinic steps with my heart going like a bilge pump in a storm.

Ilse was waiting at the wharf, and she was not alone. Brannoch stood beside her, and Brannoch's face had gone the color of old rope, and his one eye wasn't on me. It was on the far bank, where a black-hulled boat with no name on it was sliding up the channel with the current, running dark in broad daylight, and tied at its bow rail—a marker I recognized from the checkpoint at Two Sisters, from the cartel's territory, from the part of the ledger Ilse had shown us only once and then wrapped away.

"They're not here for us," Brannoch said quietly. "Look at the dock they're taking. That's the magistrate's private berth."

The black boat touched the pilings. A man stepped off, unhurried, in a coat too fine for the river, and the dockhands who had been laughing a moment before found reasons to be elsewhere, and I understood that Corven's town had just been introduced to the third party, and that the ambiguity I had come here to drown in was about to catch fire.

"Back to the boat," Ilse said. "Now. And Oscar—"

"I know."

"Whatever he built here," she said, "it's about to be the battlefield."

The man in the fine coat turned, and across the water, deliberately, he looked at me.
