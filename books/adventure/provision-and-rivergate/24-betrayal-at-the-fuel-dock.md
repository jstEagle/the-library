## 24. Betrayal at the Fuel Dock

The fuel dock at Sallow Bend had been rotting since before the war that nobody named, and Brannoch trusted it the way he trusted most things built by other men: with one eye on the pilings and one hand on his knife.

He went alone to negotiate the diesel. That was the arrangement, and he'd insisted on it himself back at Two Sisters—*let me do the talking, I know these dock rats, they'll skin a stranger and salt the hide.* It had sounded like prudence. It had even been prudence, once. Now, standing on boards gone soft as bread under his boots, watching the fuel man's boy crank the old hand pump while the river sucked and gurgled at the pilings below, Brannoch understood that he had insisted on coming alone for an entirely different reason.

Because he knew who would be waiting.

The boy finished pumping and wandered off to smoke behind the shed, and the fuel man counted coins into a tin with the slow ceremony of a priest, and then a voice came from the shadow of the covered barge moored at the far end of the dock—a voice Brannoch had last heard eleven years ago in a card room in Marrow Quay, right before he'd put a knife through a table and gone out a window.

"Old One-Eye. Still upright. The river must be short of miracles."

Brannoch did not turn around right away. He let himself have those few seconds of ordinary world—the smell of diesel and wet rope, the heron stalking the shallows, the sound of Ilse laughing somewhere up the bank because Oscar had said something dry about the boat's paint job. He let himself have it the way a drowning man lets himself have one more breath above water, knowing what comes after.

Then he turned.

Vetch sat on a coil of rope like he owned it, like he owned the dock, the river, and possibly the rain. He was smaller than Brannoch remembered, or maybe everything else had gotten bigger—the chest, the stakes, the two fools up the bank who trusted him. Vetch wore a good coat that didn't fit the country and a smile that fit everything.

"You're dead," Brannoch said. "I heard you were dead."

"You heard *hoped*, maybe." Vetch spread his hands. "Dead men don't collect debts, Brannoch. That's the whole trouble with dying. Somebody has to keep the books."

"I paid."

"You paid *some*. You paid the way a leaking boat pays the sea—one bucket at a time, forever." Vetch reached into his coat and produced a ledger of his own, small, oilcloth-wrapped, and Brannoch felt his stomach drop through the rotten boards into the brown water below, because he knew that ledger. He'd watched entries made in it by lamplight while he drank and lost and drank again. "Four hundred and sixty crowns, principal. Interest compounded at the house rate, which you'll remember is generous compared to what happens otherwise. Call it nine hundred now. Call it a thousand by monsoon's end."

"I've got maybe eighty."

"I know exactly what you've got. I know what your boat cost, what your brandy costs, what you lost at dice in Two Sisters the night before you met your new friends." Vetch's voice stayed pleasant, conversational, the voice of a man discussing weather. "That's the thing about old chains, old friend. They don't rust. They just get longer."

Up the bank, the laugh came again, and this time Brannoch turned toward it without meaning to. Oscar was crouched by the fire ring they'd dug, feeding sticks to a flame that fought the drizzle, and Ilse sat wrapped in the tarp with the chest between her feet like a dog she couldn't stop touching. Three people around a small fire. He'd had crews before, dozens of them, men and women who'd have sold him inside an hour and whom he'd have sold faster. This was not that. This was the first time in thirty years on the water that somebody had asked him about his shoulder—the old wound—and actually waited for the answer. The first time anybody had cooked for him since his wife died, and Ilse hadn't even made a ceremony of it, just handed him the bowl like it was nothing, like feeding him was simply what happened next.

That was the cruelty of it. Vetch hadn't come to threaten him. He'd come at the exact moment there was finally something worth taking away.

"The people you're running," Vetch said, following his gaze with professional interest. "The pilot and the clerk's granddaughter. We know about the chest, Brannoch. We've known since Sallow Bend got word of a one-eyed trader buying a rotten hull in a hurry. The family is very interested in family money coming home."

"It's not their money."

"No?" Vetch shrugged. "Neither is your debt, but here we are. The world runs on other people's claims." He stood, brushed off his coat, and Brannoch saw the shape of the trap complete itself, elegant as a fishhook. "Here's how it goes. You light the lamp on your mast tonight. Green glass, if you can find one—if not, any lamp, we'll know it. We'll know where they sleep, where the chest sleeps, and my friends will handle the rest, quiet, professional, no mess on your boat if you play it straight. And your ledger—" he held it up, then, with a flourish Brannoch might once have admired, dropped it into the stove of the fuel man's shack, where it curled and blackened among the coals. "Gone. Ash. You owe nothing to anyone ever again. Clean paper, old friend. A whole new life."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the debt lives, and the debt collects, and it collects from whoever's nearest when the collectors arrive." Vetch smiled wider. "You've seen how my colleagues collect. You used to be one."

Brannoch said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't begging, and he had never begged in his life, and he was not going to start on a rotten dock in front of a man who'd enjoy it.

"One night," Vetch said. "Lamp lit, we're square. Lamp dark, we come find you anyway, and we start with the girl, because she's the one who matters to them, and you'll get to watch what your loyalty bought." He clapped Brannoch on the shoulder—the good one, deliberately, the courtesy of a man who knows all your wounds—and walked away down the dock into the drizzle, whistling something tuneless, a small gray man growing smaller until the fog took him.

Brannoch stood a long time. The fuel man's boy came back from his cigarette and looked at him and looked away, quick, the way people look at a man who's just been given a sentence. Maybe word traveled. Maybe it always had, and every kindness Brannoch had been shown on this river had been priced in advance by someone keeping books he never saw.

He walked back up the bank slowly, buying time with each step, and the smell of the fire reached him—woodsmoke and the river and, underneath, the ghost of whatever Ilse had fried that morning—and he thought: *this is what it smells like, home. Thirty years too late, and this is what it smells like.*

"Well?" Oscar looked up, firelight catching the planes of that guarded face. "What's diesel run us?"

"Robbery," Brannoch said, and made himself grin, and found to his horror that the grin still worked, that thirty years of practice could carry a lie across his face smooth as grease. "But I robbed him back on the price. We're fueled and provisioned. We can make the Two Sisters reach by tomorrow evening if the current holds."

Ilse smiled at him over the chest. "You're a terrible old fraud, Brannoch."

"Aye," he said. "That I am."

* *

Night came down on Sallow Bend like a lid.

They ate late and talked little; the monsoon had them all in its grip now, that pressure behind the eyes, the river running high and fast and brown with everything it was eating off the land. Oscar turned in early in the boat's cramped cabin, claiming the watch change at midnight. Ilse stayed up transcribing—she thought nobody knew, but Brannoch had seen the candle burning past two every night, seen her copying ledger lines in a hand so small it could hide in a boot heel, and he had said nothing, because a man planning betrayal learns quickly to hoard other people's secrets like coins.

By eleven the rain had settled into its long steady work, drumming on the deck planks, filling the world with white noise. Good weather for signals. Bad weather for conscience.

Brannoch sat in the stern under the awning with a bottle he wasn't drinking and worked the problem the way he'd worked a thousand problems on this river: coldly, piece by piece, no sentiment.

Option one: tell them. Wake Oscar, lay it out—Vetch, the debt, the lamp. Then what? Oscar would want to run, but the boat needed the night at anchor to hold its patched seams, and running meant the checkpoint at Two Sisters anyway, which the cartel controlled, which meant they'd be met either way. Telling them bought nothing except that he wouldn't be the one who did it. Except he'd get to be clean while they drowned.

And they'd look at him the way people look at a thing that's failed. He'd seen that look aimed at others his whole life and had always been grateful it wasn't aimed at him, and now, at the end, he discovered he'd rather burn than see it.

Option two: the lamp. Lit, and the debt dies, and Vetch's people come in the night, and—what? Quiet, professional, no mess on your boat. He almost believed it. He wanted to believe it the way a gambler wants the next card. But he'd been on the collecting side once, young and hungry, and there was no such thing as quiet, and there was no such thing as no mess, and the girl mattered to them, which meant the girl was the point. Vetch had told him the truth precisely because the truth was the hook. *We start with the girl.* Not a threat. An itinerary.

Option three—

There was no option three. That was the whole design of chains. You spent your youth forging them link by link, drink by drink, debt by debt, and then one night on a rotten dock you discovered the chain had been leading somewhere all along, and the somewhere was a lamp on a mast, and the only choice left was which direction you faced while you betrayed the first people who'd ever fed you without a price.

He got up. His knees cracked. The rain kept its rhythm on the canvas overhead, patient as interest compounding.

The mast lamp hung in its bracket where it had hung since before he owned the boat, a brass thing gone green with age, and the glass was clear, no green pane anywhere aboard, and some animal part of him exhaled with relief—*can't be done, no green glass, fate has spoken*—and then he saw the bottle of river-glass dye in the provisions crate, bought at Two Sisters for marking waterlines, and understood that the universe was not going to save him from himself. It never had.

He climbed. The mast swayed under him, slick with rain, and the river moved beneath the hull with that deep muscular patience it had, and from up there he could see the whole bend: the black water, the far bank dissolving into fog, the cabin where Oscar slept with a pistol under the pillow and a lifetime of departures folded up tight as charts, the glow of Ilse's candle through the porthole, still working, still copying, still carrying her grandmother's war alone down a river full of wolves.

*They'd do it for you,* said something in him that sounded like his wife, dead nineteen years, who had never once lied to him. *You know they would. The pilot pulled a stranger out of a flooded channel on instinct. The girl took a bullet's worth of risk for a man she'd known a week. These are the ones who'd do it for you, and you're climbing a mast to sell them.*

"Aye," Brannoch said out loud, to the rain, to the dead, to nobody. "That I am."

But he lit the lamp.

His hands did it almost without him, the way hands do the unforgivable things—trimming the wick, striking the match, cupping the flame against the wind until it took. Light bloomed in the wet dark, small and obscene, a star planted where no star should be, and somewhere downriver or upriver or hidden in the fog of the far bank, eyes he couldn't see marked it and began to move.

He stayed up the mast longer than he needed to, because coming down meant it was done. Below him the boat creaked at its anchor, holding. The candle in the porthole went out—Ilse finally sleeping—and the boat became just a shape in the rain, a small wooden island carrying everything it had: a chest full of stolen history, a pilot's buried grief, a clerk's smuggled truth, and an old man's fresh, unforgivable debt, all of it riding on rotten planks over moving water.

Brannoch climbed down at last and sat in the stern and finally drank, one long pull, and then another, and watched the lamp burn and hated it and could not make himself reach up and snuff it, because snuffing it changed nothing now—they'd seen it, or they hadn't, and either way the die was cast, and a man who bet crooked all his life doesn't get to fold honest at the end.

Toward the small hours, through the rain, he heard it—or thought he heard it—the soft knock of an oar against a gunwale, out in the fog where oars had no business being. Once. Twice. Then nothing, just the river talking to itself.

Brannoch set down the bottle. He looked at the cabin where Oscar slept, and at the dark porthole where Ilse dreamed of ledgers, and he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding all night: however many hours remained, they were fewer than he'd thought, and they belonged to him, and there was exactly one thing left in the world he could still choose.

He picked up the boathook and began, quietly, to wake the pilot.
