## 25. Fire on the Water

The river at night had a sound Oscar trusted, and that sound was wrong.

They'd tied up below the bluff at a bend called the Widow's Elbow, where the current slowed and gathered itself like a man pausing before a long speech. Brannoch said it was good holding ground. Ilse said the name was bad luck. Oscar said nothing, because Oscar had learned over forty years of flying and one season of drowning slowly in a leaking boat that when two people who knew the river disagreed, the truth was usually somewhere under both of them, cold and waiting.

So it was the silence that woke them. Not a noise—the absence of one. The frogs along the bank had been going all evening, a wet electric chorus, and then they stopped, all at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone dangerous walks into it.

Oscar lay in the narrow bunk beneath the wheelhouse deck and listened to the boat breathe. The *Marta B*—Brannoch's rotten, beloved, half-sunk argument of a vessel—breathed differently than a plane. A plane held its breath. A boat sighed. The bilge pump they'd jury-rigged after the last failure ticked and gurgled like a patient heart, and Oscar had fallen asleep to that rhythm for eleven nights now, and tonight it was ticking alone.

No frogs. No current-song off the bluff. And underneath everything, so faint it might have been imagination, the low burble of an engine running without lights, somewhere upstream, idling.

Oscar was out of the bunk and moving before the thought finished forming. That was the pilot's inheritance: the body decides before the mind consents. Forty years of fog landings had built a person who acted on the first wrong note, because the second note was usually the treetops.

Ilse was already awake. Of course she was. She sat up in her corner of the cabin with the ledger chest between her feet and her grandmother's transcriptions wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and her eyes in the dark were wide open and completely calm, which frightened Oscar more than screaming would have.

"You heard it," she whispered.

"The frogs stopped."

"Ten minutes ago." She'd been lying there listening to the silence for ten minutes, saying nothing, deciding something. "I counted."

"Why didn't you wake us?"

"Because I wanted to be sure." A pause. "And because I kept hoping I was wrong."

That was Ilse entire, folded into three sentences. Hope as a delay tactic. Certainty as a burden she carried so no one else had to.

Oscar put a hand on the hull. Through the planks, faint as a pulse through skin, came the vibration of engines—not one now. Two. One upstream, holding position. One downstream, closer, moving with the current and no lights, the way water moves when it means to take something.

"They're not going to board us," Oscar said. "Boarding's for people you want alive."

Brannoch appeared in the wheelhouse doorway with his rifle already in his hands and his one eye catching what little light the stars offered. He looked, as always, like a man carved out of river driftwood by someone who'd run out of patience halfway through. But his voice was steady.

"Downstream boat is a patrol skiff, converted. I've seen it at Two Sisters. Cartel runs it with a mounted light and six men." He worked his jaw. "Upstream will be the fireboat. They bought it off the dredging company two seasons back. It carries diesel drums."

"Ilse," Oscar said quietly. "Get the copies ready to move."

"They're ready. They've been ready since Two Sisters."

"Then get yourself ready."

* * *

It happened fast, the way disasters do—all the slow dread of the evening compressed into ninety seconds of unbearable clarity.

The downstream skiff came around the bend with its searchlight blazing on, sudden as an accusation, pinning the *Marta B* to the black water like a moth to a card. Voices through a megaphone, words that didn't matter because everyone aboard already knew the grammar: *cut your engine, come out, bring the cargo.* Brannoch stood in the wheelhouse with his rifle down—he wasn't a fool, six men and a mounted gun against one old trader—and shouted back something about salvage rights and maritime law, buying seconds the way he bought everything, cheap and loud.

And the upstream boat came down the channel dark and silent and opened its side like a drawer.

Oscar saw the flame before understanding it. A gout of orange light, a hiss, an arc—and then burning diesel was landing on the river itself, spreading across the surface in a sheet of fire that walked toward them on the current, unhurried, inevitable, beautiful in the way that only things designed to kill can afford to be.

"They're going to burn us off the water," Brannoch said, and there was something almost admiring in it. "Cut us loose from our own anchor line so we drift into it. Cheap. Clean. No bodies with holes in them washing ashore with questions attached."

The megaphone voice repeated its demands. The fire crept closer, eating the river, and the heat of it reached the *Marta B* ahead of the flames themselves—a dry breath, a promise.

Oscar's mind did the thing it always did in crisis: went flat and practical. *Wind from the northwest, pushing the burn downstream faster than the current. Anchor line holds us in the kill zone. We cut loose, we drift past the fire's leading edge in maybe forty seconds if we're lucky, and the skiff is sitting right where we'd drift.*

"We cut the anchor," Oscar said. "We go through the gap between the fires before the sheets close. Brannoch, can this old girl steer cold?"

"She'll answer the rudder if the river lets her. That's all steering ever is."

"Ilse—"

"No." She already had the chest's handles in her grip. "I'm not leaving it."

"It's thirty kilos of iron and paper—"

"It's forty years of dead people," she said, and her voice cracked for the first time since Oscar had known her, one clean fracture down the middle of all that calm. "My grandmother carried these ledgers out of Vessany in a flour sack while soldiers burned her office. She walked nine days. I am not putting them back in the fire. I would rather go in with them."

There was no time to argue with that kind of arithmetic. There never is.

Oscar took the hatchet from its brackets by the wheelhouse door and swung once, twice, three times at the anchor line. The rope was river-swollen and fought back, fibers parting grudgingly, and each second stretched like taffy while the fire-sheet came walking. On the fourth blow the line parted and the *Marta B* lurched free, and the river took her the way rivers take everything—immediately, totally, without negotiation.

"Rudder!" Oscar shouted, and Brannoch threw his whole body against the wheel, and the old boat groaned and began, blessedly, unwillingly, to answer.

They slid toward the gap. The burning sheet upstream, the burning sheet drifting down from the diesel pour, and between them maybe twenty meters of black water narrowing every second. Heat pressed against Oscar's face like a hand. Embers came aboard, small orange visitors dying on the wet deck. The megaphone behind them had gone silent—the cartel men watching their work do itself.

Then the skiff's engine roared, and its searchlight swung, and it began to move to close the gap ahead of them.

"They're going to pinch us into the burn," Brannoch snarled.

And Ilse, standing amidships with the chest locked in her arms like a child, said, "Then put me ashore."

"What?"

"The south bank—the shallows past the bluff—if you can nose me in for five seconds, I'll wade out with the chest and draw them. They want the cargo. Let them chase the cargo instead of the boat."

"That's insane—"

"It's arithmetic," she said, using Oscar's own word like a knife handed back handle-first.

There was no time. There was never any time. The gap was ten meters wide now and closing, the skiff cutting across their bow, the fire roaring on both sides loud enough to shout over, and Oscar hauled the wheel hard south and drove the wounded, listing, rot-patched *Marta B* straight at the shallows with every intention of doing exactly what Ilse asked and no intention whatsoever of letting her do it alone.

The hull kissed mud. Ilse went over the rail with the chest before the boat even stopped rocking, splashing into waist-deep water, and the skiff's searchlight snapped to her instantly, exactly as she'd predicted, the light and the men's attention swinging away from the *Marta B* like a compass needle finding north.

Which left the boat exposed. Which left the boat burning.

Because an ember had found the fuel-soaked canvas aft, and the canvas had found the deck seam, and the deck seam had found forty years of Brannoch's neglect and patchwork, and fire, given a home like that, settles in and stays.

* * *

Later, Oscar would remember it in fragments, the way pilots remember crashes: not as continuous time but as a series of still photographs with gaps of darkness between.

A photograph: the aft deck in flame, orange light climbing the wheelhouse wall, Brannoch beating at it with a blanket that caught fire too and had to be thrown overboard trailing embers.

A photograph: the skiff nosing toward the shallows where Ilse stood hip-deep, chest above the waterline, backing away step by step, and men in the bow reaching for her.

A photograph: Brannoch seeing it. Seeing the men reach for her. Seeing the rifle leaning against the wheelhouse frame.

A photograph: Brannoch raising the rifle and firing—not at the men, at the searchlight, once, twice, and the light exploding into glass-shower darkness, and the skiff's bow swinging away from Ilse in confusion, and Brannoch stepping out from cover to adjust his aim, to keep their eyes on him, to make himself the target, deliberately, the way a man throws his coat over a barbed-wire fence so someone else can cross.

A photograph: the muzzle flash from the skiff. Small. Almost polite.

A photograph: Brannoch folding. Not dramatically—no cry, no stagger-and-clutch. Just folding, like a chair whose leg finally gives, going down onto the deck he'd patched and cursed and loved, and the rifle sliding from his hands across the planks to rest against Oscar's boot.

The gaps between the photographs were full of fire and shouting and the river hissing where the burning sheet met the shallows, and Oscar moved through them doing things without deciding to do them: hauling Brannoch by the collar toward the bow, feeling the wet warmth soaking through the old man's shirt, hearing him say, in a voice of terrible mildness, "Mind the rifle, she's loaded," as though the bullet in his side were a scheduling inconvenience.

Ilse came back through the shallows with the chest and climbed aboard weeping and silent at once, which shouldn't be possible and was. Together they got Brannoch flat on the foredeck, and Oscar pressed both palms against the wound and felt the terrible intimacy of another person's blood between their fingers, hot as the fire behind them.

The *Marta B* burned astern. The skiff, blind and wary, held off in the channel, its men arguing in the dark—they hadn't signed on for a gunfight, apparently; arson was one thing, return fire another. The fire-sheets drifted apart, spent, leaving the river smoking and black and stinking of diesel.

And the chest—forty years of dead people, nine days in a flour sack, four thousand kilometers of river—slipped.

Not far. Not gone. Ilse had set it down to lift Brannoch properly, and the *Marta B*, settling by the stern as the fire ate her seams, rolled, and the chest slid the last meter across the tilted deck and went over the rail with a splash that sounded, in that moment, like the whole story ending.

Ilse made a sound Oscar had never heard a human being make. Half scream, half prayer, all loss.

But the boat was settling in the shallows' edge, and the chest had gone over into knee-deep water, and Oscar—hands still red, heart still hammering, everything in them screaming to stay with Brannoch—heard their own voice say, flat and pilot-calm, the voice from the fog, the voice from every emergency that had ever made them who they were:

"Tie him down so he doesn't roll. I'm getting the chest."

The water was warm from the fires. The chest was heavy as sin and twice as damning. Oscar hauled it up the muddy bank and wedged it under a root system above the waterline, and came back down the bank with their arms empty and shaking, and knelt again in Brannoch's blood on the scorched foredeck.

The old man's eye found Oscar's. His face was gray as river clay, but he managed the ghost of his usual expression—that lopsided, unrepentant grimace of a man who has made a bad bargain and intends to enjoy it anyway.

"Well," he said. "Told you she was a good boat."

"She's burning, Brannoch."

"Aye." A wet cough. "But she burned slow. Gave us time. That's all a boat's ever for." His hand found Oscar's wrist and gripped it with shocking strength. "Listen. The mission clinic. Past the bend, up the creek, Father Anselm's place. You know it?"

"I know it."

"Tell him Brannoch sent you. Tell him—" and here the one eye glittered with something that might have been humor or might have been fever, "—tell him I finally paid my tab."

The skiff was retreating upstream, its engines muttering, its business unfinished and its courage spent. The *Marta B* settled deeper, flames climbing her wheelhouse into the night sky, and the smoke rose straight up in the windless dark like a signal only the dead could read.

Oscar held the wound. Ilse held the lantern. The river carried the smell of diesel and char out toward the sea, carrying the news ahead of them, telling everyone downstream exactly what they carried and exactly what it cost.

And above the tree line, far off but coming closer, Oscar heard the thing they'd been dreading since the Widow's Elbow went silent: rain. The monsoon's first fat drops, hitting the burning deck with a hiss like the river itself drawing breath.

They had a wounded man, a drowned-again chest, a dead boat, and twelve kilometers of creek and trail between them and the only roof on this stretch of river.

"Hold on, old man," Oscar said. "We're not done with you. You still owe us a navigation."

Brannoch smiled, and closed his eye, and let himself be carried.
