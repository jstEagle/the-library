## 26. Salvage

The river gave the chest back the way it took everything else—slowly, grudgingly, and only after it had finished deciding what to keep.

We found it at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the monsoon sky: a gray light like dishwater, the rain thinned to a fine cold needle that pricked rather than drowned. The boat was a mile downstream of where she'd burned, wedged in the shallows off a mudbank furred with reeds, listing hard to port where the fire had eaten through her starboard strakes. She looked like something a child had made from matches and then regretted. The chest had gone over the side during the attack—I had seen it go, had felt Oscar's hand close on my wrist and drag me down before I could reach for it—and all night I had lain awake on the deck of Brannoch's skiff doing arithmetic I didn't want to do: current, depth, silt, the weight of iron-bound cedar in moving water.

The arithmetic said we'd never find it. The river said otherwise.

It sat in eighteen inches of water at the channel's edge, half-buried in sand, its brass fittings greened overnight, a gar of some kind nosing at it curiously and then losing interest. Ilse Vessany's inheritance, delivered by flood. I stood knee-deep in the brown water and laughed until it turned into something else, until Oscar waded out and put a hand between my shoulder blades and said nothing, which was the right thing, because there was nothing to say that the river hadn't already said better.

* * *

Getting it into the skiff took both of us and most of an hour. The chest weighed as if it were full of stones instead of paper; maybe it was. Maybe guilt has mass. We rocked it, dug beneath it with our hands, pried with an oar until the oar cracked, and finally walked it up in increments, tilting and shoving, tilting and shoving, while the mud sucked at our ankles and the rain stitched the surface of the water into a million small wounds.

Oscar worked in silence, jaw set, hair plastered flat, and I watched them the way you watch someone carry something heavier than cargo. They had refused to sleep. All night they had bailed the skiff with a dented pot, one-handed, because their other arm stayed curled around Brannoch's shoulders where he lay under the tarp, breathing in that wet, rattling way that bullet-holed men breathe when the bullet went through meat and not through anything that ends you quickly.

"He'll want to know we got it," I said, when the chest finally came to rest in the bilge water among the fish and the ash.

"He'll want to know if it was worth it." Oscar looked at me across the chest, and the fog-light made their face unreadable, which I had learned was itself a kind of reading. "Was it? Worth his blood?"

I put my palm flat on the wet cedar. Underneath my hand, sealed and swollen but intact, lay forty years of theft: my grandmother's ledgers, copied in her clerk's hand, page after page of names and numbers that proved the Provision—the fund that fed the river towns, that built their clinics and schools and granaries—had been bled dry by the very families who now governed the interior. Corven's signature on the worst page. My grandmother had died carrying copies of this truth upriver in a false-bottomed trunk, and I had spent eleven years following her ghost through every archive and customs house between here and the coast.

"It was worth mine," I said. "That's the only answer I have."

Oscar held my gaze a moment longer, then nodded once and turned to bail.

That was the thing about Oscar Yarrow I understood best, maybe because we were built from the same defect: they never asked for certainty. Only for honesty about the uncertainty. Two liars, once. Now two people who had run out of lies worth telling.

* * *

Brannoch woke when we lifted him, which was more than I'd hoped for.

The one-eyed trader had taken the bullet high in the left shoulder—a through-and-through, the mission doctor would say later, missing the bone by a finger's width and the artery by less—but in the gray morning light, with his skin the color of old candle wax and his breath coming shallow and fast, through-and-through looked a great deal like dying. He weighed almost nothing. A man who had seemed carved from river timber turned out to be mostly hollow, mostly years.

"Boat," he rasped, as we eased him against the gunwale. His eye found the burned hulk down the channel and fixed on it. "My boat."

"She's gone, Brannoch."

"I know she's gone." His hand closed weakly on my sleeve. "I want it said she went fighting. Not sinking. Fighting. There's a difference, girl, and the river knows it even if the insurance men don't."

"There'll be a wake," I promised, and meant it, though I didn't know yet how large a promise that would become. "The whole river will drink to her."

"The whole river," he muttered, settling back, "drinks to everything. That's why nothing gets done." But his grip loosened, and something in his face unclenched, and I understood I had given him the only eulogy he wanted: not grief, but company.

Oscar started the skiff's ancient engine on the fourth try. We turned downriver toward the mission bell at Sainte-Anne-du-Basque, which I could hear before I could see it—a thin bronze voice carrying across the water through the rain, calling in the sick and the sorry from every bend of the channel. Forty years ago, the Provision had paid for that bell. I thought about that the whole way in. The money stolen from these towns had still, somehow, bought the thing that was now going to save the man who helped us steal it back. The corruption had a terrible generosity to it. It built while it robbed. Maybe that was why it had lasted so long. Nobody audits a benefactor.

* * *

The mission clinic sat on a bluff above the confluence, whitewashed walls gone gray with mildew, a corrugated roof singing under the rain. The doctor was a Kessari woman named Amaury who had been patching river people for thirty years and had the manner of someone who had stopped being surprised by wounds sometime in her second week. She looked at Brannoch, then at us, then at the chest Oscar carried up the bluff steps on their back, and her eyebrows performed a brief, eloquent conversation among themselves.

"Gunshot?"

"Cartel," Oscar said.

"Ah." As if that explained everything, which locally it did. "Table three. And you two—" she pointed at the burns on Oscar's forearms and the gash along my scalp where a splinter had caught me during the fire, "—you can bleed in the waiting area like civilized people."

They worked on Brannoch for two hours. I sat in the waiting area on a bench polished smooth by decades of anxious backsides, listening to the rain on the tin roof and the small sounds from behind the curtain: instruments, water, the doctor's low voice, and once—a sound I will keep longer than any ledger line—Brannoch swearing with tremendous creativity, which meant he was alive and angry, which meant he was going to be insufferable again soon, which meant he was going to live.

Oscar sat beside me, not touching, the way they did everything. After a while they spoke without looking at me.

"When he's stable, I'm going to ask you something, and I need you not to lie."

"All right."

"Last night, when the chest went overboard—you reached for it before you reached for the rail. Before you reached for him." Oscar's voice was flat, careful, a pilot's voice reading instruments. "I need to know which one you'd have saved. If you could only save one."

The question hung in the wet air. Behind the curtain, Brannoch swore again, beautifully.

"You already know," I said.

"I want to hear it."

"The chest." I turned and looked at them straight on, because they deserved that much and so did I. "Eleven years, Oscar. My grandmother smuggled those pages out of a country that doesn't exist anymore, and she died for them, and everyone she trusted either sold her or buried her. If I let that chest sit in the silt to save one old man—one man who signed on knowing the price—" My hands were shaking. I made them stop. "Yes. The chest. And I hate that it's true, and I'd do it again, and that's the whole of me. That's what's in the box along with the ledgers."

Oscar nodded slowly. Then, quietly: "He'd have done the same. For us. He already did—he took that bullet getting you clear of the fire, and he knew exactly what he was buying with it." A pause. "I think that's what frightens me. Not that you'd choose the truth over a life. That all three of us would."

I had no answer for that. Outside, the rain kept falling on the Provision-built roof, over the Provision-built town, above the river that kept all accounts and forgave none of them.

* * *

Brannoch surfaced near evening, propped on mission pillows, bandaged to the collarbone, demanding brandy and being denied it with a firmness that suggested Doctor Amaury had denied sturdier men than him.

He found me sitting on the floor beside his cot, transcribing. I had the chest open at my feet—Doctor Amaury had allowed it into the ward on the condition that it be covered with a blanket, "because whatever's in there is making the whole room nervous"—and I worked by lamplight with my grandmother's codebook open on my knees, copying columns of figures onto fresh paper in a cipher hand she'd taught me when I was nine, disguised as a game about secret princesses. Some games turn out to be inheritances.

"That's the third night running you've done that," Brannoch said. His voice was gravel and rust, but his eye was clear. "You sleep at all, girl?"

"Enough."

"Liar." He shifted, winced, continued anyway, because Brannoch regarded pain as a negotiating position rather than a fact. "How much is left?"

"Two-thirds. The fire got some of the outer correspondence. The core ledgers are sound. Grandmother packed them in oilcloth." I glanced up. "Why?"

"Because I've been lying here doing sums." He stared at the ceiling. "The cartel hit us within a day of the checkpoint at Two Sisters. Within a day of my forged papers passing through their man's hands. Papers I'd used before, same route, same stamp, never a problem." His jaw worked. "I know what that means. I've known since I woke up. Somebody read those papers and reported the route, and there's exactly one person on this boat who owed the cartel money."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the ward, a patient coughed in their sleep.

"Brannoch—"

"Don't." He raised his good hand, and for a moment the trader's bluster fell away entirely and underneath it was just an old man, hollowed out, ashamed. "Don't soften it. I've been waiting for you to ask since I woke up, and every hour you didn't ask was worse than the bullet." He looked at me, one-eyed, level. "When the time comes, I'll say it plain. To both of you. But not yet. Let me get upright first. A man should confess standing, or near enough."

I set down my pen. I thought of the wake I'd promised him, the whole river drinking to his boat, and understood suddenly that promises are cheaper than they look and heavier than they cost.

"Brannoch," I said carefully, "whatever you're carrying—it can wait until you're strong enough to carry it properly. That's not mercy. That's tactics. A confession made half-dead gets forgiven too easy, and I don't intend to forgive anyone cheaply ever again."

Something moved across his ruined face—relief, or fear, or gratitude, or all three wearing each other's clothes.

"Vessany," he said, using the name I'd finally told him, the erased one, my grandmother's, "you're a colder creature than you let on."

"I learned from the river."

* * *

Oscar came in past midnight, soft-footed, expecting me to be asleep. I wasn't. I was on the last pages, and my eyes burned, and the lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil, and outside the monsoon had settled in for the long haul, a steady drumming roar like the whole sky clearing its throat.

"How is he?" I asked.

"Asleep. Actually asleep, no performance." Oscar lowered themselves onto the floor beside me, back against the cot frame, and looked at the spread of transcribed pages with an expression I'd come to know: the pilot assessing weather. "Amaury says he'll live. Says he'll fly a desk for a season and complain the entire time."

"He'll complain for two seasons. Minimum."

"Mm." Oscar was quiet a moment. Rain on the roof. The lamp flame leaning. Then: "I checked the chest tonight. While you were sleeping, before you say it. Just looking. And Ilse—" They turned, and in the low light their face was stripped of everything, all the pilot's calm gone, and what was underneath was younger and more frightened than I had ever seen them. "There's a page missing. Near the end. The binding's cut clean—not torn, not lost. Cut. Someone removed it deliberately, recently, by the freshness of the cut. Someone who knew exactly which page mattered."

The cold went through me like river water in February. I pulled the ledgers toward me and riffled to the end with fingers that had stopped obeying me, and there it was: a gap in the sequence, a stub of paper shaved flush to the spine, precisely where my grandmother's index—her precious, meticulous index—listed a final annex. *Annex F. Disbursements, protective. Names withheld.*

"Withheld," I whispered. "She wrote *names withheld*. She hid one page herself, or someone hid it from her, and I've been chasing a chest that isn't even whole—"

"Or," Oscar said slowly, "someone removed it between Vessany and here. Forty years is a long time for a chest to float. Salvagers. Smugglers. Anyone could have opened it and taken one page worth more than all the rest."

One page worth more than all the rest. A list of names, protected disbursements—whoever was hidden on that page had been paid from the stolen fund to guard it. Paid to keep the Provision's thieves safe. And someone, somewhere, had thought that page dangerous enough to cut out with a blade.

I looked at Oscar. Oscar looked at me. And we both arrived at the same thought at the same moment, because we had become the kind of people who did that now, and neither of us wanted to say it aloud:

Whoever cut that page out might still have it.

And whoever wanted it badly enough to burn our boat and shoot our friend might not be finished.

Down the ward, Brannoch slept on, breathing slow and even, keeping his confession coiled inside him like a spring. I dipped my pen, turned to a fresh sheet, and began to write down everything I could remember about every hand that had ever touched this chest—including, for the first time, ours.

Outside, the river rose another inch in the dark, patient as arithmetic, keeping its accounts.
