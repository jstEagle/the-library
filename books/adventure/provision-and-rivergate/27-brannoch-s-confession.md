## 27. Brannoch's Confession

The mission clinic smelled of carbolic and river rot, and Brannoch had decided, somewhere in the third hour of lying on a cot too short for him, that he hated it less than he deserved.

The bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder, high and clean, the kind of wound that bled theatrically and killed almost nobody. Sister Ameline had dug it out by lamplight while Ilse held his good hand and Oscar stood at the foot of the cot like a man at a graveside, and Brannoch had made jokes through all of it because that was what he did, that was the whole architecture of him—jokes laid over rot the way clapboard is laid over a bad hull.

Now the jokes were quiet. The lamp was turned low. Rain worked the tin roof in long silver sheets, and beyond the mosquito netting the river moved in the dark with the patient sound of something that would outlast everyone in the room.

He had been awake most of the night doing arithmetic. Not the ordinary kind—the kind a man does when he knows the sum is coming due. Forty years of trading on this river, and he had never once paid a debt clean. There had always been a way to roll it forward, to trade it sideways, to drown it in a fog of brandy and bluster. But this debt had come due with a gun in a boy's hand on a burning deck, and the boy had shot Ilse's sleeve and missed Brannoch's heart by the width of a prayer, and the reason the boy had found them at all was a lamp signal from the fuel dock at Marrow Bend, and the lamp had been lit by Brannoch.

He turned his head on the flat pillow and looked at the two of them. Ilse had fallen asleep in a chair dragged up against the cot, her grandmother's transcription satchel still strapped across her body even in sleep, like a woman who had learned that documents were the only things you could trust to stay. Oscar stood at the window with the netting pulled aside, watching the rain, one hand pressed flat against the frame as if checking whether the world still held.

Brannoch had known them for a season and a half. He had sold them a rotten boat and called it a bargain, and they had called him a thief, and somehow out of that transaction the three of them had built something none of them had a name for. He had bailed water beside them through the night the pump died. He had navigated the Two Sisters checkpoint on nerve and terrible brandy. He had taken a bullet getting Ilse clear of the fire, and that part was true, that part was clean, and he had told himself on the burning deck that the clean part outweighed the rest.

It did not. He had known it did not from the moment the boy's muzzle flash lit the water red. A debt paid in blood against a debt incurred in lamplight—the arithmetic did not balance. Blood bought silence. It did not buy forgiveness, and forgiveness was the only currency he had ever actually wanted, though he'd have shot a man before admitting it.

"Brannoch." Oscar's voice, low, from the window. "You're doing your thinking face. It looks like constipation."

There it was. The joke laid over the rot, offered to him like a rope. He could take it. He could laugh, and they could have one more easy night, and the confession could wait until the river carried them somewhere else, some other dawn, some other year. He had been postponing his own honesty for sixty years. He was very good at it.

The rain filled the silence instead.

"No," Brannoch said. His voice came out gravelled, unused to this particular road. "Leave the netting down a minute. Come sit. Both of you. I've got a thing to say and I'd as soon say it once."

Ilse woke the way she always did—completely, instantly, her hand going to the satchel before her eyes opened. She looked at him, then at Oscar, and whatever she read in his face made her sit up straight and say nothing.

Oscar came and leaned against the foot of the cot, arms folded, and Brannoch thought: there it is, that stance, the pilot's stance, the man who has learned that everything he loves can be taken away between one departure and the next, so he stands where he can see the whole room.

"Go on," Oscar said.

Brannoch looked at the ceiling. There was a water stain up there shaped like a fish, or maybe a hand. He had been studying it for hours and it kept changing, which seemed about right for the truth.

"You remember Marrow Bend," he said. "The fuel dock. The night we took on diesel and that fat factor kept asking questions about our cargo."

"I remember," Oscar said. "You went to settle the bill. You were gone a long time."

"I was gone a long time because I wasn't settling the bill." Brannoch closed his eyes. It was easier to say it into the dark. "There's a man on that stretch of the river. Name of Oduya. Runs the cartel's paper side—loans, debts, the quiet kind of ownership. I've owed him since before either of you were born to this river. Started small, like it always does. A cargo I couldn't insure. A season that went bad. A loan against the boat, then a loan against the loan. Forty years, and every year the debt grew faster than I could sail." He laughed, one dry note, no humor in it. "You know how a debt works when it's old enough? It stops being money. It becomes a leash. And a man on a leash will do anything the hand holding it asks, because the alternative is to find out what's at the bottom of the river with a chain round his ankles."

Ilse's voice was very quiet. "The signal lamp."

"The signal lamp." He said it and felt the whole rotten structure of himself come down at once, like a pier in flood. "Oduya's people had been asking after the chest since Two Sisters. Word travels on this river faster than water. And at Marrow Bend, the factor—he's Oduya's cousin, I learned that after, or maybe I always knew and let myself not know—he sat me down with a bottle and said, Brannoch, one lamp. One flash from the dock when you cast off. That's all. The debt goes to zero. Forty years of chains, gone, for one flash of light."

"And you lit it," Oscar said. Not a question.

"I lit it." Brannoch opened his eyes and made himself look at them, because a man who couldn't look at the people he'd sold didn't deserve the word confession. "I told myself a hundred stories on the way downriver. That Oduya's hunters would only scare you. That they'd take the chest and leave the three of us. That I'd find a way to warn you first. That the debt was bigger than you were, that family is a luxury for men without leashes." He shook his head. "All lies. Every one. I lit that lamp because I'm a coward with forty years of practice, and because some rotten part of me has always believed that everything on this river can be bought, and I wanted to believe my own freedom was for sale at a fair price."

The rain hammered. Somewhere out in the dark a night heron called, that raw, grieving sound they make, and Brannoch thought the bird had the right of it.

Ilse had not moved. She sat with her hands in her lap, and her face was the face she wore over the ledgers—unreadable, careful, a woman who had spent years learning that trust was a document you signed in your own blood. He had seen her look at Sable that way. He had never expected to have it turned on him.

"You took a bullet," she said finally. "Getting me clear of the fire."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because it was true." His voice cracked and he let it. "That's the whole filthy truth of me, girl. Both things are true. I lit the lamp and I took the bullet and neither cancels the other. I'm not asking you to do the arithmetic in my favor. I'm not asking anything. That's the point of saying it now, on my back, with a hole in me—I can't dress it up, I can't trade my way out, I can't roll it forward. You know everything. Do what you want with it."

Oscar had not spoken since *and you lit it*. Brannoch had watched pilots read weather all his life, and he watched Oscar read this the same way: the long stillness, the eyes moving, the mind running the storm front forward to see what it would cost.

"Oduya's man," Oscar said at last. "The boy with the gun. He knew where we'd anchor. He knew the watch rotation. That wasn't the lamp alone."

"No." Brannoch swallowed. "The factor gave me a frequency. I called it in from the boat's set the night before. Anchorage, heading, the lot. The lamp was for confirmation." He barked that dry laugh again. "You see how it works? One sin is never one sin. It's a ladder. You climb down it rung by rung telling yourself the next one's the last."

"And Oduya's debt," Ilse said. "Is it zero now? Did they pay it?"

Brannoch was quiet a long moment. "I don't know. I was bleeding on a deck when they came. Whatever paper Oduya holds on me, it's still paper. That's the thing about men like him. The debt was never the point. The leash was. A man who's paid off is a man who's free, and Oduya doesn't sell freedom. He'll have a new price. There's always a new price."

"Then you're still on the leash," Oscar said.

"I'm still on the leash." Brannoch said it flat, and it was the truest sentence he'd spoken in forty years. "Which is why I'm telling you this now, while I'm laid up and useless and can't do any more damage. Because the next time Oduya's hand pulls that leash, I don't know what I'll do. I'd like to tell you I'd hang myself before I'd sell you again. But I told myself I'd never light that lamp, either. A man my age should know better than to make promises about his own courage. Courage is weather. It comes and it goes."

Silence. The rain eased a little, shifting to that long steady fall the monsoon did between squalls, and the river's voice came up underneath it, patient and enormous.

It was Oscar who moved first. Brannoch had expected anger—had, in his bleakest arithmetic, expected Oscar to walk out and take Ilse with him and leave an old man to heal alone in a mission clinic, which would have been no more than fair. Instead Oscar pulled the chair closer, the one Ilse had slept in, and sat down on its edge, forearms on knees, close enough that Brannoch could see the lamplight in their eyes.

"When I was sixteen," Oscar said, "I flew for a man who beat me at cards every week and docked my pay for it. I stayed two years. You know why?"

Brannoch shook his head.

"Because he was the first one who let me near a plane, and I thought that debt was worth anything. I thought owing someone was the same as belonging to them." Oscar's voice was level, but there was something underneath it, the old river under the ice. "It took me a long time to learn the difference. And I learned it from a magistrate who pulled me out of a wreck, so believe me, I know how it feels to owe a man your life and watch him spend it."

"Oscar—" Brannoch started.

"Let me finish. I'm not good at this. I get one try." Oscar looked down at their hands a moment, then back up. "You sold us. That's true, and I'm not going to sand it down for you, because you didn't sand it down for us, and that's the only reason we're having this conversation at all. Most men would've taken it to their graves. Most men on this river have taken worse to their graves. You could've waited until your shoulder healed. You could've waited until Oduya's next leash-pull made it convenient. Instead you're telling us while you're helpless, which is the hardest time to tell a thing, and the only time it counts."

Brannoch's eyes were burning. He blamed the carbolic.

"So here's what I know," Oscar went on. "You bought a rotten boat and sold it to us like it was a bargain, and it was a bargain, because that rotten boat is the only reason any of us are alive. You bailed all night with your hands until they bled. You bluffed a checkpoint with forged papers and brandy so bad it could strip paint. And when the shooting started, you didn't run—you went *toward* the fire, for a woman you'd known a season, and caught a bullet for it." Oscar's jaw tightened. "I don't know what to do with a man like that. Neither do you. That's the whole problem with us, isn't it? None of us is clean. Ilse lied to me the first day she boarded. I've lied to both of you about half my life. And you—" the ghost of something crossed their face, not quite a smile, "—you're the most honest liar I've ever met."

"So what are you saying?" Brannoch managed. His voice had gone rough as river gravel.

"I'm saying the debt's paid." Oscar said it simply, the way they said everything that mattered. "Not because the bullet bought it—blood doesn't balance books, you said so yourself, and you were right. It's paid because you told the truth when it cost the most, and because a family that can't survive a betrayal isn't a family, it's a cargo manifest. We're not cargo, Brannoch. Not to each other."

Ilse made a sound. Small. Brannoch turned his head and saw her press the back of her hand to her mouth, and he understood that he was seeing something almost nobody on this river had ever seen: Ilse Vessany—whatever her true name was—crying. Not for the grandmother in the ledgers. Not for the burned villages. For a one-eyed old smuggler on a cot.

"You're both idiots," Brannoch said, and his voice broke clean in half on the second word.

"Probably," Ilse said, wetly, and then she laughed, and then he was laughing too, which hurt the shoulder so badly he saw stars, and he laughed anyway, because the alternative was weeping and he had a reputation, such as it was.

Oscar put one hand on his ankle, over the blanket—the kind of touch that said *I'm here, I'm not leaving* without spending a word on it—and said, "But understand me. Oduya still holds your paper. Which means every mile downriver, we're sailing with a hole in the hull we can't see. So from here to Rivergate, you tell us. Anything he asks, anything he offers, any leash he pulls—you bring it to this family first. Not because we'll decide for you. Because you don't carry it alone anymore. That's the whole deal. That's the only deal."

Brannoch looked at the ceiling, at the water stain that had finally resolved itself into something like a hand, open, palm up. Giving or asking; he supposed it had always been both.

"Deal," he said. And then, because the old architecture of jokes was still standing, barely: "Though I'll warn you, the interest on *this* arrangement is going to be murder."

"It always is," Ilse said. She reached over and squeezed his good hand once, hard, and let go, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was enough.

* * *

Sister Ameline chased them out at midnight, in the way of mission sisters on every river in the world—gently, absolutely, with a lantern and no discussion. Oscar and Ilse went to the veranda of the pilot's shack behind the clinic where the mission let travelers sleep, and Brannoch heard them talking low through the wall, and then he heard nothing but rain.

He slept, finally, without arithmetic. It was the first time in forty years.

He woke before dawn to the sound of an engine on the river—not the putter of a trading boat but the low, disciplined thrum of something government-built, and he was upright before he remembered the shoulder, and the pain dropped him back onto the cot with a grunt that brought Oscar through the door in three strides, knife already in hand.

"Easy," Oscar said. "I see it."

Through the window, gray in the gray light, a patrol boat came up the channel flying no colors at all, which on this river meant one of two things: cartel, or magistrate. It slowed at the clinic dock. A single figure stepped off—a tall figure, moving carefully, soaked to the knees, one arm bound in a sling made of what looked like torn shirt.

Brannoch squinted against the dawn. Then he started to laugh, which hurt, and he laughed anyway.

"Well," he said. "Either the river's finally gone mad, or that's Corven's enforcer coming up our dock with her tail between her legs—and by the look of her, she didn't swim here by choice."

Oscar was already moving for the door.
