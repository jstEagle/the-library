## 28. Corven in Person

The river went glass-still an hour before he came.

Oscar noticed it the way a pilot notices a change in engine note—not with the ears but with the spine, some older instrument that lived below thought. The monsoon had blown itself out two days past, and what remained was this: a wide brown reach of water between drowned hills, mist standing on it in columns like the ghosts of smokestacks, and the patched boat moving through it at Brannoch's careful half-throttle because the starboard shaft bearing had begun to sing a note none of them liked.

Ilse sat amidships with the transcription pages wrapped in oilcloth against her chest even in her sleep. She'd taken to sleeping that way after the fuel dock, after Brannoch's signal, after the fire—curled around the copies like a dog around a bone she expected to be stolen. Brannoch stood at the wheel with his bandaged shoulder propped against the frame, one eye on the channel markers and one eye, as always now, on Oscar, reading forgiveness or its absence in every glance.

The stillness came down over all of it like a lid.

"River's holding its breath," Brannoch said quietly.

"I know."

"Nothing good holds its breath, boy. Only things waiting to strike, or things about to die."

Oscar didn't answer. They were two days from Rivergate by water if the rapids behaved, four if they didn't, and the hearings would open whether they arrived or not. Every mile had felt borrowed since the fire. Now the river itself seemed to agree.

It was Ilse who saw him first. She woke without a sound—that particular skill of hers, surfacing from sleep like something rising through deep water—and looked astern, and said, "Boat," in a voice that made both men turn.

* * *

It was a skiff. That was the wrongness of it, the thing that stopped Oscar's breath in their throat. Not a patrol launch, not Sable's black-hulled gunboat with its twin engines and its air of institutional menace. A skiff. Wooden, old, painted the faded green of government surplus forty years out of service, with a single oarsman sitting amidships pulling easy, unhurried strokes.

And standing in the bow, one hand on the painter, gray-haired and straight-backed in a plain traveling coat, Magistrate Aldous Corven.

Alone.

"He's mad," Brannoch breathed. "He's come out here alone. He's either mad or—" The old trader's eye narrowed. "Or he wants us to know he's not afraid of what we'll do. Which is worse."

Ilse had the pistol out of the satchel before Oscar could speak. Oscar put a hand over it, gently, and pressed it down flat against the boards.

"No."

"You don't know that he's alone. You don't know what's behind that mist—"

"I know." And Oscar did know, in the way they knew weather and current and the weight of cargo, the way they knew the sound a friend makes when he steps onto your wing. "That's his own boat. He used to take me up the Vessany channels in it. Before." They swallowed. "He'd want to come alone. It's the point of it. If you can't trust a man who comes to you unarmed, he figures, then there was never anything to offer you at all."

"That's exactly the kind of arithmetic that gets people killed," Ilse said, but she let the pistol go back into the satchel, and she moved to stand at the rail beside Oscar, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was its own kind of statement.

Brannoch cut the throttle. The boat glided, ticking, losing way. The skiff came on through the mist with its single oar knocking softly against the rowlock, and the silence got bigger and bigger until it seemed to have weight, until Oscar could feel it pressing on them like altitude.

Corven brought the skiff alongside with the ease of a man who had done ten thousand landings on ten thousand banks. He didn't ask permission. He simply laid a hand on the rail, and for a moment—the space of three heartbeats—he and Oscar looked at each other across eighteen inches of wet wood and thirty years of debt.

He had aged. That was the first shock. In Oscar's memory Corven was iron: the magistrate who had waded into burning wreckage at Kettle Reach and dragged a seventeen-year-old apprentice pilot out of a cockpit full of smoke, who had signed the papers that gave that nobody a license and a route and a reason to keep living. Iron didn't age. But the man gripping the rail now had a face like a riverbank in dry season, cracked and settled, and his eyes were the same pale gray they had always been, and they were looking at Oscar with something that no enforcer, no summons, no letter had ever carried in them.

Something that looked almost like grief.

"Hello, Yarrow," he said.

"Magistrate."

"Corven," he corrected softly. "I'm not wearing the office today. Just the coat." He stepped aboard without ceremony, nodded once to Brannoch—who touched his cap brim with the wary courtesy of one riverman to another—and inclined his head to Ilse, who stared back with her grandmother's stubbornness written all over her face. "Miss Vessany-Errai. Or should I say, Miss Errai. Your family name has been a great deal of trouble to a great many people."

"It's been trouble to the people who erased it," Ilse said.

"Yes," Corven said. "It has." He said it without flinching, and that frightened Oscar more than any threat could have.

* * *

They drifted. Nobody suggested otherwise; the current took the boat sideways into the slack water behind a drowned willow, and there they hung, mist folding around them, the world reduced to a circle fifty yards across with five people in it—one rowing slowly in a figure eight to hold position, one at the wheel pretending not to listen, and three in the middle of everything, arranged around a sealed chest like mourners around a coffin.

Corven looked at the chest for a long time. He didn't touch it.

"Do you know why I pulled you out of that wreck?" he asked suddenly, turning to Oscar. "At Kettle Reach. Everyone assumed it was duty. A magistrate's obligation. It wasn't."

"You never said why."

"No. I never said." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin as paper. "Because I'd been flying that same mail run myself, twenty years before you were born. Before the maps changed. I knew every bend of it, and I knew what it does to a person—the fog, the loneliness, the way you start believing you don't deserve to land anywhere twice." His voice dropped. "I watched you build yourself into exactly the kind of pilot I'd been. Never the same field twice. No names held too close. Cargo only, nothing that could anchor you. I told myself it was wisdom. I told myself I'd given you freedom."

"And now?"

"Now I think I taught you how to disappear, and called it saving you." He turned fully to face Oscar, and the mist moved behind him like a curtain. "I've come to make you an offer, Yarrow. My last one. And I want you to hear all of it before you answer, because I will only say it once, and because—for whatever my word is worth to you now—there is no threat attached to it. None. What happens after is out of my hands either way."

"Nothing's ever out of your hands," Ilse said.

"Everything," Corven said quietly, "has been out of my hands for forty years. That is rather the entire problem."

* * *

He spoke the way he must have spoken in courtrooms, in council chambers, in the closed rooms where the Provision's fate had been decided over brandy while the river towns ate boiled grass. Measured. Unhurried. Each sentence set down like a stone in a wall.

"The fund was real," he said. "Understand that first. Whatever else you've read in those ledgers, understand that the Provision began as the truest thing this country ever built. Grain for the flooded districts. Clinics on every reach. Schools where there had been nothing but reeds and fever. I was a young clerk when it started. I believed in it the way you believe in sunrise." His hand tightened on the rail. "And then the war came, and the maps changed, and Vessany became a name we weren't allowed to say, and the money kept flowing—but into whose hands? Into the hands of the families who wrote the new maps. My family among them. Yes." He met Ilse's stare without blinking. "My name is in that ledger too. Page eleven, if your grandmother transcribed faithfully. I signed disbursements I knew were theft. I signed them because the alternative was to be removed, replaced by someone worse, and to lose whatever small power I had to steer what remained."

"What remained," Ilse repeated. Her voice was flat as slate. "You mean the schools. The clinics. We've seen them. We stopped at Marrow Bend. Beautiful buildings. Full of children." She leaned forward. "Built on grain that never reached the villages that starved. My grandmother's village. The one that isn't on the map anymore. Did you sign that one too, Magistrate? Was that also steering what remained?"

The blow landed. Oscar saw it land—saw the crack move through the old man's composure like ice shifting on a pond. But he didn't look away.

"Yes," he said. "I signed that one too."

Nobody spoke. The oar knocked. Somewhere far off, hidden in the mist, a bird cried twice and quit.

"So you know what I am," Corven went on, and his voice had gone rougher now, less courtroom, more riverbank. "I have not come to argue otherwise. I've come because in nine days the hearings open in Rivergate, and there are two ways they end. In one, that chest arrives, and the ledgers are read aloud, and every name on every page becomes a weapon—and not just mine. Yours too, Miss Errai. Your grandmother smuggled state property out of a collapsing nation. Do you imagine the families will let that be framed as heroism? They will make her a thief. They will make you a smuggler. They will make the boy—" he glanced at Oscar, and something in his face twisted— "they will make the pilot who ferried it all an accessory, because that is what committees do with inconvenient people. The truth will come out, yes. Shredded, diluted, buried under procedure, and everyone who carried it will be ruined as a bonus."

"And the other way?" Oscar asked. Their voice sounded strange to them, very quiet in all that mist.

"The other way," Corven said, "the chest goes into the river tonight, at this spot, where no one will ever find it. The copies burn. The hearings open and find nothing, and close, and the Provision stays a fond memory in the schoolbooks. And in exchange—" he drew a folded paper from his coat, and held it out, and did not step closer— "this."

Oscar didn't take it. So he set it on the chest and opened it flat, and read it aloud anyway, as though he needed to hear his own voice commit to it.

"A full pardon for every debt on your record, Yarrow. The fines from the Kettle Reach incident—expunged. The grounding order from the Halverton year—voided. Your license restored without condition, in your own name, which I notice you haven't used on a manifest in six years." Oscar flinched. He saw it. He continued, gentler. "A hangar at Rivergate Field, deeded outright. A stipend from funds that—I'll be honest—are themselves stolen, so at least they'd be stolen honestly for once. And—" 

He stopped. For the first time, the courtroom voice failed him.

"And the record corrected," he said. "Kettle Reach. The official file says pilot error. It says the instructor, Tomas Wrenn, attempted a rescue he was not qualified for and died as a result of his own miscalculation." He looked up, and his gray eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them. "I can't bring him back. No one can. But I can rewrite what the river towns believe about him. I can give him back his name. Every child at every flying field from here to the delta would grow up knowing that Tomas Wrenn flew into a storm to save his student, and succeeded, and died in the trying—and that his student spent the rest of his life worthy of it. That is what I'm offering. Silence, wealth, and a dead man's honor restored."

The mist drifted. The boat rocked. Somewhere inside Oscar, in the place where the fog always lived, something enormous and slow turned over.

* * *

Tomas Wrenn. Twenty-two years dead, and the name still landed in Oscar's chest like a dropped wrench.

They had never told anyone the whole of it—not Brannoch, not Ilse, not the priests at the mission clinics or the traders at the fuel docks. That Oscar had begged Wrenn to fly that day. That the storm report had been ambiguous and the medicine urgent and the student impatient, and Wrenn had looked at the sky and then at the kid in the passenger seat and said, *well, someone's got to teach you what weather really looks like,* and grinned, and rolled the plane out. That the last thing Oscar heard before the world went white was Wrenn's voice on the radio, calm as Sunday, talking them down through a channel no one had ever survived. That the inquiry had blamed the instructor because blaming the orphaned apprentice would have been indecent, and Oscar had let them, because the alternative was admitting that the storm had been survivable and the decision to enter it had been theirs.

That every departure since—every field left unlearned, every name held at arm's length, every rule carved into the bones—had been penance performed in motion.

"You think I don't know what that name costs you," Corven said softly. "I've watched you carry it for twenty years, Yarrow. I've watched you refuse every home field, every steady job, every hand held out. I offered you this same bargain once, in smaller letters—a clean record, a fresh start—and you took it, and I told myself I'd saved you." He shook his head slowly. "I'm offering it again in large ones. Take it. Go be a pilot with a name and a hangar and a town that knows your story. Let the dead rest. Let the river keep what the river keeps."

Oscar looked at the paper lying open on the chest. The handwriting was beautiful. It was always beautiful. Even the sabotage orders, probably. Even the letter that had promised to bury the past.

Then they looked at Ilse.

She was watching them, and she wasn't pleading. That was the thing about Ilse—she never pleaded. She simply stood there with her grandmother's oilcloth bundle against her ribs and her chin up and her eyes saying: *whatever you choose, I'll row.* And behind her, at the wheel, Brannoch had gone very still, one eye fixed on Oscar with the expression of a man watching another man decide whether to jump.

And Oscar thought about the old woman at Marrow Bend, the one who had wept over the ledger names, who had kissed the page where her village appeared one last time before the ink of history ran. Thought about her hands. Thought about what it would mean for her, for all of them, for every erased name on every drowned map, if this paper were signed and this mist closed over everything forever.

Wrenn would have flown into it. That was the whole of it, wasn't it. That was the man. He flew into weather other people called impossible, carrying things other people called too heavy, for people other people had decided didn't matter. And the official record called it miscalculation.

Oscar reached out and closed the paper. Folded it, once, along Corven's crease. Held it a moment.

Then they tore it in half. Then halves into halves, and let the pieces fall into the brown water, where they lay flat for a moment like lilies and then darkened and sank.

"Tomas Wrenn doesn't need the record corrected," Oscar said. Their voice didn't shake, which surprised them. "He needs the truth next to it. He flew into that storm because I begged him to, and he knew it was bad, and he came anyway, because that's who he was—and the reason the record lies is the same reason your ledgers lie, Magistrate. Because somebody powerful needed the story to end differently than it did." They stepped closer, close enough to smell the river on the old man's coat, close enough to see that his hands, hanging at his sides, had gone white-knuckled. "You saved my life. I know it. I'll say it at the hearings, in front of everyone, if it comes to that—you pulled me out of the fire and gave me the sky. I have loved you for twenty years the way you love a father you can't forgive. But you're asking me to trade every village that starved for one dead man's name, and he would rather rot unnamed in that channel for eternity than have me make that trade. He flew *toward*, Corven. All his life, toward. And you're asking me to be the man who finally taught me to fly away."

The mist held still around them, as if the river itself were listening.

Corven looked down at the water where the pieces of his offer had vanished. When he raised his head, all the magistrate had gone out of his face, and what remained was just an old clerk, very tired, standing in a boat on a river he had helped rob.

"Wrenn would be proud of you," he said. "That's the cruelest thing anyone's ever said to me, and I said it to myself." He straightened his coat. "Then it's done. Hear what happens now, and remember I warned you. I won't order anything—I lost that authority somewhere in the last hundred miles, or perhaps I surrendered it when I chose to come alone. But there are others who answer to my name whether I will it or not, and they know where you are, because I was followed out of Rivergate, and I was permitted to follow you." His eyes went briefly, terribly, toward the mist astern. "You have until nightfall. Maybe less. Get downriver. Don't stop. And Yarrow—" he paused at the rail, one leg already over, an old man making an old crossing— "when they read the names aloud in that hall, read mine loudest. Don't soften it. I'd rather be damned accurately than forgiven generously."

He stepped down into the skiff. The oarsman turned them, unhurried, and the green boat slid back into the mist from which it had come, and the sound of the oar knocked twice, three times, and faded, and the river closed over the place where he had been as though he had never existed—which, Oscar thought, was precisely what he had spent forty years doing to other people.

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved.

Then Brannoch cleared his throat, spat ceremonially over the side, and said, "Well. Either that was the most honest man on this river or the best liar, and I've known enough of both to tell you it changes nothing. Nightfall, he said."

"He said he was followed," Ilse said. She was already moving, unwrapping the oilcloth, checking the pages, her hands quick and certain. "Which means whoever followed him knows exactly where we are. Which means—" She stopped. Looked up at Oscar. "We need to know what's behind us. Right now."

Oscar climbed to the wheel beside Brannoch and looked back down the long grey reach they had crossed, into the standing columns of mist.

Far off—so far it might have been light on water, might have been a trick of the dying afternoon—something moved against the haze. Low. Black. Moving fast.

Coming on.

"Brannoch," Oscar said quietly. "How much river is there between here and the Two Sisters rapids?"

The old trader's eye measured the distance, and the light, and the singing bearing, and the sky going the color of a bruise.

"Not near enough," he said, and pushed the throttle forward until the whole boat shuddered awake.
