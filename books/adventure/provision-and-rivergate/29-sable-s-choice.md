## 29. Sable's Choice

The order came at dusk, written in Corven's own hand, which was how Sable knew it would never be spoken aloud.

*The boat will pass the Narrows before the second bell. See that it does not arrive.*

No name on the boat. No name for what *see that it does not arrive* meant. Forty years of service had taught her the grammar of such sentences: the missing words were always the same ones, and they were always hers to fill in.

Sable read it twice by lamplight in the patrol boat's cabin, then held the page over the flame and watched Corven's careful hand curl into black lace and rise. The ash smelled like a burned letter, which was all it was, which was all it had ever needed to be.

Outside, the river ran high with monsoon water, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past the mooring pilings as if the current were showing off what it could take. Rain came and went in long gray curtains. Somewhere upriver, a leaking boat carried a pilot who owed her nothing, a woman full of other people's secrets, and an old smuggler with a bullet hole in him—and somewhere behind them, in a district where the streetlamps still worked because his office paid for the oil, Magistrate Corven sat waiting to be told it was done.

She had pulled people from this river. She had pulled Oscar Yarrow from worse than water.

Sable banked the lamp low, checked her sidearm out of habit rather than intention, and did not load it.

* * *

She had been Corven's enforcer for eleven years, and before that she had been his shadow for six more, and before *that*—before any of it—she had been a dock girl in Two Sisters with salt-cracked hands and a father who drank the ferry money. Corven had found her at fourteen, caught stealing from a Provision warehouse, and instead of calling the constables he had crouched down to her height, looked at the tin of nails under her arm, and said: *Hungry, or building something?*

Building, she'd said. A raft.

He'd given her the nails. He'd given her work. He'd given her, over the years, a version of herself she could live inside: capable, feared, necessary. The magistrate's right hand. The woman who went where the law got muddy. She had believed, for a long time, that the mud was on other people's boots.

Then came the chest, and the ledgers, and the list of names, and the slow terrible arithmetic of everything she had enforced.

She had boarded their boat at the checkpoint weeks ago and let them go, and she had told herself it was strategy—let them run, follow them to whoever they met, map the whole conspiracy in one clean sweep. That had been true, mostly. But there had been a moment on that deck, looking into Yarrow's face across the gunwale, when she had understood that she was not hunting them so much as escorting them toward something, the way you walk a condemned man to the scaffold and find yourself hoping, against your post, for a pardon.

Now the order in her hands asked her to be the scaffold.

* * *

The Narrows at night were a place the river made for killing. The channel pinched between two rock shoulders, the current doubled, and in monsoon season the eddies below the second shoulder could hold a hull and turn it, slowly, like a hand considering an apple. Every smuggler on the river knew them. Every honest pilot dreaded them. Corven knew them too—he had grown up on this water, before the maps changed, back when Vessany was still a country and the Provision was still a promise.

That was the thing Sable kept circling, these last weeks, the way the eddies circled their drowned trees: Corven had been young once. He had signed those pages not as an old man protecting his comforts but as a rising one, hungry, clever, certain the money would do more good in his hands than in the fund's. And some of it had. Schools. Clinics. The hospital at Rivergate with its clean white walls. She had seen the ledgers' terrible double columns—the towns starved on one side, the towns raised on the other—and understood that corruption, done at scale, could wear the face of generosity for forty years.

It didn't make it less theft. It just made it theft with architecture.

Her patrol boat idled above the Narrows, lights doused, riding the rain. Below, around the first bend, the patched-up riverboat would be coming—she'd tracked its progress through three informants and one very frightened fuel-dock boy. Brannoch at the tiller, if he could still stand; the woman Ilse navigating; Yarrow wherever Yarrow was most useful, which was everywhere, because that was the kind of pilot they were. The kind who landed twice in the same place for people, eventually. The kind who broke their own rules.

Sable stood at the rail and let the rain hit her face until she was soaked through, because cold water clarifies, her first sergeant used to say, and he had been right about almost nothing else.

Here was what she knew that Corven did not: she had already sent word to Rivergate. Three days ago, through a midwife in Two Sisters who owed her a debt older than her service—a route schedule, copied in her own hand from Corven's private files. The dates the annex records would be moved. The nights the burn permits had been quietly issued. The names of the clerks assigned to feed the fire. It was sitting now, wrapped in oilcloth, in the hands of someone whose only loyalty was to a birth registry, traveling toward the hearing commission like blood returning to a limb.

She had not decided, when she sent it, whether she was betraying him or saving him. She still hadn't. Both things felt true, the way both columns in the ledger felt true.

But the order in the ash had decided the rest of it. There is a difference between enforcing a man's law and being his knife, and she had crossed it years ago without noticing, and tonight the knife was being asked to cut the only people on this river who were trying to put the ledger back in balance.

* * *

They came around the bend at the second bell, exactly as predicted, running dark except for a single shielded lantern at the bow.

Sable watched them enter the Narrows through her glass: the boat wallowing low, listing slightly to port, Brannoch visible at the tiller with his arm bound tight against his ribs, and beside him a figure she recognized by posture alone—Yarrow, leaning into the current like the boat was an extension of their body, reading water the way other people read weather. Behind them, Ilse, bailing. Even at this distance Sable could see the rhythm of it: dip, heave, dip, heave. The boat was taking water faster than one pair of arms could argue with.

All she had to do was nothing.

That was the elegance of the order, and she admired it even as it sickened her. No shot fired, no blade drawn. Just a patrol boat parked above the worst water on the river, its presence enough to keep them in the channel instead of risking the shallows, its silence enough to guarantee no warning reached them about the deadfall log the flood had wedged across the lower throat two days ago—one of her men had reported it, and the report had gone into Corven's file, and no buoy had ever been set. The river would do the work. The river left no fingerprints.

Sable looked at the lantern bobbing in the dark. She thought about the wreck she'd found weeks ago—not her doing, another hunter entirely, the cartel's people—and how she'd stood in the floating debris and felt, for the first time in eleven years, like a woman standing inside her own future.

Then she did the thing that could not be undone.

She lit the signal lamp. Three flashes, red, the old Two Sisters code every river kid learned before they learned letters: *deadfall ahead, keep to the starboard shelf.*

For a moment, nothing. The lantern below kept its steady bob. Then it swung hard, once, twice—an acknowledgment—and the boat heeled over, engine straining, clawing toward the starboard shelf where the current was ugly but survivable.

And from the darkness upstream, answering her light, came a second lamp. Green. Three flashes.

Not Yarrow's boat. Something bigger, running without running lights until that moment, and the green code was wrong—old cartel grammar, the kind that meant *we see you*—and Sable's stomach dropped through the deck plates as she understood, all at once, what she had just done. She had shown herself. To them. The third party, the hunters who had wrecked their own patrol boat upriver, who wanted the chest badly enough to kill for it, who now knew exactly where the magistrate's enforcer was and exactly what she'd just revealed herself to be.

The big boat's searchlight snapped on and swept the water, and found her.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do, in pieces.

The cartel boat opened up—not at Yarrow, not yet, but at her, raking the Narrows with a long burst that chewed the rail off her patrol boat's port side and punched holes in the cabin wall an inch above where she'd been standing. She went down behind the engine housing as splinters sang past her ears, and through the thunder she heard the riverboat below do the smartest thing possible: kill its lantern and run for the starboard shelf, trusting the dark, trusting the code, trusting—for reasons she could not fathom—a woman who had hunted them for weeks.

Bullets hammered the water. Sable crawled aft, started her engines, and put her nose downstream, straight into the fight's geometry, because there was one move left and it was a stupid one: get between the cartel boat and the Narrows. Make herself the target. Buy ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds was a life on this river. Ninety seconds was the width of a channel.

She came out of the dark at full throttle with her own horn screaming, and the cartel boat—surprised, greedy, committed to the kill—swung to meet her, and for eleven seconds the two boats closed on each other through the rain like animals deciding, and then everyone blinked. They sheered off. She sheered off. The riverboat, in that gap, slipped past the deadfall with barely a hand's width to spare—Sable saw it in her mirror, saw the hull kiss the log and grind along it and live—and vanished into the black water below the Narrows, running free.

Free. The word sat strangely in her chest.

The cartel boat did not pursue her. It turned downriver after the smaller prize, and Sable pushed her throttles until the engines screamed, cutting the angle, staying between, playing shepherd to sheep she'd been ordered to slaughter while wolves closed from behind. For twenty minutes they ran that way through the monsoon dark, three vessels strung along the current like beads on a wire, until the cartel boat, deeper-drafted and heavier, lost the channel in the flooded shallows and grounded hard enough that Sable heard the impact a mile away—a crack like a door closing.

After that, the river belonged to the rain again.

* * *

She found them at dawn, beached in a reed cut below the Narrows, patching the hull by gray light. She came in slow, hands visible, weapon holstered and—this time deliberately—unloaded, the magazine in her pocket where they could see her leave it on the thwart.

Brannoch met her at the gunwale with a boathook and the expression of a man who had buried better surprises. "You've got nerve," he said, "coming here."

"I've got information," Sable said. "The nerve is incidental."

Yarrow stood up from the hull patching, hands slick with pitch, and looked at her for a long moment. There was no gratitude in the look, and Sable respected that. Gratitude was for gifts. What Sable had done at the Narrows wasn't a gift—it was a debt payment, decades late, to a river that had been keeping accounts.

"You flashed the old code," Yarrow said. "Two Sisters red."

"My father ferried there." She shrugged, and the shrug cost more than she let it. "I gave Corven eleven years. Last night I gave the river thirty seconds. I'm trying to balance."

Ilse emerged from the cabin, wary as a cat, and Sable took the folded papers from inside her coat—dry, because she'd kept them dry the way she kept everything, against all sense, against all odds. Corven's route schedule. The annex rotation. The burn permits, initialed. The clerk assignments. Everything the hearings would need to catch the fire before it caught the records.

"Rivergate has a copy," she said. "Through the midwife network. This is yours—in case mine doesn't arrive. In case I don't." She set the packet on the thwart between them, and her hand stayed on it one breath longer than it needed to. "He'll know by tonight what I did. He doesn't send second orders. He sends people."

"Then don't be here when they come," Brannoch said.

"No," Sable agreed. "I won't be."

She stepped back onto her own deck. The rain had thinned to mist, and the light was going gold at the edges, and downriver, faint as a memory, lay the city where everything would be decided. She looked at the three of them—the pilot, the thief's granddaughter, the old smuggler with his arm strapped to his ribs—and understood that she was looking at the thing Corven had built his whole life to avoid: witnesses. Living, stubborn, unkillable witnesses, floating down his river with his signature in their cargo hold.

"The hearings open in four days," she said. "He'll contest jurisdiction. He'll contest provenance. He'll stand there in his good coat and be reasonable, and half the commission will want to believe him, because believing him is cheaper than the alternative." She untied the line. "So bring everything. Bring copies. Bring originals. Bring the old woman from the village who wept, if you can. Make it too heavy to bury."

"And you?" Yarrow asked. "Where does an enforcer go?"

Sable considered the question honestly, which was more than she'd given it in eleven years.

"Somewhere with a different river," she said.

She cast off. As her boat swung into the current, she heard Brannoch call something after her—half blessing, half insult, the traditional dialect of the Rivergate—and she raised one hand without turning around, because some farewells are easier in silhouette.

She did not go downriver. She turned upriver, into the mist, toward the erased villages and the country that used to be Vessany, carrying nothing but her sidearm, her regrets, and a working knowledge of every back channel the law had forgotten. If Corven sent people after her—and he would—they would find a woman who knew how to disappear, because she had spent her whole career teaching other people how.

By nightfall she was fifty miles north, trading her patrol boat for a fisherman's skiff and her name for no name at all. By the following dusk, word reached her in a river tavern, passed mouth to ear by a barge cook who asked nothing: the packet had arrived. The commission had it. The hearings would proceed.

Sable bought one glass of the terrible local brandy, drank it standing, and walked out into the rain a free woman—which is to say, a hunted one, which is to say, finally, honest.

Behind her, downriver, the last rapids waited, and beyond them a burning city, and beyond that a courtroom where a pilot who had spent a lifetime refusing to land was about to plant both feet on solid ground and speak.

She hoped the ground held.

She had reason to believe it would. She'd seen what Yarrow's hands could do with a hull, a channel, a chance. Some people, she thought, stepping into the skiff, are built for departures. And some—against every rule they ever wrote for themselves—are built, at the very end, for arrival.
