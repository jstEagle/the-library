## 30. The Last Rapids

The river above Two Sisters had a sound I had never heard it make before. Not the low conversational mutter of a channel at flood, not the slap and suck of water against a hull. This was a roar, continuous and animal, coming up through the boat's ribs like something enormous breathing under the water. The monsoon had swollen the Rivergate past every mark Brannoch had ever known, and the last forty miles to the city funneled through a gorge the old pilots called the Throat.

We were going to sail through it in a patched boat with a dying engine and a man who could barely stand.

"Say it again," I said, because I needed to hear it one more time. "The order of the channels."

Brannoch sat propped against the wheelhouse door with his bandages leaking rust at the edges, one eye closed against the spray, the good one fixed on the water ahead. He'd spread a chart on the deck planks the night before and drawn the rapids from memory—every rock, every eddy, every lie the river told—using a carpenter's pencil and the back of a flour sack. The chart was soaked now, the pencil lines bleeding gray, but he didn't need it. He'd been running cargo up and down this water since before I was born. The map was in him.

"Green water left of the split rock," he said. "Always left, no matter what it looks like. The right channel's a graveyard—three wrecks I know of, maybe four." He coughed, wet-sounding, and I pretended not to hear it. "Then the river bends hard at the burnt landing. You'll want to hug the far bank through the bend, because there's a ledge under the surface that'll open this boat like a tin of sardines. After the bend, the water goes wide and slow for half a mile. That's your rest. Breathe there. Because after that—"

"After that, the Throat."

"After that, the Throat." He looked at me with the one eye. "And I won't lie to you, Yarrow. I've been through her eleven times in forty years. I've seen her eat boats that were better than this one."

I looked at the boat. She was a shame and a wonder both—hull planks scarfed and nailed over the fire damage, seams caulked with tar and prayer, the engine hood wired shut where shrapnel had peeled it back, the wheel wrapped in rope because the wood had split. Ilse had sewn the canvas awning herself with sail twine and a bent needle, and it flapped now in the wind off the gorge like something trying to escape. She'd named the boat, in the end. *Provision.* Brannoch had laughed until his stitches pulled.

"She'll hold," Ilse said from the bow. She said it the way she said everything—as a fact she'd decided in advance and would not be argued with. She had the chest between her feet and one hand on the line she'd rigged to the anchor. Her hair was plastered to her face and she hadn't bothered with it in two days. "She held at Two Sisters. She held through the cartel's wake. She'll hold."

"She held at Two Sisters because the cartel's boats were shooting wide," Brannoch said. "The river doesn't shoot wide."

The rain came sideways off the gorge walls, warm and relentless, the monsoon in full voice now. The banks had vanished days ago—whole villages gone to their rooftops, cattle swimming, the river a brown sea wearing the landscape like a drowned man wears his clothes. Somewhere behind us, Sable's warning was still true: Corven's men would be burning records in Rivergate, and every hour we lost was a page gone to ash. Somewhere ahead, the hearings. The chest between Ilse's feet. The truth in a boat held together with tar.

I put my hand on the throttle. The engine coughed, caught, settled into its broken rhythm—*put-put-CLANK*—a heartbeat with a limp.

"Brannoch," I said. "You should be lying down."

"I should be dead," he said. "I'm not. Sit me by the wheel and shut up." He dragged himself up onto the crate I'd placed there, wedged himself against the wheelhouse frame, and pointed his chin downriver. "Green water left of the split rock. Take us in, pilot."

* * *

The first mile was almost kind, which was how the river lied.

Water the color of strong tea, moving fast but smooth, carrying us on the flood like it wanted to help. I let the current do the work and saved the engine, and for a few minutes I almost believed the day might be survivable. Ilse came aft and crouched beside me at the wheel, shouting over the wind.

"The copies are in oilcloth," she said. "In my shirt. The chest is—"

"I know what the chest is."

"The chest can sink," she said. "The copies can't. If it comes to that, you take me and you let it go."

I looked at her. Rain ran off my chin. "You've spent six years chasing that chest."

"I spent six years chasing the truth. The chest is just the box it came in." She gripped my shoulder, once, hard, and went forward again to her post.

The split rock announced itself as a white scar in the middle of the current, water parting around it in two long V's of foam. And the river, true to form, made the left channel look like the wrong one—narrow, dark, the green water Brannoch had promised barely visible under a skin of churn. Everything in me that had ever flown a plane wanted the wide right channel, the open water, the room to maneuver. Twenty years of instinct said *go where there's space.*

"Left," Brannoch said quietly. "Green water. Trust me."

I went left.

The channel was barely wider than the boat. The current took us and *shook* us, the hull slamming off the current seams, spray coming over the bow in solid sheets. Rocks slid past close enough to touch, black and streaming, and the sound was enormous, water roaring off the split rock on our right like a train that never passed. Ilse crouched low over the chest. Brannoch never raised his voice. "Ease her left. Left. There. Hold her there." And I held her there, both hands white on the wheel, the engine screaming, the boat threading the needle while the needle moved.

Then we were through, and the river widened, and my breath came back to me all at once like surfacing.

"Rest water," Brannoch said. "Breathe."

I breathed. Ahead, the burnt landing rose out of the rain—a rotted dock, a chimney standing alone in the trees, the black ghost of a fire forty years old. The Provision had built that landing. The Provision had built half the river, and half the river had burned or rotted or been sold out from under the people it was meant for, and the money that should have rebuilt it sat in the ledgers under Ilse's feet, in the vaults of the families who governed us, signed for by the man who once pulled me out of a wreck and gave me my first job.

"Far bank through the bend," Brannoch said. "Ledge under the surface. Hug it close and don't flinch."

* * *

The bend nearly killed us.

The current at the burnt landing swung the bow out before I could correct, and for one long second we were broadside to the flood, the whole river leaning its weight against our flank, the water pouring over the gunwale on the low side in a green cataract. Ilse went down on her knees over the chest. Brannoch grabbed the wheel with me, his hands over mine, his weight half mine to carry, and we hauled her around together, the engine at full throttle, the prop churning air and water both, until the bow came round and bit and we were pointing downriver again with the ledge a pale seam of broken water off our port side close enough to spit across.

"Close," Brannoch said. It might have been a navigation report. It might have been a prayer.

The rest water after the bend was half a mile of relative calm, and we used it the way he'd told us to. Ilse bailed—she'd been bailing all morning, a dented kettle in her hand, tireless, mechanical, water over the side and back to the bow. I checked the engine, which was running hot, the patched hood ticking like something counting down. Brannoch drank water and ate nothing and watched the river with his one eye, and I saw him doing arithmetic behind it.

"Talk to me," I said, sitting beside him on the crate.

"The Throat's a mile and a half of standing waves," he said. "The drop's not the problem. The drop's nothing—ten feet, maybe twelve, spread over the mile. The problem is the holes. Water goes over a ledge and curls back on itself, and a boat that goes into a hole doesn't come out. She just turns and turns until she's full." He looked at me. "There are four holes I know of. There may be five. The fifth one's new, maybe. The flood moves things."

"How do we miss them?"

"We don't miss them. We read them." He tapped his temple. "A hole looks like a smooth tongue of water coming off a ledge—flat, quiet, almost gentle. The river's best liar. You watch for the smooth water and you go anywhere else. Anywhere else is fine. The smooth water is death." He almost smiled. "Same as people, if you think about it."

I thought about Corven. I thought about his voice on the river, alone in his launch, offering me my old debts and my mentor's memory back like a man laying cards on a table. *The smooth water is death.*

"Brannoch. If we hole—"

"If we hole, you get Ilse on the highest rock you can find and you keep the copies dry, and that's the whole of your duty, and you don't waste one second of it trying to save an old man who's had forty good years on this river and would trade every one of them for what's in that chest." He said it flat, like freight rates. "I've made my will. It's one line. *The boat was named right.*"

"Brannoch—"

"Yarrow." He put his hand on my arm, and it was heavier than a hand should be. "I ran this river for forty years and I never once carried anything worth the carrying. Rum, rifles, tax men, lies. You two came along and put something in my hold worth dying for. That's not a tragedy. That's a windfall. Now—" he nodded forward, and his voice changed, and the old trader was gone and the pilot came up underneath, the way it comes up in all of us when the ground drops away—"there's the sound. You hear it? That's her. That's the Throat."

I heard it. Under the rain and the wind, rising, a sound like the end of the world being rehearsed.

I went to the wheel. Ilse came aft and put her hand flat on the chest. The rain thickened. The gorge walls closed in, black rock streaming, and the river between them went white from bank to bank.

* * *

The Throat did not look like water. It looked like the sky had fallen in and was trying to get back up.

Standing waves the height of the wheelhouse. Foam in ropes and pillars. The whole mile and a half of it boiling, and through the boil, if you knew how to look, the structure—channels, seams, the dark green lines where the water ran deep and true, and between them the smooth tongues Brannoch had promised, flat as glass, lying.

"Read it to me," I said.

"Enter left of the big wave—the one standing alone, like a church." His voice was steady as a compass. "Ride the seam down. There'll be a hole off your right at the halfway mark, smooth water, you'll want it, it's lying. Stay in the rough. Then the river dog-legs right around a submerged shelf—follow the foam, foam marks the deep. Last quarter mile it all funnels left against the cliff. There's an eddy behind the cliff point. If we make the eddy, we're through. If we miss the eddy—"

"What's past the eddy?"

"Rivergate," he said. "Or the bottom of Rivergate."

I put the throttle to the stop. The engine clanked and screamed. The boat leaned into the current like a animal into a harness, and we went in.

The first wave took us and threw us. I have been in weather that bent aircraft. I have landed in crosswinds that tore the doors off their tracks. Nothing in twenty years of flying prepared me for the moment the river picked up forty feet of patched timber and its three passengers and *shook* us, the bow climbing until the sky was all there was, then dropping into the trough with a slam I felt in my teeth, in my spine, in the roots of my hair. Water came over us entire—not spray, not sheets, the *river*, a ton of it at a time, and the boat rose out of each drowning shaking the water off like a dog.

"Seam!" Brannoch roared. "Hold the seam!"

I held the seam. The dark green line ran down the throat of the rapid like a vein, and I flew it the way I'd flown fog-bound channels a thousand times—instruments gone, horizon gone, nothing but the line and the trust. The smooth water slid past on our right, flat and inviting and murderous, and Ilse saw it and shouted something and I shouted back *I know* and we went on in the rough, in the chaos, in the honest violence where the water at least told you what it was doing.

The dog-leg came up fast. The foam marked the deep water around the submerged shelf, a curving ribbon of white, and I followed it, the boat heeling, the patched planks groaning below my feet like a choir in pain. Somewhere in the groaning was a seam letting go. Somewhere in the groaning was the sound of the bilge getting a new friend. Ilse was bailing without being told, kettle flashing, and Brannoch was on his feet—*on his feet*, bandages red, both hands on the wheelhouse frame, half-dead and wholly alive, shouting the river to us like a man introducing old friends by their worst qualities.

"Cliff! Left against the cliff! Eddy behind the point! Everything, Yarrow! Everything she has!"

I gave her everything. The engine screamed its broken scream, the prop bit, the bow swung for the black cliff wall that filled half the world, and for three seconds we were pointed straight at forty feet of wet stone with the whole river shoving us into it, and I did not flinch, because Brannoch had not flinched, because the foam said the deep water ran true to the last moment and then swung.

It swung. The cliff slid past close enough to take paint. And behind the point, sudden as a held breath released, the water went still—the eddy, a great slow whirl of brown calm, and we came into it sideways and around and around, and the roar of the Throat fell behind us like a door closing.

* * *

We drifted in the eddy for a long time. Nobody spoke.

The rain kept falling. The boat turned in its slow circle, and through the canvas I could see the far bank of the river below the rapids—low, flooded, ordinary—and beyond it, faint through the rain, the smoke of a city. Rivergate. Curfew gray, annex burning, hearings waiting. The chest sat between Ilse's feet, streaming water, holding its forty-year-old secret. Brannoch slid down the wheelhouse frame onto the crate, gray as ash, grinning with his one eye.

"Twelve times," he said. "She's never had me yet."

I let go of the wheel. My hands didn't want to open. I had to peel them off finger by finger, and they shook, and I let them shake, because there was no one left to pretend for. Ilse came and stood beside me at the wheel and put her hand over mine, and we watched the smoke of the city grow out of the rain.

"She's shipping water aft," Ilse said finally. Her voice was scraped raw. "Bad seam, starboard quarter. I can caulk it from inside if we beach her."

"Beach her at the mission landing," Brannoch said. "Hour's run with the flood. Then it's carts, or mules, or feet, whatever the mission's got. The annex fire won't wait and neither will the hearings." He looked up at me. "How much fuel in you, Yarrow? In the tank that matters?"

I thought about the wreck I'd been running from for twenty years. The mentor in the channel. The rules: never land twice, never learn names, never carry anything but cargo. All of it burned down to ash somewhere back in the Throat, and what was left was a man with shaking hands and a full hold, pointed at a city that needed him.

"Enough," I said. "Take us in, Brannoch."

He laughed, coughed, laughed again. "Green water left of the split rock," he said, and pointed downriver at the smoke, and I put the wheel over, and the *Provision*—patched, drowning, named right—turned her bow for home.

Then Ilse went still at the bow. She was looking back upriver, at the Throat, at the white chaos we'd come out of, and her hand came up slow and pointed.

"Oscar," she said. "There's a boat in the rapids."

I turned. Far up the boiling mile, small as a toy, a launch with a government pennant snapping in the rain was nosing into the standing waves—coming through after us, or trying to. And on its bow, small as a grain, a figure in dark oilskins raised one arm. Not a wave. A signal. Three fingers, then a fist.

Sable's signal. The one she'd used at the checkpoint. The one that meant: *they know where you're going, and they're already there.*
