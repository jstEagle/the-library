## 31. Rivergate

We smelled the city before we saw it.

Brannoch noticed it first—he was at the tiller, half-healed and stubborn as driftwood, his bandaged shoulder propped against the gunwale like it had grown there over the last hundred miles. He lifted his chin into the wind and breathed in through his nose the way he did when he was reading water, or weather, or trouble.

"Smoke," he said.

I tasted it a moment later. Not cookfire smoke, not the sweet resinous haze of a sawmill town, which I'd learned to know on the long downriver stretches. This was sharper. Paper. Ink. The particular bitter tang of ledgers and files and forty years of bureaucratic memory giving themselves up to the dark.

Oscar came forward from the bow, where they'd spent most of the morning coiling rope that didn't need coiling, because Oscar's hands needed work the way other people's lungs needed air. They caught the smell too. I watched it land on them—watched their whole body go still in that way of theirs, the stillness of a pilot reading fog, deciding whether to push through or turn back.

"That's not a kitchen fire," Oscar said.

"No," Brannoch said. "That's an archive."

* * *

We came around the last bend below Two Sisters with the light failing, and Rivergate opened up before us the way it must have opened for every boat for two hundred years—a sprawl of lamplit warehouses, the great stone customs house squatting on its bluff, the masts of the deepwater fleet standing up out of the harbor like a forest of winter trees. It should have been beautiful. In another life, arriving here would have been the whole point: the end of the river, the beginning of everything else.

But the sky above the magistrate's quarter was the wrong color.

Not the amber glow of a city at night—that soft bruised luminance every big settlement throws up against the clouds. This was orange. Pulsing. Alive. A column of sparks climbed off the roofline of the annex building and bent eastward in the wind, and even from this distance, even across three miles of black water, I could hear it: a low, vast crackling, like the river itself whispering.

The annex. Corven's annex. The Hall of Records, where every deed, every grant, every disbursement of the Provision fund had been filed and stamped and forgotten for four decades.

"He's burning them now," I said. "Tonight. Before the hearings convene."

Oscar said nothing. They were looking at the fire the way you look at a wound you should have dressed days ago.

Sable's warning had come to us eleven days ago, passed through a fisherman who owed Brannoch a favor and didn't ask questions: *Corven moves on the records within the fortnight. After that, whatever's in your chest is the only copy left in the world.* We had run since then the way we'd run the whole way down—by night when we dared, by day when we had no choice, past checkpoints where Brannoch's forged papers and terrible brandy bought us passage, through rain that never truly stopped, just changed its mind about intensity. We had told ourselves we had time. That Sable's information would buy us a margin. That a man as careful as Corven wouldn't move until he was certain the chest was out of play.

We had been wrong by exactly one night.

* * *

The harbor district was under curfew, but curfew in Rivergate, Brannoch explained as we nosed the patched boat into a shadowed slip behind a derelict chandlery, was a thing with texture. "Soldiers watch the avenues," he said, hauling us alongside with one good arm and a vocabulary of curses. "They don't watch the water. Nobody watches the water after dark. The river people keep their own hours." He tied off with a knot I couldn't follow and looked at us both. "You two want to walk into a fire zone during a military curfew. I've sailed with smugglers, pirates, and one woman who married a crocodile, and this is the stupidest plan I've ever heard."

"You're coming," Oscar said.

"I'm coming," Brannoch agreed, disgusted. "Because if you die, who's going to pay me back for the boat?"

It wasn't the boat he meant, and none of us pretended otherwise. Something had happened between us on the water that none of us had voted for—the slow, unwilling welding of three separate lives into something that functioned like a crew. I had spent six years alone on this hunt, sleeping in ferry stations and mission attics, trusting no one further than arm's reach, and now I found myself checking automatically that both of these impossible people were behind me before I moved. It was inconvenient. It was also, I was beginning to understand, the only reason any of us had made it this far.

We went ashore into streets gone strange.

Curfew does something to a city that darkness alone doesn't. The lamps were lit—Corven's Rivergate kept its lamps lit even in crisis, order as theater—but the people were gone, swept indoors, and what remained was architecture without life: awnings stirring in the hot wind, a child's hoop abandoned against a doorpost, shutters with eyes behind them. We moved the way Brannoch taught us, close to walls, in the seams between lamplight, and everywhere the orange glow grew stronger, and with it the smell, thick enough now to chew.

And everywhere, quietly, the sound of doors.

Not opening. Just—shifting. The small sounds of a whole city lying awake behind its shutters, listening to its own history burn. Once, passing a courtyard, I heard an old man's voice inside, low and rhythmic, and realized he was reciting something. Names, maybe. Or a prayer. In Vessany, my grandmother had told me once, they had burned records too, in the last year before the maps erased us. She had been nineteen. She said the strangest part wasn't the fire. It was how quiet everyone stayed while it burned, as if loudness might make it real.

I understood that old man in the courtyard better than I wanted to.

* * *

We reached the edge of the annex square an hour past midnight, and the heat hit us like a hand.

The annex was a broad granite building, three stories, built with Provision money in the years when the fund still built things honestly—I knew its lines from my grandmother's papers, from the architect's drawings she'd kept folded in oilcloth all her life. Now its windows glowed from within like the eye-slits of a furnace. Fire had taken the roof and was working down through the floors, and the heat rolled off it in visible waves that bent the lamplight of the square. Sparks rose in columns and scattered over the neighboring roofs, and men—fire crews, or men dressed as fire crews—played thin streams of water on the *surrounding* buildings, protecting everything except the thing that mattered.

"They're not fighting it," Oscar said flatly. "Look at the hoses. They're wetting the neighbors."

"I see it."

"Forty years of records. Every grant, every ledger copy, every signature—" Oscar's voice did something I'd never heard it do: cracked. They covered it by turning away, jaw tight, watching the fire with the expression of someone watching a plane go down with someone they loved aboard.

In the center of the square, mounted soldiers sat their horses in a loose ring, faces ruddy in the firelight, keeping back the handful of civilians who had gathered despite the curfew—old people, mostly, standing in their nightclothes in the hot wind, watching. One woman was weeping without any sound at all, her hands pressed together as though she were holding something invisible between them. An old man argued with a soldier in a low, exhausted voice, pointing again and again at the burning building, and the soldier shook his head again and again, and neither of them raised their voice, because everyone in that square understood the same thing: this fire was legal. This fire was official. Whatever grief or rage they felt, shouting about it would only get them added to it.

My grandmother had described this exact scene, forty years ago, in a different country. I had read her account so many times I could recite it. *They stood in the square and watched, and the soldiers watched them watching, and everyone was very polite, and that politeness was the worst violence of all.*

I had spent six years imagining I would arrive in time. Every map I'd memorized, every checkpoint I'd bribed, every night I'd slept with the transcriptions strapped to my body—it had all been built on the arithmetic of *soon enough*. And now I stood in the heat-shimmer with my grandmother's ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs, the only complete account of the Provision's theft left on earth, and the building that could have corroborated every line of it was collapsing inward on its own truth.

"We're late," I said. My voice sounded far away. "Six years, and we're one night late."

Oscar turned and looked at me, and in the firelight I saw something in their face that frightened me more than the soldiers did. Not despair. Calculation.

"The annex has a sub-basement," Oscar said. "Stone. My mentor used to store charts there—said it was the only room in Rivergate that never flooded and never burned. He called it the vault. If anything survived the first hour, it's down there."

"The stairs will be the problem," Brannoch said. He was squinting past the ring of horses toward the annex's flank, where a service alley ran along the wall. "Front entrance is a chimney now. But there'll be a delivery stair to the lower levels. Coal chute, maybe. Records buildings always have a second way down, because paper needs moving and clerks are lazy."

"And the soldiers?"

Brannoch smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who had talked his way past worse than eight bored cavalrymen, and whose shoulder was torn open and whose brandy flask was nearly empty and who had decided, somewhere on the river, that this was simply how his life was going to be now. "Soldiers watching a legal fire are the sleepiest soldiers in the world, girl. Their orders are to keep people *out*. Nobody's ordered them to expect anyone going *in*."

He was right. That was the terrible thing—he was right, and I heard myself agreeing, and some cold clear part of me stood apart and marveled that six years of caution had ended here, in a curfewed square, planning to walk into a burning building because the alternative was letting the last proof burn unopposed.

* * *

We circled wide, through alleys that stank of tar and hot stone, and found what Brannoch had promised: a recessed service door on the annex's river side, its frame already smoking, guarded by a single soldier who had positioned himself upwind and was watching the fire with the glazed devotion of a man who wished he were anywhere else. Beyond him, half-hidden by a stack of empty crates, a coal chute descended into darkness.

Brannoch handled the soldier. I never learned exactly how—there was brandy involved, and a story about a wife in the harbor district, and afterward the soldier sat down heavily on the crates with the bewildered contentment of a man who has been given permission to stop caring—and then we were at the chute, and the heat was on our backs like a living thing, and Oscar went first, boots-first into the dark, and I followed with the oilcloth bundle cinched tight against my chest, and Brannoch came last, muttering a prayer to a god he claimed not to believe in.

The chute was iron and steep and hot. We slid down it into a darkness that smelled of coal dust and, beneath that, impossibly, of paper—dry, dense, the smell of ten thousand files compressed into stone rooms below the flame. The sub-basement was not yet burning. But the air was wrong. It had the taut, held-breath quality of a room with fire standing on its ceiling, and along the top of the walls, where stone met timber, thin threads of smoke crept downward like roots growing in reverse.

Oscar struck a match, cupped it, and the light showed us shelves. Ranks of them, marching away into the dark, sagging under the weight of boxes and binders and rolled registers tied with string. Forty years of the interior's memory, stacked in the dark, directly beneath the fire that had been sent to erase it.

"There won't be time," I whispered. "There's thousands of documents down here. We can't carry it. We can't even find it."

Oscar's matchlight steadied. When they spoke, their voice had gone quiet and certain, the voice I imagined they used in cockpits when the fog closed in and the only options were precision or death.

"We don't need everything. We need the master register—the Provision disbursement index. One book. Every grant, every signature, cross-referenced. With that plus what's in your bundle, the hearings don't need the rest." They were already moving down the aisle, reading shelf labels in the matchlight. "Corven's name will be in it. All their names will be in it. Find me the P-section."

Somewhere above us, with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, a floor gave way.

Dust sifted down through the matchlight. Brannoch swore softly. And I stood in the dark with my grandmother's ledgers against my heart, listening to the weight of forty years shift overhead, and understood that whatever we took out of this room, we would take it out running.

Oscar's match found the shelf labels. "Here," they said. "Ilse. *Here.*"

I went toward the light.
