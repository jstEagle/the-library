## 32. The Burning Annex

The smoke reached us before the city did.

We came downriver through the last hours of curfew, the monsoon still spitting its thin gray rain, and the sky over Rivergate was the wrong color—not storm-gray, not dawn-gray, but a bruised amber that had nothing to do with the weather. Brannoch saw it first. He was at the tiller with his bandages dark at the shoulder, one eye squinting against the drizzle, and he said, "That's not cloud," in the flat voice he used for bad news, and I knew he was right before I finished looking.

"That's the annex," Ilse said.

She was standing at the bow with the oilskin bundle of her transcriptions strapped across her chest like a child. She'd hardly slept in three days. None of us had. The boat was held together with pitch and prayer and Brannoch's blasphemous opinions about both, and we had run the last forty miles of river on nerve alone, and now the city rose out of the rain ahead of us—rooftops, crane arms, the old customs tower with its green copper dome—and above the district where the magistrate's offices stood, the smoke went up in a column as straight and deliberate as a chimney's.

Deliberate. That was the word that lodged in me. Fires are messy things. This one burned like it had been given instructions.

"They're early," Ilse said. "The hearings don't open for—"

"Two days," I said. "I know."

Corven had told Sable to burn the records before the hearings, and Sable had given us the warning, and we had run the river half to death to beat the flames to the door, and we were still too late. Or we would have been, if the fire had been only a fire. But I had spent my whole life reading weather and water and the small dishonesties of machines, and what I saw in that smoke column was not a fire that had gotten loose. It was a fire being fed. Fed by hand, by men with carts, in the rain, which meant someone had decided the annex had to die tonight and had budgeted for the weather.

"Take us in past the fish market," Brannoch said. "There's a slip behind the salt sheds. Nobody watches it in curfew."

"You shouldn't come," Ilse said to him. "You're—"

"I've been shot, girl, not christened. Take us in."

* * *

The salt sheds smelled of brine and old rope, and the rain came down harder as we tied off, drumming on the tin roof like a crowd growing restless. From the water the annex had been a smudge and a column. Up close it was a world. The magistrate's records annex was a long stone building, two stories, built—of all things—with Provision money, back when the fund still built things it didn't steal from, and its windows on the river side were already orange. Firelight moved behind the glass like something alive pacing a cage.

There were men at the front, on the street side. We could hear them—shouts, the grind of cart wheels, the wet hiss of something being thrown onto flame that fought back. But the river side was quieter. A fire crew's worth of inattention. Why guard the wall that faced the water, when the water was ours and nobody's?

"Side door," I said. "Provision buildings all had river doors. Freight doors. For the ledgers coming in off the boats."

"You'd know," Ilse said.

"I'd know."

We went up the alley between the sheds with the rain hammering us flat, and I felt the old familiar lift in my chest, the one I got before a landing in fog I had no business attempting—the lift that said *you are exactly where you should not be, and that is the only place left*. I had built a life on never going back, never landing twice, never carrying anything but cargo, and here I was going back to the one city that had ever asked me to stay, to carry the heaviest cargo of my life into a burning building.

Some rules you keep until the day keeping them would make you a stranger to yourself.

The river door was oak, swollen with a century of damp, and it was locked. Of course it was. I put my shoulder into it and it gave me nothing, and behind us the fire roared and the carts ground, and Ilse said, "Stand back," in a voice I hadn't heard from her before—flat, certain, the voice of a woman who had spent years planning for doors that would not open—and she pulled a short iron bar from inside her coat and drove it into the jamb at the lock. Two blows. The door split and sagged and we were in.

Heat met us like a hand.

* * *

Inside, the annex was a cathedral of paper on fire.

The main hall ran the length of the building, and its shelves had been iron once—Provision iron, good iron—but the shelves were only the skeleton. The body was paper. Forty years of it. Registers and ledgers and land grants and shipping manifests and the gray administrative dust of a nation's interior, all of it stacked to the ceiling, and the fire had climbed the stacks like ivy and was eating downward. The smoke lay along the ceiling in a rolling black ceiling of its own, and beneath it the air was a shimmering amber, and the light was the light of the worst sunset you have ever seen, indoors, at midnight, in the rain.

The heat pressed on my face and dried my eyes and I understood, in the first three seconds, that we had perhaps four minutes and that four minutes was a gift we hadn't earned.

"Where?" Ilse shouted. Her voice was small against the fire's voice. "The master register—where would it be?"

"Registrar's cage!" I shouted back, because I had ferried clerks along this river for fifteen years and clerks talk, and every one of them had complained about the registrar's cage in Rivergate annex, the barred room at the far end of the hall where the documents that mattered lived under lock and a clerk's permanent grievance. "End of the hall! Stay low!"

We went down the aisle between the burning stacks with our coats over our heads and the fire raining small burnt feathers of paper around us—flaming fragments, curling, drifting down like the world's most terrible snow. A shelf to our left let go with a groan and a rush of orange and I hauled Ilse back by the collar as a wall of flame breathed out across the aisle where she'd been standing, and for a moment we stood in the heat of it, pressed together, her eyes above the wet cloth over her face wide and furious and unafraid, and then we ran.

The cage was at the end, and the cage was iron, and iron does not burn. That was the joke of it—the Provision built so well that the fire couldn't have what it came for without help, and the help was the men outside with their carts, feeding the flames through the windows, and the help was time. The barred door was locked. The lock was brass and old and I put the iron bar to it the way Ilse had shown me and hit it until my arms sang and it sheared and the door swung open into a room where the air was still, impossibly, breathable.

Shelves of ledgers. Boxes of registers, tied with the faded Provision ribbon, the little stamped seals I now knew better than my own signature. And on the central table, under a glass case like a relic in a church, a single volume the size of a paving stone, bound in green leather gone black with age.

The master register. Every disbursement the Provision ever made, in its own hand, in its own ink. Every name. Every signature. Corven's among them. The one book that could not be dismissed as a transcription, a copy, a forgery, a grudge. The one book the hearings could not proceed without.

Ilse saw it and made a sound I had never heard her make—small, cracked, the sound of four years of her life arriving all at once.

"Take it," I said. "I'll carry ledgers."

We stuffed what we could into the canvas satchels we'd brought, working fast, hands shaking, the heat building behind us in waves I could feel through my coat. The fire had reached the last stack before the cage. Through the bars I watched it come down the aisle toward us like a slow animal, patient, certain of its dinner.

And then the ceiling over the main hall groaned.

* * *

I have heard engines fail in a hundred ways and I know the sound a structure makes when it stops believing in itself. It is not a crash. It is a long, wet, wooden sigh, and then everything happens very fast.

"Out," I said. "Now. The way we came."

"The register—"

"I have it. *Go.*"

Ilse went. I got the master register into my arms—it weighed as much as a child, it weighed as much as everything—and I followed, and I had taken perhaps six steps down the aisle when the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. A section, near the middle of the hall, where the fire had eaten longest. The roof beams went first, black silhouettes folding, and then a ton and a half of slate and timber and burning paper came down across the aisle between me and the river door, and the world turned orange and then black and then orange again, and the heat that hit me was not heat anymore but a physical country with borders I was standing inside.

I went down. I remember the register under me—I turned as I fell, the way you turn to keep a child from the ground, some instinct older than sense—and I remember the fire breathing over my back, one long exhalation, and my coat catching, and my hands beating at my own shoulders, and the thought, absurd and clear as a bell: *the fog always lifts, the fog always lifts.*

Then someone had my ankles and was dragging me, and the orange country receded, and I was being pulled through a gap in the burning wreckage that I would swear had not existed a moment before, and Ilse's voice was in my ear saying words I couldn't parse, only the tone, which was fury, which was the tone of a woman who had decided I was not permitted to die in here.

The river door. Rain. The rain hit my burned shoulders and I screamed, I'm not ashamed to say it, I screamed like something newly born, and then Brannoch's hands were on me too, one-eyed old Brannoch who should have been a mile away, and the three of us went down the alley together with the register clutched to my chest like a stolen infant, and behind us the annex sighed again, deeper, and gave up another section of its roof to the rain.

* * *

They put me in the salt shed on a pile of sacking, and a mission nurse—summoned by Brannoch through channels I didn't ask about—put something on my shoulders and back that made the world go white and then mercifully distant. Second-degree burns, she said. Shoulder blades, the back of my neck, the backs of both arms. "You turned away from it," she said, puzzled. "Most people run. You turned your back to it."

"I was carrying something," I said.

The master register sat on a salt crate between us, steaming gently in the damp air, its green leather scorched at one corner, its pages—when Ilse lifted the cover with the tenderness of a woman handling a sleeping animal—whole. Whole. Forty years of names, every disbursement, every signature, and it had crossed the burning hall on the back of a bush pilot who never carried anything but cargo.

Ilse sat beside it and didn't touch it for a long time. She just looked at it, and the firelight from the doorway moved on her face, and I thought: *she has her grandmother's eyes when she looks at it.* I had never seen the grandmother. I knew it anyway.

"The hearings are in two days," she said finally. "They'll say the annex fire destroyed the evidence. That's the whole plan. Corven doesn't need to win the hearings. He needs them to have nothing to hear."

"He's too late," Brannoch said from the doorway, where he stood with his arm bound and his one eye on the river. "Aren't he."

"He's too late," Ilse said. And then, quieter, looking at me, at the bandages, at the whole scorched ruin of me: "You could have died in there. For a book."

"For the book," I said. "There's a difference. I spent fifteen years learning that nothing I carried mattered, because nothing I carried was mine to matter. This one's different. This one's everybody's."

Outside, the annex burned on, fed and stubborn, and the rain fought it, and neither won. Somewhere across the city a bell began to ring—the curfew bell, or an alarm, or maybe just a bell that had decided, like the rest of us, that silence was no longer honest.

Ilse closed the register and put her hand flat on its cover.

"Two days," she said. "Then I stand up in front of the whole river and read them their own names."

The bell kept ringing. My back burned where the fire had kissed it, and I found I didn't mind. Some prices you pay and would pay twice. In two days I would stand in a hearing room in Rivergate, in front of magistrates and clerks and whoever Corven could still buy, and I would say his name out loud—the man who pulled me from a wreck, who gave me my first job, who taught me the river—and I would say it knowing what it cost and saying it anyway.

The fire would take the building. It would not take the book.
