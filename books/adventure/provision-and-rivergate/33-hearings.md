## 33. Hearings

The night before the hearings opened, I did not sleep. I sat in the narrow room the mission clinic had lent us—two cots, a window that rattled in the wind off the river—and read my grandmother's handwriting by lamplight until the letters swam.

She had written small. That was the first thing you learned about Vessany clerks, she'd told me once, when I was small enough to sit on her knee and she was still willing to speak of the old country at all: *We wrote small because paper was evidence, and evidence must be easy to hide.* Her figures marched across the transcriptions in columns fine as stitching, each entry dated, initialed, cross-referenced to a page number in a master ledger that had spent forty years at the bottom of a flooded channel before Oscar Yarrow pulled it into the light. Around those columns, in the margins, she had written her own notes—the human arithmetic the official record left out. *Rice allotment for Kelder's Ford: 40 percent of need. Difference diverted.* And beside it, smaller still, almost a whisper of ink: *The Halloway account again. They are not even careful.*

I had grown up on those margins without knowing what they were. She used to copy them out as exercises when she taught me penmanship. *Make your sevens like this,* she would say, guiding my hand, and all the while I was practicing my letters on the names of thieves.

Now the names were going to be said aloud, in a room with a dome ceiling and a public gallery, and my hands would be the ones holding the pages.

* * *

They came for us an hour after dawn. Not soldiers—clerks, which in Rivergate is nearly the same thing but better dressed. Two young women with satchels and a manifest, who checked our copies against the register Oscar had carried out of the burning annex with his eyebrows singed and his lungs full of smoke, and who treated him with the reverent awkwardness people reserve for men they have read about in the morning edition. He hated it. I watched him shrug off their thanks the way a man shrugs off wet clothes, eager to be out of them.

Brannoch could not come. The bullet had gone through his shoulder and out the back, missing everything that mattered by what the surgeon called "a finger's width and the grace of God," and he was still too weak to stand unassisted. When we left the clinic he caught my wrist—not Oscar's, mine—and held it with a grip that surprised me, coming from so ruined a man.

"You'll want to look at the commissioners," he said. "Not the gallery. The gallery is weather; it blows where it blows. You watch the ones behind the table. A man's face tells you nothing. But if a commissioner's hand goes flat on the table—" he pressed his palm down against the blanket, "—that's a man deciding something. You mark who decides things, girl. That's where the river bends."

"I'll mark it," I said.

"And Ilse." His one eye found mine. It was strange, how quickly I had stopped seeing the patch and started seeing only the eye, brown and shrewd and tired. "Whatever they ask you. Answer slow. Liars answer fast. Truth can take its time."

* * *

The hearing chamber had been a granary exchange once, back when Rivergate measured its wealth in what came downriver instead of what stayed hidden in it. They had kept the bones of the building—the great timber arches, the high windows set with glass gone wavy and green with age—but hung it now with bunting in the colors of the Interior Assembly, and the effect was of a festival dressed over a funeral. Under the dome, the air smelled of dust and hot wood and too many bodies, and the light came down in long slanted columns full of drifting motes, so that the whole room seemed to be quietly burning without heat.

Oscar walked beside me through the crowd in the corridor outside, close enough that our shoulders touched. He had shaved. Someone—I suspected the mission sisters—had found him a jacket that almost fit. He looked like a man wearing another man's life, and he knew it.

"There'll be questions about me," he said. "About how we got the chest. Don't protect me. If they ask whether we broke salvage law, say yes."

"Did we?"

"Probably." Almost a smile. "Say it anyway. A hearing smells a lie faster than a dog smells rain, and if they catch you shading one thing, they'll shade everything you said."

"And if they catch me telling the truth badly?"

"Then you're in good company." He stopped at the doors. Through them I could hear the room beyond, the shuffle and murmur of three hundred people finding seats, the particular hush of a crowd that has come hoping for blood. "Ilse." He turned, and for a moment the noise fell away from us both. "Forty years your family kept this quiet to stay alive. One hour of saying it out loud won't kill you. I know it feels like it will. It won't."

"How would you know?"

"Because tomorrow I have to do worse." He said it lightly, but his hand, when it briefly touched my shoulder, was not light at all. Then the doors opened, and the sound of the room came through like water through a breach, and there was no more time for either of us.

* * *

The commission sat seven at a long table beneath the dome's highest point, beneath a painted emblem of the Provision itself—a sheaf of grain crossed with a river key—that forty years of official history had made into a saint's icon. The chairman was a dry stick of a man named Aldous Renk, famous for two things: never interrupting a witness, and never forgetting one. To his left sat the commissioners from the interior districts, and I marked them as Brannoch had told me to, their faces and their hands, and one pair of hands in particular—Commissioner Vay's, soft and ringed, which lay very still on the table through the whole of the opening statements, still as something waiting.

The counsel for the inquiry read the charges into the record in a voice like a man reading a shipping manifest: diversion of funds, falsification of disbursement records, conspiracy among named persons, spanning four decades and eleven river districts. The words *Provision* and *theft*, spoken together in that formal room, made a sound I had waited my whole life to hear and found, hearing it, strangely small. Forty years of my grandmother's fear, and it fit inside a sentence.

Then they called my name, and I walked the long floor alone.

* * *

What I remember of the hour that followed is not an hour. It is pieces, sharp as broken glass and just as separate.

The chair. Its height above the floor, so that my feet did not quite rest flat, and the small indignity of that.

Renk's voice, unhurried: *"State your name for the record, and your connection to the documents."*

"Ilse Marchetti. My grandmother, Anouk Vessany-Marchetti, was a disbursement clerk in the Provision's third district office. These are her transcriptions of the fund's ledgers, made between the fall of Vessany and her death, from originals she carried out of the country."

*"And the originals?"*

"In a chest recovered from the Serevan Channel this spring. Also entered into evidence." I did not look toward Oscar. I had decided beforehand that I would not, that looking at him would be looking for courage instead of having it, and I held to that.

The questions came slowly at first, and I answered them the way Brannoch had told me—slowly, letting truth take its time. Where the ledgers had been. How they survived. Why no one had produced them in forty years, and here I paused, because the true answer was my grandmother's whole life: the forged papers, the border crossings, the years she would not open the box even to teach me my letters, the way she burned the wrapping but never the contents, the way she died with the location of the chest in her mouth and gave it to me in a whisper, at the end, like a coin passed under a door.

"She believed the Provision would be investigated one day," I said. "She believed it because she had seen the order for the investigation signed, and then seen the order rescinded, and then seen every man who signed the first signature promoted. She understood that documents do not decay. Only the will to read them does. So she kept them alive until the will came back."

A murmur went around the gallery at that, and Commissioner Vay's hand moved—one fingertip tapping, twice, and then flat. Flat. I marked it.

Then counsel brought out the ledgers themselves, and the room changed.

They had built a stand for them, glass-topped, lit from below, and when the transcription pages were laid beside the recovered originals and the two hands were shown to match—her small stitched sevens, her marginal shorthand, forty years apart and identical—the sound that went through the chamber was not a murmur. It was lower than that, older, the sound a crowd makes when it recognizes something it has always half-known. Three hundred people realizing that the schools and clinics and grain barges of their childhood had been bought with money meant to buy three times as much, and that the difference had gone into certain family accounts whose names were being read aloud, one by one, in a voice like a shipping manifest.

Halloway. Corven. The Ferrenzi trust. The Ashcombe estate. Names that owned the newspapers, endowed the hospitals, sat on the boards of the banks that had financed this very inquiry. I watched each name land in the room like a stone dropped into still water, the ripples spreading outward through the gallery, and I thought: *Grandmother, look. Your small handwriting, filling a hall.*

* * *

It was near the end that Commissioner Vay spoke for the first time.

He had waited, courteous as a spider, through two hours of ledger entries and chain-of-custody testimony, and when he finally leaned toward his microphone the whole room leaned with him.

"Madam Marchetti. A question of interpretation, if you will." His voice was warm, unhurried, the voice of a man who has never needed to raise it. "These documents show diversions. We can agree on that—they are quite thorough. But diversions require a counterparty. Money does not simply vanish; it goes somewhere, into someone's keeping. Your grandmother's records identify the signatories on the outgoing side. They do not—" and here he smiled, gently, reasonably, "—they do not identify everyone on the receiving side, do they? There may be accounts, intermediaries, jurisdictions... unnamed. Is that not so?"

And I understood, in the space of one heartbeat, what he was doing. He was not defending the accused. He was widening the crime until it dissolved—until theft became system, system became era, era became nobody's fault, or everybody's, which amounts to the same thing. I had watched my grandmother's country die of exactly this reasoning.

So I did not answer fast. I let the silence sit while I took from my folder the last page of her transcriptions—the page I had saved, the one I had shown to no one, not even Oscar—and I laid it on the stand before me, and I read aloud the line she had copied in her smallest hand, the marginal note she had written the week before the maps erased Vessany:

*"If they ever ask why I kept these: tell them the receiving accounts are not hidden. They are simply waiting for someone brave enough to read the numbers backward."*

Then I looked up at Commissioner Vay, and I said: "Yes, Commissioner. There are unnamed accounts. My grandmother traced eleven of them. Their numbers are in the pages before you, and they route, ultimately, through a trading house in which your own family has held interest since before the hearings were dreamed of. Shall I read the entries?"

The silence that followed lasted four seconds. I counted them by the ticking of the great clock above the gallery, a clock that had hung in the granary exchange since before anyone now living was born. In those four seconds, Commissioner Vay's hand lay flat on the table, pressing down, pressing down, as if he could hold the whole room steady by force. Then he withdrew his question, thanked me for my service, and sat back, and the ripples went out through the gallery again, wider this time, and did not stop at the walls.

* * *

Afterward there was a corridor, and noise, and newspapermen with their hats pushed back, and Oscar finding me through all of it the way a man finds a fixed point in fog. He didn't congratulate me. He just stood beside me while the room emptied, and after a while he said, "Your grandmother's sevens. I saw them on the screen. They're good sevens."

"They are," I said, and discovered I was laughing, or crying, or some tide moving between the two. "Forty years of hiding, and it took one hour."

"One hour," he agreed. "And tomorrow's mine." And I felt the change in him then, the way you feel weather coming—his stillness, the set of his shoulders, a man standing at the edge of the water he has avoided his whole life. Because tomorrow they would call Oscar Yarrow to the stand, and ask him about the chest, and the sabotage, and the offers, and in the end they would ask him to say a name he owed his life to, in front of the whole river country, with no fog to land in and nowhere left to depart to.

That evening Brannoch sent word from the clinic, one line, in a nurse's handwriting because his own still shook:

*Marked who decided things. Now find out what Vay decides next. Rivers bend fast when the bank gives way.*

And by morning, we would learn exactly how fast—for the annex fire had not been the last match struck in this city, and the name Oscar carried to the stand was already, in certain quiet rooms, being priced.
