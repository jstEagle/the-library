## 34. Oscar on the Stand

They gave me a room under the hearing hall to wait in, and I understood the joke of it immediately: a windowless cell with a kettle and a chair, the kind of room you put a person in when you want them to feel like cargo. I had spent twenty years being cargo's opposite—the thing that moved, the thing that left—and now the state of Rivergate had me in a box with a door that locked from the outside, and I could hear the rain on the stone above me like a crowd that hadn't decided yet whether it was applause.

Brannoch sat with me for the first hour, his shoulder still bound, his one eye doing the arithmetic of the room. He didn't say much. He'd brought a thermos of coffee that tasted like tar and river water, and he poured me a cup without asking, the way he'd poured a hundred cups on the boat through the monsoon, and that small ordinary kindness nearly undid me worse than anything the enforcers had managed.

"You don't have to do it the way they want," he said finally. "You know that. Rooms like this, they want you scared. Scared people talk fast and short. Give 'em slow."

"I don't know how to be slow in a room," I said.

"You flew slow through fog for twenty years. Same thing. You just can't see where you're going, so you trust the instruments." He tapped my chest with one blunt finger, once, and left it there a second longer than he needed to. "You've got instruments."

Then a clerk came for him—his statement was scheduled after mine, and I heard him arguing cheerfully with the clerk about whether his one eye constituted a disability or an advantage—and I was alone with the kettle and the chair and the sound of rain, and I did the thing I had never once done in twenty years of flying.

I stayed in a room and thought about what I was about to do.

* * *

They came for me at ten minutes past nine. The clerk was a young woman with ink on her fingers and a face trained into perfect blankness, and she walked me up a spiral stair and through a door, and the sound of the hall hit me like weather.

It was bigger than I'd expected. The Provision hearings had been running for six days and the city had gone half-mad with them; the galleries were full, and the overflow stood along the walls, and there were more people in that room than lived in some of the river towns I'd been supplying since before they had electric light. I found faces in it the way you find landmarks through fog. Ilse, third row, spine straight as a mast, her grandmother's transcriptions stacked in front of her in a leather folder gone soft with handling. Brannoch's one eye gleaming near the back, arms crossed, radiating the specific patience of a man who has survived worse rooms. And near the front, flanked by two lawyers and an aura of expensive stillness—Magistrate Corven.

He looked older than the last time I'd seen him, which had been on the river, alone, offering me everything. Older, and smaller, the way powerful men look smaller when the room is finally bigger than they are. But when our eyes met, he did the thing he had always done, the thing that had saved my life once and bought my loyalty for two decades after: he inclined his head, very slightly, as if to say *I see you, Oscar Yarrow. I have always seen you.*

And I felt the old gravity take hold of me, that pull like current under calm water, and I understood that this was the last of it. That the whole hearing, the whole chest, the whole rotten history of the Provision, had narrowed down to this: whether I would let a man who once pulled me from a wreck tell me what the truth was worth.

The presiding officer—a dry, unhurried woman named Advocate Senn, with spectacles on a chain and the voice of someone who had outlasted four governments—asked me to state my name for the record.

"Oscar Yarrow," I said. "Pilot. Bush runs, the Rivergate and the interior. Twenty-two years."

"And your occupation, Mr. Yarrow, is it correct that you have carried medicine, mail, and freight to settlements along the Rivergate without interruption since you were nineteen?"

"Except when the fog says no," I said, and a ripple went through the gallery, because everyone in that room knew the fog, and Senn allowed herself the ghost of a smile.

"Then let's begin where you began. With the chest."

* * *

Here is what I had decided, in the locked room, with the kettle going cold: I would tell it in the order it happened, the way it happened to me, without ornament and without mercy—not theirs, not mine. I had spent twenty-two years being a man nobody could pin down, a name on no manifest, a plane that landed twice in no place. I had built invisibility like a house and lived in it like a hermit, and I had told myself it was prudence, it was professionalism, it was the fog's own wisdom. Sitting in that room I had finally admitted what it was. It was fear with a flight plan.

So I told them everything, and I told it slow, the way Brannoch said.

I told them about the storm, and the flooded channel below Kettle Bend, and the dark shape snagged in the drowned willows that I hauled aboard out of salvage habit before my brain caught up with my hands. I told them about the seal—Vessany, the double-river crest, the nation the maps had eaten forty years ago—and how the sight of it had gone through me like cold water through a boot.

"I knew what it was," I said. "Everyone my age knows what it was. You just don't expect the river to hand it to you."

I told them about opening it—no, I corrected myself, about *failing* to open it, the chest locked, the lock stubborn, and about the passenger I'd taken on at Two Sisters because she had money and I had rules about not asking questions. Ilse, who had looked at the seal and lied so badly about it that her lying was practically a confession.

Advocate Senn let me talk. She had a way of letting silence do her cross-examining; when I paused, she'd just look at me over her spectacles until the silence got heavy and I kept going. It was, I realized somewhere in the second hour, the same technique the fog used.

I told them about the medicine run to Harrow's Landing, and the trader who'd leaned over his scales and mentioned—too casually, the way men mention things they've been paid to mention—that someone was offering good money for word of a recovered chest. Vessany markings. Sealed. "Someone paid for the *word*, understand," I said. "Not the chest. The word. That's how I knew it wasn't salvage law I was up against. Salvage law doesn't buy whispers."

I told them about the summons from Corven. I said his name and the gallery made a sound like the river shifting ice, and Senn had to gavel it quiet, and Corven's lawyers stood and objected to tone, to sequence, to God knew what, and Senn overruled them with two words and told me to continue.

I told them the truth about Corven and me. All of it. That was the part I had decided on in the locked room, the part I knew would cost me, and I made myself do it without flinching.

"Magistrate Corven saved my life," I said. "I was nineteen. I put a plane into the river at the Sorrel narrows—engine ice, my fault, my inexperience, my fault entirely. He had a fishing camp up there and he pulled me out of the water himself. Broken collarbone, half-drowned. He set the bone with river whiskey and a belt, and he kept me at that camp for a month, and when I could lift my arm again he gave me my first contract. Medicine runs. He said—" and here my voice did something I hadn't authorized it to do, cracked, just once, and I waited it out the way you wait out turbulence, "—he said the river needed someone who'd fly when nobody else would. I have owed him my life for twenty-two years. I want that in the record. I want it clear that what I'm about to say is not ingratitude. It's arithmetic. The chest doesn't care who pulled me out of the water. The ledgers don't care. The people who starved don't care. And I decided, in a room with a kettle, that I don't get to care either—not if caring means burying it."

Senn looked at me for a long moment over her spectacles. "Continue," she said, and her voice had changed, just slightly. Warmer. Or perhaps just more careful, like a pilot reading weather.

* * *

I told them about the sabotage. The fuel stop at Merrow's field, and the plane that wouldn't start, and the mechanic who found the water in the tanks—clean water, river water, poured in through the filler caps, and the caps' gaskets scored with a knife so they'd look like old wear. I told them about standing in the rain next to a dead airplane with a sealed chest in the back and understanding, with a clarity I had never once achieved in the air, that Magistrate Corven was not asking for the chest anymore. He was *taking* it. Quietly. The way you take something you can't ask for legally.

"The sabotage was professional," I said. "Not military. The kind of work you buy from a man who fixes planes and owes someone a favor. But the decision behind it—that was not a mechanic's decision. That was the decision of someone who could reach a fuel field two hundred miles from the capital and make a man like Merrow afraid to talk. There are maybe four people in the interior with that reach. I've flown for three of them."

"Name the fourth," Senn said, dry as driftwood, and the gallery laughed, and I didn't.

"I'll name the one who matters."

I told them about the letter. The one that reached me through Tomas Wren, my old instructor's friend, at the mission dock—the letter in Corven's own hand, on magistrate's paper, offering to make my debts vanish, all of them, the hangar lien and the fuel notes and the two seasons of bad contracts, in exchange for the chest and my silence. I had brought the letter. I handed it to the clerk, and I watched it travel up the aisle in an evidence tray like a small drowned thing, and I watched Corven's lead lawyer lean over to whisper and Corven not look at it. Not look at it the way you don't look at a wound.

"Read the last line," Senn said.

I had it memorized anyway. "'The river forgets, Oscar. That is its mercy, and it can be yours.'" I said it out loud and the hall went so quiet I could hear the rain on the roof, and I thought: *the river doesn't forget. That was always his mistake. The river keeps everything. That's what a river is—a place that carries what it's given until it's given somewhere else.*

I told them about Sable. About the checkpoint where she boarded us and let us go too easily, and I said her name—Enforcer Sable, of the magistrate's river service—and I watched a door near the back of the hall open and close, and I didn't see who came in, but Brannoch's eye tracked it, and his jaw set, and I filed it away.

I told them about the cartel. About the third party, the hunters who weren't Corven's, the burned patrol boat at Sable's Wreck, the fire on the water at the fuel dock, the chest going over the side, Brannoch taking a bullet getting Ilse clear. I said Brannoch's name and pointed him out in the gallery, bandaged and glaring, and I said, "He was shot keeping her alive. He had sold us out a week before—his debts, his old chains, not his heart—and he took a bullet anyway, and I want the record to show that people are not their worst transactions." Senn raised an eyebrow at that, at the editorializing, but she let it stand. I think maybe she'd been waiting twenty years for someone to editorialize.

I told them about Corven on the river. Alone in his launch, no escort, the last offer: silence, wealth, and—"this is the part that was meant to work," I said, "and it nearly did"—the mentor's memory restored. Wren's name cleared, the old disgrace struck from the record, the man who taught me to fly given back to me whole.

"And what did you say, Mr. Yarrow?"

I looked at Corven. He looked back, and there was no threat in it anymore, and no warmth either. There was just a tired man watching the last door close.

"I said no," I told the hall. "I said: you gave me my life once. I'm giving it back. It's the only thing I have that you want."

* * *

The cross-examination, when it came, was a long smooth exercise in trying to make me small. Corven's lead counsel was a silver-haired man named Voss with the manner of a doctor delivering bad news gently, and he tried, in order: that I was a salvage thief; that I was a smuggler of undocumented cargo; that my testimony about the sabotage was inference dressed as fact; that my relationship with Ilse Vess—her real name, spoken aloud in that hall for the first time in forty years, and I saw Ilse's hands go flat on the rail in front of her—compromised my objectivity; that a man who had spent two decades flying without manifests, without fixed routes, without names, was hardly a reliable narrator of anything.

That last one. That was the one that mattered, and I think Voss knew it, because he saved it for last and leaned on it.

"Mr. Yarrow. You have just described twenty-two years of deliberate anonymity. No fixed address. No passenger lists. You've told this commission yourself that you never learned names too well. Why should this commission believe the testimony of a man who has made a career of being unfindable?"

And the hall waited, and somewhere in the galleries a baby made a small sound and was hushed, and the rain kept on, and I found Brannoch's eye and then Ilse's, and I thought about the instruments.

"Because being unfindable was a lie I told myself," I said. "I told myself it was prudence. It was fear. I watched my mentor die in the Sorrel narrows because he flew routes no one else would, and I decided the safest thing was to be no one, to owe nothing, to land nowhere twice. And I want to tell this commission what that cost, because I think it's the same price this whole interior has been paying for forty years." I could hear my own voice going out over the hall like a transmission, and I let it. "When you make yourself invisible, you don't stop seeing things. You just stop being responsible for them. I flew over towns the Provision built and towns the Provision emptied, for twenty years, and I saw both, and I said nothing, because saying something would have meant being someone. Being someone with an address. Being someone who could be found and asked questions." I turned, deliberately, and looked at Corven. "Magistrate Corven taught me to fly. He also taught me, without ever meaning to, that the safest place in the world is the cockpit of a plane that's already leaving. And I'm done. I'm on the record. My name is Oscar Yarrow, I live at the Rivergate municipal hangar, bay three, and anyone who wants me can find me there."

The silence after that was the kind you get above a storm ceiling—total, pressurized, temporary. Then Senn said, "The commission thanks you," in her driftwood voice, and the gavel came down, and the hall broke into a sound like the river in flood.

They walked me out through a side door, down the spiral stair, back toward the room with the kettle. The clerk with ink on her fingers went ahead of me, and at the bottom of the stair she stopped and turned, and her trained blankness slipped for exactly one second.

"My grandmother's village was on the list," she said. "The one from yesterday. The emptied ones." Then she went back up the stairs, and I stood in the stone corridor with the rain-sound overhead and my hands shaking now, only now, the way they always shook after a hard landing and never during.

Brannoch was waiting at the door. He didn't ask how it went. He poured me coffee from the thermos, and it still tasted like tar, and I drank it anyway.

"You named him," he said.

"I named him."

"And now we find out what this river does with the truth." He squinted up the stairwell, toward the hall, toward the galleries, toward whatever was moving through the city beyond them like weather. "Because it's got a choice to make. And so does that commission. And I've known rivers a long time, Yarrow, and I'll tell you what I know—" He stopped. His one eye had gone to the end of the corridor, where a woman in a plain gray coat stood by the exit door, half in shadow, not trying to hide.

Sable.

She didn't come toward us. She just looked at me, and touched two fingers to her hat brim—an old river salute, or a warning, I couldn't tell which—and then she was gone through the door, out into the rain.

Brannoch exhaled slowly. "Well," he said. "That's either very good or very bad."

Above us, faint through the stone, the hearing hall was called back to order. The verdict was coming.
