## 36. What Stays

The hearings ended on a Thursday, and by Friday the city had already begun to forget.

Ilse felt it happening the way you feel a fever break—the heat going out of a thing all at once. For one hour on the stand she had been the most visible woman in Rivergate, her grandmother's transcriptions spread across the clerk's table like the entrails of a nation, and every face in that hall had turned toward her with the same expression: hunger and horror braided together. Now the newspapers had moved on to a dockworkers' strike, to a scandal in the capital's water commission, to a photograph of Magistrate Corven stepping out of his townhouse in a pressed grey suit, unconvicted, unbowed, his hand raised in something between a wave and a benediction. The theft was confirmed. The families were named. And the river of attention had already found its next channel.

Ilse stood at the window of the mission clinic's back room—she had not gone back to the boarding house; it felt too much like a place you stayed—and watched rain come down on Rivergate in long grey sheets. Brannoch was two doors down, sleeping off the fever that came and went with the bullet wound, complaining even in his sleep about the quality of the mission's coffee. Oscar was somewhere across the city, at the annex, or the hangar, or wherever a person went when they no longer had a reason to run.

And the chest sat under the cot, wrapped in oilcloth, holding nothing but paper and weight.

That was the strange arithmetic of it now. The copies were the cargo—that had been true since the night Ilse first confessed to transcribing the ledgers by lamplight while the monsoon hammered the deck above her head. Every page, copied twice, the duplicates hidden in separate places: one set sewn into the lining of her coat, one set in a tin box buried beneath the floorboards of this very room. The chest itself was an empty shell wearing its own history, a reliquary without a relic. It had crossed four hundred miles of river, survived fire and flood and a cartel's guns, and what remained inside was only the original paper—the thing everyone had been willing to kill for, and which now, officially authenticated, belonged to a record office that would shelve it in a climate-controlled room where no one would ever weep over it again.

Ilse had thought, for years, that this moment would feel like victory. She had rehearsed it in the dark of a hundred rented rooms: the ledgers read aloud, the names spoken into the public record, her grandmother Vessa Marta Ilse Kovač—clerk third class, erased along with everything else—restored to history by her granddaughter's stubbornness. She had imagined weeping. She had imagined lightness.

What she felt instead was the specific grief of a journey ending before its destination did.

Because the hearings had covered the fund. They had covered Corven, and the families, and the laundering through the cartel's shipping concerns. What they had not covered—not once, not in six weeks of testimony—were the villages. The places that had existed on no map for forty years because the maps themselves had been part of the erasure. Sarnovets. The twin settlements at Kell's Bend. The fishing towns up past the Two Sisters whose names had been struck from the survey rolls so thoroughly that even the people who'd grown up in them sometimes doubted they'd existed. The Provision had built half the river towns, yes—but the other half had been starved *by* the Provision, their allotments diverted, their existence inconvenient to the ledger, and when they died the death was recorded as weather.

The old woman in the village that remembered—that was the name Ilse had given it in her mind, though it had a real name too, one she'd written down carefully—had held the transcription pages against her chest and said: *My mother waited for that money until the day she died. She thought the river had lost it.*

The river hadn't lost it. Men had spent it. And somewhere upstream there were more mothers' daughters who still believed in the river's carelessness, because the truth had never traveled that far.

Ilse turned from the window and looked at the chest.

*You're not done,* she told it. *Neither am I.*

* * *

She went to find Oscar at the hangar, because some conversations you needed to have standing in the right place, and this one belonged among the smell of dope and fabric and engine oil, under the high corrugated roof where the rain made its own percussion section.

Oscar was under the plane—or what remained of the plane. Corven's men had sabotaged it at the fuel stop a lifetime ago, and the repairs had proceeded in the slow, piecemeal way of things done by someone who wasn't sure yet whether they wanted the machine to fly again. The cowling was off. The propeller leaned against the wall like a retired soldier. Oscar's legs stuck out from beneath the fuselage, and when Ilse's boots splashed across the concrete, they didn't move.

"I can hear you thinking," Oscar said, from under the engine. "It's loud."

"Is it?"

"Louder than the rain." Oscar slid out, sat up, wiped both hands down a rag that had stopped being useful sometime last month. There was grease on one cheekbone and something new in the set of the mouth—a stillness that hadn't been there on the river. "You've decided something."

Ilse laughed despite herself. "You used to say you never learned names too well. Now you're reading my mind."

"I never learned names. I learned you." Oscar said it plainly, without ceremony, the way you'd report the weather, and then seemed to hear it and looked away, busying the hands with the rag. "Go on. Tell me."

"The chest goes to the museum."

Oscar nodded slowly. "The river museum. The one on the embankment with the paddle-wheel out front."

"They want it. The curator came to the hearings—sat in the third row every day, taking notes. He asked me afterward, very politely, as if he were asking to borrow sugar. He said—" Ilse closed her eyes to get the words exactly right, because exactness mattered here. "He said, *A thing like that shouldn't live in a vault. A thing like that should be where children put their hands on the glass.*"

"And the copies?"

"That's the part I have to tell you sitting down, probably, so let's both pretend I'm not nervous." She pulled a crate over and sat, and Oscar stayed cross-legged on the floor, and the rain filled the pause like a third person clearing their throat. "I'm taking them upriver. All of them. To the villages."

Silence. Not the empty kind—the kind where someone is being very careful.

"Sarnovets," Ilse said. "Kell's Bend. The settlements past the Two Sisters. Every place whose name got struck from the rolls. I'm going to read them their own history, Oscar. I'm going to stand in whatever's left of those towns and read aloud the line items that prove their grandmothers didn't imagine the famine, that the money existed, that it was taken. My grandmother smuggled these ledgers out of Vessany in a flour sack with her body wrapped around them, and she died on a dock in a country that had already forgotten her country existed, and I will not—" Her voice cracked, and she let it, because she was done smoothing herself over for anyone's comfort. "I will not let the truth stop at the courthouse door just because the courthouse is comfortable."

Oscar was quiet a long moment. Then: "How long?"

"A season. Maybe two. However long the villages take."

"You'll go by boat."

"There's no other way to reach half of them. The channels silted after the erasure. Half the maps are lies and the other half are optimistic." She smiled, thin. "Brannoch knows a trader running supplies as far as the Two Sisters. After that, I'll walk if I have to."

"And the coat?" Oscar's eyes flicked to her shoulders, where forty years of stolen history rode in the lining, lighter than a wool jacket had any right to be. "You'll carry them on your body through cartel water."

"I carried them through worse."

"No," Oscar said quietly. "You carried them through a burning boat and a leaking hull and a checkpoint where a man with forged papers bluffed a cartel with brandy. That was with us. This would be alone."

"It would."

Another silence, longer. Outside, the rain eased from sheets to strings, and the light through the hangar's open door went the color of pewter. Ilse watched Oscar's hands, which had gone still around the rag—still in the way of a person holding something down.

"You could come," Ilse said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't take it back.

"I know." Oscar looked at the plane. At the propeller leaning against the wall. At the hangar doors and the grey river beyond them, and Ilse understood that she was watching a person measure the distance between two lives—one measured in miles, one in mornings. "I almost forgot how to stay anywhere," Oscar said finally. "Then I remembered I'd never actually tried."

"So that's a no."

"That's a *not yet*. Which on the river means the same thing but takes longer to sink." Oscar stood, offered a grease-dark hand, pulled Ilse to her feet. "The chest goes to the museum. When?"

"Tuesday. The curator wants to open it publicly. He wants—" She hesitated. "He asked if you'd be there. Both of us. He said the chest should arrive with witnesses."

Oscar huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Witnesses. We've been fugitives for a season and now we're witnesses."

"That's what winning looks like, apparently. You get reclassified."

* * *

Tuesday came bright, the first clean morning in weeks, the kind of day the river seemed to apologize for the monsoon. The embankment was crowded beyond anything Ilse had expected—not a crowd, exactly, but a gathering: dockhands off shift, women from the market with baskets set down at their feet, a cluster of mission nurses, old men in coats too heavy for the weather, and near the front, in a wheeled chair pushed by one of the clinic orderlies, Brannoch, shaved and furious about it, his bandaged shoulder making him look like a man who'd dressed hastily for his own funeral.

They brought the chest down the gangway between them. It was heavier than it had any right to be, or perhaps Ilse's arms had simply learned its weight so well that carrying it differently felt like carrying a stranger. Oilcloth peeled back on the museum steps. The seal of Vessany caught the sun—the double river and the broken crown, the emblem of a country that existed now only in wax and memory—and a sound went through the gathering that Ilse had never heard before and would never forget: not a gasp, not a murmur, but a low collective exhale, like a tide drawing back, like forty years leaving a room all at once.

The curator unlocked the case himself. Inside, on a bed of dark cloth, beside the ledgers and the correspondence and the list of names, he placed a single blank card, and on it, in careful ink:

*Recovered from the flooded channel below Kell's Bend. Carried by many hands. Belonged to everyone.*

"Belonged to everyone," Brannoch repeated from his chair, gruff, testing the phrase like a bad tooth. Then, quieter, to no one: "Aye. That'll do."

Children pressed to the glass within the hour, exactly as the curator had promised. Ilse watched a girl of maybe eight trace the Vessany seal with one finger, hovering a hair's breadth above, and saw the girl turn to her mother and ask a question, and saw the mother kneel and begin to answer, and Ilse had to walk to the rail and look at the water for a while.

This was what letting go was, she realized. Not the opening of hands. The discovery that the thing had never been yours to hold—that it had passed through you the way the river passes through a country, belonging always to the banks, never to the current.

* * *

They loaded the copies onto Brannoch's friend's supply boat on Friday, in the blue hour before dawn, when the embankment lamps still burned and the mist stood on the water in columns like the ghosts of masts.

Ilse had expected the goodbye to be hard. What she hadn't expected was how small it would be. No speeches. Oscar walked her down the dock with a lantern, and Brannoch followed at his own pace, refusing help, and the three of them stood at the gangplank in the cold while the trader's boy stowed crates overhead.

"Two Sisters first," Brannoch said. "Tell the ferryman Ostrek that Brannoch says his brandy debt is paid. He'll feed you whether he believes it or not."

"And after the Two Sisters?" Oscar asked.

"After that, walk north until the maps run out, then keep going." Brannoch dug in his coat and produced a parcel wrapped in sailcloth, shoved it at her with the delicacy of a man handling something explosive. "Coffee. Real stuff, not the mission's dishwater. And a letter of introduction to a woman at Sarnovets who owes me a favor and hates owing it, so she'll be extra generous. Go on. Take it before I change my mind and drink the coffee myself."

Ilse took it. It was warm from his body. She found, abruptly, that she couldn't speak.

Oscar stepped closer. In the lantern light, the face she had first seen across a passenger manifest—wary, amused, already halfway to gone—was steady and fully present, and Ilse understood that this, too, was something the journey had built: not trust exactly, and not love in any word she knew, but the knowledge of exactly where another person kept their courage and their wounds.

"Read them loudly," Oscar said. "In the squares. Wherever there's still a square. Don't soften the numbers."

"I never soften numbers."

"I know. It's why I let you navigate." Oscar's mouth quirked. Then, softer: "Come back downriver when the season turns. The hangar will have a name on it by then. I haven't picked it yet."

"Pick something Vessany," Ilse said. "Something they erased. Let the maps start lying in reverse."

Oscar considered that, and something moved behind the eyes—grief and purpose shaking hands. "Maybe I will."

The boy called the departure. Ilse climbed the gangplank with the copies riding against her spine and her grandmother's name in her mouth like a coin under the tongue, and at the rail she turned and looked back one last time—at the two figures on the dock, the lame trader and the grounded pilot, the family she had found in the worst waters of her life, shrinking into the lamplight as the boat swung its bow upstream.

The river took her north. Behind her, Rivergate dimmed. Ahead, the mist parted in slow columns, showing her the channel markers, then the far banks, then the first pale suggestion of the country that had been erased—and waiting somewhere inside it, patient as sediment, the villages that did not yet know they had been avenged.

She unwrapped Brannoch's parcel, poured coffee into the cap of a thermos, and drank it scalding, watching the water open ahead of her like a road.

At the Two Sisters, the ferryman Ostrek read Brannoch's note twice, went pale, and fed her like a queen. And it was there, in the ferry house, drying her socks by his stove, that the boy who ran messages between the settlements mentioned—casually, wiping down the counter, not looking at her—that a pilot had come through the week before, asking after the channels north of the bend. Asking, specifically, about Sarnovets.

Asking, the boy said, in a voice that made the ferryman's wife go quiet in the kitchen.

Ilse set down her cup very slowly, and asked him to describe the pilot.
