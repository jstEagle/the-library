## 37. The Hangar

The hangar smelled the way all hangars smell—oil and rust and old canvas, the ghost of every flight that had ever ended inside it—but this one smelled like mine.

I stood in the doorway a long time before I went in. That was the thing nobody tells you about coming home: it doesn't feel like arriving. It feels like standing at the edge of a room you've been thrown out of, waiting for someone to tell you you're still not wanted.

The plane sat where they'd towed her, nose to the river door, one wheel chocked with a split log because whoever'd done it hadn't bothered to find anything proper. Sable's men had gone through her in Rivergate before the hearings started—looking for what, I never learned—and they'd left her gutted in the particular way officials gut things: everything unscrewed and catalogued, nothing actually broken, a paper tag on the fuel line like a mortuary bracelet. *Item: carburetor float. Condition: serviceable.* As if she were dead and they were listing her effects.

She wasn't dead. She was waiting. There's a difference, and I could hear it in the way the morning wind moved through the open doors and made the control cables hum, low, like a throat clearing.

"You going to stand there all day," Brannoch said from somewhere behind the tail, "or are you going to help me get this cowling off?"

He was already elbow-deep in the engine compartment, sleeves rolled over forearms mottled and scarred where the cartel bullet had gone through him six weeks ago, his one eye narrowed at the manifold like it owed him money. He moved stiffly still. The clinic at Two Sisters had patched him well enough to argue, which by their standards was a full recovery.

"How long have you been here?"

"Since dawn." He didn't look up. "Somebody had to make sure the river rats didn't strip her for aluminum."

"Nobody strips planes for aluminum."

"Somebody would've tried." He straightened, wincing, and wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last decade. "Word gets around. The pilot who pulled the chest out of the channel. People come to look. Some of them look with their hands."

I came into the light under the corrugated roof and put my palm flat against her fuselage. Cool metal, river-damp, real. Forty years of my life I'd spent touching machines only long enough to leave them again, and here I was, and my hand wouldn't move.

"Well," Brannoch said, watching me with that eye of his, the one that saw more than the two most people are born with. "You going to cry, or are we working?"

"We're working."

"Good. Hand me the spanner. And don't hand me the wrong one, or I'll tell everyone in Rivergate the famous salvage pilot can't tell a nine from an eleven."

* * *

We worked through the morning, and the work was its own kind of prayer, the kind I'd forgotten how to say.

There is a language between a pilot and a machine, and it isn't words. It's torque and tolerance, the specific resistance of a bolt that's seated right versus one that's about to strip, the way a wire harness lies flat when it's happy and kinks when something upstream is wrong. My hands remembered all of it even while the rest of me was still deciding whether I belonged anywhere. That's the mercy of work. It asks nothing of your heart. It just asks for your hands, and your hands are always honest.

The carburetor went back in clean. The fuel lines flushed clear—the tags came off one by one, and I kept them, folded them into my breast pocket without quite admitting why. The magneto tested true. Brannoch crawled under her with a trouble lamp and pronounced the landing gear "ugly but honest," which from him was a eulogy and a christening both.

It was past noon when he said it, casual as passing a tool, head still buried in the wheel well.

"So. When do you fly out?"

I didn't answer right away. I torqued down the last of the cowl fasteners and listened to them click home, four small sounds, each one final.

"I don't know," I said.

He backed out from under the wing and sat down hard on an oil drum, breathing a little heavy, and looked at me across the hangar floor with its drifts of dust and pigeon feathers and forty years of other pilots' leavings.

"You don't know." He turned it over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "Oscar Yarrow, who once told me—I quote, because I have the memory for it and you don't—that a man who sleeps two nights in the same bed starts to rot. You don't know."

"The monsoon's late this year," I said. "River's high. Runways upriver are soup."

"It's October. The monsoon ended three weeks ago and you know it better than I do."

"I'm waiting on parts."

"The parts are sitting in that crate you haven't opened."

I looked at the crate. He was right. It had come up from Rivergate on the mail barge two days ago, stenciled with the supplier's mark, sitting unopened by the hangar wall like a guest I hadn't invited. Everything I needed to fly out was in it. Everything I needed to stay was standing in front of me, wiping grease on his trousers, waiting for an answer I didn't have yet.

Brannoch watched me not answer, and then—he was capable of this, though he rationed it like wartime sugar—he let it go.

"There's coffee," he said, hauling himself up. "Terrible coffee. Ilse sent it up from the mission clinic with a note that says if you drink it black you're a coward and if you drink it sweet you're a sentimentalist, so choose your sins."

"What did you choose?"

"I drank it both ways and told her she was right twice." He shuffled toward the little office cubby at the hangar's end, where somebody—Ilse, months ago, in another life—had left a tin mug on the desk. "Come on. The engine will keep. Engines always keep. It's pilots who don't."

* * *

The coffee was terrible. I drank it black, then added sugar to the second cup out of spite, and Brannoch grinned at me around his own mug like a man who'd won a bet with himself.

The office had a window looking out over the field, such as it was—a mown strip of grass between the hangar and the levee, the river beyond it running wide and brown-gold in the afternoon light, barges crawling upcurrent with their smoke smeared sideways by the wind. On the sill, someone had set a jar of wildflowers. Purple loosestrife and river daisies, the kind that grow in the mud along the Provision canals, the ones the old woman in the remembering village had pressed into Ilse's hands.

I knew whose flowers those were. I knew whose handwriting was on the note taped to the coffee tin. I knew whose transcriptions of the Vessany ledgers were sitting in a courthouse safe in Rivergate right now, and whose testimony had emptied half the seats in the gallery and filled the other half with reporters.

And I knew, the way you know weather coming, that Ilse was packing.

She'd been packing for a week. Not saying it. Doing it the way she did everything—quietly, thoroughly, in the margins, so that by the time she told you it was already fact. The copies were going upriver. Every erased village, every town the maps had scrubbed of Vessany's name, every place where an old woman might read a ledger line and finally learn why the school never got built. She'd shown me the route once, drawn in pencil on the back of a hearing summons, and her finger had stopped at the top of the page, at the blank space above the last marked channel, and she'd said, "Nobody's flown supply up there since the erasure. Nobody's carried medicine there in forty years."

She hadn't asked me to come. That was the worst of it. She'd drawn the route and folded the paper and put it away, and she'd looked at me with those gray eyes that lied badly and hoped loudly, and she'd said nothing at all, because she understood—maybe better than I did—that a man who's spent his whole life leaving can't be pushed toward staying. He has to walk into it himself, and it has to hurt, and it has to be his.

"Yarrow." Brannoch set his mug down. "Look at me a minute."

I looked.

"That plane out there—" he nodded through the window at her, silvering in the sun, "—she'll be ready to fly tomorrow. Day after, certainly. And there's nothing keeping you in Rivergate. Corven walked. The hearings are done. Your name's in every paper from here to the coast, half of them calling you a hero and half calling you a liar, and neither lot will remember by Christmas. You could be over the ridge by Thursday and nobody would think less of you." He paused. "Except me. But I think less of everybody, so it balances."

"And you?" I said. "What's keeping you?"

"My debts are paid, my boat's at the bottom of the river, and my body's held together with clinic thread and spite." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him; I watched him pay for it. "A man my age doesn't start over. He just decides where to finish. I'm deciding." He looked out at the river. "I'm leaning toward here. There's a wake coming, you know. For the boat. Half the town's coming. Word travels."

"A wake for a boat."

"She was a good boat. Rotten, leaky, ugly, and good. She earned it." He drained his coffee and stood. "So did we, some of us. Come on. Cowl's not going to fit itself, and I want to see her whole before dark."

* * *

We finished at dusk.

The last job was the smallest: the placard. Every bush plane I'd ever known carried one, riveted inside the cockpit door—registration number, owner's name, home field. Mine had always read the same way for twenty years: a registration, my name, and *home field: none*, because the registrar in Rivergate had required something and I'd written *none* and dared him to object. He hadn't. Nobody ever objects to a man who's telling the truth about having nowhere.

Brannoch held the new placard while I drilled the holes, and I'd written it out the night before in ink, no crossings-out, because I'd known by then—even if I hadn't let myself know—that this was coming:

**OX-114 — OSCAR YARROW**
**HOME FIELD: RIVERGATE**

Four words. It took me twenty years and a stolen chest and a burned boat and a bullet in a friend to write them, and the drill bit went through the aluminum like it was nothing at all.

"There," Brannoch said, when the rivets were set. He stepped back and looked at her, whole now, cowl fitted, gear checked, fuel tanks sealed and full, the evening light running down her flanks like water. "She's a beauty. Ugly, but a beauty."

"You already used that joke."

"It's a good joke. Good jokes bear repeating. Like bad coffee." He clapped me on the shoulder, gently, on the uninjured side, which was his way of saying things he couldn't say. Then he gathered his tools, because Brannoch treated tools the way other men treated children, and headed for the hangar door. "Lock up when you're done. Or don't. Nothing in here worth stealing except the plane, and nobody steals a plane with a name on the hangar."

I stood alone with her in the blue hour, the river wind coming through the doors, the first stars pricking out over the levee.

Home field: Rivergate.

I opened the cockpit door and climbed in, just to sit—not to fly, just to sit where I'd sat ten thousand times, hands resting on the yoke, the placard new and bright at my shoulder. From here I could see the whole river road, the lamps of the town coming on one by one, the annex roof where the records had burned, the courthouse where Ilse had stood for an hour and spent a lifetime of hiding. Somewhere out there, upriver, in the dark past the last marked channel, were villages that hadn't seen a supply plane in forty years. Villages with names that had been taken from them. Villages that were going to get their names back, one pencil line at a time, if a certain stubborn woman had anything to say about it—which she always did.

My hands rested on the yoke, and for the first time in my life they weren't gripping it.

That was how I knew. Not with a decision, exactly. Decisions are loud things, doors slamming, engines turning over. This was quieter. This was the way fog lifts—you can't point to the moment, but suddenly the channel is there in front of you, and you can see all the way down it, and you wonder why you ever thought there was nothing there but white.

I'd spent my whole life learning how to leave. Nobody had ever taught me how to arrive. But it turned out arrival wasn't a skill. It was just a choice, made small and daily, the way you check a fuel line or torque a bolt: *stay today. Stay tomorrow. Keep staying until staying is the shape your life makes.*

I climbed out and locked the hangar, and then stood a moment in the dark with my hand flat on the door, feeling the metal hold the day's warmth.

Behind me, footsteps on the gravel road—two sets, one uneven, one quick and light. Brannoch's voice, rumbling something I couldn't catch. And Ilse's laugh, carrying across the field the way her voice always did, too big for her, too honest for hiding.

They came up the drive together, lantern swinging, and Ilse stopped when she saw me standing there in the dark beside the hangar with the new name painted over the door, and I watched her read it, and I watched her understand it, and I watched her decide—deliberately, the way she decided everything—not to make it easy for either of us.

"Good," she said. "Because I need a pilot."

Brannoch grinned in the lantern light. "Told you she'd ask."

"She hasn't asked anything yet," I said.

"No," Ilse agreed, and held up a rolled chart, the route penciled up its whole length, past the last marked channel, into the blank. "But I'm about to."
