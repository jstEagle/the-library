## 38. A Wake on the Water

The boat had a name, once. I'd painted it myself, thirty years back, on the transom of her first hull before the river ate that hull and gave me the second, and then the third, and then the patched and scorched and half-drowned thing that carried two fools and a chest of dead men's paper down through the monsoon to Rivergate. *Magpie*, she'd been. For the bird that steals bright things and hoards them in a nest of thorns. My brother chose it. My brother's been dead longer than most of the people at this wake have been alive, and I never once said his name aloud in all that time until tonight.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Old traders get ahead of themselves. It's the river's doing — it teaches you that everything arrives at once or not at all, and so you learn to grab the whole mouthful when it comes.

* * *

The town of Rivergate's edge — the mudflat quarter, the one the maps call the Landing and the people call the Steps — turned out in a way I hadn't seen since the flood year. Word travels down a river the way oil travels on water, thin and fast and finding every crack. By the time the sun went down behind the fuel docks there must have been two hundred souls on the bank, and more coming in from upriver in longboats with lanterns hung off their prows like a procession of fireflies that had decided, for one night, to be orderly about it.

I stood at the top of the Steps with my bad arm still in its sling — the bullet had gone through the meat of the shoulder, clean in, clean out, and the mission doctor said I'd fly a kite again someday, which was his idea of a joke, since the only thing I'd ever flown was debt — and I watched them come, and I'll tell you honestly, I didn't know what they'd come for.

I'd have understood it if they'd come to gawk. A town likes a spectacle. The hearings had been in every paper for a month; the name Vessany, which forty years ago had been scraped off every signpost and schoolroom wall, was suddenly back in the mouths of everyone from the fishmongers to the magistrate's own clerks, said in the careful hushed voice people use for a ghost they've decided to be polite to. The chest itself had gone to the river museum, behind glass, with a placard, and I'd stood in front of that placard for a long time the day they installed it, reading my own name in the provenance note — *recovered from the flooded Vessany channel by O. Yarrow, I. Vess, and B. Brannoch* — and feeling like a man reading his own headstone and finding it misspelled.

But they hadn't come to gawk at a museum. They'd come for the boat.

*Magpie* lay on the mud below the Steps where the salvage crew had hauled her, and she was a ruin. The fire had taken her stern cabin to the ribs. The starboard side was stove in where the cartel's second boat had rammed us, and the patch we'd thrown over it in the shallows had peeled back like old skin. Her name was still legible on the transom, though — the fire had blackened the wood around it but the letters themselves, the ones I'd painted with my brother laughing at me for using too much oil in the paint, had survived. *MAGPIE*. You could read it by lantern light from the top of the Steps, and people were reading it. You could see them mouthing it.

"They think she's theirs," Ilse said beside me. She'd come up quietly, the way she does, though she's gotten less quiet over the months — the hearings had burned something out of her, or burned something off of her, and what was left underneath was a woman who stood with her weight on both feet. "The boat. Look at them. Half these people crossed on her at some point, or their goods did, or their letters. I asked the woman selling the grilled corn. Brannoch, she says, that boat carried my mother to the clinic at Two Sisters when the fever came. That boat carried the schoolbooks. That boat carried my brother home in a coffin and wouldn't take payment."

"Did it," I said.

"You didn't know?"

"I never learned names too well." I said it and heard whose rule I was quoting, and Ilse heard it too, and we both looked down the bank to where Oscar stood at the water's edge with their back to the crowd, talking to the salvage master about the engine block, which was the only part of the boat worth anything now and which Oscar had already, I knew, arranged to have pulled and stored in the hangar. Not to sell. To keep. Oscar hadn't said that out loud. Oscar doesn't say things like that out loud. But I've watched a lot of people on a lot of boats, and I know what it means when a person saves the engine and lets the hull go.

* * *

The old river custom — and it's an old one, older than Vessany, older probably than the Provision, older than anything the hearings were going to untangle — is that when a working boat dies, she gets a wake, and the wake is held on the water, and everyone who ever rode her brings a light.

I hadn't held one in thirty years. The last one had been for my brother's boat, which was also my brother, more or less, the way a man and his work get braided together on a river, and after the fire on the water that took him I'd made a private arrangement with myself that there would be no more wakes, because a wake means you stop, and stopping means you remember, and remembering was a thing I'd gotten very good at not doing. One eye's good for that, actually. People think the loss is the seeing. It isn't. The loss is that half your past sits permanently in the dark, and you learn to let it stay there.

But Ilse had gone through the Landing two days before, house to house, with a list of names she'd transcribed out of the ledgers — not the thieves' names, the other names. The clerks. The ferrymen. The midwives the Provision had paid, the lock-keepers, the schoolteachers, the whole quiet civil service of a country that had been erased so thoroughly that its own descendants didn't know it had existed. And she'd found, in that mudflat quarter, people whose grandparents had those names, and she'd told them about the wake, and that's why they came. Not for a ruined hull. For the country the hull had accidentally been carrying.

"You understand what you've started," I said to her.

"No," she said. "Tell me."

"You've started a funeral for a nation, and the nation didn't get the notice until today, and now it's going to stand on a mudflat eating grilled corn and crying about it, and there's no procedure for that. No hearings. No verdict. It's just going to be people."

Ilse looked out at the crowd for a while. The lanterns were coming down the Steps now, dozens of them, handed out from a barrel of river-oil lamps that the salvage master had donated without being asked, and each person took one and lit it from their neighbor, and the flame went down the line faster than I could track.

"Good," she said.

* * *

We launched the lights at full dark.

The custom is you wade out or take small boats, and you set your lamp on the water beside the dead boat, one light for every soul she carried, and you don't count, because the river counts. The current took the lamps and strung them out downstream in a long trembling line, past the hull, past the fuel docks, out toward the channel markers, and from the bank it looked like the river had grown a spine of fire, or like the boat had a soul after all and it was leaving, and leaving in order, and leaving slowly, the way the decent dead do.

Oscar stood shin-deep in the water setting the first lamp, and I want to write down what that looked like, because I don't trust my memory to keep it and this paper might.

Oscar Yarrow, who never lands twice in the same place, who never learned names, who carried nothing but cargo for twenty years because cargo doesn't die on you — Oscar stood in the river in front of two hundred strangers and set a lamp on the water with both hands, careful, like it was a patient, and then stood there watching it go, and the light came up off the water onto their face, and everyone on the bank could see it, and Oscar let them see it. That was the whole miracle, if you want one. Not the chest, not the hearings, not the ledgers. A person who had spent a lifetime being unseeable, standing in a river being seen, and not bolting.

The crowd had gone quiet. River-quiet, the hush you get when the water's doing something worth watching. And then an old woman near the front — the corn-seller, I think, or her mother — started to sing. Not a hymn. A work song, a hauling song, the kind ferrymen used to sing pushing against the current, and I knew it, God help me, I knew it from before, from Vessany, from the erased time, and my throat closed up like a hatch in weather.

Because here's the thing I hadn't said out loud yet, the thing I'd been hauling around since the clinic, since the confession, since Oscar sat on the edge of my cot and forgave me without making me beg for it, which was worse than begging, which was infinitely worse, because it meant the debt was real and the forgiveness was free and I'd have to live with the arithmetic: I had been on the river a long time, and I had carried a lot of cargo, and I had never once asked what was in the crates. That was my rule, same as Oscar's, and I'd told myself it was professionalism. It was cowardice with a professional coat on. Half the cartel's money had moved on my decks in the bad years, in boxes marked as machine parts and dried fish, and I'd sung hauling songs over it and never asked, because asking would have made me a witness and witnesses drown. The Provision's stolen silver had passed under my one good eye a dozen times, headed for the same families the hearings were naming, and I'd taken my fee and kept my mouth shut and called it survival.

And then a chest floated by, and I took it aboard, and for once the bright thing the magpie stole was the one bright thing on the whole river that wanted stealing.

So when the song went up, I sang. Badly. Off-key, with a voice like a rusted winch, in a language half the crowd didn't know and the other half only knew from grandmothers. I sang for my brother. I sang for the boat. I sang for Sable, who was somewhere downriver or upriver or nowhere, half-drowned twice over and gone from all our lives like a lamp the current took. I sang for the old woman's mother and the coffin at Two Sisters and every crate I never opened. And the crowd picked it up, the way crowds do, and the song went down the bank and out over the water and tangled up with the line of lights, and I stood there in the mud with my sling and my bad eye and my worse history, and I was, for the first time since I was a young man with two eyes and a brother, public. Known. Filed under my own name in the open record of a town that had decided to keep me.

Found family, Ilse called it once, around the driftwood fire, back when it was still a dangerous thing to say. Made public, I'd call it now. There's a difference. A family you find can stay hidden. A family the town sees — that one's permanent. That one has a name on a hangar and a seat at the corn-seller's stall and a lamp on the river with your debt attached to it, paid.

* * *

Later, when the lamps had gone out around the bend and the crowd was thinning into small knots of people reluctant to end the night, the three of us sat on the Steps with our feet hanging over the water, which is a stupid thing for a man my age to do, and I did it anyway.

Oscar had the engine block story half-told — the salvage master was going to clean it, true it, keep it oiled, and there was a hangar at the north end with room in it and, Oscar said, in the tone of someone reading a weather report, a name going up over the door next week. *Yarrow & Brannoch, Air and River.* I hadn't agreed to it. I hadn't needed to. Oscar had just started telling people it was true, which is how Oscar makes things true, apparently, and I found I didn't mind. A man can spend thirty years being nobody's partner and then one morning discover he's a signboard.

"Ilse," I said. "When do you go?"

She didn't pretend not to know what I meant. She'd been packing for three days, not hiding it, the transcriptions in oilcloth, the copies of the copies, the whole smuggled country in two satchels that weighed less than a crate of dried fish. Upriver. To the erased villages, the ones whose names weren't on any map anymore, to hand people their own grandparents' signatures and let them do whatever they wanted with them.

"Three days," she said. "After the last of the hearings. There's a trader running a barge as far as the old Vessany locks, and past that I walk."

"Alone?"

"For a while." She looked at me, and then at Oscar, and whatever she saw in our faces made her almost smile. "Not alone alone. You two would slow me down. One of you can't walk on that arm and the other one can't walk anywhere without an engine."

"Rude," said Oscar.

"Accurate," said Ilse.

The river moved under us, black and patient, carrying the last of the light out toward the sea or the source, depending on how you think about it. Down at the waterline, *Magpie* settled into the mud a little deeper, going about the business of becoming a story. In three days Ilse would be gone upriver with the truth in a bag. In a week the hangar would have a name on it. In a month, I supposed, there'd be letters — Ilse wrote letters, I'd learned that, real ones, on paper, sent the slow way, because some things you don't trust to the current or the radio — and the letters would come to a hangar with a name over the door, to two men who had one, and that was the whole of it, that was the entire astonishing arrangement: we had addresses now. We had places where news could find us.

I was thinking that, feeling it settle in my chest like a well-set load, when the boy came down the Steps.

He was one of the hearing clerks, still in his day clothes, running, which on those stairs is a good way to die, and he came skidding to a stop in front of us with a telegram flapping in his hand and no breath left to read it with. He held it out toward Oscar, then seemed to reconsider, and held it toward all three of us, which was correct, because nothing on this river had belonged to only one of us since the night the chest came aboard.

Oscar took it. Read it. Read it again, the way you read something twice when the second time is for the people standing next to you.

"It's from Two Sisters," Oscar said. "The mission clinic." A pause, the kind of pause a river leaves before the rapids. "Sable checked herself out three days ago. Left against medical advice." Another pause, longer. "And she left something for us. A package. The clerk says she told them—" Oscar stopped, and looked upriver, where the water runs dark toward the old locks and everything Ilse was about to carry and everything we thought was finished.

"She told them it was Corven's route book," Oscar said. "The real one. The one from before the hearings. And a note that says — *you'll want this before the appeal.*"

The appeal. The word went through the three of us like cold water through a bilge. Because Corven had walked, and walked men appeal, and appeals need records, and somewhere upriver a burned annex and a vanished enforcer had just stopped being finished business.

Ilse stood up first. She was already looking at the current, doing the arithmetic of barges and days.

"Well," I said, and reached down for the lamp I'd set beside me, still burning. "Somebody ought to see her safely to the locks."

Oscar looked at me. Ilse looked at me. And the river, which takes everything, gave us back one more errand.
