## 39. Upriver

The fog came off the Rivergate in the hour before dawn the way it always had, rising off the water like breath off a sleeping animal, and for the first time in nineteen years of flying I was on the ground to watch it happen.

I stood on the apron with a mug of coffee going cold in my hands and watched the river disappear into itself. The mist took the far bank first, then the moored barges, then the pilings of the fuel dock, until there was nothing left but white and the sound of water moving somewhere beneath it. Once—that felt like another man's life now—I would have been airborne by now, climbing through that white on instruments and instinct, gone before the town woke up, my name known to no one at all.

Now my name was painted on the hangar door behind me. YARROW AIR SERVICE, in letters Brannoch had insisted on doing himself, because, he said, if a man was going to be fool enough to stay in one place, he ought at least to be fool enough to sign his work. He'd gotten the lettering slightly crooked—the R leaned like a drunk against the A—and when I'd pointed it out he'd said that was so people would know a human being had done it and not a machine, and I hadn't argued. There was no arguing with Brannoch about anything anymore. There was only feeding him, bandaging him, and listening to him complain, which was its own kind of love, though neither of us would have used the word out loud.

The plane sat where I'd left her the night before, tied down under the lean-to, still wearing the new fabric on her left wing where the cartel fire had never touched her but the years had. Six months of work. New cylinders, new wiring run clean through the fuselage, a propeller I'd traded two months of ferry runs to get. She wasn't the same airplane she'd been. Neither was I. We were both patched, both heavier than we used to be, and both, somehow, more willing to carry weight.

I finished the coffee, checked the sky—high thin cloud, wind out of the southwest, no weather coming that mattered—and went inside to load.

* * *

The cargo manifest hung on a clipboard by the hangar's side door, and it was the most beautiful document I had ever owned, though it looked like nothing: a page of pencil lines in Brannoch's crabbed hand. Insulin, cold box, Dr. Amara at Two Sisters. Sutures and penicillin, the mission clinic at Corven's Reach—they still called it that, the district; you can't rename a place by verdict, only by time. School books, forty kilos, the village at the old Vessany ford. Seed potatoes. A crate of nails. Two sacks of salt. And at the bottom, in a line all its own, underlined twice:

**Letters — do not forget the letters again or I will hear about it from four separate grandmothers.**

I hadn't forgotten them. I'd been carrying letters since the second month, ever since the morning an old woman named Maren Oduya had stopped me on the dock outside the hearings hall—I was smoking, which I'd taken up again during the trial weeks, and quit again the day after—and said, without any introduction at all: "You're the pilot who brought the chest down. My son is at the mission past Two Sisters. Do you fly there?"

I said I did.

"Then you'll take letters," she said, and handed me three envelopes sealed with candle wax, and that was the whole conversation. That was how it started. By the third trip there were eleven envelopes. By the sixth there were thirty, and a woman at the fish market paid me in smoked eel, and a boy of maybe nine gave me a drawing of an airplane done in charcoal on the back of a flour sack, and I kept the drawing pinned above the manifest board where I'd see it every morning, because it was the first thing anyone had ever made for me that assumed I'd come back.

That had been the old rule, once. Never land twice in the same place. Never learn names too well. Never carry anything but cargo. I'd built those rules the way a man builds a seawall, one stone at a time, after the channel where Aldous went down, after every departure that got easier because it was practiced. The rules had kept me alive. They had also kept me alone, and I had told myself for nineteen years that those were the same thing.

They weren't. I knew that now the way you know a thing after it has cost you everything: completely, and too late to matter, except that it does matter, because knowing it is what lets you build something different.

So now I carried letters. Names, too. I knew that Dr. Amara at Two Sisters took her coffee bitter and her payment in news. I knew the schoolteacher at the Vessany ford was teaching the children a language that had been erased from the maps, out of a primer Ilse had copied by hand, and that the word for *river* in old Vessany was a word that meant *the thing that remembers*. I knew the mission clinic's roof leaked on the east side and I'd promised to bring sheet tin on the next run, and I had written it down, and I would bring it, because that was what a man with a home field did. He came back. That was the whole miracle of it. He came back.

* * *

I lifted off at first light, up through the fog with the wheels leaving the grass strip wet, and the river opened beneath me like a road being unrolled ahead of my own movement. This was the part that had never changed, the part no hearing or chest or grave could touch: the moment the ground fell away and the world became geography instead of obligation. The Rivergate ran silver below me, braided around its islands, brown-green and patient, carrying everything toward the sea and keeping nothing. I banked south along the channel and the sun came up over the flooded timber and turned the whole valley gold, and I thought, as I did most mornings now, that Aldous would have hated how happy this made me. He'd have said happiness made a pilot sloppy. Then he'd have grinned and asked to take the controls.

Two hours to Two Sisters. I dropped low over the checkpoint bend—no checkpoint anymore; the cartel men had melted away after the hearings, their patron families suddenly very interested in being respectable—and saw the mission clinic's tin roof winking through the trees. The strip there was a mowed pasture with a windsock made from a woman's red dress, and I landed the way I'd learned to land everywhere: slow, soft, ready to leave.

But not ready to leave. That was different. Ready to *stay a while*, which was a thing I'd never once been, and which turned out to feel almost identical to readiness, except warmer.

Dr. Amara met me at the fence with the cold box already open in her hands, because she'd heard me coming—everyone heard me coming; the whole valley knew the sound of my engine the way they knew the church bell—and she talked while she checked the insulin vials, talk being half her payment. The hearings had changed things slowly, she said. The Provision money was being re-administered, badly, bureaucratically, but administered. Two of the implicated families had settled quietly rather than face trial. Corven's name was mud in the papers and gold in the interior towns, depending on who you asked, and mostly nobody asked, because people were busy. People were building. There was a new school going up at the ford with Provision funds, actual funds, tracked, audited, real.

"And your enforcer," Amara said, handing back the empty cold box. "Sable. Someone said they saw her up past the falls. Running boats."

"Running boats," I repeated.

"That's what they said." She shrugged. "People change trades. Some of them even change."

I thought about Sable standing on my deck with river water still dripping off her, telling me about the burning annex because she owed me a life and chose to pay it forward instead of collecting. I hoped it was true, the boats. I hoped she was somewhere the water was fast and nobody gave her orders. Some debts you don't collect. You just let them float.

* * *

At the Vessany ford I delivered the school books and the seed potatoes and forty-one letters, and I stayed for lunch, which I had never once in my life done on a delivery stop, and which felt so strange that I kept checking the sky out of habit, expecting the fog of my old rules to roll in and make me leave. It didn't. The teacher—a round, patient man named Fess whose grandmother had been Vessany—fed me rice and dried fish and showed me the primer, Ilse's handwriting marching across every page in that tight, slanted script I'd last seen by lamplight in a leaking boat.

"She sends a new chapter every few months," Fess said. "Whoever carries it. Last time it was a one-eyed trader with a boat that smelled like brandy and regret."

"That sounds like Brannoch," I said.

"He said you'd say that."

We laughed, and the children laughed because the grown-ups were laughing, and none of them knew what the seal on the chest had cost, or what the ledgers had named, or why an old woman in a village downriver had wept when she read the names of the men who'd starved her mother. They just knew the words for *river* and *home* and *remember*, in a language the maps had tried to kill, and they said them the way children say anything: easily, as if the world had always contained them.

Ilse was somewhere upriver past the head of navigation, in the erased villages, carrying transcriptions and primers and whatever else she'd decided the truth needed in order to live. I got a letter from her maybe every six weeks, carried hand to hand down the trade routes until it reached Brannoch, who read it first, claimed he didn't, and then quoted it back to me verbatim while pretending he hadn't. Her letters were short and practical and, between the lines, the warmest things I owned. *The pass villages are rebuilding the shrine at Kell. Your engine sounds terrible in the recording Brannoch sent—check your number three cylinder. I found grandmother's sister's granddaughter. She has her hands.* That was all. That was everything. I wrote back the same way: cylinder fixed, weather bad, potatoes planted, thinking of you, which I put once, plainly, in the fourth letter, and she answered with a paragraph about river levels that somehow made it clear she'd read that line eleven times.

* * *

I was back on the strip by late afternoon, tying down in the long gold light, when Brannoch came limping across the grass with his bad leg and a piece of paper in his fist, walking the way he walked when he wanted to seem casual and failing completely.

"Letter came down on the afternoon barge," he said. "Marked urgent. Upriver postmark." He held it out and then, characteristically, didn't let go of it right away. "You want me to—"

"No," I said, and took it.

It was Ilse's handwriting, but wrong—hurried, the slant broken, written standing up somewhere, maybe on a boat rail. I opened it there on the strip with the evening coming down and the river talking its endless talk behind me.

*Oscar.*

*I need to tell you this myself, before it reaches you some other way, because it will reach you some other way, and I'd rather you heard it from me.*

*In the last months of the fund, before Vessany fell, someone had to move the money off the books so the thieves could reach it. Ledgers don't lie by themselves. Someone hid the true accounts inside the false ones, page within page, and smuggled them out—that's how the chest survived at all. Grandmother was one of the clerks. I've always known that. What I found last week, in the pass villages, in a copy of the master register that survived the annex fire, is the rest.*

*There were three clerks. Grandmother. A man named Odile Vessany's own cousin. And a bush pilot who flew the true ledgers out one page at a time, hidden in medicine crates, on routes no one watched, because no one watches the man who carries nothing but cargo.*

*Oscar. The pilot's name was Aldous Renk.*

I read it twice. The river kept talking. Somewhere behind me Brannoch shifted his weight and said nothing, which was the kindest thing he'd ever done.

Aldous. My mentor. The man who taught me the rules—never land twice, never learn names, never carry anything but cargo—who died in the channel I still couldn't fly over without looking down. Who taught me to be invisible, and had been invisible himself, all along, hiding the truth of a nation inside the routine of a supply run. Every rule he'd given me had been armor. I'd thought he was teaching me how to survive. He'd been teaching me how to hide something worth surviving for, and I'd worn the armor so long I'd forgotten there was anything underneath it.

And Ilse had known. Or suspected. And flown it upriver to check, alone, because that was what Ilse did with unbearable things: she carried them carefully, by hand, the long way, so they wouldn't break.

I folded the letter along its creases, twice, the way I folded everything I intended to keep, and put it inside my jacket, over the heart, where pilots keep the things they can't set down.

Brannoch cleared his throat. "Bad news?"

"I don't know yet," I said honestly. "Ask me tomorrow."

He nodded slowly, looking at the sky, at the plane, at me, an old trader taking the measure of a bargain he couldn't see the whole shape of. "There's room in the boat," he said at last. "If you're thinking of going up there. River's low till the rains. Good running, this time of year."

I looked upriver, where the fog was gathering again over the water, over the channels and the erased villages and the woman who had just rewritten the dead man who made me. The engine ticked as it cooled, patient, ready. Nineteen years I'd flown away from things. It was a strange and steady feeling, standing on my own strip, beside my own name, wanting—with my whole patched, heavy, willing heart—to fly *toward*.
