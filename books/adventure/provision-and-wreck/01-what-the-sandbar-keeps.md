## 1. What the Sandbar Keeps

The river told Ada Okafor everything before it told anyone else. Fifteen years of listening had taught her its grammar: the flat gray hush of water about to rise, the nervous chatter of a channel rearranging itself, and now, in the last week of the dry season, the long low mutter of a river giving things back.

She stood at the wheel of the *Marrow*, one hand resting on the brass rail worn smooth by her own palm, and watched the sandbar emerge like a spine from the shallows below Crooked Bend. It hadn't been there in March. Rivers kept their own ledgers, and every low-water season they opened them for inspection—silt accounts, drowned cargo, the occasional bones of boats that had trusted the wrong depth. Most years the ledger held nothing but driftwood and old net. This year it held something with corners.

"Bar's come up off the point," Tumo called from the bow, his voice carrying over the engine's laboring rhythm. He was nineteen, all elbows and appetite, and he read water the way other boys read letters—instinctively, hungrily. "There's metal in it. I can see the shine."

"Don't touch it," Ada said, because she said that every time, and because this time the shine had looked wrong to her too. Not bottle-glass green, not the dull pewter of an old propeller. Something deliberate.

Bex came up from the engine room wiping her hands on a rag that had once been white, her gray-streaked hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of electrical wire. She was fifty-one and built like the machinery she loved: squat, efficient, prone to overheating when provoked. She squinted at the bar.

"That's a chest," she said. "Sealed chest. You don't seal a chest unless you're putting something away you mean to find again."

"Or something you mean to lose forever," Ada said, and brought the *Marrow* alongside anyway.

She could have told herself later that curiosity made the decision. That would have been a lie. What made the decision was the shape of the thing half-buried in wet sand—the proportions of it, the iron banding, the way the sand had arranged itself around it as if the river had been cradling it, keeping it, all these years. Ada knew that shape. She had known it at forty yards, and she hated herself for knowing it.

They went out at low afternoon light, Tumo up to his thighs in the shallows digging, Bex hauling on a line, Ada standing ankle-deep in water so warm it felt like breath, not touching anything, directing nothing, watching her hands tremble against her will. The chest came free with a sucking sound, the river reluctant, and Tumo whooped and Bex swore cheerfully and Ada said nothing at all.

On deck, in the last of the light, they turned it over. Iron-banded cedar, gone black with decades underwater but sound beneath, and stamped into the lid—a seal.

Tumo ran his thumb across it. "What's this? Some kind of sun?"

"A rising river over three hills," Bex said slowly. "That's Kessara. That's the Republic's mark."

Ada heard the words from a great distance, through water, through fifteen years. The Republic of Kessara had been dead longer than Tumo had been alive. Its borders were lines on maps nobody printed anymore; its ministries were ruins where goats grazed; its name was spoken only by exiles who drank too much in river towns and by syndicate men who spoke it carefully, the way you speak of a debt you hope is unenforceable.

"We should put it back," Ada said.

Bex looked at her. Bex had been looking at her for fifteen years with those shrewd, oil-dark eyes, cataloguing the things Ada didn't say, and she'd never once asked. Now she said, quietly, "You know what's in it."

"I know what the seal means."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's the same thing enough." Ada turned toward the wheelhouse. "Throw it back. Whatever it is, it belongs to the mud."

But Tumo had already found the seam, and Tumo had already wedged the crowbar into it, and Tumo—who had grown up stateless in a tent city on the lower delta, who had never been a citizen of anything, who collected stamps and coins and scraps of flags from countries that no longer existed—was saying, "Captain, please. Please. It might be papers. It might be *records*. People lost everything when Kessara fell. My mother used to say if we could just find one document, one real paper with her name on it—"

The crowbar bit. The lock, eaten soft by years of river, gave with a crack that sounded to Ada like something much larger breaking.

Inside, wrapped in tarred canvas that had done its work faithfully, lay documents. Bundles of them. Permits, concessions, land grants, each bearing the embossed seal of the Republic of Kessara, each signed and countersigned in fading ink. Tumo lifted the top one reverently, and Ada saw his eyes move across the header, and she saw the moment he found the name, because his whole body went still in a way it never did.

He looked up at her. He didn't say it. He was kind enough not to say it. But Bex leaned over his shoulder, and Bex had no such scruples, or perhaps simply no room left for them after fifteen years.

"*Ezra Okafor*," Bex read aloud. "Surveyor-General. Co-holder of concession." She looked up. "Your father, Ada."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere downstream a fish bird screamed once and quit. Ada stood very still on her own deck, in her own life, which had taken fifteen years to build and consisted of exactly three things: a boat, a crew, and a silence.

Her father had been the Republic's surveyor. Her father had drawn the lines—every line, the boundaries and the grants and the concessions that had carved the river country into parcels for men who never touched the water. And when the Republic fell, when the mobs came for the men with stamps and seals, Ezra Okafor had put his daughter on a boat and told her one thing: *Be no one. Be nowhere. The river forgets.*

So she had become no one. She'd changed nothing on paper because there was no paper to change; she'd paid no taxes because there was no government to tax her; she'd piloted cargo and passengers and contraband indifferently up and down a river that belonged to no nation anymore, invisible as silt. Fifteen years. She had welded the door shut on her own history and thrown the key in the current, and she had believed—she had *needed* to believe—that the river kept nothing.

And here was the river, opening its hand. Here was her father's name, embossed and official, riding up out of the sand like a body given back after a drowning. The river hadn't forgotten. The river had been keeping his secret all along, patient as sediment, waiting for a dry season low enough to show what it held.

"Captain?" Tumo said softly.

She realized she was gripping the rail hard enough to whiten her knuckles. She realized both of them were watching her, and that whatever she did next would tell them something true about her, and that she had spent fifteen years making sure nobody knew anything true about her at all.

"There are more under that one," Bex said. "Dozens. Concessions, permits—whole tracts. Whoever sealed this meant it to survive." She paused. "Or meant it to be found."

Ada looked at the chest. At the neat bundles, the careful tar, the iron bands. Her father had been meticulous. He had taught her to read currents and draw boundary lines with the same steady hand, and she had loved him without understanding, until the fall, until the night he pushed her boat off into the dark and stayed behind on the burning dock, and she had understood everything at once and too late.

"He hid them," she said. Her voice came out flat, unfamiliar. "From the syndicates. From the exiles. From everyone who'd kill to hold a claim on this river." She swallowed. "Or he hid them from me. So I'd never have to choose."

"Choose what?" Tumo asked.

She didn't answer. Downstream, far off, a boat engine droned—not the loose rattle of a fisher, but the even, expensive purr of something with money behind it. It was probably nothing. On this river it was usually nothing.

"Seal it back up," Ada said finally. "We'll decide at Anchor Rock. Nobody says a word of this at the landing. Nobody."

Bex wrapped the canvas around the documents with surprising gentleness and closed the lid. Tumo lashed the chest down beneath the tarpaulin, and Ada went back to the wheel and put the *Marrow*'s nose into the current, and for the first time in fifteen years her hands were not entirely steady on the rail.

Behind them the sandbar settled back into the shallows, keeping what remained. Ahead, the evening came down purple and gold, and somewhere in it, gaining or not gaining, the expensive engine purred on.

Ada had spent fifteen years being no one. The river, it seemed, had other plans.
