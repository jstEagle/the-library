## 2. Every Mile a Bargain

The chest sat on the deck between the cargo winches for three days before Ada could bring herself to open it again.

She had told herself she wouldn't. She had told Bex and Tumo the same, in the flat voice she used for weather warnings and fare disputes, and they had nodded the way crew nod at captains—agreeing to her face while their eyes said something else. Tumo kept glancing at it as he coiled line, the way you glance at a dog you know is going to bite someone. Bex didn't look at it at all, which was worse.

On the third evening, moored in the reeds below Kilenge Bend where the water ran slow and brown as tea, Ada opened it again because not opening it had become its own kind of noise.

The permits were there under the lamplight, fanned across the chart table like a hand of cards nobody wanted dealt. Heavy paper, watermarked with the Kessaran sunburst, the ink still sharp after fifteen years in the dark of the riverbed. Her father's name appeared on four of them—*Elias Okafor, Surveyor-General, co-holder*—written in the careful clerk's hand of a government that no longer existed. The fifth permit was different. Older. It named land along the upper reaches, past the gorges, past the last marked mile on any chart Ada owned: *the source waters, per original grant.*

She touched the signature and felt nothing, which was a lie, and then felt grief, which was the truth underneath.

Fifteen years she had built her life the way you build a levee—one sandbag at a time, one unremarkable season at a time, never letting anyone see how high the water really stood. She'd changed her boat's name twice. She'd let people believe she came from downcoast, from nowhere, from a family of ferrymen who drowned poor. And all that time the river had been holding her father's name in its fist, waiting for a dry year to open its hand.

"You're doing the thing," Bex said from the engine-room hatch, "where you stare at paper like it owes you money."

"Go grease something."

"I greased everything. There's nothing left to grease." Bex came up the steps anyway, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last season. She looked at the permits without touching them. "Syndicate launch came by this morning. While you were sleeping."

Ada's head came up. "Here?"

"Flying trade colors. Polite as a tax collector." Bex shrugged, but her jaw was tight. "Left a card. Said the gentleman aboard would appreciate a conversation at your convenience. He's anchored at Kilenge landing."

Ada looked at the card. Cream stock, embossed, absurdly clean for the river country. *Meridian Consolidated Extraction — V. Sarr, Regional Liaison.* Beneath it, in ink: *A matter of mutual prosperity.*

"They know what I have," Ada said.

"Everybody knows what you have. You pulled a republic-stamped chest out of a sandbar in front of forty witnesses at the fish market. You might as well have run it up a flagpole."

* * *

V. Sarr received her aboard the syndicate launch with the courtesy of a man who had never once been refused anything he actually wanted.

He was small and neat and gray, with the kind of face designed to be forgotten, and he served her tea in real porcelain while his gunboat's deck guns—covered with canvas, badly—sat twenty feet behind him. The tea was excellent. That frightened her more than the guns.

"Captain Okafor." He pronounced her name correctly, all three syllables, and she understood that he had practiced. "I'll be brief, because I respect your time and because brevity is a kindness on the river. Meridian will pay you two hundred thousand crowns for the contents of the chest. Unmarked, untraceable, delivered tonight. In addition—" he set a second envelope beside the teapot, "—a letter of safe passage. Any port, any season, any flag. No syndicate vessel will ever trouble you again. You could run cargo until your hair goes white and never think about us."

"And if I say no?"

Sarr smiled gently, as if she'd asked whether the river would keep flowing. "Then other parties become interested. The exiles, for instance. They want the same documents, Captain, but they won't pay you—they'll spend you. They'll make you a banner and march you upriver ahead of them, and when the shooting starts, banners are what bullets go through." He refilled her cup unasked. "Two hundred thousand. Your father's mistakes, settled. A long life on quiet water. It is, genuinely, the best offer anyone will ever make you."

And the terrible thing—the thing she turned over that night at the wheel while Tumo slept and the reeds whispered against the hull—was that she believed him. Not the threat. The arithmetic. Two hundred thousand crowns was a new boiler. It was Bex's license back, her debts gone, Tumo's mother moved off the floodplain before the next wet season. It was every hard winter she'd ever steered through, paid off in an evening.

It was also, she realized with a coldness in her stomach, exactly the shape of the offer her father must once have accepted. Prosperity, freely given, in exchange for looking away from whose land it came off of.

She hadn't decided anything when she finally slept. But she dreamed of the chest filling with water, and in the dream she was the one holding it shut.

* * *

**BEX**

Bex couldn't sleep either, so she went down into the engine room, where the problems made sense.

An engine wants things from you and tells you plainly what they are. Fuel, coolant, clearance, time. Fifteen years ago the Republic had revoked her engineer's ticket—not for anything she'd done, but for the strike she'd helped organize at the Yalo yards, for the word *syndicate* spoken too loudly in a hearing room full of men who owned the hearing room. She'd spent fifteen years running engines illegally, beautifully, under names that weren't hers, and here was a letter in cream stock offering to make her legal again. All she had to do was mention, casually, to a polite man, which route a certain captain meant to take upstream.

She'd written the note. That was the sick part. It was sitting in her breast pocket right now, folded small, and she kept touching it like a wound.

Above her, boots crossed the deck. Ada, walking her insomnia off. Bex knew her step the way she knew valve knock.

The thing was, Ada had pulled her out of a debtor's dock at Kalume with nothing but a bad reputation and good hands, and had never once asked what the charges were. Had stood in front of a harbormaster and lied—fluently, convincingly—to keep Bex aboard. And Bex was going to sell her route for a stamp on a piece of paper.

She fed the note into the firebox and watched the flame take it, and felt lighter and more ashamed than she had in years, both at once, which she supposed was what forgiveness felt like from the inside before anyone had actually granted it.

* * *

They came at midnight, poling out of the reeds in dugouts with their lamps hooded—six of them, maybe eight, moving with the silence of people who had done this before. Ada met them at the rail with a lantern raised and a flare gun held loose at her side, and the lead man lifted empty hands.

"We only want to talk, Captain. We've been talking to everyone else for fifteen years. Nobody listens."

He was young—younger than her—and he wore a Kessaran officer's coat cut down to fit, the sunburst buttons polished like relics. Behind him the others had faces she recognized from market days, from ferry queues: river people, exile people, the ones the republic's fall had left standing in the wreckage holding nothing but memory.

"The permits expire in forty days," he said. "You've read them. After that, every claim dies with the paper—unless the originals are recovered from the source vaults and lodged with a living authority. The old grants. The true ones. My grandmother farmed the banks above the second gorge. She died on the road when the syndicates came. If those originals surface, her grandchildren get their ground back. If they don't—" he spread his hands, "—then the land belongs to whoever can afford the lawyers. Forever."

"And you want me to carry you up there."

"I want you to *want* to. There's a difference, and I'm told you're honest enough to know it." His eyes went past her, to the chart table where the permits lay. "Your father's name is on those papers, Captain. Half our people curse it. Half light candles to it. Which half are you?"

She didn't answer. She watched the dugouts fade back into the reeds, and she stood a long time at the rail with the flare gun cold in her hand, thinking about banners and bullets, about levees and water, about a man dead fifteen years whose handwriting she still couldn't look at without her chest tightening.

At dawn she found Bex in the engine room, black-smudged, avoiding her eyes, and Tumo on deck already casting off lines neither of them had ordered him to cast.

"Upstream," Ada said. "Past the gorges. Forty days."

Bex looked up sharply. "The source? Nobody runs the source. The charts stop. The syndicate has a gunboat on the bend and we've got—" she gestured at the whole tired boat, "—this."

"Yes," Ada said, and took the wheel, and pointed the bow at the hills where the maps gave out.
