## 3. Upstream of the Law

The river changed its mind three times a day above the Kessara line, and by the fourth morning it had stopped pretending to be navigable at all. Ada stood at the wheel of the *Marrow* with her hands light on the spokes, reading water the way her father had once read land—looking for the lie in it. The channel braided and rebraided through gravel shoals the color of old bone. Somewhere under that gravel lay the drowned streets of towns that had never been on any map he signed.

"Depth's falling," Tumo called from the bow, sounding the lead and chanting the numbers like a prayer. "By the mark, five. By the mark, four."

"Starboard a point," Ada said, though she was already turning. The *Marrow* answered the way an old dog answers—a half-beat late, grudging, loyal anyway. Fifteen years Ada had kept this boat in safe water: fuel runs, mail runs, the dull honest work of a woman who wanted to be forgotten. Now every mile upstream was a mile further from anywhere the law reached, and she found she could not decide whether that felt like freedom or like being swallowed.

Behind them, low and patient, the gunboat held its distance. It had appeared at dawn two days back—gray hull, no name painted, a wake like a knife being drawn slowly across the river's back. The syndicate didn't need to catch them. It only needed to remind them it was there.

Bex came up from the engine room wiping her hands on a rag gone gray with grease, and Ada watched her glance back at the gunboat a moment too long.

* * *

That night they tied up in the lee of a bluff where fig trees grew out of cracks in the rock, and Bex did not come to eat. Ada found her in the engine room by lamplight, sitting on an upturned crate, turning something over in her fingers. A radio handset. The syndicate's frequency hissed faintly from the speaker, open, waiting.

Ada did not say anything. She stood in the doorway and let the silence do the talking, and after a while Bex set the handset down as if it were hot.

"They offered me my license back." Her voice was flat, scraped clean of excuse. "Full restoration. Signed papers. All I had to tell them was which gorge you meant to run, and when."

"I know," Ada said.

"You knew?"

"I've known since the second night. You sleep facing the stern now. You used to face the bow." She came down the ladder and sat on the opposite crate, knees almost touching Bex's. "I spent fifteen years learning how people look when they're carrying something they shouldn't be. I should know. I invented the posture."

Bex laughed, or made a sound near enough to it, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "They said I'd lost my license because of a fault in my judgment. Eleven years I've believed that. Eleven years I've been nobody, greasing somebody else's engine, and they walk up out of the water and say one sentence—*we can make you whole again*—and I—" She dropped her hands. "I keyed the mic, Ada. I got as far as saying your boat's name."

"The *Marrow*," Ada said. "Which is also mine. So really you'd have been selling yourself too."

"Don't make it small."

"I'm not making it small. I'm telling you what I know about it." Ada looked at the handset lying between them, its little green light still breathing. "The day the republic fell, I burned my travel papers so no one could trace where I'd come from. Then I spent a week sick with grief over the ashes. That's what it does to you—it makes you betray yourself first, so the betrayal afterward feels familiar. They don't buy people, Bex. They just find the wound and hold a lamp over it."

Bex was quiet a long time. The engine ticked as it cooled, ticking away the hours like something counting down. "Forty days," she said finally. "Thirty-one now. And we haven't even reached the gorge."

"No."

"And if we run it at night—"

"Then we run it blind, and the river does what it likes with us." Ada stood, took the handset, and pitched it into the bilge tank, where it sank with a small, decent gurgle. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow I'll want your engines angrier than they've ever been."

Bex caught her hand before she climbed the ladder. Her grip was grease and callus and shaking, just slightly. "Why aren't you angry?"

Because I have been angry for fifteen years and it has fed me nothing, Ada thought. Because the first person I ever forgave is going to have to be myself, and I am practicing on you. What she said was: "Ask me again when we're through the gorge."

* * *

The gorge announced itself a day later as a sound before a sight—a long rolling thunder under the hull, felt in the soles of the feet before it reached the ears. The river narrowed to a third of its width and dropped through black walls of stone hung with fern, and the water inside those walls was not water anymore but a white animal, furious, alive.

Ada studied it from the last wide bend until dark. There was no daylight way through; the syndicate gunboat sat downstream like a cork in a bottle, and any run made in the open would end with rifles on the banks. But the syndicate's pilots were rivermen too, and rivermen feared the gorge at night more than they feared losing a prize.

"We go at moonrise," she told Tumo and Bex on the foredeck, the permits' expiration hanging over all of them like weather. "Bex, I want everything. If the engine gives out in there, we're kindling. Tumo, you're my eyes—I'll steer by your voice if the lamps die."

Tumo grinned, young and terrified and game. "And if I shout the wrong number?"

"Then we'll both be wrong together," Ada said, "which is what family means."

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It landed among the three of them and stayed there, unargued-with, true.

They entered the gorge at moonrise with the searchlamps hooded to slits, and the world became sound and violence. The walls rose on either side close enough to touch—or would have, if touching them hadn't meant dying. The river threw the *Marrow* left, right, lifted her bow skyward over a standing wave and slammed her down hard enough to crack a seam somewhere aft. Bex's voice came up the voice-pipe steady as a metronome—*holding, holding, give me thirty more seconds*—and Tumo crouched at the bow calling depth and rock in a chant that wavered but never broke.

There was a moment—Ada would remember it always—when the moon went behind the wall and the lamps guttered and she was steering entirely blind, trusting two voices in the dark, and she realized she was not afraid. Fifteen years of fear, and here at the bottom of a stone throat with death on every side, she felt instead a fierce, absurd gladness. Whatever waited at the source, whatever her father had been, this—the wheel in her hands, these voices in the dark—was hers. Chosen, not inherited.

Then the river ended its argument. The gorge spat them out into a broad, slow valley flooded to the treetops, silent as a held breath, and the *Marrow*, having given everything, gave one thing more: her hull ground onto a submerged ridge with a shudder that threw them all to the deck, and stuck fast.

Ada picked herself up. Ahead, maybe half a mile, a structure broke the glass-still floodwater—an arch of dark stone rising out of the drowned valley, carved with the seal of the Republic of Kessara, its lower courses already swallowed by the river that had been sent to forget it.

The archive vault. Her father's grave of paper.

Behind them, far down the gorge, a gray shape nosed cautiously into the moonlight, running dark, closing.

"High water or not," Ada said quietly, "we swim the rest."
