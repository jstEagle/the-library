## 4. The Source Gives Nothing Back

The vault was not what Ada had imagined in forty days of imagining. She had pictured iron, and there was iron—a door set into the cliff face above the drowned terraces, green with a century of spray—but it stood open. The river had done the syndicate's work for them, or perhaps no one had ever come to close it. Water sheeted down the rock in silver threads, pooling on the threshold, and beyond it the archive of Kessara lay in the dark like a drowned man's pockets turned out.

She went in alone. Tumo held the lamp at the doorway; Bex stayed with the boat, grounded on the shingle below, her engine tools spread around her like a surgeon's instruments she had no patient for.

Inside, the shelves were stone, cut into the living rock, and the documents that had built a republic and unmade a country lay swollen to pulp or dried to lace. Ada moved along them with her fingers, reading spines by touch: census rolls, tax ledgers, the great leather folios of land grant. Her father's name was in some of these. She could feel it without opening them, the way you can feel a storm before the wind arrives.

In the deepest alcove, on a shelf above the flood line, sat a speaking-cylinder of brass, its wax seal intact, and beside it a single envelope bearing her name—not *Ada Okafor*, but *Ada*, in her father's cramped surveyor's hand, the hand she knew from the margins of maps he'd made of rivers she'd spent fifteen years pretending she'd never seen.

She almost left it there. That was the truth of it, and she would carry the shame of the hesitation longer than any other part of this night. Her thumb hovered over the seal for the length of three breaths, and in those breaths she was eleven years old again, waiting at a window for a father who explained everything too late.

Then she cracked the wax.

The cylinder hissed when she opened it, releasing air older than her exile. She pressed the playback stud against her ear, the way they'd taught children to do in the republic's schools, and her father's voice poured into her skull—thinner than memory, worn at the edges like a coin passed through too many hands.

*"If anyone finds this, let it be understood. I, Emeka Okafor, surveyor of the Third Concession Commission, did falsify the boundary grants of the upper river. The communities of the source valleys—the Kiremba, the Ashwe, the people of the drowned terraces—held these lands by use and by memory, by graves and by gardens. I drew lines through their lives because men with guns asked me to, and because they paid me, and because I told myself the alternative was my daughter growing up an orphan."*

Ada's knees found the stone floor. She didn't remember sitting.

*"I have watched the permits pass from hand to hand for thirty years, each holder certain they own something real. They own paper. I made the paper. There is nothing underneath it but the river, which was never ours to give. If my daughter reads this—Ada. I did not do it for you. I did it because I was afraid, and I dressed the fear up as love afterward. Do not inherit this. Do not avenge it. Go home by water."*

The cylinder clicked off. In the silence after, the drip of the spring through the vault sounded like applause, or laughter, or rain on a roof over a family at supper—she couldn't decide which, and stopped trying.

She knelt a long time. When she finally rose, her legs were old woman's legs, and the envelope in her hand contained the certified duplicate of the concession permits, stamped and sealed, valid until dawn.

Outside, the sky in the east had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

* * *

They came up the gorge at first light, the syndicate gunboat riding low and mean, its wake slapping the cliffs in flat echoes. Ada stood on the shingle with the documents under her arm and her crew behind her—Tumo with a boathook he had no intention of using, Bex with grease to her elbows and her revoked license folded in her breast pocket like a talisman she'd finally stopped praying to.

The agent came ashore alone. He was younger than his voice had been on the radio, and tired, and he looked at the permits in Ada's hands the way starving men look through restaurant glass.

"Captain Okafor. Forty days ago I offered you enough to retire three times over. My offer stands until—" he checked his watch, theatrical, "—sunrise. Which is now."

"It is," Ada said.

Behind him, upriver, other boats were arriving—flat-bottomed, crowded, flying no flag she recognized but one every river child knows: the exiles, the Kiremba, the Ashwe, the descendants of the dispossessed come to watch the expiration of the lie. They lined the banks in silence. An old woman at the front held a cooking pot, as if she'd come prepared for whatever happened next to become a meal shared among strangers.

"You understand what happens if you burn those," the agent said. "Every claim voids. The syndicate loses everything it bought. So do they." He nodded at the banks. "No grants honored. No reparations. Nothing. You'll be burning their inheritance too."

"I know," Ada said. "That's the trick of it. He built the whole thing out of paper so that the only choices left were paper choices. Sell it, fight over it, or burn it." She looked at the old woman with the pot, and the old woman looked back, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with law. "But there was never anything underneath. He said so himself. There's just the river, and the people who never left it."

"Captain—"

"My father falsified the grants," she said, loud enough to reach the banks. "He wanted everyone to know. Now everyone does."

She struck the match on the second try. The first broke, and Bex stepped forward wordlessly and struck another off her own thumbnail, engineer's hands, sure hands, and handed it over lit.

The permits caught slowly, the way old paper does, reluctant, then all at once. Ada held them until the fire reached her gloves, then crouched and set the burning bundle on the wet stones at the waterline, where the river came for it in long gray tongues, took it, worried it, and carried it away in flakes of orange light that guttered and died somewhere past the first bend.

The agent watched until the last of it was gone. Then he closed his notebook, and something in his face loosened—not forgiveness, but the relief of a man released from a job he'd never believed in. "There will be consequences," he said, without conviction.

"There always are," Ada said. "That's what a river is."

* * *

They floated home with the current doing half the work, which felt, after the gorge, like grace. Tumo cooked on the back deck—real food, the old woman's people had loaded them with smoked fish and ground provisions and a jar of pepper sauce that made Bex cry and claim it was the wind. The gunboat did not follow. Somewhere behind them, upriver, people were walking into valleys that had never legally belonged to anyone, which meant they belonged to everyone who tended them, which meant, Ada thought, they had always been exactly where they were.

On the third evening, Bex found her at the wheel and leaned against the housing, watching the water go gold.

"So," she said. "Fifteen years of being nobody. What now?"

Ada considered it. The river slid by, brown and patient, keeping whatever secrets it kept, giving up only what it chose. Her father's voice was still in her ear, but fainter now, going the way of the ash. *Go home by water.* She was on the water. Whatever this boat was, it was home, and the two people on it had chosen her more honestly than blood ever had.

"Now we take passengers," Ada said. "Upstream, downstream. People with goods, people with grief, people running from things." She glanced at Bex. "People with revoked licenses."

Bex snorted. "You'll regret that."

"Probably."

Ahead, the bend opened onto the long reach they'd run a hundred times, and moored at the far landing, small in the evening light, was a boat Ada didn't recognize—new paint, unfamiliar lines, a figure standing at its rail watching them come. Tumo appeared at the wheelhouse door, squinting.

"Captain? We know them?"

Ada put her hand on the wheel and brought the *Provision* around, slow and steady, toward whatever it was.

"No," she said. "But the river does. Let's go find out how."
