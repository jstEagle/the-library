## 1. Ten Fathoms, No Answers

The river at Greywater Bend ran the color of old pewter that morning, and Mirabel Underhill loved it the way you love a thing that has already taken something from you — carefully, and with your eyes open.

She stood on the deck of the *Magpie*, her salvage barge, watching the navy launch cut its slow patrol line across the bend. The boat was white-hulled and smug, its ensign snapping in a wind that smelled of cold iron and coming rain. A cordon buoy bobbed fifty yards downstream of the wreck site, painted in warning stripes, with a placard stenciled in letters too small to read from here but large enough in meaning: RESTRICTED WATERS. NAVY SURVEY OPERATIONS IN PROGRESS. UNAUTHORIZED DIVING PROHIBITED BY ORDER OF THE ADMIRALTY.

Mirabel had read that placard so many times she could have recited it in her sleep. She had also, in the last six weeks, violated it eleven times.

"Twelve," she said aloud, to no one, and began checking her gear.

The *Magpie* rode low in the water, heavy with the honest junk of her trade: chain, cable, a rusted ship's bell she'd raised off the *Cormorant* two seasons back, coils of rope gone soft as old hair. Her air compressor squatted amidships like a patient dog. Everything on the boat had been patched, welded, mended, or stolen fair and square from the river itself. Ten years she'd worked this stretch of the Vell — ten years since her father's boat came down the current empty, engine turning, lines trailing, and no Tobias Underhill aboard.

They'd called it an accident. The coroner's report used the word *drowning* four times, as though repetition could make it true. Mirabel had been nineteen then, and she had believed it the way you believe things when believing them is easier than not. She had spent the decade since learning otherwise by inches: the way the navy boats patrolled this one bend more diligently than any harbor; the way survey teams came upriver every few years with sealed crates and closed mouths; the way questions asked in the wrong tavern got answered with silence, or worse, with kindness.

So she dove. And she watched. And she waited for the river to give back what it had taken.

* * *

The wreck lay in ten fathoms, which was deep enough to kill a careless diver and shallow enough to make lying about it unforgivable. She'd found it in spring, when the meltwater shifted the silt bar and exposed a rib of black timber that shouldn't have been there. It took her three dives to understand what she'd found: a hull, iron-framed, forty feet if it was an inch, sunk upright and deliberately, ballasted with stone. Not a shipwreck. A *placement*. Someone had put this vessel on the bottom of the Vell on purpose, and then the navy had come along and stretched a cordon around it like a bandage over a wound.

Today's dive had one purpose. Six days ago, working the stern in near-zero visibility, her glove had brushed something that wasn't timber, wasn't stone, wasn't silt. Something square-edged and riveted. She'd marked the spot with a brass tag on a length of signal wire, because a good salvager never trusts memory underwater, and then she'd surfaced before her air went short, because a dead salvager finds nothing ever again.

Now she went back for it.

The water took her the way it always did — a shock of cold at her neck, then a closing-over, the world above dissolving into a ceiling of trembling silver. Below ten feet the light went green. Below twenty it went to a brown-green murk where her lamp carved a cone out of nothing. She equalized her ears, checked her gauge, and descended hand over hand down the shot line she'd rigged to a mooring ring on the wreck's forward rail.

Ten fathoms. Sixty feet of river pressing on her chest like a patient hand. Down here the Vell stopped being scenery and became a country with its own laws: move slowly, touch nothing twice, and never — never — turn your back on the current.

The hull rose around her, furred with zebra mussels and a soft black rot that came away under her fingers like wet paper. She followed the rail aft, counting frame bays by feel, her lamp beam swallowed almost whole by the murk. Fish scattered at the edge of the light, quick silver question marks. Somewhere above, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, she could hear the navy launch's engine making its rounds. They passed over the wreck every twenty minutes. She had timed them. She had eleven minutes of work and she intended to use nine.

Her glove found the brass tag. Good. She unclipped the signal wire and let it drift, and felt along the frame until her fingers walked into the thing she'd touched before: a box. Iron, riveted at the corners, roughly the size of a sea chest but heavier than any chest had a right to be — she could tell that just from the way it sat in the silt, settled deep, refusing to shift. A strongbox. Sealed. And stamped.

She scraped the growth away with her knife, careful as a surgeon, and held her lamp close.

The stamp was a circle within a circle, and inside the inner ring, a device she didn't recognize: a stylized spring or fountainhead, water rising from a point, flanked by two letters she read upside down before righting herself — *V.S.* Vell Source? Vell Spring? Beneath it, smaller, almost worn away: a serial number and the word *PROPERTY*.

Property of whom? The Admiralty didn't stamp its stores with springs. Survey equipment came marked with crown stamps and grid references. This was something else — older, or more secret, or both. The metal of the box itself bothered her too. It wasn't ordinary iron. When her knife tip touched it, she felt a faint tremor run up through the haft into her wrist bones, a vibration like a struck tuning fork heard through a wall, though nothing had struck anything. She pulled her hand back. The sensation stopped. She touched it again. There. That hum, barely there, like the river itself purring through the metal.

Her father had been a diver thirty years. He'd taught her everything she knew, and one of the things he'd taught her was a rule he'd never explained: *If the river gives you something that doesn't belong to it, take it before the river changes its mind.*

Mirabel took it.

Getting the strongbox to the surface was a lesson in humility. It weighed perhaps two hundred pounds dry, and water made liars of all weights, but even so it fought her — not alive, nothing so dramatic, just stubborn, settling deeper each time she levered it, as if the silt were reluctant to surrender it. She rigged a lift bag, filled it from her spare bottle, and the box rose grudgingly, swaying beneath her like a hanged man. She swam it clear of the hull, following her knotted line up through the green dark, and broke the surface behind the *Magpie*'s lee rail with her lungs burning and her heart going like a drum in a parade.

"Ho," she gasped, to the boat, to the river, to nobody. "Ho."

* * *

By noon the strongbox sat on her deck under a tarpaulin, and Mirabel sat on an overturned crate beside it, eating bread and cheese and pretending she wasn't staring at it every four seconds.

"You're staring at it," said Dree.

Dree Oakes was her deckhand, cook, and conscience, a broad-shouldered woman of fifty who had lost a husband to the Vell and gained, in some transaction Mirabel had never fully understood, a fierce and unsentimental devotion to Mirabel herself. She was scrubbing pots with the violence of the righteous.

"I'm supervising it," Mirabel said.

"You're staring at it like it owes you money."

"It might." Mirabel set down her bread. "Look at the stamp."

Dree looked. She was quiet a long moment, hands dripping suds. "That's no navy mark I know."

"No."

"And you've been diving under their noses for—"

"Twelve times now, yes."

"—and they haven't caught you, which means either you're very good or they don't much care about the wreck, only about who comes near it." Dree dried her hands. "Which is worse, if you think about it."

Mirabel thought about it. She thought about it constantly. The cordon wasn't guarding the wreck from salvage — a sunken hulk had no value anyone would admit to. The cordon was guarding the wreck from *attention*. From eyes. From questions. Somebody wanted that hull forgotten, and somebody else had wanted it sunk in the first place, and neither somebody was talking.

She pried at the strongbox lid with a crowbar and got nowhere. The seal was a wax-and-lead affair poured into the hasp, stamped with the same spring device, and beneath it the hinges were pinned, not screwed — designed to be opened by exactly one tool in exactly one place, and she didn't have it. She could cut it open with a cold chisel and an hour's work, but something stayed her hand. Partly superstition — her father's other rule: *never break a seal you don't understand; seals are messages, and messages are worth more unopened.* Partly something colder: if the navy discovered the box missing and found it broken on a salvage barge fifty yards outside their cordon, she'd hang for espionage before supper.

"So we wait," Dree said.

"We wait." Mirabel re-tarpaulined it. "And meanwhile I want to know what a navy survey team is actually doing up here. They've drawn water samples for six weeks. You don't need six weeks of water samples."

"What do you need six weeks of?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She looked upstream, where the Vell narrowed toward the gorge country, toward the high grey hills where the maps went vague and the river went nameless. Every chart she owned — and she owned charts going back ninety years — went soft in the same place. Rivers were charted from source to mouth; that was the whole pride of cartography. But the Vell's headwaters appeared on no chart she'd ever seen, as if the surveyors had agreed among themselves to leave a hole in the world.

Her father had headed upstream once. She knew that much. Three days before his boat came down empty, a lockkeeper at Harrow Weir had seen the *Lark* — his old boat, the one Mirabel still kept, couldn't sell, couldn't burn — heading upriver against the current, loaded light, moving fast. Heading toward the hole in the map.

"Dree," she said. "When I was a girl, did my da ever talk about the upper river? Above the gorge?"

Dree's scrubbing slowed. "He talked about everything. That was the trouble with him." She wrung out a rag. "But that last season, he went quiet about it. Asked me odd questions. Whether I'd ever seen navy men buy supplies in bulk without a contract. Whether I thought a man could drown in three feet of water." She shrugged, uncomfortable. "I told him to stop poking. He laughed at me."

"And then?"

"And then he didn't come home." Dree said it flatly, the way you say a thing you've said to yourself a thousand times to keep it from growing teeth. "Girl, I love you, but I'll say it plain: whatever's up that river ate your father, and it's been chewing on the navy ever since, and you keep swimming closer to its mouth."

Mirabel didn't answer. The wind had come around, carrying the smell of the launch's coal smoke, and out on the bend the white hull made another of its patient turns. Twenty minutes. Always twenty minutes. Precision like that wasn't caution. It was ritual. It was a wound being tended.

She went below to her cabin — a space barely bigger than a coffin, crowded with charts, her father's diving helmet mounted on the bulkhead like a saint's icon — and pulled out the one document she'd never shown anyone. Not a chart. A page from her father's logbook, the final entry, recovered from the *Lark* when she drifted ashore:

*T. Underhill, sole entry, undated:* **Found it. God forgive me, I found it, and now I understand why they sink boats instead of stories. If I don't come back, M. should know the river keeps what it's given. Tell her the spring sings.**

*M. should know.* Mirabel. He'd known he might not come back, and he'd written to her anyway, in the shorthand of a man who trusted his daughter to finish his sentences. *The spring sings.* She'd turned those words over for ten years like a coin worn smooth. Springs didn't sing. Water didn't sing. Unless—

Unless it was water running over something that hummed. Something like the metal of a strongbox stamped with a fountainhead, whose vibration she could feel in her wrist bones sixty feet down.

Mirabel sat very still in the cabin while the *Magpie* rocked on the current, and let the two halves of her life click together with a sound she felt rather than heard. The wreck. The stamp. The cordon. The hole in the map. Her father's last words. They weren't separate mysteries anymore. They were one mystery, wearing different clothes, and it lived upstream, in the place where the charts went blind.

Above her, on deck, Dree called down: "Boat! Navy launch, coming alongside — officer asking for the owner by name!"

Mirabel closed the logbook and slid it under the floorboard where it had lived for ten years, and climbed the ladder into the daylight with her face arranged into the blank pleasant competence of a woman with nothing to hide.

The launch bumped against the *Magpie*'s hull. A lieutenant commander in a spotless uniform regarded her across the gap of dirty water, and behind him stood two sailors with rifles slung, and behind *them*, riding low on the grey current, came something Mirabel had never seen on the Vell before: a second boat, civilian-rigged, half-swamped, towing debris.

A body, she realized. Or what the river had left of one.

"Miss Underhill?" the officer said. "We've brought you a customer. He was asking for you before he went under. Said—" the officer consulted a notebook, distaste thinning his voice— "said you'd want what he's carrying. Said you'd promised."

"I haven't promised anything," Mirabel said. "I've never seen him."

"Yes," the officer said, stepping aside so she could see the ruined man being lifted onto her deck, water pouring from him, one hand locked around a oilskin packet like a drowning man around a spar. "That's what he said you'd say."
