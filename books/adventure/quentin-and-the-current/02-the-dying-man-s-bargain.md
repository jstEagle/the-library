## 2. The Dying Man's Bargain

The river gave up its dead at dawn, and Mirabel Underhill had learned to be awake for it.

She was on the yard's lower deck with a mug of chicory going cold in her hand when she heard the sound — not the usual slap of driftwood against the pilings, not the groan of the mooring lines as the current shifted with the night's rain, but something wetter and more deliberate. A dragging. A catching. Like a body snagging on the old chain net she kept strung below the waterline to catch salvage that floated down from the wrecks upstream.

She set the mug down without looking at it.

Ten years on this stretch of the Verrin had taught her the river's vocabulary the way other people learned their mothers' voices. She knew what a drowned deer sounded like when it came around the bend, knew the hollow knock of an empty barrel, the whisper of kelp-mats moving like slow green ghosts. This was none of those. This had weight to it, and weight meant metal, and metal meant either money or trouble, and in her experience the two arrived wearing each other's coats.

The fog was still peeling off the water in long gray ribbons when she went down the ladder to the catch-net platform. Her boots rang on the iron grating. Below her, tangled in the chain mesh like a moth in a web, was a man.

He was face-down, which was how the river preferred its offerings, arms spread wide as if he'd been embracing the whole cold length of it. His coat was good leather once, now black with water and something darker that wasn't water. One of his legs was hooked through the netting at an angle that made her stomach tighten. And he was moving — barely, but moving, one hand opening and closing against the chains in a rhythm too stubborn to be anything but alive.

"Damn it," Mirabel said, to the man, the river, and the morning generally, and went for the boat hook.

Getting him onto the platform took everything she had. He weighed what a drowned man weighs — double, treble, waterlogged through — and twice his boot caught the grating and she nearly lost him back over the edge, back into the brown-green dark where she'd spent half her life and where, she suspected, she would eventually spend all of it. But she got him. She rolled him onto his back on the planking and knelt over him in the fog-damp light and saw what the river had been trying to tell her.

There was a hole in him, high on the left side of his chest, punched clean through the leather coat. Not a boating wound. Not a snag-hook. A bullet, and a recent one, because the blood still came when she pressed her palm flat against it, warm as bathwater against her glove.

His eyes opened. They were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a man who'd spent decades squinting into glare off water or snow, and they found her face with an effort that seemed to cost him more than the bullet had.

"You're the diver," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through mud. "Underhill."

Mirabel's hands did not stop working. She was already stripping off her gloves, already tearing the oilcloth from the emergency kit bolted under the platform rail, already folding a compress against the wound. "I'm the diver," she agreed. "Hold still."

"Knew you would be." Something moved across his ruined face that might have been a smile. "Saw your buoy markers coming down. Red-and-white. Nobody else runs red-and-white on this reach." His breath whistled. "Came looking for you."

"People who come looking for me usually want something raised," she said. "You're the first one who wanted himself lowered."

The smile again, briefer. Then a cough took him, deep and wet, and when it passed there was blood on his lips and his hand had found her wrist with a grip that surprised her — strong, desperate, the grip of a man spending his last coin all at once.

"Listen." He pulled her closer. Up close she could see the map of his life written on him: sun-cured skin cracked like old varnish, a beard gone gray at the chin, fingers scarred and split from cold work. A prospector. A placer man, maybe, or worse — one of the fools who chased rumors up the dead ends of the Verrin's headwaters, where the maps thinned out and the navy's survey buoys began. "My name's Corliss. Edmun Corliss. I need you to hear me, diver, because I haven't got the time to say it twice."

"You've got time if you let me stop the bleeding—"

"I haven't." It wasn't self-pity. It was arithmetic. She recognized the tone; she used it herself when pricing a wreck. "They shot me at Miller's Bend. Two men in a navy launch, no markings, no colors. They think I'm dead. They'll come to make sure." His grip tightened. "So we do this fast, and we do it right."

Mirabel glanced up the river. The fog was burning off in earnest now, and the far bank was emerging tree by tree, black pines standing in their own reflections. The water ran high and fast with the night's rain, carrying leaves and broken branches past the yard like a procession. Somewhere upstream, two men in an unmarked launch were drifting down that same current, patient as the river itself.

"Inside," she said. "If we're doing this fast, we're doing it where I can actually work."

* * *

She got him into the drying shed, the long low building where she hung wetsuits and patched hulls and kept the tools of her trade in careful, loving order. She laid him on the tarpaulin she spread for exactly this purpose — though in ten years she'd never once used it for a man — and cut away the coat and shirt from the wound while he watched her with those pale, unblinking eyes.

The bullet had gone through high, above the lung, she thought, but it had torn things on the way, and he'd been in the water long enough that the cold had done half the killing already. She cleaned it, packed it, stitched it with the curved needle she used on dive-suit tears, working quickly and without ceremony. He never made a sound. That frightened her more than screaming would have.

"There's a doctor in Harrow Bend," she said, tying off. "Two hours downstream. I can run you there when the fog lifts fully—"

"No doctors." He said it flatly, the way a man says *no priests*. "No navy, no constable, no doctor who files reports. You know why."

She did know why. Everyone on the middle Verrin knew why, even if nobody said it aloud: the upper river belonged to the Navy Survey Office now, and the Survey Office did not like witnesses, and a man with a navy bullet in him was a witness whether he lived or died. She tied the last knot and sat back on her heels and looked at him, really looked, and felt the old restlessness stir in her chest — the same feeling she got over a wreck whose manifest didn't match its cargo.

"Why you?" she asked. "Why me? Why here?"

Corliss reached inside what remained of his shirt and drew out an oilskin packet, small as a hymnal, tied with waxed cord. Even soaked, even bloodied, he handled it the way she handled her father's compass: like the last true thing in the world.

"Because of this," he said. "And because your name is Underhill, and I'm gambling that means what I think it means."

The shed was very quiet. Outside, the river ran on, indifferent, eloquent, saying nothing.

"My father dove this river," Mirabel said carefully. "Before me."

"He didn't just dive it." Corliss fumbled at the cord with swollen fingers; she leaned in and cut it with her knife. "He *questioned* it. Tobias Underhill. Best wreck-man on the Verrin, and the only one who ever asked the Survey Office why a salvage channel needed armed picket boats." He unfolded the oilskin with trembling hands. "I prospect the headwaters. Have for thirty years. And eleven years ago, I found something up there that the navy has been killing to keep quiet ever since. Your father found it first. That's why he's dead."

The words landed in her like a hook finding flesh. She had spent eleven years telling herself a kinder story — a cable snapped, a valve failed, the river simply took him, the way rivers take everyone eventually. She had built her whole life on that story the way a barnacle builds on a hull. And here was a dying stranger scraping at it with one dirty fingernail.

"You don't know that," she heard herself say.

"I know what they did to my partner," Corliss said. "I know what they did to the Hollis family at Miller's Bend, burned them out for asking about the survey teams. And I know—" he lifted the oilskin open at last "—what this is."

Inside lay a sheet of waterproof paper, hand-ruled, covered in a cartographer's fine script and a prospector's cruder annotations. A map. Not the whole river — just the upper reaches, the forbidden miles above the navy cordon, drawn in ink that had faded and been traced over and faded again. And there, near the top, where the paper creased from years of folding, a small insignia stamped in wax that had gone amber with age: a circle bisected by a wavy line, with three dots beneath.

Mirabel knew that mark. She had pulled it off a sealed strongbox not three weeks ago, forty feet down in the dark of a wreck the navy swore held nothing but coal.

Her mouth went dry.

"That's the source," Corliss said quietly. "The spring. Where the Verrin is born. There's a reason they closed the upper river, diver, and it isn't navigation. It never was." His breath was shortening; she could hear the wet rattle gaining ground beneath his words. "I'm dying. I know it, you know it, so don't waste kindness lying to me. All I want — all I have left to want — is my girl."

"Your girl?"

"Wren. My daughter." For the first time, his voice cracked, and the crack went straight through Mirabel's ribs like ice through a hull. "Nineteen now. Sharp as a filleting knife, stubborn as her mother was. When the shooting started — when I realized they'd finally stopped warning me and started hunting — I sent her upriver ahead of me. Told her to go to ground in the high country until I came for her." He pressed the map into Mirabel's hands. It was warm from his body, absurdly, impossibly warm. "That was nine days ago. I went back for our gear and they were waiting. I've been running and swimming since. And I am not going to make it, and she is up there alone, and every hour I lie here bleeding is an hour someone else might find her first."

"Find her yourself," Mirabel said. "Let me get you to Harrow Bend, patch you proper—"

"Diver." He caught her hand. His grip was failing now; she could feel it ebbing like a tide. "Look at me. Do I look like a man with hours?"

She looked. She was a salvager; she assessed damage for a living; she knew a hull beyond saving when she saw one, however much she wanted to lie. Corliss smiled at whatever crossed her face.

"There it is," he said. "Honest. Good. I need honest." He nodded at the map. "This gets you past the cordon, past the checkpoints, up to the spring country. Wren knows the marks I taught her — chalk signs, trail codes. If anyone can read her trail, it's someone who reads water the way you do. And if the navy finds her first..." He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. She had seen the burned-out homesteads along the banks; she had dived wrecks that sank too neatly, too completely, with too few survivors.

"I'm a salvager," she said. "I raise cargo. I don't carry messages. I don't play nursemaid, and I sure as hell don't go poking at whatever the Survey Office is hiding, because unlike you I intend to keep breathing."

"Yes," Corliss said. "All true. And none of it matters, does it?" His pale eyes held hers, and there was no plea in them at all — only a terrible, level certainty. "Because you've already seen the stamp. You pulled that box up and you've been losing sleep over it ever since. I can see it in you, diver. You're your father's daughter down to the bone. You can't leave a question unanswered any more than he could. It's what killed him." A cough took him, long and ruinous, and when it passed he was weaker, and he knew it, and he kept talking anyway, faster now, a man racing the tide. "So I'm not asking you to be brave. I'm asking you to make a trade. The map for the promise. My daughter for my secret. That's all I have left, and it's enough, and you know it's enough, or you'd have thrown me back in the river already."

Mirabel looked down at the map in her hands. The fine cartographer's lines. The crude annotations in Corliss's hand — *cold here even in summer*, *do not trust the east bank*, *the water sings*. The wax seal with its circle and wave and three dots, matching the strongbox rotting in her evidence locker, matching the nightmares she'd been having since she surfaced with it.

Outside, far upstream, faint as a memory, she heard the low diesel thrum of a boat engine coming down the current.

Corliss heard it too. His eyes flicked toward the door, then back to her, and he did not panic. He simply waited, the way a man waits at a card table with everything he owns in the pot.

"The promise," Mirabel said at last. Her voice sounded strange to her, distant, as if someone else were speaking through the shed wall. "You want my word. On what terms?"

"On the only terms that matter." He gathered strength from somewhere — the last of it, she understood, the final reserve tank, blown all at once. "Find her. Keep her safe. Don't hand her to the navy, don't hand her to anyone, and don't stop looking until you've looked her in the eye and told her what happened to her father. That's the whole bargain. No payment. No conditions. Just your word, given to a dying man, on a river that doesn't forget."

The engine note grew. A mile off, maybe less. In the fog-thinned morning it carried clearly across the water, unhurried, official.

Mirabel thought of her father's compass in its drawer upstairs. Thought of ten years of diving other people's wrecks because she couldn't bear to dive her own. Thought of the strongbox with its sealed lid, sitting in her locker like a debt unpaid.

She was, by every measure that mattered, a practical woman. She knew the cost of things. And she knew — with the cold clarity of a diver checking her gauges — that she was about to promise something enormous, something that would take her past the cordon and into the teeth of whatever the navy was guarding, before she had any idea what it would truly cost.

She promised anyway.

"I'll find her," Mirabel said. "I give you my word. On the river."

Something in Corliss's face let go then — not death, not yet, but peace, arriving early like a guest who couldn't wait. He exhaled, long and slow, and his hand slipped from hers, and he smiled at the rafters of the drying shed as if he could see clear through them to the high country where his daughter waited.

"She likes honey in her tea," he murmured. "Tell her... tell her the map was always hers..."

The engine sound rounded the upstream bend.

Mirabel folded the map into her shirt, next to her skin, and stood, and went to the window with her heart hammering a diver's rhythm — slow, controlled, counting.

Down the gray-gold current, riding the morning fog, came a navy launch. Unmarked. Gray-hulled. Two figures at the rail, sweeping the banks with field glasses, patient as the river itself.

They were coming for the map.

They were coming for her.
