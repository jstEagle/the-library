## 3. Navy at the Door

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a neighbor's knock.

Mirabel Underhill knew every kind of knocking her yard had ever produced. Fishermen wanting their propellers freed of snagged line knocked twice, apologetic. The widow Corrigan, who paid in pies and gossip, rapped a little tune she'd learned from her late husband. Debt collectors — there had been two of those over the years — hammered with the flat impatience of men who wanted to be somewhere else.

This was none of those. This was six knocks, evenly spaced, hard enough to shiver the frost off the door hinges. It was the knock of someone who did not need to be let in so much as informed that he was coming in.

She was already awake. She had been awake most of the night, sitting on the edge of her cot with the prospector's map unrolled across her knees, tracing by lamplight the shaky ink lines that climbed the river past every place she'd ever dived, up into country marked only in rumor. *The spring,* the dying man had rasped. *They'll kill for it. They have killed.* And then, before the end: *Wren. Find my Wren.*

The map lay now inside the oilskin wrapping of her logbook, and the logbook sat on the table beside a cold cup of coffee, and Mirabel stood very still in the middle of her salvage yard and listened to the river running brown and fast below the bluff, as if it too were holding its breath.

Six knocks again.

She crossed the yard, kicked the sleeping dog gently so he'd wake looking fierce, and opened the door.

There were four of them. Navy, though they wore no ship's colors — grey coats, grey caps, the kind of grey that was designed to be forgotten the moment you looked away. In front stood a tall man with a face like a closed ledger: Lieutenant Commander Ashford, if the prospector's fevered warnings had left her any doubt. He had the kind of handsomeness that had been sanded down by rank until only the usefulness remained. His eyes went past her immediately, cataloguing the yard — the winch, the dive tanks lined against the shed wall, the crane arm, the boat hoist dripping river water onto frozen mud.

"Mirabel Underhill." Not a question. "Lieutenant Commander Ashford. Naval Survey Division." He produced papers without quite offering them. "We're conducting an inquiry regarding a drowning. A prospector, washed downstream two nights ago. You recovered a body."

"I recovered a man," Mirabel said. "He died in my yard."

"May we come in?"

It was phrased as courtesy. It was not courtesy. She understood that the way one understands current — not by thinking but by the pull against the body. Behind Ashford, three sailors had already begun to spread out with the unconscious choreography of men who had done this many times. One drifted toward the shed. One toward the boathouse. One stood where he could see both the front door and the back path to the water stairs.

Every instinct she owned said *say yes, say nothing, be small.* That was how you survived inspections, tax men, drunk captains, all the large indifferent machinery of the world. You let them look at what didn't matter and you kept what did.

But the map mattered. And something else mattered more, something she hadn't named yet even to herself: the man had died with his hand gripping her wrist, and the last thing he'd done on this earth was trust her.

"Mind the dog," she said, and stepped aside.

* * *

They were thorough in the way water is thorough. Nothing dramatic, nothing torn or broken — just a patient, total attention. The sailor in the shed lifted every tarp, ran his fingers along the underside of every shelf. The one in the boathouse actually waded in to check beneath the hulls of her punts, coming up with numb red hands and mud to the elbows. Ashford himself walked through her cottage as though he were touring a museum of her life, pausing at the photograph on the mantel — her father, young and grinning, in diving dress older than she was, standing on this same dock with a string of catfish like a trophy.

"Your father," Ashford said. "Elias Underhill."

"You know him?"

"I know the name." Something moved behind the ledger-face, quick and gone, like a fish under ice. "He worked the upper river. Disappeared, what, eleven years ago?"

"Ten years, seven months, and some days I don't count." She heard her own voice go flat and knew she'd given him something. "Boating accident. So the coroner wrote."

"Mm." Ashford turned from the photograph. "The prospector. Did he give you anything? Papers, charts, personal effects? Families want these things returned. We're very diligent about returning things."

The lie, when it came, surprised her by how easily it fit in her mouth. She had spent forty years being the woman who told you exactly what your hull would cost and exactly how bad your fouled screw looked, because truth was the only currency a salvage diver could spend twice. Now she heard herself say:

"He had nothing. His coat was rotted through. Whatever he carried, the river took."

Ashford looked at her. It was not a long look, but it was a complete one, top to bottom, the way she looked at a wreck before committing to the descent — checking for structural honesty. She made herself meet it. She thought about the oilskin packet under the floorboard beneath the cold stove, where she'd moved it at midnight, and she thought about breathing slow the way she did at thirty feet when panic offered itself.

"The river takes everything eventually," Ashford agreed. "That's rather its nature." He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was warm. "You're a well-regarded diver, Miss Underhill. Diligent. Honest, they say. It would be a shame if diligence got tangled up in matters above your depth."

"Is that a threat, Commander?"

"It's a depth reading." He tipped his cap. "Men drown in deep water. Even good ones. Especially good ones — they keep going down after things they should leave down." He glanced once more at the mantel photograph, and this time she caught it, the flicker, and filed it away where she filed everything: *he knew her father. Or knew of him. Knew enough.* "We'll be on the river some weeks. Survey work. If you recall anything — anything at all — the launch is moored at the customs dock."

Then they were gone, boots crunching frost, the grey launch pulling away from her water stairs with that obscene navy quietness, and Mirabel stood in her own doorway feeling the strange new weight of what she'd done settle onto her shoulders like a wet dive suit.

She had lied to an officer of the law. It should have felt worse than it did. What it felt like, mostly, was the moment in a descent when you pass out of the light and commit — when turning back costs more than going on.

* * *

She waited an hour, working the winch with unnecessary noise, watching the bend of the river through the shed's cracked window. Then she dug up the floorboard, took the oilskin packet, and carried it to the dive tank.

The tank was her father's design — eight feet of riveted iron sunk half into the ground behind the shed, fed by a pipe from the river, used for testing gear and storing chain. It held nine feet of black, freezing water, and at the bottom, wedged in among the old anchor chain where no search would waste its time, she sank the map inside a spare air cylinder, capped and weighted. Anyone who wanted it would have to dive for it, in water cold enough to stop a heart, in the dark, blind.

Let them. She'd grown up in that kind of dark. It was the only bank she trusted.

She stood over the tank afterward, watching the surface go still, and the dog leaned against her leg, and she said aloud, to nobody, to the dead man, to the river:

"I don't know who Wren is. I don't know why the navy wants your map. But I keep what I'm given."

The words sounded foolish in the empty yard. They also sounded like a contract.

That evening she copied the map by lamplight onto plain chart paper — every line, every bearing, every one of the prospector's cryptic marginal marks — and folded the copy into her coat, because a secret kept in only one place is a secret half-lost. And it was while she was blowing out the lamp, the original safe in its iron dark and the copy warm against her ribs, that she saw the light on the water.

A boat, running dark, sliding up her water stairs with the ease of someone who knew these banks. A single figure stepped out, swayed, caught the mooring post, and called up in a voice roughened by drink and something older than drink:

"Miss Underhill. You've got the mark of the source drawn somewhere in this house, or I'm a cartographer's ghost. And I am — God help me — still technically a cartographer."

She put her hand on the copy in her coat pocket and watched the stranger stumble toward her door, and understood that the river had begun spending her faster than she'd planned.
