## 4. Quentin Hale, Cartographer

The Gilded Anchor smelled of spilled porter, pipe tar, and the particular despair of men who had once been somebody. Quentin Hale occupied his usual corner table—the one nearest the back door, a habit that had outlived its necessity but not his instinct for it—and watched the rain come down on Harrow's Landing like the river trying to reclaim the town early.

He was three fingers into his fourth glass and working on the arithmetic of ruin. Eleven coppers left. Rent owed at the rooming house since the first of the month. His coat had a burn hole from a stove he didn't remember falling against, and his hands—his hands, which had once drawn coastlines so fine the Admiralty framed one—hadn't held a pen steady in two years.

That was when the woman came in.

He knew her by reputation before he knew her face. Mirabel Underhill. The salvage diver. Half the waterfront talked about her the way sailors talk about weather: with respect and a certain fatalism. She'd pulled bodies and brass off the riverbed for a decade, and she'd done it alone, which in Harrow's Landing was either madness or a statement.

Tonight she looked like neither. She looked tired in the way that goes past the body into the bone, and she carried herself carefully, the way people do when they're carrying something breakable inside them. She took a stool at the end of the bar, ordered nothing at first, then changed her mind and asked for whatever was cheapest, and sat hunched over it like a woman praying over a candle.

Quentin might have gone on watching her with the idle curiosity of the drunk if she hadn't opened the oilcloth wallet in her lap to check something—and if the lamplight hadn't caught what was inside.

A sketch. Ink on tracing paper. And at its corner, small and precise even in copy, an insignia: a circle quartered by a wavy line, with three dots above the wave.

His glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

He knew that mark the way a man knows the scar on his own palm. He had drawn it himself, once, forty fathoms under a mountain, with numb fingers and a pencil stub, because the Admiralty's orders said chart everything and their other orders said chart nothing, and Quentin Hale had been young enough and honest enough to believe those instructions could not both be obeyed.

* * *

He crossed the room before he had decided to. Later he would remember the crossing as a kind of drowning in reverse—coming up toward something instead of away from it.

"Madam." His voice came out rustier than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Forgive me. That mark. May I see it?"

Mirabel Underhill turned on her stool and looked him over with the unhurried assessment of someone who assessed things for a living—wrecks, currents, men. He watched her take in the coat, the burn hole, the four empty glasses, the hands. He watched her arrive at her conclusion, and he couldn't blame her for it.

"You've got the wrong idea," she said. "I'm not selling."

"I'm not buying." He sat down on the next stool without invitation, because standing was becoming complicated. "I'm asking where you copied it from."

"Why?"

"Because I drew the original."

Something moved behind her eyes—not belief, not yet, but the recalibration of a woman who dealt in submerged objects and knew that some of them were worth more than they appeared. "That's a strong claim from a man who can't stand up straight."

"It is." He laid his right hand flat on the bar. It trembled faintly; he let her see it rather than hide it. "Two years ago I could have drawn that insignia blindfolded, in ink, to survey standard. I did draw it. In a cavern under the headwaters of the Vell, where no chart is supposed to exist. They cashiered me for it. Took my commission, my pension, and my name off the rolls. Lieutenant Quentin Hale, Royal Cartographic Service, struck through in red ink." He smiled, and knew it was a bad smile. "So you'll understand why the sight of it in a salvage yard sketch makes me feel as though I've seen a drowned man walking."

She was quiet for a long moment. Around them the Anchor went on with its noise—dice, argument, a fiddler tuning badly—but between the two of them there opened a silence with weight to it, the kind of silence that exists underwater.

"The source," she said finally. Not a question. A test.

"The spring," he corrected softly. "It isn't a source the way rivers have sources. It's—" He stopped himself, glancing at the room. Old habit. In the last year of his service he had developed the habit of stopping himself mid-sentence, and it had not saved him. "Not here."

"My yard," she said. "Midnight. If you can walk that far."

"I can walk anywhere," Quentin said, "if the destination has a floor."

* * *

Her salvage yard sat where the town gave up and the river took over—a sprawl of winches, chain, tarped hulls, and a long shed housing a dive tank big enough to float a skiff. Lanterns hung from hooks. The rain had softened to a fine drifting mist that made everything gleam.

She lit a lamp on a workbench scattered with tools and calipers, and spread the sketch under it, weighting the corners with wrenches. Up close, Quentin saw it was a careful copy—her hand was steadier than his now, which stung—and beside it lay the original: a map on greased canvas, hand-drawn, annotated in a cramped upright script.

"A prospector's," she said. "He washed into my yard two nights ago with a bullet in him. He traded me this for a promise."

"What promise?"

"To find his daughter."

Quentin studied the map. It was good work for an amateur—soundings marked, landmarks noted, distances paced and estimated with a prospector's pragmatism. But it was the annotations that made his breath go shallow. Depths. Temperatures. Notes on current direction *inside* the spring itself, which meant the man had been down there, or close enough to talk to someone who had.

"This is the upper Vell gorge," he said. "Past the navy cordon. Past the survey stations they don't admit exist." He tapped the insignia. "This mark is theirs. Survey Section Nine. It doesn't appear on any public document. I only ever saw it stamped on sealed orders."

"And you copied it into your charts."

"I charted what I found. That was my crime." He pulled out a stool and sat, because his legs had opinions. "I was assigned to hydrographic work on the Vell in '61. Routine, they told us. Depth soundings for navigation. But our orders kept pushing us upstream, past every sensible limit, into the gorge. And the soundings kept coming back wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong the way dreams are wrong. Lines that doubled back. Compasses that wandered. And the water—" He laughed, briefly, without humor. "You dive, Miss Underhill. You know what clear water looks like. This was clear the way glass is clear, and cold the way a vault is cold, and at the bottom of it, in a cavern the size of a cathedral, there were veins of metal growing out of the rock like coral. Grey-silver. It hummed. You could feel it through the suit, through your teeth. Our instruments spun near it. Our compasses lied."

Mirabel had gone very still. It was the stillness of a diver checking her air.

"They collected samples," he went on. "Small ones. The metal warped them too—bent, folded, reshaped themselves in the specimen boxes. The reports went up the chain and came back stamped with that insignia and a single word: *Continued.* No explanation. We continued. For eleven months I mapped that cavern, and every chart I drew was classified beyond my own clearance, which is a joke worth savoring—mapping a place I wasn't allowed to know existed. Then a new officer arrived. Ashford. Lieutenant Commander Ashford. He reviewed my work, praised it to my face, and reported me for unauthorized surveying of a restricted site. My own charts were used as evidence against me. They burned most of them. I kept one." His voice flattened. "They took that too, at the tribunal. Along with everything else."

"And the metal? The program?"

"Gone quiet. Or gone deeper. I stopped being told anything the day I stopped being anyone." He looked up at her. "Now you tell me why a prospector died carrying a map to a place the navy pretends is empty."

She told him. Not all of it—he sensed she was rationing, the way divers ration air—but enough. The wreck below the cordon. The strongbox with the insignia. Her father, ten years gone on this same river, officially lost in an accident, officially mourned, never officially explained. The dying man's promise. The daughter, Wren, somewhere upstream or nowhere at all.

When she finished, Quentin sat looking at the lamp flame for a while.

"Your father," he said slowly. "Underhill. There was an Underhill on the civilian contract crews. Diver. They hired locals for the deep work because navy men refused the site after the first season." He saw her face change and wished, not for the first time, that honesty were a kindness. "He was there, Miss Underhill. Before my time, but the older hands spoke of him. Best cold-water diver on the river, they said. And then the crews were rotated out and sworn to silence, and his name stopped coming up."

"He drowned," she said. "That's what the file says. Equipment failure."

"That's what files are for." He said it gently. "I believed the tribunal's version of myself for almost a year. Drunk, insubordinate, unstable—it was all very convincing, because I was doing my best to become it. Then one morning I understood that they hadn't destroyed my reputation because I'd failed. They'd destroyed it because I'd succeeded. Because I'd seen the thing and written it down, and writing things down is the one sin a secret cannot forgive."

The rain ticked against the shed roof. Somewhere out in the dark, the river ran on, indifferent, carrying its cargo of secrets toward the sea.

Mirabel reached across the bench and turned the prospector's map so it faced him fully.

"I need to go upstream," she said. "Past the cordon, past the survey stations, up the gorge to the spring. I promised a dying man. And I think—" her voice caught, steadied; he pretended not to notice, because that was a courtesy divers extend each other— "I think my father's answers are at the top of that river. I need someone who knows the water above the cordon. Every reach, every rapid, every channel the navy thinks it owns."

"You need a cartographer."

"I need you sober enough to read your own memory."

Quentin looked at his hands. Trembling. Two years of trembling, two years of the Anchor's cheap porter and cheaper oblivion, two years of being a footnote in a tribunal record. He tried to remember the last time anyone had asked him for the thing he was actually for.

"No one's asked me to be useful since they struck my name," he said. "Do you understand what you're asking? They'll do to you what they did to me, and worse, because you're not navy and they don't owe you a tribunal. Ashford doesn't leave loose ends. He leaves examples."

"I know."

"And you're going anyway."

"I promised."

There it was. Plain as a sounding line dropped into black water. Quentin Hale had spent two years learning every species of cowardice, and he recognized courage when it sat across a workbench from him wearing a diver's calluses and a dead man's promise.

"Then I'm coming," he said. "On conditions. First—you tell me everything you find, everything you learn, no matter how ugly. I spent years being managed by men who thought truth was a ration. Never again. Second—if we find your father's story, it gets written down. Properly. Charted, dated, witnessed. Whatever it costs."

"And third?"

He almost smiled. "Third, you let me draw the map. It's been so long since my hands remembered what they're for, and I'd like to die knowing they worked at least once more."

Mirabel Underhill studied him for a long moment, the way she must study a wreck before committing to the descent—weighing whether what lay beneath was treasure or trap.

"All three," she said, and spat in her palm, and held it out.

He took it. Her grip was cold from the night air and strong as a capstan, and as they shook on it, Quentin felt something shift in his chest—an old mechanism engaging after long disuse, gears that had been rusted solid beginning, painfully, to turn.

* * *

He walked back to the rooming house through the wet streets, soberer than he had been in two years and feeling every mile of it. At his door he paused, key in hand, and looked north, upriver, into the dark where the gorge began.

Somewhere up there was a cavern he had mapped and been unmade for mapping. Somewhere up there was a girl named Wren, alive or not. Somewhere up there, very likely, was Ashford, patient as tide.

And somewhere up there, perhaps, was the answer to why the navy had spent a decade and a dozen lives guarding a spring.

He went inside, dug his old drafting kit out from under the bed where shame had buried it, and blew the dust off the case. The instruments were green with tarnish. He sat down on the floor and began, slowly, clumsily, to clean them—one by one, by lamplight, like a surgeon sharpening knives for an operation he might not survive.

He was still at it when, through the thin window, he heard boots in the street outside. Several pairs. Unhurried. The tread of men who did not need to hurry because they owned the ground.

They stopped at the rooming house door.

Quentin sat perfectly still, a tarnished dividers in his hand, and listened to the knock begin.
