## 5. Upstream

The boat had been a barge once, or the bones of one, before some forgotten owner had bolted a cabin to her spine and called her a riverboat. Mirabel had bought her for less than the price of her own diving gear, and in three days of work at the salvage yard she'd made the old girl seaworthy in the way that mattered most: she would float, and she would steer, and everything beyond that was negotiation.

She stood on the deck at first light with tar up to her elbows, watching Quentin Hale pick his way across the yard like a man walking on someone else's legs. He was sober this morning — mostly sober — and it sat strangely on him. Without the drink he looked younger and older at once, the lines around his eyes deepened by clarity rather than gin. He carried a canvas roll under one arm and a battered leather case in both hands, and he set the case down on the deck with the tenderness other men reserved for children or instruments.

"That's your chart kit," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Forty years of surveying." He didn't look at her. "Every river between here and the headwaters. Soundings, current tables, shoal marks. The navy took my commission but they never thought to take my notes."

"You kept them."

"I kept them the way you keep a wound," he said. "Not because it's useful. Because it's yours."

Mirabel wiped her hands on her trousers and looked out over the water. The river ran wide and brown-green here below the yard, slow as poured honey in the morning calm, and it gave nothing away. That was the thing about rivers that land people never understood: they looked like roads. They were not roads. A road went where you decided. A river went where it had always gone, and if you were wise you went where it let you.

"Checkpoint's at Gray's Narrows," she said. "Two miles up. They've had a boom chain across the channel since the wreck was cordoned. Navy cutter, two skiffs, and a lieutenant who likes the sound of his own voice."

"Ashford's man?"

"Ashford's man's superior officer is Ashford's man. They're all Ashford's men." She flexed her stiff fingers. "You said your credentials would get us through. I'd like to hear how, exactly, before we bet our necks on it."

Quentin opened the leather case. Inside, nested in green felt gone gray with age, lay a brass cylinder the length of a forearm, stamped with the Admiralty cipher, and beside it a folio of orders so yellowed they might have been written by candlelight — which, knowing Quentin, they might have been.

"Survey Warrant, First Class," he said. "Mine. Never formally revoked — they cashiered me for what I *charted*, not for the right to chart it. Legally speaking, I'm still entitled to conduct hydrographic surveys on navigable waters." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "The navy writes rules the way rivers cut channels. Broadly, and then forgets where they go."

"And the checkpoint officer?"

"Will be twenty-five years old, cold, wet, and desperate not to make a decision. You show him paperwork with enough seals on it and he'll wave us through just to make the problem someone else's." He closed the case. "It worked when I was thirty. The trick is that no one downstream ever checks upstream. Everyone assumes the authority lies behind them."

Mirabel looked at him a long moment. There was a flimsiness in the plan that should have frightened her, and did, a little. But there was also something else underneath it — the particular steadiness of a man who had already lost everything a river could take and had come back to the water anyway. She knew that species. She was one of them.

"All right," she said. "Cast off."

* * *

The engine caught on the third pull, coughed, caught again, and settled into a loping knock that Mirabel found oddly comforting. Honest machinery told you the truth about itself. Boilers and pistons didn't lie; they complained, and you listened, and you fixed them, and that was the whole of the contract. People were harder.

Gray's Narrows announced itself before they saw it: the banks drew in, the current quickened from honey to vinegar, and the water began to talk — a low muttering against rock that deepened as the channel narrowed. Then the boom came into view, a great black chain sagging between two piling towers, draped with signal flags, and beyond it the navy cutter riding at anchor with the bored elegance of a knife left in a drawer.

"Easy on the throttle," Quentin murmured. He had put on a coat Mirabel hadn't seen before — navy blue, salt-faded, brass buttons polished within an inch of their lives. He stood at the bow like the coat remembered how to be worn. "Let them come to us. Curiosity is better than compliance. A boat that slows down has something to hide. A boat that holds its course expects to be passed."

The skiff came out anyway, four oars and a midshipman with a speaking trumpet, and Mirabel brought the boat down to a crawl and let them hook alongside. Up close the midshipman couldn't have been more than nineteen, cheeks chapped raw by river wind, and he looked at Quentin's coat the way a dog looks at an old master's whistle.

"State your business on restricted waters."

"Hydrographic survey, warrant number—" Quentin recited a string of digits with the flat boredom of a man who had said them ten thousand times, "—renewal soundings, Gray's Narrows to the upper reaches. Currents have shifted since the last charts. Your gunboats draw too much water to be guessing." He held out the brass cylinder and the folio together, and the midshipman took them like a man accepting a live coal.

The boy read. The wind moved the flags. Somewhere above the narrows a kingfisher hit the water like a thrown stone.

"This says... this authorizes passage to the *headwaters*."

"It authorizes survey of all navigable reaches," Quentin corrected gently. "Whether any given reach remains navigable is precisely what I'm paid to determine. Do you have a copy of the standing survey regulations aboard? No? Then I'll thank you not to improvise them."

It was beautiful, Mirabel thought. It was like watching a man pick a lock with his own name.

The midshipman handed back the papers, hesitated, and then made the mistake Mirabel had been counting on — the mistake every lonely boy on every lonely posting makes. He wanted to be seen as competent. "The chain can't be dropped without the cutter's signal," he said. "But I can note your warrant in the log and have the watch open the gate section. If — if that's regular procedure."

"It is," said Quentin, who had invented it forty seconds ago.

They waited while a winch groaned and a section of the boom chain swung aside, black links dripping, opening a gap barely wider than the boat. Mirabel took her through it dead slow, hands light on the wheel, feeling the current lean on the hull like a drunk leaning on a friend, and then they were through, and the narrows fell away behind them, and the river opened ahead into country neither of them had seen from the water.

Quentin exhaled for what sounded like the first time in a mile. "I haven't done that in fifteen years."

"Noticed. Your hands shook."

"My hands always shake. This time it wasn't the gin." He came aft to the wheelhouse, and something in his face had changed — the exile look burned off, briefly, by the simple animal pleasure of having gotten away with it. "You handled her well. In the current, I mean. Most people fight it."

"Fighting the current is how you drown," Mirabel said. "My father taught me that. Among other things."

Quentin was quiet for a moment. "I read the file they kept on him. After. What there was of it."

"There was a file?"

"There's a file on everyone who goes past Gray's Narrows and doesn't come back." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "Yours will exist now too. Started the moment that boy wrote your name in the log."

Mirabel turned the wheel a degree and watched the far bank slide. So it had begun already, then. The ledger. Somewhere in a navy office a clerk was opening a page with her name at the top, and every mile upstream would be written beneath it in a hand getting steadily less clerical.

"Good," she said, and meant it, and saw that Quentin understood why.

* * *

By afternoon the river had changed its mind about being gentle. The valley narrowed, the hills came down to the water in dark shoulders of pine and granite, and the current ran fast and glassy over gravel bars you could see ten feet down — beautiful water, killing water, the kind that looked like a painting right up until it ate your boat. Mirabel had grown up on this reach and it still made her throat tighten, the way a familiar staircase makes a sleepwalker nervous.

Quentin spread his old charts across the wheelhouse desk and weighted the corners with engine parts. "Rapids start at Miller's Teeth, six miles on. In my day there was a runnable channel along the north bank, close in under the bluff." He tapped the faded line of soundings. "But that was before the survey teams started blasting. They may have moved half the riverbed."

"Then we'll find out the way rivers prefer," Mirabel said. "Slowly, and screaming."

He laughed — a rusty sound, surprised at itself — and for a while they ran the boat in companionable silence, and Mirabel discovered she was doing something she hadn't done in ten years: talking to someone about her father without it tasting like ash.

"He used to say the river keeps accounts," she said, watching the water. "That nothing you take from it stays taken, and nothing you give it stays given. I thought it was just something divers said. Now I'm starting to think he meant it literally."

"They usually do mean it literally, the old ones." Quentin rolled a chart closed. "My grandfather fished this same water. He claimed there were pools that remembered drowning men. Wouldn't net them after dark." He glanced at her. "What happened to him? Your father. If you don't mind my—"

"I mind," Mirabel said. "But you'll hear it eventually, so it may as well be now, while the river's doing the listening." She kept her eyes on the channel. It was easier that way. "Ten years ago he took a contract. Salvage, supposedly — a snagged barge above the narrows. He went up with two deckhands and came back as a report. Boat found adrift. Gear intact. Men never recovered. The navy sealed the reach for 'ordnance survey' and paid my mother a settlement that bought silence and a headstone with no body under it."

"And you've dived the river ever since."

"I've dived the river ever since," she agreed. "Looking for a boat that was found adrift. Which means looking for whatever made the crew leave it, or whatever made sure they couldn't stay aboard. Ten years of wrecks and snags and none of it adding up — until a dying prospector bleeds out in my yard holding a map to the exact stretch of water the navy won't let anyone chart." She finally looked at Quentin. "You were cashiered for charting it. He died for keeping it secret. Somebody ought to write a book about what this river does to people who pay attention."

"The book would be short," Quentin said. "Everyone pays attention eventually. That's the trap."

Miller's Teeth announced themselves the honest way: white noise. It came up the valley like weather, a low continuous roar that Mirabel felt in the deck plates before she heard it properly, and by the time they rounded the last bend the rapids filled the channel ahead — a quarter mile of broken water, granite teeth bared through the foam, the whole surface shrugging and folding like something alive trying to turn over in its sleep.

"North channel," Quentin said, chart in hand, voice steady in the way of a man gripping a rail inside himself. "Under the bluff. Hard to starboard, then hold the line — the rocks on the left are shallow enough to see, which means the ones on the right are deep enough to kill."

Mirabel took the boat in.

There was no describing it afterward and she never tried. The world became water and noise and the wheel fighting her like a living thing, spray coming over the bow in sheets, the engine screaming as she gave it everything and the current took half of it back. The bluff wall rose on their right, streaming, close enough to touch, and once — only once — the hull struck something soft and grinding that lifted her heart into her mouth, and then slid free, and they were through, dropping into the long green pool below with the roar fading behind them like applause from a crowd that had decided, this time, to let them live.

They drifted. Steam rose off the cooling engine. Water ran off the cabin roof in bright threads. Somewhere a hawk turned high over the pines, indifferent as arithmetic.

Quentin sat down slowly on a coil of rope. "Fifteen years," he said again, "and I still can't decide whether I love this river or I'm at war with it."

"It's not either," Mirabel said. Her hands, she noticed, were shaking now too, and she let them. "It's a witness. Everything that happens on this water, it sees. And it forgets nothing — it just files things where nobody thinks to look." She nodded upstream, where the valley bent away into country that grew darker and quieter with every mile. "Somewhere up there is the place it's been filing my father for ten years."

Quentin followed her gaze. Then he frowned, and leaned forward, and raised one hand toward the eastern bank, where a thread of smoke stood up thin and straight above the treeline — too straight for a campfire, too deliberate for a lightning strike.

"Settlement smoke?" he asked.

"No settlement on this reach," Mirabel said quietly. "Not anymore."

She put the wheel over and the boat swung upstream toward the smoke, and the river carried them forward, patient and beautiful and armed, toward whatever it had been keeping.
