## 6. The Burned Village

The river told them before their eyes did.

Mirabel smelled it first — that sweet, greasy reek of wet char that clings to a burned place for weeks after the flames have gone, seeping out of the ground like something the earth itself was trying to cough up. Then came the silence. A river settlement, even a poor one, even a small one, made noise from half a mile off: axes, dogs, children, the slap of fish on cleaning boards. This stretch of the Verrin had nothing. The current moved past the bend with a hush that felt deliberate, like a congregation lowering its voice at a funeral.

"Smoke's too old for yesterday," Quentin said beside her at the rail. He'd been studying the treeline through his cracked spyglass for the better part of an hour, and his hands had gone very still in the way she was learning to read as fear rather than calm. "Ten days. Maybe two weeks."

"You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell it from the way the gulls have stopped circling." He lowered the glass. His eyes were bloodshot — he'd slept badly, or not at all; he drank less than his reputation suggested but slept worse than either of them liked to admit. "Gulls follow boats because boats mean scraps. There are no boats. Whatever happened here, nobody's come back to fish."

Mirabel eased off the throttle until the patched old *Magpie* slid rather than chugged, her hull whispering against water the color of gunmetal. The morning fog was burning off in rags. And then the village appeared around the bend, and Mirabel felt the breath go out of her in a slow, involuntary way, the way air leaves a diver who has just seen something below him in the dark.

It had been a pretty place once. She could see that. Thirty-odd houses on stilts along the bank, a drying shed, a chapel with a green copper spire gone black as a burnt match. The fire had taken the roofs first and then worked down, leaving behind a forest of charred posts rising out of the mud like the ribs of some enormous drowned animal. Ash lay over everything in a fine grey silt, and where the tide of it had run down to the waterline it had dried into a crust that looked almost like snow, if snow were the color of grief.

Nobody had come back to rebuild. Nobody had come back at all.

"Survey teams," Mirabel said quietly. It wasn't a question. It was the phrase from the prospector's map, written in a dying man's shaking hand: *they call themselves survey teams.* She'd thought, reading it in the lamplight of her salvage yard, that it might be exaggeration — the fever-talk of a man shot full of holes and full of grievance. Standing here, breathing the ash, she understood it had been understatement.

Quentin didn't answer right away. He was gripping the rail, and she saw his knuckles whiten, and when he spoke his voice had a strange flat quality, the tone of a man reciting something he'd tried hard to forget. "There was a village called Harrow Bend on my charts. Population two hundred and six. Fishery, ferry crossing, chapel. I charted it myself, eleven years ago." He swallowed. "It isn't on any navy chart anymore. I checked, before we left. The whole reach of river between the gorge and the falls has been... removed. Redrawn as unpopulated."

"They erased it."

"They erased worse than villages." He turned away from the rail. "Bring us in. Downstream side, by the drying shed. If anyone's left, they'll be watching the water, and they'll trust a wreck-diver's boat sooner than they'll trust anything that looks official."

* * *

They found the first body not in the village but above it, which told Mirabel everything about how the burning had been done.

He was in the trees on the slope, a man of perhaps fifty, laid out under a rough shroud of birch bark weighted with stones — buried, or as near to buried as one person alone could manage. Whoever had done it had taken care. The bark was tied with net-cord in neat knots, the kind of knots a fisherman ties without thinking, and someone had set a clay cup at the head of the grave, filled with rainwater, the way you'd leave a drink for a traveler.

Wren's father had taught her that the dead tell you how they died by where they lie, and Mirabel had learned it again in ten years of pulling bodies out of the Verrin. This man hadn't died running. He hadn't died fighting. He'd been laid down by hands that respected him, and that meant whoever survived this place had spent time here afterward — days, maybe — alone, doing the work of mourning that should have belonged to a whole community.

The village itself held more graves. Seven, all told, marked with crossed paddles driven into the ash-mud. But there were house-foundations with no graves at all, and doorways with no answers, and Mirabel counted the stonework of hearths and did arithmetic she didn't want to finish. Two hundred people, Quentin had said. Where were two hundred people supposed to go when soldiers burned their homes? Not downstream — the checkpoint would have logged them. Not into the hills — the hills were navy survey territory now, patrolled.

Upstream, then. Or nowhere.

"Mirabel." Quentin's voice came low from the chapel doorway. "You need to see this."

Inside, the chapel had burned hot enough to crack its own bell, which lay in the wreckage of the tower like a split chestnut. But the back wall — stone, older than the rest, older maybe than the village — still stood, and across it someone had painted, in tar or pitch that the fire had only half-consumed, a single line of letters tall as a man:

**WE ONLY WANTED THE RIVER TO STAY OURS**

Below it, smaller, added later by a different hand, in charcoal:

*If you are reading this, do not go to the spring. They watch it day and night. They took our men in diving suits and brought back three of them changed, and the rest they did not bring back at all.*

Mirabel stood in the ruined chapel with ash sifting down through the broken rafters onto her shoulders like grey snow, and she read the words twice, three times, and felt them settle into her the way ballast settles into a hull — heavy, permanent, changing how she sat in the water.

"Brought back changed," she repeated. "Changed how?"

"I don't know." Quentin had gone pale. "But I know what the spring does to compasses, Mirabel. I've seen it. If it does that to iron needles—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I was cashiered for drawing a map. That's what I told myself, all these years. That they destroyed me over ink and paper. Standing here, I think the map was never the crime. The crime was what the map showed."

* * *

They found the survivor at dusk, and she nearly put a fish-gaff through Quentin's throat before Mirabel talked her down.

She had been watching them since morning — that much became clear. She'd let them walk her village, read her wall, count her graves, and she had waited, patient as a heron, until they returned to the boat and Mirabel was alone on deck checking the mooring line. Then the reeds at the water's edge parted and there was a woman standing hip-deep in the shallows with a gaff leveled at Mirabel's chest, and Mirabel had ten years of instinct to thank for the fact that she didn't move for the weapon, because moving would have ended everything.

The woman was perhaps sixty, lean as driftwood, with white hair cut short and practical and eyes the pale grey of the river in November. Her clothes were army blankets resewn into a coat. Around her neck, on a cord, hung a small tin locket, dented, and her thumb kept finding it and pressing it flat against her sternum like a talisman.

"You're not navy," she said. It wasn't a question either, but it demanded an answer.

"No," Mirabel said. "Salvage. Mirabel Underhill. My partner's name is Quentin Hale."

Something moved behind those grey eyes at the second name. Something old and cold. "Hale. The cartographer. The one they broke." She spat, delicately, into the water. "We knew about you, Hale. Word comes down the river even when the navy thinks it owns the river. You drew the source on a chart and they took your commission and your name and left you your life, and everyone said, look what happens to a man who tells the truth." The gaff didn't waver. "So why are you here now? Truth-telling again? Look what it got us."

"It got me drunk for eleven years," Quentin said from the deck, quiet and level. "And it got your village burned while I drank. I'm here because I'm done paying that bill."

The gaff stayed up a moment longer. Then the woman looked at Mirabel — a long, measuring look, taking in the diver's rope-scarred hands, the salt-and-river calluses, the way she stood balanced on a wet deck the way other women stood on dry land — and something in her posture shifted, the way a lock shifts when the right key finally turns.

"My mother was a pearl-diver on the coast," the woman said. "I know diver's hands when I see them. Put that boat against the bank and come to my fire, both of you, and mind you step where I step, because I've spent two weeks learning which ground is watched."

Her name was Odile Ferrant, and she had been the schoolteacher of Harrow Bend for thirty-one years.

* * *

Her camp was a hollow in the hillside above the village, screened by young pines, invisible from the water. A fire pit dug into the slope so the light couldn't be seen from the river. Snares. A smoke rack with three small fish. One blanket, one pot, one book — a child's primer, water-stained, kept wrapped in oilcloth with a tenderness that told Mirabel it was the last thing she'd saved.

Odile fed them fish and bitter tea and talked the way survivors talk: evenly, precisely, with long silences where the feeling had been cut out so the telling could continue.

"They came at night, the first time. Six weeks ago. A steam launch, no markings, and men who said they were a survey team mapping flood levels. Polite. They bought eggs. They paid in good coin. They asked questions about the fishing upstream, about the current past the gorge, about whether anyone swam in the deep pools." She stared into the fire. "Old Tomas Ferro told them the truth, because why wouldn't he? We're river people. We answered honest questions honestly."

"And then?" Quentin asked.

"Then they started diving." Her thumb went to the locket again. Pressed it. "In the pools below the canyon mouth, where the spring feeds in. Men in hard-hat rigs, the kind with the air hose to a barge. They dove for nine days. On the fourth day, one of them came up screaming about lights in the water. On the seventh, another came up and wouldn't speak at all, ever again, not a word for the rest of his life as far as we know. And on the ninth day they brought up metal." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible in the firelight. "My husband was hired as a deckhand to work the winch. Eleven crowns a day. He came home the first night and said the metal sang. That's what he said. *It sings, Odile. When it comes up through the water, it sings, and the compass needle spins like a frightened bird.*"

"What happened to him?" Mirabel asked, though she already knew the shape of the answer, the way you know the shape of a wreck before you've cleared the silt off it.

"He wouldn't stop talking about it. In the tavern, on the dock, to anyone. He wrote to the provincial paper." Odile's voice stayed level; only her hands betrayed her, pressing the locket harder and harder, as though trying to push it through her chest. "The survey team came back on a Tuesday. Not six men this time. Forty. And they didn't ask questions. They gave us one hour and a promise that anyone who cooperated would be resettled with compensation, and they put the men who'd worked the dive barge on one boat and the rest of us on another, and my husband was on the first boat, and I have never seen him again." The fire popped. Somewhere up the dark slope, a snare-wire ticked in the wind. "I came back. Don't ask me how. I came back and I buried who I could find and I have lived in these woods eating snared rabbit for two weeks, waiting to see if anyone in this whole world gives a damn what was done here."

Mirabel set down her tin cup. Her own hands weren't steady either, and she let them be seen, because it seemed suddenly important that this woman see that the story had landed where it should.

"The prospector," she said. "A man named Corliss. He came downstream shot through, dying. He gave me a map to the spring and asked me to find his daughter, Wren. Did she pass through here?"

Understanding dawned slow in Odile's face, and then grief, and then — strangely — relief, as though the burden she'd carried alone for two weeks had just been set down among other shoulders. "Corliss. Yes. He came through ahead of the burning — he'd been prospecting the high country and came down for supplies, and he heard the dive barge singing-metal talk same as everyone. He went upriver like a man with a lit fuse under him. And his girl—" Odile closed her eyes. "Wren came through four weeks ago. Alone. Sixteen years old and carrying a rifle too big for her and asking after her father with a face like flint. I fed her. I told her what I'm telling you. She listened the way you're listening — like every word was a stone she was adding to a wall she meant to build."

"She went on upstream?"

"Toward the canyon. Toward the spring." Odile leaned forward, and the firelight carved her face into planes and shadows. "Listen to me, diver. There were marks on the trail markers going up — little coded cuts, the kind Corliss taught his girl when she was small, river-signs, smuggler-signs. She was leaving a trail. Either for him to follow or for anyone who might help her. Three cuts angled left means *water route safe*. A circle with a cross means *danger, soldiers*. And the last mark I saw, four miles up, was fresh-cut and deep." Odile's grey eyes fixed on Mirabel's. "A circle with a cross, and beneath it, a single horizontal stroke. Danger, soldiers — and *someone following me*."

The word landed between them and neither Quentin nor Mirabel touched it for a moment. Someone else was hunting Wren. Someone who moved fast enough to leave a frightened girl cutting warning signs into trail-markers as she ran.

"Ashford," Quentin said at last, hoarse. "Lieutenant Commander Ashford keeps a forward element above the gorge. I saw the requisition orders before they cashiered me — a 'hydrographic detachment.' If Corliss's map exists, if a dying man passed it to someone downstream—"

"Then they'll want the map," Mirabel said. "And they'll want the girl who might know where it went. And they'll want anyone who came asking after either." She looked at Odile. "Come with us. There's room on the boat."

For the first time, the schoolteacher smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, that smile — all the warmth burned out of it, leaving only iron. "No. Someone has to stay who knows these woods, who knows which ground is watched, who can bury the next ones if it comes to that. This village was mine for thirty-one years." She pressed the locket one final time. "But take this."

She worked the dented tin locket off its cord and pressed it into Mirabel's palm. Inside, behind a smudge of clouded glass, was a curl of hair and a scrap of paper folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth. Mirabel opened it by firelight. On it, in a careful hand, was written: *Tomas Ferro, deckhand, and forty-one others, taken Tuesday. Remember us.*

"Witnesses," Odile said. "That's what they're afraid of. Not divers, not maps. Witnesses. You carry that upstream, Mirabel Underhill. You make them afraid of it again."

Mirabel closed her hand around the locket until the tin edges bit her skin, and made the only vow that had ever come to her easily, the kind you make with your whole blood: *remember us.*

They cast off in the last grey light, the *Magpie* sliding silent past the black spire of the chapel and the seven paddle-marked graves and the tar-lettered wall — WE ONLY WANTED THE RIVER TO STAY OURS — and Mirabel stood at the wheel with the locket around her neck and the promise in her mouth like a stone.

Behind them the burned village sank back into fog. Ahead, up the darkening channel, the canyon walls had begun to rise on either side of the river like the jaws of something patient, and pinned to the last trail-marker on the near shore, catching the lantern-light as they passed, was a fresh-cut sign that had not been there that morning.

Three cuts, angled left. Water route safe.

And beneath it, scratched shallow and hurried, as if by someone with no time and no light: a single horizontal stroke.

Someone was following Wren — and they were close enough now that the trail was being marked for *them*.
