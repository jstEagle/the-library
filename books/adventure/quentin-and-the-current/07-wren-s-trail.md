## 7. Wren's Trail

Quentin Hale had spent eleven years drawing rivers he was forbidden to name, and in that time he had learned that a map was never finished — it was only abandoned. The same, he was beginning to suspect, was true of guilt.

He sat in the stern of the *Magpie* with Mirabel Underhill's copied sketch spread across his knees, the morning fog still burning off the water around them, and tried to make his hands stop shaking. Not from the drink. He'd been dry for three days now, and the tremor had settled into something quieter and more permanent, a fine vibration like a tuning fork left ringing in his bones. It was the insignia. It was the sight of that stamped circle — the spring coiled inside a triangle of river — rendered in Mirabel's careful pencil on salvaged chart paper, and the way his whole body had recognized it before his mind did, the way a man flinches from a stove long after the burn has healed.

"You keep looking at it like it owes you money," Mirabel said. She was at the tiller, barefoot, steering them through the slack water below the burned village with an ease that made him envious. Ten days on the river together and she moved through its moods the way other people moved through rooms they'd grown up in.

"It owes me more than money," Quentin said. "It owes me a career. A reputation. Roughly a decade." He turned the sketch over, then back again. "You've been staring at this too. Don't think I haven't noticed."

"I've been staring at the river."

"You've been staring at the river *through* this." He tapped the insignia. "There's a difference, and I know it because I invented it. That's what cartography is, Mirabel — deciding what people see when they look at a place. The navy taught me that much before they took everything else."

Mirabel said nothing, but she eased off the throttle, and the *Magpie* slowed until it was barely holding against the current, drifting. That was her way of giving him the floor. He'd learned that too: she didn't grant conversations, she created room for them and let you decide whether to fall in.

"Tell me about the marks," she said instead.

And there it was — the thing he'd been circling since dawn, since before dawn, since he'd woken at first light with the sketch pinned under his boot heel and realized what he'd been looking at all night without seeing.

Because the sketch wasn't only a map. The prospector — Corliss, Wren's father, the man who had died in Mirabel's salvage yard with a bullet in him and a bargain on his lips — hadn't just drawn the route upstream. He had drawn it the way a man writes a letter he expects to be intercepted. There were annotations in the margins, small and deliberate: a chevron beside the second bend above the gorge. Three short strokes stacked like a fence rail where the river split around a gravel bar. A circle with a tail, hooked like a fish, near what looked like a cliff face. Quentin had taken them for surveyor's shorthand, the private notation of a rough-handed man who'd learned drafting from a manual and improvisation.

But he'd seen that shorthand before. Eleven years ago, in a chart room in the naval depot at Harrow's Bend, when a survey team came down from the upper river with field notes no one would let him catalog.

"They're trail signs," Quentin said. "River signs. An old system — pre-navy, pre-survey. Pilots used it on the upper reaches before the charts were standardized, back when knowing the water was worth more than owning it. The navy suppressed the notation decades ago because—" he smiled without any humor in it, "—because a river full of literate pilots is a river full of witnesses. You can't cordon a waterway if everyone on it can read it."

"And Corliss could read it."

"Corliss could *write* it. Different thing, rarer thing. And he taught someone." Quentin looked up at her. "The chevron means *high water here, pass on the far side*. The three strokes are a cache mark — something buried or hidden, three days' travel apart, a chain of them leading somewhere. The hooked circle—" His throat tightened. "The hooked circle means *shelter*. Overhang, cave, a place to get out of the weather. They're not navigation notes, Mirabel. They're instructions. Follow the chain, use the shelters, and you'll arrive alive."

Mirabel brought the boat alongside a deadfall snag and killed the engine. The silence that rushed in was enormous, river-silence, the sound of a great volume of water doing exactly as it pleased.

"He drew it for Wren," she said. "Not for me. I was the courier. He was dying and I was the fastest thing between him and his daughter, so he gave me the letter and hoped I'd deliver it." Her jaw worked. "Except he didn't give me the letter. He gave me the *map*, and the letter was underneath the whole time, and I've been carrying it upside down for two hundred miles."

"You couldn't have read it anyway."

"No." She laughed, short and bitter. "That's supposed to make me feel better? That I promised a dying man I'd find his daughter and then spent a week steaming past her messages because I don't speak the language?" She pushed off the snag with a boathook, harder than she needed to, and the *Magpie* swung back into the current. "How do I read it, Hale? All of it. Every mark. If I'm going to follow a girl up this river I'm going to follow her properly."

* * *

They spent the afternoon anchored in the lee of a shale bank while Quentin reconstructed the notation from memory, and it was worse than he'd expected, because memory kept offering him things he didn't want.

He remembered the survey team at Harrow's Bend — four men, river-burned, tight-mouthed, carrying brass tubes sealed with wax. He remembered being ordered to file their field notes unexamined, which was itself an order no honest cartographer could obey, and obeying it anyway, and hating himself in the small cowardly way that weak men hate themselves, efficiently and without consequence. He remembered the notation in those notes matching Corliss's marginalia stroke for stroke, which meant either that Corliss had been navy once, or that the navy had learned its secrets from men like Corliss and then burned the bridges behind them.

He remembered, most of all, the reason he'd been cashiered. Not the charting of the source — that was the charge, but it wasn't the crime. The crime was that he'd drawn the source *beautifully*. He'd rendered the canyon and the spring and the metal-bearing strata with a loving hand, and the board had understood, correctly, that beauty is a form of publication. A map that makes a place lovable makes it findable. He had committed aesthetics against the Crown's interest, and they had taken his commission, his pension, and his name from the rolls, and he had spent eleven years drinking in river towns and telling himself the story where he was the martyr.

Sitting on the deck of the *Magpie* with a dead prospector's code spread out before him, Quentin finally admitted the truer version: he hadn't been punished for revealing the secret. He'd been punished for wanting to. And the wanting had never left. It was sitting in his chest right now, warm as gin, whispering that the source was real, that the metal was real, that he could be the man who mapped it at last — and beside that whisper, another voice, Mirabel's voice, saying *find her, find her, find her*, and Quentin understood that for the first time in eleven years he had two hungers and only one body, and one of them was going to win.

"The marks go in sequence," he told Mirabel, laying out the sketch weighted with four dive weights at the corners. "Chevron, strokes, circle, and then—" he pointed to a smudge low on the page, almost erased, as though Corliss had written it and then tried to take it back, "—this. A double cross-bar over a vertical line. In the old notation it means *danger beyond, turn back*. But it's been struck through. Once, hard."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning either 'the danger is gone,' or 'I know the danger and I'm going anyway.'" Quentin met her eyes. "A father wrote this. Which do you think he meant?"

Mirabel was quiet a long moment. Out on the water, a heron lifted off the shallows with that prehistoric slowness they have, all legs and patience, and the current carried it downstream while it flapped upstream, fighting, gaining nothing, refusing to land.

"He meant both," she said finally. "That's what fathers do. They warn you and they believe in you in the same breath, and they die not knowing which one you heard." She gathered up the sketch with a tenderness Quentin had never seen her show an object. "First mark. Where's the chevron fall?"

"Second bend above the gorge. Two days, if the water's kind."

"The water won't be kind." She was already moving, checking fuel, coiling line, doing the hundred small tasks that were her liturgy. "Get your charts. We'll navigate by his marks and correct by yours. Between the dead man and the drunkard we might manage."

"That's the most generous thing anyone's said about me in years."

"Don't get used to it." But she said it while looking at the sketch, and Quentin saw — clearly, the way he saw coastlines, the way he saw the truth in a place's shape — that she was afraid. Not of the river. Of the marks. Because every mile upstream was a mile further from the law, the yards, the life she'd built over her father's grave, and toward whatever it was that put a bullet in Corliss and a cordon on a wreck and a cashiering on Quentin Hale.

* * *

They found the first mark at dusk on the second day, and it was wrong.

The chevron should have been painted or carved at eye level on the outside of the bend, where a passing boatman would see it — that was the grammar of the system, Quentin explained, signs were sentences, placement was syntax. This one was carved high, eight feet up the rock face, and fresh. The stone dust was still pale in the grooves; rain hadn't softened the edges; moss hadn't begun its slow reclamation. Weeks old at most. Days, possibly.

"Corliss died ten days ago," Mirabel said flatly. "In my yard. With a hole in him."

"So he didn't cut this." Quentin stood in the bow with a lantern, tracing the chevron's geometry without touching it. The cuts were confident, economical, made by someone strong and unhurried. "Someone else knows the notation. Someone following the same chain."

"Wren."

"Wren would be reading the old marks, not making new ones. And she wouldn't cut *high* — the height's wrong, it's for someone scanning from a boat, marking progress for a boat behind them." He lowered the lantern. "This is a relay sign. Someone is moving upriver ahead of us and leaving breadcrumbs for whoever comes next. Either they expect company—"

"—or they're herding it," Mirabel finished.

The word dropped into the dark between them and neither picked it up, because picking it up meant examining it, and examining it meant admitting what it implied: that Ashford's men weren't searching the lower river for a map anymore. They were on the water ahead. That the cordoned wreck, the burned village, the shot prospector — that these weren't a wall but a funnel, and everyone who knew anything about the source was being channeled upstream into it, and the channel was marked, helpfully, in a dead language only three living people could read.

Quentin thought of the survey team at Harrow's Bend, four men and their wax-sealed tubes, and wondered how many of them were still alive, and hated himself for wondering it in the tone of a cartographer estimating attrition rather than a man counting friends.

"We should turn around," he said. It cost him something to say it, and he watched Mirabel register the cost — the ex-cartographer volunteering to abandon the source, the one thing left of his old self. "Two of us, one patched boat, no guns worth the name, chasing a girl we've never met into a gorge that the navy has been quietly emptying for a decade. As a navigator I rate our odds at—"

"She's sixteen," Mirabel said. "Maybe seventeen. Her father bled out on my floor holding my hand and the last word he got out was her name, and I said yes. I said yes, Corliss." She turned and looked at him, and in the lantern light her face was all planes and iron, the face of a woman who had already paid for this promise in advance and refused to default. "You can put me ashore anywhere you like, Hale. But the boat goes upstream."

"I wasn't proposing to put you ashore." He climbed back over the gunwale. "I was establishing the record. When they write up the inquiry — *if* anyone writes it up — I want it noted that the cartographer objected."

"Noted," she said, and something in her mouth that lived where a smile would be in other people flickered and was gone. "Start the engine."

* * *

They ran through the night and the next day, and the river narrowed and quickened beneath them, and on the afternoon of the third day, in a slot of gray rock where the water ran green and cold, they found the second mark — and with it, proof.

The three stacked strokes, the cache mark. Cut into a leaning slab beside a gravel bar, and below it, half-tumbled into the water, a flat stone that didn't match the others, placed, not fallen. Mirabel dove for it — of course she dove, Quentin was learning that Mirabel answered every question with her lungs — and came up gasping in the freezing current with a waterproof oilskin roll clutched in one fist.

Inside were letters. A dozen of them, tied with wire, wrapped in waxed canvas, dry as bone. Letters in a young woman's hand — sharp, slanted, furious even in ink — addressed to *Papa*, and letters in a rougher hand addressed to *Wrennie*, unsent, drafts, a correspondence conducted across a distance that had swallowed both ends of it.

And on top of the bundle, folded separately, a single sheet in the father's hand, dated six weeks past:

*If you find this before I do, Wrennie — go to ground at the hooked circle and stay there. Men came asking after you in Harrow's Bend. Navy men, but not proper ones, no names, no papers. Your mother's brother sold your description for twenty dollars and I mean to have a conversation with him about it. Stay off the water. Stay off the water. Stay off the water.*

Three times. A man hammering a nail.

Quentin read it twice while Mirabel wrung the river from her hair, and felt the hope in his chest curdle precisely as the plan had promised it would — sharpening, hardening, taking on edges. Because the letter changed everything. Wren wasn't lost. Wren was hiding, deliberately, competently, from men who had bought her description. And the marks on the rocks — the fresh chevron, cut high, cut recently —

"They're not hers," Mirabel said. She had arrived at it a beat behind him and was already pale. "The new marks. They're the navy's. They found Corliss's old signs and they're extending the chain, cutting new ones, because they know she reads them. They're writing to her in her own language. Telling her the path is safe." Her voice went very quiet. "Telling her to come out of hiding."

"Luring her upstream," Quentin said. "To the source. Where Ashford needs—" and he stopped, because the rest of the sentence assembled itself with terrible clarity, the way a chart assembles when the last sounding drops into place. Ashford needed the daughter. Not for punishment. For *leverage*. The prospector had known the source, known the metal, and had gone to ground rather than deal — and a man with no price will sometimes acquire one, late, in the shape of a child. "They're not hunting her, Mirabel. They're *farming* her. Growing her trust with her father's own alphabet, harvesting her fear, walking her up the river one friendly mark at a time into—"

"Into the gorge." Mirabel was already at the tiller, already feeding fuel, the letters thrust inside her coat against her heart like contraband. "How far to the hooked circle?"

"Four days. Three if we run the narrows at night, which we shouldn't."

"Then we run the narrows at night." She looked back at him once, and Quentin saw that the fear was still in her face but it had changed states, solidified, become something that could be steered. "Every hour we lose, she gets a letter she believes and a mark she trusts. Every hour, her father's own love walks her closer to Ashford's guns." The engine caught, roared, settled. "Somebody's been forging a dead man's handwriting, Hale. Time somebody answered it."

The *Magpie* swung upstream into the narrowing green water, and the gorge opened ahead of them like a sentence neither of them wanted to finish reading.
