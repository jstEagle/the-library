## 8. The Toll Keeper

The gorge announced itself long before they saw it.

Mirabel heard it first as a change in the river's voice — a low, grinding mutter beneath the water-sound, like stones being chewed somewhere ahead. Then the walls began. The banks that had been soft with alder and gravel all morning drew inward, steepened, and rose into cliffs of grey granite streaked white with old high-water marks, so that by midday they were running through a corridor of stone with the river coiled at the bottom of it like something captive and furious.

Quentin stood at the bow with his charts forgotten in their oilcloth, reading the water instead. "Serpent's Throat," he said. "That's what the navy called it. Officially it doesn't exist — no survey was ever sanctioned past the lower falls — but every smuggler on this stretch knows it by its proper name." He glanced back at her. "The Toll."

"You said there was a way through."

"I said there was a *price*. Whether it's payable is another matter entirely." He tapped the sketch she'd copied from the dead man's map, where a small inked symbol sat at the gorge's mouth: a circle quartered by two crossed lines, like a wheel seen edge-on. "That mark isn't cartography. It's an invitation. Or a warning, depending on how you read it."

"And who collects?"

"Her name is Odile Vasseur," Quentin said, and for the first time since she'd known him he sounded almost afraid. "They call her the Toll Keeper. She held this passage before the navy ever bothered to chart it, and she'll hold it after they've given up on it. If your prospector marked her wheel on his map, then either he paid her toll once—" he hesitated, "—or he owed her one."

The boat slid into the gorge's first shadow, and the temperature dropped ten degrees as though they'd passed through a door.

* * *

Mirabel had grown up on wrecks, and she knew the grammar of sunken things: how a hull told you the story of its dying if you knew where to put your hands. The gorge spoke the same language. Halfway up the northern wall, wedged in a crevice forty feet above the current line, lay the ribs of a river steamer, black with age, its paddle-wheel housing split open like a broken chest. Higher still, barely visible in the cliff-shadow, hung the prow of something smaller — a barge, maybe, or a gunboat — hoisted out of the water and left there like a trophy nailed to a door.

"She doesn't just take tolls," Mirabel said quietly.

"She keeps accounts," Quentin answered. "And she displays them."

The narrows opened without warning into a basin — a round pocket of green water where the river paused to gather itself before the thunder of the falls downstream. And moored across the entire width of the exit channel, hull to hull, sat six boats: dories and patched steam launches and one converted naval cutter, lashed together with chain and strung from bank to bank with tarred rope hung thick with rusted bells. A floating fence. Beyond it, the channel narrowed again into white water, and beyond *that*, the upper river ran silver toward mountains that had no business being so close.

A woman stood on the deck of the cutter.

She was older than Mirabel expected — sixty, perhaps more, with a face browned and seamed like driftwood and a fall of iron-grey hair bound at the nape with copper wire. She wore a man's oilskin coat over a dress that might once have been fine, and she held a long-handled gaff hooked casually over her shoulder the way another woman might carry a parasol. She did not call out. She simply watched them come, and the watching had weight to it, and Mirabel found herself shipping oars without having decided to.

"Wheel-mark on your paper," the woman said when they were twenty feet off. Her voice carried easily across the water, unhurried, pitched like someone accustomed to being heard over rapids. "Show me."

Mirabel held up the copied sketch. Odile Vasseur studied it for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes — not surprise exactly, but recognition, the way a diver recognizes a familiar wreck under new silt.

"Where did you get that?"

"From a dying man," Mirabel said. "He washed into my salvage yard with a bullet in him. He traded me that map for a promise."

"A promise." The word came out flat. "To find his girl. Wren."

The bottom dropped out of Mirabel's stomach. "You know him."

"I knew four men who went up this river looking for that spring over thirty years." Odile set the gaff down and came to the rail. "Your father was the fourth."

* * *

They were brought aboard the cutter, which surprised Mirabel until she understood it wasn't courtesy — it was quarantine. On the cutter's deck she could see everything: the bell-line, the six boats, the dozen hard-faced men and women lounging along the rails with the loose-limbed readiness of people who were very good at violence and bored of waiting for reasons to use it. Odile Vasseur ruled this passage the way a spider rules a web, and everything here was silk.

She poured tea from a dented tin pot into three cups, and her hands were steady, and her eyes never left Mirabel's face.

"Underhill," she said. "Elias Underhill. Salvage man out of Greywater Bend. Big shoulders, quiet voice, laughed like a door hinge. Dived in water cold enough to kill a lesser man and came up smiling." She handed Mirabel a cup. "You have his chin. And his habit of looking at a person like you're taking their measurements for a coffin."

"He disappeared when I was eleven," Mirabel said. Her own voice sounded far away. "Navy report said his boat was lost in the lower reaches. Spring flood. They sent my mother a form letter and a flag nobody in our family had earned."

"The flood." Odile repeated it without inflection, tasting the word and finding it rotten. She drank her tea, set the cup down, and looked out at the gorge wall where the dead steamer hung in its crevice. "I pulled that boat's engine out of the shallows two miles below the source canyon, Miss Underhill. Intact. Not stove-in, not burned, not flooded. Someone had shut it down, neat as church, and walked away from it. Floods don't do that."

The tea was hot and bitter and Mirabel didn't taste any of it. "Then what happened to him?"

"He reached the spring." Odile turned back, and now her voice was quieter, and somehow worse for it. "Thirty-one years ago your father stood where I'm going to tell you the spring stands, and he saw what grows there, and he understood it faster than the navy men who'd been hunting it for a decade. He understood what it would be *used* for. So he did what salvage men do with dangerous cargo." A thin smile. "He scuttled it. Collapsed the approach shaft, drowned the lower gallery, and made the source unreachable — or so he believed. Then he started back downriver to fetch help, or witnesses, or dynamite, I never learned which. The navy caught him at the falls."

Quentin had gone very still beside her. "Ashford's predecessor," he murmured. "Commander Roke. That was during the first survey."

"Roke himself," Odile confirmed. "And Roke didn't want a trial. Trials make documents, and documents float. So Elias Underhill went into the water above the falls with his wrists bound to his own dive belt, and the official record says spring flood, and a little girl in Greywater Bend grew up diving wrecks because the river took her father and she meant to take something back."

Mirabel set the cup down because her hands had begun to shake, and she would not let this woman see it. Ten years. Ten years of diving that same stretch of river, of sweeping the silt around every snag and shoal, half-hoping and half-dreading that she'd find him — and the whole time he hadn't been *lost*. He'd been murdered for the same secret that was sitting folded in her breast pocket, drawn in a dying man's hand.

"They let you live," she said. "Knowing all this. Why?"

"Because I keep the toll, and the toll is silence." Odile's gaze flicked to Quentin, lingered. "Though it seems the navy's memory has gotten longer lately. They've been through twice this season with survey barges and too many guns. They're reopening what your father closed, Miss Underhill. And they're not being gentle about the people who remember."

* * *

The toll, when Odile finally named it, was not money.

"Passage costs nothing," she said. "Passage has always been free for anyone carrying that map, because the man who drew it paid me years ago with information I've been sitting on like a hen. No — what I want is smaller and heavier." She leaned forward. "When you find Wren — and you will find her, because she's up there, she's been up there since before the navy's latest push, living wild above the treeline like her father taught her — you tell her about me. You tell her Odile Vasseur kept her father's last letter safe for six years waiting for someone worth trusting with it. And then you bring her down this river alive, both of you, past my bells, and I'll consider the account settled."

"Her father's letters are with her," Mirabel said slowly. "The prospector said—"

"The ones he wrote her, yes. But Elias wrote letters too, before he went up. To a woman in the valley he trusted. She died before she could pass them on, and they came to me, and I have been keeping a dead man's words warm for thirty years because there was no one else." For just a moment the driftwood face cracked, and Mirabel saw underneath it something tired and old and grieving. "I am not sentimental, girl. I am *inventory*. And some inventory gets heavy."

She gave them the passage signal — three pulls on the bell-line, and the chained boats swung apart on their anchor lines like a gate — and as they pushed off she caught Mirabel's arm at the rail, her grip strong as a winch.

"One thing more. The navy's forward camp is past the second bend. Ashford's men. They've taken to shooting first and dragging afterward." Her eyes were flint. "Your father died because he thought one honest man could drown a secret. Don't make his mistake twice. Take people with you. Come back the way you went. Promise me that much, at least, since you're so free with promises."

Mirabel looked at her — at the grey hair bound in wire, at the dead steamer hanging in the cliff, at thirty years of silence kept like a debt — and felt something shift in her chest that had been locked since she was eleven years old. Grief, she realized, was just love with nowhere to go. But this — this had somewhere to go. Upstream. Into the gorge. All the way to the source.

"It's not a promise," she said. "It's an inheritance."

Odile Vasseur watched them for a long moment, then released her arm and stepped back, and the driftwood face closed like a door.

"Then spend it well," she said, and rang the third bell herself.

* * *

They shot the middle channel with the falls' spray cold on their necks and the upper river opening ahead of them, wide and bright and treacherous, and for a mile neither of them spoke. It was Quentin who broke the silence, standing at the bow with the wind pulling at his coat, and when he turned his face was strange — lit and hollowed at once, like a man seeing a country he'd only ever charted from rumor.

"The shaft your father collapsed," he said. "If the navy's reopened the approach, they'll have divers in the lower gallery within the week. Which means whatever's left of his gear—" He stopped. "Mirabel. His kit would still be down there. Thirty years in the water, but that metal doesn't corrode, nothing corrodes down there, and if his slate survived—"

"If his slate survived, it names names." She said it flatly, because saying it any other way would crack her open. Her father's handwriting. His last observations, sealed in the dark water all this time, waiting the way Odile had waited. Waiting the way she herself had waited, sweeping silt over a grave that wasn't a grave.

The river bent north around a shoulder of black rock, and the current quickened, and Mirabel put her back into the oars and let the work hold her together.

It was Quentin who saw it first — a glint far downstream through the gorge mouth they'd just cleared, where the light came off steel instead of water. He raised his glass, and lowered it very slowly, and his voice when it came was stripped of everything but fact.

"Gunboat," he said. "Flying Ashford's pennant. She's holding station at the narrows — she's not following us." He passed the glass to Mirabel, and through it she saw the grey hull sitting patient as a headstone, and the signal lamp on its mast beginning to blink: three short, three long, three short.

Not pursuit. A message, relayed upstream, ahead of them.

Every camp on the river between here and the source now knew their names.

"Well," Mirabel said, and shipped the glass, and took up the oars. "Then we'd best be worth the telling."
