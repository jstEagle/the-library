## 9. Ashford Closes In

Quentin Hale had spent eleven years learning to read water the way other men read faces, and the river below the Toll Keeper's gorge was telling him a lie.

It told the lie beautifully. The current ran smooth and green in the long pool below the smuggler's weir, folded into slow braids that caught the late sun like hammered pewter. A stranger would have called it kind water, easy water, a stretch to make up lost miles under a full moon. But Quentin saw the way the driftwood rode the surface — not drifting at all, really, but circling, held in an eddy that spun off the eastern bank where no eddy should be. He saw the seam of colder water threading downstream from the north channel, visible only as a faint roughening, a whisper of texture on otherwise glassy skin. Cold water came from depth. Depth on this reach meant the old quarry cut, and the quarry cut meant the navy's gunboat could sit in it with engines cold and masts lowered and wait for them like a pike waits under a lily pad.

"She's there," he said. "Port side of the pool, in the quarry shadow. Has been since before dusk."

Mirabel didn't look up from the engine. She knelt on the deck plates with her sleeves rolled and her hands buried to the wrist in the guts of their patched riverboat, coaxing fuel through a line she'd spliced twice already since the gorge. Grease streaked her forearms to the elbow. In the slanting light she looked less like a salvage diver than something the river itself had assembled out of silt and stubbornness — which, Quentin thought, was not far from the truth.

"How sure?" she asked.

"Sure enough that I've been wrong about it exactly once in my career, and it cost me everything." He kept his voice low. Wren was forward, coiling line with the mechanical diligence of someone who needed her hands busy so her mind wouldn't drown her. The girl hadn't spoken more than ten words since the Toll Keeper's story — since the words *your father died at the source* had landed on Mirabel like ballast dropped from height. "The eddy's wrong for the wind. There's coal smoke in the air that isn't ours. And every bird that nests on the east bank went quiet an hour ago."

Mirabel sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on a rag that was past saving. She looked at him across the engine housing, and he watched her do the arithmetic he'd already done: one patched boat against a gunboat; three people against whatever complement Ashford carried; night coming down over water they couldn't outrun.

"Options," she said. It wasn't a question. It was how she worked — the same way she dove, he suspected. Name the situation plainly, then go down into it.

"We run for it in the dark," Quentin said. "We might make the narrows before they light us up. Might."

"And if we don't?"

"Then we're wreckage floating toward a wrecking crew."

"Next option."

He had known this moment would come. He had felt it gathering for two days, ever since the Toll Keeper — Vessa, she was called, though Quentin remembered her by another name from another life, from charts he'd drawn in a room that smelled of ink and ambition — had pressed a key of dried fish into Mirabel's hand and told her the truth about her father. Quentin had watched Mirabel absorb that truth the way stone absorbs rain: slowly, invisibly, all the way through. And he had understood that whatever happened next, he was done being a man who protected his own skin first. That man had drowned years ago in a court-martial hearing. What remained was something rawer and less useful and, he hoped, better.

"There's one more option," he said. "But it costs."

* * *

They tied up in the willows above the old towpath, where the bank grew soft and private and the boat's lantern wouldn't show. Wren built a fire small enough to cup in your hands — the girl made fires the way she did everything now, minimal, efficient, as though warmth were a debt to be repaid — and they ate the Toll Keeper's fish without much talk. Across the black mirror of the pool, the quarry shadow lay undisturbed. Patient as arithmetic.

Quentin went aft to the cargo locker where he kept his charts.

Not copies. Not the sanitized survey sheets the Admiralty had let him keep when they cashiered him — those were public record, stripped of anything worth knowing. These were the others. Forty-one sheets of linen-backed paper, hand-inked, rolled in oilcloth against damp: the work of his life. Every sounding he'd ever taken, every contour he'd ever interpolated from a lead line and a prayer. The upper river in detail no government chart possessed and no government wanted anyone to possess. The gorge approaches. The quarry cut. The canyon above the falls where the water came out of the earth singing, though he hadn't known what the singing meant when he'd measured its depths at nineteen years old with cold-numbed hands and a heart full of the future.

He unrolled the top sheet just to look at it once more. His own handwriting, young and precise: *Sounding 214, 11 fathoms, bottom of white sand, unusual warmth.* Beneath it, in the margin, a note in his older hand, added after the trial: *They took my commission. They cannot take what I know.*

He almost laughed. They were about to take what he knew. He was going to hand it to the fire himself.

"You're quiet," Mirabel said behind him. She'd come aft without sound — divers moved like that, he'd noticed, as though the world were always water and always listening.

"I'm deciding," he said, "whether I'm the kind of man who can actually do this, or only the kind who says he can."

She looked at the open roll of charts. He saw her understand, and he saw what followed the understanding — not pity, which he could have borne easily, but recognition, which was harder. She knew what it was to have your life's work be a wound.

"Ashford has your surveys already," she said carefully. "Doesn't he? The originals went to the Admiralty when—"

"When they cashiered me, yes. The official set." He touched the edge of the top sheet. "But these aren't the official set. These are the ones I drew after. Corrections. Additions. The channels I found that I never reported because reporting them felt like handing a knife to a man I'd already seen use one." He looked up at her. "There are back-channels on these sheets, Mirabel. Ways through the narrows that Ashford's own pilots don't know, because I never gave them to him. If I burn them tonight, we lose them forever. So does he. And the head start we buy is the difference between his crew spending tomorrow searching dead water and finding us alive in it."

Wren had drifted aft too, silent as her father's daughter, and stood at the rail with the firelight moving on her face. She said nothing. She didn't need to. Quentin had spent enough days with the girl to know that her silences were verdicts, and this one, he thought, was in his favor.

"Burning them doesn't bring your commission back," Mirabel said quietly. "Or your name. It doesn't give you anything back."

"No," he agreed. "That's rather the point."

* * *

He did it in stages, because a man should have to feel each page go.

The willow bank was soft mud and old leaf mold, and the fire Wren had built he fed until it stood knee-high, the first real flame they'd allowed themselves in four days. Then he knelt beside it with the oilcloth roll across his thighs like a sleeping animal, and he began.

The early sheets first — the ones from his first survey season, when the river had been a romance and he'd been its devoted fool. He watched the gorge take shape in ink and then in ash, the contours curling orange at their edges before collapsing into black flakes that rose on the heat and vanished upstream. His instructor at the cartographic school had told him once that a chart was a promise: *every line you draw, you draw for the man who comes after you, whose life may hang on your honesty.* He had believed it absolutely. He had built his whole self on it. And then the Admiralty had taken his honest lines and used them to fence off a spring that killed people, and the promise had curdled into complicity, and Quentin Hale had spent eleven years drinking in riverside towns and telling himself that a ruined mapmaker harmed no one.

Burning them felt nothing like ruin. It felt like surgery.

"The narrows sheet," Mirabel said softly, when he reached it. "You're certain?"

He held it a long moment. The back-channel was there in his fine young hand — a slot of deep water behind the shattered ledge, six feet wide, passable at medium stage with a boat drawing less than four feet, invisible from the main channel. Their boat drew three and a half. It was, quite possibly, the only reason any of them would see sunrise on the far side of Ashford's guns.

"Certain," he said, and fed it in.

The paper caught with a sound like a held breath released. For a moment the back-channel blazed entire — every sounding, every bearing, the little annotation in the margin reading *Hale, Q., discovered this alone, age twenty-two, best day of my life* — and then it was gone, and the best day of his life was smoke climbing the willows, and Quentin found, to his genuine surprise, that he was smiling.

"What?" Mirabel asked.

"Nothing. It's only—" He turned another sheet, considered it, gave it to the flames. "I thought this would feel like dying. It feels like putting down weight I forgot I was carrying."

Wren crouched opposite him across the fire. Her eyes were bright and unreadable, the way they always were, but she reached out and took the next sheet from his hands before he could offer it — took it gently, the way you take something fragile from someone shaking — and laid it on the coals herself.

"They can't have them," she said. It was the longest sentence she'd offered anyone in two days. "Whatever else happens. They can't have them."

"No," Quentin said. "They can't."

They burned the rest together, the three of them, feeding the fire in silence while the pool below stayed dark and the coal smoke mingled with the navy's and the river ran on indifferent beneath it all. Sheet by sheet, year by year, the young cartographer Quentin Hale dissolved into ash and rose off the water in pieces. What remained kneeling at the fire afterward was thinner, older, lighter — a man with nothing left to lose except the two people warming their hands beside him, which was, he reflected, the first possession he'd ever had worth defending.

* * *

They cast off at moonrise.

No lamps. Mirabel at the tiller with Wren on the bow sounding with a pole, calling depths in a whisper, and Quentin amidships doing the one thing left to him: navigating from memory. It was strange and liberating, memory. With the charts gone, the river lived nowhere now but inside his skull, and he found he knew it better than he'd ever known it on paper — every bend arriving in his mind a half-second before it arrived in the world, every shoal remembered as a texture, a sound, a pull on the hull.

The pool passed without incident. The quarry shadow gave up nothing but its own darkness, and Quentin chose to believe the gunboat's crew had grown complacent after dark rather than that luck had simply not yet finished with them.

Then the narrows.

He talked Mirabel in by feel and recollection, standing at her shoulder with his lips near her ear, his voice barely louder than the water. "Ledge ahead, thirty yards, broken on the western end. Hard to port. Now hard. *Hard.*" The boat leaned, the current seized them, and for nine endless seconds they slid along a wall of rock close enough to touch, the main channel's roar on their right hiding them as thoroughly as any curtain. Then the slot opened — the back-channel, his back-channel, exactly as a younger man had sworn it existed — and they were through, into slack water beyond, into the long dark miles where the river narrowed and the canyon walls began to rise and the source waited somewhere above like a held note.

Behind them, faint across the miles, a searchlight woke and swept the pool they'd left, painting the willows white, finding nothing but ash on a mudbank and the ghost of smoke already scattered.

Mirabel exhaled. Wren sat down abruptly on the deck plates, as if her legs had only now informed her of the evening's events. Quentin looked back once at the distant light, then forward at the black water running toward the mountains, and felt the strangest thing settle over him — not safety, nothing so foolish as safety, but certainty. Whatever Ashford took from him now, whatever the river or the navy or the metal-that-sang demanded as final payment, the man aboard this boat was no longer the man the Admiralty broke. That man had gone into the fire with his charts, willingly, page by page.

What remained was crew.

"Dawn in four hours," he said. "If we hold this pace, we reach the canyon mouth by tomorrow night."

Mirabel nodded, eyes on the dark ahead. Then she frowned, and tilted her head, and Quentin heard it a heartbeat later himself — carried down the channel on the cold upstream air, thin and rhythmic and unmistakable.

Engines. More than one. Behind them, yes — but also, very faintly, ahead.
