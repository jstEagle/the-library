## 10. White Water

The gorge had been closing on them all morning.

Mirabel felt it in the way the light changed — the sun that had lain warm and broad across the water since the burned village now came down in narrow blades between walls of granite, and the river beneath it ran dark as poured ink. The air smelled of wet stone and cold iron. Somewhere ahead, low and constant, was a sound she knew the way a child knows its mother's voice: white water. Not the chatter of riffles or the gossip of shallows, but the long, rolling roar of a river that had decided to stop being navigable.

Quentin stood at the bow with his charts — what was left of them, the salvaged corners he'd refused to burn even after sacrificing the rest to Ashford's gunboat — and he did not say anything for a long time. That silence told her more than any words would have.

"How bad?" she asked.

He turned. In the blade-cut light his face looked older than it had a week ago, the drunkard's softness gone out of it, replaced by something leaner and more frightened and more honest. "The Kestrel Narrows," he said. "I charted them once. From the bank, at flood stage, because no one with sense would run them from the water." He tapped the paper in his hands. "Half a mile of falls stacked like stairs. Boulders the size of churches. And one channel through the middle that I never saw anyone take, because everyone who tried it went into the rocks."

"Then we portage."

"Look at the banks." He gestured with his chin. She looked. The left wall rose sheer out of the current, sixty feet of wet black stone without so much as a ledge for a goat. The right was better only in the way that drowning is better than burning: a talus slope of shattered rock, steep and loose, that would take them two days to climb with the boat broken down and carried piece by piece — two days exposed on open ground while Ashford's gunboat worked its way upriver behind them.

They had heard the gunboat's engine once, yesterday evening, far downstream and faint as a memory. It had been enough. Quentin had stood on the stern listening until the sound faded, and then he'd said, quietly, "He's not turning back," and neither of them had slept well after that.

"We run it," Mirabel said.

"You haven't seen it."

"I've been diving wrecks since I was fourteen, Quentin. I've pulled bodies out of water gentler than this and water meaner. I know what rivers do." She put her hand on the wheel, on the wood worn smooth by her father's hands before they were hers. "And I know what Ashford does. I'll take the rocks over the navy."

Quentin looked at her for a moment longer, and then he nodded, once, the way men nod when they've decided to follow someone off a cliff and want it noted that they did it with their eyes open. "Then steer where I tell you and nowhere else. And tie yourself to something. When she goes over — and she will go over — you want to be part of the boat, not cargo."

* * *

They spent an hour preparing, and Mirabel made herself slow about it, because haste on a river is just fear wearing work clothes. They lashed everything loose to the thwarts. They double-tied the fuel drum and the strongbox with its source-metal fragment, which hummed faintly against her palm whenever she touched it now, a sound she felt in her teeth more than her ears. They rigged lines fore and aft. Wren — who had come down from the treeline hard and silent and full of edges, who spoke to Mirabel only when the speaking was necessary — coiled rope without being asked, her small quick hands doing the work of someone twice her age, and Mirabel caught herself watching the girl and thinking of promises, and of how heavy a promise gets when it starts walking beside you.

"Stay low amidships," Mirabel told her. "Hold onto the mast step. Whatever happens, don't let go."

Wren looked up at her with her father's gray eyes and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. But she took hold of the step, and she held on.

The entrance to the narrows announced itself first as a change in the current — a long smooth tongue of water sliding toward the drop, deceptively calm, the river drawing breath — and then as sound, enormous, filling the gorge wall to wall, a roar that Mirabel felt enter through the soles of her boots and live in her chest. Spray hung in the air ahead like fog. Through it she could see the first fall: a curtain of white three feet high spanning half the channel, and beyond it chaos.

"Starboard!" Quentin shouted. "Hard starboard — the seam, follow the seam!"

She threw the wheel over. The boat answered sluggishly, loaded deep as she was, and the current took the stern and swung it like a door. For ten seconds Mirabel steered by Quentin's voice alone, his arm stabbing forward out of the spray — *left, left, NOW right* — threading the seam of green water between two brown tongues that wanted to wrap around the hull and roll her. Then the first fall took them.

It was not like going over a waterfall. It was like the world ending briefly and then, surprisingly, continuing. The bow rose, hung, and the river fell away beneath them, and for one weightless instant Mirabel was flying — and then twelve tons of patched riverboat slammed into the trough below and the impact went through her spine like a hammer through a nail. Water came aboard in tons, cold as a slap, streaming off her shoulders, tearing at everything lashed down. The boat groaned, wallowed, and came upright.

"Second drop!" Quentin screamed. "Thirty yards — bear left — LEFT—"

She bore left. Too late, or not late enough; the second fall hit them quartering, and the boat slewed sideways, and Mirabel saw the boulder.

It rose out of the boil dead ahead, black and glistening, bigger than her salvage shed, wearing a hat of foam. The whole remaining channel bent around it, and they were pointed at its heart.

"QUENTIN—"

"I see it! Hard aport — give me everything—"

She gave it everything. The rudder bit, the hull groaned, the bow swung — inches, feet, a desperate arc — and the boulder slid past their starboard side close enough that Mirabel could have touched it, could have read the fossil shells embedded in its face. The current slammed them away from it like a hand shoving a child clear of a stove.

And then something below decks went *crack*, and the wheel went dead in her hands.

She knew the feel of it instantly, the way a driver knows a snapped rein: the wheel spinning free, sweet and useless, the rudder below no longer answering anything but the river's whim. A branch, a rock, something down there in the freezing churn had fouled or broken her, and now the third and longest fall was coming up fast, and they were entering it sideways.

"Hold on!" she screamed — to Wren, to Quentin, to God if He was taking visitors — and the world went white.

* * *

The third fall did not throw them. It swallowed them.

Mirabel came back to herself underwater. That was the first thing — the shock of it, the sudden total silence except for the roar coming through the water itself, a vibration more than a sound, the river's heartbeat conducted straight into her bones. She was still tied to the wheel, thank God for her own paranoia, the line taut above her, and the boat was above her somewhere, upside down or nearly, a great dark keel blocking out the light. Air bubbles streamed past her face, silver ropes rising toward a surface that kept changing its mind about where it was.

Her lungs were already burning. She climbed the line hand over hand, found the transom, found the gunwale, and surfaced inside the pocket of trapped air beneath the overturned hull — a low roaring cave of darkness lit green-gray from the sides, where the river ran past within reach of her face.

"Wren!" Her voice bounced off the hull inches above the water. "Quentin! Sound off!"

A cough in the dark. Then Wren's voice, thin and furious and alive: "Here! I'm here!"

"Quentin?"

A pause. Long enough that Mirabel's heart stopped being a heart and became a fist. Then, from the far end of the air pocket, ragged: "Still aboard. Barely. My shoulder's — it's fine. It's not fine. Is the boat supposed to be upside down?"

"The boat is having opinions." Mirabel got her bearings. The hull under her hands was intact — she could feel no new breaches near her, though the crack that had taken the rudder was somewhere aft. The river outside was carrying them, hull-over, down the tail of the narrows, and every few seconds the whole cave of air lurched as the wreck struck something and spun. Sooner or later they'd hit something that didn't let go, or the air pocket would find a hole and leave them all at once.

She thought fast, the way her father had taught her to think underwater: *the panic is loud, but the problem is quiet. Find the quiet problem.*

The rudder. If it was merely fouled — wrapped in the mooring line that had torn loose, jammed with debris — it could be freed. If it was broken, they were done, and everything after that was just geography. Either way, someone had to go down and look, and there was exactly one person aboard who could hold her breath past the point where other people started seeing spots.

"Listen to me," she said. "I'm going under the hull. If I'm not back in four minutes—"

"Don't," Wren said. Just that. One word, cracked down the middle.

"—in four minutes, you get to the bank however you can. Quentin, get her there. Promise me."

"Mirabel—" Quentin began.

"*Promise.*"

Silence, and then Quentin said, "I promise," in the voice of a man who understood exactly what promises cost, because he'd watched her make one and follow it up a river into hell.

Mirabel filled her lungs — three deep breaths, the last one held hard — and dove.

* * *

The cold hit her like a wall of nails.

Even in summer the mountain rivers ran snowmelt, and this one had been born in a glacier. The cold closed on her chest immediately, squeezing, trying to make her gasp, and she fought it down with discipline older than fear: *small breaths, don't fight the water, be a fish, be a leaf.* She pulled herself along the overturned hull toward the stern, feeling her way in the green murk, and the current tore at her like dogs at a rag.

The rudder, when she found it, was a horror.

Not broken. Fouled — but fouled badly. The mooring line from the bow, torn loose in the second drop, had wrapped itself three times around the rudder stock and then wound tight, cinching the blade hard over, and the whole snarl had snagged a drowned tree limb as thick as her thigh, which had wedged itself under the hull and lodged against the skeg. The boat wasn't steering because the boat was dragging an anchor made of its own rigging and half a forest.

She got the knife out. The current made the work obscene — every time she braced against the stock to cut, the river shoved her sideways, and the limb flexed, and the line went slack and then bar-tight again like something alive and fighting her. She cut one wrap. Two. Her fingers were going from pain to a distant, dangerous numbness, and her lungs were beginning to send up alarms she couldn't ignore much longer. Three wraps. The limb shifted, grinding against the skeg, and the whole wreck yawed, and the current picked her up and slammed her against the rudder blade hard enough to drive the air out of her in a burst of silver.

Panic came up her throat like floodwater. *Be a fish. Be a leaf.* She caught the stock, hauled herself back down into position, and sawed at the last wrap with arms that belonged to someone else.

The line parted.

The limb tore free, the rudder swung loose, and the wreck — freed of its drag — accelerated. The current grabbed the hull and Mirabel both and flung them downstream, and she was suddenly no longer under the boat at all but tumbling alone in open water, rolling end over end in the green thunder, with no air in her chest and no idea which way the surface was.

This was the moment. Every diver knew it. The moment the river asked its real question: *what are you willing to pay?*

She'd paid before. Ten years ago, in a way, she'd paid her father — given him to this same river, or whatever had taken him from it, and built a life on the loss like building a house on a grave. She had paid Quentin's trust and Ashford's law and the salvage yard she might never see again. She had paid Wren's father, in a sense, by promising to find what he couldn't keep.

But the river wanted more. The river always wanted more.

Her vision was going dark at the edges, blooming with little lights. She stopped fighting. That was the trick, the one her father had written in the margin of her first diving manual, the one she'd never fully believed until now: *when you're lost underwater, stop. Let the water decide. It knows the way out.* She went limp, spread her arms, and let the current have her — and the current, indifferent, magnificent, rolled her twice more and then spat her upward into light.

She broke the surface gasping, coughing, alive, fifty yards below the last of the falls. Behind her the wreck was righting itself — she saw it happen, the hull rolling ponderously over as the trapped air escaped, Quentin and Wren clinging to the mast step like barnacles, the boat half-swamped but floating, floating, wallowing in the calmer water below the narrows with its rudder swinging free behind it.

Mirabel swam. It took everything she had left, which wasn't much — arms that wouldn't obey, a body shaking with cold, lungs full of fire — but she swam, and Quentin saw her and got the boat turned by main strength, and Wren leaned over the gunwale with her father's gray eyes wide and wet and furious, and together they hauled her aboard like a landed fish.

* * *

They grounded her on a gravel bar a mile downstream, in the first pool calm enough to bail the boat, and Mirabel lay on the stones in the weak sun and shook and shook and could not stop.

Quentin covered her with the oilskin and built a driftwood fire with hands nearly as unsteady as hers, and Wren — Wren sat down beside her, cross-legged, and said nothing for a long time. Then she reached out and took Mirabel's frozen hand in both of hers and held it, hard, the way you hold something you've almost lost.

"That was the stupidest thing I've ever seen," Wren said finally. Her voice wobbled and she hated it, Mirabel could hear her hating it. "You promised my father you'd find me. You didn't promise to die doing it."

"No," Mirabel agreed. Her teeth chattered through the words. "The dying was optional."

"It wasn't funny."

"It was a little funny."

And Wren laughed — one short, cracked, unwilling laugh, the first Mirabel had heard from her, there on the gravel bar with the steam rising off the stones and the fire catching and the river running past, huge and cold and satisfied, having taken its price and let them keep their lives. Mirabel held the girl's hand back and thought about her father, and about how the river had taken him too, and about how she was beginning to understand that he hadn't lost to it. He'd traded with it. The way she was trading. Everything worth having on this water was bought with something else.

Quentin crouched by the fire, feeding it sticks, and when he looked up at her his face had gone strange — not afraid anymore, but alert, the look of a man who has remembered something important at the worst possible time.

"Mirabel," he said slowly. "That last pool below the falls. Before we grounded. Did you see the bank? The north bank?"

She tried to call the image up through the fog of cold and exhaustion. Green water. Gravel. And — yes. Something else. "There were marks. Cut into the rock. Fresh ones."

"Not fresh." Quentin's jaw tightened. "Old. Survey marks. Navy cuts — I'd know them anywhere, I helped set standards for them." He stared upstream, toward the narrowing gorge and the roar that never quite died, and his voice dropped. "Those marks are twenty years old, Mirabel. They were cut the year your father disappeared. Which means the navy didn't find the source last season."

He met her eyes across the fire.

"They've known the way almost as long as you've been asking questions. And someone marked the trail — recently — with signs I think only Wren can read."

Wren went very still. Slowly, she turned her head upstream, toward the canyon walls, and Mirabel saw her father's gray eyes go bright and hard as struck flint.

"He's alive," Wren whispered. "Daddy's marks. He's *alive*."
