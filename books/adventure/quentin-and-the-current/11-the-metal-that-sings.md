## 11. The Metal That Sings

Quentin Hale had spent eleven years telling himself the source was a rumor he'd invented to ruin his own life. It was easier that way. A man could survive his own folly; what he could not survive was the certainty that he had been right all along, and that being right had cost him everything.

So when Mirabel's dredge hook came up out of the green water below the gorge with something tangled in its net — something small, dark, and wrong in a way his eyes understood before his mind would admit it — Quentin felt the old vertigo open beneath him like a trapdoor.

"Careful," he said, and heard how thin his voice came out.

Mirabel looked up from the winch, gloves slick with river water, hair plastered to her forehead. "It's just ore. Some kind of slag from the wreck above the falls."

"It isn't slag." He stepped forward before he could stop himself, then made himself stop, made his hands stay at his sides. Eleven years of discipline, and it all dissolved at the sight of a lump of metal no bigger than a fist. "Don't touch it with bare hands. And for God's sake, don't bring a compass near it."

She paused, one eyebrow arching — that look she gave him when the ex-cartographer in him started talking like a man reciting scripture. But she set the fragment down on the deck planking without argument, which was its own kind of trust, and Quentin crouched beside it as though approaching a sleeping animal.

Up close, it was worse than he remembered.

The fragment was roughly ovoid, dense beyond its size — when Mirabel had lifted it, her arm had dipped as if the river itself were reluctant to give it up. Its surface was not smooth but *grained*, marked with fine parallel striations like the growth rings of some impossible tree, and between those lines ran a luster that no forge had ever produced: not silver, not iron-grey, but a deep moving sheen, as though light were slow to leave it. Water still clung to it in beads that refused to run off, standing on the surface like tiny lenses.

And it was humming.

Not loudly. Not even certainly — Quentin had to hold his breath and lean close, close enough that his own pulse competed with it, to be sure. But it was there. A tone at the very floor of hearing, steady as a struck bell an hour after the strike, rising faintly through the deck boards and into the soles of his boots. The boat's engine ticked and cooled around them; the river muttered against the hull; and through all of it, patient and indifferent, the fragment sang.

"That's not possible," Mirabel said quietly.

"No," Quentin agreed. "It isn't. And yet."

He fetched his compass from the chart locker — his father's compass, brass-cased, the one instrument that had survived the navy's confiscation of everything else — and held it two feet from the fragment. The needle swung lazily, drunkenly, seeking north and finding instead some private direction of its own. He brought it closer. At six inches the needle spun slowly, continuously, like a thing drowning. At three inches it simply lay flat against the glass, defeated.

Mirabel watched this with the particular stillness she brought to everything underwater — the stillness of a woman who had learned that panic kills. "The prospector's map," she said. "The marks along the river. They weren't landmarks, were they. Not exactly."

"They were readings." Quentin sat back on his heels. His mouth had gone dry. "Declination anomalies. I charted them myself, eleven years ago, before I understood what they meant. Every compass within a mile of the source lies. The error grows as you approach. I thought it was magnetic ore in the canyon walls — a surveying nuisance, worth noting and nothing more." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, hollow as a dropped bucket. "I noted it. In my official report. I drew the navy a map straight to the thing that would end my career, and I apologized for the inconvenience of the needle."

* * *

They moored that evening in a cutbank willow, upstream of the burned village, downstream of whatever waited ahead, and Quentin built a small fire because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Mirabel sat across from him with the fragment wrapped in canvas between them, positioned downwind of the fire like a guest who might overhear.

"Tell me," she said. "All of it. You've been feeding me pieces since the salvage yard — Ashford's name, the survey teams, your court-martial. Tonight you're going to tell me the whole shape of it, because tomorrow we go into the gorge, and I don't dive blind."

Quentin fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"The program was called Meridian," he said. "That much I can say now; the Official Secrets Act expired with my usefulness. Hydrographic Office, special charts division. On paper we were mapping navigational hazards on the upper tributaries. In truth, we were looking for something the Admiralty had known about since before either of us was born — reports going back a century. Wrecked boats whose compasses failed. Prospectors who came downriver rich and mad. A spring, somewhere in the headwaters, where the water came up through metal that didn't behave like metal."

He glanced at the wrapped bundle. "What you pulled up today is a piece of it. Torn loose, probably, by their own blasting. Which means they've been working the source for years, and carelessly, and that carelessness is the only reason either of us is holding proof."

"What does it do?" Mirabel asked. "Besides sing and lie to compasses."

Quentin was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"When I was cashiered," he said finally, "the charges were falsification of charts and unauthorized disclosure. Both true, in their way. What I actually did was copy my final survey — the full anomaly readings, the approach to the source — and send it to a man at the Royal Society. A geophysicist. I thought it was science. I thought someone should know." He turned the stick in his fingers. "Six weeks later the Society man had resigned his fellowship and moved to Canada, and I was standing in a courtroom watching men I'd served with look through me. And one of them — a captain, senior, the sort whose face you never forget — leaned over during recess and told me, very kindly, that the difference between treason and patriotism was which side of the map you stood on when the metal was needed."

"The metal was needed for what?"

"For weapons." The word came out of Quentin flat and cold, stripped of all his usual ornament. "Mirabel — the fragment hums because it holds a charge. Not electricity as we understand it; something older, something in the metal itself, something the water gives it. It doesn't just distort a compass needle. Distortion is the smallest thing it does. Scaled up — and the deposits at the source are scaled up beyond anything in the mining records — it can derange every magnetic instrument within miles. Ship's compasses. Gun-laying systems. The new torpedoes they're building that steer themselves by magnetism. Every device a modern fleet depends on to find its enemy — blinded, all at once, or worse than blinded: *deceived*, pointed wherever the metal wishes."

Mirabel had gone very still. "Or," she said slowly, "you could put it in the enemy's waters. Their harbor. Their fleet anchorage."

"And watch their entire navy steer itself onto the rocks in a fog," Quentin said. "Yes. Or shield your own ships so no magnetic mine can touch them. Or build a bomb casing that no instrument can track. There are men in whitehall who have spent twenty years and any number of lives learning exactly what this stuff can do, and they are not finished learning. That's why the river is closed. That's why there are survey teams instead of miners — because officially there is nothing here. That's why the villages burn." He looked at her across the fire. "You asked me once why a cartographer gets court-martialed over a map. Because the map was never the crime, Mirabel. The crime was the place. The place is worth more than the people on it, and everyone up the chain has done that arithmetic and signed the answer."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned to a red heart and Mirabel slept fitfully in the bow, Quentin sat alone with the fragment unwrapped in front of him.

He knew better than to touch it. He touched it anyway — one fingertip, briefly, the way a man tests a stove he's been warned about twice.

It was warm. Not hot — warm the way a living animal is warm, warm from the inside, and the hum traveled up through his fingerbone and settled somewhere behind his sternum, and for one disorienting moment Quentin felt the river. Not heard — *felt* — the whole weight of it moving past the hull, every current and undertow, the drowned log two fathoms down, the cold vein of snowmelt threading the warmer water. Then it passed, and he snatched his hand back, and his heart slammed against his ribs like a prisoner at a door.

*This is why people die,* he thought. Not because the metal is evil. Metal has no morals. People die because a thing like this makes ordinary men feel the way he felt in that single second — chosen, enlarged, certain — and certainty is the most dangerous cargo on any river.

He thought of Ashford then, and was surprised to find not hatred but a kind of horrified recognition. Ashford believed. That was the terrible thing about him. Somewhere in Whitehall's arithmetic, Ashford had convinced himself that owning the source would end wars faster than it started them, and men who believe that do not burn villages in anger. They burn them in sorrow, and then they file the report.

The fragment sat in the firelight, singing its one note, warping the night around it.

Quentin wrapped it again, carefully, and stowed it in the chart locker next to his father's compass — and noticed, with a small cold shock, that the compass needle had begun, very faintly, to tremble even through the locker's oak door.

He looked upstream, where the gorge walls had narrowed to a black V against the stars, and where — if the old readings held true — every compass they owned would soon be useless, and every mile would have to be read the way Mirabel read water: by eye, by ear, by the feel of the current against the hull.

Which was when he heard it.

Faint, far downstream, carried on the night air the way sound carries over water: the low diesel knock of a heavy-engined boat, running without lights, coming upriver fast.

Ashford hadn't found the decoy yet. Ashford was still behind them.

But not, Quentin understood, by much.
