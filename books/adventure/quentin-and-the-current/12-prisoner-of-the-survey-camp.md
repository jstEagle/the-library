## 12. Prisoner of the Survey Camp

They took her rifle first, then her boots, then her dignity, in roughly that order.

Mirabel came awake to canvas walls and lamplight and the taste of river mud still coating her teeth. The last thing she remembered was the weir — the exchange gone wrong in a way she hadn't yet had time to untangle, Ashford's men rising out of the willows like herons startled into violence, Quentin shouting her name as they dragged him one direction and her the other. Then a rifle butt across the shoulders, or maybe just hands; she'd have bruises either way. Now she sat on a folding camp stool inside a tent that smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the particular institutional cleanliness of the Royal Survey Service, which was to say: nothing about it smelled like the river at all.

Her wrists were bound with cord, not iron. Someone had wrapped a blanket around her shoulders. Both courtesies told her more than any interrogation would have. They wanted something from her, and whatever it was, it required her cooperative rather than broken.

The tent flap opened and Lieutenant Commander Ashford came in carrying two tin mugs and a leather folder under his arm.

He looked different out of his dress uniform — field kit, sleeves rolled, forearms brown from the sun. He looked, Mirabel thought with a kind of weary disgust, like a man on holiday. He set one mug within reach of her bound hands, tea by the smell of it, strong and over-steeped, and settled onto the camp cot across from her with the ease of someone visiting an old friend.

"You're not hurt," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Your men were gentle."

"They're under orders to be." He blew across his own mug. "I want you to understand something before we begin, Miss Underhill. I did not want this. Every escalation between us has been a door you opened, not one I chose."

"The checkpoint at Miller's Bend," she said. "The burned village. The man who shot Hollis through the chest while he floated downriver toward me begging for help for his daughter. Those doors?"

Ashford's face didn't move, but something behind it did — a flicker, quickly banked. "Hollis," he said softly. "So that's where the map came from."

She said nothing. Silence was a tool she'd spent ten years sharpening, down there in the green dark where talking got divers killed.

He set down his mug and picked up the leather folder, and everything in the tent changed.

It wasn't dramatic. He didn't open it, didn't offer it, didn't even look at it. He simply held it, the way a card player holds a hand he already knows wins, and let her look at it. Plain government leather, brass corner caps worn dull, a paper tag tied with string. On the tag, in faded clerk's ink: *UNDERHILL, T. — PERSONNEL & INCIDENT FILE.*

The name went through her like cold water through a wetsuit.

Thomas Underhill. Her father. Drowned — *presumed* drowned, the word the navy had always used, carefully, precisely, *presumed* — eleven years ago on a survey run upriver, when she was nineteen and still believed the Service was a family you joined rather than a machine that consumed its parts. She had written letters for two years afterward. Polite letters. Then less polite ones. Then one letter, the last, asking only for his effects, and received back a form letter signed by a clerk whose signature looked like a spider drowned in ink.

No effects. No file access. No body. *Presumed.*

"You've been looking for that folder your whole adult life," Ashford said quietly. "Don't bother denying it. I've read your service record too — yes, you had one, apprentice diver, sponsored by your father, terminated at your request after the incident. You were a good diver, Mirabel. Your father's daughter in every way that mattered to the water."

"What do you want for it?" Her voice came out flat. Too flat. She heard the effort in it and hated that he would hear it too.

"The map."

* * *

They talked for a long time, or rather he talked and she listened, because that was the shape the navy preferred for conversations. Outside the tent, the forward camp went about its business — the clank of a compressor, men's voices, the lap of the river against pilings. Somewhere downstream, Quentin was in another tent exactly like this one, and she made herself think about that, made herself hold both of them in mind at once, because Ashford wanted her thinking only about the folder. That was the trick of it. The whole architecture of the offer was built to narrow her world down to a single object and a single dead man.

"The source is real," Ashford said. "You know that now. You've seen what comes out of it — Hale showed you the fragment, I assume, or found it himself back when he was still mine. A metal that grows. That hums. That bends compasses and holds a charge no battery chemistry can match and weighs half what steel does. Do you understand what that means, in practical terms? It means the nation that controls the spring controls the next century. It means my survey teams — yes, the ones that burned your village, I won't insult you by pretending otherwise — are doing work that will be taught in academies long after everyone in this tent is dust."

"And the people in the way get dusted along with them."

"People get in the way of history constantly," Ashford said. "It's history's fault, not mine." He leaned forward. "Here is what I'm offering, plainly, because I find plainness disarms people better than threats. Give me the map — the true map, Hollis's original, with his annotations, because we believe it marks approaches to the source cavern that our own surveys missed. In exchange: the file. All of it. And safe passage downriver for you and Hale, with the incident closed. You go back to your salvage yard. You dive honest wrecks. You grow old. Everyone gets what they actually want."

"And Wren? Hollis's daughter? Does she get to grow old?"

Something crossed Ashford's face then that she couldn't read — not guilt, she thought, but calculation wearing guilt's clothing. "Wren Hollis is a complication," he said. "Complications can be resolved humanely or otherwise. That outcome is also negotiable."

There it was. The hook under the bait. She'd known it would be there; a man like Ashford never offered anything clean.

"I want to read it first," she said. "Before I decide anything. If I'm going to trade eleven years of my life for eleven years of paper, I'll know what the paper says."

Ashford studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, like a man approving a student's answer, and that condescension nearly made her spit at him.

"Read it here," he said. "I'll have the cord cut. And Mirabel — the file doesn't leave the tent, and neither do you. We'll speak again at dawn."

* * *

The young sailor who cut her bonds wouldn't meet her eyes. Good, she thought. He still had enough conscience left to be ashamed, which meant he was salvageable, which meant the Service hadn't gotten all of him yet. She rubbed her wrists and waited until the flap fell shut behind him, and then she sat very still for a full minute, listening to the camp, counting footsteps, learning the rhythm of the place the way she'd learn the rhythm of a current before committing to a dive.

Then she opened the folder.

The first pages were ordinary and almost unbearable for their ordinariness. Service forms. Medical evaluations. A photograph of her father at thirty, stiff-collared, squinting against some long-gone sun, looking so much like her that her throat closed. His handwriting filled the margins of his own evaluation reports — little jokes, little complaints, *this clerk cannot spell 'buoyancy'* scrawled beside a supervisor's note. She had forgotten his handwriting. Eleven years, and she had forgotten the specific slant of it, the way he pressed too hard on downstrokes, and now here it was alive again on cheap paper, and she had to put the folder down twice in the first ten pages just to breathe.

Then the incident reports began, and the ordinariness ended.

The official account was three paragraphs long. Survey vessel *Kestrel*, routine hydrographic run, sudden flood event in the upper gorge, vessel lost with all hands, search suspended after four days, remains unrecovered. *Presumed.* She knew those three paragraphs by heart; versions of them had been quoted back to her for a decade.

But the file was thick, and thick files hide things, and Thomas Underhill had been a thorough man who kept copies of things in places his superiors didn't know about. Tucked between the third and fourth carbon sheets was a letter in his own hand, unsent, folded small, addressed to no one — or perhaps to whoever eventually dug this deep. To her, maybe. Fathers are allowed to hope like that.

*Mirabel — if you're reading this then either the Service has finally grown a conscience or you've grown stubborn enough to dig one out of them. Either way: listen.*

*The spring is real. What grows there isn't ore, it's something older and stranger and it SINGS, Mira, I swear to God it sings in the water, and Commander Voss wants it dredged and shipped south before anyone outside the Service knows it exists. I refused to sign the second survey. I told him the cavern structure won't take the blasting. He told me patriotism isn't a menu. There were six of us who objected. As of this writing two have been transferred somewhere I can't trace and one has resigned and gone quiet in a way that frightens me.*

*If anything happens to me it will be called a flood. Remember that word. When they say flood, ask what the water level was. When they say accident, ask why the survey boats were recalled the day before. I loved the river my whole life and I am not afraid of it. I am afraid of the men who think it belongs to them.*

*Find the spring before they strip it. Or seal it. Whatever you choose, choose it with your eyes open. And forgive me for leaving you the kind of inheritance that comes with enemies attached.*

*— Da*

She read it four times. By the fourth reading she could recite it, and by the fifth she noticed what grief had hidden on the first pass: the date. The letter was dated nine days before the *Kestrel* was lost. Nine days in which her father had known exactly how much danger he stood in, had written it down, had hidden it in a file he must have known the Service would seal — and had gone upriver anyway.

Not because he was ordered. Because he chose. Because sealing the spring or exposing it mattered more than coming home, and he had trusted her — arrogant, beloved, impossible man — to understand that someday.

The rest of the file confirmed the letter in the Service's own bloodless language. Internal memoranda noting "personnel irregularities" aboard the *Kestrel*. A requisition order for demolition charges signed out two days before the "flood event" and never returned to stores. A memo from Voss — Ashford's predecessor, Ashford's teacher, she realized, the whole rotten lineage of it — recommending the incident be classified and the surviving families given "standard closure correspondence without further engagement." Standard closure correspondence. The spider-drowned-ink signature. The form letter. Eleven years of her life, filed under *without further engagement.*

She sat in the lamplight with her father's last words in her lap and felt the temptation Ashford had built for her, and it was a real temptation, that was the hell of it. Here it all was. The truth, certified, stamped, complete. Take the trade, walk away, live. She could spend the rest of her life with these pages. She could sue, publish, grieve properly at last with facts instead of ghosts. The official truth was sitting right here, purchasable, and it was worth more than she had ever expected to own.

And underneath it, quiet as a current under ice, ran the other truth: the file was a leash. Every page of it was Ashford's property, issued at his pleasure, revocable at his pleasure. A truth you rented was not the truth. And the price tag hanging off it was a map that would lead his dredges straight into the singing dark, and a girl named Wren marked down as a *complication* to be resolved.

Her father had chosen the river over his own life. He had not done it so that his daughter could trade his killer a map for a folder full of paper confessions.

Mirabel folded the letter along its old creases, careful, reverent, and slid it into the waistband of her shirt, flat against her skin, where the lamplight and the sailors and the Lieutenant Commander of His Majesty's Survey Service could not see it. The rest of the file she closed and squared and set on the cot, neat as a clerk.

Then she looked around the tent — really looked, the way she looked at a wreck before committing to penetration. Canvas walls. Lamp. The compressor's rhythm outside, changing shift. The river close enough to hear, saying the only honest thing in the camp: *come down, come down, come down.*

She was Mirabel Underhill, salvage diver, and she had spent her whole life going into flooded places other people called impassable.

Somewhere near the camp's edge, she'd bet her father's compass, there was a mine adit. Old workings, abandoned, the gorge was riddled with them — Hollis's map had shown three. And a flooded shaft, she knew from long experience, was just a wreck you entered vertically.

At dawn, Ashford would come for his answer.

She intended to be gone before the lamp burned down.
