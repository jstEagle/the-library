## 13. The Long Swim

They took her boots first.

That was the mistake — or she chose to believe it was, because believing it was a mistake was easier than believing Ashford was careless, and Ashford was not a careless man. They took her boots, her knife, the brass clasp from her hair, and they put her in a tent at the edge of the survey camp with a canvas cot, a blanket that smelled of creosote, and a guard who yawned every ninety seconds like clockwork. They left her the file. That was the cruelty of it. Ashford had understood something about her in that long hour across the table: that grief could be used as a leash as surely as iron. He had given her her father's file and let her read it by lamplight, page after page of navy shorthand and redaction bars, and then he had left her alone with it, certain she would still be sitting there when morning came.

He was nearly right.

Mirabel sat on the cot with the file open on her knees until the lamp burned low, and she read the last page again — the one with the date, the one with the phrase *recovered effects: compass, dive log (partial), one boot* — and she thought about how a man could be reduced to a single boot in an official record, and how her mother had waited eleven years for even that much and never received it. The paper trembled very slightly in her hands. She let it. There was no one to see, and a body ought to be allowed its honesty somewhere.

Then she closed the file, squared its edges, and set it on the cot exactly where a woman would leave it if she intended to come back for it.

Because here was what eleven years of salvage diving had taught her that no file could hold: every wreck has a way out. Every sealed hull, every collapsed deck, every tomb the river builds around the drowned — there is always a gap, a seam, a place where the water remembers it used to be free. You only had to stop looking at the thing the way it wanted to be looked at. A camp was just a wreck on land. And this particular wreck had been built on top of an old mine.

She'd heard it in the guards' talk that evening, half-bored complaints carried on the wind off the water: the pump house running all night, the sump below the survey shed flooding again, the old workings under the ridge filling up since the spring operations raised the waterline. An adit — a mine entrance — cut into the hillside behind the camp, boarded over years ago, drowned to the roof now. The navy had posted no sentry on it. Why would they? No man could swim a flooded shaft in the dark. The water in those workings would be snowmelt-cold, black as ink, full of rotting timber and dead air pockets and rusted rail lines, and it would go back into the hill nobody knew how far.

No man could. But they hadn't counted on her, because men never did, and that had been the whole quiet engine of her life.

She lay down and listened to the camp breathe. Generator hum. Boots on gravel, changing the watch. The river beyond the wire, talking to itself in the dark the way it always did, indifferent, patient, keeping its appointments. Somewhere in the compound Quentin Hale was locked in the chart room, and she made herself think about that now, deliberately, the way you press on a bruise to know how bad it is.

That was the ugly part. She had built the whole plan around him being taken, and he had agreed to it, and his agreeing did not make it clean.

*They'll watch me,* he'd said, in the two minutes they'd been allowed standing together when the patrol brought them in, both of them wet and bound at the wrists, performing defeat. His voice had been flat, cartographer's voice, reading coordinates off a map only she could see. *A disgraced mapmaker who led them upstream? I'm the prize they can show their superiors. You're just the diver who found the map. They'll hold me close and hold you loose.* His eyes had flicked once, briefly, toward the ridge behind the camp. Toward the dark seam of the old adit he'd marked on a chart eleven years ago and never forgotten, because forgetting was the one thing Quentin Hale could not do. *The workings run under the whole ridge. My old survey shows a ventilation shaft coming up past the tree line.*

*You'll be bait,* she had said. Not a question. Making him say it too.

*I'll be useful,* he'd said. *There's a difference, and I intend to survive long enough to argue it.* And then the guards had pulled them apart, and he had said, loud enough for the guards, bitter enough to be believed: *This is what comes of trusting salvagers,* and she had hated him a little for how well he played it, and hated herself more for needing him to.

Now, in the dark of the tent, she pressed the bruise again. *I'm using him.* The thought sat in her chest like swallowed stone. He was drunk-voiced and brilliant and afraid — she had seen the fear under the irony since the first night in her yard, the fear of a man whose country had erased him — and she was going to leave him in a navy cage while she swam into a flooded hill. If Ashford decided the map wasn't worth the trouble of a prisoner, if some junior officer with a rifle and a grudge got impatient —

No. She turned the thought over and set it down. Quentin had chosen. He'd said so, and she had watched him say it, and she owed him the respect of believing he meant it. What she did not owe him was dying beside him in a camp that had a door only she could use. Two prisoners rescued nothing. One prisoner loose might yet rescue everything — Wren, the truth, and, God willing, a hungover cartographer with a talent for surviving his own decisions.

The guard yawned. Ninety seconds. She began counting.

* * *

Getting out of the tent was almost insultingly easy, which told her how thoroughly Ashford believed the file had leashed her. She waited for the watch change, the overlap when two sets of boots talked gravel instead of one, slipped the tent's rear stake — loosened hours earlier with a sliver of tin she'd worked free from the cot frame, working blind, working slow, the most patient three hours of her life — and went out into the cold on her belly like smoke.

The camp lay in the bowl of the valley under a fat, clouded moon, lanterns burning yellow at the wire and the dock. The gunboat sat at its mooring downstream, a black shape with a blacker shadow, and she gave the water a wide berth. Behind the survey shed, the hillside rose steep and pine-dark, and at its foot, where the ground wept constant seepage, stood the adit: rough-timbered mouth, boards nailed across it, a navy stencil faded on the crossbeam — *SURVEY SHAFT 4 — CONDEMNED.*

The boards came away with a pry of her shoulder, one nail singing faintly against another, and she froze in the noise's wake and counted thirty heartbeats and heard nothing but the pump house chugging its idiot rhythm. Then she was inside, and the dark closed over her like water.

The tunnel ran level for twenty feet, ankle-deep in icy runoff, and then the floor dropped away and the water rose — knee, waist, chest — and then there was no floor at all and she was swimming, submerged, into the throat of the hill.

Cold hit her like a blow. Not river cold; *mine* cold, the still preserved cold of water that had sat in the dark for years, and it clenched her chest and tried to make her gasp, and she rode it down the way she'd learned at ten fathoms and fifty: don't fight the shock, breathe through it, let the body scream and answer it with arithmetic. Stroke. Stroke. Stroke. Her hand found the guide rope someone had rigged along the left wall decades ago, hemp gone soft as cloth, and she followed it down into absolute black.

She had planned this part in her head a hundred times since the guards' conversation, and the plan was simple and terrible: swim the flooded section on one breath, find the first air pocket, breathe, repeat. Old mines were honeycombed with dead ends and rise-shafts, and where rock dipped and trapped air against a ceiling, a swimmer could live. Where it didn't, she drowned, and that was the entire contract. She had signed contracts like that before, with the river, with wrecks, with the sea itself. This was only the same signature written in colder ink.

Her shoulder struck timber. She stopped, explored with her fingers: a rotted prop, angled, its base furred with white mold. Beyond it the tunnel bent. She bent with it, and her head broke surface into a pocket of air that tasted of iron and old stone and was, nevertheless, the sweetest breath of her life.

She hung in the black water, treading, and breathed, and in the breathing the thoughts she'd held off came flooding in with the air.

Quentin's face when the guards dragged him toward the chart room — not defeated at all, she realized now, but *calculating*, already measuring the room, already the cartographer of his own captivity. *I'll be useful.* She clung to that. She also let herself feel the other thing, the true thing: that she had looked at a friend in handcuffs and seen a diversion, and part of her had thought *good*, and that part was her father's daughter, the part that survived, and she didn't know whether to bless it or drown it.

*Come get me out of this,* he'd murmured, right before they pulled them apart, so quietly it might have been imagination. *I've read enough charts to know you always find the deep channel.*

"Deep channel," she whispered into the dark, and the dark ate the words, and she dove.

Three more flooded runs. Three more air pockets, each smaller, each fouler. Once her hand plunged into something soft and yielding that moved, and she made herself not think about it, made herself count strokes instead, because thinking about it meant surfacing into panic and panic in a flooded mine was just drowning with extra steps. The rope ended. She followed the rail line instead, fingers on rusted iron, and where the rails forked she took the branch that trended upward, feeling the slope in the water's pressure, in the ache of her ears, in the way the cold seemed to thin.

Then the pocket above her was different — moving air, tasting of pine and rain — and she rose into it and found not a dead pocket but a shaft, vertical, rough-walled, and far above it a coin of grey sky with the silhouette of branches crossing it.

The ventilation shaft. Quentin's chart, eleven years old, true as scripture.

She climbed. The walls were slick, the holds rotten, and twice a foothold crumbled and dropped her back a body-length with her heart slamming, but she was Mirabel Underhill, and she had been climbing out of impossible places since before she could write, and she climbed. When her hand finally broke the surface crust of leaves at the top and closed around a living root, a root with bark and moss and *sun-warmth* in it, she nearly laughed, and what came out instead was a sob, one, quickly strangled, spent like a coin she couldn't afford.

She came up out of the hole into the grey before-dawn light among the pines above the treeline, and lay on the wet needles shaking, and let herself shake, because there was no one to see and a body ought to be allowed its honesty somewhere.

Then she got up, because Wren was somewhere ahead of her on this mountain, and promises do not wait for the shaking to stop.

* * *

From the ridgeline she looked back once.

The camp lay below in the blue dawn haze, small as a model, smoke rising straight from the mess fire. At the chart room, figures moved — two, three, a fourth carrying something flat. A stretcher? No. A table. They were carrying a table out onto the dock, and around it other figures gathered, and even at this distance she could read the posture of them: the leaning-in of men attending an examination.

They were interrogating him. Or preparing to. And every eye in that camp would be on Quentin Hale and whatever they thought he knew, because he had made himself the story, had walked into their hands radiating importance, had spent himself as coin so that she could spend this night underwater.

*Don't give them anything good,* she whispered, *and don't give them anything bad. Be boring. Be worthless. Be the drunk they remember.* As if he could hear her. As if the wind up here carried words down instead of only carrying hers away.

She thought about going back. The thought was honest and enormous and she examined it fully, turning it in the light like a recovered artifact: go down, surrender, trade herself for him. It would be clean. It would cost the promise — Wren, alone on this mountain with hunters behind her; the truth, sealed forever in Ashford's ledgers; her father's eleven unanswered years, buried deeper than the file could dig them. Everything bought with tonight's ugliness, forfeited for the comfort of a clean conscience.

Quentin would tell her not to. Quentin would make a joke of it, some dry line about sunk costs and bad charts, and mean every word.

"I'm coming back for you," she said aloud, to the camp, to the man in it, to the river beyond it that kept all accounts. "You hear me? I'm coming back."

The wind took it. The camp went on smoking its little fires. She turned uphill.

The ground rose steeply through the pines, and she moved the way her father had taught her to move through wreck interiors — slow, deliberate, leaving nothing disturbed — though there was no one behind her yet. There would be. By midday Ashford would know she was gone, and by afternoon he would have men on this ridge, and somewhere between here and the source canyon, Wren was hiding with her father's letters and a hardened heart and, if the prospector's coded marks meant what Mirabel thought they meant, a trail only Mirabel could follow.

She found the first mark an hour after sunrise, scratched into bark at eye height beside a game trail: three short strokes and a hook, weathered weeks old, half-healed with sap.

Wren's father's code. The same system the dying man had scratched into the margin of his map — *upstream, high ground, trust the marks, not the river* — the language fathers teach daughters when they suspect the world will come hunting. Mirabel knelt and touched the cuts gently, the way she touched things recovered from wrecks, things that had waited a long time for someone who could read them.

"He sent me," she told the mark, and the mountain silence took it without comment.

Above the treeline the world opened — scree and stunted pine and a sky going hard and clear — and somewhere up there, in the rocks, a girl was watching her approach through a rifle scope or a gap in the stones, deciding whether the woman climbing toward her was rescue or wolf. Mirabel straightened, showed her empty hands, and climbed.

Behind her, far below, the camp was waking. Ahead of her, the marks went on. And in her chest, beside the stone of what she'd done to Quentin, a new thing had kindled — small, hard, banked like a coal carried in a wind: the certainty that she would stand in front of Ashford again, and that next time she would not be the one in borrowed boots.
