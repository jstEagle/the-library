## 14. Wren Found

Wren had learned to count her days in birdsong.

The thrushes started first, an hour before the light came over the eastern ridge, and she had trained herself to wake with them — not because she feared being caught asleep, exactly, but because a person who slept past the birds was a person who had stopped listening, and listening was the only thing keeping her alive up here. Above the treeline the world was stripped down to its essentials: stone, wind, snowmelt, sky. No trees to shelter behind. No smoke to hide. Just granite shoulders rising out of the river valley like the vertebrae of something enormous that had lain down to die a long time ago and was still, somehow, breathing.

She crouched at the mouth of the cave she'd found on her third day above the pines — a shallow throat of rock behind a fallen slab, invisible from below unless you knew to look for the seep of water staining the stone dark. She'd known to look. Her father had taught her that too, along with everything else: how to read water stains, how to cache food against bears, how to walk on scree without leaving a print worth following.

How to leave marks only family could read.

The letters were wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, against her chest, where they'd lived for six weeks now. Eleven of them. She had read them so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth at the folds, and she could recite most of them from memory anyway, but she kept them there all the same, because a letter against your heartbeat is different from a letter in your head. It was proof. He had written *I will come for you when the work is done* eleven times in eleven different ways, and every one of those sentences was a lie now, or worse than a lie — a promise, which was the same thing as a lie except it cost someone more when it broke.

Below her, far down where the valley floor ran silver and loud, the river went about its business. And somewhere down there — she'd seen the smoke two mornings ago, a thread of it rising from the narrows where no honest traveler would camp — someone was coming up.

She'd been watching the trail since dawn. Two figures, small as beetles at this distance, working their way along the canyon's lower shelf. One moved like a man who knew maps — pausing, checking, correcting. The other moved like nobody Wren had ever seen move before: low, economical, reading the water as much as the ground, head turning toward the river every few minutes the way other people checked the sun.

A diver. She'd guessed that much by the second hour. You didn't look at moving water like that unless you'd been under it, unless you'd learned its moods from the inside.

Wren shouldered her pack and began to climb.

Not away. That was the trick of it, the thing her father had drilled into her until it lived in her bones: when someone tracks you, don't run *from* them, run *above* them. High ground was information. High ground was choice. From the ridge saddle she could watch the whole approach, see whether they traveled alone, see whether they stopped at her last mark — the three-stacked stones with the split twig laid across, the one that said *danger, turn back* in the language her father had invented for just the two of them, one winter, bored, snowed in, never dreaming she'd need it.

They reached the mark near midday.

Wren watched through the spyglass her father had traded a rifle for — his judgment, always, that knowing things beat shooting things — and felt her chest go tight around the letters. The tall one, the map-man, crouched at the cairn and turned the split twig over in his fingers with the care of a priest handling relics. Then he looked up. Not around. *Up*, straight at the ridge line, straight at the saddle where she sat, and even at half a mile she saw him say something to the woman beside him, and the woman looked up too, and did not search, and did not scan.

She just looked, the way you look at a door you already know is open.

*Come down,* that look said. *We know the marks. We were sent.*

Wren stayed on the mountain until dusk anyway. She owed them nothing, whoever they were, and she had learned — the hard way, the way you learn things when the tuition is your whole family — that people who came bearing promises were the most dangerous kind of weather.

* * *

Her father had made her one promise too many, and it had begun, as these things do, with a map.

He'd drawn it himself, the summer she turned fourteen, on the back of a flour sack, sitting cross-legged on the porch of their cabin while she mended nets. *If anything ever happens to me,* he'd said, not looking up, casual as a man talking about rain, *you take the north trail to the burned pine, and from there you follow the marks.* And he'd shown her: the stacked stones, the bent branches, the charcoal arrows hidden under lichen where only a kneeling searcher would find them. A trail of family secrets running upstream into country the navy had closed, marked on no chart, leading to a place he named only as *the spring*.

She'd laughed at him. She remembered that, and it still shamed her — the bright, dismissive laugh of a girl who thought her father was telling stories, who thought promises were the sort of thing you made in warm kitchens and kept without ever being tested.

*Humor me, Wrennie.*

So she'd humored him. She'd memorized the trail. And then in the autumn the navy men had come to the cabin with their questions and their clean boots, asking where he'd gone, when he'd be back, whether he'd left papers, and she had lied with her father's own calm face and told them nothing, because he'd taught her that too, though he'd never once said it aloud.

Six weeks ago she'd climbed the north trail for real. The marks were still there. He'd maintained them, she realized — walked the route within the last season, refreshing the bent twigs, restacking the stones — which meant he'd meant to come back this way. Meant to bring her. The trail ended at a ledge above a side-canyon, at a cache box containing food, a knife, a coil of rope, and a note in his blunt carpenter's hand:

*Gone to the spring to finish it. If I'm not back in ten days, don't follow. Go downstream. Find the salvage yard at Miller's Bend. Ask for Underhill.*

That was the first time Wren had ever heard the name. She'd said it to herself a hundred times since, walking the high country, eating cold beans, hating it. *Underhill.* A stranger. A name her father had trusted more than his own promise to come home.

And now, apparently, Underhill was climbing her father's secret trail, reading his secret marks, coming to collect whatever it was he'd promised a dying man.

Because that was the other thing Wren had pieced together, from the map-man's careful questions shouted up at the empty hillside that evening — polite questions, respectful ones, *we're not with the navy, we're looking for a girl whose father sent us* — from the way the woman had finally simply sat down on a boulder and waited, patient as stone, as if she understood exactly what Wren was doing up there and why. Someone had died. Someone had died holding a map, and had bought a stranger's word with it, and now the stranger had come to pay.

Promises. They got everywhere. They killed men and orphaned daughters and sent divers climbing into the clouds after girls who wanted nothing in the world but to be left alone with their grief and their grudge.

Wren decided, lying in the cave with the wind worrying the rock overhead, that she would let them find her in the morning. Not because she trusted them. Because she was nearly out of food, because winter came early up here, and because — she admitted it only in the dark, only to herself — because some small traitorous part of her had been waiting six weeks for someone to say that name out loud, and she needed to hear what they'd say next.

* * *

They came up at first light, and Wren watched them climb, and by the time they crested the saddle she had positioned herself on the high rock above the trail with her father's old sling-shot loaded and her voice steady, which took more work than the sling-shot.

"That's far enough."

The woman stopped instantly, hands loose and visible, and looked up, and Wren got her first true look at her: mid-thirties maybe, wind-burnt, dark hair cut short for practicality rather than style, eyes the flat grey-green of river water over slate. She carried herself like the river too — coiled, watchful, capable of sudden violence but mostly interested in going around obstacles rather than through them.

"You're Wren," the woman said. Not a question.

"Who's asking?"

"Mirabel Underhill." A pause, and Wren watched her weigh something and decide to spend it. "Your father sent me."

"My father," Wren said, "is dead." She heard her own voice crack on the word and hated it, hated the weakness, hated this whole arrangement, hated the morning sun warming her back while her hands shook on the sling. "Dead men don't send anybody. Dead men get dug up by strangers who want what they were carrying."

The map-man flinched. The woman didn't.

"You're right," Mirabel Underhill said. "He didn't send you anything. He was dying when I met him, shot through, half-drowned in my own salvage slip. He gave me a map and I gave him a word, and I've spent three weeks learning exactly how expensive that word was going to be." She took a step forward, slow, deliberate, palms still open. "I'm not here to take anything from you, Wren. I'm here because a man died in my yard holding your name in his mouth, and I told him I'd find you, and I keep my promises even the stupid ones. Especially the stupid ones."

"And then what?" Wren's voice came out harder than she intended. "You find me, you hand me over, you collect whatever's coming to you, and you go back to your honest little wreck-diving life? Is that the shape of it?"

Something moved across the woman's face — not quite pain, but its neighbor. "There isn't an honest little life to go back to. The navy burned my yard yesterday. Or the day before. I've lost track." She glanced at the map-man. "Quentin Hale. He used to chart for them. Now they'd shoot him on sight. We're both of us well past collecting anything."

Wren looked at them for a long moment. The wind came off the glacier field and pushed at her back like a hand between the shoulder blades, urging her downhill, urging her onward, and she resisted it on principle, the way she'd resisted everything since the day the navy boots stood on her father's porch.

"They killed him," she said. "Didn't they. He didn't die in some accident. He went to the spring to *finish it* — that's what his note said, finish it — and they killed him for whatever finishing meant."

Mirabel Underhill was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, rougher, the voice of someone speaking from inside a wound of her own. "My father disappeared on that same river ten years ago. I spent a decade thinking it was an accident. Last week a smuggler queen told me he died at the source, protecting the spring, same as yours. So no, Wren. I'm not here to collect. I'm here because the river took both our fathers and lied to us about it, and I intend to make it give them back."

Above the treeline, sound carried strangely. Wren heard the words land and echo off the rock, and heard, beneath them, the distant constant voice of the river itself, and for one unguarded moment she let herself imagine her father at thirty, at forty, standing on some ledge like this one, saying almost those exact words to someone almost exactly like this woman.

Then she hardened again, because hardening was the skill she had, the inheritance that mattered.

"Show me the map," she said. "His map. The one he gave you."

Mirabel hesitated — a flicker, quickly mastered, but Wren caught it, and caught what it meant. There was something wrong with the map, or something special, or both. Interesting. She filed it away.

"I can't show it to you," Mirabel said carefully. "But I copied it. Every line." She drew a folded paper from inside her jacket and held it up.

"Throw it."

She threw it well, an easy underhand lob that Wren caught one-handed without shifting her stance. On the paper, in a firm draftsman's hand, was her father's map — the flour-sack map, the one he'd redrawn before he left, the one with the spring marked at its heart inside a circle stamped with an insignia Wren knew better than her own name: the coiled serpent-and-anchor of the river source, the mark that had been branded into her nightmares since childhood.

At the bottom corner, in her father's handwriting, were four words she had never seen before:

*For Wren. Finish it.*

The paper trembled in her grip. Six weeks of cold and hunger and grief stood up inside her all at once, and Wren had to lock her knees to stay upright on the rock, and she knew — she knew with terrible clarity — that these two strangers below her had just handed her the only weapon that mattered.

Not the map. The words. *Finish it.* Her father hadn't gone to the spring to hide, or to steal, or to survive. He'd gone to end something. And he'd written her name on the ending, which meant it wasn't Mirabel Underhill's promise anymore, and it wasn't Quentin Hale's charts, and it wasn't the navy's secret.

It was hers.

Wren climbed down. She slung the shot-pouch over her shoulder as a gesture of peace she wasn't sure she felt, and she crossed the scree to stand in front of Mirabel Underhill, close enough to see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the salt lines on her jacket, the bruised knuckles of a working diver's hands.

"I'm not coming with you because I trust you," Wren said. "Understand that. I'm coming because you knew my father for one afternoon and I knew him for nineteen years, and if anyone finishes what he started, it's going to be me. You can walk ahead. You can carry things. But the promise you made him—" she held up the copied map, folded it, and put it deliberately into her own coat, next to the letters, next to her heartbeat— "that belongs to me now."

For a long moment Mirabel said nothing. Then, slowly, she smiled — a tired, crooked, genuine smile, the first Wren had seen on her — and something in it made Wren understand, suddenly and uncomfortably, why a dying man had chosen this woman to hold his last word.

"He'd have liked you," Mirabel said. "You argue exactly like him."

Behind her, Quentin Hale cleared his throat and pointed east, down-valley, where the morning haze was lifting off the canyon.

Down at the narrows, where no honest boat had any business being, a grey gunboat was nosing upstream against the current — navy grey, gun-grey, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that already knew where it was going.

Mirabel followed his gaze, and Wren watched the tired smile die on her face.

"Well," Mirabel said quietly. "Ashford found the decoy."
