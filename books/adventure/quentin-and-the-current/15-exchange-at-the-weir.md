## 15. Exchange at the Weir

They came for him at dawn, the way the navy did everything — punctually, and with too many men.

Quentin heard the gunboat before he saw it, the low diesel knock carrying up the gorge like a fist knocking on a door that had no business being knocked on. He was sitting on the deck of the *Magpie* with a tin cup of river-boiled tea going cold in his hands, watching mist peel off the water, and for one long moment he let himself pretend the sound was something else. A mill upstream. A fallen log rolling over rapids. Anything but the flat, mechanical patience of His Majesty's Navy coming to collect a debt.

"Quentin." Mirabel's voice from below, quiet, already awake. She'd slept in her diving coat with her boots on. He suspected she'd done more sleeping in boots than out of them since the day a dying man had washed into her salvage yard.

"I hear it," he said.

Wren was already at the rail, a small shape in an oversized oilskin, her father's letters bound against her chest under the coat like a second heartbeat. She said nothing. She had learned, somewhere above the treeline in whatever weeks she'd survived alone, that silence could be a weapon, and she carried it the way other girls carried knives.

The gunboat rounded the bend below the weir and Quentin's stomach performed its old familiar maneuver — the one it had learned at the court-martial, the one that felt like being charted by someone else's hand. Ashford stood at the bow. Of course he did. The man never sent subordinates to do what his own presence could make worse.

* * *

They met on the shingle beach below the old salmon weir, because Ashford insisted on neutral ground and because the weir itself — that crumbling lattice of timber stakes driven into the riverbed a century ago by men who'd wanted to catch what the current brought them — seemed, to Quentin, almost comically apt. A trap built to hold what the water carried downstream. Today the water carried him.

Four marines flanked Ashford, rifles slung but hands near. Mirabel stood opposite with Wren behind her and the map case under her arm, and Quentin stood between them all like a coin about to change purses.

"Lieutenant Commander," Mirabel said. Her voice was level. Quentin had spent three weeks learning her voices — the one for clients, the one for the river, the one for grief at three in the morning — and this was none of them. This was a new one, forged recently, and it frightened him a little.

"Miss Underhill." Ashford removed his cap, which was either courtesy or theater. "You've had time to consider my offer."

"You've had time to consider mine. The map for Hale. Unharmed. Unfollowed."

"One of those conditions is within my power." Ashford's gaze slid to Quentin, and there was something in it that wasn't contempt — that was worse. Recognition. Pity, even. "Hale. You look terrible."

"I've been assured it's the company."

Something twitched at the corner of Ashford's mouth. Ten years ago they'd shared a wardroom, shared charts, shared a bottle of bad sherry the night Quentin finished the survey that ended his career. There had been a version of Ashford then who laughed easily. Quentin wondered if that man was still in there, or if the source-metal had gotten into him too — warping his compass, bending everything true around him into something that pointed where the Admiralty wanted.

"The map first," Ashford said.

Mirabel didn't move. "Hale walks to me first."

"Miss Underhill—"

"You're standing on my river, Commander. My father bled into it before you ever posted your little signs. We do this in the order I say, or we don't do it, and you can spend another week dragging the gorge while your quarry climbs the canyon without you." She let that land. "You know they're ahead of you. Your forgery detection is good, but your boats are slow, and your divers are afraid of white water. I've seen them work."

Ashford studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded once, and two marines stepped aside, and Quentin walked.

It was perhaps forty yards of wet shingle, and it was the longest walk of his life, because every step of it he waited for the rifle butt, the net, the change of mind. He thought absurdly of his burned charts — the ash of ten years' work still drifting somewhere downriver — and of Mirabel's face when she'd told him what she meant to do, and of the strange arithmetic of loyalty: that it could be spent like currency by people who'd only just learned to trust each other.

He reached her side. She didn't look at him. That was discipline, not coldness; he understood that now. Any look between them would have been a confession, and Ashford read confessions the way Quentin read coastlines.

"The map," Ashford said.

Mirabel held out the case. A marine took it, checked it, passed it up. Ashford opened it right there on the beach, wind worrying the paper, and Quentin watched him read the hand-drawn contours of the upper gorge — watched him believe them, because they were beautiful, because they were *plausible*, because Mirabel Underhill had spent three nights redrawing them by lamplight with Quentin dictating from memory and Wren correcting the depths her father had taught her, and because a lie built by three honest people is sturdier than most truths.

"Depth soundings match the original survey," Ashford murmured, half to himself. "The approach channel. The—" He stopped. Looked up. And for one horrible instant Quentin saw the flaw in all of it: that Ashford had been a cartographer's colleague once, and cartographers learn to smell forged water the way horses smell wolves.

Then Ashford closed the case. "A bargain observed," he said. "For today."

"What does that mean?" Wren asked. Her first words of the morning, and they cracked across the beach like a stone skipped hard.

Ashford looked at her — really looked, and Quentin saw something flicker there, some ledger entry being made — and said, "It means, child, that maps are promises, and promises are only as good as the men who keep them. Go home. All of you. The river above the gorge is closed by order of the Admiralty, and I have drowned better swimmers than you for less."

He turned. The marines folded around him. The gunboat's engine rose in pitch, and the wake came ashore in small insulting waves as the vessel swung back downriver toward the camp at the burned village.

Nobody spoke until the sound of it was gone.

* * *

Back aboard the *Magpie*, Mirabel finally let herself shake.

Quentin found her in the wheelhouse with both hands flat on the chart table, head bowed, breathing the way divers breathe on the bottom — slow, deliberate, counting. He poured tea he didn't drink and set it near her hand and waited, because he had learned that about her too: she came up from deep places on her own schedule.

"He believed it," she said at last. Or asked it.

"He believed it enough. Which is the same thing for about a day."

"A day." She straightened. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely ferocious. "A day is the canyon. If we run the narrows tonight and climb at first light—"

"It's also a noose," Quentin said quietly. "Ashford isn't stupid, Mirabel. He'll find the error. Maybe tonight, maybe tomorrow when his divers report the approach channel runs four fathoms shallower than your ink says. And when he finds it, he won't be angry. He'll be *certain*. Certain men don't negotiate. They come up that canyon with everything he has."

"Then we go now." She was already moving, hauling the cover off the engine hatch. "Wren! Get the lines in!"

Wren appeared in the wheelhouse doorway, and Quentin saw the girl look from Mirabel to him and back, doing the same arithmetic they were all doing, and he saw something else — the moment she decided. Not to follow. To *choose*. It happened in her face the way weather happens over water.

"My father drew that map," Wren said. "The real one. He died holding it. And you gave Ashford a lie drawn on top of his bones." Her chin came up. "Good."

She went forward to the lines. Mirabel exhaled something that might have been a laugh or a prayer.

"She's going to be trouble," Quentin said.

"She's going to be *fine*," Mirabel said. "Trouble's the family trade."

* * *

They ran the lower narrows in the last of the light, and Quentin worked the depth pole in the bow with his sleeves frozen to his elbows, calling the channel he had once charted and been ruined for charting, and there was a black joy in it he hadn't felt since the academy — using the knowledge that had cost him everything as a weapon instead of a wound.

Twice the hull kissed rock. Once Mirabel swore with a fluency that would have embarrassed a dockside chaplain. But the *Magpie* was a patched, stubborn, ugly little boat, and so were her crew, and they came through the narrows into the long pool below the source canyon as the stars came out, hard and cold and indifferent.

They beached in a fold of the bank where the alders grew thick, and Mirabel killed the engine, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Quentin sat on the shingle with his back against a boulder and tried to feel safe and failed, because safety had left his vocabulary a decade ago. Mirabel crouched beside him, checking the demolition charges one last time — the canvas satchel of them, salvaged from her own blasting shed, that they'd hauled two hundred miles of river on the strength of a promise and a dead man's map.

"If this works," he said, "if you bring that cavern down — the metal stays buried. The secret dies. Ashford's whole career dies with it. He knows that. He'll come up this canyon knowing exactly what you intend, and he will not stop at the waterline this time."

"I know."

"He offered you your father's file. He'll offer you worse."

"He offered me a *version*," Mirabel said. "My father's death with the edges filed off, stamped and archived, official. I've spent ten years wanting those pages, Quentin. Do you know what I learned? I don't want the file. I want the truth. They're not the same document." She looked up the canyon, where the walls went up and up into darkness and the river's voice changed pitch, deeper, older, like something clearing its throat underground. "Tomorrow I dive the spring. Tomorrow I finish what he started."

"And after?"

She was quiet a moment. "After," she said, "I promised a dying man I'd find his daughter. I found her. But a promise isn't finished when you find someone, Quentin. It's finished when they're *safe*. So after — wherever the current puts us — I suppose I keep her safe. All three of us. Some kind of salvage outfit. Honest wrecks." A ghost of a smile. "Charts optional."

"God spare me," Quentin said, and meant: *yes.*

Above them, far up the canyon wall, a light blinked. Once. Twice. Deliberate.

Wren saw it first. She caught Mirabel's arm without a word, and they all three went flat among the alders, and Quentin's heart began the old drumming as the light repeated its patient signal — not navy pattern, not any signal code he knew.

But Wren knew it. He heard her breath catch.

"That's my father's mark," she whispered. "Three-and-one. The prospector's call. Someone's up there, signaling *all clear* — or asking *who comes*." She turned to Mirabel, and in the starlight her face was fierce and young and terrified. "There's someone in the canyon who knew my father. And Ashford's gunboats are already turning around."

Downriver, faint but unmistakable, the diesel knock resumed — faster now, urgent, coming *upstream*.

The day was spent. The night had begun.
