## 16. The Last Mile of River

The decoy bought them a day. It cost them everything else.

Mirabel felt the change in the air before she understood it — a pressure behind her ears like the moment before a dive, when the water takes hold of you and decides what it will do with your body. They had left the weir at first light, Quentin limping on a knee that hadn't forgiven the gunboat's deck, Wren moving ahead of them both with the silent economy of someone who had lived alone too long to remember how to walk beside people.

By noon they stood at the mouth of the source canyon, and Mirabel stopped pretending it was anything but what it was: a throat.

The river poured into it from above in a series of white steps, and the walls rose sheer on either side — grey granite seamed with quartz that caught the light and threw it back cold. There was no path. There was barely a ledge. What passed for one was a seam of broken rock no wider than a coffin lid, stitched along the eastern wall by centuries of freeze and thaw, disappearing around buttresses of stone that jutted out over the current like the prows of wrecked ships.

"Nobody climbs this," Wren said. Not a question. An accusation against the landscape itself.

"People climb worse," Mirabel said. "People climb worse for less."

She checked her pack the way she checked her gear before a deep dive — straps, buckles, weight distribution, the demolition charges wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom where her hands could find them without looking. Two charges. Enough, if the surveyor's arithmetic Quentin had scratched onto birchbark held true, to bring down the roof of the spring cavern and send the whole cathedral of it down into the deep aquifer forever. She did not let herself think about what else might come down with it. She did not let herself think about the dive gear that would still be lying there when the water closed over it — her father's gear, forty years old, waiting in the dark for a daughter who had only just learned he'd ever been there.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes flat and you're somewhere else." Wren's eyes were pale as river ice, her father's eyes, and they missed nothing. "My da did it too. Right before he'd decide something terrible and not tell me about it until it was done."

Mirabel shouldered the pack. "Then we have that in common, him and me."

* * *

The first hour was almost kind.

The ledge held. The sun came over the western rim and warmed the rock enough that their fingers stopped aching, and below them the river ran green through its narrows, beautiful in the way rivers are beautiful when they are not trying to kill you — which is to say, deceptively, temporarily, with an indifference that Mirabel had long ago learned to read as honesty. The river didn't hate her. The river didn't love her. The river simply continued, and everything that happened inside it happened because someone chose to be there.

Quentin climbed like a man reading a map he'd drawn himself and then tried to forget. Twice he paused at junctions in the rock and said, without being asked, "There'll be a chimney ahead," or "The ledge drops here; stay right of the seep," and both times he was exactly correct. Ten years ago the navy had sent him up this canyon with instruments and orders to chart everything and report nothing. He had charted it. He had reported it. And they had taken his commission, his name, and his life's work for the trouble.

"How far?" Mirabel called across a gap where the ledge crumbled into scree.

He consulted nothing but memory. "Three miles of river. Maybe five of climbing, the way we're going. The cavern opens just past the last fall — there's a shelf above it, big enough for a camp. That's where the survey teams staged from." His jaw worked. "That's where I staged from."

Wren, ahead of them, said nothing. She had said very little since the weir, since watching her father's letters go into Mirabel's coat instead of a fire, since hearing Mirabel explain in six sentences why the map she'd handed Ashford was drawn in Mirabel's own hand, with errors seeded through it like rot through fruit. Wren had listened, and looked at Quentin — bruised, half-starved, traded back to them like cargo — and then looked at the canyon wall, and started climbing.

But now she stopped. She crouched on the ledge, one hand flat against the granite, head tilted.

"What is it?" Mirabel whispered.

"Listen."

Mirabel listened. Wind in the canyon throat. Water on stone. The small ticking sounds of the rock warming in the sun. And beneath all of it, so faint she might have invented it —

— a sound like a bell struck underwater.

She knew that sound. Every salvage diver knew that sound. It was the note a ship's hull sang when something struck it, transmitted through water and steel into bone. But there was no ship up here. There was nothing up here but rock and river and the three of them clinging to the wall like lichen.

And then, threading through the bell-note, another sound. One she knew even better.

An engine. More than one. Coming up the gorge from downstream.

* * *

They pressed themselves into a fold of rock while the sound grew, and Mirabel made herself do the arithmetic she most wanted to avoid.

Ashford had the map. He'd had it since the exchange at the weir — had taken it from her hand with the particular courtesy of a man who believes he has won, trading Quentin back across the stones like a prisoner of an older, more civilized war. And the map was false. Mirabel had redrawn it from memory over two nights by lamplight, copying her dead prospector's confident lines and shifting every bearing by degrees small enough to pass a glance and large enough to lead a survey party into a box canyon ten miles short of the source. She had even aged the paper with tea and smoke. She had handed a lie to Lieutenant Commander Ashford and watched him tuck it inside his coat like scripture.

One day, she'd told Quentin. A lie like that buys one day.

The day was spent.

Through her field glasses — salvaged off a drowned navy courier two seasons ago, and never yet so useful — she found them. Two boats working up the gorge below, gunmetal-grey, riding low. On the lead boat's foredeck, a figure with glasses of his own, sweeping the canyon walls methodically, top to bottom, the way a man searches when he already knows what he's looking for and only needs to find where it's hiding.

Ashford had reached the forgery's end. He had followed her beautiful lie into the box canyon, found nothing there but a dry waterfall and his own reflection in his officers' faces, and understood — and now he was coming back angry, and angry men with gunboats do not search ledges politely.

"He'll put men ashore at the gorge mouth," Quentin murmured. "Climbers. He knows the canyon's the only way up. He always knew; he just needed the map to tell him how much time he had." A bitter breath. "I taught him that. When I was useful."

"Then we climb faster than his climbers," Mirabel said.

"And if we don't?"

Mirabel lowered the glasses. Below, the lead boat's engine note changed pitch as it turned into the current. Above, the canyon went up and up into a haze of light, and somewhere at the top of it, past the last white fall, the spring waited with her father's compass and her father's bones and a metal that sang.

"Then we get there first anyway," she said. "Dead or otherwise. Because if he reaches that spring before we do, nobody ever comes down again — not us, not Wren, not the villages downstream whose water he's already poisoning. I've buried enough of this river's dead, Quentin. I won't dig his graves for him."

* * *

They climbed.

The afternoon went from warm to cruel as the sun swung off the western wall and the eastern ledge — their ledge — fell into shadow. Cold crept up through boot soles and into knuckles, and the rock itself changed character, growing slick where springs bled out of seams, freezing in thin skins of ice that glittered like fish scales. Mirabel moved the way she moved underwater: deliberately, testing every hold before trusting it, keeping three points of contact and her breathing slow. Wren climbed like a squirrel, like a rumor, like someone who had grown up scrambling up slag heaps and mine tailings while her father dug for metal in the hills. Only Quentin slowed them, and he knew it, and the knowledge sat on him visibly, adding weight no pack carried.

At the second hour the ledge died.

It simply ended — a clean fracture where a slab the size of a chapel had sheared away sometime in the last century and gone into the river, leaving a gap of fifteen feet with nothing below it but white water and, below the white water, black water, and below that, stone.

"There was a crossing here," Quentin said hollowly. "A rope bridge. Survey crews rigged it. It won't still—" He stopped. Looked harder. "It's still anchored."

It was. Or the ghost of it was: two iron rings driven into the rock, and from the far ring, a frayed tail of cable hanging down the opposite wall. The span itself was gone, dropped into the current years ago.

Mirabel measured the gap with her eyes the way she measured a wreck's hatchway. Fifteen feet. A running jump, loaded, on wet rock, with a drop that would kill before the water finished the job. No.

Not a jump. A traverse.

She uncoiled her line — the good line, gutta-percha jacketed, rated beyond anything a salvage yard had business owning — and began to rig an anchor. Behind her, downstream and far below, she heard the unmistakable grind of a boat's hull touching gravel. Men shouting. Boots on stone.

"They're in the canyon," Wren said. Flat. Factual. Terrified in the way that only truly brave people are terrified, which is quietly.

"Then we stop talking and start moving." Mirabel set the piton with two blows, hung the line through it, and paid it out across the gap. "Quentin. You cross first."

His head came up sharply. "You're sending the slowest man across the death-trap first?"

"I'm sending the man who doesn't trust his knee across while he's still fresh, and I'm sending him first because if the anchor fails, I'm the one who has to live with having rigged it, and I'd rather test my own work on someone who'll forgive me." She met his eyes. "Also, you're the only one of us who knows what's at the top. If we lose anyone, it can't be you."

Something moved through his face — gratitude and shame tangled together, neither willing to yield to the other. He clipped in. He crossed badly and bravely, swinging out over the void with his bad leg dragging, and when his boots hit the far ledge he sat down hard and laughed once, a cracked sound, and waved them on.

Wren crossed like she'd been born on a rope. Mirabel went last, hauling the line behind her, and halfway across — suspended over thirty feet of churning green water with her whole life hanging on two inches of iron — she allowed herself one look downstream.

Torches. Already. Dotted along the lower ledge like a string of orange beads, and rising. Climbing. Moving fast, moving in relays, moving like men who had done this before and been trained by someone who knew this exact route.

Ashford wasn't searching anymore. Ashford was converging.

* * *

Night caught them on the shelf above the last fall, and it was a mercy, and it was a trap.

The shelf was exactly as Quentin remembered: broad enough for a camp, sheltered by an overhang, close enough to the thunder of the final cascade that voices were useless and sleep was a negotiation. From here, Mirabel could see the cavern mouth below — a black seam in the canyon floor where the river simply ceased to be surface and became depth, pouring into the earth itself. The source. Her father had stood on this shelf. Her father had looked down at that same black water, wearing gear forty years out of date, knowing what waited below, and had gone under anyway.

She took out the compass.

It had come to her in pieces of story — the toll keeper's tale, the survivor's testimony, the file she'd read in Ashford's camp with her heart hammering like a fouled pump. Her father, Elias Underhill, salvage diver, lost with all hands in a routine wreck survey. Routine. She turned the brass case in her hands. Inside, under the crystal, in faded pencil on the card, in a hand she recognized from the margins of her childhood books: *M — if I don't come back, the river keeps what matters. Follow it up.*

Follow it up. She had. It had cost her a salvage yard, a reputation, the law's protection, and nearly three lives, and it had one mile left to run.

"We rest two hours," she said. "No fire. Then the last fall, then the cavern, then it's done — one way or the other, it's done by morning."

Wren sat with her knees drawn up, her father's letters in their oilskin bundle on her lap, staring down at the black seam of the source. "What if it's not what he thought?" she said suddenly. "What if we break it open and there's nothing? No metal. No answers. Just a hole in the world."

"Then we'll have made a hole in the world for free," Mirabel said, "and I've done worse work for pay."

That startled a ghost of a laugh out of her. Quentin, wrapping his knee by feel in the dark, said quietly, "It's real. I sampled it. I held it." He was quiet a moment. "It hums. You'll hear it before you see it. Like the whole cavern is a struck bell that never finishes ringing."

Mirabel went still. *A bell struck underwater.* The sound on the ledge, hours ago, that she'd filed under imagination.

"It's singing now," she said. "We heard it on the climb. Through the rock."

Quentin's face changed. "Through the rock? At that distance?" He shook his head slowly. "Then the deposit's bigger than I sampled. Much bigger. Mirabel — the charges—"

"Will be enough," she said, "because they have to be."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, torchlight flickered on the canyon walls — two bends down, maybe three. Hours behind them, not days. Hours.

She lay back against the cold stone, pack strap in one hand, and did not sleep. She listened to the river pour itself into the earth, and to the deep, patient bell-note underneath it, and to the small sounds of Wren finally drifting into exhausted sleep, and she thought about her father's last message written in pencil under glass: *the river keeps what matters.*

"Keep us a few hours longer," she whispered to it. "That's all I'm asking. Keep us till morning."

Two bends down, the first torch rounded the shoulder of the cliff, and the climbing lights began to rise.
