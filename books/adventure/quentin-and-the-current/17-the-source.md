## 17. The Source

The canyon closed around them like a throat.

For three hours they had climbed where the river forgot to be a river and became something older — a white roar wedged between walls of black granite, the water so channeled and furious that spray hung in the air like a permanent rain. Mirabel climbed with her father's compass in her breast pocket, its needle still spinning uselessly, and she had stopped trying to make it point anywhere. It pointed at nothing. It had been pointing at nothing since they'd found the fragment of the metal two days ago, and some part of her understood that the needle wasn't broken. It was honest. There were places on this earth where north simply gave up.

"Here." Quentin's voice, hoarse from shouting over the water. He stood on a shelf of wet stone where the canyon bent sharply left, and he was pointing not at the river but at the wall behind it — a seam of darkness behind the falling water itself. "The survey reports called it the Curtain. I never saw it. I only charted what the instruments told me, and the instruments went mad here."

Mirabel looked at him. Then at Wren, who stood a pace below, soaked through, gray-lipped from the cold, holding her father's oilskin packet of letters against her chest as though it were the last warm thing in the world.

"You don't have to come," Mirabel said. She had said it twice already. She said it again because the third time felt like the last time she'd be able to offer.

Wren's jaw set in a way that made her suddenly, startlingly, look like the dying man who had bled out on the floor of Mirabel's salvage yard. "He sent me letters for six years," she said. "Every one of them ended the same way. *If anything happens to me, go to the source.*" Her voice cracked but held. "I'm going."

* * *

They went in single file behind the waterfall, into a slot of stone that the falling water had polished smooth as glass, and the roar of the river fell away behind them by degrees until it became a heartbeat, then a memory, then silence.

The silence was the first shock. Mirabel had spent eleven years listening to rivers. Silence, underground, was a sound she had no name for.

Then the light began.

It came from below — that was the impossible thing, the thing her mind kept rejecting and re-approaching like a dog circling a strange object. A glow rose out of the flooded depths of the cavern, pale gold shot through with veins of green, and it moved. Not flickered. *Moved*, slow and deliberate, the way light moves through water when the water itself is alive with something.

The cavern opened around them the way a cathedral opens — vaults of stone rising beyond the reach of their lamp, buttresses of flowstone descending like frozen organ pipes, and everywhere, everywhere, the glow from beneath, painting the ceiling gold. The air smelled of minerals and cold and something faintly sweet, like ozone after lightning, like the moment before a storm decides.

"Oh," Quentin said softly. Just that. The cartographer who had burned his life's work in a stove two nights ago stood at the edge of the flooded gallery with his mouth open, and all his charts, all his ruined career, all his cashiering and disgrace — none of it had prepared him. He had been exiled for drawing a rumor. The rumor was this.

Mirabel walked to the water's edge.

Below her, perhaps four fathoms down — she could read depth the way other people read print — the spring floor bloomed. That was the only word. It *bloomed*. Out of fissures in the living rock grew formations of the metal: branching, coral-like, feathered with filaments that swayed in the upwelling current though the current barely stirred the silt. And each filament pulsed with that inner gold-green light, in rhythms that almost matched each other and never quite did, so that the whole garden beneath the water seemed to breathe — a slow, vast, patient breathing, like something asleep rather than something dead.

Her compass came out of her pocket without her deciding to take it out. The needle spun, spun, spun — and then, as she held it over the water, it slowed. Stopped. And pointed straight down.

Pointed at the source.

Eleven years. Eleven years of diving wrecks on that river, of hauling up other people's lost cargo while her own loss stayed down there somewhere, unmarked, unmapped. Eleven years of telling herself her father had been careless, or unlucky, or drunk — because the alternatives were worse. And now his compass, the twin of hers — he'd bought them together, she'd been nine, she remembered the shop, the smell of brass polish, his hand warm on her shoulder — now his compass would have pointed here too. Had pointed here. Had led him up this same canyon, behind this same curtain of falling water, into this same breathing light.

And he had never come back down the river.

* * *

She saw the gear before she understood what she was seeing.

It lay on a ledge below the waterline, visible through the uncannily clear water — clearer than any water she had ever dived, clear as air, as though the spring refused even the courtesy of obscurity. A dive suit. Old pattern, canvas and brass, the kind that had been old-fashioned fifteen years ago. Weight belt still buckled. Helmet resting beside it, faceplate toward the surface, as if set down carefully rather than dropped.

Not scattered. Not torn by current. *Arranged.*

Mirabel was in the water before Quentin could speak. The cold hit her like a blow — snowmelt cold, deep-earth cold — but she'd been diving freezing water since she was twelve and her body knew the work better than her mind knew grief. Three strokes down. The pressure of the spring's upwelling pushed against her like a hand, gentle but insistent, saying *not yet, not yet,* and she kicked through it.

The helmet came up in both hands.

Brass, green with age except where her fingers wiped it clean. And stamped on the crown, small and neat, the maker's mark — and beside it, scratched with a knife point in a hand she knew better than her own heartbeat, because she'd seen it on every crate and cleat and doorframe of her childhood:

*M.U. — for my girl. The current goes on.*

The words blurred. The water in her eyes might have been the spring's or might not have been. She surfaced slowly, holding the helmet against her chest the way Wren held the letters, and when she reached the ledge neither Quentin nor Wren said anything at all, which was the only kindness available.

"He came here," she said finally. Her voice sounded strange in the cathedral hush, small under all that stone. "He found it. Before them. Before Ashford, before the navy, before any of it — he found it first."

Quentin crouched beside her, water dripping from his hair. "The toll keeper said—"

"I know what she said." Vessa's voice, that night in the gorge: *Your father didn't drown by accident, girl. Men don't arrange their own gear.* "He found it. And then someone followed him. Or he tried to keep someone else from finding it." She turned the helmet in her hands. "There's no body. You understand? There's no body because—" She stopped. Because the possibilities were all unbearable. Because he'd gone deeper, into the fissures where the metal grew, and the spring had kept him. Because he'd climbed out the far side and chosen not to come home. Because men in navy survey suits had—

"Don't," Wren said quietly. It was the first word she'd spoken since the water. "Don't decide how he died down here. My father wrote me letters for six years and I decided a hundred times how he'd died, and every version hurt different and none of them were true." She knelt and put her hand flat on the helmet, over the scratched words. "You'll know or you won't. But you don't get to choose it in advance just because choosing hurts less than not knowing."

Mirabel looked at her — this hard, half-feral girl who had survived alone above the treeline for weeks, who trusted promises less than weather — and heard her own father's stubbornness speaking through a stranger's mouth.

"When did you get wise?" she asked.

"About the time I got hungry enough to eat things you wouldn't believe," Wren said.

Despite everything — despite the ache in her chest and the armed world closing in behind them — Mirabel laughed. It came out cracked and wet and real.

* * *

They explored while the light lasted, though the light, Mirabel suspected, lasted always.

The cavern ran back farther than their lamp could reach, galleries branching off the flooded main chamber like the roots of an inverted tree. In one gallery they found the survey camp's forward cache: crates stenciled with the Admiralty arrow, coils of cable, a winch, sample jars sealed in wax — and inside the jars, fragments of the metal, dim now, half-asleep, their glow guttering like coals banked for the night. Cut from the living growth. Taken from the water. And dying, Mirabel thought, the moment they left it. Whatever the metal was, it wasn't a mineral to be mined. It was grown, and it grew only here, in this current, in this dark, fed by whatever rose from the deep aquifer beneath.

"It can't be moved," Quentin said, turning a jar in his hands, watching the fragment inside pulse weakly, sadly. "Ashford must know that. The samples degrade. Which means everything — the cordons, the cover-up, the drowned wreck, the burned village, your father, Wren's father, all of it — it was never about extracting the metal." He set the jar down with exaggerated care, as though it might hear him. "It's about controlling the place. Whoever holds the source holds the only supply in the world. They don't need to take it out. They need to keep everyone else away."

"And to do that," Mirabel said, "they needed the river. All of it. Every mile charted, every village cleared, every diver warned off or buried." She looked down at the glowing garden beneath the water. "My father wasn't salvaging a wreck. He was diving their cordon. Checking their fences. And when he found the fence had a hole in it — when he found *this* —"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. The cavern finished it for her, in its gold-green breathing light, in the arranged gear on its ledge, in the patience of stone.

Wren stood at the water's edge, and Mirabel watched her take out one of her father's letters — the last one, the one with the ink smeared by river water — and hold it open in the glow. The girl's lips moved as she read it again, though she surely knew it by heart. Then she folded it away and looked up at Mirabel with eyes that were too old.

"He wrote that the spring sang," Wren said. "I thought he'd gone soft in the head. But listen."

Mirabel listened.

At first there was nothing — the silence, the drip of seepage, the distant memory of the river. But underneath, so low she felt it in her sternum before she heard it with her ears, there was a tone. A hum. Rising from the water, from the metal, from the deep places of the earth, steady and resonant and unmistakably, impossibly *harmonious* — multiple notes braided together, shifting, alive. The fragment they'd pulled from the rapids had sung the same song, faintly, warped and lonely. Here, the whole choir sang it.

The metal that sings. The spring that grows it. The compasses that point only here, as if the source were the one true north of the world, and every other direction a polite fiction.

Her father had heard this. Had floated in this water, in this light, hearing this music that no chart could hold and no report could describe — and had known, the way she knew now, that the world above would tear itself apart over it. That men like Ashford would come with winches and wire and wax-sealed jars, and men like the ones who burned the village would come behind them, and the singing would end, or worse, it would continue — owned, fenced, weaponized, its song conscripted.

"The current goes on," she said aloud, reading the scratch on the helmet once more. "That's what he wanted me to know. Not where he died. That the river keeps moving. That you can't fence it forever."

Behind them, far back down the passage, a sound broke the cathedral hush.

A boot on stone. Then another. And a voice, carrying down the galleries with the calm of a man who has never once doubted his right to be wherever he stands:

"Miz Underhill. I confess I hoped you'd make it this far. It saves us both a great deal of unpleasantness — and there is something here I very much want to show you."

Lieutenant Commander Ashford stepped into the gold-green light, and behind him, hands bound, flanked by two sailors, walked Quentin Hale — who looked at Mirabel and gave her the smallest, steadiest nod, as if to say: *I knew the price when I burned my charts. Pay it forward.*

Mirabel closed her hand around her father's compass, needle straining downward toward the singing water, and turned to face him.
