## 18. Ashford's Argument

Lieutenant Commander Elias Ashford had not slept in four days, and he had decided that this was a kind of discipline. Sleep was a concession to the body, and the body had never once been asked its opinion on matters of state. So he stood at the mouth of the source canyon with his collar wet from the gorge mist and his boots caked in the red clay of the survey road, and he watched the water come down out of the mountain in a voice like a cathedral organ, and he felt — not for the first time in his career, but more keenly than ever — that he was standing at the exact center of everything that mattered.

The canyon narrowed here to little more than a throat. The river, which a hundred miles downstream was a broad brown artery carrying barges and drowned secrets and the whole commerce of the valley, was up here a white ribbon threaded through black stone, and at its head, somewhere above the last falls, behind a wall of rock that his engineers had spent three years and eleven lives trying to map, was the spring.

He had come up the canyon with six marines and no artillery. That had been a calculation. Mirabel Underhill would hear the boats coming an hour before they arrived; the gorge echoed like a drum. Guns would only teach her to run, and a woman who had swum the flooded mine shaft at Kestrel Bend — the report of that escape had unsettled him more than he admitted to his subordinates — was not a woman who could be run down. She had to be *met*. She had to be reasoned with, or if not reasoned with, then shown something so true and so heavy that reasoning became unnecessary.

He had brought her father's file for that purpose. It sat in the oilskin satchel against his ribs, and he was aware of it the way a priest is aware of a relic.

The marines spread along the ledge below the falls, checking sight lines out of habit rather than expectation. Ashford waved them back. He wanted the approach to look like what it was: one man, unarmed, wet to the knees, come to talk.

He found her an hour later, and the finding of her was itself a kind of answer to a question he had been carrying since Kestrel Bend.

* * *

She was sitting on a shelf of stone above the last pool, her dive gear laid out beside her with the neatness of a woman who maintained her tools the way other people maintained their souls, and she was looking at the rock face behind the falls with an expression he recognized because he had worn it himself, once, in a chart room in the capital, the first time he'd seen the resonance readings from this canyon. It was the expression of a person whose understanding of the world had just been permanently enlarged and permanently diminished in the same instant.

She heard him before he was within thirty yards. Of course she did. She turned, and her hand went to the knife at her belt, and then — he noted this with professional interest — it came away again. She wanted to hear what he had to say. That was something to work with.

"Commander Ashford," she said. Her voice carried over the water without effort. Divers' voices. They learn to spend breath like coin.

"Miss Underhill." He stopped at the edge of the pool, deliberately, and let her see his empty hands. "I've come without my marines. They're two miles downstream and under orders not to follow."

"You'll forgive me if I don't find that reassuring. Your orders have a way of changing."

"They do," he agreed. "That's rather the point of orders." He nodded at the rock face, at the seam of pale metal that ran through the black stone like a vein of light through coal — the seam she had clearly already found, because the water above the pool was singing. He could hear it from here, that thin harmonic hum that had driven two of his survey men to desert and one to madness. "You've seen it."

"I've seen it."

"And your compass is lying to you, and your teeth ache, and you've been hearing the hum in your sleep for the past day, and you're telling yourself it's the falls." He took a step closer, onto the wet stone, and spread his hands. "It's not the falls."

"No," she said quietly. "It's not the falls."

* * *

He sat down on a boulder, because standing over her would be a mistake, and because his knees had opinions of their own after four days without sleep. The spray came off the falls in a fine cold veil. Somewhere below, a heron lifted off the pool and beat away down the gorge, and Ashford watched it go with something like envy.

"May I tell you a story?" he said. "It's not a short one, and you're not obliged to believe any of it, but I'd ask you to hear it to the end. You've come a long way to hear it, whether you knew that or not."

She didn't answer, which from her, he was learning, was assent.

"Eleven years ago I was a junior hydrographer on the *Meridian* survey. Do you remember the *Meridian*? No — of course you wouldn't. She was a navy vessel, and her charts were classified before the ink dried. We were mapping the deep channel of this river for navigation. That was the cover story, and it was even mostly true. And in the third summer, off a drowned village called Harrow's Landing — you'll have seen what's left of it — we brought up a core sample from the riverbed that should not have existed. A metal that doesn't match anything in the periodic tables as they were taught to me. It holds a charge the way a bell holds a note. It warps a compass needle at fifty yards. Put a current through it and it will sing a frequency that does things to the human nervous system that I have watched happen and will spend the rest of my life not describing to you.

"The Admiralty's first instinct was to bury it. That's the instinct you'd expect, and it's the instinct you'd have, and for about a year that's what we did. The sample went into a vault. The survey crew was dispersed with signing bonuses and signing oaths. I was promoted, which is the navy's way of buying a man's silence at retail.

"And then the war scares began. You'll remember them — everyone remembers them. The border fleets massing in the eastern sea. The new guns on the northern forts. And a man I will not name sat me down in a room with no windows and put the sample on the table between us and said: *Ashford, this thing in front of you could end war.*" He looked at his own hands. "And then he said: *or it could end it very quickly, from one side.* And he asked me which future I preferred. As if I were being offered a choice. As if the metal cared."

Mirabel had not moved. The spray beaded on her hair. "And you chose," she said.

"I chose," Ashford said. "That's what I've come to confess, if you want to call it that. Not the cover-up — the cover-up was policy, and I don't apologize for policy. I mean the choosing. Everyone in this business eventually has to choose, and the choosing never feels like a moment. It feels like a Tuesday. It feels like paperwork. And then one day you look up and you're the man who keeps the secret, and the secret has a budget, and the budget has a body count, and you tell yourself it's still Tuesday."

"How many bodies?" she asked. Flat. A diver asking depth.

"Eleven in the survey teams. Two villagers at Harrow's Landing who saw what they shouldn't have. A prospector named Corliss, shot on the river by men I did not directly order but whose orders I signed." He watched her flinch at the name and did not pretend not to notice. "And a salvage diver named Underhill, who found the spring in — it would have been the year your father disappeared — and who was told, in the politest possible language, to forget it, and who did not forget it, and who drowned in the lower gorge in an accident that my predecessor's report describes in language I have never been able to read twice."

The pool roared softly under the falls. Mirabel's face did something very small and very controlled, and Ashford understood that he had just handed her the worst gift of her life, and that he was going to keep handing it to her, because the alternative was a lie, and he had come — God help him — to stop lying.

* * *

"I have her father's file," he said, after the silence had done its work. He drew the oilskin satchel around and set it on the stone between them. "Everything. The *Meridian* logs. His last survey notes — he was better at reading the resonance than any man we ever hired, and I say that with the bitterness of a man who watched him do in a week what took my teams a season. The incident report. The coroner's findings and the two pages that were removed from the coroner's findings. And a letter he wrote to you that was intercepted and never delivered. I've read it. I'm not proud of that either. It's yours. It's been yours for eleven years. The navy just kept forgetting to give it back."

She looked at the satchel and did not touch it. "What does it cost?"

"Nothing," Ashford said. "That's the part you won't believe, so let me say it plainly: the file costs you nothing. It's not a payment. It's an argument. I am not a man who buys things with paper, Miss Underhill. I'm a man who has watched what happens when the wrong people learn where this spring is, and I am asking you to consider what you're about to do with the same clarity I was asked for, in that room with no windows, eleven years ago.

"Because here is what I know that you don't. Three weeks ago a foreign survey vessel was photographed off the eastern cape. Two weeks ago a man with a foreign accent and a very good cover story was pulled out of a tavern in the river towns asking after upstream salvage rights. Last week my own signals section intercepted a transmission in a naval cipher that is not our naval cipher. The world has noticed the hum, Miss Underhill. It noticed it the moment your friend Hale's charts leaked — and they *did* leak, whatever he told you; that's how I found him, and it's how they'll find this canyon. You are not standing at the beginning of a story about truth. You are standing at the end of one, and the only question left is who holds the spring when the last page turns.

"If you breach that roof — and I know that's what the demolition wire in your kit is for; I've read the manifest of every powder purchase on this river for a month — the spring collapses into the deep aquifer, the metal becomes unrecoverable, and the secret becomes worthless. I understand the appeal. Believe me. There are nights I've stood where you're standing and wanted exactly that. But understand what you'd be buying. You'd be buying a world in which the only people who know this metal exists are the people who already have samples in their vaults. The navy has samples. Others have samples. The source dies, and what remains is a race between armed men over fragments — no spring, no steward, no one who knows the metal well enough to disarm it. You wouldn't be burying the secret. You'd be orphaning it."

"And the alternative?" Her voice was still flat, but there was something underneath it now, and Ashford recognized it, because he had carried it for eleven years. It was the sound of a person listening to their own conscience argue with their own intelligence, and hating that the intelligence was winning ground.

"The alternative is the navy," he said. "Not the navy as a slogan. The navy as a room with no windows where tired people try to make terrible choices slowly, with oversight, with oaths, with the memory of every mistake written down and locked away so the next tired person makes a *different* terrible mistake. The metal could end wars — I told you that's what they said to me, and I've had eleven years to decide whether it was a lie, and my conclusion is that it wasn't. Resonance damping could make a fleet's guns deaf. It could make a mine invisible to every instrument but the human eye. In the right hands it ends the age of bombardment. And the right hands, Miss Underhill — this is the whole of my argument, and I know how it sounds, and I'm going to say it anyway — the right hands are hands with rules. Not clean rules. Not good rules. But *rules*. Written down. Argued over. Audited by men whose whole careers are built on finding fault. Your alternative is a spring in a hole in the ground and a promise you made to a dying stranger. I respect the promise. I've read enough of Corliss's letters to respect it. But promises are not institutions. Promises die with the people who make them. Institutions are how the dead keep faith."

* * *

Mirabel was quiet for a long time. Then she reached out — not for the satchel, but past it, and picked up a handful of the pale metal fragments that had washed down into the pool's shallows, and held them out on her palm between them, and the hum rose through her arm and into the stone and Ashford felt his fillings ache and his compass-bearing instincts dissolve into a warm directionless fog.

"My father's compass," she said. "You said his gear is still down there. In the spring."

"It is."

"Then you've known, for eleven years, that he made it to the source. That he didn't drown in the lower gorge at all. That your report is a lie from the first sentence." She closed her hand. The hum deepened. "And you came up here to offer me his file and a lecture on institutional memory."

"I came up here," Ashford said, "because I am tired, and because I have watched this canyon eat eleven good people, and because I looked at your file — yours, Miss Underhill, the file we keep on *you* — and I saw a woman who dives wrecks for a living and gives the dead their names, and I thought: perhaps she is the one person in the world who can look at this metal and see what I see and still find a third answer. I came up here hoping you'd prove me wrong about everything. That is not a thing a man in my position says often. It may be the last time he says it at all."

"Then hear my third answer," Mirabel said, and she stood, and the fragments fell from her hand back into the pool, and the water took them with a sound like a struck bell, and Ashford saw, in the way she looked past him up at the black roof of rock above the falls, that he had already lost — that he had perhaps lost the moment he handed her the satchel, because he had given her the truth, and the truth was the one weapon in this canyon that cut only one way.

"You said the world has noticed the hum," she said. "You said the secret is already out. Then burying it isn't orphaning it, Commander. Burying it is the only way to make sure that what's out there — your samples, their samples, the fragments in every vault — stays fragments. Dead ends. Curiosities. A metal with no source is a metal with no future, and every navy on earth will spend thirty years learning that the hard way." She picked up the oilskin satchel and held it against her chest like a child. "I'll read his letter. I'll read all of it. And then I'm going to do what he was doing when your navy killed him."

"And what is that?"

"Finishing it," Mirabel Underhill said, and turned toward the falls, toward the black seam of singing stone, toward the roof of the cathedral he had spent three years and eleven lives trying to keep closed — and Ashford, four days unslept, stood alone on the wet rock and put two fingers to his mouth and whistled the signal that brought his marines running up the gorge at the double.
