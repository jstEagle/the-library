## 21. Miles, Not Years

The *Kestrel* sat low in the water the morning they raised her first honest wreck — a grain barge gone down off Miller's Bend eleven years before, her hold full of nothing but wheat turned to black silt and the bones of two mules nobody had ever come back to claim.

Mirabel stood on the deck in the gray half-light and felt the river move beneath her feet like something alive and finally, after everything, indifferent to her. That was the strange gift of it. Downstream, past the gorge, past the burned village, past all the miles where men had died for a metal that sang, the current didn't know their names. It carried barges and driftwood and the occasional drowned secret, and it did not care which.

"Bottom's at nine fathoms," Quentin called from the wheelhouse, chart spread across his knees even though he could have drawn that stretch of river from memory with his eyes shut. He still spread the charts anyway. She'd learned that about him — the ritual mattered more than the information. A man who'd had his life's work taken away needed to keep his hands on paper. "Silt's soft. You'll want the air line, not the rig."

"You want to tell me how to dive now?"

"I want to tell you that you dove a flooded mine shaft three weeks ago and came up coughing blood," he said mildly, "so yes. Today I do."

She let him win, mostly because he was right. The mine shaft had cost her something she hadn't fully inventoried yet — a rattle in her chest on cold mornings, a thinness in her breath when she pushed too hard. Dr. Amory in Harrow's Landing had listened to her lungs, frowned, and said the word *scarring* in the careful voice doctors used when they meant *permanent*. Mirabel had thanked him, paid him, and walked out onto the dock where Wren was mending net, and neither of them had said anything about it, because some prices you don't discuss. You just carry them.

The barge came up over four days. Wheat silt, mule bones, a cargo manifest sealed in oilcloth that made old Tomas Ellery's widow weep when Mirabel brought it ashore — proof, at last, of what had happened to him, of where he'd been when the river took him. The insurance settlement would be small. The gratitude was not. Mrs. Ellery pressed a jar of plum preserves into Mirabel's hands and held on a moment too long and said, "You gave him back to me," and Mirabel had stood there in the yard with the jar going cold against her palms and thought: *this*. This is what salvage is supposed to be. Not strongboxes stamped with navy insignias. Not springs that grow metal in the dark. Just a widow, a manifest, an answer small enough to hold.

They ran the operation out of a lean-to yard on the Harrow's Landing waterfront that Quentin had leased under a name he invented on the spot — *Hale & Underhill Salvage, Est. this year* — and if the name had a certain grim comedy to it, neither of them remarked on it. There were three of them and one patched riverboat and a diving rig older than Wren, and by the end of the first month they'd turned a profit of exactly forty-one crowns, and Mirabel had never in her life been prouder of money.

* * *

Wren took to the water the way her father must have once — without ceremony, without fear, as though the river owed her nothing and she owed it nothing either, and between those two debts there was simply room to work.

She was seventeen and moved like someone older. The weeks above the treeline had done that, Mirabel knew. You don't come down off a mountain after surviving alone through the turn of the season unchanged; you come down harder at the edges and softer somewhere hidden, and you don't show anyone where. Wren read her father's letters every night by lamplight, all of them, in order, though she'd long since memorized them. Then she folded them back into their oilcloth packet and put the packet inside her coat, inside her shirt, against her skin, and slept.

It was Wren who found the second body on the grain barge — not the mules, but a man, wedged in the collapsed companionway, preserved by cold silt into something almost peaceful. She worked the recovery herself while Mirabel held the line, and afterward she sat on the deck with her boots dangling over the side and said, "My father used to say the river keeps what it's given honestly."

"He wasn't wrong."

"He was wrong about one thing." She didn't look at Mirabel. "He said promises were heavier than gold. He left me a map's worth of them and it nearly got us both killed." A pause. The current talked underneath them. "But I'm glad you kept it. I want that understood. I'm glad, and I hate it, and both of those are true, and I don't expect you to make sense of that because I can't."

"I won't try," Mirabel said. "I've got a decade of things I can't make sense of. It gets easier to just carry them."

Wren nodded slowly, and then, in the way of people who have decided something important and will not announce it, she reached into her coat and drew out one letter — a single sheet, worn soft as cloth at the folds — and looked at it for a long moment before putting it back.

Mirabel asked no questions. But she remembered.

* * *

Quentin drank less now. Not none — Mirabel found the bottle behind the chart case twice in the first month and said nothing either time, and the third time there was no bottle, and that was how she understood he was fighting it rather than feeding it. He redrew charts in the evenings, working from memory, the whole navigable length of the river from the sea to the gorge, every shoal and snag and sunken piling rendered in his precise, beautiful hand. When Mirabel asked why — since the navy had his originals and would hang him before they returned them — he said, "Because they're mine. Because I made them, and making them was the only honest thing I ever did with my life, and I find I want them back."

"And after the gorge?" she asked. "Past the toll water? Are you drawing that too?"

His pen stopped. For a moment the lamplight caught the scar along his jaw, the one he'd gotten at the source canyon, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been grief or might have been relief.

"No," he said. "That part of the river doesn't exist anymore. I intend to keep it that way."

He was drawing the maps to sell, it turned out — fair copies, sold to the steamboat lines and the timber outfits, signed *Q. Hale*, his real name, unhidden, almost defiant. "Let them come find me," he said when Mirabel raised it. "Ashford isn't reporting failure to anyone. You saw his face. A man who lets three civilians walk out of his operation doesn't file paperwork about it. He wants the whole business buried deeper than we buried the spring." He smiled, thin and crooked. "We're safe precisely because we're embarrassing."

It was true, mostly. But Mirabel had spent ten years learning what rivers do with things people want buried, and she knew that buried was not the same as gone.

* * *

Autumn came down the valley in stages, gilding the birches first, then the oaks, then setting the whole shoreline alight so that the river ran between walls of fire-colored leaves and the light on the water went the color of hammered brass. They worked the Miller's Bend wreck until it was stripped, then took a contract raising a sunk dredge at Corley Shoals, then another, word spreading the way word does in river towns: *the new salvage outfit, the ones who found Ellery's manifest, the woman diver who goes down in water that would kill a Navy man.*

Mirabel heard that last one in a tavern in Corley and laughed harder than the joke deserved, because the truth was she'd nearly died in water a Navy man would have found merely inconvenient, and the difference between her and the Navy was not courage. It was only that she had more reasons to keep going down.

On a bright cold morning in late October, with frost on the mooring lines and steam rising off the current, Wren came to the edge of the dock where Mirabel was coiling line and said, "Today."

Mirabel looked up. Wren's face was set in that mountain-hard calm, but her hands, Mirabel noticed, were shaking.

"Today what?"

"The letters." Her voice came out steady on the second try. "I've been carrying them upriver and downriver and against my skin for two months, and he wrote them to me, not to me-as-a-thing-I-carry, and I think—" She stopped. Started again. "I think he'd have wanted them in the current. He loved the river. He hated what was at the top of it, but he loved the river itself, and I think if I keep holding them I'm keeping him from wherever he was trying to go."

Mirabel set down the coil. "You want company?"

"I want the boat," Wren said. "I want to go out to the deep channel, past the bend where you can't see the yards anymore. And I want—" and here the steadiness cracked, just once, a hairline fracture across all that hardened survival, "—I want somebody there who promised. Because you're the last person who heard his voice besides me, and I think he'd want that too."

* * *

They went out midmorning, the three of them, though Quentin stayed at the wheel and kept the engine low and said nothing at all, understanding without being told that this was not a ceremony that wanted witnesses so much as it wanted presence. The *Kestrel* slid down the silver-brown current past the shipyards, past the ferry landing, past the last leaning pilings of the old tannery, and around the long bend where Harrow's Landing disappeared and there was nothing in the world but water and trees and sky.

Wren stood at the rail with the oilcloth packet in both hands.

She opened it carefully, the way you open something you've opened a hundred times, and took out the first letter, and read it aloud. Her father's voice came through the words — that dry, wry, stubborn voice Mirabel remembered from a dying man on the floor of her salvage yard, trading a map for a promise. *"Wren — if you're reading these, I was right about the spring and wrong about everything else..."*

When she finished, she leaned out over the rail and let the page go.

The current took it instantly, the way the river takes everything, folding it under and carrying it south, a white flicker on brown water, gone.

Letter by letter, she did it. Some she read aloud. Some she only read to herself, lips moving, and released in silence. One — the last one, written in a shakier hand, dated days before he washed shot and half-drowned into Mirabel's yard — she read twice, and her voice broke on the second reading, and Mirabel moved to stand beside her and put a hand on her shoulder and Wren let her, which from Wren was a kind of surrender and a kind of trust, both.

*"Don't let them make you hard, girl. Hard breaks. Be like water instead."*

The last page went into the current. Wren watched it go until it vanished, and then she stood at the rail a long time with the wind pulling at her hair, and when she finally spoke her voice was quiet and clear.

"He's where he was going now."

"Yes," Mirabel said, because it was true, or true enough, and sometimes true enough is the mercy you owe a person.

Quentin cut the engine and let them drift. The river talked under the hull. Somewhere upstream, behind the collapsed rock of a flooded cavern, a spring that had grown singing metal for longer than any nation had existed lay dark and silent and unreachable, keeping its secret under a hundred feet of stone and water, and the world above went on buying wheat and hauling timber and not knowing, and that was the peace Mirabel had bought, and she found she could live on it.

For a while.

* * *

The trouble came, as trouble does, dressed as work.

A man came down from the capital on the mail steamer in the first week of November — a clerkish man, gray-suited, spectacles, a leather document case chained to his wrist like he expected thieves at every landing. He found the Hale & Underhill yard at dusk, standing on the dock with the lamps lit and the day's last dive gear drying on its racks, and he introduced himself as Mr. Pell, of the Admiralty Hydrographic Office, and he was very polite, and everything he said was polite, and Mirabel had spent enough years around navy men to know that politeness was the uniform they wore when the guns stayed home.

"We're compiling a revised survey of the upper watershed," Pell said, smiling at Quentin across the chart table. "Routine. Decennial revision. We understand you once produced charts of... considerable accuracy in that district, Commander Hale. Before your, ah, separation from the service."

"My charts are for sale," Quentin said evenly. "Sea reach to the gorge. I don't chart above the gorge."

"Above the gorge is precisely—"

"Then you'll want another surveyor."

Pell's smile didn't move, but something behind it recalculated. He turned the smile on Mirabel instead, and she met it with the flat patience of a woman who had lied to a lieutenant commander's face and flooded a cathedral cavern and could certainly outlast a clerk.

"Miss Underhill. Your reputation precedes you. The diver who works water no one else will touch." He set a card on the table between them. White stock, embossed, official. "We retain private contractors, from time to time. Salvage assessment. Well compensated. The work would be... upstream."

Mirabel picked up the card. Turned it over. Blank on the back. She thought of her father's compass lying on the stone floor of a sealed spring, placed there by her own hand, a message to whoever came next: *someone knows.* She thought of Ashford's face in the last hour at the weir — not defeated, no, never defeated, only postponed, a man filing his failure in the deepest drawer he owned and turning the key.

She thought of the decennial revision, and how the Admiralty did not send chained document cases and polite clerks on routine errands to disgraced cartographers in river towns.

"We'll consider it," she said, and put the card in her pocket, and shook his hand, and watched him walk up the dock into the dusk with his case chained to his wrist.

When he was gone, Quentin said quietly, "You're not considering it."

"No."

"But they're going back up there. To the source. They're going to dig."

"They're going to dig," Mirabel agreed, "and they're going to find a hundred feet of collapsed rock and a dead spring and a compass that isn't theirs." She turned the card over once more in her fingers. "And then they're going to start asking themselves how the rock got collapsed. And who else knew. And whether the people who knew are still downstream, running a salvage outfit, diving honest wrecks, telling widows the truth about their dead."

Wren appeared in the doorway of the lean-to, lamplight behind her, her father's absence finally settled into something like peace on her face. She read the room the way she read water — instantly, completely.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

Mirabel looked at the card. At the river, dark now, moving south, patient as it had always been, carrying whatever it was given toward the sea.

"Long enough to get the boat ready," she said. "Not long enough to get comfortable."
