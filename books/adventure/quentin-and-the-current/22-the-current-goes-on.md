## 22. The Current Goes On

The orders came down in the third week of October, when the river had turned the color of gunmetal and the mornings came with frost on the mooring lines. Wren read them twice, standing at the survey barge's rail with her breath smoking, and understood that Commander Ashford had not given up. He had simply changed the shape of his wanting.

*Re-survey of the upper gorge. Diver deployment authorized. Objective: confirmation of structural integrity of collapsed formation.*

Confirmation. As if a mountain could be asked politely whether it still stood.

She folded the order and put it in her coat pocket, where it lay against her hip like a stone she'd chosen to carry. No one on the barge looked at her twice. That was the whole of her cleverness, and it had taken two years to perfect: a girl of nineteen, quiet, competent, unremarkable, who mended dive hoses and logged air pressures and never once let her eyes linger too long on anything. The navy had hired her back onto its own barges because it needed hands and because names were easy to forget and easier to forgive, or so they believed. They did not know they had hired the daughter of Elias Vane, prospector, dead man, mapmaker of the thing they'd spent three years trying to pretend was a rockfall.

They did not know what she kept.

* * *

The dive began at dawn, as all their dives did, because Ashford's replacement — a careful, joyless man named Lieutenant Sorrell — believed dawn light penetrated the flooded shaft best. Four divers in the rotation. Wren was third on the line, tending gear, checking gauges, handing men their masks with the flat courtesy of someone who had no opinions about anything.

The source cavern lay beneath four hundred feet of new water. Mirabel's charges had brought the roof of the world down, and the spring that had sung in the dark for longer than any chart recorded now drowned in silence, sealed under rubble and cold pressure, its metal unreachable, its secret worthless — that was the official finding, signed by three officers who had never gotten wet. *Formation collapsed. Vein inaccessible. Further expenditure unjustified.* The navy had walked away from it the way a man walks away from a woman who has refused him in public: loudly at first, then quietly, then not at all, but always looking back.

Sorrell looked back. Wren watched him do it every day.

"Vane." He said her surname without warmth. "You've checked the line reels?"

"Twice, sir."

"And the reserve bottles?"

"Bled and refilled yesterday, sir. Gauges true."

He nodded, satisfied, and turned to his lead diver, a broad silent Cornishman named Tregose who had been down the old access shaft eleven times and come up each time reporting nothing but stone. Eleven times. Wren had counted. She counted everything now — the days, the dives, the depth readings, the small lies the river told through her instruments. She had learned patience from Mirabel Underhill, who had once told her, in the gray light after everything, that the water keeps what it's given and gives back only what it chooses, and that a person who wanted truth from a river had better be prepared to wait like a stone waits.

Wren had been waiting like a stone for two years.

Tregose went down at first light. His line paid out smooth and slow, forty feet, eighty, a hundred and twenty, into the flooded mine adit that was the only approach left. Wren tended the reel with her gloved hand and felt each foot of rope pass over her palm like a pulse.

At a hundred and sixty feet, the line stopped.

Not snagged — stopped. A pause, the particular hanging stillness of a man who has seen something. Then it moved again, faster, and the signal pulls came quick and urgent: *come up, come up, come up.*

They hauled him into the gray morning coughing, mask half off, eyes wide in a face gone the color of the water. Sorrell crouched over him. "Report. What did you find?"

Tregose's hands were shaking. He held them out, and lying in one palm, streaming river water, glinting dully in the dawn, was a compass.

Brass. Old pattern, civilian make, the kind a prospector carried. The glass crazed across one quadrant. The needle swinging lazy and wrong, the way needles swing near the metal that sings — except there was no metal down there anymore, nothing but drowned stone and four hundred feet of black water, and still the needle would not settle north. It swung and swung, pointing at nothing, pointing, Wren thought, at everything.

"Where," Sorrell said slowly, "was this."

"Ledged in the rubble, sir. Just past the collapse face. Sitting upright. Placed." Tregose swallowed. "Placed deliberate, sir. Like a marker. Like — " he hesitated, and Wren saw him decide against the word — "like something left for someone to find."

The barge went very quiet. Frost smoked off the rails. Somewhere downstream a loon called, one long rising note, indifferent and ancient.

Sorrell took the compass and turned it over. On the case back, worn nearly smooth by years of a thumb rubbing it, was an inscription. Wren already knew what it said. She had known since she was nine years old, sitting on her father's knee in a lean-to camp while he showed her how to hold the instrument level.

*E.V. — All directions are one direction. Follow the water home.*

Her father's compass. The one Mirabel had found in the source cavern beside his dive gear, the one that should have been buried under a thousand tons of collapsed mountain. The one that was here now, in a navy lieutenant's hand, because Wren herself had carried it up through the flooded dark on a night dive fourteen months ago and set it on that ledge with her own frozen fingers, upright, deliberate, facing the entrance.

A message. Not to the navy. Through them.

*We know. We remember. We are watching.*

Sorrell turned the case over twice, frowning at the inscription, at the wrongness of the needle. "Instrument from the original survey party," he said at last, in the voice men use when they are building a wall out of words. "Lost in the collapse. Nothing more. Log it and seal it in evidence."

"Yes, sir," said the yeoman.

But Tregose was still looking at the water. And Wren, coiling the dive line with steady hands, caught the look he gave the compass — not fear exactly, and not greed, but the look of a man who has been diving these waters for months and has begun, in the wordless way of men who work the deep, to understand that the river is keeping something, and that his officers have been lying to him about what.

That was enough. That was how it started. Not with maps or manifestos, but with one diver holding one impossible object and feeling the first hairline crack run through his faith in the men who paid him.

Mirabel had taught her that too: *truth doesn't flood in like the spring did. It seeps. You just have to keep the channels open.*

* * *

They weighed anchor that evening, the survey concluded, the finding pre-written: *formation stable, vein inaccessible, no further action recommended.* Ashford's signature would go on it somewhere, three layers down, the last fingerprint of a ruined man clinging to a file. Wren stood at the stern rail as the barge backed away from the gorge, watching the black water close over the place where the spring had been, where her father had died, where Mirabel had ended a war before it started and lost her whole life doing it.

Downstream, thirty miles below the checkpoint, a lamp burned in a salvage yard on the riverbank. She knew the shape of that light the way she knew her own heartbeat. By spring the *Magpie* would be running honest wrecks again — Mirabel and Quentin Hale arguing over charts in the wheelhouse, the two of them having chosen each other over every flag ever stitched. And Wren would be there. She had letters to finish scattering, her father's words, one handful at a time, into the current that had taken him and given her back a family made entirely of broken people who kept their promises anyway.

But not yet. Not quite yet.

She waited until the barge rounded the first bend and the gorge fell into shadow behind them. Then she took out her notebook — the plain government-issue logbook no one ever checked — and beneath the day's air pressures and depth readings she wrote a single line in her father's cipher, the code he'd taught her with charcoal on slate:

*The compass is found. The channel holds. Send more.*

Because the compass was only the first message. Because somewhere under that mountain, past the collapse face, in pockets of air the navy's charts swore did not exist, the metal still grew in the dark, patient as geology, singing its one note to the water. Because secrets drowned are not secrets destroyed — and because the people who had taken everything from her father, and from Mirabel, and from the burned village and the toll keeper and every soul along three hundred miles of river, deserved to spend the rest of their careers looking over their shoulders at black water, wondering what it knew.

Wren closed the logbook. Ahead, the river ran on through the frost-dark country, wide and cold and keeping, carrying everything toward the sea and giving back only what it chose.

It had chosen her.

She turned up her collar against the wind and watched the water go by, and somewhere far beneath the keel, deeper than any line would ever reach, she imagined she could almost hear it — faint, patient, unmistakable —

the sound of metal, singing.
