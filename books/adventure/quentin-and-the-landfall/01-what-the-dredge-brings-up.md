## 1. What the Dredge Brings Up

The river gave up its dead slowly, and it never gave them up all at once.

Ines Eastwick had learned that lesson before she learned her letters, standing on the deck of her father's dredge while the grapples came up dripping — fence posts, wagon beds, a church bell once, green with forty years of silt, that rang one cracked, mournful note when it swung against the boom and made the whole crew cross themselves without quite knowing why. The river kept what it drowned. It only lent things back when it felt like it, and it never lent anything back clean.

So when the winch cable went taut that morning with a sound like a struck bone, she did not think *treasure*. She thought *weight*, and weight on the Marrow meant trouble wearing whatever clothes it pleased.

"Easy on it," she called down to Tam, who worked the winch with the patience of a man who had spent thirty years coaxing things out of water that did not want to give them. "That's not a snag. Snags don't pull like that."

"No, Captain," Tam said, and the cable sang, and the boom groaned out over the brown water, and something came up out of the Marrow turning slowly, shedding silt in ropes and ribbons, catching the early light in a way that made Odile lean out from the engine housing and stop wiping her hands.

It was a chest. Lead-lined — Ines could tell from the way it hung, dense and graceless, the way lead things hang — bound in iron at the corners, and stamped on every face with a seal she had not seen in the flesh since she was a girl: a bridge over three waves, crowned.

Vessany.

Nobody on the deck said the word. Nobody had to. Forty years ago the Republic had signed the treaty, opened the sluices, and let the reservoir waters walk up the Vessani valley like a patient landlord taking possession, and ever since, the drowned nation had existed only as a word people lowered their voices to say, the way you'd speak near a fresh grave. The bridge-and-waves had been scraped off boundary stones, burned out of books, hammered flat on door plaques. And here it was, rising out of the river on her own cable, bright as an accusation.

"Set it on the hatch cover," Ines said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "Gently. It's been down there long enough."

* * *

They brought it up an hour before dawn and did not open it until the sun was fully up, because Ines Eastwick ran her boat by rules and one of the rules was that you did not open strange cargo in the dark. The *Kindly Tern* sat moored off the snag line, her dredge arms folded, her boiler ticking as it cooled. Mist stood on the water in columns. Somewhere upstream a lock horn sounded, long and low, and the sound came down the valley like the river clearing its throat.

The chest sat on the hatch cover, weeping river. Odile had gone over it twice with a boathook's worth of caution and then, losing patience, with her hands. No trap. No chain. The lock was a simple tumbler, crusted but honest, and it gave to Odile's pick with a click that Ines felt in her teeth.

"Captain's privilege," Odile said, stepping back.

Ines looked at the lid a moment longer than she needed to. Quentin had come aboard at first bell — he was always aboard at first bell, that boy, materializing out of the dock fog like he'd been grown there — and he stood at her shoulder now, nineteen years of lean hunger and quick hands, watching the chest the way he watched everything: like it might be a door.

"Well," Ines said, to no one and everyone. "Nothing down there gets better for waiting."

She opened it.

The smell came first — cold iron and river mud and, beneath it, something older and drier, the smell of a room that had been shut for a very long time. Wool. Wax. Paper. Inside, packed in tarred canvas gone stiff as bark, lay the contents of a nation's attic.

Regalia first, because regalia is what nations pack last and lose first. A chain of office, its links black with tarnish but still heavy, still real. A circlet that was less a crown than a suggestion of one, silver worked into the shape of the bridge-and-waves. A sword with a scabbard wrapped in oilcloth, and when Ines lifted it — she did not mean to, her hands simply did it — the balance of it was so perfect it made her angry. Somebody had loved the making of that sword.

Under the regalia, documents. Ines lifted them out in bundles tied with waxed cord, and the wax seals were Vessani, and the paper was good paper, the kind the Republic had stopped making because good paper was a kind of pride and pride was what they'd drowned the valley to kill.

"Manifest," Tam said quietly. He had the eye for lists that a lifetime of cargo work gives a man. "There's a manifest here, Captain. Sealed inventory. It's — " He stopped. He read it twice. Ines watched his lips move.

"Say it, Tam."

"It's a registry," he said. "Not cargo. People. Names, ages — births. It's a record of who went into the water, or who went out ahead of it. There's — " he turned a page with one finger, careful as handling a bird — "there's a birth record here. Dated the year of the flooding. An infant. It says — " and here Tam did something Ines had never seen him do, which was lose his place in a list. He found it again. "It says the child lived. It gives a delivery mark. A chain of custody, Captain. Somebody carried this child out. Somebody signed for him."

"Name," Ines said.

Tam read it, and his voice went strange, and the mist on the water seemed to lean in to hear.

"Quentin," he said.

* * *

Later — much later, when the chest was locked again and the crew had scattered to their work with the careful loudness of people pretending nothing had happened — Ines sat alone in her cabin with the manifest and a lamp, and read the delivery mark until the letters stopped being letters.

It was a chandler's mark. A hook over a wave, struck twice, once shallow and once deep, the way a man strikes a stamp when he's in a hurry and ashamed of hurrying. Ines had grown up watching that mark go onto crates of rope and barrels of pitch and every crate of oranges that ever came upriver at Christmas. She had watched her father strike it a thousand times, his big thumb braced on the back of the stamp, and she had never once thought about what it would mean to see it on something that mattered.

*Quentin. Born the year of the water. Delivered alive. Received at Marrow's Landing, lower docks. Signed.*

Her father had signed it. Her father, who had been dead eleven years, who had taught her to read water the way other fathers taught daughters prayers, who had told her once, drunk on plum brandy at the winter mooring, that the river forgives nothing and forgets less, and she had thought he was talking about weather.

The lamp hissed. Ines turned a page and found a column of boats — names, dates, tonnage — and beside some of the names, in a different hand, a small notation she did not yet understand but already, in the marrow of her, feared. She closed the ledger. She opened it again. She was her father's daughter; she read a thing twice before she believed it, and three times before she despaired of it.

On deck, she could hear Quentin coiling line, his boots on the planks, the easy rhythm of a boy who belonged to the boat and the dock and the gray morning the way the herons belonged to the shallows. River-found, they'd called him, and everyone had agreed it was a fine thing, a lucky thing, a thing the Marrow did once in a generation out of sheer whimsy. Ines had helped raise him. She'd taught him knots before letters. She had never once asked herself the question that was now sitting in her chest like ballast: *found from where?*

The delivery mark said *her father*. The registry said *her docks*. The seal on the chest said a nation that no longer existed had put its last living heir into the current, and the current had carried him, all unknowing, straight back to the one family that had both saved him and — the ledger's second hand, those small notations, she could not stop seeing them — perhaps sold the rest.

Ines stood at her cabin window and watched the mist burn off the Marrow, and thought: *the past has weight.* Her father used to say it about cargo. Overloaded boats rode low and slow and drowned in weather that lighter boats laughed at. The past has weight; you can carry it or you can sink under it, but you cannot put it ashore, because there is no shore that will have it.

And now it had come up on her own cable, out of her own river, stamped with her father's shame and a dead king's bridge-and-waves, and it was sitting on her deck not four fathoms from the boy whose name was written inside it.

A horn sounded upstream. The lock gates would be opening, and with the morning barges would come the harbormaster's launch, and the harbormaster's launch, these days, carried passengers — gray men with gray papers who had been asking questions at the landing for a week, polite questions, questions about manifests and deliveries and the year of the flooding, as though forty years of silence had suddenly developed an appetite.

Ines put the manifest in the strongbox under her bunk. She put the strongbox key on the chain around her neck, where her father's key had hung, where her mother's had hung before that.

Then she went up on deck, into the cold bright morning, to look at the boy the river had given back.
