## 2. The Boy from the Manifest

The chest sat on the deck of the *Marrow's Grace* like a thing that had drowned and refused to stay drowned.

Quentin had been mending net when they brought it up — he'd heard the winch change its note, that strained, grudging sound the old drum made when the load was heavier than the river owed them — and by the time he'd crossed to the rail, Ines was already standing over it with her boots planted wide, the way she stood over anything she didn't trust. The morning fog hadn't burned off yet. It lay along the water in gray ropes, and the chest lay in it, lead-sheathed, furred with forty years of silt except where the dredge tines had gouged clean stripes down its lid.

And in one of those stripes: a seal. A crown above three wavy lines.

He knew that mark. Everyone on the river knew it, the way you know a scar on your own father's face. Vessany. The nation that wasn't. The country that had signed itself away and then gone under the reservoir water before most of its people had finished packing. They taught it in school as a cautionary tale about treaties, a paragraph long, no names. *Dissolved by accord. Lands ceded. Population resettled.*

Resettled. That was the word they'd used. Quentin had always thought it sounded like moving furniture.

"Touch nothing," Ines said, without looking at him.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

He had been. His fingers were already itching with it, the way they itched at any locked thing — crates, cabins, other people's letters. Nineteen years on the docks taught you that every sealed object was a question, and questions were the only currency a dockhand ever got rich in.

They opened it in the wheelhouse, because Ines said lead-lined chests that sit in cold water for four decades don't belong in open air, and because — though she'd never have admitted it — she wanted walls around whatever came out of it. Odile cracked the hasps with a pry bar while Ines kept her hand flat on the lid, holding it down half the time, as if the contents might bolt.

Inside, under oilcloth gone stiff as bark: regalia wrapped in felt. A circlet of dark silver, unornamented, almost plain, the kind of crown a country makes when it wants to say *we are not fancy, we are here*. Folded documents, their edges bloomed brown. And a ledger — not a ship's ledger, not a cargo manifest exactly, though it wore the shape of one. A registry. Names in columns. Dates. Destinations.

Ines went through the papers first, fast, her lips moving. Quentin watched her face do something complicated and then go very still, the way water goes still over a deep hole.

Then she handed him the ledger without a word, and he understood she was giving him a weight she couldn't carry alone, though he didn't yet know why.

*Registry of the Vessani Evacuation, Final Convoy.* He read the column heads. *Name. Age. Vessel. Receiving party.* The handwriting changed partway down — different hands, different nights, ink from different pots. Someone had kept this list alive the way you keep a fire alive, feeding it whatever would burn.

He was halfway down the second page when his own name stopped him.

Not his name exactly. But close enough that the wheelhouse seemed to tilt two degrees and hang there.

*QUENTIN. Infant, est. 3 months. Vessel: Lark's Errand. Receiving party: E.*

Three months old. *Lark's Errand* — he knew that name too, but from where? Not from the harbor; no boat by that name worked these waters now. And *E*. One letter, standing in for a whole life. Standing in for a woman who'd pulled a basket out of the reeds at Grayling Bend and carried it up the dock path and never once, in nineteen years, told him more than *the river gave you to me, and the river doesn't lie, but it doesn't explain either.*

"Captain," he said. His voice came out wrong, flattened, like a voice heard through a hull.

"I see it," Ines said quietly.

"This is — this is me."

"I know."

"How can you *know*?"

She took the ledger back gently, the way you take a knife off a child. "Because I've seen that mark before," she said. "On delivery crates. On my father's hand when he came home smelling of night runs." She looked at the seal stamped on the ledger's cover, and something moved behind her eyes that he'd never seen there before, and couldn't name, and would spend a long time trying to. "Because my family kept this book, Quentin. Or some of it. Some of them did."

*Some of them.* He'd think about that word *some* later, and what it was hiding. Later. Right then there was only the vertigo, the slow sick rotation of everything he'd stood on.

He'd always known he was river-found. It wasn't a secret; Grayling Bend was a small world and secrets drowned there faster than boats. The kids had chanted it at him until he learned to chant it first, taking the fun out of it the way you take the sting out of a nettle by grabbing it hard. *River-found, river-found, no ma, no pa, just mud and water.* He'd made himself a story out of it, a good one, the kind you tell yourself at sixteen and believe at seventeen and stop examining at eighteen: a mother fleeing something, a basket, a prayer, a hero of a harbormaster who happened along. He'd given himself parents the way you build a raft — from whatever floated.

But there had never been a *where*. River-found meant found *in* the river, and the river ran through eleven towns and two countries and one nation that wasn't anymore. He'd never had a direction to point his longing in. Longing without an address is just weather. You learn to live under it.

Now here was an address. Here was a column, a date, a vessel, a single letter *E*, and above it all a crown over three wavy lines, and the lines were the river, he realized — the Vessani arms were the river, their whole drowned country had put the water on its shield like a promise or a wound — and the vertigo came up through the soles of his boots like cold off the lake.

His identity hadn't broken. That was the terrible thing. It had *cracked*, the way river ice cracks in thaw — one report, loud as a rifle, and then a thousand hairline fractures running silent in every direction, and you could stand on it still, for now, but you knew, you *knew*, that between the surface and the black water there was nothing left solid.

"I need air," he said, and went out onto the deck, and gripped the rail, and breathed fog.

* * *

The fog burned off by noon, which Quentin would remember later as the day's last kindness.

He spent the middle hours doing what dockhands do when the ground opens under them: work. He coiled line. He scraped barnacles off the pilings with a dull blade, letting the rhythm of it hold him together the way stitches hold a wound. Odile passed him twice and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy. Once he caught himself saying the facts aloud under his breath, testing them like floorboards. *Born in Vessany. Put on a boat called Lark's Errand. Given to someone whose name starts with E.* Each sentence held. Each sentence also dropped him further from the boy who'd woken up that morning.

It was mid-afternoon when he saw the strangers come down the harbor road, and even before he saw the cut of their coats — city wool, river-wrong, too clean for Grayling Bend — he knew what they were. You learn to read people on the docks the way you read water. These two moved like men reading a place for exits and witnesses. One was tall and gray and forgettable on purpose; the other was younger, with a clerk's satchel and a smile he deployed like paperwork.

They went into the harbormaster's office. Of course they did. Everything official came through that door eventually, and everything unofficial pretended to.

Quentin told himself it was coincidence. Republic men came through twice a year with tariff schedules and safety inspections nobody asked for. He told himself that for almost an hour, scraping barnacles, until the tall one came back out and stood at the top of the dock ramp and simply *looked* — down the row of moored boats, past the fish stalls, past the net-menders, until his gaze reached the *Marrow's Grace* and stopped on the shape of a lead-lined chest visible through her open cargo hatch.

The look lasted three seconds. It might as well have been a knock on the door.

Quentin's hands went still on the piling. Under him, through the soles of his boots, the dock hummed with the river's old patient motion, and he thought: *they're coming for it. Whatever that chest is, whoever that boy in the ledger is, they've come forty years late and they've come anyway.* The ice under him crackled another inch.

He walked to the office. He didn't let himself decide to; his legs decided, and he followed them the way you follow a dog that smells rain coming.

Inside, the clerk-smiled one was asking Harbormaster Pell routine questions in a routine voice — traffic records, dredge schedules, anything unusual brought up this season — and Pell, old Pell, who'd known Quentin since before Quentin knew himself, was answering in the roundabout way of river folk, generous with nothing, polite about it. When Quentin stepped in, dripping piling-scrape and nineteen years of Grayling Bend off him, both agents turned, and the tall one's eyes did a quick inventory of his face that Quentin felt like a pat-down.

"Afternoon," the young one said pleasantly. "You work the *Grace*?"

"I live on her," Quentin said. Which was true, and more than true, and he heard Ines in his own voice as he said it and didn't understand why.

"Interesting boat." The clerk smiled again. "We're compiling records. Relics of the old annexation turn up sometimes — sentimental value, mostly. If your captain salvages anything with certain markings, the Republic pays fair recovery fees. Keeps things tidy." He produced a card, laid it on Pell's desk, squared it to the desk's edge with one finger. "Tidy matters. Unregistered artifacts especially."

*Unregistered artifacts.* Quentin thought of the circlet wrapped in felt. Of the ledger with his name in it, sitting thirty feet away in a wheelhouse drawer. Of Ines's face when she'd said *some of them did*, and how much history that little word had been carrying.

"We'll pass it along," he said.

When they'd gone — the tall one lingering at the ramp a moment too long, memorizing the *Grace*'s mooring lines, Quentin was sure of it — Pell exhaled through his nose and looked at Quentin over his spectacles.

"Boy," he said, "whatever that chest is, your captain's going to have to decide something tonight. And so are you."

Quentin looked at the card on the desk. Plain stock, plain type, a ministry seal he didn't recognize and an address in the capital. Then he looked out the window at the *Marrow's Grace*, riding low and faithful at her moorings, and at the wheelhouse window where lamplight was just coming on, and behind that lamplight, a woman who had raised him on river law and straight answers was sitting with a dead nation's papers in her lap, deciding.

The ice was cracking everywhere now. He could hear it under everything — under the gulls, under the water, under his own heartbeat. And somewhere beneath the cracking, far down in the black, he could feel the current changing, pulling upstream, toward the mountains, toward the source, toward whatever a name was worth when the country that issued it had been erased from the map.

He went back to the boat. The lamp was lit. Ines was waiting, and the look on her face told him the deciding was done.
