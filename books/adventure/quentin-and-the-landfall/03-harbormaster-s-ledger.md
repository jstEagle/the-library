## 3. Harbormaster's Ledger

The ledgers lived in the back room of the harbormaster's office, behind a door that stuck in wet weather and a lock that hadn't turned honestly in twenty years. Ines had to lift the handle just so — the way her father had shown her when she was small enough to stand on his boots — before the bolt gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside, the air was paper and lamp oil and river damp. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, sagging under forty years of the Eastwick family's handwriting: cargo manifests, toll receipts, snag reports, weather notes kept in a hand that started strong every January and faded to a scrawl by December. Her father's ledgers. Her grandfather's before him. The whole history of this stretch of the Vell written in iron-gall ink that had gone brown as old blood.

She lit the lamp, set the chest's documents on the desk beside her, and began.

She told herself it was method. A captain didn't act on a feeling; she checked her charts, sounded her channels, read the water twice before she trusted it once. So: method. She would trace the delivery mark on the manifest — the little stamped cipher of an oar crossed over a key, pressed into the lead lining of the chest — through the office records until she found who had handled it and when, and then she would know what she was carrying, and knowing, she could decide.

But her hands were not steady, and she knew why. The mark had been familiar the moment Odile scraped the river mud off the lead. Familiar the way a scar is familiar. She had seen it ten thousand times on ten thousand crates her father signed for, sealed, and sent downriver, back when she was a girl sitting on the loading dock with her legs dangling over black water.

*Eastwick & Sons, River Agents. All freight verified.*

Sons. There had only ever been one son, and he'd had one daughter, and both of them were dead or dredging snags now.

She started with the year of the flood. Forty years ago the treaty men had come to Vessany with maps and arithmetic — *your valley will be flooded for the reservoir; your nation is dissolved by consent; here is compensation* — and the Vessani had said no, and the water had come anyway. She had been born three years after. She had grown up on a river whose headwaters drowned nine towns, and she had never once thought about what lay under the still water above the gorge. Nobody did. That was the point of drowning something.

The ledger for that year was the fattest on the shelf. She carried it to the desk and opened it, and the smell rose up out of the pages like something exhaling.

Her father's hand, young then, confident, each letter standing at attention. Entries in the spring: *Freight, sealed, 14 crates, consignee upstream.* Then again in June. Again in August. Sealed freight moving *upriver*, against every current of commerce, paid in coin rather than credit — and always marked with the oar-and-key.

Ines turned pages slowly. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a night bird worked the shallows.

Refugees. It assembled itself in her mind with terrible clarity: the sealed crates were not crates. Boats running at night, manifests falsified, Vessani families smuggled up past the checkpoints into the high country while the republic's surveyors measured their valley for its grave. Her father had been part of it. Her father — Aldous Eastwick, who had taught her to read water before she could read print, who had made her swear the river law with her hand flat on the bitt: *you take aboard what the river sends you, and you put ashore what the river asks you to put ashore, and you don't ask the river to explain itself* — her father had run people out of a drowning country, quietly, entry by careful entry, because someone had to and no one else would.

She sat with that for a long time. It should have been enough. It would have been a fine thing to carry, if it had been all.

It wasn't all.

Near the back of the ledger, bound in after the ordinary months, was a section she'd never seen as a child, because children weren't allowed in this room and she had obeyed, mostly. Separate pages. Separate ink — a paler ink, official, the gray-blue of government forms. And a different hand entirely, except that it wasn't different at all. It was her father's, disguised: the letters slanted, the loops broken, but the spacing was his, the little hook he made on his sevens was his.

A second account. Payments received from the Office of Interior Security, Republic of the Vell. And against each payment, a report.

*Convoy observed departing mile 61, night of the 14th. Nine souls, est. Cargo listed as salt fish.*

*Reported to cutter *Vigilant*. Intercepted at the Narrows.*

And beneath that, in her father's true hand, small, cramped, as if the pen had been pressing hard enough to go through the page:

*Sunk. All hands. Payment logged.*

Ines closed the ledger.

She sat very still in the lamplight and listened to her own breathing, which was the breathing of a woman keeping herself from being sick. Outside, the river went on doing what rivers do, indifferent, enormous, carrying everything down toward the sea without comment.

He had logged which boats sank. That was the phrase that would not leave her. Not *which boats he sank* — though the difference was thinner than she wanted it to be — but *logged*. Recorded. Filed between the toll receipts and the snag reports, in the same book where he'd written her birth weight in the margin of a June page. He had taken the republic's coin to tell them where the desperate were, and he had written it down, because Aldous Eastwick wrote everything down, because a ledger that lied was worse than no ledger at all — some twisted code of his, some accounting he must have believed balanced itself: rescue in one column, betrayal in the other, and the boy in the chest alive at the bottom of the river to prove the first column had ever existed.

Except the boy hadn't stayed sunk. Quentin had washed up on her docks like driftwood from a wreck she'd never known her own family had caused, and she had fed him and taught him knots and never once asked the river to explain itself.

*You don't ask the river to explain itself.* She heard her father's voice say it and wanted, for the first time in her life, to break something of his.

The shame came up slow, then all at once, hot behind her eyes. Shame for him. Shame sharper than grief, because grief forgives the dead their weaknesses and shame does not. She was heir to both columns. Every plank of her boat had been bought with money that had, somewhere along its way, passed through the Office of Interior Security. Every knot she knew, every channel she loved, every instinct she trusted — all of it inherited from a man who had sold drowning people to their drowners and called it bookkeeping.

And the agents would come. She had known it since the chest came up out of the mud, known it the way you know weather. A nation does not drown a country and then forget to check whether anything floated loose. The chest was forty years old, but the seal on it was fresh wax over old brass, and fresh wax meant someone had resealed it recently, meant someone had hidden it recently, meant someone was looking for it *now*.

She blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and let the resolve arrive, because it did arrive, the way it always did — not as fire but as ballast. Something settling low in her, shifting her center of gravity, making her steadier and harder to tip.

They would not have the chest. They would not have the boy. Whatever her father had been, whatever she was heir to, the river law said *you take aboard what the river sends you*, and the river had sent her Quentin, and she had put him ashore nowhere yet, and she did not intend to start.

* * *

They came at midmorning, as she was coiling line on the foredeck of the *Magpie*, and they came politely, which was how she knew they were dangerous.

There were two of them, walking down the dock planks with the particular gait of people who had never trusted a boat in their lives. The tall one wore a gray traveling coat too good for the riverfront and carried a leather document case. The short one carried nothing at all, which worried her more. Behind them, at the harbormaster's window, the clerk was suddenly very interested in his paperwork.

"Captain Eastwick." The tall one stopped at the gangway and produced a warrant folder without opening it, the way a man shows a gun he doesn't want to draw. "Inspector Corvane, Office of Interior Security. This is Sergeant Vael. We're conducting routine inspections of vessels engaged in salvage work."

"Routine," Ines said. She set the coil down slowly. "We haven't had a routine inspection on this dock since before the war."

"New administration." He smiled with exactly half his mouth. "Your dredge brought up cargo yesterday. We'd like to see it."

"The dredge brings up cargo every day. Tires. Fence posts. Once a piano." She wiped her palms on her thighs. "Most of it goes to the county reclaim yard."

"This cargo would be older. Lead-lined. Possibly bearing foreign markings." His eyes went past her shoulder, cataloguing the deck: the winch, the boiler housing, the hatch to the hold. Cataloguing, she noticed, with a professional's economy. "Under the Antiquities and Navigation Act, I'm obliged to inspect any recovered property of historical significance. You're welcome to observe the search, Captain. Refusal, of course, becomes its own kind of answer."

Behind him, the short one — Vael — stood with her hands folded and her gaze fixed on a point slightly left of Ines's heart. Young, Ines thought. Younger than Quentin, maybe. There was something wrong with her stillness, something held, like a woman standing in cold water refusing to shiver.

Ines looked at them and thought: *they don't know where it is.* That was the whole shape of the morning, once she read it right. Warrants and courtesy and a sergeant who wouldn't meet her eye — none of it was confidence. They knew a chest had surfaced, or suspected, or feared, and they were walking the docks asking nicely because they had nothing better. If they knew, there would have been soldiers.

"Search away," she said, and stepped aside from the gangway. "Mind the coamings. She's an old boat and she bites."

They spent two hours. They were thorough — Corvane tapped the hull seams, opened the lazarette, made Vael crawl partway into the hold with a lantern while he stood in the well deck consulting a list that seemed to grow longer the emptier the boat proved. Ines leaned on the rail and watched them and felt the new ballast in her hold them steady. Let them look. The chest was not aboard. The chest was wrapped in tarpaulin under the floor of Odile's engine room, beneath a false layer of bilge slime she'd laid down herself at four in the morning, and the documents were sewn into the lining of her oilskin coat, against her ribs, warm as a second heartbeat.

Quentin had helped her do it without asking why. That had frightened her more than the inspectors — how easily the boy took to concealment, how naturally his hands found the work. River-found, he'd said once. Maybe the river teaches faster than we think.

Corvane emerged last, brushing dust from his coat cuffs, and paused at the gangway with the document case tucked under his arm.

"You're planning a run upstream soon, I understand. Fuel requisitions, lock fees." He smiled his half smile again. "The gorge closes early this year. Calving season. I'd hate to see a fine boat like this caught above the narrows when the ice comes down."

"We'll mind the charts," Ines said.

"Do." He descended the gangway. At the bottom he turned, and for a moment the bureaucratic polish dropped away and she saw the thing underneath it, plain and patient. "Captain. Property of the Republic has a way of surfacing. So do debts. Your family name appears in our files more often than you might expect."

Then they were gone, up the dock, past the clerk, out into the bright indifferent morning.

Ines stood at the rail until the *Magpie*'s shadow had the water to itself. Upstream, far off, the glacier gorge lay open for nine days, maybe less, and beyond it the drowned country and whatever the chest would make of them. Downstream, the sea, and sense, and surrender.

She went below, woke the banked boiler, and rang the bell for steam.

By nightfall they would be through the first locks. By dawn, outlaws.
