## 4. Cast Off Before Dawn

I didn't sleep. I lay in my bunk above the engine room with the chest's registry page folded under my pillow like a stone, and I listened to the *Marrow* breathe — the tick of cooling iron, the lap of water against her hull, Odile's distant cough through two decks of planking — and I tried to understand how a name could be a door.

*Quentin Vessany-Orleth, born the third of Thawmonth, forty years past the Drowning. Living issue of the House of Orleth.*

Living. The word sat on my chest all night like a cat that wouldn't be shooed.

Somewhere below me, Ines was awake too. I could tell by the way the deckboards creaked at odd hours, by the smell of pipe smoke drifting up the companionway around the second bell, by the fact that when I finally gave up on sleep and came down to the galley, she was already there, sitting with her boots up on the opposite bench and her father's ledger open on her knees, not reading it so much as glaring it into confession.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look worse."

"Good. Misery loves company." She closed the ledger with a slap that echoed off the tin ceiling. "They'll come for the chest at first light. Harbormaster sent word an hour ago — two agents from the Interior Ministry, riding a cutter out of Corvane. They want to 'inspect' us."

"Inspect what?"

"Everything. Us. Me." She turned the ledger over in her hands as if weighing it. "Quentin, listen. There's a version of tonight where I hand them the chest, apologize for the confusion, and go back to dredging snags until my hands give out. That's the sensible version. That's the version my father would have taken."

"And?"

"And I've spent forty years of other people's lives paying for what my father took." Her jaw worked. "I'm not handing you over. Not the chest, not the name, none of it. So we're leaving. Now, before light, before that cutter ties up at our berth."

The word *we* went through me like cold water through a wetsuit. "Leaving where?"

"Upstream."

"The gorge doesn't open for—"

"Nine days. Which is why we leave now, at low water, and run the lock chain before the toll office wakes up and remembers they own it." She was already moving, pulling her oilskin from its peg, and there was something in the set of her shoulders I'd seen maybe three times in nineteen years: the night the flood took the lower docks; the night she pulled me out of the river myself, half-drowned, eleven years old and stupid with cold; the night they buried her father. A woman deciding something the river couldn't argue with.

"Ines," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted. "If we run, we're not captains anymore. We're smugglers. Outlaws."

She stopped with one arm in her sleeve and looked at me across the galley's yellow lamplight, and for a moment I saw her measure me — not the boy she'd fished from the water, but whatever I was becoming, this thing with a dead nation stitched to its name.

"Aye," she said. "That we are. You can still step off, Quentin. Walk up to the harbormaster's yourself, tell them everything, and be a free man with a clean record by noon. I won't think less of you."

And God help me, some part of me wanted to. Some part of me wanted nothing more than to be nobody again, a dockhand with rope-burned palms and a bunk above the boiler, whose biggest worry was whether the *Marrow*'s starboard paddle bearing would last the season.

But the page was under my pillow. My name was in a drowned king's registry. And the river had never once asked me who I was before it carried me somewhere.

"No," I said. "Cast off."

Ines smiled — a small, hard, river-smile, gone as fast as it came. "Then get Odile. Tell her we're running the chain at low water and she has ten minutes to bring the boiler to pressure without waking the whole harbor."

"She's going to kill us."

"Not before breakfast," said Ines, and went to take the wheel.

* * *

Odile did not kill us, though I judged it a near thing. She heard me out standing in the engine room doorway in her union shirt and one boot, arms crossed, face lit from below by the firebox glow, wearing the expression of a woman doing long division on the cost of loyalty.

"Low water," she said. "You want to run the lock chain at *low water*."

"That's the plan."

"On a boat drawing four feet six, through a channel that drafts three feet at spring tide."

"That's why we need you awake."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she spat into the bilge — a habit Ines pretended not to see — and started pulling levers. "Get the stern line off the bollard, boy. Quietly. If you wake the customs launch, I'm telling them you made me do it."

By the third bell before dawn the *Marrow* slid off her berth with barely a whisper, running dark, no lamps, no whistle, her paddles turning slow enough to stir the water without slapping it. The harbor lay asleep around us — masts like winter reeds, the gaslights along the quay burning low and orange, gulls muttering in their sleep on the pilings. Somewhere behind us, at the harbormaster's office, a lamp was already lit. Men were already asking questions. But questions travel at the speed of paperwork, and paperwork sleeps late, and the river does not.

We ran the channel on memory. Ines stood at the wheel with the binnacle lamp hooded to a coin of red light, calling soundings to herself under her breath like prayers — *three fathoms, two and a half, two* — and Odile fed the boiler just enough steam to answer the helm and no more. I stood at the bow with a boathook I had no idea how to use, feeling useless and wide awake and more alive than I had ever been in my life, which frightened me more than the agents did.

The lock chain stretched across the river at the old toll narrows like a rosary of black beads, each link taller than my shoulder, hung between two stone towers the Republic had inherited along with everything else it hadn't drowned. By day, a ferryman cranked it down into a slot in the riverbed and charged you for the privilege of passing. By night, at low water, the topmost links rode barely a hand's width under the surface.

Barely.

"Odile," Ines called softly. "Can you hold her at bare steerage? No more?"

"I can hold her at a crawl if the current lets me."

"It will. It always does." Ines brought us around the point, and the towers rose up on either side, wet stone gleaming faintly, and the chain lay ahead of us in the black water like a sleeping serpent. "Quentin. Bow. When I say, watch the links. Call them as we cross. If we touch, call it loud."

My mouth had gone dry as August. "And if we touch?"

"Then we're aground on a Republic lock chain at dawn with a stolen national treasure in our hold, and we improvise." She glanced back at me, and in the hooded lamplight her eyes were bright and terrified and grinning all at once. "So don't let us touch."

I watched the water. The chain came up at us slow as a dream — a shadow, then a shape, then a great barnacled shoulder of iron sliding under our keel, close enough that I could have leaned down and touched it. I called the depth of it without knowing I was speaking. "*Clear.* Clear. Clear—" and then the stern was over, and the propeller wash swirled up silt that smelled of forty years of drowned things, and we were through.

Nobody cheered. Odile opened the throttle a quarter-turn, which from Odile was a war whoop, and the *Marrow* gathered way and ran for the open narrows with the dawn coming up gray behind us like a tide of milk.

* * *

They found us anyway. Of course they found us. Paperwork travels slow, but cutters travel fast.

I was below, lashing the chest down in the hold — my hands kept wanting to touch it, to confirm it was real, that I was real — when I felt the change in the engine note. A hesitation. Then Odile's voice through the speaking tube, flat as slate: "Smoke astern. Funnel smoke. Military."

I came up the ladder two rungs at a time. Behind us, maybe two miles back where the river bent around Harker's Point, a white shape was coming on at speed, throwing a bow wave like a plowshare, the Republic's blue-and-slate ensign snapping at her masthead. Even at that distance I could feel the arrogance of her — all that horsepower, all that certainty, chasing down one old snag-boat with a boiler older than I was.

"How fast is she?" I asked.

"Faster than us," said Odile. "Faster than anything. She'll have us in twenty minutes if we stay in the main channel."

"So we don't stay in the main channel." Ines was studying the water ahead with an expression I'd learned to read over nineteen years: she wasn't looking at the river. She was looking at her memory of the river. "There's a bar," she said slowly. "Off Gull Island. The survey charts show it as shoal water, four feet, impassable. The charts are wrong. My father showed me once, when I was your age. The channel runs true along the island's north shore — deep enough, but narrow as a church aisle, and if you're a foot off, you're on the sand and done."

"And the cutter?" Odile asked.

"The cutter's crew reads charts." Ines spun the wheel, and the *Marrow* heeled gently toward the island. "Hold on to something."

What followed, I will remember until I die. We ran the island's north shore with the sandbar's pale shoulder visible off our port side, close enough to count the ripples the current combed across it, while the channel — Ines's secret channel, her father's secret, handed down like everything else in her family, both the gift and the guilt — carried us over water so shallow I could see every stone on the bottom, every startled pike hanging motionless in the weed. The cutter came roaring up the main channel behind us, and I watched through the stern glass as her officer realized, too late, what we were doing — saw him hesitate, because his chart said death, and his orders said caution, and neither of those things was worth his hull.

We shot the gap. The *Marrow* trembled once, her keel kissing the bar with a long shudder like a held breath released, and then we were through into deep water beyond, and the cutter was swinging wide around Gull Island the long way, losing us minute by minute, mile by mile, until her funnel smoke was just a smudge on the morning.

I laughed. I couldn't help it — I stood at the stern rail with the wind tearing at me and laughed until my ribs hurt, wild and helpless, because we had done it, we had actually done it, and no one had ever told me that fear and joy were the same animal wearing different coats.

Then I thought about what we'd done — really thought about it — and the laugh died in my throat. There was no un-casting-off. No going back to the berth, the ledgers, the ordinary mornings. Every bridge behind us was burned, and I had helped strike the match, and somewhere behind us men in gray coats were writing our names into a different kind of ledger entirely.

Ines appeared beside me at the rail. She didn't say anything for a while. Neither did I. The river ran green and gold in the new sun, and ahead of us the country climbed toward hills neither of us knew.

"Guilt's part of the fare," she said at last, quietly. "Everybody pays it. Just don't let it take the wheel."

Before I could answer, Odile's head came out of the engine room hatch, and her face was wrong — tight, gray, careful.

"Captain," she said. "You'd both better come look at this. Somebody's been in my stores. And I count one bedroll too many in the forward hold."
