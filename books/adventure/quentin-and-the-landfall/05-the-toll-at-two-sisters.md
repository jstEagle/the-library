## 5. The Toll at Two Sisters

Two Sisters sat on the river like a woman leaning on a fence rail, all its weight thrown to the downstream side. The town had been built in the shadow of the toll bridge — a great iron span with chains that could be raised to stop anything smaller than a barge — and it had grown fat and cynical feeding off the traffic that the bridge caught. Every boat that passed paid, and every boat that paid stopped long enough to drink, and every boat that drank left money in the till of somebody who never asked where the money came from.

Ines knew the town the way she knew her own hands: by its scars. There was the rope-burn on the pilings from the flood year. There was the tavern called the Anvil & Oar, rebuilt twice and burned once. And there was Brannoc Hale, sitting on his usual bench outside the tollhouse as if he'd been waiting forty years for her boat to come around the bend.

He hadn't changed much. He'd been old when she was young, and now he was simply older, the way stones get smoother rather than smaller. His beard had gone the color of birch bark, and his eyes were still the pale, patient gray of a man who watched water for a living.

"Eastwick," he said, as the *Vesper* slid against the toll dock. "Thought you were dead."

"Not yet." She threw him the line. "What's the toll?"

"For you? Nothing." He didn't smile. That was the trouble with Brannoc; when he wasn't smiling, something was wrong. "For your cargo, we'll have to talk."

The word *cargo* went through her like a fishhook through the palm. She kept her face still, the way her father had taught her — keep the face still, Ines-girl, because the river reads faces faster than it reads charts — but inside, everything began to move.

"I don't carry cargo," she said. "I carry passengers. One passenger. My engineer. Myself."

"Mm," said Brannoc, and looked past her shoulder, upriver, toward the gorge that was not yet open, and then back downriver, toward the smoke of the Republic cutter that had been following them since the narrows, too far off to catch them and too stubborn to quit. "Come inside. Bring the boy if you trust him. Leave the chest."

"I don't leave the chest."

"Then bring the chest," he said, "and understand that everybody in this town is going to know you brought it."

* * *

The Anvil & Oar smelled of tallow and tar and the particular sourness of beer brewed by someone who hated brewing. The common room was half full, which at midmorning meant it was fully attended by people who had nothing better to do than watch a river captain walk in with a lead-lined chest under one arm and a nineteen-year-old boy behind her trying to look like furniture.

Brannoc took the corner table, the one with the wall at his back and the window at his side, and waited until the barmaid had brought four cups nobody had ordered and gone away again.

"Open it," he said.

"No."

"Ines." He said her name the way you'd say the name of a dog you'd once loved and later had to put down. "I've known you since you were small enough to fit in a bait bucket. I taught your father to read current. So I'll say this plainly, because plainness is the last courtesy I can afford you. A Republic courier came through two days ago. He carried a warrant with no name on it — just a description. Lead-lined chest, Vessani seal, riverboat out of the lower docks, master named Eastwick. They're paying for information in gold, Ines. Real gold, minted, not promises. Half this town would sell you for less than they'll be offered by nightfall."

"Then why haven't you?"

"Because I'm owed a debt," he said, "and I collect debts in person." He turned his gray eyes on Quentin, who had gone very quiet in the way that Ines had learned meant his mind was running fast and dark. "And because what's in that chest belongs, by every law older than the Republic, to someone in this room. I'd wager my tollhouse it isn't me."

Quentin said nothing. But his hand had moved, without his seeming to notice it, to rest on the chest's iron band, and Ines saw it, and Brannoc saw it, and the whole room might as well have seen it.

"The offer," Brannoc said, "is this. Give me the regalia. Crown, scepter, whatever pretty metalwork the drowned kingdom packed into its little lead coffin. I'll melt it down or move it south or bury it in a peat bog, whichever keeps it from shining. In exchange: safe passage through Two Sisters, my silence, and the silence of everyone whose silence I own — which is most of the people worth owning. You keep the papers. Papers don't shine. Nobody kills for paper unless they've read it first."

"And the cutter?" Ines asked.

"Will lose you above the bridge. There are channels above the bridge that even their charts don't hold, because their charts were drawn by men who drowned drawing them." He drank. "It's a fair price, Eastwick. It's more than fair. It's charity, and I hate charity, which is how you know it's genuine."

Ines looked at the chest. Under her hand, through the iron band, she could feel the cold of the lead, the way you feel winter coming through a wall. Inside was a crown that had belonged to a country that no longer existed, and a birth record that said the boy standing behind her was the reason the crown still mattered to anyone.

"No," she said.

Brannoc closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when a card he expected doesn't fall. "No?"

"It's not mine to give. And it's not his to sell." She stood. "We pay the toll in coin like honest smugglers, and we go on."

"There is no *on*, Ines. Not without me. The gorge opens in nine days — nine, maybe eight if the thaw runs hot — and when it closes, it closes for a year. A year is a long time to outrun a republic." He leaned forward. "Take the deal."

"I said no."

She felt Quentin behind her, silent as ballast. She did not turn around. If she had turned around, she would have seen what Brannoc saw: not agreement, exactly, but not refusal either. Something more dangerous than either. A boy doing arithmetic.

* * *

They took a room above the chandlery because Brannoc insisted, saying the Anvil's beds were crawling with listeners, and because Ines wanted walls she could count. Odile went down to the engine to nurse the boiler, which had developed a knock on the ascent like a debtor at the door. That left Ines and Quentin alone with the chest between them on the narrow bed, and neither of them touching it, and both of them aware of it the way you're aware of a sleeping animal.

"Nine days," Quentin said finally.

"Eight, maybe."

"And after that, a year."

"A year," she agreed.

He was looking at the window, at the iron bridge, at the far bank where the Republic cutter lay at anchor with its steam up, patient as a spider that has already webbed the doorway. "Brannoc's offer was good," Quentin said.

"It was cheap."

"It was *good*, Ines. A crown for our lives. That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's the best trade anyone's ever offered me. Do you know what I'm worth otherwise? Nineteen years, and the sum total is a bunk on your boat and a share of whatever the dredge brings up. Somebody offers a crown for that, and you call it cheap."

She heard it then — not anger, not quite, but the thing underneath anger, which is hurt wearing armor. "Quentin—"

"They killed a country," he said. "They flooded it. They put a nation underwater and wrote it out of the maps, and now there's one piece of proof left that it ever had a future, and it's sitting on a bed in a chandlery over a toll town, and I'm supposed to trade it for *passage*?" His voice cracked and he hated that it cracked; she could see him hating it. "I didn't ask for any of this. I didn't ask to be river-found. I didn't ask to be in a manifest. But it's *mine*, Ines. For the first time in my life something is actually mine, and everyone keeps telling me the smart thing is to give it away."

"The smart thing," Ines said quietly, "is usually what survival calls itself when it wants to be flattered."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she admitted. "It isn't."

He sat down on the edge of the bed, next to the chest but not touching it, and for a long moment the only sound was the river under the floorboards and the knock of the boiler drifting up from below, steady as a pulse.

"If I sold it," he said, almost to himself, "just the crown — just the metal — we'd be through the bridge by morning. Through the narrows by tomorrow night. Free. And nobody would ever have to know what I gave up, because nobody would ever have to know I was anything at all." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Would you stop me?"

It was the hardest question anyone had ever asked her, and she had spent three days learning harder ones. Her father's ledgers were still folded against her heart like a knife she couldn't take out. *Boat sank. No survivors logged. Payment received.* She had inherited a rescue and a betrayal in the same blood, and she had not yet told him, and every hour she didn't tell him the lie grew another ring like a tree.

"I'd ask you what it was worth," she said. "And then I'd let you decide. Because it's yours. That's the whole point of it — it's the only thing that's ever been yours, so it has to be yours to keep or yours to spend. I won't make that choice for you, Quentin. I've made enough choices for other people's children."

Something moved across his face — gratitude and grief in the same expression, like light and shadow crossing a field.

"But hear me," she said, and sat beside him, close enough that the chest's cold reached them both. "Whatever you decide, decide it looking at the whole ledger. Brannoc buys the crown and sells the silence, fine. But the papers stay real. The name stays real. And the day comes — it always comes — when someone reads a name in a ledger and starts asking questions again. You can't sell the truth in pieces. It only sells whole."

"Then maybe I sell it whole," he said. "To Brannoc. To the Republic. To whoever pays in lives instead of gold."

"You could," Ines said. "You could also throw it in the river. You could also wear it. You could also burn it. All of those are yours." She stood, because staying seated beside him was becoming unbearable, because the ledger against her ribs was getting heavier by the hour. "Sleep on it. We cast off before dawn either way."

"And if I've decided by then to sell?"

"Then I'll help you carry it to Brannoc myself," she said, "and I'll hate every step, and I'll do it anyway. That's what family is. It's carrying things you hate for people you love."

He looked at her then the way he had looked at her when he was six and she'd pulled him out of the spring flood — like she was solid ground in a world that mostly wasn't.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the chair by the window with her father's ledger open on her knees, reading entries she had read a hundred times since the chest came up, hunting for something she'd missed, some entry that would tip the scale of her father's soul back toward rescue and away from betrayal. The lamp burned low. Below, the town of Two Sisters settled into its nighttime business of drinking and lying, and the cutter at anchor kept its lights burning like a held breath.

Somewhere past midnight, the floorboard in the hall creaked.

Not Odile — Odile walked like a dropped anchor. Not a stranger — strangers knocked. It was the particular soft, deliberate tread of someone trying not to wake her, and failing, and knowing it.

She closed the ledger. She did not get up. She listened to Quentin cross the room in the dark, listened to the whisper of the chest's lid opening, listened to the small metallic sound of a crown being lifted and weighed in two young hands — the sound of a kingdom, forty years drowned, being appraised by the boy it was left to.

Then she heard him sit back down, and the lid close, and nothing else for a long time. Just breathing. Just the river.

When she finally slept near dawn, it was without dreams, and she woke to find Quentin already on deck, casting off lines in the gray light, his face set in an expression she couldn't read — and the crown still in the chest, or not; she wouldn't know until they were past the bridge, and by then it would be too late to ask, and maybe that was the point.

Above the tollhouse, the iron chains hung slack over the water, waiting to rise.

Brannoc stood on his bench in the cold morning air, watching her boat come down to the lock, and raised one hand — in farewell, or warning, or appraisal. Behind him, the gorge road climbed north into clouds, nine days open, maybe eight.

Ines took the wheel.
