## 6. Rapids and Reckonings

The river changed its mind about us at dawn.

We'd been climbing for two days, the *Maren Voss* laboring against a current that grew more opinionated by the mile, and somewhere in the night the water had stopped being brown and started being white — not with foam exactly, but with light, the way milk goes when you stir something into it. Glacial flour, Odile called it. Rock ground to powder by ice older than any nation on the map. The whole river upstream of the gorge ran the color of a dead man's eye.

I was on the bow line when I heard Ines swear.

She swore maybe four times a year, and each one was a small ceremony. This one came up through the deck plates and into my boots before it reached my ears, which meant it had come from the engine room, which meant it was about the boiler, which meant we were in trouble of the kind that doesn't wait its turn.

"Quentin!" Her voice through the speaking tube was stripped down to wire. "Get aft. Now."

I went. The stern wheel was hammering like a thing trying to escape its own body, and the river ahead had arranged itself into something I'd only ever seen from shore: a standing wave the height of the pilothouse roof, then another behind it, then a stretch of water so torn up it looked less like a river than an argument. The Two Sisters rapids. Every pilot on the lower river had a story about them, and every story ended with somebody else's boat.

Ines came up the ladder two rungs at a time, her hands still black with coal dust, and took the wheel without looking at me.

"Odile's hurt," she said. "Boiler seam let go near the firebox. She threw herself over it." A muscle worked in her jaw. "Steam took her arm. She's alive. She's telling me not to stop, which is the most Odile sentence ever spoken, but I can't work engine and wheel both, and I can't hold us here, because here—" she nodded at the water ahead— "is where boats die."

The current took the stern and swung it, casual as a hand moving a chess piece. Ines caught it. Barely.

"I've never run this," I said. It came out smaller than I wanted. "I've never even seen it except from the bluff."

"You've steered everything I've thrown at you since you were twelve." She didn't look at me. She was reading the water the way other people read scripture, lips almost moving. "And I can't throw you off this boat today, because there's no today unless someone steers. Take the wheel. Hands where mine are. Do what I say and nothing else."

I put my hands on the wheel. Hers stayed on top of mine for three heartbeats — I counted them, God help me, I counted them — and then they were gone and she was down the ladder and the boat was mine.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about steering white water: it isn't like steering at all. On open water you point the boat where you want to go. In the Sisters, the river points you, and your whole job is to disagree with it slowly enough, and precisely enough, and at exactly the right moments, that it doesn't kill you for arguing.

Ines's voice came up the tube in short iron sentences. *Starboard thirty. Ease. Hard port — now.* The wheel fought me like a live animal. The first standing wave took the bow and lifted it, and for one long, weightless second the *Maren Voss* was flying, and I could see down the throat of the rapid — white on white on white, and a dark seam threading through it that Ines kept calling like a rosary: *the seam, find the seam, starboard ten, there.*

We hit things. I don't have a complete inventory. Rocks, certainly. Once, something that groaned — a snag or a drowned tree or the rib of some earlier boat's ambition. The boiler gauge, visible through the engine room door Ines had propped open, climbed into numbers I'd been taught meant run. Steam screamed out of a relief valve in a white plume that the wind tore apart and threw back in my face, hot as breath.

Somewhere in the middle third of the rapid, the river did the thing the stories all warned about. It split us. One channel went left around a fang of granite; one went right, narrower, meaner, half-hidden under overhanging ice-slick rock. Ines's voice said *left*, and the current said right, and for the length of one swallowed heartbeat I understood that I was alone up here, that no voice through a tube could reach my hands fast enough anymore, that this was the moment every pilot eventually meets when the river asks who is actually driving.

I drove.

Right channel. I don't know that I chose it so much as committed to it — hauled the wheel over, gave the boat to the water, and steered the disagreement one wave at a time. The overhang scraped the hurricane roof with a sound like a giant drawing a nail down slate. Something below decks crashed. The wheel bucked hard enough to skin my palms, and I held it anyway, because letting go wasn't a thing my hands would do, and I remember being surprised by that — surprised that my own body had decided, before my head did, that this boat was mine to keep.

Then the channel bent, and dropped, and ended, and the Sisters spat us out into long green eddy-water as suddenly as a cat dropping a mouse. Alive. Moving. Upriver.

My legs quit. I sat down right there on the pilothouse floor, in the coal dust and the broken glass from the gauge lamp, and shook.

Ines came up the ladder with Odile's good arm over her shoulders. Odile's left sleeve was gone above the elbow, wrapped in wadding and tar-cloth, her face gray as the glacial water, and she looked at me slumped on the floorboards and said, in a voice scorched rough:

"He took the right channel."

"I know," Ines said.

"Nobody takes the right channel."

"I know," Ines said again, and there was something in her voice I'd never heard aimed at me before. Not pride exactly. Recognition. The way you look at a boat that has just proven it will float.

* * *

We tied up that evening in a fold of the canyon where the water went quiet and the walls rose sheer on both sides, red stone streaked with white birdlime, close enough together that the stars came down a slot. Odile slept in the cabin under a blanket soaked in willow-bark tea, her bandaged arm propped on a pillow, refusing with impressive venom to be called brave. Ines sat with her a long time. I stood my first proper anchor watch alone, on the bow, listening to the river argue with itself in the dark.

That was when I heard the noise.

It came from aft — a soft, rhythmic knocking, too regular for the river, too careful for cargo shifting. I told myself it was the rudder stock. I told myself it was cooling metal. Then I remembered that the lazarette hatch had been dogged shut when we cast off from the toll town, and it was open now, and open from inside means someone opened it.

I went back along the deck quiet as I knew how, which was quiet enough, because docks teach you that. I put my hand on the hatch cover. I counted to three, for no reason except that three felt like courage.

I threw it open.

A woman was curled in the dark among our spare line and tar pots, and she moved faster than anyone I'd ever seen move — up and armed in the same motion, a knife low and ready, her eyes finding mine in the lamplight with a flat professional calm that frightened me more than the blade did. Republic uniform trousers under a plain coat. Short hair going gray at the temples. A sergeant's bearing, worn like a second skeleton.

"Don't," she said, when I drew breath to shout. "You'll wake the captain, and then one of us does something we'll both regret, and I'd rather not. I've been aboard six days, boy. If I'd wanted you dead, you'd never have known I existed."

Six days. Through the narrows. Through the sandbar. Through the toll town, where I'd lain awake wondering if we'd be betrayed. She'd been under our feet the whole time, eating our food when the watch turned, breathing our air, and I felt the violation of it crawl up my neck like ice water.

"The chest," I said. "You're after the chest."

Something crossed her face — too quick to name, gone before I could read it. "Am I?"

"Ines!" I shouted anyway. Because whatever else she was, she was a stranger with a knife on Ines's boat, and there are limits to curiosity.

* * *

They stood her against the pilothouse wall while Ines searched her, and she let it happen with the patience of someone being delayed rather than caught. They found the knife, a folded paper of identity papers stamped with the Republic's triple tower, and nothing else — no orders, no warrant, no letter of commission.

"Sergeant Corla Vael," she said, when Ines asked. "Fourth Border Regiment, retired eight years. Currently employed by no one you'd believe."

"You're Republic army," Ines said.

"I was born in Vessany," Vael said, "which makes me whatever the Republic says I am until the day it stops saying it. And I boarded your boat in the toll town because the people I answer to asked me to make sure the boy reached the source alive." Her eyes came to me, and they weren't cruel. That was somehow worse. "Whether he wants to be reached or not."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, Odile turned over in her sleep and moaned once.

"And if he doesn't want to be?" Ines said quietly.

Vael looked at her for a long moment. "Then we're all on the wrong boat, Captain Eastwick. Aren't we."

Nobody answered her. Nobody had to. Outside, the river ran on toward the gorge, and the glacier country ahead stood white under the stars, waiting nine days — no, eight now — to close its single door for another year.
