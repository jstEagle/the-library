## 7. The Stowaway's Truth

The river gave them a night of mercy at Grayfall Bend — a slough behind a drowned willow island where the current went soft as spilled milk and the mosquitoes were the only thing hunting them. Ines tied off the *Maren Voss* with two lines and a prayer, banked the boiler to a whisper, and posted herself in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low.

She had not told Quentin yet that she'd found the hatch rope cut and re-tied with a soldier's knot. She had found it three days ago, above the Two Sisters tolls, and she had said nothing, because saying it aloud would make the thing real: someone had been living in her boat's belly like a tick, through the narrows, through the sandbar run, through all of it.

Odile knew. Odile always knew. The engineer had simply begun carrying a wrench within reach of her hand at all hours, which was Odile's way of filing a formal complaint.

Ines waited until the boy was asleep in the pilothouse bunk — she could hear him breathing through the open door, that deep, unguarded rhythm he'd had since he was six years old and afraid of thunder — and then she went down into the hold alone, lamp in one hand, boathook in the other.

The stowaway was sitting up against the coal bin, waiting for her.

"You knew," Sergeant Vael said. Not a question. Her voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had rehearsed this conversation on a long cold ride inside a hull. "The knot. You've known since the tolls."

"I wanted to see what you'd do." Ines set the lamp on a crate between them, so the light fell on both their faces and neither's hands. "You've had three days to put a knife in my boy or scuttle us in the worst water on the river. Instead you bailed bilge during the rapids. So either you're very patient, or you're not what I thought."

"And what did you think?"

"That you were Republic," Ines said. "That you'd let us do the hard part — find the gorge, find the source — and then signal the cutters waiting downstream."

Vael almost smiled. It was a tired thing, worn thin at the edges. "There are no cutters downstream. There is no one downstream. Captain Eastwick, I have been running ahead of my own government since Corren Landing." She drew something from inside her jacket — slowly, showing her fingers the whole way — and laid it on the crate.

A coin. Old silver, black with age, but the stamp was legible under the lamplight: a bridge with seven arches, and beneath it, in the old script, *VESANYA*.

Ines had seen that coin exactly once before, in a ledger drawer, pressed flat between pages her father had never let her read.

"Seven-arch bridge," Vael said. "It stood in the capital until the treaty engineers blew the levees. My grandmother carried four of these out of the flood in her shoe. They're worthless now — except to us." She looked up, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "I'm not Republic. I was born Vessani, in a camp above the snowline, in a country that exists only on paper my grandparents kept dry in oilcloth. And I was sent to find you."

"To find me?"

"To find the chest. To find him." Vael's gaze flicked upward, toward the pilothouse, toward the sound of sleeping breath. "Quentin. The heir."

* * *

Ines sat down on the coal bin across from her, because her legs had opinions about standing.

"Tell it plain," she said. "I've had enough riddles for one season."

"Plain, then." Vael folded her hands like a woman settling in for a confession she'd practiced against a mirror. "There are eleven thousand of us left. Less, now. Camps in the high valleys, enclaves in three foreign ports, families scattered so thin the name Vessani is mostly a recipe and a lullaby. For forty years we've had nothing — no land, no papers, no claim the Republic honors. And every year the Republic's surveyors push a little higher into the valleys, and every year another camp is moved along, because we are squatters on land that used to be ours and the maps say so."

"The crown lands," Ines said quietly.

"The crown lands. The one parcel the treaty never extinguished — a technicality, a lawyer's ghost, but a real one. If the rightful heir stands on that ground and claims it, before witnesses, with proof of blood—" Vael tapped the crate, beneath which the lead-lined chest sat like a sleeping animal, "—then the exiles have a legal foothold. A nation again. Small. Poor. But *ours*, and on paper no republic can burn."

"And they sent one sergeant in a stolen uniform to do it."

"They sent me because I volunteered, and because I don't look Vessani anymore. Forty years does that. I look like anyone." Vael's mouth twisted. "And because the council argued for a year about whether crowning a boy raised on foreign docks was salvation or farce, and while they argued, the calving season came. So I came alone. I was supposed to make contact at Two Sisters. I missed the rendezvous when your boat cast off early, and by then the only way to follow you was inside your hull."

Ines looked at her a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, the willow island creaked against its own roots.

"You could still be lying," Ines said. "You could be the best liar the Republic ever built."

"I could be." Vael didn't flinch. "But here's what I can't lie about, Captain, because it's the reason I volunteered, and it's the reason you should throw me off this boat if you want to — and I'll go, I won't fight you." She leaned forward into the light. "Your father's ledgers. Have you read the convoy entries? The ones from the last spring of the flood year?"

The river seemed to go quiet around the hull. Even the mosquitoes, Ines thought later, had held their breath.

"There was a convoy," Vael said, when Ines said nothing. "Eleven boats. The last refugees out of the lowlands, after the treaty was signed and the levee charges were already set. They paid smugglers to take them upstream past the inspection points. Your father ran the middle stretch. He logged every boat that passed his station — and the Republic paid him for those logs, because the logs told the gunboats exactly which hulls to stop, which ones carried people instead of cargo." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Nine of the eleven were taken at the Sorrow Narrows. The passengers were registered, processed, dispersed. Most of them died in the resettlement camps within five years. The two that made it through carried infants. One infant was listed in the manifest of a certain lead-lined chest."

Ines heard her own heartbeat. She heard Quentin breathing above her, slow and trusting, and the two sounds refused to fit inside the same world.

"The manifest," she said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "The registry page. His name was on it."

"He was the last boat's cargo," Vael said gently. "Your father took the Republic's money for nine boats' worth of blood, and then — I don't know why, Captain, no one knows why — he let the tenth through. He falsified one entry. He wrote *lost with all hands* on a boat that reached your docks, and he put a baby in the arms of whoever was standing there, and he never spoke of it again for the rest of his life."

Ines thought of her father's hands. Big knuckles, river-scarred, patient with knots and children alike. She thought of the ledger drawer he'd locked, and the locked drawer inside it, and the way he'd looked at Quentin sometimes across the dockyard — that long, unreadable look she'd taken for mere fondness, or guilt over some small debt, and which now reassembled itself in her memory into something else entirely.

He had known. All those years, hauling the boy toward a pilot's license, teaching him the river the way you teach someone the only country you can give them — he had known exactly whose son he was saving, and whose daughter he was making complicit just by letting her love the child.

"Say the rest," Ines said.

Vael hesitated. "Captain—"

"*Say it.*"

"The ninth boat. The one that stopped at the Sorrow Narrows the same night your father's log pointed the gunboats at it." Vael's eyes didn't leave hers. "The manifest says the family aboard was named Vael."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the hatch. Ines sat with it. Nine boats. One logbook. A father who had sold nine boats and saved one child, and the arithmetic of that would never balance, not in any currency the living spent.

"I didn't tell you to wound you," Vael said quietly. "I told you because you deserve to know what you're carrying upriver. Not just the chest. Him. He's going to stand on that mountain and be asked to be a king by people who will use his name as a weapon, and when the Republic comes — and they will come — they will dig up everything, including your father's ledgers, and they will hand the boy the story of how he was bought and bartered before he could walk. I'd rather he hear it from this crew than from an interrogator."

"You'd rather he hear it from *me*," Ines said.

"Yes," Vael said. "Because whatever else is true, he trusts you. And trust is the only crown he actually owns."

* * *

Ines climbed back up into the night with the lamp guttering and her hands steady in the way that meant nothing in her was steady at all.

She made it three steps across the deck.

Quentin was leaning against the pilothouse doorframe, arms crossed, barefoot on the cold boards, and his face in the starlight was the face of a man watching a building come down.

"How much," she said.

"All of it." His voice was very quiet. "From the coin. Maybe from before the coin — I woke when you went below and I couldn't sleep anyway, and the hold carries sound." He pushed off the doorframe. "Nine boats, Ines. Your father's log. My mother's boat."

"Quentin—"

"Was she on it?" He stepped closer, and his eyes were bright and terrible. "Sergeant Vael's family. Was my rescue the reason her people—"

"Don't." Ines reached for him and he stepped back, and her hand closed on river air. "Don't do that arithmetic tonight. No one survives that arithmetic."

"Somebody has to!" It cracked out of him louder than he meant, and somewhere in the willows a bird went up in a clatter of wings. "Everyone keeps deciding things *for* me — the exiles, the Republic, your father, you — everyone decides, and I get carried. Downriver in a chest, upriver in a boat, through rapids I didn't pick. Well, I'm done being cargo." He was shaking. Nineteen years old and shaking on her deck, with the whole dark river listening. "Tomorrow we run the gorge. Fine. But you don't touch the wheel, and you don't touch the truth, and you don't decide for me ever again. Do you understand me, Captain?"

The title landed like a slap. *Captain.* Not Ines. Not the name he'd called her since he was six and she'd fished him out of a live well.

"I understand you," she said.

He stood there one more moment, jaw working, and then he turned and went up into the pilothouse and shut the door, and through the glass she watched him sit down at the wheel and put his hands on it, ten years too early and twenty years too late, gripping the spokes like a drowning man grips a spar.

Behind her, from the hatch, Sergeant Vael climbed quietly into the night and stood beside her, and neither of them spoke.

Above the eastern ridge, past the black teeth of the gorge, the glacier caught the first gray light and burned cold and white — and Ines saw, even at this distance, even with the naked eye, a crack of blue fire opening along its face.

Calving season had begun.
