## 8. Into the Gorge

The gorge announced itself before we saw it — a cold that came down the river like a hand closing around the throat of the water. Odile felt it first in the boiler, said the steam was running sullen, and I felt it in my teeth, a high thin ache like biting foil. Then the walls rose out of the morning mist on either side of us, blue-white and ancient, and the river narrowed to something hardly wider than the *Marrow's Luck* herself.

"Calving season," Ines said from behind me. Her voice was flat, working-voice, the voice she used when feeling would only get in the way. "We've got hours. Maybe six. After that the face lets go and nothing floats through here but slush."

I didn't answer her. I hadn't answered her in two days, not with anything that mattered. I gave her the wheel's heading instead, hands where hers should have been, and watched the water.

The channel split three ways below the first bend. The left ran smooth as poured glass and just as treacherous — glass meant shallow, meant rock wearing a skin of meltwater. The right churned white around a drowned boulder field. The middle was the worst of both: fast, deep, and threaded with drift ice riding down from the face above, chunks the size of coffins turning slow circles in the current.

I took the middle.

"You'll want to feather the port paddle when we come to the narrows," Ines said. "The eddy line will grab—"

"I know."

She went quiet. Through the engine-room hatch behind me I heard Odile say nothing at all, which was its own kind of commentary, and then the steady knock-knock-knock of her stoking iron, keeping her opinions where they belonged, in the firebox.

The truth was I *didn't* know this stretch. Nobody alive knew it well; the gorge changed every season, shrugged off its old channels and cut new ones the way a sleeper turns in bed. What I knew was what Vael had told me the night before, hunched over Ines's father's charts by lamplight while Ines slept — or pretended to sleep — in the stern cabin. What I knew was what the river itself was telling me now, through the soles of my boots and the tremor in the wheel and the smell of the air off the ice, which smelled like nothing, like absence, like the inside of a stone.

So I read it fresh. Every second of it, fresh.

* * *

The first ice came down an hour in.

There was no warning you'd have believed. A sound like a door slamming somewhere far above, echoing off the walls until you couldn't tell direction or distance, and then a shadow sliding down the blue face of the glacier, and then the crack — a report like a cannon, like the sky splitting — and then the fall. A slab the size of a hay barn sheared away and hit the river maybe four hundred yards ahead of us, and the wave it threw came back up the channel to meet us standing up, green and tall as a fence.

"Hard aport!" I shouted, though there was no one to shout it for. My own hands were already doing it. "Odile — full astern, NOW—"

The wave took us broadside anyway. The *Luck* climbed it like a thing alive, groaning, the cargo shifting below with a sound like furniture in a flood, and for one long suspended moment I looked straight down into the trough and saw the rocks waiting there, fanged with old rope and older timber, the bones of everyone who'd ever misjudged this water. Then we came down the far side and the hull slapped flat and we were through, wallowing, shipping water over the gunwales in bright sheets.

Ines had braced herself against the pilothouse rail. She was looking at me. Not at the river — at me — and there was something in her face I couldn't bear, some mixture of pride and grief and the apology she still hadn't spoken, and I turned back to the wheel because the wheel was honest. The wheel only asked what I could give it.

"Port side," Odile called up through the hatch, coughing steam-smell. "We're taking water. Slow. Seepage around a seam, maybe worse."

"How much worse?"

"Ask me again in ten minutes."

* * *

It was worse.

The wave hadn't opened the seam; it had woken an old wound, some scar from a grounding years before Ines ever owned the boat, and now the river was finding its way in along the grain of the hull like ink along a blotter. We tied off in an eddy behind a shelf of rock — the only calm water for miles, a pocket the glacier seemed to have forgotten about — and Odile got the pump going while Ines crawled the bilge with a lantern, reading the leak the way she read everything, by touch and long experience.

I stood at the wheel and did not help. It wasn't spite, exactly. It was that helping meant standing next to her, and standing next to her meant hearing her breathe, and hearing her breathe meant remembering the night Vael's voice came out of the dark hold and said the words I couldn't unhear:

*Your captain's father sold the convoy. Marked which boats carried refugees. The Republic paid him per sinking.*

*Your boat is built on that money.*

I had grown up on these decks. I had learned letters tracing the faded paint of her name. Ines had pulled me out of the spring flood when I was nine, gone in after me without a rope, without telling anyone, and held me above the water with arms like capstans while the river tried to take us both. None of that was a lie. That was the unbearable part. None of it was a lie, and none of it canceled the ledgers either, and the two truths sat inside me like stones in a mill, grinding.

"We can't stop here long," Vael said beside me. She'd come up quiet, the way she did everything. She was watching the glacier face with a soldier's flat attention. "That shelf won't hold if the calving starts early. And the exiles' scouts watch this reach — if we're seen anchored, the Republic's cutter upstream of us hears about it by nightfall."

"The cutter." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Of course there's a cutter."

"There's always a cutter," Vael said. "Question is whether we're seaworthy enough to outrun one."

Below, Ines's lantern light stopped moving. A long moment, then her voice, muffled through the deck: "Quentin. Get down here."

* * *

The breach was the size of a fist. Not a seep after all — the wave had driven a splinter of glacier ice, or half a rock, clean through the outer planking between two frames, and the river came through it in a bright arcing rope, hissing where it hit the hot bilge boards. Ines crouched over it with her sleeve already soaked, pressing her palm flat against the hole as if she could hold the whole river back by stubbornness alone.

"I need it plugged and patched from outside," she said. "Water pressure's doing half our work for us — someone goes over the side, drives a tapered plug in, nails a patch plate over it. Two minutes' work in a shipyard. Down here it's two minutes in freezing water against the current."

"I'll go," Vael said immediately.

"No," I said.

They both looked at me.

"It's my boat to work," I said, though it wasn't, it was hers, it had always been hers, and saying it felt like theft and like inheritance at once. "You're the one who knows how long the calving gives us. You watch the face. If it starts, you scream."

Ines's hand was still pressed to the hole. Water ran between her fingers, over her wrist, dripping off her elbow into the bilge. She looked at me for a long time, and whatever she was going to say — *be careful*, or *I'm sorry*, or any of the words that had been stacking up unspoken between us like drift ice — she swallowed all of it and only nodded once, pilot to crew, and that nod hurt worse than anything she could have said.

I stripped to my shirt. Odile found the plug — a softwood taper, tarred, kept for exactly this since before I was born — and the patch plate and a hammer wrapped in cloth so it wouldn't ring against the hull and carry downstream to ears we didn't want listening.

The water took my breath before it took my heat. I'd swum the river in June; this was different, this was river fed by a glacier's heart, and it closed around me like a jaw. I went down the side hand over hand on the lifeline, boots finding the hull's strakes, and the current pulled at my legs like hands, patient and enormously strong, wanting to peel me off and add me to the collection of bones on the bottom.

The breach was easy to find. I put my palm over it and felt the river pushing through, a cold pulse like something's heartbeat, and I thought, absurdly, *this is the same water that carried me downriver as a baby.* The same river. The chest had crossed it too, lead-lined, sealed, carrying my name in a dead language. Everything I was had come down this water, and now I hung off the side of a stolen boat plugging a hole in it with a wooden taper, and it was the truest work I had ever done.

First try, the plug skated sideways. Second try, the current shoved me and I lost my grip on the lifeline and went under — one long green silent second, rocks dim below me, the world tilted — before my hand found the line again and I hauled myself back gasping.

Third try. I set the taper square, and drove it home with the heel of my hand, and felt the flow choke, stutter, die to a trickle. Then the patch plate, hammering blind by feel, each blow cushioned in cloth, my fingers past feeling, hammering until Odile's voice came down through the hull itself, vibrating through the wood into my skull: *"Bilge's gaining! It's holding! Come up, you idiot, COME UP—"*

I came up.

* * *

Vael screamed eleven minutes later.

Not the word *scream* — a single hard shout, one syllable, *NOW*, and I was already at the wheel with my hands wet and shaking, throwing us out of the eddy and into the main channel as the glacier let go above us.

It didn't fall so much as exhale. The whole lower face of it slid at once, tons upon tons of blue ice, and the sound was not a sound but a condition, a thing that lived in the chest rather than the ears. The river stood up. White water rolled down the gorge toward us like a road collapsing, and I steered into it, because Ines had taught me — had taught all of us — that you never run from a wave, you run *through* it, bow-on, throttle open, meeting it the way you meet anything that means to kill you.

Behind me, at the engine hatch, I heard Ines's voice for the first time in two days saying something that wasn't an order. I didn't turn around. I couldn't spare the look, and I wasn't ready for the words.

But I heard them. *Hold together, love. Hold together.* Whether she meant the boat or me, I took it for both.

We punched through the surge, buried to the rails, and came out the other side into light — the gorge opening ahead like a door, the walls falling back, and beyond them, high and impossibly blue in the afternoon sun, a lake no chart had ever bothered to name.

And on its far shore, small with distance but unmistakable, smoke. Cookfires. Dozens of them.

The exiles had seen us coming.
