## 9. The Source

The gorge let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has decided not to crush.

One moment the walls were still closing, blue ice leaning in from both sides with the groan of a ship settling; the next, the channel widened, the current gentled, and the *Magpie's Debt* slid out onto water so flat and so high that it seemed less like a lake than like the sky had come down to lie on the earth and think. The air was thin and clean enough to hurt. Behind them, the glacier calved — a sound like a door slamming in a house the size of the world — and the season sealed itself behind them.

Ines stood at the wheel a long time before she trusted her hands to leave it.

They were through. Whatever waited on this water, no Republic cutter could follow until spring.

The high lake was older than any map she'd owned. Her father had spoken of it once, drunk, the only time he'd ever spoken of Vessany at all: *a lake that holds the sky so well you can't tell which is drowning*. She understood now what he'd meant. The water lay mirror-still, doubling the peaks, doubling the pale sun, and somewhere ahead — where the shoreline bent around a shelf of grey stone — smoke rose in thin, deliberate columns. Not campfire smoke. Signal smoke. Someone had been watching the gorge for them.

"Odile says we've maybe an hour of boiler left," Quentin said, coming up beside her. His knuckles were scabbed where the wheel had beaten them in the last rapids, and there was glacier-water still frozen in his hair, but he stood like a pilot now. He'd earned that stance in blood over four days of white water, and nothing that happened on this lake could take it back from him. "She wants to know if we're anchoring or landing."

Ines looked at the smoke. "Landing," she said. "We didn't come all this way to sit at anchor."

"No," he said quietly. "I don't suppose we did."

* * *

The exile camp was smaller than she expected and older than anything else she had ever seen built by human hands. Stone terraces cut into the hillside above the shore, roofed in turf and slate, connected by stairs worn hollow in their centers by generations of feet. Forty years since the flood took Vessany below the treaty line, and these people had climbed here every summer since, hauling stones, keeping fires, keeping faith with a country that existed now only as a word and a wound.

They came down to the water when the boat touched the shingle — perhaps sixty of them, old men in coats cut in a fashion forty years out of date, women with braids bound in red cord, children who had been born on this bare shelf of the world and had never seen the drowned cities they sang about. And at their head, a woman with a silver chain of office around her neck and eyes like the lake itself: deep, cold, and holding everything upside down.

"Quentin Vessany," she said, and her voice carried across the water without effort. "Son of Aldric. Last of the line."

Quentin went very still beside Ines. "That's not my name," he said. "It's just the one in a chest."

"It is the name your mother bled to keep," the woman said. "I am Chancellor Maren. I held you once, when you were three days old, in a cellar in Kolvess while the water came up the streets." She extended both hands, palms open. "Welcome home."

*Home.* The word landed on the shingle between them like something dropped from a height.

Ines watched Quentin look at those open hands, at the terraces, at the children staring at him as if he were weather they'd been promised. She watched him reach out — and stop, halfway, his hand hanging in the thin cold air — and turn instead to look at her.

Not for permission. For truth. That was worse, and better.

* * *

They gave them a hall: a long low room dug into the hillside, its ceiling black with forty years of hearth smoke, its walls hung with tapestries that had been carried up the mountain folded in flour sacks. They fed them barley soup and dark bread and fish from the lake, and Odile ate like a woman making up for nearly dying, and Sergeant Vael sat among her own people for the first time in eleven years and cried without noise, the way soldiers do.

And then, when the bowls were cleared, Chancellor Maren unrolled a map across the trestle table, weighted at the corners with river stones, and Ines saw exactly what kind of homecoming this was going to be.

The map showed the treaty line — the flood line — in red. It showed the drowned valleys of Vessany beneath the reservoir the Republic called Lake Meridian. And it showed, inked fresh, arrows. Landing points. Supply routes. A list of names under the heading *WITNESSES TO THE RESTORATION*, and at the top of that list, in a careful clerk's hand: **Q. VESSANY, HEIR OF ALDRIC**.

"The crown lands are not drowned," Maren said. Her finger traced the high country above the reservoir. "The treaty took the valleys. It could not take the mountains. By law — by *any* law, theirs or ours — the crown lands pass to the heir. If Quentin stands on that ground and claims them, the claim is real. Documented. Witnessed by every exile nation's consul we can summon before the ice closes." She looked up, and her voice softened into something that was almost gentle, which made it worse. "The Republic will have to negotiate. Or fight. Either way, boy, your name buys our people back their country."

*Your name buys.* Ines heard the transaction in it, plain as a toll gate. She'd spent her life on a river where everything had a price posted somewhere, and she knew currency when she heard it.

Quentin was quiet a long moment, looking at the map, at the red line, at the little villages marked along the drowned shore. "How many people live on the reservoir now?" he asked.

Maren blinked. "Republic settlers. Farmers. Perhaps—"

"How many?"

"Twelve thousand. Perhaps fifteen."

"And if the claim forces a war?"

The chancellor's silence lasted two seconds too long, and everyone in the hall heard it.

"We would hope," she said, "for a settlement."

"You'd hope," Quentin repeated. Not cruelly. Just weighing it, the way he weighed current before committing the boat to a channel. Ines felt something move in her chest — pride, fierce and complicated — because he wasn't dazzled. Nineteen years old, three days off the worst water of his life, and he wasn't dazzled.

Maren turned then, deliberately, to Ines. "You brought him. You ran the gorge for him. Captain Eastwick, the exiles owe you a debt no ledger holds. Speak to him. He trusts you. Tell him what his mother would have wanted."

And there it was. The hook under the bait.

* * *

He found her at the waterline after full dark, where the lake lay so still that the stars went all the way down and the boat seemed to float on night itself. She was sitting on the shingle with her boots off, letting the cold bite her ankles, an old habit from low-water summers when the river was the only honest thing in her life.

He sat down beside her. For a while neither of them said anything. That was the thing about the two of them — they'd learned each other's silences the way pilots learn channels, and this one had room in it for everything.

"What would you do?" he asked finally. "If it was yours. The name. The claim. All of it."

Ines watched a star's reflection tremble on the black water and break, and reform.

"I'll tell you a true thing first," she said. "Since you asked for true things. About my father."

Quentin turned his head. She kept her eyes on the water.

"He didn't just log which boats sank for the Republic. I told you that much, back on the river, and it was true as far as it went. But the last convoy — the one Vael's people talk about, the one that went down in the narrows with two hundred souls aboard — my father didn't just fail to warn them. He sold the route. Gave the Republic gunners the window and the channel marks. There was payment in his ledger, page after page, and the last entry is dated nine days after the convoy sank. He banked it, Quentin. He banked the money for the dead, and then he came home and taught me rope knots and river law and how to read water, and he never once — not once in twenty years — said the word *Vessany* in this house." Her voice held steady, mostly. "And the chest you came in? The mark on the manifest? That's his delivery stamp. He smuggled you out. He saved you. Both things are true about the same man, and I have spent my whole life trying to make them fit in one pair of hands, and I will tell you what I've learned."

She finally looked at him. His face was pale in the starlight, and very young, and very steady.

"They don't fit. You don't get to settle the account. You don't get to pay it off or drown it or hand it to somebody else to carry. All you can do is decide, every day, what *you're* going to be worth. My father chose the ledger. Maren chose the map. The Republic chose the flood." She reached over and put her hand on his shoulder — the shoulder that had taken the wheel in the worst water either of them had ever seen. "So when you ask me what I'd do — I'd do what I'm doing right now. I'd sit here. On this shore. With the people who actually pulled me out of the water. Because a throne is a story other people tell about you, Quentin, and a family is a thing you steer together, and I know which one keeps you alive."

He was quiet for a long time. Out on the lake, a loon called — that wild, rising, unanswered cry — and the whole inverted sky seemed to listen.

"My mother," he said at last, "bled to keep my name. Maren said that. And I believe her. But she bled to keep *me*, Ines. The name was just the rope she tied to me so someone might haul me out." He laughed softly, and it cracked a little in the middle. "I spent nineteen years thinking I was nobody found on a dock. Turns out I was somebody, and everybody wants a piece of the finding. The Republic wants me erased. The exiles want me crowned. Nobody — " he stopped. Started again. "Nobody until you ever just wanted *me* to come to breakfast."

"That's not true," Ines said. "Odile complains when you don't."

That got the laugh all the way out of him, ragged and real, and then he wiped his face with the heel of his scabbed hand and stood up, and she saw that he had already decided — had probably decided somewhere in the gorge, or on the sandbar, or the moment Maren's finger traced the red line — and had only needed to say it out loud to someone who wouldn't spend him.

"Then tomorrow," Quentin said, "I'm going to stand up in front of all of them and tell them the truth. That I'm grateful. That I'll carry the name like a flag they can look at whenever they need to remember who they are. But I won't be spent. Not for a war plan, not for a land claim, not for a dead king's chair." He looked down at her, and the starlight was in his eyes, and he was grinning — actually grinning, the dock-kid grin she'd known since he was nine years old stealing oranges off her deck. "I'm going to tell them their heir chooses the river."

Ines pulled her boots on and stood beside him, and the relief that went through her was so total it was almost grief — grief for the girl who'd inherited her father's ledgers and never been allowed to choose anything freely herself.

"Chancellor Maren is going to call that treason," she said.

"Maren can call it whatever she likes." Quentin turned toward the hall, toward the firelight spilling down the terraces, toward sixty exiles and a crown waiting in a wooden case and a war plan drawn in fresh ink. "But we should go now. Before she reads the map again."

He started up the shingle. Ines followed — and stopped, one boot on the lowest stair, because across the black mirror of the lake, far out where no boat should be, a light had appeared. One lantern. Then a second. Running lights, low and fast, moving against the current that couldn't possibly carry them.

Someone had run the gorge behind them.

Someone had come up through the closing ice.
