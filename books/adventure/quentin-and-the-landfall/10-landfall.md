## 10. Landfall

The lake at the source of the river had no name on any chart Ines had ever owned. It lay above the tree line, a sheet of green glass held in the glacier's stone hand, so still that the boat seemed to float on the sky itself. The exiles' camp stood on the far shore — cookfires, canvas, a banner stitched from forty years of longing — and between the camp and the *Magpie's Rest* stretched half a mile of water that Ines intended to fill with everything they'd carried.

Quentin stood at the rail with the chest open at his feet. He had not slept. None of them had. The crown sat on top of the regalia like a thing asleep, its old gold gone brown at the seams, and beside it the documents in their oilcloth: the birth record, the land grants, the manifest with her father's delivery mark pressed into the lead like a brand.

"You don't have to do this alone," Ines said.

"I'm not doing it alone." Quentin lifted the crown. It was lighter than it looked. He turned it over once, twice, studying it the way he studied anything — a fouled winch, a jammed lock gate — as if it were a problem that could be understood by handling. "I used to think if I knew where I came from, I'd finally know who I was." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "But I already knew who I was. You just never told me where you found me."

"The river found you," Ines said. "We just kept what it gave us."

He nodded slowly, and then, without ceremony, without prayer, he dropped the crown into the lake.

It went down without a sound. The water closed over it the way water closes over everything — completely, indifferently, keeping what it is given. Quentin fed the documents after it, one bundle at a time, watching each one sink. Last came the chest itself, lid open, empty now except for the smell of lead and forty drowned years. He tipped it over the side and let it fall.

On the far shore, someone began to shout. A figure ran down to the waterline — Vael, her sergeant's coat traded for exile wool, her arms raised, first in fury and then, as the ripples reached her, in something else. She did not wade in after it. She stood a long time, a small dark figure against the ice, and then she raised one hand — not farewell, Ines thought, but acknowledgment. A salute between people who had both lost something and both chosen it.

"Let's go home," Quentin said.

Ines took the wheel. Behind them the lake held its silence, and beneath it, somewhere in the cold green dark, a nation's proof slept where no republic could burn it and no exile could spend it. Let them make their claim on nothing. Let the boy be a boy, and the river keep its secrets.

She rang the engine bell twice, and Odile answered from below, and the *Magpie's Rest* turned her stern on history.

* * *

The gorge had changed while they were gone.

The calving had begun. From two miles off they could hear it — a sound like artillery, like the sky cracking along a seam — and when they entered the shadow of the ice walls, the air itself was falling. Chunks of glacier the size of cottages dropped from the seracs and struck the water with white detonations, and the swell of each impact rolled upriver to meet them, lifting the boat and slamming her down again.

"Odile!" Ines shouted over the din. "Everything she has!"

"Everything she has is about to be nothing!" Odile bellowed back, but the boiler roared anyway, and the paddles bit, and they drove upstream against the current of displaced water toward the narrows where the channel threaded between walls of blue ice older than any nation on either bank.

Quentin took the wheel without being asked. Ines let him. That was the whole of it now — the trust made visible, handed back and forth like a tool. He read the water the way she'd taught him, reading the way she'd learned from her father, who had learned it running boats for men he betrayed, and the chain of hands went on past all of them into whatever came next.

A wall of ice calved directly ahead.

The wave it sent was a moving hill, ten feet of green water standing up out of the channel, and Quentin did the only thing there was to do — he turned the bow into it and gave the engine everything, and the *Magpie's Rest* climbed the face of the wave like a creature deciding to fly. For one full second they hung at the crest, the whole gorge laid out below them, the far exit a thread of light between the ice — and then the bow came down and buried itself to the hatches and the world became cold and roaring and white.

They surfaced. They were still moving. Somewhere below, Odile was screaming at the boiler in three languages, and the paddles were still turning, and the light ahead was widening.

"Ines!" Quentin's voice, cracked and laughing and terrified all at once. "Is this how you measure a year?"

"No," she shouted back, gripping the wheel beside him, salt spray freezing on her sleeves though this high up it was only meltwater. "This is how we measure a day!"

They shot the last of the narrows as the gorge sealed behind them — a final calving, vast and slow and almost gentle, the glacier shrugging an entire cliff face into the channel they had just quit. The concussion lifted the stern, spun her half around, and set her down in open water pointing the wrong way, listing, smoking gently from the stack, alive.

Ines looked back once. The ice had closed like a door. Whatever was written in the ledgers of the world, no one would ever read that page again.

* * *

The republic's cutter found them three days downstream, drifting with a cracked paddle wheel and a boiler coughing rust, and boarded them with rifles and paperwork. The inspection lasted four hours. The senior agent was a thin man named Corvess who had been chasing the chest for eleven years, and when he finished going through every crate, every bunk, every seam of the hold, he stood on her deck in the rain and looked at Ines with an expression close to grief.

"It was real," he said. "You had it. And you threw it in a lake."

"I'm a river captain," Ines said. "I put things in rivers. It's most of the job."

Corvess stared at her a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, exhausted bark — and signed the release papers on her wheelhouse desk. "For the record, Captain Eastwick, your father would have logged this boat as sunk."

"My father," Ines said, "logged a lot of things wrong."

They limped home under jury rig, and the river took them the rest of the way like it had missed them.

* * *

Spring came late that year, and when it came it came all at once, the way it always does on big water — willows going gold-green overnight, the ice rotting out of the shallows with a sound like breaking bread, the docks at Harrow Bend waking up smelling of tar and fish and cut pine.

Quentin passed his pilot's examination on the second Tuesday of May. The examiner was a gray-whiskered man from the River Authority who had failed him once before on principle, and this time he shook Quentin's hand with both of his own and said, "You handle her like you were born to it," and Quentin laughed, because he had been, more or less, and because the joke belonged to him now instead of weighing on him.

Ines watched from the wheelhouse of the *Magpie's Rest*, which was patched and repainted and smelled, as always, of coal and coffee. She had thought, in the gorge, in the lake, in the long drift home, that giving up the inheritance would feel like loss. It did not. It felt like setting down cargo she had never agreed to carry — her father's guilt, Vessany's ghost, the whole drowned weight of a country that had been dead longer than the boy raised on her decks had been alive. What remained was smaller and heavier in the better way: a boat, a crew, a season, a river.

She caught herself, some evenings, counting years again. She had spent a decade measuring life in runs — snag-clearing runs, toll runs, seasons survived — because years felt like things other people got to have. Now she stood at the rail in the dusk and thought: next spring the willows again. Next spring the gorge opens again, if anyone wants to see it. Next spring, and the spring after that, and no ledger anywhere logging which boats sank.

Quentin came aboard at dusk with his license in a tin tube slung across his back like a rifle, and his own boat's papers folded in his pocket — a leaky thirty-foot snag-jumper called the *Foundling*, which Odile had pronounced "ugly but honest," which was more than most boats earned. He leaned on the rail beside Ines and they watched the lights come on down the waterfront, one by one, the harbor filling up with small warm fires against the dark.

"First run's Thursday," he said. "Upstream. Cargo's lumber, boring as anything."

"Boring is the reward," Ines said. "Nobody tells you that. Boring is what the fighting buys."

He nodded, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the river went on below them, brown and patient, carrying everything it carried toward the sea.

Then Quentin straightened, tilting his head, listening. Down the dock, near the harbormaster's office, a lantern swung — and under it stood a figure in a travel-worn coat, pack on shoulder, hair cropped short in the exile fashion, waiting with the patience of someone who had walked a very long way to stand exactly there.

"She said she wouldn't come," Quentin said quietly.

"People say a lot of things," Ines said, reaching for the lamp to light it, "before they find out where home is."

Vael saw the lamplight, and started down the dock toward them.
