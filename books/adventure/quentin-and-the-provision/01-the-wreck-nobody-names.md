## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The navy had anchored a buoy over the wreck with a sign on it that said DANGER — RESTRICTED WATERS, which was the government's way of saying *treasure* in the same voice a poker player uses when he says *fold*.

I'd been reading government signs for eleven years, and I'd learned to read them the way you read weather off the water: not for what they say, but for what they're trying to keep you from saying back to them. A sign that says *keep out* is an invitation addressed to someone else. My job was to be that someone else.

My name is Cyrus Oyelaran, and on paper I am a salvage diver of modest reputation and catastrophic finances. On paper I owe the Meridian Salvage Syndicate four thousand two hundred crowns, plus interest that accrues like barnacles. Off paper, at six-forty on a morning gray as dishwater, I was slipping my boat *Kestrel* out of the harbor at Greywater Bay with her running lights doused and her engine muffled by a blanket stuffed around the exhaust, heading north toward a wreck the charts don't name and the navy pretends isn't there.

The fog did most of the work for me. Northern coast fog in autumn is a collaborator if you treat it right. It sat on the water thick as wool, swallowing sound, erasing the coastline until there was nothing in the world but my boat, my breathing, and the slow tick of the compass card. I steered by dead reckoning and memory, which sounds romantic until you remember that dead reckoning is called that because it's how divers end up dead.

Here's what I knew about the wreck, which wasn't much, and every piece of it had cost me something:

Three years ago, a naval transport went down off the northern headland in weather that shouldn't have troubled a rowboat. No storm. No collision. The official report said *foundering, cause undetermined*, which is the maritime equivalent of *he died of natural causes* on a certificate signed by a doctor who never saw the body. The navy sealed the site within a day. They didn't raise the wreck. They didn't survey it. They put a buoy on it and forgot it, the way you forget a letter you're afraid to open.

Nobody forgets a wreck they've already emptied. You only guard what you're afraid of.

I'd heard about it from a fisherman named Aldous Pike, who told me about it for the price of a drink and then made me swear on my mother's grave never to dive it, which was easy, since my mother is alive and living comfortably in a town she'd deny ever producing me from. Then he made me swear again anyway. Fishermen are superstitious about wrecks the way priests are superstitious about confession — they know exactly what comes out of them.

So naturally, here I was.

* * *

I anchored a quarter mile off the buoy — close enough to work, far enough that anyone motoring out would look at the buoy first and see nothing but fog and compliance. The water below was black-green and flat, holding its breath. Forty fathoms down, according to the numbers I'd coaxed out of a hydrographic clerk who owed me a favor and would soon owe me considerably more silence.

I checked my gear twice, then a third time, not because I doubted it but because checking gear is a form of prayer, and I am a man who prays exclusively with his hands. Regulator. Gauge. Knife. Line reel. The brass weight belt with its worn buckle that had been my father's, though my father had been a dockworker who never dove deeper than a bottle of rum, so the belt was inheritance without provenance, which describes most of what I own.

Before I went over the side, I did the thing I always do and never tell anyone about. I said the names. Not aloud. Just in my head, the way you tap a doorframe before entering a strange house. My father, Tomas. My sister, Adaeze, who stopped writing eight years ago and whose silence I carry like a stone in my boot. And last, always last, myself — Cyrus — because a diver who doesn't include himself in the count is a diver planning to come back as less than he left.

Then I rolled into the water, and the world went quiet in the particular way that only forty fathoms of cold sea can manage.

* * *

Descent is the honest part of diving. There's no performance in it, no negotiation. You sink or you swim, and the water doesn't care which, and there's a comfort in that indifference I've never found on land. Land is where people want things from you. The syndicate wants money. The harbor master wants his cut. The landlord wants rent for a shack with a roof that leaks in philosophy as well as rain. The water wants nothing. It just holds you, enormous and unimpressed, while you do your work.

At twenty fathoms the light went from gray to a kind of bruised twilight. At thirty-five, I switched on my lamp and the beam carved a tunnel through the murk, and there it was.

The wreck lay on her starboard side on a shelf of rock, half-buried in sand, her hull furred with three years of growth. She was bigger than the reports suggested — a proper transport, maybe sixty meters, with a broken back amidships where whatever killed her had done its work. I hung in the water above her and felt the old familiar pull, the one that has gotten me into every trouble of my life: the pull of a locked room.

Every wreck is a locked room. That's the whole profession, really. People think salvage is about treasure, but treasure is just the excuse. What I actually hunt is the moment a door opens. For a few minutes at the bottom of the sea, I am the only person alive who knows what's inside. It's the closest thing to being loved that I've found that doesn't talk back.

I worked along the hull to the breach. The torn steel curled outward, which meant the explosion — if it was an explosion — had come from inside. I filed that away. Outward-curling steel on a ship that foundered in calm weather is the sort of detail that keeps a careful man alive and gets a careless one a naval escort home.

Inside, the corridor was a cathedral of silt and shadow. My lamp found bulkheads furred white, a ladder going up into darkness, a mess table still bolted to the deck with a coffee cup fused to it by rust — some sailor's last cup, three years cold. I gave it a wide berth. You don't touch a dead man's coffee. That's not superstition; that's manners.

The cargo hold was aft, past a collapsed section I had to squeeze through sideways, exhaling carefully, feeling the steel kiss my tank on both sides. Bad practice, going through alone. Everything I was doing was bad practice. Alone, deep, on a sealed site, with debts that meant I couldn't afford to come up empty and pride that meant I couldn't afford to come up at all if the syndicate found out where I'd been.

In the hold, the cargo had shifted into a hill of crates and cases, most of them rotted to lace. But near the forward bulkhead, wedged under a fallen beam that had protected it like a mother's arm, was a steel document case. Military pattern. Latched. And — this was the part that made my heart do something arrhythmic — *clean*. Not pristine, but clean. Someone had maintained this case after the sinking. Someone had been down here.

I knelt in the silt and worked the latches. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were papers. Manifests. Real ones, typed on naval stationery, stamped with dates and signatures. I fanned them in my lamplight with hands that wanted to shake and didn't, because shaking wastes air.

And that's when I understood why the navy had sealed this wreck and never raised it.

The manifests listed cargo that did not exist. Crates of medical stores bound for a station that had closed a decade before the sinking. Machine parts for a vessel class retired before the war. Tonnage upon tonnage of *general stores, miscellaneous* — the bureaucratic phrase for things someone doesn't want itemized — in quantities that would have filled a warehouse twice the size of this ship. And one line, near the bottom of the final page, in a different typewriter's font, added after the rest:

**ONE (1) ITEM, SURVEY EQUIPMENT, EXPEDITION PATTERN 1897. HANDLE PER STANDING ORDER.**

Standing order. For equipment from 1897. On a ship that sank three years ago.

I have a rule about artifacts older than my grandfather: they belong in museums or they belong to whoever's brave enough to take them, and museums pay worse. But I took no artifact that day. I took photographs — no, I didn't even have a camera rated for depth. I took memory. I read each manifest twice, the way you'd read a love letter you intended to recite later, and I committed the numbers to the vault behind my eyes that has never once leaked.

Then I folded one page — just one, the last one, the one with the impossible line — into my dive sleeve, closed the case, and left everything else exactly as the sea had arranged it.

A man can be honest in almost everything and still hang himself with the exception.

* * *

I was thirty feet into my ascent, hanging on the anchor line, watching my gauge and my shadow-thoughts, when I saw the boat.

Not mine. Mine was a quarter mile south, a smudge I'd memorized. This was a shape in the fog above me, gray on gray, moving without engine noise — oars, or a drifting hull. Through the water's skin it came to me flattened and wavering, a dark suggestion with a straight vertical line at its center that might have been a mast, might have been a man standing very still.

I stopped kicking. I hung in the cold green middle-distance between the wreck full of lies and the surface full of watchers, and I thought about the arithmetic of my life. Four thousand two hundred crowns to Meridian, accruing. Vess Halloway's voice on the telephone last month, pleasant as a knife being sharpened: *"Cyrus, darling, we don't mind what you find. We only mind what you keep."* The rent. The leaking roof. The empty chair across from my dinner plate that I had long ago stopped pretending was temporary.

And now a silent boat over a sealed wreck, and a page against my ribs describing cargo from a vanished expedition, and the growing, giddy, terrible understanding that I had just picked up a thread that somebody powerful wanted cut.

The shape above me held for a long minute. Then it moved off north, unhurried, oars dipping without sound reaching me through the water, and I finished my ascent the slow careful way, doing my stops, watching my gauge, being the competent professional I am on the outside even while the inside of me was a boy running downhill too fast.

* * *

Back aboard the *Kestrel*, I shed my gear, unwrapped the page, and read it again in the gray light. The paper was good stock, navy-issue, meant to last. The ink had held. **EXPEDITION PATTERN 1897.** I turned it over. On the reverse, faint as a watermark, someone had pressed a stamp that had mostly worn away — but the surviving arc of it showed a crest: a circle broken by a chevron, with lettering around the rim I couldn't quite resolve.

I knew the shape. Everyone on this coast knows the shape, the way everyone knows a ghost story they pretend not to believe. The crest of the 1897 Northern Survey Expedition. Forty-one men and women who sailed north to chart the range and map the interior passes, and who vanished — not all at once, not dramatically, but completely, over the course of two years, until the expeditions' sponsors declared them lost and the coast declared them legend. The Provision, the tavern stories called it. Something the expedition carried. Something worth carrying. Nobody agreed on what. Gold, said some. A new route through the range, said others. Said the oldest fishermen, lowering their voices: proof of something.

I'd heard the story a hundred times in a hundred harbors, and I'd filed it where I file all legends — under *rent money, alternative sources*. Legends don't pay interest. But legends don't get stamped onto naval manifests either, and they don't get loaded onto transports that explode from the inside in calm seas, and they don't get guarded by buoys and standing orders for three years.

I sat in my wheelhouse with the fog pressing at the windows and the page in my lap, and I felt the specific loneliness of a man holding a secret too big for one set of hands. It's a feeling I know well. I've been solitary the way a keel is solitary — necessary, load-bearing, and permanently underwater. Eleven years of diving alone, eating alone, owing alone. You get good at it. You get so good at it that you mistake it for freedom, right up until the morning you're sitting in the fog with a dead expedition's paperwork and realize there is not one person in the world you could tell without also telling them something they could use to hurt you.

That's what loneliness actually is, I've decided. Not the absence of people. The surplus of secrets.

I started the engine, still muffled, and turned the *Kestrel* south toward Greywater Bay, running the fog lines by heart. Behind me the buoy bobbed over its lie. Ahead of me the harbor waited with its creditors and its questions, and somewhere in the middle distance, presumably, the navy was already noticing that its restricted waters had been restricted by one.

I had a plan, of sorts. Dry the page. Learn the crest. Find out who else knew the manifests existed, and whether the silent boat belonged to the navy or to something worse. Sell nothing, tell no one, stay ahead of the interest.

It was a good plan. It lasted four days.

Because on the fourth day, at a tide so low the old-timers stood on the seawall scratching their heads, the sea pulled back beneath the northern cliffs and opened a door that had been shut since before my grandfather was born — and I, being who I am, walked through it.

But that, as they say, is another chapter of the same mistake.
