## 3. The Brass Key

I want to tell you I hesitated.

I've told it that way since — to Quentin, to Odile, to the navy man with the flat eyes who asked me what exactly I took from the cave, and I said "nothing," and he looked at me the way a fisherman looks at a fish that's already swallowed the hook but hasn't figured it out yet. Nothing, I said. Just looked around. Cold down there. Tide comes in fast.

But this is the version where I don't lie, because nobody's paying me to, and because lying is how I got into half my troubles and the truth is how I got into the other half.

The truth is: I saw the key, and I wanted it, and wanting won before hesitation even got its boots on.

* * *

Let me back up to the chamber itself, because I've been avoiding thinking about it and avoidance, in my experience, is just fear wearing a work shirt.

The passage from the cave mouth narrowed after the first fifty yards — a slot of wet stone you had to turn sideways to squeeze through, the kind of gap that would be a coffin if the sea changed its mind while you were in it. I'd left my pack at the entrance with a line tied off to a rock, which was either prudence or superstition depending on how charitable you felt about me that evening. Beyond the slot the tunnel opened like a throat clearing, and there it was.

A room. Under the cliffs, above the tide line — barely — a room.

My lamp found the walls first. Carved into them, repeated like wallpaper some obsessive had hung a century ago, was a crest: an anchor wrapped in a serpent, biting its own tail, over the numbers 1897. The stone around each crest was darkened with something that wasn't soot. I didn't let myself decide what it was. That's a skill of mine, not-deciding. It's kept me alive in water that had no business letting me live.

Then the lamp swung lower and found the rest, and my not-deciding days were numbered.

Crates. Six of them, stenciled with the same serpent-anchor, their lids still seated tight, wood gone silver-grey with age but sound — dry-sound, the knock of them said, when I rapped one with a knuckle. A coil of rope on iron rings, still flexible when I pulled a strand free and bent it. Tin lanterns stacked in a pyramid. And against the far wall, arranged with a care that made my chest hurt for reasons I couldn't name, a row of personal effects laid out on a canvas groundsheet: boots, paired. A coat folded into a neat rectangle. A pocket watch, open, its hands stopped at eleven minutes past four. A pipe. A tin cup set upside down on top of a journal whose cover had been waxed against the damp.

Somebody had tidied up before they died. Or somebody had tidied up after somebody else died, which raised questions I filed under Later, along with everything else.

And there — sitting on the folded coat like it had been placed there for me specifically, which is the sort of thought you get in caves and shouldn't trust — was a satchel. Leather, brass-buckled, stiff as a board with age. It lay open. Inside it, catching my lamplight and throwing it back warm, was a key.

Not a door key. Not any key I'd seen, and I've picked locks since I was nine years old and too small to be suspected of anything. This was a foot of solid brass, cast heavy as a tool, its bit cut in a pattern like fish scales running down one edge, its bow formed into the same serpent swallowing its tail. It had weight the way an anchor has weight — not mass so much as intention.

Here's the arithmetic I did in that chamber, standing in a dead man's tidy room with the sea muttering somewhere below me:

The wreck offshore — the one the navy sealed, the one whose manifests listed cargo that never existed, the one I'd been diving on spec for a syndicate that owned more of me than the bank did — that wreck was from the same era. Same decade, maybe the same year. The Provision, the legend every drunk in every harbor bar between here and the capital swears by: a cache the '97 expedition carried inland and vanished with. Gold, they say. Instruments, say the ones who read. Enough to buy a coastline, say everybody.

And here was a room that proved the expedition had stood on this coast. Had staged here. Had left behind a key and a satchel and six crates they hadn't needed, because whatever the Provision truly was, they'd carried it onward — inland, up, away.

The key went somewhere. Things with keys have doors. Doors have whats-behind-them.

I did that arithmetic in about four seconds. Then I did the other arithmetic, the one I do nightly: what I owed Halloway's syndicate, what my boat owed, what my hands were worth another season of cold water, which is less every year. Four seconds against four seconds. Wanting versus owing. It wasn't a fair fight. It has never once been a fair fight.

I took the key.

* * *

Now, in fairness to the man I'm trying to become instead of the man I was, I should report what I *didn't* take, because I surprised myself there.

I didn't touch the crates. I stood in front of them for a long minute — long enough that my lamp dimmed and I had to shake it — and I didn't pry a single lid. Partly that was sense: crates that old, sealed that well, might hold anything from preserved stores to powder, and opening them in a tidal cave with one lamp was a way to die stupid. But mostly — and I knew it even then, the knowledge sitting in my stomach like swallowed seawater — mostly it was the watch. Eleven minutes past four, stopped forever, sitting out in the open where anyone could have taken it for a hundred years and nobody had. The boots paired. The coat folded. Somebody had made a small, decent order out of dying, and walking through it with my pockets out felt like grave-robbing, and I've robbed graves, metaphorically speaking, all my life, but I'd never done it literally and something in me — some inheritance from a mother I haven't called in six years — put its hand flat on my chest and said: not this.

So I took the key and left the dead man his watch, his coat, his boots, his name — because the journal stayed shut too, though God knows I wanted it, wanted a name to hang the guilt on — and I turned for the slot in the rock.

That's when I heard the sea change its mind.

You learn the ocean's moods the way you learn a dangerous dog's. There's the ordinary voice, the one that says *I'm still here, don't get comfortable*. And then there's the other one — deeper, a pressure more than a sound, a note you feel in your back teeth and the soles of your feet. I heard the second one come up through the floor of the chamber, and every hair I own stood up and voted to leave without me.

I'd known the tide was turning. I'd planned for it. Planned is a strong word — I'd *noted* it, the way you note weather coming while you're busy doing something else, and the something else had eaten two hours I didn't have. The record low tide that had opened the cave's mouth was on its way back up, and it was coming back the way it always does on this coast: not gradually, but like a debt collector.

I got to the slot and the slot was already weeping. Water sheeted across its floor, ankle-deep and rising, cold enough to make my ankles file complaints. Beyond it, the outer passage — fifty yards of tunnel I'd walked dry — was a brown-green churn up to my knees, and the mouth beyond that was a boiling slot of white where the swell was shouldering in.

Here's a thing about drowning that the brochures don't cover: it doesn't start with water in your lungs. It starts with a decision you can't unmake. You're in the tunnel or you're in the chamber, and both are wrong, and the sea is closing the argument.

I went through the slot.

It was worse than it sounds and I'm going to make it sound bad. The water in the gap was waist-high and moving inward, which meant every step forward was a fight against a current funneled through rock, and the ceiling came down low enough that I had to duck-walk with the surge shoving me backward, and my lamp — my beautiful, stupid lamp — went out on the third step. Darkness total, except for the grey ghost-glow from the outer passage ahead, which was shrinking. I could measure my remaining time by the light. That's a clock they don't sell in shops.

Halfway through, the surge hit.

Not a wave — waves are polite, they announce themselves. This was the whole tide arriving at once through a funnel built for it, and it picked me up like I weighed nothing, which emotionally I didn't, and drove me sideways into the rock. My shoulder took it. Something in the shoulder said a word I won't repeat. The key — the brass key, my fortune, my ruin, tucked inside my jacket — drove into my ribs like a fist, and I remember having the lunatic thought, underwater, in the dark, being rolled like laundry: *at least I didn't lose it.*

Priorities. Mine are terrible. They've always been terrible.

The surge receded and dumped me on the tunnel floor in eight inches of water, coughing, one arm dead from the shoulder down, and the light at the end of the passage was maybe a hand's width tall now. Behind me the chamber was already sealing itself — I could hear the slot filling, a gurgle like a throat swallowing. In ten minutes that room would be under six feet of sea, and the dead man's watch would tick its stopped eleven-past-four in the dark until the next record tide, a year from now, or a decade, or never.

I crawled. I'll say it plainly because there's no dignity available and pretending otherwise costs more than the truth: I crawled on my elbows through rising seawater toward a shrinking grey line, dragging a dead arm, with a century-old key bruising my ribs, and I swore — out loud, to no one, to the dead man, to the sea — that if I got out I'd take the thing straight back down to the harbor authority and wash my hands of the whole rotten legend.

I have never kept a promise faster broken. But I meant it right then. Meaning it counts for something. Ask anyone who's ever meant anything.

* * *

The last twenty feet were the worst, because the worst part of nearly drowning isn't the water. It's the moment the water lets you think.

I surfaced outside the cave mouth into evening light — actual sky, actual air, gulls wheeling overhead with the indifference of creatures who've watched men drown since before men had names — and I hauled myself onto the shelf of rock below the cliff, and lay there leaking, and the sea below me was calm. Genuinely calm. Swell rolling in gentle as a cat. As if nothing had happened. As if the violence had been mine alone, projected onto innocent water.

I laughed. Lying on my back on wet rock, one arm hanging useless, salt in my throat, and I laughed until it turned into coughing, because that's the joke, isn't it? The ocean doesn't chase you. It doesn't want anything. You walk into its house and take things and it just... closes the door on schedule. The malice is entirely yours. The sea is only honest.

I got my arm working again by degrees — not broken, I decided, testing it, just loudly opposed to further use — and sat up, and the sun was going down behind the headland, and the tide was climbing the rocks below me with the patience of something that has never once lost.

The key sat in my lap where I'd dragged it out. It was warm. Body-warm, from riding against my chest through all of it, and heavier than a foot of brass has any right to be, and I sat there in the failing light turning it over, reading the serpent devouring its tail, and I understood — finally, fully, in the way you understand things only after they've nearly killed you — that I hadn't taken a key out of a cave.

I'd taken a debt. The oldest kind. The kind you owe to someone who can't collect it themselves anymore, and who left instructions anyway, and the instructions are: *carry it onward.* That's what the expedition had done. That's what the dead man's tidy boots and folded coat and stopped watch were saying, in the only language he had left: someone came after us; someone will come after us; give them this and send them up the mountain.

Or — and this was the other reading, the one I liked better, the one I held onto like a spar — it was just a key. To a vault. Full of gold. And the dead man was past caring, and the syndicate was past waiting, and Vess Halloway's interest on my boat was compounding daily like a tide of its own, and a man can't eat conscience.

Both readings fit in my hand. Both were true. That's the curse of the thing, if you want to call it that — not magic, not doom, just weight. Everything real is heavy, and I'd just picked up the heaviest object on this coast, and it didn't weigh anything at all.

I pocketed it. Of course I pocketed it. You know I pocketed it. I'm telling you the story of a man who pockets things.

I climbed the cliff path in the dark with my shoulder screaming, reached the shack above the cove, lit the stove, dried off, and ate cold beans standing up because sitting down felt like surrender. Then I put the key on the table under the lamp and looked at it for a long time, and it looked back, the way objects do when they've decided something about you.

Tomorrow, I told myself, I'd go into town. Supplies. Diesel. And maybe — purely by chance, purely in passing — I'd ask around the harbor about the '97 expedition, the way a curious man asks, the way a man with no intentions asks.

The town had a hundred souls in it, a chandlery, two bars, and — I remembered suddenly, staring at the serpent on the brass — a girl. The cartographer's kid. Dunmore's daughter, the one who'd gone feral since her father walked into the range and didn't walk out. People said she drew maps in the dirt of the harbor road, charts of places she'd never been, and that she'd once corrected a visiting surveyor's bearings out loud, in public, at nine years old.

People also said she had her father's eye for crests and marks and symbols. For the little signatures expeditions leave behind.

I looked at the key. The key looked at me.

"Tomorrow," I told it, and blew out the lamp, and slept badly, dreaming of water rising in a room full of paired boots.
