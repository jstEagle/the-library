## 4. Quentin

The trouble with nearly drowning is that it makes you philosophical, and I've never had the temperament for philosophy. Philosophy is what people do instead of getting paid.

Three days after the cave, I was still in Saltmere Harbor, which was three days longer than my contract with common sense allowed. The syndicate's interest on my boat debt compounded weekly, and every morning the *Magpie* sat at her mooring looking more like a liability and less like a livelihood. But I couldn't leave. Not because of the key — though it rode in my coat pocket like a second heartbeat, brass-warm even in the cold — but because of the crest stamped into it. A compass rose inside a broken circle, with a motto worn almost smooth: *CARRY IT ONWARD.*

I'd spent two evenings in the harbor library's shipping archives before the librarian started giving me looks usually reserved for men who steal copper. The 1897 expedition had a name — the Meridian Survey — and a sponsor list that read like a who's-who of people who died rich without ever explaining why. And then the trail went cold, because somebody had gone through the records with a knife. Pages razored out clean. That kind of thoroughness costs money, and money like that belongs to governments or syndicates, and I owed one of each.

So there I was, third morning, sitting at the chandlery counter nursing a coffee the color of bilge water, turning the key over in my palm under the pretense of admiring the workmanship, when a voice behind me said:

"That's the Provision mark."

I didn't jump. I want that on the record. What I did was set the key down slowly, finish my coffee like a man with nothing to hide, and turn around.

The girl behind me couldn't have been more than twelve. She was sitting cross-legged on a crate of mooring line like she owned the chandlery, the dock, and possibly the tide itself. Grey wool coat two sizes too big, sleeves rolled in careful cuffs, hair chopped short with what looked like fish-cleaning shears. Her eyes were the problem. Twelve-year-olds look at you like you're weather. This one looked at me like I was a chart she was checking against the coastline.

"You're mistaken," I said.

"I'm not." She nodded at the key. "Compass rose, broken circle. My da drew it forty times. He drew everything forty times — he said once was luck, twice was habit, forty times was proof." She held out her hand, businesslike. "Can I hold it?"

"Absolutely not."

"Fair." She didn't seem offended. "You've got salt in the engraving. You got it wet recently, and not just harbor wet. Harbor water leaves green on brass. Cave water leaves white." She paused. "There's only one cave on this coast that floods on a tide cycle, and it's under the north cliffs, and nobody goes in it because the navy put a notice on it that says unexploded ordnance, which is a lie, because ordnance notices get buoys and patrols, and that cave gets neither. It just gets ignored. Which means whatever's in it, they don't want found, and they don't think anyone's stupid enough to look."

I looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere behind us the chandlery man was pointedly not listening while listening with his entire body.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Quentin Voss." She said it like an answer to a question I hadn't asked yet. "My da was Aldous Voss. Cartographer. He went up into the range in spring to survey a pass and he hasn't come back, and the navy says they have no record of him, which is their way of saying they have a very good record of him." She tilted her head. "You're the diver. Oyelaran. You dive the wreck off Gannet Shoal that also officially doesn't exist. You talk in your sleep at the Anchor Inn — well, you did, the two nights you stayed there. You said 'the chamber' four times and 'not the gold' once."

"I'm going to need you to stop knowing things about me."

"That's fair too," she said. "But here's my trade: I'll stop knowing things if you start telling them. Because that key is the first thing connected to the Meridian Survey that's surfaced in this town since my da left, and I have been waiting on it for eight months, and I will wait considerably longer, because I'm patient, but I'd rather not be."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't make people soft. It makes them precise. Quentin Voss wasn't begging. She was negotiating from a position of total information, and she was twelve, and some traitorous part of me — the part that had been alone so long it had stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a stone in your boot until you take the boot off — wanted to say yes just to hear how she'd won.

I told her no anyway. I had debts, hunters, and a navy file with my name on it. Children were ballast I couldn't afford.

She took it well. She followed me for six hours.

* * *

Quentin

Following him was easy. He checked his backtrail exactly twice, both times in shop windows, both times wrong — you check reflections walking toward them, not away, or you catch your own face instead of what's behind you. Da would have wept. Da taught me tradecraft the way other fathers taught fishing: as a game, on Sundays, with biscuits as prizes. *Assume everyone is following you, Quen,* he'd say, *because someone always is, eventually, and the ones who aren't yet are thinking about it.*

He went to the harbormaster's office. Then to the ferry schedule board. Then he stood in front of the ferry schedule board for eleven minutes doing arithmetic with his eyes, which meant he was calculating whether he could run before his creditors noticed, and whether running by sea was faster than running by road, and whether the answer changed if the navy was watching the water. It does change, incidentally. The navy watches water the way cats watch birds. Roads are where amateurs go.

He bought bread, cheese, a tin of lamp oil, and — this was the interesting part — a coastal chart of the range's northern approaches. Not a passage chart. A survey chart. The kind with contour lines and bench marks. He spread it on a bollard, weighted the corners with his purchases, and started comparing it against something he kept taking out of his pocket and putting back whenever anyone came near.

I let him sweat through two false starts. Then I walked up and put my finger on the chart, on the blank space southeast of the Red Stair notation, where the contours dissolved into hachures and a printer's ghost.

"He surveyed this," I said. "That's why the map's unfinished. The survey office pulled him off it in March. He told me the mountains were lying to them and he intended to catch them at it." I looked up at him. "He also told me, the night before he left, that if anything happened to him, I should find the man who dives wrecks nobody names. I thought he was being dramatic. He was never dramatic. He was precise. So either you're that man, or the world has a strange sense of humor, and I've learned this year that it doesn't. It has a cruel sense of humor. Different thing entirely."

Cyrus Oyelaran looked down at me for a long time. He had the face of a man doing sums he didn't like — the kind where the answer keeps coming out worse the more carefully you work it.

"How much do you know about the Meridian Survey?" he said finally.

"Everything the town knows, which is nothing, plus everything my da left behind, which is considerable." I patted the satchel I'd been carrying for eight months. "His field books. His last letter. His shorthand, which I can read, which nobody else alive can, because he invented it and only taught me, because he said maps are power and power should stay in the family." I stuck out my hand again. "Partnership. Fifty-fifty. You've got the key and the diving. I've got the map and the mountain."

"You're twelve."

"I'm twelve," I agreed. "And you're broke, hunted, and navigating by guesswork. One of us should probably be embarrassed, and it isn't going to be me."

* * *

Cyrus

I want to say I held out longer than I did. The truth is I lasted another day, and what broke me wasn't her arguments. It was her father's letter.

She didn't show it to me willingly. I caught her reading it at dawn on the sea wall, hunched against the wind like a small grey bird, lips moving over the words, and when she saw me she didn't hide it — she just looked at me with those chart-checking eyes and said, "You may as well sit down. You're going to hover otherwise, and hovering is undignified in a grown man."

The letter was dated the eleventh of March, written in a hand so fine and certain it looked typeset. Most of it was in cipher-shorthand, but the last lines were plain, and I read them twice:

*If I don't come back, the trail is real and it is marked. The Provision is not what the stories say. Whatever you do, Quen, don't sell it, don't trade it, and don't give it to anyone wearing a uniform. Carry it onward. That's the whole secret. That's all it ever asked.*

Carry it onward. The motto on the key.

"The same words," I said quietly.

"The same words," she agreed. "So either your key came from the same place my da was going, or—" and here her voice did something complicated, a hairline crack in a load-bearing wall, "—or he found it. Or it found him. Either way, you walked into a cave and picked up the thread he was following, and I have been sitting in this town for eight months watching the water go in and out like it doesn't care, waiting for exactly one thread." She folded the letter along its worn creases, careful as a surgeon. "I'm coming with you. Not asking. Telling. You can decide whether I'm a partner or a stowaway, but I'm coming, and I'll tell you now that stowaways eat more than partners, out of spite."

"Quentin—"

"And before you lecture me about danger," she said, "consider that I've been living rough since April. I know which cellars flood, which boats leave at odd hours, which constable takes bribes and which takes offense. I have survived a winter alone in a town full of adults who were supposed to notice things. The danger already found me. You're not bringing me toward it. You're the first thing that's come along pointing away."

* * *

I made her a promise I shouldn't have, which is that I'd take her as far as the foothills and find her somewhere safe, and she accepted it with the serene expression of a person who knows a lie when she hears one and is simply letting me keep my dignity while I tell it.

We shook on it. Her grip was surprisingly strong, a rope-hauler's grip, and her palm was calloused in ways that told me she'd been rowing, climbing, and probably stealing for months.

"One condition," I said. "When I say run, you run. When I say hide, you hide. No debates in the open."

"No debates in the open," she agreed. "Debates after dark. I'm very good after dark."

God help me, I believed her.

We were still shaking hands when I saw the navy launch come around the breakwater — white hull, grey superstructure, moving with the unhurried confidence of an institution that owns the water it floats on. Two officers in the bow. One of them holding a folder, and the folder having weight to it, the particular weight of paper about a man.

Quentin saw them a half-second after I did. She didn't gasp or grab. She just said, conversationally, "Back door of the chandlery opens onto Cooper's Lane, and Cooper's Lane has three exits, and I've slept in all of them," and turned and walked, unhurried, exactly like a girl with nowhere in particular to be, trusting me to follow.

For the first time since I'd taken the key out of a dead man's satchel, the thing in my pocket felt less like a curse and more like a compass.

I followed.
