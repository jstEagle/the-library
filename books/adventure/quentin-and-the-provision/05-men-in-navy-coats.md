## 5. Men in Navy Coats

The trouble with living in a shack the size of a coffin turned sideways is that you hear everything. Every gull, every rope creaking against the pilings, every boot on the salt-rotted boards of the dock. So when the boots came at seven in the morning — two pairs, heavy, unhurried, the walk of men who had never once in their lives hurried for anybody — I heard them from the far side of my thin door and had roughly the length of a match burning down to decide how to play it.

I chose badly. I chose to pretend I wasn't home.

Which would have worked better if my kettle hadn't been on.

There's a particular kind of silence that follows the sound of a whistle going off in an empty house. It's the silence of men exchanging a look you can't see but can feel through the wood like weather coming through a wall. Then a knock. Then, because navy men don't really believe in knocking as a negotiation tactic, the door opened anyway, and the morning came in with them, along with the smell of engine oil and pressed wool.

There were two of them. The tall one had a face like a hatchet that had been left out in the rain — everything about it angled and a little rusted. The short one was rounder, softer, with the kind of eyes that made a living out of looking friendly and never once succeeding. Both wore navy coats, the good ones, the ones with brass that caught the light through my one salt-fogged window and threw little coins of it across my floor.

"Morning," said the tall one. "Oyelaran."

Not a question. That was the first thing I noticed, before the coats, before the eyes. They hadn't come to find out who I was. They'd come to tell me they already knew.

"You've got the advantage of me," I said, which was a lie, because I'd already placed them. I'd spent enough years around men in uniforms to know the difference between a shore patrol and something with longer teeth. These were the long-teeth kind. The kind that get sent when the regular kind has already failed.

"Commander Aldous Vane." He didn't offer a hand. Neither did his friend. "This is Lieutenant Prewitt. We'd like a word about your diving."

"My diving," I said. I poured myself the kettle's worth of tea into the one mug I owned, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "That's a broad subject. I've got opinions on weight belts you wouldn't believe."

Prewitt smiled. It was the smile of a man who had been taught smiling in a classroom and had never quite gotten the hang of the live version. "The *Meridian*," he said.

And there it was. The wreck nobody names, named, in my shack, at seven in the morning, by a man holding a folder he hadn't opened yet because he didn't need to.

I drank my tea. It was too hot and too weak, which is to say it was exactly like every other cup of tea I'd ever made. "Never heard of it."

Vane looked around my shack the way a customs man looks at luggage. There wasn't much to see — a bunk, a stove, a table with my charts on it, the good brass compass my mother left me, and a shelf of gear that any diver would read like a diary. Depth of experience in the wear on the straps. Recent work in the salt still drying on the suit hanging by the door. A man's kit tells the truth even when he won't.

"Your tender logged you at the north cliffs three days ago," Vane said. "The same day as the spring low. Unusual tide, that. Lowest in eleven years." He let that sit in the air between us like a hook in the water. "You dive unusual tides, Mr. Oyelaran. It's in your record. You go where the water lets you, and the water doesn't let you many places."

"I dive where the salvage is," I said. "The water and I have an arrangement."

"And what did the arrangement yield?"

"Kelp. A boot. Part of a boot, anyway." I shrugged. "The north coast is picked clean. Everyone knows it."

"Everyone knows it," Prewitt agreed pleasantly. "Which is what makes it interesting that you went."

I want to be honest here, in the privacy of my own head, since I wasn't going to be honest anywhere else: I am not a good liar. I'm a competent one, in the way that a man who's been broke his whole life is competent at arithmetic — out of necessity, and only up to a certain number. Small lies, single lies, lies about whether I've been drinking or where I was last night, those sit fine in my mouth. But a lie with structure, a lie with a beginning and middle and end that has to hold its shape under pressure — that's architecture, and I never learned architecture. I learned diving. And diving, at least, punishes you immediately when you're wrong.

"Look," I said, "I don't know what this is about, but if it's about the syndicate's claim on my last haul, you've got the wrong office. Take it up with Halloway's people. They've got better chairs."

"This isn't about your debts," Vane said.

"Everyone says that right before it's about my debts."

He opened the folder then, and I got my first look at what they'd actually come with, and my stomach did something slow and cold, the way it does at thirty meters when the light starts going. Photographs. My boat at the mooring. My boat under way, taken from somewhere high — the cliff road, maybe, or a glass. And a third one, grainy and long-lensed, of the north cliffs at dusk with the tide out so far the rocks stood up like the spines of drowned animals.

And at the base of the cliffs, small as a flea, a figure with a dive bag.

"Nice weather for it," Prewitt said.

I set down the mug. My hand was steady, which surprised me. Somewhere under the fear, an old and ugly part of me — the part that had gotten me through every tight spot since I was fourteen — sat up and paid attention. *You're already caught,* it said. *The question is only what they've caught you with.*

"Fine," I said. "I dove the cliffs. It's not a crime. Half the coast does it when the tide's out. There's caves under there older than your navy."

"Caves," Vane repeated.

"Caves," I said. "Rock. Water. The usual."

"What did you find in the cave, Mr. Oyelaran?"

"Kelp," I said. "And a boot."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Prewitt's smile had gone the way of all his smiles — nowhere, quickly. Vane just looked at me, and I looked back, and we both knew how the arithmetic was running. He knew I'd been in the cave. I knew he knew. What he didn't know — couldn't know, unless someone had talked — was what I'd carried out of it. And the key was at that moment sitting in the bottom of my tea tin, under the loose leaves, because I'd had the sense to move it there two nights ago and not the sense to move it further.

"Your logs," Vane said.

"My what?"

"Dive logs. All of them. Notebooks, charts, tide tables with your annotations. The syndicate's manifests you've been copying." He held out his hand, palm up, patient as a collection plate. "You'll be compensated for the paper."

"They're my logs."

"They're evidence in an ongoing matter."

"Which matter? You haven't named one. Not the wreck, not the cave, not a charge, not a statute. You've come into a man's home at dawn and you're taking his property on the strength of photographs anyone could have taken off the cliff road." I heard my own voice climbing and made myself come down off it. Fear does that to me. It comes out as anger, which is worse, because anger invites a response and fear at least keeps its mouth shut. "I want a receipt."

"You'll have one," Prewitt said, already moving toward my table with the cheerful efficiency of a man tidying up. He gathered my logs — three years of them, depths and times and bottom conditions, the closest thing I had to a diary — and my tide tables with the low-water marks circled in pencil going back two seasons. He did not take the charts, which I noted, and which told me something: they wanted the record of where I'd been, not where I might go. They were working backward. Which meant they didn't know about the vault. Not yet. Not for certain.

Or they knew exactly, and this was theater. I couldn't tell, and the not-telling sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Vane watched Prewitt work, and then, without looking at me, said: "The *Meridian* went down in 1897 with a government charter aboard. What she carried is a matter of record. What the record says is that she carried survey equipment and mail." He closed the folder. "The record is accurate. I'd advise you to keep it that way in your own thinking. Men who invent cargo for dead ships tend to invent trouble for living ones."

"I don't invent anything," I said. "I just lift what the sea gives up."

"The sea," Vane said, and for the first time something moved behind the hatchet face — not warmth, nothing so useful as that, but recognition, the look of one professional acknowledging another across a table neither of them chose. "The sea gives up what it's told to give up, Mr. Oyelaran. Everything else it keeps. And what it kept in 1897, this office has been keeping ever since. There are men above me who have spent their whole careers on that keeping. They are patient men, and they are thorough men, and they are not, on the whole, cruel men — unless they are made to be." He put on his cap. "You took nothing from that cave. I believe you. Prove me right by continuing to have taken nothing. If the water gives something up to you — a coin, a plate, a piece of brass — you bring it to the harbor office and you say it came from the *Meridian* and you'll be thanked. Quietly. Well. And we'll never trouble you again."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the next visit won't be at seven in the morning," Prewitt said pleasantly, from the door, "and it won't be just the two of us, and it won't be a shack we come to. We know about the syndicate's interest in you, by the way. Vess Halloway's people have been asking the same questions we have. You're a popular man, Oyelaran. Popular men should choose their friends early."

They went out the way they'd come in, boots on the boards, unhurried, and the door shut behind them, and the shack was just a shack again — small, cold, mine, and suddenly worth much less than it had been at six o'clock.

* * *

I sat for a long time with the tea going cold in the mug and did the thing I do instead of panicking, which is make lists. It's not courage. It's bookkeeping. A man can survive almost anything he can put in a column.

Column one: they had my logs. Three years of depths and times, and somewhere in the last week's pages, my own handwriting putting me at the north cliffs on the low tide, with a note about water clarity in the surge channel that no kelp bed in the world would explain. I'd written it because I write everything. It's a discipline. It's also, it turns out, a confession with a date on it.

Column two: they hadn't searched the shack. Not properly. A real search turns over a tea tin. Which meant either they didn't have cause yet, or — likelier, and worse — they didn't need to. They were watching. The photographs proved it. Somewhere on the cliff road or the water there was a glass and a man behind it, and everything I did from this morning forward was being read like a chart.

Column three: Halloway. Prewitt had said it almost as an afterthought, which is how you say a thing you want to land hard. The syndicate was asking the same questions. Which meant the cave had a second set of eyes on it, and Vess Halloway did not send letters. She sent men, and her men did not leave with receipts.

I took the tea tin down and pried the lid and looked at the key the way you look at a thing that has stopped being a find and started being a weight. Brass, heavy, cold, the crest worn nearly smooth under my thumb — the same crest, I now admitted to myself, that was stamped into the lead seal on the *Meridian*'s manifest, the one I'd lifted from the wreck two weeks before the cave, the one that had started all of this, the one that was itself now sitting in a syndicate strongbox as collateral on a debt I couldn't pay.

One crest. Two places it shouldn't be. And two sets of hunters, one in wool and one in oilskins, both of them now certain I had the thread.

I should have gone to the harbor office that morning. I want that on the record, in the column marked *mistakes*, which is the longest column in the book. I should have walked in with the key in my fist and said *the sea gave this up, thank you kindly*, taken my quiet thanks, and gone back to being broke in peace. It would have cost me the one thing I'd found in forty years of lifting things off the bottom that was worth more than its weight in anything.

I put the key back in the tin. I put the tin back on the shelf. And I told myself the lie that liars tell best, the one with no structure at all: *I'll decide tomorrow.*

Then I went out to check my boat, because a man under watch should behave like a man with nothing to hide, and because the boat needed checking, and because standing on the dock in the wind was better than sitting in the shack listening to my own pulse.

The harbor was doing its morning business — nets coming in, gulls conducting their usual extortion, the fish house steaming. Ordinary. Except at the end of the pier, where the old survey boat sat on its moorings with its paint peeling and its name half gone — *Kestrel*, or what was left of the word — there was a girl.

Twelve, maybe. Sitting on the bollard with her knees up, watching me with the flat, patient attention of a heron. Ragged coat, hair cut by somebody in a hurry, and eyes that went from my face to the navy launch just clearing the breakwater and back again with an arithmetic I recognized, because I'd been doing the same sum all morning.

"You're the one they came for," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"They've been asking about you at the chandlery. Two men, coats." She shrugged. "I hear things. It's what I'm for."

"It's what you're for," I repeated.

"I'm for lots of things." She hopped off the bollard and came closer, and that's when I saw what she had in her hand — a folded sheet of paper, soft with handling, and when she opened it, careful as a moth wing, I saw the lines of a hand-drawn chart and, in its corner margin, small and precise, a crest.

Worn. Familiar. The same one I'd been sleeping three feet from for two nights.

"Where did you get that?" I asked, and my voice came out wrong, too careful, the voice of a bad liar hearing a better story than his own.

"My father drew it," she said. "Before he went up the mountain and didn't come back." She looked at me the way the navy man had looked at me, like she already knew the answer and was only checking whether I'd lie. "So. Are you going to tell me you found nothing in that cave? Because everyone else in this town lies to me for free, and I was hoping you'd at least be expensive about it."

The gulls went up off the pilings all at once, the way they do when something changes below, and I stood there on the dock with the wind coming off the water and the key in its tin behind me and the girl in front of me, and I understood that the morning's arithmetic had just added a column I hadn't planned for.

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said.

And she smiled — the first honest smile anyone had shown me in days — and said, "Yeah. That's what I thought. You're terrible at that."
