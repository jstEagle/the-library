## 6. The Syndicate Calls

The knock came at six in the morning, which told me everything I needed to know before I opened the door. Nobody knocks at six in the morning out of kindness. Kindness sleeps in. Kindness has coffee first. Only debt arrives before the gulls do.

I'd been awake anyway, sitting at my table with the brass key in front of me like a confession I hadn't decided how to make. All night it had sat there catching the lamplight, throwing a little coin of gold onto the ceiling, and all night I'd been having the argument with myself that I was apparently going to keep losing: *put it back*. The tide would be low again Thursday. I could return it to the dead man's satchel, seal the chamber with its 1897 crest staring out at me like an accusation, and go back to being a man whose problems were merely financial instead of financial plus possibly cursed.

But here's the thing about me you should know early: I have never once in my life put anything back.

I opened the door on three men and one woman, which was two men more than I'd budgeted for. The woman stood slightly forward of the others, the way a prow stands forward of a ship, and she was looking at my shack — the salt-rotted boards, the dive gear hung like trophies of a war I'd mostly lost, the single window with its view of the harbor mudflats — with the expression of someone pricing a property for demolition.

"Cyrus Oyelaran," she said. Not a question. People who already know your name don't ask questions; they just hand you the bill.

"Vess Halloway." I said it back because refusing to say it would've looked like fear, and I wasn't afraid yet. That came later, around minute eleven.

"You know me."

"I know the name. Salvage syndicate. You buy paper from men who run out of luck." I leaned on the doorframe. "I'm flattered. Usually they send a clerk."

"The clerk sends letters. Letters get ignored." She stepped past me into the shack without being invited, which takes a certain build, and her two men followed her in the way weather follows a front. The third stayed outside, and I noticed he'd positioned himself by the door rather than anywhere else, which is the position of a man whose job description contains the word *door*.

My shack is not large. Four of us in it made the walls feel decorative.

Halloway picked up a jar of my spare O-rings, turned it in her hands, set it down exactly where it had been but somehow differently. "You dove the Meridian wreck Tuesday," she said. "Navy-sealed site. You went down twice, came up with documents, burned half of them in a coffee can on your own dock like an amateur, and kept the rest somewhere in this room."

The coffee can. Of course. I'd burned the pages that were water-warped past reading, thinking fire was tidy, thinking nobody watched a man burn trash on his own dock. It's a lesson I keep relearning: somebody always watches. The coast is made of eyes.

"That's trespassing on a classified site," I said. "If you're threatening to report me—"

"I don't report people, Oyelaran. Reporting involves paperwork, and paperwork involves other people reading what I know." She turned to face me fully, and up close her eyes were the flat gray of a sea right before it decides to kill you. "What I do is simpler. I collect."

She nodded to one of her men, who produced a folder and laid it open on my table — directly over the brass key, which I realized a half-second too late I hadn't hidden. Halloway looked down at it. Looked back up at me. And smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, because there was nothing in it, no warmth, no triumph even, just the mechanical satisfaction of a lock accepting a key.

Except this key was the problem.

"There it is," she said softly. "You found something. Tuesday night, low tide, record low — the almanac says lowest in nineteen years, and I read almanacs the way you read tide tables. A cave under the cliffs north of the point. You went in at dusk and came out after dark, wet to the shoulders, moving fast, and you've barely slept since. You've been to the harbor office twice asking about the 1897 survey records. You bought lamp oil, rope, and a crowbar." She ticked each item off with a finger. "A crowbar, Oyelaran. Men don't buy crowbars for wrecks. Wrecks are already open. Crowbars are for doors."

I want to tell you I played it cool. What I actually did was move my hand toward the key, and her man — the door one, now inside — shifted his weight in a way that made it very clear how that motion would end. So I put my hand on the table instead, next to the key, like we were all just friends admiring brass.

"Your contract," Halloway said, "stands at four thousand, two hundred marks. Principal, interest, and the fees for the recovery vessel you damaged last spring when you tried to lift a cannon off the *Kestrel* alone." She let that hang. The *Kestrel* incident had cost me a winch, most of my dignity, and a season's income. "You've paid nothing against it in five months. Standard practice at that point is asset seizure. Your boat. Your gear. This—" she glanced around the shack without bothering to disguise her opinion of it "—magnificent estate."

"So seize them," I said. "Take the boat. Take the compressor. I'll start over. I've started over before."

"Yes," she said. "That's what makes you expensive. Men who can start over are bad collateral. They always think there's another boat, another season, another chance." She pulled out a chair — my only chair — and sat down in it, folding her hands, settling in like she owned the chair, the table, the tide. "So I'm not here for the boat."

* * *

Here's what I knew about Vess Halloway before that morning, gathered the way harbor men gather everything, in fragments over beer: she'd started as a diver herself, twenty years back, working the same cold water I worked, and had gotten out of the water and into the paper. She bought salvage debts the way other people bought stamps. She'd ruined at least four divers I could name, and one of them, Tam Corragan, had walked into the sea the winter after she took his boat, and nobody had ever proved anything about that except everybody believed it.

And here's what I learned about her in the next ten minutes, which was worse: she was patient. Patience in a creditor is like patience in a shark. It doesn't mean mercy. It means she can afford to wait for the exact right bite.

"The Provision," she said.

The word landed in the shack like a dropped anchor. I kept my face still, which took work, because every hair on my arms had stood up at once.

"Every wreck diver knows the story," she went on. "1897, the Halvard Expedition, nine souls go into the range with a cargo they called the Provision, and none of them come out, and the cargo never surfaces. A century of divers wasting bottom time on a fairy tale." She smiled her nothing-smile again. "I don't believe in fairy tales. I believe in behavior. And your behavior for the past three days has been the behavior of a man who stopped believing in fairy tales too — because he met one."

"I dove a cave," I said. "There was nothing in it. Some old crates, rotted through. A crest carved on the wall. If you know the story, you know the crest proves nothing. Half the coast has that crest on it somewhere. Expedition stenciled their mark on everything they carried."

"All true," she agreed pleasantly. "Which is why I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm offering you a transaction." She slid the folder aside, and there was the key between us, gleaming, and she looked at it the way I'd been trying not to look at it all night. "You owe me four thousand two hundred. The key clears the debt. All of it. Today. And I'll tear up the contract in front of you."

"And if I say the key opens nothing? An old crate. A strongbox full of spoiled ship's biscuit."

"Then you've sold me a piece of brass for four thousand marks, and you'll sleep very well tonight, and so will I." Her gray eyes came up to mine. "But you won't say that. Because if it were nothing, you'd have sold it to the antiquary in town for fifty marks and been done. Instead you've kept it, slept beside it, gone back to the harbor office digging through 1897 records. You know what it opens. Or you know enough to want to find out. And that, Oyelaran, is worth more than your debt to me. That's worth everything."

The room was very quiet. Outside, a gull said something rude and wheeled away.

I looked at the key and thought about the dead man's satchel, the leather gone stiff as shell, the way the chamber had smelled of cold stone and old iron and time. I thought about the journal page I'd left behind because taking it had felt like one theft too many, and how the handwriting had shaken near the end, and how one line had stayed with me the way a splinter stays: *we did not carry it for ourselves.* I'd assumed it meant the cargo. Standing in my own kitchen with a syndicate queen sitting in my only chair, I started to wonder if it might mean something else entirely.

"No," I said.

Halloway didn't move. "No?"

"No. I'm not selling it. Not to clear the debt, not for four thousand, not for forty thousand." I heard my own voice and was surprised by it — steady, almost bored, when inside I was screaming at myself with both fists. *Are you insane? Hand her the brass and walk free. FREE, Cyrus.* But my mouth had apparently held a meeting without me and voted. "Find another fairy tale."

Something flickered behind her eyes — not anger. Recalculation. Sharks recalculate. "You understand what happens now. I take the boat tomorrow. The gear. I lien whatever you earn for the rest of your natural life, and I have lawyers who can make a lien feel like weather — everywhere, constant, impossible to escape."

"I understand."

"You nearly drowned Tuesday coming out of that cave. Tide chart says you had maybe four minutes of margin. Next time there won't be margin, and I won't care, because you'll be diving my water to pay my debt until the sea does my collecting for me." She stood. The chair scraped. "Men die holding things, Oyelaran. I've watched it. It's the least mysterious thing in the world."

"Then you've saved me the mystery," I said. "Get out of my house."

Her two men looked at her. She looked at me for a long moment, and I understood that I was being measured — weighed against the debt, against the risk, against some ledger she kept behind those gray eyes where human beings were entries. Then she nodded once, almost respectfully, the way you nod at a card player who makes a stupid bet with total conviction.

"Burn your contract, then," she said. "Since you're feeling dramatic."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because later — much later — people would ask me when exactly I'd crossed the line, when the life I'd been limping through ended and the other thing began. And the true answer is: standing at my own stove at seven in the morning, feeding a contract into the flames page by page while the woman who owned it watched.

It wasn't noble. Let's kill that idea right now. Burning the contract changed nothing legally — Halloway had copies, obviously, probably in triplicate, probably in a vault — and I knew it. Burning it was theater. It was a man with four thousand marks of debt and one piece of brass telling the world he'd rather be hunted than hollow. It was the stupidest financial decision of my life, and I have made some genuinely historic ones.

But God, it felt good.

The paper caught and curled, ink going brown then black, my name — *Cyrus J. Oyelaran, debtor* — blooming into flame and lifting off the page as smoke. Halloway watched it with her head tilted slightly, like a woman watching weather she'd predicted.

"Sentiment," she said, when the last corner died. "It's the most expensive material on earth. Divers drown chasing it. Debtors starve on it." She buttoned her coat — it was not cold — and moved to the door, her men falling in around her. On the threshold she paused, and didn't turn around, and spoke to the harbor mudflats as much as to me.

"For what it's worth, I hope you're right about the cave. I hope there's something under that mountain worth dying for, because you've just priced yourself out of living for anything else." A beat. "Thursday's tide is lower than Tuesday's. I'll have a man on the cliffs. Whatever you go back for, he'll see where you go after."

Then she was gone, down my rotten steps and along the mudflat path, her men strung out behind her like a sentence being delivered, and I stood in my doorway holding a tin ashtray full of my own signature and felt the strangest sensation in my chest.

It took me a moment to identify it, because it had been so long.

Relief.

Terrible, doomed, magnificent relief. The decision had been taken out of my hands by my own mouth, and there is a horrible freedom in that. Every path but one was closed now, and the one remaining path ran straight through a navy cover-up, a syndicate hunt, and a mountain range that ate expeditions whole — and I stood there grinning at the smoke like a man who'd just been told the fever was terminal and therefore he could stop pretending he'd rest and recover.

I went inside and packed.

Rope, lamp oil, the crowbar Halloway had mocked, dry clothes in an oilskin bag, the brass key wrapped in a sock and tucked against my ribs where it lay warm as a second heartbeat. I was rolling my dive log — the real one, the one the navy hadn't found — when I remembered the girl.

Quentin. Harbor kid, twelve or thirteen, sharp as broken glass, living rough around the chandlery since her father went into the range and didn't come back. Three nights ago, before any of this, she'd been sitting on my dock pilings eating a stolen apple and watching me clean gear, and she'd seen the key when I emptied my kit bag — seen it for half a second — and said, "That's the Halvard mark. My dad had a rubbing of it. He was hunting that expedition when he disappeared."

I'd laughed it off. Given her the apple core and sent her home, wherever home was for a girl like that.

Now I stood in my shack with a syndicate's eyes on the cliffs and a navy's interest in my logs and a key warm against my ribs, and I thought: *she recognized it instantly. She's spent her whole short life around maps and crests and expedition papers. And she has no reason to love me, and every reason to help me, and nowhere else to go.*

Which, when I write it down like that, sounds calculating. It wasn't. Mostly. There was something else under it, some instinct I didn't have a name for yet — the same instinct that had made me leave the dead man's journal in the chamber, that had made me take the key at all. Something about not carrying things alone.

Outside, on the cliffs north of the point, Halloway's man was already settling in with a spyglass to watch the tides.

So I wouldn't be going north.

I slung the bag, doused the lamp, and stepped out into the gray morning light of the harbor town, heading the opposite direction from the cave — inland, toward the chandlery, toward a stolen apple and a pair of eyes that had read a crest faster than any antiquary in the county.

Behind me, my contract cooled in its tin, ash already.

Ahead of me, a twelve-year-old was about to become the most important person in my life, though it would take a mountain range and a blizzard before either of us admitted it.

The tide, I noted, was already turning.
