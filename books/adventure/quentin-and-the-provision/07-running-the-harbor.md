## 7. Running the Harbor

I'd like to tell you I left Saltmere Bay like a man with a plan. That I had a route mapped, a boat chartered, a bag packed with sensible things like rope and rations and a change of socks.

What actually happened was that I was eating cold beans out of the tin at half past nine at night when someone tried my door handle.

Not knocked. Tried. The slow, patient turn of a hand that expected the door to be unlocked, and when it wasn't, didn't curse or knock — just went quiet. Which is worse. A drunk curses. A burglar swears. But a professional goes still, and you can hear the stillness through cheap pine boards if you've spent enough of your life listening to water move under hulls.

I set down the beans. I picked up the brass key off the table — because apparently I'd become the kind of idiot who kept it in arm's reach instead of buried somewhere sane — and I put it in my coat pocket, and I went out the back window into the alley behind my shack while three men came in the front.

They were polite about it, at least. I heard one of them say "Mr. Oyelaran?" in a reasonable voice as I dropped into the mud, and another say something about the syndicate wanting only a conversation. Vess Halloway's men always used the word *conversation* the way other people used the word *drowning*.

The alley stank of fish heads and creosote. I went left, away from the harbor road, because going right meant lamplight and witnesses, and witnesses in Saltmere Bay were worth exactly what you paid them, which was usually nothing. My heart was doing something acrobatic and unbecoming. Behind me, the door of my shack opened, and a flashlight beam swept the room I'd just vacated, and a voice said, calm as tide tables: "Back window."

So much for the head start.

Here's the thing about running through a fishing town at night: everything is either a trap or an obstacle, and often both. Crab pots stacked like cairns. Nets hung to dry, turning alleys into curtains. Boats on skids where boats should not be. I knew Saltmere's bones better than most — I'd been diving her waters for six years, hauling up other people's losses — but knowing a town and fleeing through it are different skills, the way knowing how to swim and drowning slowly are different skills.

I cut through the net-drying yard behind Pell's chandlery, which was a mistake, because the nets at night are exactly the color of nothing, and I went face-first into a wall of hemp that smelled like forty years of herring. Somewhere behind me, boots crunched on shell. Two sets now. Maybe three. They weren't running, which frightened me more than running would have. Men who run are worried. Men who walk are certain.

I got untangled, lost some skin, gained some perspective, and came out the far side of the yard onto the shingle below the sea wall, where the town keeps its smaller boats hauled up for winter. And there she was.

Quentin.

Sitting cross-legged on the deck of a beached survey boat like she'd been waiting for me specifically, which, as it turned out, she had.

"You're loud," she said. "For a man being chased."

"How did you—"

"Your shack lit up like a stage play. Three men, front door, no lanterns. Nobody visits you, Cyrus. You told me so yourself, Tuesday, in the tone of a man bragging." She stood up on the deck, small and steady against the starlight. "Get under the boat. There's a gap between the keel and the shingle. Go on."

I want it noted that I am a grown man, a professional diver, a person who has stared down thirty fathoms of black North Atlantic without flinching, and a twelve-year-old told me to hide under a boat, and I hid under the boat.

The boots arrived maybe ninety seconds later. I lay in the cold sand with my cheek against weed-smelling timber and watched three shapes pass along the tideline, flashlights hooded, moving with the unhurried competence of men who were being paid by the hour and would find me eventually regardless. One of them stopped ten feet from the boat. I held my breath until colors started swimming.

"Nothing here," he said finally, and moved on.

When they'd gone, Quentin knelt and peered under the hull at me. In the dark her eyes were all that showed, two bright points above a grin.

"Under there," she said, "you look exactly like a crab."

* * *

We gave it twenty minutes before we moved, which was nineteen minutes longer than I wanted and probably saved my life. Quentin spent them doing something I couldn't follow — small movements, hands busy in the dark, a whisper of canvas and rope. When she finally said "now," and we slid out from under the hull, I found she'd rigged the boat's tarpaulin to look undisturbed, weighted its edges with stones, and generally made the world believe nothing had happened here tonight.

"Your father taught you that?"

"He taught me that people see what they expect to see. He said a map is just a lie everyone agrees on." She said it lightly, but there was a catch underneath, the kind of catch you hear in a hull that's taken a knock. Her father had been gone four months. She talked about him the way sailors talk about weather that killed their friends — constantly, casually, and never quite all the way through.

The boat was called the *Meridian*. Twenty-two feet of old survey launch, mahogany planks gone silver, a little auxiliary engine that coughed like a consumptive. Her father's boat. It sat on a cradle above the high-tide line among a dozen others, and I understood then what she'd been doing all evening: not hiding me. Preparing.

"There's a slip at the north end," she said. "Old one, past the ice house. Nobody uses it since the cannery closed. If we can get the Meridian down and floated before they think to look at the water—"

"They'll look at the water," I said. "Halloway's people will look at the water first. It's the only way out of this town."

"Not the only way." She looked at me with an expression I'd learn to know well over the coming weeks, the expression of a girl who had done arithmetic other people hadn't gotten to yet. "But it's the fastest, and fast matters tonight, because those navy men who took your logs? They're not staying at the Anchor House. I checked. Their launch is fueled and their lights are on. They're leaving tonight too, or they're waiting for something, and either way we don't want to be here when they decide."

"When did you check all this?"

"After I saw your shack light up. While you were being a crab."

There was, I reflected, being dragged by the sleeve through the dark toward the north end of Saltmere Bay, a version of my life where I was the competent one. Where I was the adult. Six years of salvage work will let you build that version pretty comfortably, right up until the night a sixth-grader out-thinks you twice in an hour and you spend part of it hiding under a boat.

The ice house loomed up black against the lesser black of the sky, and beyond it the old cannery slip — rotted pilings, a ramp of green-slick concrete running down into water flat as poured lead. Getting the Meridian off her cradle was the work I could actually do. I'd launched and recovered boats in worse conditions with worse help. We laid rollers — Quentin knew where the chandlery kept its spares, of course she did — and we walked the hull down the ramp inch by inch, both of us straining, neither of us speaking, the whole town holding its breath around us.

Halfway down, a flashlight bloomed at the top of the harbor road.

Quentin saw it before I did. Her hand closed on my arm — small fingers, surprising strength — and she pulled us both down behind the ice house's loading dock, into the smell of ancient fish slime and ammonia, and we crouched there while the beam swung across the slip, across the ramp, across the dark water where the Meridian now sat half-floated and rocking gently against the pilings like a guilty conscience.

Two figures on the road. Navy coats. Not Halloway's men — these moved differently, official, checking angles rather than searching shadows.

"The cave," Quentin breathed. "They're watching the water because of the cave."

"They don't know I have the key."

"They don't need to know. They just need to not let anyone leave." She was quiet a moment. Then: "Can you swim it?"

"Swim what?"

"Push the boat out past the point, quiet, and row from there. Engine's too loud. I can hold the tiller straight if you pull." She said it the way she said everything, as though it were obvious, as though the only question was why I was slow.

"Quentin. If they catch us, they take me in for questioning, and you go to whatever passes for authorities here, which is nobody, which means—" I stopped, because finishing that sentence meant saying out loud what happens to a twelve-year-old with no parents and no papers in a company town, and I found I couldn't.

"I know what happens to me," she said, in a voice with no self-pity in it at all, which was somehow the worst thing I'd heard all week. "Do you?"

The searchlight swept away up the road. The moment was a door standing open, and I have spent enough of my life watching doors close to know better than to stand in one admiring the view.

"Yes," I said. "Let's swim."

* * *

The water was the kind of cold that doesn't hurt at first. That's the trick of it, the lie of it — the North Atlantic greets you almost gently, and then, five minutes in, presents its bill. I pushed the Meridian out from the pilings with my shoulders under her transom, feet finding purchase on nothing, and swam her out low and slow, breathing through my nose in little sips, while Quentin lay flat in the stern with the tiller lashed amidships and her chin on her arms, steering by stars she named silently to herself. I could see her lips moving. Her father's daughter, navigating by the same sky he'd taught her.

Behind us the navy launch sat at anchor with its riding lights on, close enough that I could hear voices carrying over the water, two officers arguing about fuel or orders or the price of tea. We passed them at fifty yards. Forty. At thirty yards a gull — a gull, at midnight, with the personal timing of all agents of chaos — went up off the water beside us shrieking like a murdered soul.

Both men came to the rail.

I sank. Full exhale, down into the black, hanging in water cold enough to stop thought, holding the gunwale with one hand while the boat drifted on above me and my lungs began composing strongly worded letters of complaint. Through the hull I felt rather than heard the officers' voices, indistinct, unhurried. Counting. I counted with them. At sixty I surfaced inside the shadow of the hull, gasping as quietly as a man can gasp, which is not very.

The officers had gone back to their argument. The gull, its work done, flapped off to ruin someone else's evening.

"Cyrus." Quentin's whisper came down through the planks, thin and urgent. "You're bleeding."

I put a hand to my forehead and it came away warm and wet. The net-yard, or the ramp, or the pilings — the night had been generous with edges. "It's nothing."

"It's a trail. Blood carries on water. Papa said sharks can smell it a mile off, and even if there aren't sharks, there are eyes." A pause, and then, with terrible practicality: "Get in the boat. Row. Rowing is quieter than dragging a body through the water, and you're starting to look like a body."

I got in the boat. I rowed. My shoulders burned, my head throbbed, and somewhere off our port bow the navy launch's lights grew smaller and then, blessedly, swung away as its engine turned over — heading out to sea on whatever errand owned them, away from us, away from the cave, away from the key sitting in my coat pocket like a small brass sun.

We cleared the point as the sky in the east went from black to the color of a bruise deciding whether to heal. Quentin shipped the tiller, started the little engine — it caught on the third try, coughed, caught again — and brought us around south with the competence of someone who had done this before, many times, with a taller person in the bow.

For a while neither of us spoke. The town fell away astern, a scatter of lamps going out one by one as Saltmere went to bed believing itself safe. Halloway's men were still in it somewhere, walking streets I'd walked an hour ago. The syndicate's reach ran up every road out of that bay like rot through a hull. But roads, it turned out, were not the only way out, and I owed that knowledge to a child in an oversized oilskin jacket.

"You could have stayed home," I said finally. "You could have let them take me and been clear of it. Whatever this is — the key, the crest, all of it — it's mine. My mess."

She didn't answer right away. She was watching the compass — her father's compass, brass-cased, the glass scratched — and when she spoke she didn't look at me.

"You said the crest on the key was from the 1897 expedition. The Provision expedition." A wave lifted us and set us down. "My father's last letter had that crest drawn on it. Corner of the page, small, like he didn't want anyone to see it. He wrote, 'Found the mark, Q. Going up to confirm.' And then he didn't come back." She adjusted the tiller a degree that meant nothing to me and everything to her. "You're not my mess, Cyrus. You're my map to him. So sit down before you fall down, because I'm not explaining to the coast guard why I lost the diver."

I sat down. There was blood drying on my collar and salt in my throat and a syndicate contract burning a hole in my future, and none of it — none of it — felt as heavy as the look on that girl's face when she said *he didn't come back*, and none of it felt as light as the fact that she'd said *the diver* like I was hers to lose.

Somewhere ahead, the range stood up against the dawn, white-shouldered and indifferent, keeping whatever it kept. The Meridian puttered south. And I understood, with the clarity that only terror and gratitude produce in equal parts, that I had crossed some line tonight that had nothing to do with tides or debts — that the chase was no longer about what I was running from.

It was about who I was running with.

Quentin glanced back once, at the town, at the sea between us and it, and then forward, at the mountains, and her jaw set in a way I recognized from photographs I hadn't seen yet and a man I'd never meet.

"Hold on," she said. "Papa's letter says the trailhead starts at a river mouth sixty miles down the coast. And Cyrus?"

"Yeah?"

"We're not stopping anywhere they've ever heard of us."
