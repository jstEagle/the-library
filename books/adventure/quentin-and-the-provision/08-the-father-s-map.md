## 8. The Father's Map

The thing about grief that nobody tells you — because the people who know are too busy being sad, and the people who don't know are too busy saying things like *time heals* — is that it doesn't feel like sadness. It feels like a job. A job with no wages, no days off, and a supervisor who never shows up but somehow always knows when you've slacked.

I'd been doing that job for eleven months. Every morning I woke up in the loft above Marta's net shed, and every morning I checked the door to see if Papa had come back in the night. He hadn't. Eleven months of not. I was getting very good at it.

Which is why, when Cyrus Oyelaran sat across from me on the overturned dory with a brass key stamped with a crest like a compass rose wearing a crown, I didn't cry. I did inventory.

*One key. One stranger. One crest I have seen exactly once before, in ink, in my father's handwriting, on a map he thought I couldn't read.*

I could read it. I was nine when he started teaching me his shorthand, mostly so he'd have someone to complain to about the Survey Office's cartographers, who he said drew coastlines "like they were guessing." Papa believed children should be useful. I intend to be useful my whole life out of spite.

"You keep looking at this like you've seen it before," Cyrus said. He turned the key over in his big diver's hands, careful, the way you hold something that might be load-bearing.

"I've seen the crest," I said. "Not the key."

"Where?"

"That's a trade question."

He blinked. Most adults blink when I say things like that. It's one of the few reliable pleasures left in my life.

"A trade," he repeated.

"I show you where I've seen it. You tell me where you got the key." I hugged my knees up on the dory's hull. The harbor smell came off the water behind him — tar, fish blood, the green rot under the pilings. Home smells. Or what passed for home now. "And don't lie. You're bad at it. I watched you talk to Officer Brannic yesterday outside the chandlery and you lied about your own name."

"I lied about—" His ears went red. Divers' ears go red easily; there's all that pressure down there rearranging things. "That was tactical."

"It was terrible. You called yourself 'Mr. Smith' while standing next to your own boat with your name painted on it."

He looked at the boat. The boat said OYELARAN in flaking white letters, large enough to read from the breakwater. He looked back at me with the expression of a man recalculating his entire assessment of a situation.

"Tactical," he said again, weaker.

"Cyrus," I said. "I'm twelve, not stupid. There's a difference and it matters."

* * *

Here is what I knew, laid out flat the way Papa taught me to lay out survey data — facts first, feelings later, and never let the feelings hold the pencil:

Fact: Papa worked for the Coastal Survey for nineteen years. He mapped the northern range twice, the fjords once, and the karst country until the Office told him to stop wasting paper on "geological curiosities."

Fact: Last spring he took a private commission. He wouldn't say who paid. He packed the good theodolite, the one with the brass fittings, and kissed the top of my head and said, "Keep the ledger, Q. If I'm late, you know what to do."

Fact: He was due back June third. It is now nearly April of the following year.

Fact: In November, a fisherman found his survey satchel wedged in rocks at the mouth of Greywater Creek, forty miles inland, empty except for a tin of pencils and a letter addressed to me.

I had read that letter four hundred times. I could recite it the way other kids recited sea shanties. But I had never shown it to anyone, because showing it meant admitting he wasn't coming back, and admitting that meant the job of grieving would get bigger, and I was already working double shifts.

Now there was a man on my dory holding a key with Papa's crest on it, and the job had just changed scope.

* * *

We went up to the loft. I made him wait on the ladder rung while I checked that everything was where it should be — ledger on the desk, letter in the tin, map under the floorboard by the stove. Habit. Papa's habits, kept running like a lighthouse nobody needs anymore but I light anyway.

The loft was one room. Bed, desk, stove, and the wall.

The wall was Papa's real monument. He'd papered it with charts — the whole northern range, from the coastal hills to the high passes, drawn in his own hand because the official maps were, in his professional opinion, "drawn by men who hate mountains." Every contour was his. Every elevation note. And threaded through them, in his shorthand, ran a line of symbols I'd spent eleven months decoding, because if he couldn't come home to explain them, then explaining them was my job now.

Cyrus stepped in and stopped like he'd hit glass.

"Your father drew all this?"

"He drew all of it. The Office maps are copies of his work with the interesting parts removed." I pulled the tin from the shelf, took out the letter, and held it a moment longer than necessary. Feelings later, Quentin. "You first. Where did you get the key?"

So he told me. The cave under the cliffs, the record low tide, the chamber with the 1897 crest cut into stone, the dead man's satchel, the key. He told it plainly, which I appreciated, and he didn't embellish, which surprised me, and when he got to the part about nearly drowning he didn't make himself heroic, which surprised me more. He said, "The water came back faster than I climbed," and shrugged, as though almost dying were a scheduling problem.

"You took only the key," I said. "From a room full of whatever else."

"There was money in that room. Coin. Enough to clear what I owe and then some." He looked at the chart wall instead of at me. "I left it."

"Why?"

"Because taking the key felt like inheriting something. Taking the coin felt like robbing a grave." A pause. "Also the tide was coming in and I'm not fast on land."

There it was — the wry deflection of a man who felt things too big to say straight. I recognized it because I used it myself. Fine. We spoke the same language.

I handed him the letter.

* * *

Papa's hand was small and exact, even at the end. Even afraid, maybe. Read it yourself; I've earned the right to make someone else do the work:

*My Q —*

*If you're reading this, I was right about the trailhead and wrong about the timing, or right about both and unlucky, and either way I'm sorry, because you hate being sorry-for.*

*I found it. Not the treasure — there isn't treasure, I think that's the whole trick of it — but the trailhead. The Provision is real, but it isn't what the stories say. The 1897 people carried something through the range and cached it, and the navy has been looking for it ever since, and I believe the reason the Office pulled me off the karst surveys is that somebody upstairs got asked questions by somebody in a uniform.*

*I'm going up ahead of the snows. The pass I've marked — the one I call the Kettle, west shoulder of the range — closes early most years. If I'm not back by June, take the ledger, take the map, and decide for yourself whether to follow. That's not permission. You never needed permission. It's just me telling you that you're allowed to want to.*

*The shorthand on the western sheet is yours now. I finished teaching you because I wanted you to be able to find me. Forgive me for making finding me into homework.*

*— P*

Cyrus read it twice. When he looked up, his eyes were wet, and he pretended they weren't, and I pretended not to notice, and that mutual pretending was, I would later understand, the foundation of our entire relationship.

"The Kettle," he said. "West shoulder."

"High pass. Closes with the first real snowfall. Some years that's November. Some years—" I tapped the window, where the peaks stood white to the waist already, and it was only April. "Some years it's early."

"And your father went up alone."

"With gear for six weeks. He had till the snows. He thought that was enough." My voice did something I didn't authorize. I clamped down on it. Inventory, Quentin. Facts. "It's been eleven months. I know what that means. I'm not going up there to find him alive. I'm going up there because the letter says the Provision isn't treasure, and because the navy confiscated three of Papa's old survey folios from the Office two months after he vanished, and because somebody in a uniform asked Marta last week whether 'the Dunmore girl' still had his papers."

Cyrus went very still. "They've been asking about you?"

"They've been asking about the *papers*. I'm incidental. Which stings, frankly."

"And you have the papers."

"I have the map." I turned to the wall, to the western sheet, and put my finger on the line I'd traced a hundred times in the dark. "Look. This symbol — circle with a notch — appears here, here, and here. Waystations. I found the pattern in his older folios from the karst years. He marked them as 'provision points.' That word again. Provision. He wasn't being poetic. The 1897 expedition built supply caches along their route, and my father found the trailhead to the last one."

"And the pass?"

I moved my finger north, up through the contours, to a saddle between two peaks drawn with unusually dense hatching — Papa's shorthand for *this will try to kill you*.

"The Kettle. Here. It drops into a high valley the official maps show as impassable, because the official maps were drawn by men who never climbed it. Papa surveyed it in '09 from the far side. He wrote in the margin—" I switched to reading his shorthand aloud, which always made my chest hurt, because it was his voice, compressed into ink. "*Passable six weeks a year if the wind cooperates and God is distracted.*"

"When does it close?"

"First sustained snow. Could be October. Could be—" I looked at the peaks again. White to the waist in April. "Could be sooner than anyone likes."

Cyrus stood in front of the wall for a long time. I watched him do arithmetic — I could see it moving behind his face, debts and divers and navy officers asking about cave chambers, all of it being weighed against a brass key and a dead cartographer's shorthand.

"You want to follow his route," he said finally.

"I want to finish his route. Those aren't the same thing." I took the letter back and put it in the tin, lid on, click. "He found the trailhead. He marked the waystations. He mapped the pass. Everything he did was leading somewhere, and then he stopped, and nobody finished it. Nobody *could* finish it. The shorthand's mine. The map's mine. The wanting-to is mine." I made myself look at him straight. "You inherited a key, Cyrus. I inherited instructions. Yours opens a door. Mine tells us which door."

He laughed — short, startled, real. "How old did you say you were?"

"Twelve. Keep up."

* * *

We planned until the lamp burned low. That's the part I'll remember longest, I think, when I'm old — not the fear that came later, not the mountain, but this: two strangers bent over a dead man's charts, our heads close together, building a route out of his lines like we were setting bones.

Cyrus knew tides and currents and how much weight a body can carry. I knew contours and campsites and which of Papa's margin notes meant *danger* versus which meant *beautiful, dangerous*. Between us we stitched together something neither of us could have made alone, and I caught him glancing at me sideways more than once with an expression I decided not to name yet.

"We'll need a guide," he said, near midnight. "Real one. Mountain-certified. Neither of us knows the high country."

"I know the high country on paper."

"Paper's not ice." He rubbed his face. "There's a woman. Odile Ferran. Used to guide out of the valley towns. She lost a client two seasons back and nobody will hire her since."

Something cold walked down my spine, quiet, polite, and sat down in the corner of the room without introducing itself. *Lost a client.* Two seasons back. Spring. The season Papa went up.

"What was the client's name?" I asked.

Cyrus shrugged. "Never heard it. Story is she turned back and the client kept going. Up into the northern range." He yawned, jaw cracking. "Why? You know her?"

"No," I said, which was true.

But the cold thing in the corner shifted its weight, and I lay awake long after Cyrus bedded down on the floor boards, staring at the ceiling beams, doing the kind of arithmetic Papa never taught me — the kind where the numbers are dates and the answer is a shape you don't want to look at directly.

Spring. Two seasons back. Turned back. Kept going. Alone.

No. Too neat. The world wasn't that neat. Papa always said coincidence was just a pattern you hadn't surveyed properly yet.

Fine. Then we'd survey it.

I got up in the dark, lit the lamp low, and added a line to the ledger in my best copyhand, the way I did every night, keeping the books of a business with one employee and no income: *Route drafted. Guide required. Pass open — barely. Weather unknown. Father's position: unknown. Intentions: known.*

Underneath, smaller, where nobody would see it but me:

*Hold the pencil, Q. He'd want the lines straight.*

Outside, the peaks stood white under a fat indifferent moon, and somewhere up there past the Kettle, past the waystations, past the place where his trailhead began, the Provision waited — whatever it was — patient as snowfall, and closer than any of us knew.
