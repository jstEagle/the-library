## 9. Hiring a Disgraced Guide

There are three ways to learn that your name has been struck from the guide rolls of the Corvane Range, and I have had the misfortune of experiencing all of them.

The first is the letter, which arrives on expensive paper and uses words like *regrettably* and *standards* and *in light of the incident*. The second is the look on the faces of outfitters when you walk into their shops — a particular sliding of the eyes, as though you were a stain on an otherwise clean counter. The third is the silence. That's the one that gets you. For eleven years my name was spoken in every basecamp hut from Harrow's Landing to the Red Stair trailhead, usually with affection, occasionally with profanity, always with respect. Now it isn't spoken at all. It sits in rooms like a dropped tool nobody wants to pick up.

So when the knock came at my door in Lower Tarnish — three knocks, uneven, like someone who wasn't sure he wanted to be there — I already knew it wouldn't be the guild offering me reinstatement.

I opened the door on a man built like salvage: dense, salt-cured, with the kind of face that had been underwater more often than above it. Behind him stood a girl of maybe twelve, wearing her suspicion like a coat two sizes too big for her.

"You're Odile Ferran," the man said.

"Depends who's asking."

"Cyrus Oyelaran." He didn't offer a hand. Smart. "This is Quentin."

"I know who you are," the girl said. Not to me — to him, correcting him, as if my identity were a fact she'd been assigned to guard. She looked me over the way her father must have looked at contour lines: reading elevation, judging steepness, deciding whether the route was worth the risk. Whatever she concluded, she kept it behind her teeth.

"And I know who *you* are," I said to Oyelaran. "The diver. The one who pulled things off the navy wreck last month and then went quiet about it. Harbor talk travels faster than weather."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt exactly. Guilt would have been easier to work with. This was calculation, quick and tired, a man doing sums he didn't like.

"We need a guide," he said. "Into the range. High route, before snowfall."

"Everyone needs a guide before snowfall. Most of them want one whose name doesn't make porters quit."

"We can't hire anyone else."

"Now *that*," I said, "is the most honest thing anyone's said to me all year. Come in before the whole street starts counting my visitors."

* * *

My house in Lower Tarnish is small and smells of rope wax and old tea, and I keep it that way because comfort is a thing you stop trusting after the incident. I put water on for tea because hospitality dies last even in disgraced women, and I watched them while it heated.

The girl sat on the edge of the bench like she expected to be told to leave at any moment. Her pack was military surplus, too big for her, straps re-punched by hand — small neat holes, a child's awl-work but careful. Her boots had been resoled twice. On her lap she held a canvas map case the way other children hold a cat: possessively, with fingers that knew where not to squeeze.

Oyelaran stood. Men like him always stand, at first, in houses they don't own. He looked at my wall.

I should explain about the wall. Most guides keep their certifications framed. I keep mine in a drawer, because framing them felt like a joke after the guild letter came. What's on the wall instead is a single photograph: me, thirty-one years old, sunburned, grinning like an idiot, standing beside a client at the col below Mount Verrane. The client is mid-laugh, caught off-guard, holding up a thermos in salute.

Emeric Vance. Cartographer. The best mapmaker the range ever produced and the worst judge of his own limits, which is saying something in a profession full of both.

Oyelaran looked at the photograph for a long time. Then at the girl. Then back at the photograph. I watched him do the arithmetic, and I watched the answer land on his face like a dropped anchor.

"That's her father," he said quietly. Not a question.

"Was," I said. "Past tense is the operative grammar of my life these days."

Quentin hadn't moved. But her shoulders had come up around her ears, and her thumb had found the brass clasp of the map case and worried it, once, twice.

"Two years ago," I said, because if we were going to do this, we'd do it with the wound open and visible; I've no patience for people who hire me and pretend the scar isn't there. "Vance hired me to take him past the tree line in late season. Survey work. He had a theory about a pass the old expeditions used — the 1897 lot. I told him the window was closing. He said windows were for buildings." I poured the tea, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "On the third day the weather turned. I called it. Turned back. He refused. We argued at the high camp — properly argued, the kind where you say things you spend two years wishing you'd said differently. Then I took the clients who wanted to live down the mountain, and he went up alone."

"And?" Oyelaran said.

"And nothing. No body. No camp. The mountain kept him. The guild decided that a guide who lets a client walk into a whiteout alone has failed at the only job that matters, and they're right, so I didn't argue the verdict." I set his cup down harder than necessary. "That's what you're buying, Mr. Oyelaran. A woman who brought one client home alive out of two, and the arithmetic gets worse every time I do it in my head."

The girl spoke for the first time since entering. "He didn't die because of you."

"Quentin—" Oyelaran started.

"He didn't." She said it flat, the way she probably said *two plus two*. "He wrote to me. After. He sent a letter from the high country. He was glad. He said—" and here, for the first time, her voice did something human, cracked along a seam I recognized because I had an identical seam of my own, "—he said some routes you have to walk alone or they aren't yours."

I looked at her and felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does on scree when you've misjudged your footing. Because I remembered that letter. I'd carried it partway down myself — no. No, that was another letter, a different one, the one Vance had asked me to post from Harrow's Landing before the turn, the cheerful one, the one full of jokes about altitude and bad cooking. There had been no letter from above the whiteout. There couldn't have been.

Unless.

Unless he'd reached the waystations. Unless he'd written from somewhere up there and sent it down with weather, or a passing party, or some mechanism of the mountains I never learned. Two years I'd assumed the girl was comforting herself with a fiction. Looking at her now — at the certainty sitting in her like ballast — I understood she wasn't inventing anything. She had paper. She had his handwriting. Which meant Emeric Vance had been alive and writing *after* I turned back, which meant my guilt had been aimed at the wrong target for two years, which meant—

Which meant nothing yet. Guilt is loyal. It doesn't surrender its post just because new evidence arrives. But it does, I noticed with something like alarm, shift its weight.

"Show me the letter," I said.

She looked at Oyelaran. He gave a small nod, and I noted that too — the girl deferred to him, but only after checking, only as a choice. Interesting arrangement. Interesting man.

She opened the map case with those careful fingers and drew out a folded page gone soft at the creases, handled a thousand times. I read it standing, because my knees had opinions about sitting.

It was his hand. Unmistakably. The cramped, slanting script of a man who wrote on his knee in wind, letters leaning east like trees on a exposed ridge. And the content —

*— found the marker stone, Q-bird, the crest matches the harbor stories, it's real. Going higher. If the pass holds open I'll be at the Hollow Summit inside a fortnight. Don't let anyone tell you I was reckless. Some cargo you carry because no one else will. Keep the maps dry. — P.*

*Cargo.* Not treasure. Not gold. Cargo. Carried.

I set the letter down and discovered my tea had gone cold without my permission.

"The Hollow Summit," I said. "You want me to take you to the Hollow Summit. In late season. Following a dead cartographer's notes, with a salvage diver who won't say what he's really after, ahead of the early snows."

"Yes," said Oyelaran.

"No," said Quentin, at the same moment. They looked at each other. "Not *dead*," she amended. "Missing. Missing is different."

"Missing is different," I agreed, and heard my own voice do something strange with the words. "Missing means the mountain hasn't finished the sentence."

* * *

They stayed for supper because I made them — I find people lie less with food in front of them — and over barley and smoked fish I conducted the interview properly, the way I'd have vetted any client, except backwards, because this time I was the one being hired and I intended to be choosy about my employers.

Oyelaran lied to me four times. I counted.

He lied about why he needed to go high ("research," he said, for a man who clearly hadn't read a book since school). He lied about who else might be following (his eyes flicked to the window when he said *nobody*, and windows are where watchers live). He lied about how much money he had (a man with money doesn't count coins before paying a ferryman; I'd seen him do it at the landing). And he lied about the key.

Ah yes. The key. You thought I hadn't noticed.

He wore it wrong. That's the tell most people miss — not that a man carries something valuable, but *how* he carries it. Valuables get stowed. This he kept against his body, inner shirt pocket, left side, over the heart, and his hand went to it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth: constantly, unconsciously, checking that it still hurt. When Quentin mentioned the expedition crest — she'd seen it, drawn it from memory once, her father's sketches matched — his whole body went still in a way no innocent man's does.

And the girl. The girl watched him watch the key, and said nothing, and I understood that the two of them had an agreement of silences between them, a treaty older than their acquaintance had any right to. She knew what he carried. She'd chosen to travel with him anyway. Children make those bargains when the alternative is being alone, and I hated him a little for letting her, and hated myself more for recognizing the shape of it.

"So," I said, clearing plates. "Terms. Mine, not yours."

He straightened. "Name them."

"One: I set the pace, the route, and the turnaround times. When I say we go down, we go down. No arguments, no appeals. I've buried enough of my judgment in that range."

"Agreed."

"Two: you tell me what the key opens. Not today. Before the tree line ends. I don't lead blind clients into the high country — I've done it once and it cost me everything."

His jaw worked. "Before the tree line," he said. "Fair."

"Three." I looked at Quentin. She looked back, chin up, daring me to treat her like luggage. "The girl carries weight. Real weight. She eats a climber's portion and pulls a climber's share. I don't babysit. Babysitting gets people killed because it splits the leader's attention."

"She's carried forty pounds over scree already," Oyelaran said, and there was something in his voice that wasn't pride exactly, or wasn't only pride. Worry wearing pride's coat.

"Then she'll do," I said, and Quentin's expression didn't change but something behind her eyes banked itself, satisfied, like a stove turned low.

"Four," I said, and this one I made myself say slowly, because it was the true one and true terms are the hardest to price. "If Emeric Vance is up there — if there's anything of him up there, a camp, a cairn, papers, bones — we stop. However long it costs us. However close the snow is. That's not negotiable and it's not professional. It's just mine."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, the first wind of the season came down off the range and tested the shutters, the way it does in September, like a landlord checking whether the tenants intend to stay.

"Why?" Oyelaran asked. Not unkindly. "Why agree at all? Your name's mud. Taking this job buries it deeper if it goes wrong, and it sounds like it's going to go wrong."

And there it was, the question I'd been circling since the knock, the one I'd been avoiding because the honest answer was none of my business and all of my business at once.

Because I owed a dead man a turnaround I'd gotten right and a summit I'd refused him. Because his daughter had a letter proving he'd climbed on without me, which meant the choice had been his, which meant my penance had been levied by the wrong court. Because the word *cargo* had been eating a hole in me since supper — cargo carried because no one else will — and I had spent two years carrying mine alone. Because the guild could strike my name from a ledger, but the mountain keeps its own register, and I had a line in it unpaid.

None of which I intended to say to a liar with a key over his heart.

"Reasons of my own," I said. "You keep yours, I'll keep mine. That's term five, if you're counting."

He almost smiled. It looked unfamiliar on him, like a tool used for the wrong job. "We leave when?"

"Day after tomorrow. First light. You'll both want to sleep tonight — you won't get proper sleep again for weeks." I stood, and they took the hint, gathering their too-big pack and their precious map case. At the door, Quentin stopped.

"You knew him," she said. "My father. Before."

"I guided him six times. He was the worst cook and the best company I ever had above the tree line."

"What was he like? Up there. Really."

I considered lying to her, for mercy's sake, and rejected it — she'd had enough of people's mercies; they sit on a child like snow load on a roof.

"Like a man who belonged somewhere the rest of us were only visiting," I said. "He'd stand at a col with the wind trying to peel him off the rock, and he'd laugh. Actually laugh. I used to think it was foolishness. Now I think it was the rarest kind of sense."

She nodded once, filed it away in whatever archive she kept behind her sternum, and followed Oyelaran out into the dark.

* * *

I didn't sleep, naturally. I spent the night doing what disgraced guides do: inventorying.

Rope, good for two more seasons. Tent, patched, honest. Stove, temperamental but loyal. Rations for three, thin — I'd need to buy through at Harrow's Landing, which meant facing the outfitters' sliding eyes, which meant I'd already decided to go, hadn't I, somewhere between the photograph and the letter, and all this interviewing had been theater for an audience of one.

I dug out my old survey maps of the high country and spread them under the lamp, and there it was, the route her father's letter implied: up the Verrane approaches, past the old waystations — the 1897 expedition had built them, stone huts, some still stocked, the guild's folklore insisted — through the karst, and then the part that made my tea go cold all over again. Because beyond the waystations the maps went vague, and vague on a mountain is another word for graveyard, and the only marked line through that vagueness was in a hand I knew. Emeric's. Slanting east like trees in wind. A dashed line climbing toward the Hollow Summit, annotated in his cramped script:

*Red Stair — cut steps, ancient, sound. Trust the steps, not the scree.*

He'd found a route and walked it alone, and now his daughter meant to follow it with a stranger's key in a stranger's keeping, ahead of the snows, pursued by whoever it was Oyelaran wouldn't name.

I should refuse, I told the ceiling. I should write them a note, recommend the long safe route, and let my disgrace keep me warm.

But the wind was rising off the range, and it smelled the way it had smelled that September two years ago, and I lay in the dark listening to it test my shutters and understood, finally, what I'd known since the photograph: that the mountain had given me a sentence two years ago and I'd read it wrong. *Turned back* wasn't the end of it. It was only the clause. Somewhere up there, above the whiteout, the rest of the sentence was waiting, and it had my name in it, and possibly Emeric's, and possibly — God help me — a way to read the whole thing aloud to a girl who deserved more than fragments.

At first light there was a knock at my door. Three knocks, uneven.

Too early. Hours too early. And when I opened it, Cyrus Oyelaran stood on my step gray-faced, glancing up and down the empty street, and behind him Quentin was already looking at the mountains, and neither of them had slept either.

"They found my shack," he said. "We leave now."
