## 10. First Ascent

I've guided forty-one parties into the range, and I can tell you within the first hour which ones will come back. It isn't the gear. It isn't the fitness. It's whether they argue with the mountain or with each other. The ones who argue with each other survive. The ones who argue with the mountain are the ones I end up carrying.

So when Oyelaran and the girl showed up at my door at dawn with two rucksacks between them and a combined total of zero arguments, I gave them until noon before I started forming opinions.

The girl had braided her hair tight against the wind and packed her father's maps in oilcloth, which told me she'd been listening to someone who knew what rain does to paper. The man had new boots. Unbroken boots, on a man about to walk three hundred kilometers of broken country. That told me something too — that he was used to buying his way out of problems rather than walking through them.

"Rule one," I said, shouldering my own pack, which weighed less than either of theirs because I'd spent twenty years learning what a mountain actually asks of you versus what you think it might. "We move at my pace. Not his pace, not yours. Mine."

"What is your pace?" Quentin asked.

"You'll find out."

She didn't smile, but something behind her eyes did, and I thought: *this one will be trouble.* The good kind or the bad kind remained to be seen.

* * *

The foothills start gentle the way a lie starts gentle. A green shoulder of land, sheep tracks, a stream you can cross without wetting your knees. I took them up through the birch at a pace I'd have called moderate for a fit client and watched what happened.

By the second hour, Oyelaran was breathing through his mouth. By the third, he'd stopped trying to make conversation, which was a mercy, because everything he'd said so far had the specific texture of a man filling silence so nobody could ask him anything real. He talked about tide tables. About hull integrity. About the syndicate's rates, in general terms, as though mentioning your debts casually makes them smaller.

He never once mentioned the key.

It was in his left breast pocket. I don't know how I knew that. Maybe the way his hand drifted there every time we stopped, a small unconscious check, the gesture of a man confirming a heartbeat. Maybe it was just that everything else about him was loose and easy except that one pocket, which he guarded like a wound.

I'd agreed to take this job for reasons I hadn't shared, and one of those reasons was standing right in front of me wearing unbroken boots and lying by omission with every breath. I wanted to know what a salvage diver from the coast was doing with an 1897 expedition crest on a piece of brass. I wanted to know it badly enough to walk him into the ground until the truth fell out of him like a stone from a worn pocket.

Not yet, though. First, the ground work.

* * *

We camped the first night below the treeline, in a hollow I've used a dozen times, and I made them build the fire while I watched. Oyelaran built it like a sailor — quick, efficient, slightly wrong for altitude, where you want the heat reflected not just radiated. Quentin fixed it without being asked, shifting two stones and banking the coals, and then pretended she hadn't, watching him instead of me to see if he'd noticed.

He hadn't. She noticed that he hadn't. Something passed over her face — not quite disappointment, more like an entry in a ledger.

"You knew my father," she said to me, across the fire. Not a question. She'd decided it was true somewhere on the trail and was now presenting it for confirmation.

"I guided him," I said. "Once."

"Once."

"He liked shortcuts." I fed a stick to the flames. "That's all I'll say tonight."

"That's not all you know."

"No," I agreed. "But it's all I'll say."

Oyelaran looked between us like a man watching weather change over open water. The girl held my gaze for a long moment — twelve years old and holding a stare like a grown woman with a grievance — and then she nodded once, filed it away, and asked Oyelaran to teach her a knot for the tent line.

I lay awake after they slept and listened to the wind come down off the icefields, and I thought about the last time I'd camped in this hollow, and who had been sitting where the girl was sitting now, drawing in a notebook by firelight, asking me questions I didn't answer.

* * *

Day three, I stopped being gentle.

There's a route up through the scree below Cwm Ddu that no sensible guide uses. It's loose, steep, and pointless — there's a good path two valleys east. But it tells you everything. Who panics. Who freezes. Who lies about being tired. And most importantly, it takes eleven hours instead of six, and tired people stop performing.

I didn't tell them any of this. I just said, "Today we go up," and pointed.

Oyelaran lasted four hours before his boots started punishing him for their newness, and to his credit he didn't complain once. He just got quieter and slower and kept glancing back at the girl to make sure she was managing, which was costing him more than the climb was. That interested me. Selfish men don't spend attention like that. Whatever he was — debtor, liar, thief of dead men's satchels, and I'd already guessed at least two of those — he wasn't traveling alone out of convenience.

Quentin, meanwhile, was navigating.

I noticed it first as a small frown. She'd drop behind, consult nothing, and then call out a correction: "The line bends left above that grey band." Or: "There's water over the next rise; Papa's notes say seepage here in autumn." No map in her hands. Just a head full of her father's terrain, cross-referenced against what she saw, checked and rechecked.

"How do you know that?" I finally asked, when she called a col ninety minutes before it appeared.

"Papa taught me to read the ground backward." She scrambled up beside me, breath short but steady. "You look at where you're going and imagine walking home from it. If the way home doesn't make sense, neither does the way there."

"That's not a standard method."

"It's better than standard. Standard methods are written by people who've never been lost." She said it without arrogance, the way you'd recite scripture, and I understood I was hearing a dead man speak through his daughter. It sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Because I knew exactly how Elian Voss navigated. Backward. From the destination home. He'd explained it to me once, grinning, on a ridge I would later turn my back on, and I had told him it was clever and unnecessary, and he'd laughed and said, "Odile, one day you'll need to find your way to something everyone says isn't there."

Then I'd turned around at the storm line and gone down, and he'd gone up, and only one of us had needed the method since.

"Your father," I said, "was the best navigator I ever worked with."

She went very still. For a second I saw her as what she actually was — a child standing in the wreckage of the only person who'd ever chosen her — and then she locked it away behind that ledger-keeping face and said, "I know," and walked on.

Grudging respect, I noted. Forming. Against my will, against every plan I'd made about keeping these two at arm's length until I learned what I needed to learn.

* * *

The scree nearly broke Oyelaran in the afternoon. A slope of grey shale, thigh-deep in places, the kind of ground where every step gives back half of what you put in. His boots were bleeding him by then — I could see the dark patches at the heels — and halfway up he slipped, slid ten meters in a rattle of stone, and stopped himself on his forearms with a sound I felt in my teeth.

Quentin was moving before I was. Down the slope, sure-footed as a goat, planting her feet exactly where the rock was solid — reading it, reading it the way her father read everything, from the bottom up — and she got a hand on his pack strap and held him until he found his footing.

"I'm fine," he said.

"You're bleeding."

"I'm fine," he said again, and there was something underneath the words, some old reflex, a man who'd learned that admitting damage gets you left behind. I recognized it. I'd heard it in my own voice once, on a different mountain, turning back down toward safety while someone else went up.

"Camp's in thirty minutes," I called down. "Move."

They moved. And I stood there a moment longer than I needed to, watching the girl haul a grown man up a slope she shouldn't have been able to read, and I thought: *Elian, you absolute fool, you taught her everything except how to be small.*

* * *

That night, high under the first bare ridgeline, I checked their gear while they slept. It's my prerogative as guide and my habit as a suspicious woman, and I've never apologized for either.

Oyelaran's pack was a study in a life being abandoned. Rope, decent. Lamp, coastal pattern. A folded contract, burned at one corner and smoothed flat again, kept anyway — people keep the things that ruined them, I've never understood it. And wrapped in a sock at the bottom, because he apparently thought a guide wouldn't inventory a sock: a coil of navy signal wire, stolen or salvaged, and a sea chart of the northern coast with three caves penciled in, two crossed out.

One cave uncrossed. The one under the cliffs, I'd wager, the one the low tide opened like a mouth this season.

And in his breast pocket, the brass. I didn't touch it. I didn't need to. I'd seen the crest stamped on wax seals and waystation doors and one rusted strongbox in a museum case in the harbor town: the Provision expedition, 1897, nine souls gone into the range and one — allegedly — come back out, mad and empty-handed, raving about a cargo that was carried and never found.

Every treasure hunter on the coast had chased that story for a hundred years. The navy had sealed half the coastline over it. And now it was sleeping in the pocket of a drowning-in-debt salvage diver who lied badly and looked at a twelve-year-old girl like she was the first thing he'd ever wanted to protect more than himself.

I put the sock back. I sat outside under the cold stars and let the wind explain my options to me.

Option one: take the key in the night, walk out, sell the information to Halloway's people, who I knew were already sniffing the trail — I'd seen their sign at the trailhead, boot prints too clean, too expensive. Option two: turn them both around tomorrow, claim weather, wash my hands.

Option three.

Option three was the one where I stayed, and found out what Elian Voss had died climbing toward, and maybe finished the thing I'd abandoned on a ridge seven years ago. Option three was the one where I stopped being a woman avoiding a debt and started being a guide again, whatever that cost.

I'm not a sentimental woman. But I looked through the tent flap at the girl asleep with her father's maps under her arm like a stuffed animal, and the diver asleep with his hand over his breast pocket even in his dreams, and I thought: *the mountain doesn't care what you carry. It only cares whether you carry it together.*

* * *

Morning came white-rimed and clear, the peaks ahead sharp as cut glass, and the snowline sitting lower than it had any business sitting in early autumn. I stood looking at it while the others ate, doing arithmetic I didn't like.

"We're behind," I announced. "From today, we move at my real pace."

"This wasn't your real pace?" Oyelaran asked. His voice was hoarse. Good.

"This was me deciding whether to keep you." I kicked dirt over the fire. "Congratulations. You're both still here. Barely."

Quentin almost smiled. Almost. Then she unrolled her father's map on a flat rock, weighted the corners, and traced our line with one finger — and stopped.

"Odile." Her voice had changed. All the child gone out of it. "This mark. Here, at the head of the valley." A small symbol in her father's shorthand, ink faded to brown. "I thought it meant *water*. It doesn't. I found the same mark in his last letter." She looked up at me. "It means *waystation*. There's one of theirs up ahead. An 1897 station. Papa found it."

The wind moved through the pass behind us, and I swear the whole range leaned in to listen.

"And there's something else," she said quietly, eyes on the map, on the little brown mark that had rewritten our route. "The letter says whoever built it left a journal. And the journal says what the Provision actually is."

Oyelaran's hand went to his breast pocket. Mine went to my knife out of pure habit. And far to the east, faint as a fly, a sound reached us across the valleys that none of us wanted to hear:

An engine. High up. Sweeping the ridgelines.

"Navy," I said, and started striking camp.
