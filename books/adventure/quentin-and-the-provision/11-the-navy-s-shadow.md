## 11. The Navy's Shadow

The morning the helicopter found us, we were eating cold oats and arguing about Quentin's boots.

"You can't walk forty miles in those," Odile said, pointing with her spoon at the girl's feet as if the boots had personally insulted her.

"They've gotten me this far," Quentin said.

"This far was a boat ride and a road. The mountain doesn't care how far you've gotten."

I kept my head down over my oats, which is a skill I'd perfected over thirty-one years of other people's arguments. We'd made eleven miles out of the foothills since dawn yesterday, following a logging trace that had given up on being a road sometime before the war, and we were camped in a fold of ground where an old landslide had dropped a curtain of alder across the slope. Good cover. Odile had picked it without breaking stride, which was one of several reasons I was glad she was on our side of whatever this was.

Then the sound came up the valley.

You learn to hear machines differently when you live off salvage. Fishing boats have a rhythm to them, workmanlike, predictable. This wasn't that. This was the flat, patient throb of something military, and it was getting louder from the south, sweeping up the valley like a hand feeling along a shelf for a particular jar.

"Down," Odile said, and there was no argument left in anyone.

We flattened under the alders. Through the weave of branches I watched it come — a grey shape, unhurried, flying a search pattern so methodical it looked almost bored. It ran the valley north to south, swung wide over the ridgeline, came back. Somebody up there was reading a map and checking boxes. Valley one: clear. Valley two: clear. Valley three: two divers, one child, one disgraced guide, all of whom are now extremely interested in your schedule.

It passed directly overhead once. Close enough that the rotor wash pressed down through the branches and shook loose a fine rain of last year's leaves onto my neck. I lay there with my cheek against the cold ground and thought about the officers who'd sat in my shack three weeks ago with their clipboards and their polite questions, and how badly I'd lied to them, and how much time they'd apparently spent not believing me.

The helicopter moved on up-valley. It didn't come back. But we all stayed flat for another ten minutes anyway, because that's the thing about a machine like that — you don't know what it saw, only what it sounded like, and sound lies.

Odile rolled onto her back and exhaled through her nose. "They're running valleys," she said. "Grid pattern. That's not a training flight."

"No," I said.

"That's a search."

"I got that part."

She turned her head and looked at me. Quentin did too, from the other side, and I suddenly understood what it was like to be a specimen pinned under glass by two pairs of very different eyes — one pair calculating, one pair trusting, both waiting for me to explain why the navy was spending aviation fuel on us.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. Here's some of it."

* * *

Here is what I told them, there under the alder curtain with leaf-rot in my collar:

That after I dove the wreck — the one nobody names, the one with the manifests that shouldn't exist — I went looking for quiet water to decompress and think, and the tide went out further than I'd ever seen it go, and there was the cave. That inside the cave was a chamber cut into the rock, sealed, marked with a crest: a lantern over crossed keys, and the date 1897. That everything in it was arranged like a room someone meant to come back to. That I took one thing. One. A brass key out of a dead man's satchel, because my hands decided before my head did, and by the time my head caught up the tide was coming home and I nearly joined the dead man in his inventory.

I did not tell them about the manifests. I did not tell them what was written on them, or whose signature was at the bottom, or why a navy quartermaster in 1911 would have signed for cargo bound for a ship that officially sank in 1897. That part stayed behind my teeth, where it had been living rent-free ever since.

"And the key opens what?" Odile asked.

"I don't know yet. Something in the range. Quentin's father's letter pointed the same direction."

"Convenient."

"Everything about this is inconvenient," I said. "That's how you know it's true."

Quentin had gone very still, the way she does when she's working a problem instead of feeling it. "The men in the harbor," she said slowly. "Halloway's people. They knew about the cave too?"

"Halloway's been watching me dive for two years. If I found it, she knows I found it." I picked a leaf off my sleeve and didn't look at either of them. "And the navy confiscated my logs, which means they know roughly where I was diving, which means—"

"—they know about the cave too," Quentin finished.

"Everybody knows about the cave," I said. "The cave is the most popular place in the province and I'm the only idiot who went inside."

Odile sat up. She has a way of sitting up that makes the whole landscape sit up with her. "So we have a syndicate behind us and a navy above us, and both of them want a key you're carrying in your jacket, and you've been letting us walk daylight hours through open valleys like tourists."

"I was managing risk," I said.

"You were managing nothing. You were hoping." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Hope isn't a route plan, Oyelaran."

No argument there. Hope had never once paid my mooring fees.

"So we go dark," Quentin said.

We both looked at her. She was already pulling her father's map case out of her pack, already unrolling the corner of it against her knee, and there was something in her face I recognized from mirrors — that look of a person who has decided that if the world insists on being dangerous, it can at least be dangerous on their terms.

"Night travel," she said. "Dawn camps. Sleep in the trees, move in the dark. Papa used to survey that way when he didn't want company. He said the mountains belong to whoever's awake for them." She tapped the map. "There's a bench of forest above the river gorge. No trails, no huts, no reason for anyone to fly it slow. We climb to it tonight and stay on it until we hit the pass approach."

Odile studied the map over her shoulder for a long moment. Then she looked at me. "Well? You're the one they're hunting. Do we trust the dead cartographer or do we trust your hope?"

"The dead cartographer," I said. "Obviously."

"Good answer." She started kicking dirt over the fire pit. "We move at last light. Everyone sleeps now, in shifts. And Cyrus—" she pointed the spoon at me again; the spoon was becoming her instrument of authority "—when we stop tonight, you're going to tell me the rest of it. Not all of it. I can see you rationing. But more than you gave the girl just now, or I start making decisions without you."

"Yes, ma'am," I said, and meant it, mostly.

* * *

We climbed out of the gorge country as the light died, single file, no lamps. Odile led. Quentin navigated behind her, calling corrections in a whisper every so often — *bearing's drifted left, Papa says the bench cuts east here* — and I brought up the rear carrying most of the weight, which felt appropriately symbolic.

Walking at night does something strange to your head. Your world shrinks to the circle of ground your feet can find, and everything outside it stops existing except as sound. Alder stems brushing your shoulders. Water somewhere below, talking to itself. Behind us, once, very faint, the helicopter again — or another one like it — working a valley two ridges east. Odile stopped us in the black under a spruce and we stood there for five minutes listening to it hunt somewhere we weren't, and then she moved on, and I followed the sound of her boots like a man following a voice out of a dream.

We made the bench around midnight. A shelf of old-growth timber, moss to the ankles, the river a silver rumor far below. Odile called a halt against the trunk of a cedar big enough to be a building, and we made cold camp — no fire, no stove, just shared silence and half a block of cheese divided three ways.

Quentin was asleep in nine minutes. Twelve-year-olds can do that; they drop into sleep like stones into wells, no matter what's chasing them. I envied it the way I envied her father's maps, her certainty, her whole inherited architecture of knowing where she was. I had spent my adult life deliberately not knowing where I was, and it turns out that skill doesn't transfer.

Odile waited until the girl's breathing evened out. Then she settled across from me, knees up, forearms on them, and said, "Talk."

So I talked.

Not the manifests. Not yet. But closer. I told her about the officers in their navy coats sitting in my shack, going through my dive logs page by page while I stood there sweating like a guilty man, which is the worst way to stand when you actually are one. How the senior one — Commander somebody, a face like a closed ledger — asked me had I ever recovered documents from the northern wreck, and I said the wreck was stripped before I ever touched it, which was a lie so lazy it should have been arrested on its own. How he thanked me politely, took my logs, and told me the site was closed under a classification order he wasn't authorized to name, and that continued diving activity would be viewed as, and I quote, *unhelpful.*

"Unhelpful," Odile repeated.

"It's navy for 'we will ruin you and feel bad about it later.'"

"And you believe the wreck and the cave are connected."

I turned the brass key over in my hands. In the dark it was just weight and temperature, body-warm, heavier than any object its size had a right to be. "The crest on the chamber matches stamps in the manifests I pulled off the wreck. Same lantern, same crossed keys. Whatever the expedition carried in 1897, part of it went down with that ship, and part of it went up into the mountains, and somebody in the government has been keeping that story shut for a hundred years." I paused. "There's more. I'm not ready to say it. When I am, you'll get it first, before the girl. That's the deal I'm offering."

Odile was quiet long enough that the river got loud again.

"Why not tell her everything?" she finally asked. "She's earned it. She's carrying more of this than either of us."

"Because she's twelve, and because what I'm holding isn't a treasure map, it's a fuse, and I've watched what happens to people who stand too close when fuses find their powder." I heard myself and winced. "Also because if I say it out loud it becomes real, and I've been surviving on it maybe-not-being-real for three weeks."

"That's not a reason. That's a symptom."

"I'm aware."

She nodded slowly, and I braced for the speech — the *trust is a rope, you hold it or you cut it* speech that guides keep in their kit next to the spare carabiners. It didn't come. Instead she said, quietly, "The client I lost. Quentin's father. He also had a piece of paper he wouldn't show me. A letter. I told myself it was survey business." Her jaw worked. "People die of other people's secrets, Cyrus. I've seen it done."

"I know," I said. "I'm trying to make sure I'm the only one in the blast radius."

"Nobody's ever in the blast radius alone. That's the first thing the mountain teaches." She pulled her hood up and lay back against the cedar. "Sleep. Dawn watch is yours. And Cyrus — the deal's accepted. First, before the girl. Don't make me regret the exchange rate."

I sat watch while they slept, turning the key over and over, and the secret sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, and I thought: soon. Soon I'll put it down. Just not tonight. Tonight the valleys were full of rotors and the snow line was creeping downhill in my imagination, and a man is allowed to carry his stone one more day.

* * *

Three nights we traveled dark. It worked better than it had any right to. Quentin's father's bench held true — a corridor of unbroken timber angling northeast along the spine of the foothills, avoiding every valley, every hut, every place a helicopter pilot would think to look twice. On the second night we heard rotors twice and were under canopy both times before they crested our ridge. On the third night we heard nothing at all, which was somehow worse, silence being the sound the navy makes when it's stopped searching blind and started searching smart.

We learned each other's night habits. Odile walked like a metronome and never spoke unless the terrain changed. Quentin counted her steps under her breath — her father's trick, she said, for keeping honest distances in the dark — and could tell you within fifty paces where we'd hit any landmark on the map. Me, I listened. Thirty years of diving teaches you to build the world out of sound: current, breath, the creak of a hull, the wrong kind of silence. I listened to the mountains the same way, and on the third night, an hour before dawn, I heard something that didn't fit.

A click of metal on stone. Far upslope. Once, then deliberate silence.

I caught Odile's shoulder. She froze mid-stride, and I watched her head tilt, cataloguing, dismissing, reordering. Ten seconds. Twenty. Then she altered course without a word, taking us down and east, away from the sound, adding a mile to the day, and when we finally made our dawn camp in a hollow of juniper she said only: "Someone's pacing us. Above and behind. One person, moving careful. Could be a hunter." A beat. "Could be."

"Could be Halloway's," Quentin said. She'd woken at the course change; she always woke at course changes, as if sleep were just another survey she kept one eye on.

"Or the navy's," I said. "Spotters on foot after the helicopters come up empty. That's how I'd do it, if I were them."

"If you were them, we'd already be caught," Odile said, and I chose to take it as a compliment.

We slept in shifts through the short grey day. I dreamed about the cave — I'd been dreaming about it nightly, the water coming back in, the walls going green and dim, the dead man's satchel floating up past my face like it wanted exchanging — and woke with my heart going hard to find Quentin sitting beside me, map across her knees, watching the tree line with an expression far too old for her face.

"Cyrus." Her voice was low. "Look at the pass."

I looked. Through a gap in the juniper, the range stood up against the morning sky, and the pass we were aiming for — the gap her father's letter had named — wore a new white line across its throat. Thin. Fresh. Snow, where there had been bare rock two days ago.

"That's early," she said. "Papa's notes said the pass holds to mid-month. It's not mid-month."

I looked at the white line a long time. Somewhere behind us, a spotter was pacing our trail. Somewhere ahead, winter was pacing the calendar. And in my jacket, against my ribs, the brass key sat warm as a held breath, waiting for the door it belonged to, and for the truth I still owed the two people sleeping and waking beside me.

"We'll make it," I said, because she needed to hear it and because I'm a liar in at least two directions now.

Quentin didn't answer. She was already re-rolling the map, marking our line with a stub of pencil, and the set of her mouth said she'd heard the lie, filed it, and forgiven it — provisionally, the way you forgive weather.

Behind us, faint but unmistakable in the still morning air, a helicopter engine started up.
