## 12. Vess on the Trail

I smelled them before I saw them, which is either a compliment to my nose or an insult to their hygiene. Woodsmoke and wet wool and that particular sourness of men who've been pushing hard on rations they didn't pack enough of. Four hours behind us, maybe less if the weather held for them, which it wouldn't, because the weather had stopped holding for anybody.

We'd made the tree line by midmorning, and I had called a halt at a granite shoulder where the trail bent north into a shallow bowl of scree — ten thousand tons of broken rock waiting patiently for someone stupid to walk across it. I left Cyrus and the girl with the packs under an overhang and went up alone to look back the way we'd come.

That's when I saw the smoke. Not ours. Ours was dead since dawn; I don't cook where I sleep, and I don't sleep where I cook, and I generally don't do either where other people can find me. This smoke sat in the valley mouth like a flag planted by someone who wanted to be found — or wanted *us* to think they didn't care about being found, which is its own kind of message.

Four figures on the lower trail. Moving fast. Too fast for sightseers, too organized for lost hunters. They moved the way I used to move, before the blacklisting, before Ferran became a name people said quietly and then changed the subject. Rope spacing even. Rest stops short. One of them ranging ahead like a hound that had been given a scent.

I crouched behind a boulder with lichen the color of old dishwater and did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. They'd started from the harbor town. So had we. We had a two-day head start and they'd eaten most of it in a day and a half, which meant either they knew the route or they had someone who did.

And then the ranging one came around the bend below me, and the arithmetic stopped being arithmetic and became something with teeth.

Tomas Reyes.

I knew him by the way he walked before I knew him by the face — that rolling, heel-first stride he swore was easier on the knees and everyone else swore was just Tomas refusing to hurry for anyone, including gravity. Eleven years we'd roped together. Eleven years of routes from the Cordillera Blanca to the Alps, of storms waited out in tents barely bigger than bread loaves, of him teaching me the trick to reading avalanche slopes by the sound snow makes when you knock on it. He carried me out of a whiteout on the Dru once, on his back, singing something obscene and off-key to keep us both from thinking about the drop.

Now he was ranging ahead for Vess Halloway, and the drop was behind me, and there was no song for that.

* *

I climbed down to the overhang slower than I needed to, buying time to decide what my face would do when it got there. Faces are equipment. You maintain them.

Cyrus looked up when I dropped the last meter into the hollow. The man has a diver's stillness — he can go from motion to absolute quiet between one breath and the next, like something switching itself off. It should have been unnerving. Mostly it was useful. The girl, Quentin, was crouched over her father's map with a pencil stub, annotating it in that dense little shorthand of his, and she didn't look up at all, which told me she already read my mood better than Cyrus did.

"Company," I said.

"How many?" Cyrus asked.

"Four. Climbers. Good ones." I let a beat pass. "One of them's good because I trained beside him."

Quentin's pencil stopped.

Cyrus watched me the way he watches water — patient, waiting for whatever's underneath to show itself. "Friend of yours?"

"He was." I sat down on my pack because my legs had opinions about standing. "Tomas Reyes. We worked together eleven years. He taught me half of what I know about snow. The other half I learned from falling in it."

"And now he's working for Halloway."

"Now he's working for Halloway." I turned my hands over and studied the palms as though they might explain something. "After the accident — after I lost the client — nobody would hire me. Blacklisted guides don't get sympathy, they get distance. Tomas was the only one who kept calling. For about a year. Then the calls got shorter, then they stopped, and I told myself he was busy." I shrugged, which is what you do when the truth is too heavy to lift properly. "Busy for Halloway, apparently. Halloway pays well. Halloway always pays well. That's how she buys people — not with threats, with comfort. She finds out what you're tired of and she sells you the end of it."

"What are you tired of?" Quentin asked. She has a way of asking questions like she's setting a piton — small, precise, and load-bearing.

"Broke," I said. "Which is why I'm here, which is why he's there, and the difference between us is about four hundred meters of altitude and one bad decision each." I pulled the map toward me. "Enough biography. They're four hours back and moving like they're paid by the summit. We need to not be where this trail goes."

Cyrus nodded slowly. "Can we outrun them?"

"No. Not with the girl's legs, not with your hands—" I glanced at the bandage he'd been pretending wasn't there since the karst, "—and not uphill against men who climb for wages. We don't outrun them. We misplace them."

Quentin looked up. There was a gleam in her eye that I recognized from mirrors, from every guide who ever loved a route more than was sensible. "How?"

I pointed at the bowl of scree above us, that great gray apron of shattered rock fanning down from the ridge like something the mountain had vomited and never cleaned up. "There."

"That's not a route," Cyrus said. "That's a geology lesson."

"It's a decoy," I said. "Scree holds tracks like a ledger. Anyone crossing it leaves a record. So we write them a story."

* *

The story went like this: three travelers, exhausted, losing their nerve, deciding to cut east along the ridge toward the low col instead of holding the high line west. A party in trouble. A party making mistakes.

To write it, we actually had to walk it — there's no faking track sign on scree, not to eyes like Tomas's. So we crossed the fan, all three of us, spaced wide, boots deliberate, dragging our poles to gouge honest-looking furrows. At the far side I built a cold camp impression: a flattened patch of grass where a tent might have been, a scatter of oatmeal dust, a fire ring with ash I'd been carrying in a tin for exactly this purpose, because a guide who doesn't plan to lie on a mountain isn't a guide, she's a tourist with opinions.

Then, while the light was still good, we came back — but not the way we'd come. We climbed the eastern rib of the bowl on bare rock, where we left nothing, and looped north along the ridge on stone and old snow, and dropped into a hidden gully that Quentin found before I did.

She was ahead of me, actually, reading her father's map in the failing light, and she stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into her. "Here," she said, tapping a mark like a bird's footprint. "He wrote *shelter, dry, wind lies*. Wind lies means the map shows wind coming from the south but the rock funnels it from the east, so camp facing south looks sheltered and isn't. He used to say maps tell the truth but the mountain edits."

Her voice did something on the last word — a small crack, quickly mortared over — and I pretended not to hear it, because that's a kindness sometimes, letting people keep their mortar.

The gully delivered us onto a bench below the ridge with a view back across the whole gray fan. We made cold camp there, no fire, sharing a single blanket between the three of us because warmth shared is warmth doubled and I'd rather be close than comfortable. Quentin fell asleep between us inside twenty minutes, the way children do, as though sleep were a country with open borders. Cyrus stayed awake longer, turning the brass key over in his fingers in the dark like a man worrying a tooth.

"You knew him well," he said finally. Not a question. He does that — asks things without asking, so you can answer or not.

"Well enough to know he'll read the scree in an hour and know it's false within another hour," I said. "Tomas taught me the cold-camp trick. He'll recognize his own signature."

"Then why bother?"

"Because knowing it's false and proving it's false are different expenditures of time, and time is the only currency out here. Every hour he spends doubting the fan is an hour we spend on the ridge." I paused. "Also because I want to see his face when he works it out."

"Why?"

"Because I'm angry," I said, "and anger wants witnesses."

Cyrus was quiet a moment. "It's more than that."

Of course it was more than that. It was eleven years and a whiteout and a man's voice singing filth into the wind to keep me from being afraid, and it was the year of phone calls getting shorter, and it was the specific humiliation of learning that the person you'd have died for had, without ceremony, gone to work for the thing hunting you. But I wasn't going to hand all that to a man I'd known nine days, so I said, "Go to sleep, Oyelaran," and he did, mostly.

* *

They hit the scree fan at first light.

I'd taken the watch — I take most of the watches; sleeping deeply in company is a skill I lost somewhere around the accident — and I watched through the field glasses as four dots resolved into men at the base of the fan. They did everything right. Spread out, tested footing, read the track sign aloud to each other. And then the ranging one — Tomas, always Tomas, Halloway would use him like a blade because blades are for using — stopped dead in the middle of the gray waste, and even at two kilometers I could see the shape of it: the stillness of a man reading a sentence he wrote himself.

He crouched. Touched the ground. Stood. Turned slowly, scanning the ridgeline, and I swear to you the man looked directly at our bench, at the exact fold of rock where I lay, and held there for five full seconds.

"He knows," I breathed, though there was no one awake to hear it.

But then he did something strange. He turned back to the others and gestured — east, along the false route, toward the low col. He pointed them at the lie and walked them into it.

For a long moment I couldn't make sense of it. Then I could, and it was worse.

He knew the tracks were false. He knew *I* had made them, because nobody else alive builds a cold camp quite that neatly — he'd teased me about it in Zermatt, called it *Ferran's furniture*. And he'd sent them anyway. Because Tomas Reyes had looked up at this ridge, at the place where I was lying, and decided that the fastest way to end this was to let the others chase a ghost while he came alone for me.

Or — and this thought arrived colder than the morning — he'd sent them away because whatever he intended to say to me, he didn't intend to say it in front of witnesses.

I woke the others. We moved. And all that day, climbing the ridge with the wind trying to peel us off the mountain, I kept glancing back at the empty gray fan and feeling the particular dread of a woman who has just realized that the person following her isn't following the trail.

He's following her.

* *

By late afternoon we'd gained the saddle below the western peaks, and the range opened ahead of us like a book to its hardest chapter: the high line, the one her father marked, the one that ran toward the pass and the Red Stair beyond it. Behind us, the valley was filling with cloud. Ahead, the sky had that bruised, metallic look I like least in mountains, the look that says the weather has stopped negotiating.

"We can't stop here," I said. "No shelter, no water, exposure all night if the front comes early."

"There." Quentin pointed. Below the saddle, tucked against the northern wall, stood a dark shape that could have been rock and was, according to her father's map, a waystation — one of the 1897 expedition's supply huts, if the map could be believed, and so far the map had earned belief the way few things do.

We reached it as the first flakes came down, fat and slow, the mountain clearing its throat. The hut was stone-footed, timber-walled, older than anything else on this ridge except the ridge, and when I got the door open — swollen, protesting, then giving with a groan like a debt collector — the smell that came out was pine resin and cold iron and time.

Inside: shelves. Blankets wrapped in oilcloth. A stove with split wood stacked beside it, dry as the day it was split. And on the table, centered with the deliberateness of a message meant to be found, a journal with a canvas cover gone soft as cloth.

Quentin reached for it. Cyrus reached for it. Their hands met, and they both withdrew, and I took it, because someone had to, and because I'm the one who reads dead men's handwriting without flinching.

Most of it was route notes, ration tallies, the usual grim poetry of expeditions. But near the end, in a different, shakier hand, one entry stood alone:

*Day 61. We argue about what we carry. Marta says the Provision is the seeds. Ede says the medicines. Aldous says the letters, and that a testament is heavier than gold and worth more. They are all wrong and all right. The Provision is not a thing. It is what remains when a thing is shared.*

I read it twice. The second time, my voice wasn't steady, so I handed it to Quentin and let her read it aloud, and hearing it in a twelve-year-old's voice did something to the words that my voice couldn't have done.

Cyrus stood by the window with the brass key in his fist, watching the snow thicken, and I saw him mouthing the last line to himself like a man testing whether a rope will hold.

Outside, the storm closed the world to arm's length. Somewhere behind us — east, on a false trail, or west, off-trail entirely, coming straight for me — Tomas Reyes was walking in it.

And I found I couldn't decide which frightened me more: the man Halloway hired, or the friend who might be coming to warn me.
