## 13. Quentin's Shortcut

My father used to say that maps were arguments. Every line you drew was you telling the mountain, *I know better than you do.* He'd say it with chalk dust on his sleeves and a pencil behind his ear like it was a flower, and I'd roll my eyes because I was nine and nine-year-olds are contractually obligated to roll their eyes at their fathers.

I wasn't rolling my eyes now.

We stood at the mouth of the karst at first light, the three of us, looking at a landscape that looked like a giant had taken bites out of the earth and gotten bored halfway through. Limestone everywhere — grey teeth of it, ridges and pinnacles and holes going down into dark that didn't apologize for itself. The proper trail, the one Odile wanted, curled away east around all of it, a polite detour that would cost us a full day we did not have. The snow line had climbed overnight. You could see it from camp: a white hem creeping down the peaks like the mountain pulling up its blankets early.

"There," I said, and put my finger on my father's map.

The map was the one thing of his I hadn't let out of my sight since the harbor. Oilcloth-wrapped, corner-burned, covered in his shorthand — little hooks and dashes that looked like bird tracks to anyone else but were, to me, as legible as a street sign. The route through the karst was marked in his confident hand: a dotted line threading between two sinkholes, past a feature he'd labeled with a tiny drawing of an arch, ending at a notch he'd called THE DOOR in letters so large they practically shouted.

"That's not a route," Odile said. "That's a dare."

"It's a day," I said. "Maybe more. The long way adds eighteen kilometers of switchbacks and a river crossing. If the snow comes down while we're on those switchbacks—" I didn't finish it. Nobody finishes sentences like that anymore. We'd all gotten good at leaving the ends off things.

Odile crouched over the map, her ruined hands — still bandaged from the Red Stair, still stiff — held carefully away from the paper like she was afraid she'd smudge it. She'd been different since the Stair. Not softer exactly. More *present*, the way a person is present after they've caught something falling.

"Karst eats people," she said flatly. "You can't see the ground under you. Limestone dissolves underneath and leaves a crust, and the crust doesn't care how clever your map is."

"My father surveyed this whole range," I said. "He walked every one of these lines."

"He walked them alone," Odile said, and there was something under the words, some weight I couldn't name yet, "in summer, probably, with good light. It's autumn now. We've got maybe six hours of usable daylight and a boy who's already fallen into one hole this trip."

Cyrus, who had been uncharacteristically quiet, looked up from rewrapping his ankle. "I resent being described as a boy who falls into holes."

"You fell into a crevasse," Odile said.

"I was *invited* down by the ice. There's a difference." He limped over and looked at the map over my shoulder. I felt him reading it — really reading it, the way he read water before a dive, looking for the lie underneath. Then he tapped the arch symbol. "This. What is it?"

"A limestone arch," I said. "His note says if you keep the arch on your left shoulder, the safe line runs straight to the Door. He wrote—"

I stopped. Because I was about to recite it, and reciting it out loud would make my voice do something embarrassing.

*Trust the stone that sings,* his note said. *When the ground stops singing, stop walking.*

"What does it say?" Cyrus asked gently. He had a way of asking gently that made lying feel like kicking a dog.

"When the ground stops singing, stop walking," I said. "It's a karst trick. You stamp, and hollow ground sounds different from solid ground. He taught me when I was seven. We'd play it as a game." I stamped once, experimentally, on the turf at the karst's edge. A deep, round *whum*, like a drum under a blanket. Solid enough. For now.

Odile exhaled through her nose, which for her was roughly equivalent to a eulogy. "One condition. Rope between us the entire way. Twenty feet, no less. And if I say turn back, we turn back, map or no map."

"Agreed," I said, too fast, because agreeing fast is how you win arguments against adults who are already losing.

* * *

The karst was beautiful the way knives are beautiful.

Grey blades of rock rose out of grass gone gold and rust, and between them the ground dipped and swelled like something breathing. Water had been working this place for a million years, patient as interest accruing, dissolving tunnels and rooms and cathedral spaces beneath our feet that no one would ever see. My father had loved karst country. *The mountain shows you its skeleton here,* he said once. I remembered thinking that was a strange thing to love. Now, roped twenty feet from two people whose lives were briefly my responsibility, I understood it differently. Skeletons tell you where everything soft went wrong.

We moved in Odile's order: her first, me second, Cyrus last, the rope slack between us but never loose. Stamp, listen, step. Stamp, listen, step. The morning sun came up red through haze and made the limestone glow like embers, and for a while it was almost pleasant, almost a game, almost seven years old again with my father's hand around mine showing me how to hear the earth.

"The arch should be visible by now," I said, an hour in, consulting the map. "Over that next ridge of stone."

It was. When we crested, there it stood — a natural bridge spanning a shallow ravine, weathered to lace at its edges, impossibly delicate for something that had held its own pose for ten thousand years. Exactly where my father had drawn it. Something loosened in my chest that I hadn't known was tight.

"That's him," I said. "That's exactly him. He drew it before he ever saw it, from old survey notes, and there it is."

Cyrus smiled at me sideways. "Your father drew a door on this map too. I'm starting to think he liked doors."

"He liked knowing what was on the other side of them," I said. "He said most people spend their whole lives on one side of every door and call it safety."

We passed under the arch. Its shadow fell across us cool as well-water, and I kept it on my left shoulder just like the note said, and the dotted line ran true, and for another hour the world cooperated.

Which should have been my first warning. In my experience — and I was twelve, so my experience was limited but intense — the world cooperates right up until it decides to make a point.

* * *

It happened in a stretch of ground that looked like every other stretch of ground. That's the thing about sinkholes. They don't announce themselves. No sign, no scar, no helpful skull-and-crossbones. Just grass, and then not-grass.

I was in front — Odile had rotated us after lunch so she could watch Cyrus's bad ankle from behind — and I stamped, and the ground sang its low solid song, and I stepped forward onto it, and it sang again, and I stepped again—

And behind me, Cyrus's footfall made no sound at all.

No whum. No drum-under-blanket. Nothing. The silence where a footstep should be.

"*Stop!*" I screamed, and spun, and saw him mid-stride, weight rolling forward onto his lead foot, and the ground under that foot was already flexing — visibly flexing, the turf dimpling inward like skin over a drumhead pressed by a finger. The crust was going. The crust was going and he was committed, his momentum carrying him onto ground that had decided, sometime in the last million years, to stop being ground.

He's a big man. Big men don't stop. Big men fall, and they fall hard, and they take whatever's attached to them along.

The rope snapped taut.

I had it wrapped twice around my forearm — Odile's rule, twenty feet, no less — and the pull hit me like a train. I went down on my knees, skidding across limestone that flayed my palms through my gloves, and I dug in, heels, elbows, teeth if I had to, and screamed the only useful word left in the world:

"ODILE!"

She was already moving. I'll give her that forever; she was already moving before I finished screaming. She dropped her pack where she stood, threw herself flat, got her boots braced against a rib of rock, and took the rope in her bandaged hands without one single hesitation, and the sound she made was not a scream, it was lower than that, a sound from somewhere older than words.

Cyrus went through.

Not all the way. The crust broke like the shell of an egg and he dropped to his chest in the hole, arms windmilling, and then his hands found the rim — found the lip of intact crust — and he hung there with his boots churning over a darkness that breathed cold air up at us. I heard the rope sing. I heard Odile's boots grind backward an inch, two inches, her whole body a lever against the pull. I heard myself making a sound I didn't decide to make.

And I heard — faintly, horribly — below his boots, down in the dark, a hollow echoing *clunk*. Like a boot striking the wall of a room. A room. Under the grass. Big enough to echo.

"Hold!" Odile roared. "Quentin — anchor — wrap it on the rock, NOW!"

There was a boulder the size of a barrel three feet to my left. I scrambled to it, dragged the rope's midpoint around it, took the strain off my arm and put it on stone, and the whole system went rigid — Odile, the boulder, me, the rope, Cyrus — one machine with a man's life running through its middle.

"Feet wide," Odile grunted to him, conversational, as if discussing weather. "Spread your weight. Don't kick. Feel for the edge with your toes — there's thicker crust north of you, feel for it."

"I feel," Cyrus said, and his voice was steady, dive-steady, the voice of a man hanging over black water telling himself it's just water, "a lot of nothing."

"Feel again."

He felt again. His boot found something. He pulled — one arm, then the other, hauling his chest onto the thick crust, and then it was hands and knees and crawling, ugly and graceless and alive, away from the broken rim until Odile called hold, and then he lay flat on the grass breathing like a bellows, and the rope between us sagged, and nobody said anything for a very long time.

The hole he'd left was maybe four feet across. I crawled to its edge — Odile barked at me not to, I did anyway, because I was twelve and grief makes you rude — and looked down. The dark went further than a body length. Further than two. Cold air rose out of it, smelling of wet stone and old, old time. Somewhere down there, my boot-echo's cousin was still fading in a chamber no map had ever drawn, because the mountain keeps its rooms private.

I sat back on my heels. My hands were shaking. My father's map was shaking in them.

*When the ground stops singing, stop walking.*

He'd written it for this. For exactly this. Some afternoon years ago, kneeling on this same indifferent turf with his pencil behind his ear, he had stamped and listened and thought: *someone I love might walk here someday, and I won't be there, so I will leave my voice in the margin instead.* And his voice had worked. His voice had saved a salvage diver's life today, four years after the sea took him.

The map wasn't paper. It never had been. It was him, still arguing with the mountain, still winning.

* * *

We gave the hole a wide berth and made camp early, in a sheltered fold between two pinnacles, because Odile said shock walks slower than people and catches up at dusk. She was right. Cyrus shook for an hour by the stove, wrapped in everything we owned, drinking tea he didn't taste, while Odile rebandaged her hands — the Red Stair bandages were ruined, bloodied through again, and she wrapped them without a flicker, like it was someone else's hands.

I couldn't look at those hands without my throat doing things. So I looked at the map instead.

"You knew," Cyrus said finally, from inside his cocoon. His voice was rough. "On the trail. You heard it before I did."

"You didn't sing," I said. "The ground. Your step went quiet." I smoothed the oilcloth flat across my knees. "Everyone thinks surveying is drawing. It's not. It's listening. That's the whole secret. My father said instruments get you close, but your ears get you exact." I hesitated. "He also said a map is a promise you make to someone you haven't met yet."

Cyrus looked at me across the stove flame for a long moment. "Then he kept it," he said. "Today. To me."

"He kept all of them," I said, and my voice cracked on *all*, because *all* included the last promise, the one about coming home, and the mountain had collected on that one differently. I bent over the map so they wouldn't see my face do what it was doing.

Odile's shadow fell across the page. She crouched, wincing, and studied the dotted line ahead — past the Door, up toward the high country, toward a small symbol I'd been trying not to stare at all evening: a square with a flag, and beside it, in my father's shorthand, a word.

Waystation.

"Tomorrow," Odile said quietly. "Through the Door, half a day, and we reach his waystation." She paused. Her thumb hovered over the symbol, not touching it. "If the 1897 expedition built it, and your father found it..." She didn't finish either. We were all experts now at unfinished sentences.

But I finished it inside my head, where it was safe: *then he touched what they touched. Then part of him is still in there. Then tomorrow I get to stand where he stood.*

Cyrus fed another stick to the stove. The firelight moved on his face, and I noticed — really noticed, for the first time since the harbor — that he wasn't watching the flames. He was watching my father's map. Watching it the way a man watches a locked door when he's carrying a key.

And far below us, down in the dark rooms under the karst, water was still dripping, still carving, still keeping the mountain's secrets — including, I suspected, at least one more that my father had found and written down in bird-tracks I hadn't translated yet.

Because there, in the margin beside the Door, in pencil gone silver with age, was a line I'd skipped past a hundred times, thinking it was a smudge.

It wasn't a smudge. It was shorthand. And tonight, by stove-light, I finally read it.

*They left a keeper's journal at the station. Read it before you open anything.*

I looked up at Cyrus. He was still watching the map. Still watching me watch it.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we need to talk about what my father knew."
