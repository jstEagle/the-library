## 15. Odile's Confession

The storm arrived the way bad news does — first a rumor on the wind, then a certainty, then a weight on the roof you couldn't ignore no matter how hard you tried.

We'd reached the 1897 waystation by mid-afternoon, Cyrus half-carrying his pack, Quentin walking ahead with the certainty of a girl who'd memorized her father's map so thoroughly she could probably redraw it in the dark. The building sat in a shallow saddle below the treeline, stone-walled and turf-roofed, and if you didn't know it was there you'd have walked past it as a bump in the hillside. Quentin knew. Of course she knew.

Inside, it was dry, and dry was a currency I'd learned to count in years ago. There was a stone hearth, a stack of old split wood the expedition had left seasoned and someone — the navy, probably, on one of their quiet inspections — had left alone. Bunks along one wall with canvas still stretched over them. Shelves. And the journal, which Cyrus had been reading by lamplight for two hours as though it were scripture and he a man looking for a loophole in his own damnation.

I fed the fire. It's what I do. When a group goes quiet in a bad way, I feed the fire, check the rope, count the rations, do the small competent things that let me avoid the large incompetent ones. It's a skill. It has kept me employed, such as employment has gone, for eleven years.

The wind came up around dusk. By full dark it was a living thing against the walls, and the snow came sideways, and I stood at the small window with a mug of tea I wasn't drinking and watched the world disappear.

Three days, I estimated. Maybe four. The pass would hold. We'd be slower than I wanted but faster than the snow, if the snow behaved, and snow never behaves, but you work with what the mountain gives you and you lie to yourself about the rest.

"You knew this weather was coming."

Cyrus, from the bunk. Not accusing. Just noting, the way he notes everything, with that salvager's flat calm that I'd decided — provisionally, against my better judgment — was honest rather than performed.

"Knew it was possible," I said. "Possible and coming are different animals. One you plan for. The other you outrun."

"And this one?"

"This one we shelter from. There's a difference between caution and cowardice, and the mountain doesn't care which one you call it."

He grunted and went back to the journal. Quentin was asleep in the far bunk, or I thought she was — twelve-year-olds sleep the way cats do, in shallow suspicious installments, and I should have remembered that, because remembering it might have saved me from what happened next.

Cyrus set the journal down. "It says here the Provision was carried. Not stored. Carried." He said it quietly, half to himself, the way a man talks when he's rearranging furniture in his head. "Every waystation, they mention carrying it forward. What kind of treasure do you carry through a range like this?"

"The kind you're afraid to leave behind," I said. "Or the kind that isn't treasure at all."

"You've thought about it."

"I've thought about a lot of things on this trail." I turned from the window. And here is where I should have stopped, should have done what I do best, which is nothing — the small competent silence, the fire fed, the rations counted. But the storm was pressing on the walls, and the lamplight was the color of old confessionals, and Cyrus was looking at me with the particular patience of a man who has already lied to me about the key and knows I know it, and there is a kind of equality in mutual concealment that loosens the tongue.

"I lost a client," I said. "Four years ago. That's the black mark. That's the story everyone in the valley tells with the appropriate head-shake. Lost a client on the Red Stair approach, autumn route, weather like this." I nodded at the window. "They took my certification. Not formally — there wasn't a formal anything, there never is — but the outfitters stopped calling, and the syndicate put me on a list, and a list in a small country is the same as a wall."

Cyrus was quiet. Then: "What happened?"

And I told him. God help me, I told him, because the storm was loud and the fire was warm and I had been carrying it the way the 1897 expedition carried their Provision, afraid to set it down, and a body can only carry so much before it starts looking for a waystation.

* * *

His name was Emile Vasseur, and he was a cartographer, and he hired me in September four years ago to take him into the high range on a survey that made no sense.

That was the first thing I should have heeded — the senselessness of it. He wasn't surveying for timber or mineral rights or any of the ordinary reasons men with instruments go into mountains. He was following a trail, he said. An old one. He had letters — he wouldn't show me all of them, but I saw enough — and a map with annotations in a shorthand I didn't recognize, and a conviction that sat on him like a coat that fit too well to take off.

He told me about the 1897 expedition the way you'd tell someone about a relative. Not the official version — the official version was a footnote, an expedition that went into the range, mapped a corridor, and came back diminished, two men fewer, with a story about a cache they'd established and never revisited. Every schoolchild in the coastal towns learns the footnote and forgets it by supper. But Emile had found the letters. The real ones. The ones the footnote was built to cover.

"He wasn't looking for treasure," I said to Cyrus. "I want that understood. Whatever you think the Provision is — gold, salvage, whatever a man with your debts thinks — he wasn't in it for that. He was in it because a cartographer can't abide a blank space. And the 1897 expedition left one. Right here." I touched the map we'd been traveling by — Quentin's copy of her father's, the pass corridor, the line of waystations. "They mapped everything and then they stopped. Deliberately. And he needed to know why."

"So you took him up."

"I took him up. We came through the foothills the way we came, and the weather was — " I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Everyone always asks what the weather was. As if the weather is the villain of the story. The weather was ordinary. Ordinary autumn weather, a front moving through, nothing I hadn't walked a dozen clients through. We were two days from the first waystation when he told me where we were actually going."

"The Hollow Summit," Cyrus said.

I looked at him. "You've read further in that journal than you let on."

"I've read enough to know the name." He said it carefully, and I noted the care, and filed it, because a guide who isn't noting things is just a person walking uphill.

"Well. He told me at the worst possible moment, which was characteristic of him. Brilliant man. Terrible timing. He wanted to go past the waystations, past the pass, up the Red Stair, to the summit. In October. With one guide and a pack of instruments." I fed the fire, because my hands wanted occupation. "I told him no."

"And he?"

"He said he'd go without me." I watched the flame take the new wood. "And here's the part I've had four years to turn over, four years of lying awake in rooms in towns where nobody will hire me, and I still don't know if I did the right thing, which is the worst thing a person can carry, an act with no verdict. Because I believed him. I believed he'd go alone. He was that kind of man — the kind whose certainty is load-bearing. So I did the thing that seemed like the responsible thing. I turned back. I told him I'd wait at the waystation. Two days, I said. If you're not back in two days, I'm coming after you, and we're going down together, and you can hate me the whole way."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls the wind found a gap and worried at it.

"He didn't come back," Cyrus said. Not a question.

"He didn't come back." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "I waited the two days. Then the weather closed in — a real storm, the kind that makes this one look like a draft under a door — and when it broke I went up as far as I could go alone, which wasn't far, and I found nothing. No tracks. No sign. The mountain had simply taken him the way it takes people, without malice, without even noticing. And I went down, and I reported it, and the report became the story, and the story became: Odile Ferran lost a client. Turned back. Left him."

"Did you leave him?"

"I left him." I said it flat, because flat was the only way it could be said. "That's the truth of it, whatever the certification board would have made of the nuances. A guide's job is to say no and make the no stick, and my no didn't stick, and instead of forcing it I made a bargain — I'll wait, I'll give you your two days — and the bargain was just cowardice wearing a harness. A real guide would have sat on him. A real guide would have dragged him down the mountain by the collar and let him fire her and told the story for the rest of her life about the client she manhandled off a summit. I chose the version where I got to be reasonable. And he died in the reasonable version."

Cyrus was silent a long moment. The lamplight moved on his face. "You didn't know he had a daughter," he said.

"I didn't know he had anyone. He was a closed man. He talked about his work, his letters, the expedition. Never about a home." I turned the mug in my hands. "I found out after. From the outfitter who'd forwarded his mail. There was a girl in the harbor town. Mother gone, father gone, living with — it was never clear with whom, and then it became clear it was with no one. I wrote to her. Twice. I never sent either letter. What was I going to say? *Your father hired me and I let him walk into the range alone because it was easier than fighting him, and I've been unemployable ever since, which is the mountain's only justice and it isn't enough.*" I shook my head. "You don't send that letter. You carry it."

"Odile." Cyrus's voice had changed — quieter, careful, the voice of a man approaching something. "The client's name. You said Vasseur."

"Emile Vasseur."

He looked at the far bunk. And I followed his look, and I understood, in the way you understand a crevasse bridge has failed only after your weight is on the far side of the decision — too late to be useful, exactly in time to be terrible.

The far bunk was empty.

* * *

She was standing by the window, in the dark, where the lamplight didn't reach. She had her father's map rolled in one hand — she slept with it, I'd seen, tucked against her chest like a child with a stuffed animal, and I had thought: there's a girl who knows what she's lost. I had not thought: there's a girl who will hear everything.

How long had she been awake? How much had she heard? There is no way to know these things in the moment, and no way to unknow them after.

"Quentin," Cyrus said, gently, starting to rise.

"Vasseur." She said the name the way you'd test ice. "Emile Vasseur." Her voice didn't shake. That was the worst part. A shaking voice you can comfort. Hers was level and small and absolutely awake. "That's my father."

"Quentin — " I started.

"You turned back." She came out of the dark into the lamplight, and her face was the face of a girl doing arithmetic she'd been doing for four years and had just been handed the final number. "You were *with* him. You were the last person — " She stopped. Started again, and the levelness cracked down the middle. "He didn't just vanish. He didn't just walk into the mountains and get lost. He was *with someone*, and that someone came down, and everyone in the valley knows your name and nobody ever — " Her breath caught. "Nobody ever told me there was a *guide*."

"They wouldn't have," I said. "The story that travels isn't the true one. It's the short one."

"You *waited*." The word came out of her like something thrown. "Two days. You gave him two days. In October. And then a storm, and then you went up *alone* — one person, after a storm, to look for a man on the Red Stair — and then you went home. And you wrote me letters." Her voice climbed. "You *wrote me letters* and you didn't send them. You knew. You knew there was a girl in the harbor town with no father and you sat on your guilt for four years because it was *easier* — "

"Yes," I said.

It stopped her. I think she'd been braced for excuses — I've watched people brace for excuses the way they brace for a blow, and the blow of plain agreement lands differently. Her mouth was open around the next accusation and it had nowhere to go.

"Yes," I said again. "It was easier. Every choice I made on that mountain was the easier one, dressed up as the responsible one, and your father paid for my convenience, and you paid for it too, four years of it, and no letter I could have written would have un-paid a single day of it. I'm not going to stand here and tell you about my two days of waiting like they were a virtue. They were a down payment on my own forgiveness, and the bank never accepted them."

"You should have come after him," she whispered.

"I should have come after him." 

"You should have *dragged* him — "

"I should have dragged him down by the collar and let him hate me, and he'd be alive to hate me." I held her stare, because she deserved a person who didn't look away. "There's no version of this where I tell you I did my best. I didn't do my best. I did the thing that let me sleep, and then it didn't even let me sleep, and that's what you've caught me carrying up your father's trail, Quentin. Not redemption. Just the weight, and no place left to set it down."

The wind slammed the window. Neither of us moved.

"You knew the crest," she said at last, quieter, and the quiet was worse than the shouting. "On the key. The first night. You looked at it like you'd seen it before."

"I'd seen it on his letters. The one time he showed me."

"You knew this was his trail."

"I suspected. Then I knew." I didn't soften it. "The waystations, the shorthand on the map — it was his hand, Quentin. I guided him long enough to know how he drew his elevations. I knew from the first camp. And I didn't say it, because I wanted — " My voice failed and I made it work. "I wanted to see it through. For him. For the blank space. Call it penance, call it theft, call it whatever you like. I took this contract knowing whose daughter I was guiding, and I told myself the mountain would balance the books."

"The mountain doesn't balance anything," she said. "That's what my father wrote. The mountain doesn't keep accounts. *People* do." She quoted him the way other children quote scripture, and it landed on me like scripture. "That's what you never understood. You keep talking about the mountain's justice, the mountain's verdict. The mountain doesn't care. It's just people. It was always just people, deciding what they owe each other."

And she turned and went to the far end of the station, past the bunks, into the old storeroom, and pulled the door until it caught, and the latch sound was the smallest, loudest thing in the world.

* * *

Cyrus and I sat by the fire and didn't talk for a long time. The storm worked at the roof. The journal lay closed between us like a witness who'd said too much.

"You knew her father was missing," I said finally. "The day you met her. She told you."

"She told me he went into the range surveying and never came back." He turned his mug slowly. "I didn't connect it to you. I'm not sure I connected it to anything. I was running from the navy and a woman named Halloway, and a twelve-year-old who could read a chart like a priest reads Latin was the best thing that had happened to me in a year." He looked up. "But you. You knew from the first camp, you said. You've been guiding his daughter on his last route for two weeks and you said nothing."

"Would you have told her?" I asked. "In my place. With your secrets — the cave, the key, the navy, whatever it is you're still not telling me. Would you have opened your own vein on the chance it bought her something?"

He was quiet. "No," he admitted. "But I'd have been wrong, and tonight proves it. Secrets don't keep on a mountain. The altitude pushes them out of you eventually. Better to bleed on your own schedule."

"That's the first honest thing either of us has said to the other," I said, "and I hate how much better I feel for it."

He almost smiled. Then the storeroom latch clicked.

She came out with her father's map and the journal — she'd taken the journal from the table while we talked, and neither of us had noticed, which told me exactly how rattled I was — and she set them both down on the table between the three of us with a deliberateness that made my spine straighten.

"I'm not going to say it's fine," she said. "It's not fine. It's maybe never fine." Her eyes were red and her voice was steady and she was twelve years old and the bravest person in the room. "But my father didn't turn back and neither am I, and I'm not doing this with two people who are lying to each other by omission every single day. So." She put her hand flat on the map. "New rule. Everything. From here to the summit. Cyrus tells us what the navy actually wants. Odile tells us what she knows about the 1897 route that she's been holding back because she thought it was hers to hold. And I tell you both what my father's letters actually say, all of them, including the part I haven't said out loud because it scares me."

Cyrus and I looked at each other over the lamplight.

"What part?" he asked.

Quentin's hand trembled on the map — the first tremble, the only one. "The part where he wrote that the Provision isn't the only thing up there," she said. "The part where he wrote that the 1897 expedition didn't just leave something behind." She looked at the storm-black window. "They left something *behind them*. And my father's last letter says it's still following the trail."
