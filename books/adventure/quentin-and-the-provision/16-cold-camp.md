## 16. Cold Camp

The waystation had walls, a stove, and the particular silence of two people who had decided to stop talking to each other. I'd have preferred wolves.

Quentin sat on the far bunk with her father's map spread across her knees, tracing routes she'd already memorized, her back a wall of twelve-year-old architecture aimed at Odile. Odile stood at the window with binoculars she wasn't using, watching snow clouds stack up over the range like a debt coming due. Between them, the room held about as much warmth as the glacier we were trying to cross.

I tried the direct approach first, because I am, at heart, an optimist with poor judgment.

"So," I said, "anyone want tea?"

Nothing. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind found a new note in the rocks and held it.

"Tea," I repeated, holding up the tin. "It's the expedition's own tea. From 1897. It's either delicious or it's a biohazard, and honestly either way it's an experience."

Quentin turned a page of the map without looking at it. Odile adjusted the binoculars.

Right. Mediation by beverage: failed. I put the kettle on anyway, because doing something is better than doing nothing, and because the smell of anything hot might crack the ice in that room. While it heated, I took stock of our situation, which is a habit from diving — when things go wrong down there, you inventory what you've got before you panic.

What we had: one waystation, stocked by dead men a century ago and eerily well-preserved for it. A storm building over the peaks, arriving weeks ahead of any schedule Quentin's father had marked. Two keys — well, one key and one ice-carved artifact that fit it like a hand fits a glove. A route over the Red Stair and across the glacier to a summit called Hollow, where something waited that was not treasure, if the waystation journal could be believed. And three people who could not stand to be in a room together, which struck me as a design flaw in the whole enterprise.

What we didn't have: time, trust, or enough food to be casual about it.

The journal had done its work on me the night before. *The Provision is carried, not kept,* some long-dead lieutenant had written, and I'd lain awake turning that over like a stone in my pocket. I'd come into this for salvage money. Somewhere between the cave and the crevasse, the job description had changed, and nobody had sent me a memo.

The kettle sang. I poured three cups and set them out like place settings at a table nobody wanted to sit at.

"Quentin," I said. "You need to eat. And drink. Feelings are no excuse for dehydration."

"I'm not thirsty." First words she'd said in six hours. They landed on Odile like thrown stones.

"You are. You've been climbing since dawn."

"Then *she* can carry me."

Odile set the binoculars down with great care, the way you set down something you'd rather throw. "That would be difficult," she said quietly, "while you won't speak to me."

"I'm speaking now."

"Not to *me*."

"That's the point."

And there it was again, the wound laid open in the middle of our shelter. Odile looked at me — a look that said *your turn* — and I realized with sinking clarity that I had been elected mediator of a conflict I understood only in outline. Odile had lost a client on this range. The client had been Quentin's father. She had turned back; he had gone on alone; he had not come back. Quentin had overheard the confession through a door that should have been shut.

I picked up my tea and sat down on the floor between the bunks, because standing felt too much like taking sides.

"Okay," I said. "Here's what I know about running away from things."

Nobody asked me to continue. I continued.

"When I was nineteen, my mother remarried a man named Gerald, who owned a car dealership and a smile he used on customers and, apparently, on her. He didn't like me. That's not quite true — he liked me fine the way you like weather you can't control, meaning he resented it. Anyway, when they moved to Perth for his business, there was a conversation about whether I'd come. I say 'a conversation.' It was more of a scheduling conflict. My mother said, 'Cyrus has his work here,' and Gerald said, 'Of course, of course,' in the tone men use when they've already won, and I said nothing at all, because saying nothing meant I got to pretend I'd chosen it."

Quentin had stopped tracing the map. She was watching me sideways, the way you watch a fire you're not sure is safe.

"They wrote twice the first year," I went on. "Once the second. Then Christmas cards, unsigned, the kind with the printed signature. I kept those for a while. Then I kept diving instead, because water doesn't ask you why you're down there. You go deep enough, everything above you goes quiet, and quiet starts to feel like peace even when it's just absence."

The stove ticked. The wind rose a register outside.

"The thing about running away," I said, "is that you get very good at it. You practice until it's reflex. And then one day you're thirty-four years old and you realize you've run so far you can't remember what you were running from, only that you're still moving, and everyone you might have stayed for is a day's travel behind you and getting further."

I looked at Odile. She was watching me with an expression I hadn't seen on her before — not guilt exactly, but recognition. The face of someone hearing their own story told badly by a stranger.

"And the thing about turning back," I said to her, carefully, "is that sometimes you turn back and it's the right call and it saves your life and you hate yourself forever anyway. Both things are true. The mountain doesn't care which one you pick."

"He didn't have to go alone." Quentin's voice came out small and furious. "She was his guide. That was the whole *job*."

"It was," Odile said.

"You took his money."

"I did."

"And then you quit."

"I did that too." Odile crossed the room and sat on the edge of the opposite bunk, hands loose between her knees, a climber's posture — weight forward, ready to bear load. "Your father hired me in spring, four years ago. He wanted the high route past the Red Stair. I told him it was early in the season. He said his daughter was turning nine and he wanted to bring her something back that would matter. I liked him. That was my first mistake — guides shouldn't like their clients, it makes you stupid. On the third day the weather turned the way it's turning now, early and mean, and I made the call to descend. He refused. We argued. I told him I'd take him up myself when the window opened, and he said there wouldn't be a window, that windows were for people with time, and he started up alone with half our rope and none of my advice."

She stopped. In the pause I heard the wind working at the mountain, patient as erosion.

"I reported him missing on the fifth day," she said. "They found his camp. They never found him. The inquiry took my certification because I'd left a client on the mountain, and the truth is they were right to, because I did. What they don't understand — what I couldn't say in that room — is that leaving him was the only choice that let one of us live. And I have spent four years knowing that being right feels exactly the same as being guilty."

Silence. Quentin had gone very still, the map forgotten in her lap.

"You knew his name," she said finally. "When we met you. You knew it before I said it."

"Yes."

"You looked at me like—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "Like I was a ghost."

"I looked at you like you were his daughter," Odile said. "Because you have his exact frown. He used it on me the whole time I tried to talk him off that ridge."

Quentin's jaw worked. Whatever she was feeling was too large for the room, and she did what children do with feelings too large for rooms: she folded them smaller and smaller until they cut her hands.

"I'm still angry," she announced.

"Good," Odile said. "Anger's load-bearing. Keep it."

"But I'm going to sleep now." She lay down facing the wall, map still clutched, a treaty neither signed nor torn up. "And tomorrow I'm still not talking to you."

"Understood," Odile said gravely, and I swear the woman almost smiled.

* * *

I took first watch, partly because someone had to and mostly because I wanted the window. The storm had arrived while we talked — not the blizzard itself but its outriders, spits of white ticking against the glass, and above us the peaks had gone from black to a dusty, unwilling grey, as though the mountain had been dusted with flour by a careless baker.

First snow. Weeks early. Quentin's father's map gave us a margin, and the margin had just been spent.

Odile came to stand beside me near midnight, quiet as her profession required. Below us the valley was already erasing itself. Above, the stars showed in ragged patches between cloud, hard and close.

"She'll come around," I said.

"Don't flatter yourself into believing you caused that," Odile said. "You told her about Gerald and the car dealership."

"Gerald's a compelling figure."

"She came around because you told her the truth badly, and she recognized it. Children always know the difference between a story and a confession." She paused. "Why did you tell her? You've never told anyone. I watched you lie to navy officers with better craft than that."

I thought about it honestly, which is not my usual mode.

"Because she lost her father to this mountain," I said, "and I lost mine to a car dealership, and both of us have been pretending the loss was a choice ever since. And because she's twelve, and somebody has to show her that adults survive being honest, or she'll grow up thinking silence is strength." I shrugged. "Also the tea was terrible and I needed to fill the silence."

Odile huffed — her version of laughter, I was learning. "Halloway's climbers will have holed up somewhere south of us. When this clears, they'll move fast to make up time. So will we."

"I know."

"And Cyrus." She was quiet a moment. "What you said earlier. About turning back. About being right feeling like guilty." Her eyes stayed on the snow. "Thank you. Don't make me say it twice."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

Behind us, a small voice from the dark of the bunks: "I'm awake. And I heard that. And tomorrow I'm *still* not talking to her."

"Understood," Odile and I said together.

But a minute later, half-dreaming, Quentin added, "The pass closes in eleven days. Maybe ten. Papa's notes say the cornices fail after the first real snow." A pause. "We'll need to leave at first light. Both of you."

*Both.* Not *her*. I caught Odile's eye in the dark, and neither of us smiled, because smiling at it would have jinxed it.

Outside, the snow thickened against the glass, and somewhere below us in the whiteout, I knew, Vess Halloway was counting her climbers and her patience, and finding both sufficient. Eleven days to the pass. One family, barely assembled, held together with anger and honesty and terrible century-old tea.

I put another log on the fire and settled in to keep watch, and for the first time since I'd taken that brass key out of a dead man's satchel, the thing I was guarding wasn't treasure.

It was the sound of two people breathing in a warm room behind me — and I would have fought the navy, the syndicate, and the whole indifferent mountain to keep it.

I just didn't yet know how soon I'd have to.
