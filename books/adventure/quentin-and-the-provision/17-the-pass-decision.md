## 17. The Pass Decision

**Cyrus**

The morning we reached the decision, the mountain made its argument first.

We came out of the treeline onto a shelf of granite above a valley the color of old pewter, and the wind hit us like a door swung open. Below, the long route wound west along the river — a gray thread stitching its way toward the far pass, ten days of walking if the weather held, which it would not. Above us, the range stood up on its hind legs, white to the collar, and the snow line had come down overnight like a landlord changing the locks.

I'd seen weather like that once before, off the Azores, when the sea went from companion to enemy in the space of a cup of tea. You learn to read that kind of shift or you learn to drown. The mountain was telling us the same thing the sea had: the easy way was closed.

Odile stood at the shelf's edge with her hands on her hips, doing that thing she did where she looked at terrain the way an accountant looks at a ledger — coldly, completely, finding the losses before the gains. Quentin stood beside her, holding her father's map the way other children hold a blanket.

"The long way's dead," Odile said. No preamble. She'd stopped pretending to soften her verdicts somewhere around the scree field. "Ten days at best, and the front coming down the coast. We'd be caught on the river flats in a whiteout with no shelter for six miles in any direction. I won't lead that."

"So we go back," I said, and hated the words even as they left my mouth. Going back meant the syndicate, the navy, Halloway's climbers somewhere behind us on the trail. Going back meant the key in a strongbox and Quentin in an orphanage and me in whatever cell they built for men who burn their contracts.

Odile didn't even look at me. "I said I won't lead it. I didn't say it was the only option."

She turned and pointed up. Not at the pass — at the red wall east of it, where the rock showed through the snow like a wound through bandages. A seam of crimson stone, near-vertical, running up and up until my neck hurt following it.

"There," she said. "The Red Stair."

Quentin's breath caught. I heard it. A small sound, but in that wind it carried.

"That's a wall," I said. "That's not a route, that's a dare."

"It's a route." Odile's jaw did the thing it did when she was daring me to argue. "Cut steps. Ninety years old, maybe older. Her father marked it."

Quentin unrolled the map with hands that weren't quite steady, and there it was — the red line her father had drawn up the face of the wall, and beside it, in his cramped cartographer's hand, the words I'd seen her trace a hundred times without either of us understanding them: *Stair. Two days. Do not attempt in wind.*

"Do not attempt in wind," I read aloud. I gestured at the valley, at the air itself, which was busy trying to remove my hat and my ears and my general will to live. "What would you call this?"

"Wind," Odile said. "Not *in* wind. There's a difference. In the Stair's shadow the air falls still. I've seen it from the east ridge, years ago. The wall makes its own calm." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes — that locked room she kept in there, the one with Quentin's father in it. "He knew that too. That's why he wrote it the way he did. He wasn't warning us off. He was telling us the window."

I looked at the wall for a long time. I am not a brave man. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept. I am a man who is good at a small number of frightening things — cold water, dark holds, holding my breath past the point where the body files complaints — and I have learned that competence looks like courage from the outside, the way a lamp looks like the sun if you never get closer. But vertical rock in autumn with a child in the party and winter at our backs was not cold water. Cold water, at least, ends quickly.

And yet.

I thought about the navy men in my shack, their polite voices and their hands on my dive logs. I thought about Vess Halloway's smile, which was the most expensive thing about her. I thought about the way Quentin had looked at me on the survey boat, the night we ran the harbor — not grateful, exactly. *Deciding.* Deciding whether I was the kind of man who went back or the kind who went up.

"Two days on the Stair," I said. "Or ten on the flats, in the dark, with Halloway's climbers eating our dust and the snow eating us. That's not a decision. That's arithmetic."

Odile almost smiled. Almost. With her it was a whole facial event, like an eclipse.

"Then we decide it properly," she said. "All three. Nobody goes up that wall because the others shamed them into it. Not on my rope."

* * *

**Quentin**

They both looked at me, and I hated them for it.

Not really. But in that moment, with the wind pulling my hair out of its braid and Papa's map snapping in my hands like a live thing, I hated them a little, because I knew what they were doing. They were giving me the choice so that whatever happened on that wall, it would be mine. Papa used to do that. *You can come or you can stay, Quen, but whichever you choose, you chose it.* I used to think it was fairness. Standing on that shelf, I understood it was something harder. It was trust, and trust is heavier than fairness. Fairness you can carry in your pocket. Trust you carry on your back, uphill.

I looked at the Red Stair.

It didn't look like a stair. It looked like the mountain had been cut open and the inside was red — a deep, arterial red, the color of the clay bricks behind the chandlery stove, the color Papa's hands went when he worked the press. The steps, if they were steps, were scratches at this distance, a ladder drawn by someone with a very steady hand and a very poor sense of self-preservation.

But Papa had drawn it.

That was the whole argument, and I knew it was a child's argument, and I made it anyway, to myself, in the privacy of my own skull where nobody could tell me I was being foolish. He had drawn it. He had stood where we were standing — maybe on this exact shelf, maybe with this exact wind trying to take his hat — and he had looked at that wall and he had not turned away. He had climbed it or he had meant to. And he had written *do not attempt in wind* instead of *do not attempt*, which meant he believed there was a right way up. Papa never believed in the wrong way up. He believed every route had a key, the way every lock does, and your job was to find the moment the tumblers lined up.

The window, Odile had called it.

I thought about the last letter, the one I'd shown them in the foothills, the one that said *I have found where the trail begins and I am going up to finish it.* He'd been alone. That was the part that lived in my chest like a stone, the part I didn't say out loud because if I said it out loud it would get bigger. He'd been alone, and the mountain had taken him alone, and I was not going to be alone, and I was not going to let the mountain win by making us turn around and take the slow road and get buried in the flats like the mountain wanted.

Mountains don't want things. Papa would correct me if he could hear me. *Anthropomorphism, Quen. The mountain is indifferent; that's its whole trick.* But standing there, I swear the Red Stair looked like a dare, and I have never once in my life walked away from a dare. It's my worst quality. Papa said so. He said it laughing, though.

"I'm going up it," I said.

My voice came out smaller than I wanted. The wind took some of it. So I said it again, louder, into the wind, the way you have to.

"I'm going up the Stair. It's his route. He drew it for us — I think — I think he drew it for whoever came after, and nobody ever came after, and I'm not leaving it for the snow to erase."

Cyrus crouched down to my level, which he did sometimes, and which I pretended to find insulting because I'm not that short, actually. His face had the look it got when he was being honest on purpose, which was rare enough to be alarming, like a solar eclipse.

"You know what's up there," he said. Not a question, but I answered anyway.

"Steps cut in rock, ninety years old, probably rotten. Cold. A drop." I made myself say the next part. "A long drop."

"And you still want it."

I looked at the wall. I looked at his face, which was scared — I could see it, under the weathered tan, the way you can see a wreck under clear water — and he was asking me anyway, and he wasn't going to overrule me either way. That was the thing about Cyrus. He was a liar by trade, a good one, and he never lied to me about the things that counted. It was the strangest thing I'd ever learned about an adult.

"I still want it," I said.

He nodded slowly, like a man signing something.

* * *

**Odile**

I had climbed the Red Stair once, eleven years ago, from the east, in June, with a client who paid double for routes that weren't on any map. I remembered it the way you remember a fever. Three thousand feet of cut steps laid by hands that had no business being as good as they were — the 1897 expedition, almost certainly, the same madmen who'd stocked the waystations — and the steps held, mostly, and the ones that didn't held long enough. I remembered the stillness in the wall's shadow, the way the wind parted around that red rock like a river around a bridge piling, and I remembered standing at the top looking down at the valley and thinking: *this is the finest route I will ever climb, and I will never bring anyone here, because it will kill someone someday.*

Eleven years later I was bringing a twelve-year-old.

I want it understood that I ran the calculations before I pointed at that wall. I have lost one client. I have stood in a church in a black coat and listened to a eulogy I deserved more than the deceased did, and I have carried the arithmetic of that day through every route since: *one life, weighed against the summit, and the summit lost.* I do not make that trade twice. I had told myself I would never make it twice.

But the flats were death with extra steps. That's the ugly truth of the decision, the part I didn't say out loud on the shelf: there was no safe option. There was the long way, which killed us slowly in a whiteout, and there was the Stair, which killed us quickly and honestly if it killed us at all. I have always preferred my dangers honest. A whiteout lies to you. A wall does nothing but tell the truth, at volume, the entire time.

And the girl had said yes with her spine straight. I watched her do it. I watched a child choose the wall because her father had drawn the line, and I thought: *he would have made the same face.* I knew that face. I had turned my back on that face on a col two years ago, told him the weather was turning, told him I'd wait at the waystation, watched him go up alone because I would not go with him. The guilt of that has a taste. It tastes like the rationed tea we'd been drinking for a week, thin and bitter and never enough.

I was not turning my back on that face again. Whatever it cost.

"Conditions," I said, and both of them turned to me, and I became the guide again, which is the only version of myself I trust. "We go at first light, before the sun softens anything. Roped, the whole way, no exceptions — the girl between us. We climb in the wall's shadow and if the wind finds us on the face, we stop, we anchor, and we wait. It could be an hour. It could be a day. We wait anyway. Nobody moves on the Stair in wind. That was his rule and it's a good rule and we will follow it even if it kills us, because the alternative is that it kills us faster."

"Cheerful," Cyrus said.

"I'm not paid to be cheerful. I'm not paid at all, as it happens, which makes my cheerfulness a private matter." I looked at him. He was scared. Good. Scared men check their knots. It's the confident ones who fall. "You. Can you climb?"

"I can climb," he said. "Salvage work. Ladders, hulls, anchor chains in a swell. I'm not graceful, but I don't let go."

"Grace is for dancers. Not letting go is the entire profession." I turned to Quentin. "And you. Last chance. Once we're on the third pitch there's no down — not a down you'd like. You say the word now, and I take us down to the flats, and I will lead that whiteout route and I will get us through it or die trying, and no one will think less of you. I will think less of no one. Say the word."

I watched her look at the wall. I watched her look at her father's red line. I watched her do the thing I had failed to do two years ago, which was to look at the whole truth of it — the drop, the cold, the rotten steps, all of it, unflinching — and keep her feet under her.

"The word is go," she said.

* * *

**Cyrus**

That night we camped on the shelf with the wall looming over us, red even in the dark, a darker red, like an ember keeping its own counsel. Nobody slept much, and nobody pretended to.

Around what I judged was midnight, Quentin crawled out of her bag and came and sat next to me at the fire, which Odile had built small and screened, a fire you could only see from six feet away, a fire for people hiding.

"I'm scared," she said, conversationally, the way she announced the weather.

"Good," I said. "I'm terrified. If you weren't scared I'd be worried about you."

"Odile says scared men check their knots."

"Odile says a lot of true things in a voice that makes them sound like insults. It's a gift."

She was quiet for a while. The wind moaned out in the valley, out where the long way was, out where the safe road was busy being buried. Up here in the wall's shadow, it was almost still. She'd been right, Odile had. The wall made its own calm. It felt less like shelter than like the eye of something.

"My dad would've liked you," Quentin said suddenly. "He didn't like most people. He said most people were badly drawn. But you lie like a cartographer — you know exactly where everything is, you just draw the roads wrong on purpose." She glanced at me. "That's a compliment. From him it was a compliment."

"I'm going to choose to believe that."

"You should." She pulled her knees up to her chin. "Cyrus. If I slow you down up there — if it comes down to the rope —"

"Stop," I said.

"You don't even know what I was going to —"

"You were going to tell me to cut you loose, or leave you, or some other thing you read in an adventure serial, and I'm telling you now so you don't waste breath at altitude: no. Nobody gets left. Not on this mountain, not on any mountain. I've spent my whole life traveling light because leaving things behind is easier than carrying them. I'm done with easy." I fed a stick to the fire. "You're cargo now. Annoying, talkative cargo."

She laughed — a real one, brief, startled out of her — and the sound did something to the night that the fire couldn't.

From the other side of the flames, Odile's voice came out of the dark, dry as chalk: "Sleep, both of you. First light. And Oyelaran — check your knots."

"Scared men check their knots," I said.

"Then you'll be checking them all night."

I did, too. Twice. And in the morning, when the light came up gray and clean and the Red Stair stood over us with its ninety-year-old steps and its long drop and its promise of two days between us and the pass, I checked them a third time, and Quentin checked mine, and Odile checked hers, and we tied in — the girl between us, the way the guide ordered, the way the map intended — and we walked to the base of the wall together.

The first step was cut into the red rock like a word in a sentence someone had started a century ago and never finished.

We started reading.
