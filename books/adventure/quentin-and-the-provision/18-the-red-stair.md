## 18. The Red Stair

My father used to say that a map was a promise you made to yourself before you knew what it would cost to keep it. I thought that was just the kind of thing cartographers said to make their jobs sound noble instead of tedious. Then I stood at the base of the Red Stair and understood he hadn't been being poetic at all. He'd been writing me a warning in advance, and I'd read it as a bedtime proverb.

The Stair rose out of the mountainside like something a giant had started carving and then lost interest in. Cut steps, red rock, zigzagging up a face so steep that from where we stood, most of the steps looked like lines rather than ledges. Above them, the rock went the color of old rust, streaked with white mineral veins that looked — I will not lie about this — exactly like bones.

"That's not a trail," Odile said.

"That's my father's handwriting," I said, and held up the map.

She took it from me the way she took everything lately: carefully, like it might be rigged. Her hands were still wrapped from the scree field, from the decoy route she'd set for Halloway's climbers, and she turned the paper with her fingertips only, which gave her the look of someone handling something both precious and possibly diseased.

"Cut steps," she said finally. "In serpentine and ironstone. A hundred years ago. By men who were already starving."

"By men who had a reason," Cyrus said.

"Everyone who dies on a mountain has a reason."

Nobody argued with that, because nobody could. We stood there in the cold morning light while the wind came off the glacier above and made the whole face of the Stair hiss faintly, like it was breathing. Behind us, far below and east, somewhere past two ridgelines, Vess Halloway's people were climbing too. We'd bought ourselves a day with Odile's decoy. Maybe less. The snow line had come up the valley overnight like a tide that had forgotten which direction tides go.

"We could go around," Cyrus offered, without conviction. "The long way."

"The long way puts us on the high col in four days," Odile said. "The front comes in three. Maybe two." She looked up at the Stair again, and I watched her do the thing she did — the measuring, the weighing, the cold arithmetic of a woman who had once gotten the sum wrong and paid for it with a man's life. My father's life. I watched her do it and hated how much I trusted the answer.

"Well?" I said.

"It holds," she said. "Barely. If the steps hold and the weather holds and nobody falls." She handed the map back to me. "Three of those are prayers, Quentin. I can only guarantee one."

"Which one?"

"The weather doesn't care whether we pray or not. So it's not that one."

* * *

We roped up at the base. Odile insisted on the order: herself first, me in the middle, Cyrus last, because — she explained with the flatness of someone stating a law of physics — the middle position is the safest, and the smallest person goes in the middle. I didn't argue. Twelve years old is not an argument against a guide with thirty years of mountain in her bones, even a disgraced one. Even one whose guilt sat between us at every campfire like a fourth person nobody would look at directly.

The first hundred steps were almost kind. Old work, but good work — the 1897 expedition had cut them deep, with a chisel and patience, and whoever led that crew had known stone. Each step was wide enough for a whole boot. There were even chiseled handholds in places, little slots worn smooth by a century of gloves and bare fingers.

I tried to imagine them. That was becoming a habit, imagining the expedition — the ones whose waystation we'd slept in, whose journal had called the Provision *something carried*. I imagined a man on this exact step, a hundred and twenty-some years ago, swinging a chisel in air thin enough to make your blood stupid, cutting a staircase for people he would never meet. For me, maybe. That was the part that got under my skin and stayed there. Everything they'd done, they'd done forward. Into a future they couldn't see. Into mine.

"You're smiling," Cyrus said behind me. "People don't usually smile here."

"I'm thinking about the masons."

"Don't think about anything. Feet. Hands. Weather. That's the whole list."

"Yes, Papa," I said, and heard him huff, and felt absurdly pleased with myself, and then immediately ashamed of being pleased, because the word had come out easy and warm and it wasn't aimed at him, not really, it was aimed at a man missing on a mountain, and using it lightly felt like spending money that wasn't mine.

Cyrus didn't say anything else. But I noticed, in the way you notice things when your life depends on noticing, that he climbed a little closer after that.

* * *

The kindness ran out at two hundred steps.

The rock changed — the ironstone gave way to a band of crumbly red shale, and the old steps thinned into suggestions. Some had spalled away entirely, leaving gaps you had to stretch across. The handholds became optional. The exposure came up under us like water rising in a hold: to the left, nothing; to the right, nothing; below, the talus slope we'd left that morning, now a distant apron of gray, and beyond it the valley floor, where a river I couldn't hear anymore made its silver way toward the sea and the harbor town and everything ordinary.

I did the thing my father taught me. When fear climbs your spine, name five true things. One: the step under my left foot is solid. Two: the wind is from the west and steady. Three: Odile is ten steps up and her rope is taut but slack enough. Four: I am afraid, and afraid is not the same as falling. Five: Papa cut maps of this range for eleven years and he marked this route, and he marked it because he believed in it, and he was never wrong about terrain. Never once.

It was the fifth one I leaned on. It was also, I would learn within the hour, the one I should have been more careful with.

Because here is what I found out later, piecing it together from Odile's questions and Cyrus's silences: my father had surveyed the Red Stair from below. From the col above, through his glass. He'd drawn it as a route option, annotated, *viable, pre-snowfall.* He had never climbed it. He'd trusted the 1897 report the way I trusted him — completely, at a distance, without ever putting his own boots on the stone.

Maps are promises. And sometimes the person making them is promising something they've only ever seen from far away.

* * *

It happened at the shale band, on a stretch where the steps gave out entirely and the route traversed left across a rib of rotten rock, following a ledge no wider than my boot.

Odile went first. She moved like water finding its level — testing each hold before committing weight, her wrapped hands doing work they clearly hated. I remember thinking, in the strange floating way you think things during the calm before disaster, that her hands must hurt terribly, and that she never once mentioned it, and that this was either bravery or penance and probably both.

Then it was my turn, and the world narrowed to boot-widths.

Ledge. Step. Ledge. Handhold worn smooth by dead men's gloves. The drop on my right side had stopped being a number and become a presence, something with weight and attention, something that noticed you. I kept my eyes on the rock, on the next hold, on Odile's boots above me, and I named my five true things over and over like a prayer wheel.

I was halfway across the traverse when the rock spoke.

A crack — small, dry, almost polite, the sound of a knuckle cracking — and then the ledge under my trailing foot tilted, and a slab the size of a door peeled away from the face and dropped.

I don't remember falling. I remember the rope going tight, the lurch, the swing — my shoulder slamming into the rib, the world spinning from red rock to sky to valley and back — and then the second sound, worse than the first. Not a crack. A rip. Fiber letting go, strand by strand, somewhere above me.

The rope. The rope was failing.

"QUENTIN." Odile's voice, from everywhere at once. "HANDS. GIVE ME YOUR HANDS."

I looked up. She had anchored herself somehow — knee wedged, body flattened against the rib — and she was reaching down over the edge with both arms, both ruined wrapped hands, stretched out toward me over the drop. Below my boots the slab I'd broken was long gone, a puff of dust rising off the talus like the mountain exhaling.

The rope ripped again. I dropped half an arm's length. The jolt went through my teeth.

"My hands won't—" I started, because they were shaking, because the rock was dusting apart wherever I gripped, because I was twelve and terrified and the arithmetic of it was suddenly very simple: the rope was dying, and the only other thing between me and the valley floor was a woman I hadn't forgiven.

"Quentin." Her voice changed. All the flint went out of it. What was left underneath was something rawer. "Look at me. I am not letting go. Whatever happens, I am not letting go. Now give me your hands."

And here is the thing I have decided to believe, because the alternative is unbearable: I chose it. In the half-second I had, with the rope shrieking and the drop humming beneath me, I chose to reach up. I chose her.

My fingers found hers. Her grip closed around my wrists — through the wraps, through the blood that had soaked them stiff — and she pulled, and the rope parted above us with a sound like a snapped bone, and for one endless moment we hung on nothing but her arms and my trust, and then Cyrus was there — I never saw how he crossed the traverse so fast, I never asked — with his fist knotted in my collar and the anchor rope, hauling both of us onto solid stone.

We lay on the ledge gasping. The severed end of the rope slithered past us and down, down, a yellow thread dwindling into the valley, taking the old arithmetic with it.

* * *

Afterward, Odile sat with her back against the rib and unwrapped her hands, and I wished she wouldn't, and I couldn't stop watching.

The wraps had done their job badly. The rope burn from the scree field had opened again, and beneath it the skin was torn in stripes where my wrists had wrenched through her grip — where she had held on with meat and bone when holding on cost exactly that. Her palms were a ruin of raw red. Cyrus crouched with the kit and cleaned them, and she stared at the middle distance and let him, jaw tight, making none of the sounds a person makes.

"You should have let the rope take some of it," Cyrus said quietly. "You held on too long after it started parting."

"If I'd let go to re-grip, she'd have swung." Odile flexed her fingers experimentally, winced, kept flexing. "Swinging means impact. Impact means the shale lets go. You do the sums."

"I'm doing them now. They say your hands."

"They say her ribs." She looked at me then, straight on, the first time in days she'd managed it. "How are the ribs?"

"Sore," I said. True thing number one.

They bandaged her hands into two pale clubs. She flexed them again inside the wrapping, testing, and announced she could still belay, and Cyrus said absolutely not, and she said *I said I could, not that I'd enjoy it*, and the two of them argued about friction coefficients with the flat intensity of people who needed very much to argue about something that wasn't feelings.

I sat a few feet away, my heart still slamming, and did my own sums.

Here is what I knew. Odile Ferran had turned back on a mountain and my father had gone on alone and died alone, or at least vanished alone, and for weeks I had carried that fact like a stone in my chest, cold and specific and shaped exactly like her face. Every competent thing she did, every mile she led us, every meal she cooked — I'd taken it all and held it at arm's length. *This is what she does instead of saying sorry,* I'd told myself. *This is debt payment, not love.*

But she had reached down over a drop with those hands. She had held on past the point where holding on was sane. And when she'd spoken to me — *I am not letting go* — there had been nothing in her voice but the truth.

I didn't forgive her. I want to be honest about that, because everyone in stories forgives too fast and it makes forgiveness cheap. Something in me was still standing on that ledge in the Karst, still hearing her say *I turned back and he went on.* That wasn't going anywhere. Forgiveness, I suspected, was going to be its own climb, longer than the Red Stair, with worse footing.

But gratitude had arrived ahead of it, the way the vanguard of a storm arrives ahead of the storm. Gratitude had arrived and put its weight in my chest and made room.

"Odile." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. She turned. "Thank you. For — the hands."

Something moved through her face, quick as a fish under ice. "That's the job," she said.

"No," I said. "The job is the rope. The rope quit. That was you."

She didn't have an answer for that. She looked away, down the route, at the steps still climbing above us into the red rock, and I saw her jaw work once, twice, around words she wasn't ready to spend. Finally she said, "Your father would have made the same reach."

And that — that undid me a little, though I hid it by drinking water and pretending to check the map. Because it was the first time she'd said his name-adjacent words without the guilt strangling them, and because I believed her, and because believing her meant believing that the man who raised me and the woman who failed him belonged to the same species of stubborn love, and that I was made of it too.

* * *

We climbed the rest of the Stair in the long gold light of late afternoon, unroped where the rock allowed it and roped short where it didn't, moving slow and deliberate as old men. Odile led with her bandaged hands hooked into cracks, refusing help, refusing sympathy, accepting only the occasional boost from Cyrus's shoulder, which she permitted with the grace of a duchess boarding a carriage.

Near the top, the steps improved — the 1897 crew had saved their best work for the summit pitch, as if they'd known whoever came after would need encouragement most at the end. The final twenty steps were cut broad and clean, and someone had chiseled a mark into the capstone: the expedition crest, the same one burned into the waystation door, the same one stamped on the brass key riding in Cyrus's coat.

I put my palm on it. The stone was warm from the day's sun.

"They finished it," I said. "Whatever happened to them — starving, lost, all of it — somebody climbed back down and finished the stairs."

"Somebody wanted the next people to make it," Cyrus said.

The wind picked up as we topped out onto the col, and the first thing I saw was the sky to the north. It had gone wrong. Not dark exactly — bruised, banded, stacked with cloud that hadn't been there at dawn, building along the front Odile had been counting days against. The snow line on the peaks beyond had crept visibly lower since morning, like a tide coming in over the whole range.

Odile stood beside me, hands tucked under her arms, reading the sky the way I read charts.

"How long?" Cyrus asked.

She was quiet a moment. "I said three days this morning." Another pause. "I'm revising."

Below us, the safe route curved away east, hours of exposed ground, and somewhere behind it, Halloway's climbers. Ahead, the descent into the high country, and beyond it — if my father's map told the truth — the waystation country, and the Hollow Summit.

"Two days?" I asked.

Odile didn't answer right away. When she did, she was looking at the bruised northern sky, and her voice was very even, the way it always went even when the news was worst.

"One," she said. "Maybe less. We walk tonight."
