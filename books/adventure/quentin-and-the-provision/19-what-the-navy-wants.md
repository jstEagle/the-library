## 19. What the Navy Wants

We found the cache because Quentin's feet knew things her mouth didn't.

She'd stopped dead on the trail that morning, in that way she had—like a pointer dog, like her whole body was a compass needle—and said, "Dad's shorthand. Right there." She was crouched over a boulder at the treeline, tracing a mark that meant nothing to me and everything to her: a small carved symbol, half worn by forty years of weather, that her father had chipped into the stone the last time anyone saw him alive.

Odile circled it once, arms folded, saying nothing, which for Odile was practically a speech.

"Surveyors mark their routes," Quentin said. "But this isn't a route mark. This is the one he used for *something's here*. I've seen it on his old charts. It means he found a place worth coming back to."

"Or worth avoiding," Odile said.

"Same thing, out here."

She wasn't wrong. I'd spent enough years underwater to know that the difference between treasure and hazard is usually just which one you find first.

The mark pointed us off the trail, down into a fold of the mountain where a rockslide had scoured a raw scar through the pines. And there, half-buried at the base of the slide where the snowmelt had been slowly uncovering it for decades, was a crate. Not an 1897 crate—this was newer, military-green, stenciled with numbers and a crown-and-anchor mark I'd have known anywhere, because I'd spent the last month being interrogated by men who wore it on their caps.

Navy.

* * *

Odile pried the lid with her ice axe, working the corroded nails loose with a patience I didn't feel. Quentin hovered at her shoulder. I stood back and did the math I didn't like: this crate had been dropped, not placed. The scorch marks on the rock above it, the way the slide had sheared through—something had come down here. Something flying.

"A supply drop," Odile said, reading my face. "Or a crash."

"Helicopter," I said. "Navy's been running them over these valleys for weeks. I watched one from the boat before I ever set foot ashore."

"Before the cave," Quentin said. Not a question. She was getting frighteningly good at keeping my timeline straight.

"Before the cave," I admitted.

The lid came free. Inside, packed in straw gone gray and brittle, was gear—climbing rope, cold-weather rations in tins, a signal lamp, spare parts for a radio. Standard expedition kit, if expeditions were run by the Admiralty. Odile lifted items out one by one with a professional's contempt, cataloguing. Quentin went straight for the bottom, because Quentin always went straight for the bottom, the way other people go straight for the surface.

"Paper," she said.

It was a document case, oilskin-wrapped, and inside were papers that made the hair on my arms stand up under two layers of wool. Orders. Typed, dated, initialed. And not recent—some of them were older than I was. Older than Quentin's father. One was dated the year I was born, and it used a phrase I'd heard exactly once before, in a navy officer's clipped voice across a table in a dockside office I'd been invited into and strongly encouraged not to leave:

"*Recovery and permanent interment of the Provision.*"

I read it three times. The words didn't get any softer.

* * *

We sat among the rocks and I laid the papers out in order, the way you'd lay out a dive plan, because that was the only way I knew how to make a frightening thing manageable. Oldest first.

The oldest order was a search directive from the 1930s. Aerial survey of the range. Looking for, in the language of the thing, "*anomalous expedition remnants, circa 1897.*" The next, from the fifties, authorized a ground team. The next authorized another. There were gaps—years where nothing was filed, then years where everything was, as if the navy's interest had a pulse, a fever that came and went. And threaded through all of them, in margin notes and appended memos, the same refrain:

"*The Provision is not to be recovered. It is to be located and sealed.*"

"Sealed," Quentin said. "Not taken."

"Sealed," I agreed.

Odile had gone very still, the way she did when a slope started talking to her. "You don't bury a treasure," she said slowly. "You bury a body. You bury something that hurts to look at." She looked at me. "What did those men in 1897 *find*?"

I thought about the waystation. The stocked shelves. The journal that had called the Provision something *carried*, not gold. I thought about the brass key warm in the inner pocket where it had lived against my chest since a cave that smelled of salt and dead men's leather. I thought about the fact that a century of naval power had been pointed at this mountain range, on and off, like a searchlight that couldn't quite hold still—and that every order said the same thing: find it, and put it back to sleep.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But I know what it isn't. It isn't money. Nobody seals a fortune under a mountain and posts guards on it for seventy years. You do that for something dangerous. Or something true." I tapped the oldest order. "And this says *interment*. That's a word for coffins."

Quentin had gone quiet in the way that made my chest hurt—chin down, eyes bright, doing the arithmetic of her father. He'd found the trailhead. He'd found *this*, maybe, or something like it. And the navy's interest had a pulse, and the years it spiked—Odile saw it the same moment I did, because she said it out loud:

"Your father. When did he disappear?"

"Eleven years ago," Quentin said. "Autumn."

I spread the orders out and looked for the gap. There it was. A four-year silence in the file, ending two years before Quentin's father went up the mountain—and then a flurry of memos, urgent ones, the fever spiking again. Something had been found. Something had been disturbed. And a cartographer who marked his discoveries with a symbol meaning *worth coming back to* had walked into the middle of it and not walked out.

"They didn't kill him," Quentin said. Flat. Daring me to make it gentle.

"No," I said, because lying to her had stopped being an option somewhere around the sinkhole. "But they might have made sure nobody went looking. And they might have been the reason he went alone."

Odile looked away. I didn't need her confession again; I carried it with me like a stone in my boot. He'd gone alone because his guide turned back. The navy hadn't needed to lift a finger. The mountain and the guilt had done the work for them.

* * *

We burned nothing and took only what we needed—rope, rations, the signal lamp, because Odile said waste was a sin and she was the closest thing to a priest I'd met on this mountain. The papers I copied into the back of Quentin's father's map book, in her shorthand, which she taught me in an hour by the simple method of calling me an idiot until I got it right. The originals went back in the case, the case back in the crate, the crate back under its blanket of scree. If the navy came looking for their cache, I wanted them to find it the way they'd left it. Men who bury things get nervous when the ground's been disturbed.

But I kept one page. The margin memo, the newest one, the one that had made Odile's jaw tighten. It referenced a prior operation by a code name—*LANTERN BURIAL*—and it contained a single sentence that I copied out twice because I couldn't believe it the first time:

"*Asset remains interred at Hollow Summit per original disposition. No recovery authorized. No recovery will be authorized.*"

Hollow Summit.

The name meant nothing to me. It meant everything to Odile, apparently, because she read it over my shoulder and said, very quietly, "That's not on any chart I've ever guided from."

"It's on mine," Quentin said.

We both turned. She had the map book open, her father's last letter folded beside it, and her finger was on a peak in the high country we'd been circling for days without naming—a summit the survey maps left blank, a summit her father's ink had circled twice.

"Dad wrote it in his letter," she said. "I didn't know what it meant. I thought it was just what he called it." Her voice did something small and brave. "He was following them. The 1897 men. All the way to the end."

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of the slide, and I took first watch because I wanted to be alone with it, the way you want to be alone with a bad diagnosis. Below us the valleys were black and quiet. Somewhere out there a navy helicopter had swept this same air that morning, patient as a gull.

Here's what I understood, sitting in the cold with the brass key against my sternum like a second heartbeat: I had spent a month thinking I was being chased for something I'd *taken*. That was the story I'd told myself—the salvage story, the syndicate story, the simple story where men chase you because you stole their gold. It was a comfortable story. It made me a thief, and thieves know the rules.

But the papers said something worse. The navy didn't want the Provision back. They wanted it *down*. Whatever a community of shipwrecked idealists had carried up a mountain in 1897—records, seeds, medicines, letters, a testament to something they'd built out of nothing—it was buried under a summit with a hollow name, and the full weight of the Admiralty had spent seventy years making sure it stayed there. Not because it was dangerous.

Because it was *true*. Because somewhere in the navy's buried past was a thing they'd helped put in the ground, and the testament said so, and paper outlives the men who burn it.

You don't chase a thief for a hundred miles and up a mountain in winter for gold. Gold you can buy. You do it for a secret.

And I was carrying the key to it against my chest, and a twelve-year-old who'd lost her father to this same mountain was asleep ten feet away, and a disgraced guide who'd lost him too was watching the dark with her hands wrapped around her knees, and none of us, I thought, was going to turn back now.

The thought should have terrified me. Mostly it did.

But underneath the fear, in the place where I kept the other things I didn't say out loud, something settled into place with an almost audible click, like two halves of a mechanism finding each other. I'd spent my whole life diving other people's wrecks, salvaging other people's losses, taking a percentage of grief and calling it a living. This was the first time I'd ever gone *toward* something instead of away. Toward the summit. Toward whatever the 1897 men had thought was worth carrying, and her father had thought was worth dying for, and the navy had thought was worth seventy years of lying.

Toward dawn, Odile shook my shoulder for second watch and paused, looking down at me.

"You read the papers," she said. "You understand what's chasing us now. It isn't Halloway. Halloway wants money, and money can be bargained with." Her face was a pale shape above me, unreadable. "The other thing doesn't bargain."

"I know."

"Do you?" She crouched. "Because I've guided men who thought they knew, and the mountain charges extra for the lesson." She glanced toward Quentin's sleeping bag, then back at me. "Whatever's at the top of that peak—when we open it, we don't get to un-open it. Not for us. Not for her."

"I know," I said again, and this time it was true, and we both heard it.

She nodded once and went to her watch. I lay back and didn't sleep. Above the eastern ridge, very high, a light moved against the stars—steady, sweeping, patient. A helicopter, working the valleys in the dark now, too.

They'd found their cache disturbed. Or they were simply getting closer. Either way, the fever in those old files was spiking again, and we were the reason, and the only way out was up—toward a summit that wasn't on any chart, carrying a key the navy would bury us with if it came to that.

The light swept the ridge, passed over us without seeing, and moved on.

For now.
