## 20. Ambush at the Col

There's a particular quality to silence at altitude that I've come to distrust. Down at sea level, quiet is just quiet — gulls, rigging, the harbor breathing. Up here, above the tree line, where the wind has scraped the world down to rock and lichen, silence is a held breath. It's the sound of something waiting.

I should have trusted it.

We came up to the col in the grey half-light of morning, Odile leading, Quentin between us, and me dragging the rear with my knees complaining in a language older than any of us. The col itself was a saddle of shattered stone between two shoulders of black mountain, maybe two hundred paces across, wind-scoured and bare. On the far side, the trail dropped toward a valley that looked, from up there, like someone had folded green velvet into the earth. It was the kind of place you cross quickly without ever meaning to — a threshold, not a destination.

Which is exactly what we should have done. Crossed quickly.

Instead Odile stopped at the near edge, crouched, and touched the ground with two fingers the way she always did before making decisions she didn't like. "Scree's been disturbed," she said. "Recent."

"Goats?" Quentin offered.

"Goats don't stack rocks." Odile nodded toward a cairn twenty yards out on the saddle, one that hadn't been there when we'd scouted the route from below two days ago. Someone had built it. And next to it, half-hidden by a slab, a coil of fresh rope end — cut, not frayed.

My stomach did the thing it does when I'm thirty meters down and the light goes wrong. "Halloway," I said.

"Halloway," Odile agreed, and there was no satisfaction in being right. There never is.

Quentin had her father's map out already, fingers tracing the contour lines with that quick, certain touch of hers, as if the paper remembered her father's hands and wanted to please her. "There's a low route around the north shoulder. Adds half a day."

"Half a day we don't have," Odile said. "Snow line's climbing. You can see it eating the valley behind us."

So we did the stupid thing. We crossed the col anyway, fast and low, Odile first with her ice axe loose in her hand, Quentin tucked against the rock wall on the sheltered side, me last because I'm heaviest and apparently the most expendable by unanimous unspoken vote.

We were forty paces from the far edge when the mountain spoke.

"THAT'S FAR ENOUGH."

The voice came from above and to the right, from a shelf of rock I'd have sworn was empty. Then they were simply *there* — four figures rising out of the landscape like the mountain had grown them overnight. Climbers, roped in pairs, positioned on two shelves with clean sightlines down onto the saddle. One of them had a rifle slung across his back, which was either theater or a promise, and I genuinely couldn't tell which was worse.

And stepping out from behind the largest boulder, brushing grit off her sleeves like she'd merely stopped to admire the view, came Vess Halloway.

Now, I've met sharks. I've worked for them, borrowed from them, and once — memorably — been dangled over their tank. Vess Halloway was not a shark. Sharks are honest about what they are; they eat you and move on. Vess was something rarer: a woman who genuinely believed the world was an accounting error that she'd been sent to correct. She stood on that col in expensive gear that had never once been cold, and she smiled at me the way a banker smiles at a signature.

"Cyrus Oyelaran," she said. "You look terrible."

"Altitude," I said. "You look like you took a helicopter."

"I did. Some of us plan." She walked forward until Odile shifted, axe coming up, and Vess stopped with the easy confidence of someone who knew exactly how many guns were pointed at our little family. "Let's not make this tedious. I want the key. I want the route. And I want you to stop wasting my money by breathing my air."

"Your air?"

"I financed this expedition, Cyrus. Every rope, every ration, every step you've taken since you burned your contract — you've been spending *my* investment." She spread her hands. "I'm simply here to collect."

Behind me, I heard Quentin's breath go shallow. That sound did something to me that I didn't have time to examine.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about negotiations: everyone tells you the important thing is leverage. Leverage, position, walk-away power. All true, as far as it goes. But the real currency — the one nobody puts in the manuals — is knowing what you're willing to lose. Walk into any bargain without that knowledge and the other side will find it for you, usually at the worst possible moment.

I didn't know yet what I was willing to lose. I was about to find out.

"Four climbers," Odile said quietly, not moving her eyes off the rifleman. "Two on each shelf. Crossfire covers the whole saddle. We run, we die tired."

"Thank you, Ferran," Vess said pleasantly. "I did consider hiring you, once. Before the incident."

Odile's jaw tightened but she said nothing, and I filed that away — the way Vess said *the incident*, like she'd read the file, like she'd enjoyed it.

Vess turned back to me. "The key, Cyrus. And the route through the Red Stair. Give me both and I'll let you three walk down into that valley with your health and my compliments."

"And if I don't?"

She sighed, theatrical, regretful. "Then my associate Mr. Renk—" she gestured at the rifleman, a slab-faced man with the patient stillness of a butcher waiting for a delivery "—will demonstrate why I brought him. Starting with the girl."

The world went very simple and very loud.

I want to be precise here, because precision matters in what happened next. In that moment I did not think about the Provision. I did not think about the fortune I'd chased out of a drowned cave, or the syndicate debt that had started all this, or the brass key sitting against my chest inside my shirt where it had lived for weeks, warm as a second heartbeat. I thought about a twelve-year-old girl standing at the edge of a col with the wind pulling at her coat, trying very hard not to look scared because she'd decided somewhere along the way that being scared in front of adults was a luxury she couldn't afford.

Her father had gone into these mountains carrying something and never come back. She'd followed his map across half a range to finish what he started. And Vess Halloway was going to use her as a bargaining chip because Vess Halloway had never in her life met anything she wouldn't spend.

"No," I said.

Vess raised an eyebrow. "No?"

"You heard me. No." I stepped forward, deliberately putting myself between Renk's sightline and Quentin, and I felt something shift in my chest — not fear, oddly. Clarity. Like surfacing after a long dive and taking the first real breath. "You can shoot me. You might even shoot Odile, though she'll cost you two of yours doing it. But you're not touching the girl, and every second you waste threatening her is a second the snows climb higher and close *your* window too. So let's talk like professionals."

Something flickered across Vess's face. Not anger. Recalculation. She was, whatever else she was, good at her work.

"Professionals," she repeated. "All right. Talk."

"The key isn't on me."

That was a lie. The key was pressing cold against my sternum at that exact moment. But lying, I've found, is like diving: the trick isn't holding your breath, it's managing the air you have so it looks effortless.

"Where is it?" Vess asked.

"Cached. Sealed. Somewhere only I can find." I shrugged. "Kill me and it stays in the mountains until the glaciers spit it out in a hundred years. Take me prisoner and drag me around the range looking for it, and you'll lose the pass to the snows — and so will I, which means you get nothing. My only value to you is alive, cooperative, and free."

"And why would you cooperate?"

"Because you're offering me a trade." I held up one finger. "The key and the route, delivered to you at the Hollow Summit approach — after we're through, after you've got what you actually want, which was never the legend anyway, was it? You want whatever the navy buried. The 1897 cache is just the door." I watched her face very carefully as I said it, and there — a fractional tightening around the eyes. Confirmation. "So here's my offer. You let the three of us walk now. We meet you at the summit approach in six days. I hand over the key and the route. Everyone gets paid."

"Why would I believe you'd show?"

"Because I burned my contract rather than give you the key in the harbor, and you're wondering why." I let that sit. "Because I need Halloway resources to get through what's ahead, same as you need me. Because—" and here I gave her the truth, wrapped in the lie, which is the only way truth travels well "—I'm tired, Vess. I'm tired of running, I'm tired of the cold, and I'm tired of people pointing rifles at children. Six days. Summit approach. You have my word."

Silence, except the wind moving through the col like a long exhale.

I could see her running the arithmetic. Four climbers against three, yes, but two of her four were porters, really, and Odile Ferran alone was worth two hired men in broken country. A firefight on a col with no cover meant casualties, and casualties meant explanations, and explanations meant the navy — who were also hunting this prize and would love nothing more than a reason to shut Vess Halloway down permanently. And a hostage was worthless if the hostage's guardian was the only man who knew where the key was cached.

Mostly, though, she saw what I wanted her to see: a greedy man cutting his losses.

"Six days," she said finally. "Summit approach. Both keys, Cyrus — I know there are two. Don't insult me."

Both keys. So she'd talked to someone. That was a wound I'd examine later, when I had bandages and time.

"Both keys," I agreed.

"And the girl stays with you as surety of your good behavior. If you don't appear—"

"If I don't appear, you'll never find the vault, and the navy will, and they'll bury it deeper than the last time. Your move costs you everything. Mine costs me a partner." I smiled, and I made it a diver's smile — flat, patient, bottomless. "I think I'm getting the better deal."

* * *

They watched us descend the far side of the col for the first hour. I know because Odile kept checking, and because the feeling of being watched doesn't leave your spine just because the watchers do.

We didn't speak until we were down among the boulders of the lower valley, where the wind broke apart and the pines began, thin and stubborn. Odile picked a campsite with her usual economy — water, cover, two exits — and only then did she turn to me with an expression I'd learned to read as her version of screaming.

"Cached," she said. "Sealed. Only you can find it."

"Yes."

"It's in your shirt."

"It's in my shirt."

Quentin made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. She was sitting on a rock with her arms around her knees, and she was looking at me with an expression I couldn't parse — something between fury and awe.

"You lied," she said. "To her face. With Renk's rifle right there."

"I lied to her face with Renk's rifle right there, yes."

"She would have killed us."

"She might have. Now she won't, for six days." I sat down heavily on a log, and my knees filed their formal complaint. "Six days is a lead. Six days is a head start on the Stair. By the time she realizes there's no cache, we'll be past anything her porters can climb."

"And then?" Odile asked. She was feeding twigs into a smokeless fire, not looking at me. "When she catches us — and she will catch us, Cyrus, because Vess Halloway does not lose money, she loses *people* — what's your plan then? Another lie? You've spent most of your truth on that col."

I didn't have an answer ready, which was unusual for me and unpleasant to discover. I looked at Quentin instead. She'd pulled out her father's map again, smoothing it on her knee, tracing the route to the Red Stair with her fingertip, lips moving slightly as she calculated distances the way her father must have taught her. The firelight caught the sharp bones of her face. Twelve years old, and she carried grief the way mountaineers carry rope — coiled, always ready, assumed to be part of the load.

And there it was. The thing I'd gone into that col not knowing and come out of it knowing.

I'd spent my whole adult life keeping accounts. What I owed the syndicate. What the sea owed me. What I'd lost and what it had cost and whether the ledger balanced. I'd taken the brass key because I thought it was an asset — the first real asset a salvage diver from the harbor flats had ever held. And on that col, with a rifle aimed at a child, I'd discovered that I had finally acquired something I would not trade, would not hedge, would not put up as collateral. Not the key. Not the route. Not my own skin, particularly — I'd have handed Vess my skin gladly if it bought the girl safe passage.

It was the strangest feeling of my life, richer than any hold I'd ever opened. Terror and wealth in the same breath. Because that's what it is, discovering what you'll sacrifice: it's finding treasure. Everything else — the debts, the dives, the decades of careful solitary arithmetic — was just ballast I'd been carrying because nobody had told me I could cut it loose.

"Cyrus." Quentin was watching me. "What happens in six days?"

I looked at Odile. Odile looked at the fire.

"In six days," I said, "we're going to be standing at the summit approach, and Vess Halloway is going to be waiting for a handover that isn't coming. Which means between now and then, we have to become very hard to catch, and harder to stop." I reached into my shirt and drew out the brass key, letting the firelight run along its worn crest. "And we're going to need to move faster than we've ever moved."

Quentin studied the key, then me. "You gave your word," she said. "At the col. You said 'you have my word.'"

"I did."

"That was a lie too?"

I weighed it. Lying to her felt different than lying to Vess — heavier, like diving with a full kit. "It was a lie shaped like a bridge," I said finally. "It got us across. What I build on the other side is up to me."

She considered that with the terrible seriousness of twelve-year-olds everywhere, then nodded once, and went back to her map, and I understood that I had passed some examination whose questions I hadn't known were being asked.

Odile banked the fire and looked at me across the flames. "Six days," she said. "The Stair takes two if the weather holds. That leaves four days of Vess Halloway knowing she's been played, in mountains she knows better than we do, with men she pays by the result." She fed the fire one last twig. "Sleep fast, Oyelaran. Tomorrow we start running."

I lay back on the cold ground with the key warm against my chest and the stars wheeling overhead, indifferent and enormous, and I thought about how the sea teaches you one lesson above all others: the moment you find the thing worth surfacing for is the moment the water starts closing over your head.

Behind us, high up on the dark shoulder of the col, a light blinked twice. Paused. Blinked twice again.

Signal lamps. Halloway wasn't waiting six days.

She was already moving.
