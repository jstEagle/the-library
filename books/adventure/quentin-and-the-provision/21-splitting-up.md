## 21. Splitting Up

I have made a great many bad decisions in my life, and I have a professional's appreciation for them — the way a climber appreciates a fall she didn't take. You study it. You learn the shape of it. You file it away so that next time you can recognize the ledge giving way under you half a second sooner.

This was not one of those decisions. This one I made with my eyes open, which is either growth or a new species of folly.

We came down off the col at first light with Halloway's climbers somewhere behind us — close enough that Cyrus had counted their cookfire smoke twice in the dark, far enough that we hadn't yet heard their voices on the wind. That margin would not hold. Men like Halloway's hire money fast and climb angry, and angry climbers make up distance the way water makes up ground.

"We split," I said.

They both looked at me. Quentin with her father's flat gray stare that made you feel like an unsurveyed feature on a map, waiting to be drawn in. Cyrus with the look of a man doing arithmetic he already suspects will embarrass him.

"Explain," he said.

"Halloway wants three things. The keys, the route, and nobody left alive who knows both." I crouched and drew in the frost-scummed dirt with a stick: two lines diverging from our position. "He's tracking a party of three. Three sets of boot prints, three bodies to watch for, three people to feed and slow down. A party of three moves at the speed of its weakest member and camps where the weakest member can sleep."

"Charming," said Cyrus.

"True," I said. "So we stop being three. I take the east flank — the low route through the moraine country, loud, visible, stupid. I let them see me. One woman alone reads as either bait or straggler, and either way they'll commit climbers to her, because a lone guide is the one piece of this puzzle they can interrogate."

"And we go high," Quentin said. She wasn't asking. Her finger came out and tapped the western line, up into the white shoulder of the range where my map went vague and her father's went precise. "The Red Stair approach. Papa marked it."

"The Red Stair is a rumor with altitude sickness," I said. "It's near-vertical cut steps in rock that sheds like a dog in summer. If your father marked it, he marked it because he was either brilliant or desperate, and from everything I've heard about Edouard Marchand, those were the same thing."

Quentin's chin came up. I watched her decide not to cry about it, which took effort, and I respected the effort more than I could say. There was a time — not long ago, if I'm honest, maybe forty-eight hours ago, before the waystation and the storm and the thing I confessed that I will spend the rest of my life paying for — when she would have looked to me and found nothing worth trusting. Now she looked at me and waited, which was worse, or better. I hadn't decided.

"You'd be drawing them onto yourself," Cyrus said slowly. "Alone. With no rope partner, no backup, and a head start of maybe four hours."

"I've been alone on mountains before. It's how I got disgraced, actually. Alone is my native country." I snapped the stick and stood. "And before you argue — and you will argue, because you're the sort of man who argues with weather — consider what happens if we stay together. They catch us at some col with no cover, and then it's three against six with Quentin in the middle. Or we run together and all arrive at the summit too late and too spent, and Halloway takes everything at the door. Splitting is the only arithmetic that works."

"It's not arithmetic," Cyrus said. "It's sacrifice wearing arithmetic's coat."

"Well," I said. "I know the coat well. It fits me."

* * *

He argued for another twenty minutes, which I'll admit I enjoyed more than I should have. Not the argument itself — the fact of it. Odile Ferran, blacklisted, clientless, the guide whose name other guides used as a cautionary tale over stove fires, had someone arguing for her safety. It had been long enough since that happened that I'd forgotten the sensation. It sits strangely, like a pack adjusted by someone else's hands.

The terms we settled were these: I take the east moraine at mid-morning, moving loud, leaving sign a blind man could follow. I carry one of the two tents, most of the spare food, and — this was Cyrus's addition, insisted upon with the stubbornness of a man who had nearly drowned twice in one month and learned something from it — the decoy satchel containing the brass key's twin, which Quentin had fashioned from a lump of lead and a file during two nights of storm-bound boredom. The girl had cast a forgery good enough to fool me at arm's length. Her father's blood ran true.

The real keys stayed with Cyrus, wrapped against his chest, because if Halloway's men caught me, I wanted them to catch me holding something worth stopping for. A lie is only protection if it's load-bearing.

"If they take you," Cyrus said, "you tell them nothing. Not the route, not the summit name, nothing. They won't kill you outright — Halloway needs a guide who knows the range, and dead women don't negotiate."

"They might disagree."

"Then don't get taken."

"Profound. Write it on my headstone."

Quentin said nothing through most of this. She stood apart, checking straps on a pack nearly as big as she was, with the fierce economy of a child who has learned that useful children get kept. When the argument finally burned down to embers, she crossed the scree to me and held something out.

It was her father's whistle. Brass, dented, on a leather cord gone soft with years of a neck's warmth.

"Papa's signal code," she said. "One blast means I'm here. Two means trouble, come fast. Three—" she stopped, and I watched her swallow whatever lived behind the word, "—three means goodbye. Don't use three."

I took the whistle. My hands, which have held ice axes above four-thousand-meter faces without trembling, were not entirely steady.

"I'll bring it back," I said.

"That's not why I gave it to you." She looked up at me, twelve years old and older than the mountain. "I gave it so you'd come back. Whistles work better on the living."

* * *

I want to record, here, honestly, what I felt when I turned east and they turned west, because I suspect this chapter of my life will be read by exactly no one, and honesty costs nothing when the audience is empty.

I felt relief.

Not at leaving them — God, no, though the solitude itself has always been a kind of medicine to me. The relief was this: for the first time since I turned back on that glacier and let Edouard Marchand walk on alone into the white, I was choosing risk on purpose, with my whole chest, for someone else's sake. For two years the guilt had been a stone in my boot that no amount of walking wore smooth. And here was a chance to set it down — not by forgiving myself, I'm not fool enough to think absolution comes that cheap, but by spending myself in the same currency he was spent in. Risk, freely given. If the mountain wanted a life from this expedition, it could have mine, and I would go into that trade with my eyes open, which is more than Edouard got from me.

That's not noble. I know it isn't. There's a self-destruction in it that any honest priest would flag inside a minute. But nobility and penance wear each other's coats too, and on a mountain you learn not to inspect your motives too closely at altitude. You check your knots instead.

The moraine country east of the col is a landscape built by a god with a grudge and infinite patience. Rock ground to gravel, gravel piled in ridges, ridges slumped into hollows, the whole gray mess stitched together by meltwater streams the color of milk. Bad going, slow going — except that I wasn't trying to go fast. I was trying to be seen.

I made a theater of it. I walked the skyline where the light would catch me. I built a cookfire at noon in plain view of the valley below, big and smoky, burning green scrub I didn't need to burn. I left my boot prints in every soft patch and scratched arrows into stone that pointed nowhere useful. Somewhere below and behind, I told myself, a pair of hard men with good optics were watching a lone figure move east with the confidence of someone who knows where she's going, and every hour they spent confirming that confidence was an hour Cyrus and Quentin gained on the high route.

By late afternoon I had my confirmation. Two figures, small with distance, moving along the eastern bench below me — not following my line exactly but paralleling it, keeping pace, patient as ticks. Halloway had committed his watchers to me. The trap was sprung, and I was the cheese.

I allowed myself one moment of pure professional satisfaction, standing on a boulder with the wind pulling at my hood, and then I did the dangerous thing. I thought about them. About Cyrus teaching Quentin to read cloud shapes two nights ago, his big scarred hands tracing the sky while she corrected him with her father's shorthand. About the way the girl had said *don't use three*, and the way I had meant *I promise* without saying it, because promises are just debts with better manners.

Then I moved west. Quietly now. The theater was over; the second act required me to be a ghost.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, called for me to loop south through the drainage, cross the high shoulder after dark, and rejoin the high route a day behind them — close enough to cover their backs, far enough that any pursuit tracking *me* wouldn't be led to them. It was a good plan. I want that on the record. It failed for reasons no plan survives contact with, namely weather, terrain, and the specific malice of scree.

The crossing should have taken four hours. It took nine. The shoulder I'd remembered from a survey map fifteen years old had grown a serac field in the interim — blue ice leaning over the route like a drunk over a fence — and I lost two hours finding a line beneath it that didn't put me under several hundred tons of very patient death. Then the temperature dropped through the floor of the evening and my water bottle froze solid in its sleeve, and I climbed the last pitch by starlight with my fingers working on memory and spite.

I came down onto the high route an hour past midnight, feet screaming, and found the first marker within ten minutes.

Quentin's father used cairns — small ones, knee-high, with a flat capstone angled to point the way. But these weren't his. These were fresh, stacked that day, the stones still carrying the damp of their undersides. Someone had passed through here recently, moving fast, and had marked the trail for whoever followed.

At the third cairn I found the note, weighted under a stone, written in pencil on the back of a strip of chart paper in a hand I knew — the careful, slightly formal print of a girl raised by a cartographer.

*Odile — if you're reading this you made the crossing, which Papa's map said was impossible, so I owe you an apology for doubting it. We're a half day ahead, camped at the notch below the Stair. Cyrus says wait for us. Cyrus also says a lot of things. Come anyway. — Q*

I sat down on the cold rock and laughed until it turned into something else, and I let it, because there was no one to see, and that is the only circumstance under which I permit myself the luxury.

She had left markers for me. Knowing the risk — knowing that sign works in both directions, that Halloway's men could read cairns as easily as I could — she had still spent her hours stacking stones into an invitation. *Come anyway.* As if there had ever been a question. As if the whole eastern detour, the frozen bottle, the serac field, the theater of it all, had been anything but the long way around to this: a knee-high pile of rocks and a note in pencil, telling me I was expected.

I blew the whistle once — *I'm here* — knowing they were too far to hear it, needing to blow it anyway. Then I shouldered the pack and started up the trail toward the notch, toward the Red Stair, toward whatever the mountain intended to do with the three of us next.

Behind me, far down in the eastern dark, I saw a light move along the bench where I had walked that morning. Then a second light. Then, faintly, carried on the wind shift, a sound I knew from bad years: the thin metallic ring of pitons being hammered, and voices calling to each other in the clipped cadence of men climbing fast toward something they've been promised.

They'd found my false trail's end. And they'd found the cairns.

I put my head down and climbed.
