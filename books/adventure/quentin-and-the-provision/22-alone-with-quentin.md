## 22. Alone With Quentin

Here is what nobody tells you about being twelve and alone in the mountains with a grown man who isn't your father: it's mostly silence.

Not bad silence. Not the kind that sits on your chest like my father's coat used to when I was small and he'd pretend to fall asleep on me so I couldn't sneak off to draw. This silence was the working kind — boots on scree, breath in rhythm, the creak of Cyrus's pack frame where he'd patched it with cord. We'd been on the high route for two days since Odile turned east with half our food and all of our luck, and we'd spent most of those two days not talking, because talking took breath and breath took strength and strength was the only currency we had left.

But by the third afternoon the trail crested onto a shelf of grey stone above the tree line, wide and flat as a table someone had abandoned mid-meal, and Cyrus dropped his pack and said, "We camp here. And before you argue —" because I had already opened my mouth to argue — "we're ahead of schedule. Your father's shortcut bought us that. Spend it."

I didn't argue. Mostly because he'd said *your father's shortcut* like it was scripture, and I'm not made of stone.

* * *

He built the fire small, the way Odile had drilled into us — deadwood only, tucked behind a windbreak of stacked slabs, smoke thin enough to be mistaken for ground mist. Then he did something strange. He sat down facing west, away from the fire, and just looked at the sky for so long I thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up, which Odile said divers could do, which I hadn't believed until now.

"You're staring at nothing," I told him.

"I'm staring at everything." He patted the rock beside him without turning around. "Sit. Lesson one."

"We're not doing lessons."

"We are absolutely doing lessons. You want to get over the Hollow Summit before the snows? Then you need to learn to read weather, because maps don't tell you what the sky's going to do, and your father—" he caught himself, the way he always did around mentions of Papa, gentling his voice like approaching a spooked animal, "—your father's shorthand can show you where the pass *is*, but it can't tell you if the pass will let you through."

That got me. I sat.

"Clouds," he said, pointing his chin at the western horizon. "Tell me what you see."

I looked. "Clouds."

"Cyrus Oyelaran, salvage diver, dies of frustration on a mountain shelf. His last words: 'clouds.'"

"They're white," I amended. "Fluffy. Sort of... piled up?"

"Piled up good. Now look at their bottoms."

I squinted. The undersides were flat, darker than the tops, like the clouds had been set down on an invisible shelf. "Flat."

"Flat bottoms mean they've hit stable air and stopped growing. Those are honest clouds. They'll drift over, maybe spit a little, and move on. It's the ones with *no* flat bottom you fear — the ones still building, boiling up like something cooking. You see a tower cloud growing past noon, you find shelter, no arguments." He glanced sideways at me. "Your father knew this. There's a symbol in his shorthand for it, isn't there? Little tower with lines under it?"

I stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've been watching you draw in the margins of that map for three days, and you do it without thinking. Little towers with lines. Little circles with tails. You inherited more than his eyes, Quentin." He said it plainly, no ceremony, and went back to the clouds, and I had to look very hard at the horizon for a while because apparently altitude does something to your face that makes water come out of it.

We watched the light go long and gold. He taught me the difference between weather that comes *at* you and weather that comes *around* you — how wind swinging from southwest to northwest meant a front dragging cold behind it, how a ring around the moon meant moisture stacking up high, how the mountains made their own clouds in the afternoon like breath on a window and you shouldn't panic about those. He taught me that sailors' rules mostly held on land, but the mountains cheated, because terrain funneled wind into lies.

"The sea's honest," he said. "The sea will kill you, but it tells you it's going to. Mountains gossip. They'll smile at you all morning and drop a storm on your head by two."

"That sounds like people."

"That sounds exactly like people." He smiled at the fire. "Vess Halloway smiles like a clear morning. Remember that."

* * *

By full dark the sky had scrubbed itself clean, and that was lesson two.

I'd seen stars my whole life — Papa used to take sightings with his sextant even on survey trips where he didn't strictly need them, "to keep my hand honest," he said. But Cyrus lay back on the rock with his hands behind his head and read the sky like a chart, and it was different hearing it from him. Rougher. Saltier. He named things wrong on purpose sometimes and dared me to correct him, and when I did — Polaris isn't the brightest star, it's just the one that holds still — he grinned like I'd handed him salvage.

"Find me north," he said.

I found the Big Dipper, ran the pointer stars out five lengths, put my finger on Polaris. He grunted approval. "Now find me south without it."

That was harder. He walked me through it — Orion's belt, the three stars in a row, follow them down to the horizon and that's roughly south, roughly, in these hours. Crux wasn't up yet this time of year, he said, or we'd have it easier. He showed me how the whole sky wheels around the pole star like a slow door on a hinge, and how if you know the hour you can use the wheel like a clock, and if you know the date you can use it like a calendar.

"Why do I need this? I have a compass."

"Compasses fail. Break, freeze, get confiscated, get left in a cave with a maniac chasing you. The sky doesn't get confiscated." He rolled his head toward me. "Also, your father navigated by it. Thought you should carry the same tools he did."

There it was again — that careful way he had of handing me Papa, piece by piece, like he was passing me something breakable across a gap. I decided to return the favor.

"Lesson three," I said, and went and got the map.

* * *

Papa's map was the thing I guarded harder than the keys, harder than the food. Oilcloth-wrapped, soft at the folds, covered edge to edge in his hand — and not just his handwriting, which was bad enough, but his *shorthand*, the private code he'd invented over twenty years of surveying because standard notation, he claimed, was for people with too much paper and not enough mountains.

Cyrus had been respectful of it the way he was respectful of everything of mine: carefully, from a distance, like it might bite. Tonight I spread it flat between us by firelight and said, "You keep asking me how I know the route. Time you learned to ask the map yourself."

He leaned in. In the firelight his face had that diver's look — total attention, the kind most adults never give anything.

"Everything's abbreviated," he said. "These dashes along here—"

"Slope shading. One dash, gentle. Two, steep. Three—" I tapped a section west of us, dense with triple strokes, "—don't. That's the cliff band above the Red Stair's shoulder. He drew threes there and wrote *NO* in the margin, and my father did not write NO lightly."

"And these little hooks? Like commas?"

"Water. A comma with a circle means spring, reliable year-round. Comma with a cross means seasonal — dry by late summer, which is now, so we skip those." I moved my finger down the eastern edge of the sheet. "The triangles are cairns he built or found. Filled triangle means confirmed. Outline means reported, unverified."

"There's a filled one up on that spur." Cyrus traced it. "And next to it, a symbol like a bird?"

I laughed before I could stop myself. "That's not a bird. That's a *wind*. A flag on a pole with the tail showing direction — the tail points which way the prevailing wind comes from, and the number of tail-feathers is rough strength. Three feathers, shelter every night on that spur or your tent becomes a kite."

"Feathers." He shook his head slowly. "Twenty years of professional surveyors using international symbols, and Dunmore invents *feathers*."

"He said standards were written by committee for country that doesn't exist. Flat, boring country." I smoothed a wrinkle flat with my thumb. "He had symbols for everything. Little ladder for fixed ropes or ladders found in place. An X with a dot — danger, tested. X without a dot — danger, guessed. He always said the difference between those two X's was the whole profession." My voice did something on the last part, wobbled, and I pretended it hadn't, and Cyrus pretended he hadn't noticed, which was its own kindness.

Then he pointed at a mark I'd been avoiding all evening: a small square with a diagonal slash, near the upper basin below the summit approach. "That one?"

I was quiet a moment. "Waystation. Second one. Square means shelter, slash means stocked. He marked it in his last letter too." I pulled the letter from my breast pocket — I kept it there now, against my skin, paper gone soft as cloth. "Read the postscript again. Out loud. I want to hear if it sounds different to you than it does to me."

Cyrus took it carefully. He read:

*"If you're reading this and I'm not home, the Provision is real and it is not what they say. Follow the squares. Trust the feathers. The vault wants to be found by the right hands — it has been waiting a hundred years for hands that carry things onward instead of keeping them. Tell Q the map is her inheritance. All of it. Every symbol. She already reads me better than anyone."*

Silence. Fire crackle. Somewhere downslope, a rock clattered — probably a fox, probably nothing, but we both went still until the night settled again.

"He wrote that," I said, "like a man packing a bag. Like he knew."

"He wrote it like a man who'd seen something true and wanted it to survive him." Cyrus folded the letter along its old creases and gave it back with both hands, formal as a sailor returning a flag. "Those aren't the same thing, Quentin. The first one's fear. The second one's love. I've written both kinds and I can tell you they feel identical in the moment and completely different ten years later."

"How would you know? Ten years later?"

The question came out sharper than I meant. He didn't flinch. He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"Because I have a sister," he said, "who got a letter from me eleven years ago, and it was the fear kind, and I've never fixed it. Because I left a family the way you leave a harbor — easy, at night, telling yourself the tide will bring you back. Tides don't work that way. People don't either." He looked at me across the fire. "So when I say your father loved you in ink, understand I know the difference, because I chose the other one."

I didn't know what to say to that. So I did what Papa would have done, which was answer with the map. I turned it around, found the second waystation's square-and-slash, and said, "Then we go there. Tomorrow. And you learn the rest of the symbols on the way, because if anything happens to me, you'll need to read him without me translating."

"Nothing's happening to you."

"Odile said that about Papa's route once, probably."

He winced. Fair. I felt a little bad. Not much.

"Deal," he said finally. "But same terms both directions. If anything happens to me, you take the keys and the map and you finish it. Alone if you have to. Can you do that?"

I thought about lying to make him feel better. Papa had hated liars, even kind ones. Especially kind ones.

"Yes," I said. "I can."

He nodded once, slow, like a man signing something. Then he pointed up. "One more thing before sleep. Ring around the moon."

I looked. There it was — a faint silver halo, wide and perfect, like the moon had been set inside a compass ring drawn by someone enormous.

"What's it mean?"

"Moisture, high up, ice crystals. Weather coming in a day, maybe two." He started spreading his bedroll. "Which means tomorrow we make distance while it's honest-cloud blue above us, and the day after, we're at that waystation before the sky makes good on its promise."

I copied the ring into the margin of the map, in Papa's style — a circle with short rays all around, and beside it I wrote, in his shorthand, the little symbol he'd invented for *confirmed by observation*: an X with a dot.

Danger, tested. Confirmed, seen with my own eyes.

It felt right. It felt like adding my own line to a document a hundred years long.

* * *

I woke once in the black hours, cold seeping through the bedroll, and heard Cyrus talking in his sleep. Just a few words, low and wrecked: *"Should've stayed. Should've—"*

I lay there looking at the stars through the gap in our windbreak, wheeling slow around their patient pole, and I thought about how he'd taught me all evening that the sky doesn't lie but mountains gossip, and people are worse than either, except sometimes — lying awake listening to a man apologize to somebody who wasn't there — sometimes they're better, because they can decide to change what they say.

In the morning I was going to teach him the symbol Papa used for *home*. It wasn't a house. It was a small circle with a smaller circle inside it, and underneath, two short strokes, like a thing held safe inside a bigger thing.

I hadn't known what it meant until this trip. Maps are like that. You draw the truth before you understand it.

I rolled over and slept, and dreamed of feathers on the wind, three of them, pointing east — and woke to Cyrus shaking my shoulder, his face lit grey-gold by a dawn that was already wrong, clouds boiling up over the western peaks with no flat bottoms at all, building fast, and behind us, far down the valley we'd left, a thread of smoke rising where no smoke should be.
