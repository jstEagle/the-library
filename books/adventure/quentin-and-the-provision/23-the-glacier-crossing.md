## 23. The Glacier Crossing

There is a particular sound a glacier makes when it decides it doesn't like you. It's not a roar. It's not a crack, exactly. It's more like a very old man clearing his throat in the next room — a deep, mineral *hrrm* that travels up through your boot soles and into your teeth and says, with great patience: *I have been here ten thousand years, and you have been here four minutes, and we both know how this ends.*

I'd heard it twice by midmorning on our third day above the snow line, and I was beginning to take it personally.

"Rope's good," Odile said behind me, giving my harness a yank that nearly folded me in half. She checked Quentin's next, gentler, then her own, then stood looking out across the ice field with the expression of a woman reading a letter she already knows is bad news.

The glacier spread below us like a river that had been told to hold still. It came down from the col in a broad white tongue, furrowed and ridged, and every one of those ridges was a lie. That's the thing about crevasse fields — the surface looks like nothing. Smooth snow, blue shadows, a few honest-looking undulations. Underneath, the ice has split itself into a honeycomb of slots some of which go down far enough to have weather of their own.

"You see the bridges?" Odile asked Quentin.

Quentin squinted. Her father had taught her to read terrain the way other children learned to read street signs, and I'd watched her do it now for two hundred miles — the little tilt of the head, the way her eyes went soft and unfocused before snapping sharp on something nobody else had seen yet.

"There." She pointed left of our intended line, where the snow lay in long shallow swales. "The sagging parts. Where it dips between the humps."

"And?"

"And you don't step on them."

Odile almost smiled. Almost. With Odile, an almost-smile was worth more than most people's applause. "We rope at twelve feet," she said. "Cyrus leads because he's heaviest and stupidest, so if the ice wants a sacrifice, it gets him first."

"I'm touched," I said.

"It's not a compliment. It's arithmetic."

"That's what I said. Touched."

* * *

We crossed for two hours, and for two hours the glacier behaved itself, which should have been my first warning. In my experience the sea is at its most dangerous when it goes calm, and ice, being water that learned to hold a grudge, is no different.

I'll give the crossing this much: it was beautiful. I've spent my life underwater, where beauty comes filtered through green murk and your own exhaled bubbles, and there's something about light on ice that scratches the same itch. The crevasses we stepped over — carefully, testing each bridge with an axe first — glowed from inside like lanterns. Blue light, deep as the sea, rising out of slots you could have dropped a church into. Quentin kept trying to look down them and Odile kept hauling her back by the pack straps like a cat with a kitten.

"How deep do they go?" Quentin asked, craning.

"Farther than anyone's bothered to measure," Odile said. "The ice flows. Slowly. A hundred years from now this whole field will have moved downhill and none of these cracks will be where they are today."

"A hundred years," Quentin repeated, and I saw her do the math she always did — the same math I did, the same math that had gotten us onto this frozen roof in the first place. 1897. The expedition. Whatever walked this glacier carrying the Provision had crossed ice exactly like this, and the ice had been moving ever since, folding their traces down into its blue throat.

Which is why, when I saw the dark shape in the wall of a crevasse forty minutes later, I didn't think *danger*. I thought *there you are.*

* * *

It happened the way these things happen, which is to say quickly and stupidly and with no regard whatsoever for how careful you've been.

The bridge looked solid. I tested it — axe strike, weight shift, the whole litany — and it held for my first three steps. On the fourth, the snow beneath me made a sound like a sigh, and the world opened.

I want to be precise about this, because people who fall into crevasses tend to describe it as falling, and it isn't, quite. It's more like the ground politely removing itself from under you. One moment I was walking; the next I was sliding down a chute of blue ice on my back, rope hissing off my harness, axe skittering away somewhere above me, and the light going from white to grey to a deep submarine blue in the space of perhaps two seconds.

Then the rope snapped taut and stopped me, and I hung.

Above me, a circle of daylight maybe eight feet across, edged with overhanging snow. Odile's voice came down, clipped and calm in the way that meant she was anything but.

"Cyrus. Talk to me."

"Present," I called up. "Suspended. Considering a career change."

"Anything broken?"

I flexed everything. Toes, knees, ribs, skull. Everything reported for duty, bruised but loyal. "Nothing that won't complain later."

"Hold still. Quentin, anchor. We're going to haul you up."

And here is where I should say I held still. But I've already confessed in this account to greed and guilt in equal measure, and I see no reason to start lying now.

Because when I turned my head against the rope and looked at the crevasse wall beside me, I saw the shape again. Dark against the blue. Wedged in the ice at eye level, maybe six feet away, half-encased like a fly in amber.

A satchel. Leather, gone black with a century of pressure, straps still buckled. And just below it, catching the light that fell down the slot —

Bone.

* * *

"Cyrus." Odile's voice again, sharper. "You've gone quiet. Quiet is worse than complaining."

"There's someone down here," I said.

Silence from above. Then Quentin's voice, smaller: "Someone?"

"Bones. In the ice. And gear." My breath fogged and drifted down the slot, and I watched it go, past the skeleton, down into the dark where the blue became black. "1897, if I had to guess."

The rope creaked. Above, I heard Odile muttering — arithmetic again, load calculations, angles. Then: "Can you reach it without moving off your line?"

That was Odile all over. Any other person would have said *forget it, we're getting you out.* Odile looked at a dead man in the ice and thought *evidence.*

"I can try."

"Try gently."

I'll describe what I could see, because I looked at it for a long time while I worked, and because some part of me understood even then that I was the first living soul to see it since it fell.

The man — and he was a man, the boots and belt said so, though the ice had taken everything else — lay on his side in the wall of the crevasse, tilted maybe thirty degrees, as if the glacier had caught him mid-fall and simply declined to finish the job. His coat had rotted to lace. His satchel sat above his shoulder, wedged in a constriction in the ice, and it was the satchel that had kept him from going all the way down. He'd fallen, the bag had jammed, and the glacier had closed around him like a hand.

He'd been carrying something heavy. You could tell from the way the strap had cut, even through rot, even through bone.

I swung myself sideways, hand over hand along the rope, boots braced against the ice. Six inches at a time. The cold came up through my gloves and settled into my knuckles like small animals. Below me the crevasse went down and down, and I made the mistake of looking, once, and saw the light fail into a darkness that had never once in a hundred years been interrupted.

"Don't look down," I advised myself, and looked down again immediately, because apparently I'm that kind of man.

The satchel was close enough to touch. I stretched until my shoulder screamed, until my fingertips found leather stiff as slate, and I got two fingers under the flap and pulled.

Nothing. Frozen in. Of course it was frozen in. A century of meltwater had welded it to the ice like a ship's barnacles.

I took out my knife and chipped.

* * *

Here's what I remember most clearly about the next ten minutes, and I record it because it embarrassed me then and embarrasses me now: I forgot about the rope. I forgot about Odile and Quentin standing on the surface with the wind picking up. I forgot about the snows coming early, about Halloway's climbers somewhere behind us, about the navy sweeping the valleys. I forgot about everything except the work of my knife on the ice, chip by chip, freeing a dead man's bag.

There's a word for that state. Divers know it. When you're down in the dark with a task in front of you, the world narrows to the width of your hands, and everything else — debt, fear, loneliness, the whole noisy cargo of being alive — drops away. Some men find God in that state. I find competence. It's the closest thing to peace I've ever owned, and I found it, absurdly, hanging in a hole in a glacier next to a corpse.

The flap came free with a crack like a knuckle.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job heroically, I found papers — fused into a brick, ink long since bled to ghosts — and a tin box, and beneath those, taking up most of the room, a shape I knew.

I knew it the way you know your own front door.

Keyhole-shaped. Brass, or something dressed as brass, furred green with age, heavy as sin. The twin of the key I'd pulled from a dead man's satchel in a sea cave four hundred miles north — except this wasn't a key. This was the lock's other half. Where mine had teeth, this had a socket. Where mine was a question, this was shaped like an answer.

Two pieces. One mechanism. Quentin had said it in the waystation, tracing the crest with her finger: *they're halves.* I'd believed her the way you believe weather reports — intellectually, distantly. Holding the second half in my glove, feeling its weight argue with gravity, belief arrived in full.

"Odile," I called up. My voice came out strange. "Haul me up. And then I need to show you something."

* * *

They hauled me out the way Odile does everything — efficiently, with commentary. Quentin's face over the lip of the crevasse was white as the snowfield, and when my hands cleared the edge she grabbed my wrist with both of hers and held on like I might slide back down out of sheer habit.

"You fell," she said.

"I did."

"The ground opened up and you fell into it."

"That is the traditional sequence, yes."

She hit me on the arm, hard enough to mean it, and then didn't let go of my wrist, and I let her not let go. Odile coiled rope and watched the sky, where clouds were stacking themselves over the col with the unhurried confidence of a navy fleet.

"What did you find down there," Odile said. Not a question. She'd seen my face.

I unwrapped the oilcloth right there on the ice, kneeling, wind pulling at the cloth's edges. The artifact sat in my palm, green-furred, socket-mouthed, patient. Beside it, the tin box rattled faintly when I moved.

Quentin made a sound low in her throat. She knelt too, close enough that her shoulder touched mine, and her eyes went from the artifact to me and back with that cartographer's precision.

"That's the second half," she whispered. "That's — Cyrus, that means he carried it here. The man in the ice. He was carrying the Provision's other key across this glacier, and the glacier took him."

"Or he hid it," Odile said. She crouched, forearms on knees, studying the thing without touching it. "Or he was taking it somewhere and died en route. There's a difference between losing a thing and planting it."

"Open the tin," Quentin said.

I did. Inside, dry as the day it was folded, lay a single sheet of paper and a pencil stub worn to two inches. The paper bore a crest I now knew better than my own signature — the 1897 expedition's mark, the mountain over the open hand — and beneath it, in a hand that had been steady right up until it very suddenly wasn't, five words:

*THE REST IS AT THE SUMMIT.*

Below that, in different ink, added later, shakier:

*If you are reading this, the ice gave you what it took from me. Finish it. Don't carry it alone.*

The wind came down off the col and flattened the grass — there was no grass, only snow, but it tried anyway — and for a moment none of us spoke. Quentin's gloved hand found the edge of the paper and held it flat, and I watched her read it a second time, lips moving, and I understood that she wasn't reading it as a clue. She was reading it as a letter. From a man who had died alone in the dark with a message in his fist, addressed to whoever the ice decided deserved it.

"We should mark him," she said quietly. "The man in the ice. So he's not just — a shape in a wall."

"He's been a shape in a wall for a hundred and twenty years," Odile said. "The glacier will keep him better than any cairn."

"He kept his promise, though." Quentin looked up at me. "He said don't carry it alone. And now there's three of us."

Odile snorted, but it lacked conviction. She was looking south, toward the cloud bank building over the summits, and her jaw was doing the thing it did when she was recalculating. "Weather's turning early," she said. "That's a storm stacking up, and it's a day ahead of what it should be. We move now, fast, and we pray the Red Stair approach is clear when we get there."

"And if it isn't?" I asked.

"Then we learn to pray faster."

I pocketed the artifact. It rode against my chest, cold through the layers, heavier than brass had any right to be — or maybe not heavier, just more awake. Somewhere under us, in the blue dark, a dead man kept his watch, and the glacier flowed on toward the sea at the speed of a fingernail, carrying him home by inches.

We roped up. We moved.

Behind us, once, very softly, the glacier cleared its throat — and this time, I could have sworn it sounded satisfied.
