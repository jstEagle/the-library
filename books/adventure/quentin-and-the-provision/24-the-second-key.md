## 24. The Second Key

Here is what nobody tells you about watching someone fall into a glacier: the sound is worse than the sight.

The ice didn't roar or crash the way it does in the stories Papa used to read me by lamplight. It made a small, polite noise — a *whump*, like a door closing in another room — and then Cyrus was gone, and the rope between us was humming like a plucked string, and Odile was shouting words I'd only ever heard from sailors, and I was flat on my stomach with my boots braced against a ridge of blue ice, holding on to a rope that was trying very hard to become a memory.

"QUENTIN." Odile's voice had that flat calm she got when things were worst. "Belay it around your axe. Now."

I did it. My hands did it without asking me first, which is probably the only reason they didn't shake. Papa taught me knots before he taught me cursive — "letters are for people who stay put, Q," he said, laughing, and now here I was at thirteen thousand feet tying a Munter hitch with frozen fingers because letters hadn't kept anybody put after all.

Then we hauled. It took forever and no time at all, which is how glaciers measure everything, I think. And when Cyrus came up over the lip of the crevasse, blue-lipped and wide-eyed, with snow crusted in his eyebrows like frost on a windowpane, he wasn't empty-handed.

He was holding something.

* * *

We got him to solid ground — well, as solid as anything gets up there, which is to say we got him to ice that was pretending harder than most — and Odile shoved a thermos of sweet tea at him and made him drink it in sips while she checked his rope, his harness, his extremities, muttering her checklist like a rosary. Fingers. Toes. Nose. Ears. Pulse. Pride, she added, dry as chalk, when he tried to wave her off.

"I'm fine," he said.

"You fell into a hole in the world," Odile said. "Nobody's fine. Drink."

I should have been looking at him. I want to be honest about that, since honesty turned out to be the whole currency of our little expedition — I should have been checking his fingers for white patches and counting his breaths the way Odile had drilled into us both. Instead I was staring at the thing in his fist.

It was about the length of my forearm, wedge-shaped, and it had been sitting in the ice long enough that the glacier had decided it was part of the scenery. The metal was dark, not brass — iron, maybe, or something older and stranger — and it was furred with frost except where Cyrus's hand had melted a clear patch. And set into its face, worn nearly smooth but unmistakable if you'd spent two months staring at one, was a crest.

A compass rose with a key laid across it, north to south.

The crest from the cave. The crest from the waystation journal. The crest Papa had inked into the margin of his last letter, so lightly I'd thought it was a doodle until I knew better.

"Cyrus," I said.

"I know," he said.

"There were bones down there." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. He'd told us, climbing out, before the shaking started — bones, brown and settled deep, a man in rotted canvas, and this thing wedged in the ice beside him like he'd been holding it when the crevasse took him. Or hiding it. With the 1897 men you never could tell which.

"There were bones," Cyrus agreed. He said it gently, the way he'd learned to say things to me, testing each word for sharp edges before letting it out. "Long past being anybody's tragedy, Q. Just a story the ice kept for us."

"That's exactly what a tragedy is," I said, "once enough time goes by."

Odile snorted. From Odile that was applause.

* * *

We camped early that day, in the lee of a serac the size of a church, because Odile said shock is a mountain too and you climb down from it the same way — slowly, with rest stops. She built the stove behind a wind wall and wouldn't let either of us near it, which meant Cyrus and I sat side by side against our packs with the artifact between us like a guest neither of us wanted to be rude to.

Up close, thawed and wiped down, it was even stranger. Not a key exactly. A key is a simple thing — a shaft, some teeth, a bow to hold. This had the shaft, all right, square-cut and tapering, but instead of teeth it had a row of slots cut into it, precise as piano keys, and the bow wasn't a loop but a flat plate with the crest stamped deep in the center. And along one edge ran a line of punched marks. Tiny. Deliberate.

"Papa's shorthand," I said, before I'd fully decided to say it.

Cyrus looked at me. "You're sure?"

I was already pulling out my notebook — the one I'd copied all of Papa's symbols into, back in the harbor town, back when this was still an adventure with a shape I understood. I held the page next to the punched line. The marks weren't identical; the ones on the artifact were cruder, hurried, like someone working with a chisel and cold hands and not much light. But the grammar was the same. Three strokes rising. A circle split by a bar. The little hook Papa used for *water* and also, sometimes, for *time*, because he said any cartographer worth her salt knew they were the same thing.

"It says—" I traced them with a fingertip, translating the way Papa had taught me, one symbol at a time, out loud, because saying it aloud was how you caught your own mistakes. "*Carried.* Then the split-circle, that's *divided*. Then... *joined*. Carried, divided, joined." I looked up. "And then numbers. Forty-one. Forty-one what?"

"Degrees?" Cyrus offered.

"Minutes? Days?" I shook my head. "Or nothing. Maybe forty-one was just somebody's lucky number."

"Nobody punches their lucky number into iron and drops it in a crevasse," Odile said, arriving with three tin mugs of tea and handing them out like communion. She crouched, looked at the artifact for a long moment without touching it — Odile touched mountains, not mysteries — and then said, quietly, "May I?"

Cyrus passed it to her. She turned it over twice, weighing it, and stopped.

"This isn't old work," she said. "Look at the edges. Machine-cut. Clean." Her jaw worked. "This was made in a proper shop. A foundry. In 1897?"

"The expedition had an engineer," I said. "Maren Voss. She's in the waystation journal — she built the waystation doors, she re-shod the mules' shoes twice, she complained about everyone's handwriting." I'd read those entries so many times I could hear Voss's voice, dry and tired and stubbornly hopeful. "If anyone could machine-cut this on an expedition, it was her."

"So Voss made a second key," Cyrus said slowly. "After the first one. After the cave."

"Not a second key," I said.

And then it happened — the thing Papa called the click, the moment when a map stops being lines and becomes a place. It happened in my chest before it finished happening in my head, and I grabbed the artifact and held it up beside Cyrus's brass key, which he wore on a cord around his neck like the world's heaviest necklace, and I laid them edge to edge.

The brass key's teeth. The iron shaft's slots.

They matched. Not approximately. *Exactly.* Every notch of the brass slotted into every cut of the iron, tooth into groove, like two halves of a sentence finally spoken in order.

"They're not two keys," I said, and my voice cracked right down the middle, and I didn't care. "They're one key in two pieces. You can't open anything with either one alone — that's the point, that's why there were two, that's why they sent them different directions — you have to bring them *together*. Carried, divided, joined. That's what the punching says. That's what the whole thing says. They split the mechanism so no one person could ever—"

I stopped. Because I'd heard myself.

No one person could ever open the vault alone. Which meant the 1897 people — the ones who walked out of the mountains and the ones who didn't — had trusted whoever came after to do it together. Two keys, two carriers, one door. It wasn't a lock. It was a lesson.

Cyrus was staring at the joined pieces with an expression I couldn't read, and then I realized I could read it perfectly, because it was the expression Papa used to get at the drafting table when a survey line closed true after weeks of error — relief and grief holding hands.

"One mechanism," he said softly. "One door. Two people minimum." He laughed, short and unsteady. "The navy's been hunting this thing for decades with helicopters and supply caches and God knows what else, and the whole time the answer was: you can't do it alone. It's built rude. It's built to require company."

"Voss would have enjoyed that," I said.

"Voss would have been insufferable about it," Cyrus agreed.

* * *

Odile let us have our minute. Then she stood, brushed off her knees, and pointed south with her chin, and I followed the line of her finger to the horizon and felt the minute end.

The snow line had moved.

That morning it had sat where it always sat, a clean gray-white boundary up the far peaks, patient as a fence line. Now it was coming down the slopes like water running backward — not fast, nothing so dramatic, just steady, a slow tide of white swallowing the scree fields and the brown grass and the last stubborn patches of lichen, and above it the clouds had organized themselves into something with intent. Papa would have called it a loaded sky. Odile called it worse words under her breath in three languages.

"How long?" Cyrus asked.

"Two days," Odile said. "Maybe three if the wind argues with itself. After that, this face of the range closes for the season." She looked at the two keys lying together on my notebook page, and then at Cyrus, and something passed between them that I was old enough to see and young enough to wish I hadn't. "Which means the vault is two days away or it's next summer. And next summer, I think, there will be more people looking."

"Halloway," Cyrus said.

"And the navy," Odile said. "And whoever Halloway's climbers report to, which may or may not be the same thing anymore." She picked up her mug and drained it. "So. We move tonight. Cold camp, moonlight travel, sleep in shifts. Quentin navigates, I break trail, Cyrus carries the—" she gestured at the keys, "—the argument."

"The argument," Cyrus repeated.

"You two are going to spend the whole walk debating whether to take the thing apart again," Odile said. "Don't. Carry it joined. If it wants to be together, let it be together." And she turned away to strike the camp, which was Odile's way of ending a conversation that had gotten too close to her heart.

But I saw her pause, just once, and look back at the keys. And I saw her mouth move, silent, shaping a word. I think it was *sorry*. I've gotten good at reading mouths this year. It's amazing what you learn to read when the people you love keep leaving things unsaid.

* * *

We broke camp in the blue hour, when the light can't decide what it is, and started south along the glacier's spine. Odile broke trail in front, Cyrus behind her, me last with the rope and the map and both keys wrapped in my scarf inside my coat, against my chest, where I could feel them knock together with every step. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* Like a clock. Like a countdown wearing a disguise.

Papa had carried half of this. That was the thought that wouldn't leave me alone, walking through the moon-blue dusk with the snow line creeping down behind us like a debt collector. Somewhere on this range, maybe on this very route, my father had walked with the iron piece in his pack — or maybe he'd only found the trail to it, the trailhead, the first waystation, the promise. Either way, he'd been carrying the Provision forward, the way the 1897 people intended, the way the testament said: not hoarded, not buried, *carried*. Onward. To somebody.

He never got to hand it to me himself. So I supposed I'd walked up a mountain and taken it out of his hands anyway, four years late, via a salvage diver and a disgraced guide and a glacier full of bones. Papa would have found that funny. Papa found almost everything funny, in the end, right up until the part that killed him, and I've decided — I've *decided*, the way you decide things at thirteen when deciding is the only tool you have — to inherit the funny along with the grief. They come bundled. Everything important comes bundled.

"Q." Cyrus had dropped back beside me, matching my pace without making a show of it. He'd gotten good at that. "Your hands. Let me see."

"They're fine."

"Let me see anyway."

I let him see. He turned them over in his own, checking fingertips, pressing each one gently, counting under his breath. His hands were wrecked — rope burn across both palms from the haul, a healing gash from the moraine, knuckles like weathered driftwood — and here he was checking mine. I didn't say anything about that. Some things you file away instead of spending.

"Forty-one," he said, while he worked. "You keep coming back to it."

"Because it doesn't fit anywhere. Degrees, minutes, days — none of it lands right for where we are." I frowned at the darkening valley. "Unless it's not a measurement. Unless it's a name. Or a count. Forty-one survivors? Forty-one crates? Forty-one letters?"

"Or forty-one people the vault is waiting for," Cyrus said, and then winced, because we'd both done the arithmetic on the 1897 roster back at the waystation, and the numbers didn't reach forty-one alive. "Scratch that."

"Or it's a warning," I said quietly. "Forty-one steps. Forty-one meters. Something you're not supposed to pass."

Cyrus was quiet for a few strides. The glacier ticked and groaned beneath us, settling its ancient accounts. Behind us, faint but constant, the snow line ate another slope.

"When we get there," he said at last, "whatever's behind that door — whatever the Provision actually turns out to be — I want you to know something now, while it's still hypothetical." He stopped walking, so I stopped. He looked down at me, and the moonlight made him look older and younger at once, the way it does. "Whatever's in there, it doesn't matter more than the three of us getting down this mountain. I need you to have heard me say that before we find out. So it can't be unsaid later."

I looked at him for a long time. The wind moved. Somewhere below, the snow line advanced another yard, indifferent as arithmetic.

"You're going to give it away," I said. "Whatever it is. The fortune, the discovery, all of it. You're already planning to."

"I'm planning to keep *you*," he said. "The rest is inventory."

And that was the click, I think — the real one, the one that mattered more than brass fitting iron. Papa's maps had taught me that a place only becomes real when two independent lines cross and agree. Cyrus's line and mine had just crossed, high on a glacier with a storm coming, and they agreed.

"Inventory," I said. "Fine. But I get to write the label."

He laughed — really laughed, the sound bouncing off the ice — and Odile turned around and told us both to save our breath for walking, and we walked.

Behind us, the first flakes came down. Big, slow, deliberate, like the mountain signing its name.

Ahead, somewhere past the col, past the storm, the Hollow Summit waited with its two keyholes and its one secret — and now, tucked against my heartbeat, both halves of the answer, knocking together like a clock that had finally, after a hundred and twenty-seven years, started ticking toward something instead of away from it.

Two days. One door. And a snow line rising behind us like the past itself, patient, certain, and impossible to outrun for long.
