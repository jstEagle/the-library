## 25. Odile's Signal

The signal cairn stood where Odile had promised it would be, at the mouth of the eastern moraine where the glacier's tongue met the scree, and for one whole second I let myself believe we'd made it.

Three stones stacked on a flat caprock. That was the arrangement. *I'm ahead, I'm well, take the high route and don't look back.* She'd drilled it into us at the last camp, tapping each stone with a forefinger like she was dealing cards. Three upright, all's well. Two upright, one fallen — trouble, but manageable. One upright, two fallen — come find me, and come armed.

There was one stone standing.

Quentin saw it before I did. She'd gone quiet in that way she had, the way that wasn't shyness but arithmetic — you could almost hear her father's maps turning over behind her eyes. She stopped ten paces out and said, "Cyrus."

"I see it."

"One stone."

"I can count, Quentin."

"It's the distress variant." She said it flat, the way she said everything that mattered, as if emotion were a tax she refused to pay twice. But her hand had found the strap of her pack and gripped it, knuckles whitening, and I knew the arithmetic had already run past the signal to its conclusion: *Odile is hurt, or Odile is caught, or Odile is dead.*

* * *

We'd been three days on the high route since she left us at the col with half our food, both ropes' worth of her gear, and instructions delivered in that clipped guide's cadence that made even tenderness sound like a safety briefing. *You stay high. You move at night when the sky's clear, in cloud when it isn't. You do not descend into the valley no matter how good the going looks, because good going down there is what Halloway's men will be watching.* Then she'd shouldered her pack, given Quentin a look I still couldn't parse — apology and promise braided together too tight to separate — and dropped east off the col like a stone rolling downhill, drawing Halloway's climbers after her the way a lure draws a pike.

Three days. We'd moved fast. Faster than I'd thought we could, and faster than I liked, because speed on a glacier is just a polite word for luck. The crevasse field had nearly taken me — I didn't tell Quentin about the bones I'd seen down there, the 1897 expedition's dead man frozen mid-reach toward something wedged in the ice, and I especially didn't tell her that I'd taken it. The second key rode against my chest now inside my shirt, cold as a coin from a riverbed, and every time I thought about the first key — the one Halloway's man had torn off me on the moraine — I felt the loss like a pulled tooth. Half a mechanism. We were carrying half an inheritance and hoping the other half hadn't been melted down for scrap.

And now this. One stone standing.

"We have a deadline," Quentin said. It wasn't an objection yet. It was her laying out the facts the way she laid out a map, smoothing the corners, waiting to see which fact I'd push back on. "Snow line's climbing. Your own words yesterday: three days, maybe four, before the passes shut. The Hollow Summit route needs every hour we have. If we go east after her—"

"Then we lose time we might not get back." I crouched by the cairn, not touching it. "I know the math, Quentin. I've been doing salvage math my whole life. Bottom time versus surface interval, air versus depth, what a thing costs versus what it pays. I know the math better than anybody breathing."

"So?"

"So the math says leave her." I looked up at the peaks, at the white already creeping down them like frost spreading across a windowpane. "And the math can go to hell."

Quentin stared at me. Twelve years old, orphaned by a mountain that had already taken her father, and she looked at me then the way creditors look at a debtor who's just said something inconveniently true. "That's not an argument," she said. "That's a feeling wearing an argument's coat."

"Everything I've said since I met you has been a feeling wearing an argument's coat. You're only now noticing because it's costing us something." I stood. My knee cracked — altitude, age, and the particular grievance of a body that had spent twenty years being squeezed through wrecks never built for human beings. "Odile Ferran turned back on your father once. She told us so herself, stormbound in that waystation, and it near broke this group in half. Do you understand what it means, that she went east alone anyway? That she took Halloway's whole hunting party on her back so we could walk clean? She's spent a year trying to pay a debt to a girl she thought would hate her forever. And the signal says she's in trouble. So we go."

"She'd tell us to keep moving." Quentin's voice cracked on it, just barely, a hairline fracture in the flat delivery. "She'd say the mission matters more. She'd say—"

"She'd say exactly that. And she'd be wrong, and she'd know she was wrong, because that's the difference between Odile and the rest of us. The rest of us lie to ourselves about what matters. Odile just lies badly and then does the right thing anyway." I held out my hand. Not to shake — to seal it, the way you seal a bargain when there's no paper and no witnesses except a mountain that forgets nothing. "We go east. We find her. Then we climb the Stair, hit the summit, open the vault, and beat the snows, and if anyone asks how we managed it, we'll tell them a twelve-year-old navigated us off a glacier against all sense and saved a disgraced guide's life, and nobody will believe us, and it won't matter."

Quentin looked at my hand. Then she looked at the single standing stone, and something went through her face — grief and fury and a fierce, unwilling hope, all of it arriving at once like weather off the sea. She took my hand and shook it once, hard, businesslike, a contract executed.

"If we miss the pass because of this," she said, "I'm writing it in the margin of the map. In ink. *Delayed by Cyrus Oyelaran's sentimentality.*"

"Noted. Add *and his terrible cooking* while you're at it. Full historical record."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and was gone. She was already pulling out her father's map, weighting the corners with stones, reading the eastern terrain the way other people read scripture. "If she set the distress variant," Quentin murmured, tracing lines with a grimy finger, "she'd want us to track her, which means she'd leave a trail we could follow. Her signals will continue. Look for cairns — but altered. She'll invert them. Stone on top instead of bottom, or offset. Something a navy man or one of Halloway's climbers would walk right past."

I looked at this child — this sharp, stubborn, map-blooded child — and felt the thing in my chest that I'd been refusing to name since the harbor town rearrange itself one more degree toward permanence. Somewhere between the wreck nobody names and this moraine, I'd stopped being a man salvaging a fortune and started being a man guarding a family, and the transition had happened so gradually I'd never caught it happening, the way you never catch the tide turning. You just look up one day and the water's going the other way.

"Lead on, cartographer," I said.

* * *

We tracked her east for the rest of that day and into the next, and the trail she left was Odile Ferran entire: economical, deliberate, legible only to someone who knew the grammar.

The first inverted cairn we found at dusk, tucked under an overhang where wind wouldn't eat it — a small stone balanced atop a larger one, absurd-looking, deliberate as a signature. Quentin spotted it from thirty yards. The second came at dawn on a ridge saddle, marked with a smear of charcoal on the capstone, pointing downslope toward a hanging valley. The third, midmorning, was different: the charcoal mark had been dragged, smeared sideways, hurried.

"She's moving," Quentin said, crouched over it. "Or being moved."

"Or running low on light and patience." But my gut had started doing the thing it did over wrecks, the slow cold roll that meant *something's wrong below and your body knows before your head admits it.* Twenty years of diving teaches you to trust that roll. It's kept me alive through collapsed decks and silted-out compartments and one memorable afternoon inside a torpedo tube. "Stay close. Watch the uphill side. If they're driving her, they'll post a lookout high."

They were driving her. We found the proof an hour later, in a stretch of boulder field below a rockfall scar: boot prints, many sets, military-precise, overlaid on a drag-mark where someone had stumbled and been hauled upright. And beside the trail, half-buried in gravel, a brass cartridge casing, glinting.

Quentin picked it up, turned it over, and set it down again without a word, which frightened me more than any exclamation would have. She was learning what the mountains actually contained. I hated the lesson and admired the student in equal measure.

By late afternoon we could hear them.

Voices, thin with distance, carrying off the rock the way sound carries across water. We bellied up a granite spine, cold soaking through every seam of clothing, and looked down into a shallow bowl of scree where four men in mismatched climbing gear — Halloway's hired wolves, not navy, no uniforms — had made a rough camp around a figure seated against a boulder with her wrists lashed to a piton-driven anchor.

Odile Ferran. Alive. Bloody at the temple, one sleeve dark and stiff, sitting with the particular rigid dignity of a woman who has decided that whatever happens next, it will happen to someone who chose her posture. Even at forty yards, even upside-down through my exhaustion, I knew that posture. I'd seen it in mirrors on bad mornings for twenty years.

Four guards. One fire, banked low. Rifles stacked against a rock — two of them, the others evidently carried. Terrain: steep walls on three sides, open slope falling away north, which is where they'd have their watch.

"Cyrus." Quentin's whisper was barely breath. "What do we do?"

What do we do. Twelve years old and asking me the question, trusting me with the question, and God help me, some rotten part of me thrilled at it — at being asked, at being the one whose answer mattered. The old Cyrus would have calculated odds and walked away; the old Cyrus had debts and no one to owe anything *to*. The new Cyrus lay on cold granite above four armed men, counting their rifles, thinking about a woman who'd broken herself against a mountain to buy a child time, and the answer arrived whole, unbidden, obvious:

"Wait for dark," I said. "Then we make noise on the north slope — big noise, rockfall noise — and when they send men to look, we take the other two."

"That's insane."

"Yes. Welcome to the family trade."

She exhaled through her nose — her laugh, when the situation forbade laughing — and began, without being told, to sketch the bowl in her father's shorthand, angles and distances, mapping our madness so it could at least be *organized* madness. I watched her work and thought: *her father would have done exactly this. Her father did do exactly this, somewhere on these same slopes, and it killed him.* And here was his daughter, same pencil, same precision, same refusal to be helpless.

The light died. The stars came out hard and cold, the way they do at altitude, close enough to insult you. Below, the fire burned down to coals. Two guards sat up; two rolled into blankets. Odile hadn't moved. I wondered if she could. I wondered, with a colder wonder, whether she'd heard us arrive, whether she knew, whether she was lying there doing her own arithmetic — *they shouldn't come, the smart play is the summit, please don't come* — and whether, like me, she'd decided the math could go to hell.

I squeezed Quentin's shoulder. She squeezed back, once, hard, businesslike.

"Now," I whispered, and reached for the first loose stone.

Below us, in the coal-dark of the dying fire, Odile Ferran lifted her head and looked directly up at our spine of granite — straight at us, through the dark, as if she'd smelled us coming the whole time — and very slowly, deliberately, shook her head *no.*

Then, behind her, at the edge of the firelight, a fifth man stood up from where he'd been lying hidden among the packs, and he was holding the first key in his open palm, letting it catch the firelight, letting us see it.

Letting us know he knew we were there.
