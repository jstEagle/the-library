## 26. Rescue on the Moraine

I have spent most of my adult life telling clients that the mountain does not care about them. I say it in a flat guide's voice, the way a doctor says *this may sting*, and I say it because it is true and because frightened people climb better when they stop expecting mercy.

What I have never said out loud is this: the mountain doesn't care about me either, and I have been counting on that for eleven years. Indifference is a kind of forgiveness. The mountain lost a client and did not strike me from its register; only men did that. Men with clipboards and grief-sharpened mothers and a town full of whispers. The mountain just kept being the mountain, patient as scripture, waiting for me to come back and do it properly this time.

So when I heard the whistle — Odile's signal, three short and one long, the distress variant we'd agreed on at the col — some traitorous part of me thought: *finally. Properly.*

The rest of me thought: *Halloway.*

* * *

We found her trail an hour after dawn, if you can call that bruised light dawn. The storm front had stalled to the west like a debt collector deciding whether to knock, and the sky over the moraine was the color of old pewter. Quentin read the ground the way other children read adventure serials — crouched, finger hovering, lips moving through her father's shorthand.

"She went east here," she said. "Then doubled back. See? She wants them following the first line."

"Them," Cyrus said. Not a question. He'd known since the signal. A man carrying two brass keys and a syndicate contract burned into ash knows exactly who whistles for him in the wilderness.

"Three sets of boots," Quentin said, tracing. "Maybe four. One drags on the left foot." She looked up at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't earned yet — she had not forgiven me, would not forgive me for turning back on her father, but she had decided I was still worth saving, the way you decide a rope is still worth trusting even after it's held a fall. "She's leading them in circles."

"Odile Ferran," I said, "has been leading men in circles since before she could spell *moraine*."

Cyrus checked the pistol he'd taken off Halloway's man at the col — one gun between the three of us, five rounds, and a plan that consisted mostly of the word *hurry*. "How far?"

Quentin pointed to a tongue of grey rock spilling down from the ridge, all shattered stone and meltwater channels. "The signal came from past the second lateral. Twenty minutes if we run. Thirty if we're smart."

"We've never once been smart," Cyrus said, already moving. "Run."

I want it recorded that I ran well. Eleven years of guilt makes excellent cardio.

* * *

We heard them before we saw them — voices flattened by wind, the clatter of scree under careless boots. I pulled us into the shadow of a boulder the size of a chapel and looked down the moraine's spine.

There they were. Three of Halloway's climbers, roped carelessly, spread across the rock like spilled ink. And ahead of them, hands bound in front of her with her own rope, walking with the unhurried insolence of a woman giving a guided tour of her own capture: Odile.

They'd worked her over some. Blood at her temple, dried brown. Her left sleeve torn where the rope had bitten. But her stride was hers, and every few steps she paused and gestured at the terrain as though explaining something, and I realized with a cold flood of admiration that she was still navigating them. Still burning their time. Even trussed, even bleeding, she was spending Halloway's hours like a woman who knew exactly what those hours cost.

The fourth man — the one dragging his left boot — hung back near the ridge, holding something wrapped in cloth. I didn't need to see it. Cloth-wrapped and precious and the whole reason any of us were on this godforsaken gravel: one of the keys. They'd taken it off her when they took her.

"They'll take her up the valley," Quentin whispered. "Toward the pass. They know we need the route."

"They know we need *her*," Cyrus said quietly. "That's why she's alive."

He said it without drama, the way he says everything, as though the world were a wreck site and he were simply reading the manifest. Then he started doing arithmetic with his eyes — distances, angles, the scatter of loose rock — and I recognized the look. It was the same look he'd worn in the harbor town when he lied to navy officers so badly that Quentin and I had to lie better on his behalf. A salvage diver's look. A man used to working in places where mistakes are measured in minutes of air.

"There," he said. "The meltwater channel runs behind them. If Quentin keeps low along it—"

"No," I said.

He blinked at me. In the weeks since the cave, I don't think I'd ever interrupted him. I'm the hired help; hired helps wait their turn.

"You're going to say you'll draw them while we flank," I said. "And then you're going to get shot, or worse, you're going to get *caught*, and they'll have both keys and the girl and the route, and everything you've bled for walks away in Vess Halloway's pocket. So no. Here's how it goes."

And I told them.

* * *

Here is what eleven years of disgrace teaches you: exactly where your shame lives, and exactly how much weight it will bear.

Mine lives in a snowfield below the Red Stair, in the shape of a man named Arnaud Quentin — yes, *that* Quentin, her father, though I didn't know his daughter existed until a sharp-eyed child recognized a crest on a stolen key. Four years ago I turned back at the weather line and told him the mountain was done for the season, and he smiled at me with terrible kindness and said he understood, and went up alone, and the mountain kept him.

I have replayed that turn-around ten thousand times. In nine thousand of them I keep climbing. In the other thousand I at least follow him far enough to bring something home — a note, a boot, a truth his daughter could hold. Instead I brought nothing, and the nothing has had four years to grow.

So when I stepped out onto open moraine with my hands raised and my voice pitched to carry, walking straight toward three armed men guarding the woman I'd failed twice now — once on the ice, once by silence — I felt, for the first time in eleven years, exactly where I was supposed to be.

"Gentlemen!" I called. "You're standing on a cornice terrace. I'd step left, but you've made such an effort getting there."

The nearest one — big, bearded, a climber gone soft around the middle — swung his rifle up. "Ferran. You're supposed to be tied to something."

"I am emotionally," I said. "Ask anyone in three provinces."

Behind him, Odile's head came up. Our eyes met across forty meters of broken grey stone, and I watched her understand — not the plan, she couldn't know the plan, but the *fact* of me, arriving, which was itself the plan's first movement. Something crossed her face that I will spend the rest of my life trying to deserve. It wasn't gratitude. Gratitude is cheap. It was recognition. *Oh,* her face said. *You actually came.*

"Where are the others?" Bearded asked.

"Dead in a crevasse," I said cheerfully. "Well. One dead, one dying, both very apologetic. I've come to negotiate."

"With what?"

"With information." I let my gaze slide meaningfully toward the wrapped bundle at the drag-footed man's hip. "About that key you're holding. Because here's the thing none of you have figured out — and I know, because I've seen the mechanism — it doesn't work alone."

I felt rather than saw Quentin move, low along the meltwater channel, a smear of grey coat against grey stone. Good girl. Her father's blood in her feet.

Bearded glanced at his companions. Doubt is contagious at altitude; thin air starves certainty first. "Halloway said—"

"Halloway," I said, "isn't here, and Halloway has never stood inside the vault antechamber, and I have." A lie so clean it squeaked. "Two keyholes. One door. You're carrying half a sentence."

That bought me thirty seconds of murmured conference, which was twenty-nine more than I needed.

Because here was the actual plan, the one I'd fed Cyrus and Quentin in the lee of the boulder: I was bait. Not the noble kind — the loud, annoying, attention-hogging kind. Every eye on the moraine was on me, the disgraced guide come to bargain, because everyone believes a guilty woman wants to buy her way clear. And while they believed it, Quentin — small, silent, raised on survey work, able to move across broken ground like water finding its level — would come up the channel behind the drag-footed man and lift the key off his belt with the same fingers that had picked her father's lockboxes since she was seven.

And Cyrus would do what Cyrus does, which is appear where physics says he shouldn't, because a man who dives wrecks for a living has made peace with dark enclosed spaces and the things waiting in them.

It went wrong in the way plans go wrong, which is to say: almost perfectly, and then all at once.

* * *

Quentin got the key. I will swear to that on any book you put in front of me. I saw the moment — the drag-footed man turned fully toward my performance, and her hand came up out of the channel like a heron striking, and the bundle was gone, and she was sliding back down into the shadow of it, and I nearly laughed aloud into the wind.

Then Bearded stopped listening to me.

Later I'd learn why: he'd spotted the rope. Quentin had looped the bundle's cord over her shoulder for speed, and the bright brass edge of the key had caught the pewter light for half a heartbeat. Half a heartbeat is plenty, on a mountainside, for a man whose paycheck depends on suspicion.

"STOP HER," he roared, and the moraine erupted.

Everything after that happened in the time it takes to fall. The drag-footed man spun and grabbed at empty air where his prize had been. Bearded shouldered his rifle — not at Quentin, at *me*, because I was closest and guiltiest-looking — and I threw myself sideways off the cornice terrace as the shot cracked and stone spat past my ear. Odile moved too, bless her furious heart, driving her bound hands up into the third man's jaw as he turned, and the two of them went down together in a tangle of rope and profanity.

And Cyrus came out of the meltwater channel like the answer to a question nobody had finished asking.

He didn't shoot. Of course he didn't shoot — five rounds, three targets, a child in the field. He hit the drag-footed man at a dead sprint, shoulder-first, the tackle of a man who'd brawled in harbor bars over unpaid salvage shares, and the two of them went skidding across the scree in a rattle of stone.

For one shining moment we were winning. Quentin had the key and was running for the channel. Odile was on top of her man, knee on chest, tearing at her wrist bindings against a rock edge. The drag-footed man was under Cyrus and staying there.

Then the drag-footed man found his knife.

I was fifteen meters away, scrambling up from my dive behind the boulder, close enough to see it all and far enough to change none of it. The blade came up short and ugly, and Cyrus twisted away — fast, faster than a wounded man should — but not fast enough, not clean enough. The knife opened him along the ribs, a red line sudden and shocking against his shirt, and Cyrus made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life: not a scream, just a hard exhaled grunt, the sound of a man revising his budget of pain downward.

He still didn't let go. That's the thing about Cyrus Oyelaran that I took too long to understand. He holds on. To wrecks, to keys, to orphaned cartographers' daughters, to people who abandoned him first. His hands closed on the knife wrist and stayed closed, and the two of them rolled once more, and this time Cyrus's elbow found the man's temple with the flat crack of a hatch slamming, and it was over.

The drag-footed man went limp. Cyrus knelt over him, breathing wet and ragged, one hand pressed to his side, and looked up at me across the scree with an expression of mild, bloody surprise, as though the knife had been a clerical error.

"Odile," he said. "Little help."

* * *

We won, is the summary. Summaries are lies, but we won.

Bearded and the third man broke when Odile got free and I came up the slope shouting about the navy patrol I claimed was ten minutes behind us — a bluff, but a structural one, built on the truth that Halloway's hires were mercenaries and mercenaries do not die for half a sentence. They fled west, toward the pass, leaving blood and rope and dignity behind them.

Quentin came back with the key clutched to her chest and Cyrus's arm over her shoulders before he could protest, guiding him to the shelter of the boulder with a fierce, terrified competence that broke something open in my chest. She tore his shirt open with steady hands. The cut ran along his lower ribs — long, ugly, shallow enough. A centimeter deeper and we'd have been having a very different conversation.

"It's fine," Cyrus said, in the tone of a man who has decided it is fine by executive order.

"It's stitched or it's infected," Odile said, already unpacking the kit from his pack with hands that shook only slightly, which for me, that day, was a personal triumph. "Hold still, you enormous liar."

She cleaned and closed the wound while Quentin held the flask to his lips and narrated the entire rescue back to him in a rapid, breathless voice, as though he might have missed it from inside it. And I sat back on my heels in the grey light and looked at the three of them — the diver bleeding and grumbling, the girl talking, the guide stitching — and felt the strangest sensation of my life.

The debt was paid. Not erased — debts like mine don't erase; they convert. Four years ago I turned my back on a man on a mountain, and today I walked onto open ground with rifles pointed at me so that another man wouldn't bleed alone, and somewhere in between those two facts, the ledger turned over. What remained wasn't guilt. It was something hotter and less forgiving and far more useful.

It was protectiveness. Fierce, specific, permanent. These two were mine now — not as clients, not as employers, not as penance. As people I would walk in front of, always, from here to whatever summit or grave the range offered first.

"Odile." Cyrus's voice, thin but level. "Say something. You've got the face you get before you say something."

"I was going to say," I said, threading the last stitch, "that we should move. Now. Before they regroup, before Halloway herself comes up that valley, and before—" I nodded west, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise and the wind had begun to carry that smell I know better than my own name. Cold iron. Old snow. "Before that arrives."

Quentin followed my nod. Her face went pale. "That's not a cloud."

"That," I said, tying off, "is a wall."

The first flakes came down while we were still packing — fat, slow, almost gentle, the way the mountain apologizes before it stops pretending. Behind us, far down the valley, something glinted: figures on the move, more than two, coming fast.

I got Cyrus's good arm over my shoulders, took the key from Quentin's white-knuckled grip and stowed it deep in my coat, and turned us all toward the tree line, where the map showed a hollow in the cliffs and the last shelter before the weather closed the world.

"Walk," I said. "Don't run. Running spends heat, and heat is about to be the only currency left."

The snow thickened around us like a curtain drawing shut on the last act — and somewhere behind it, I knew, Vess Halloway was still climbing.
