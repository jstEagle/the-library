## 27. The Storm Wall

**Cyrus**

The mountain told us it was coming, and we didn't listen. That's the honest version, and I've had a lifetime of practice with the dishonest ones, so you can trust me when I say I know the difference.

It was Odile who saw it first — the lenticular cloud capping the western peaks like a lens ground by a careless god — and Odile who said, in the flat voice she used for bad news, "We have perhaps four hours." And it was me who argued we could make the tree line before it broke, because I had a map in my head and a clock in my chest and the particular arrogance of a man who has survived the sea by refusing to believe it could kill him.

The sea, at least, telegraphs. It swells, it darkens, it gives you a half-day of rumor before it hits. The mountain gives you a cloud and then it gives you nothing at all.

We made the tree line with maybe an hour to spare, which sounds like a victory until you understand that the tree line at this latitude in late autumn is not a forest. It's a suggestion. Wind-bent spruce, twisted and stunted, growing sideways like they'd been trying to leave for a century and given up halfway. Quentin spotted the cave — of course it was Quentin, the girl had an eye for openings the way her father must have had an eye for lines on paper — a black seam in the limestone beneath an overhang, half-hidden behind a curtain of dead branches.

"Inside," Odile said, and for once there was no argument in any of us.

The cave was a hollow, really, a throat of stone that went back perhaps thirty feet and then pinched to nothing. Dry. That was the whole of its hospitality. Dry, and out of the wind, and — once we'd dragged the branches across the mouth and wedged them with stones — invisible from anything farther than ten feet away.

Then the storm arrived, and I learned what a wall actually sounds like.

I'd been in gales at sea. I'd been in the cave when the tide came for me, heard the water moving like something with intent. But this was different. This was the sky deciding, all at once, that nothing on this slope had a right to exist. The wind didn't howl so much as *press* — a sustained, physical weight against the stone, with the spruce outside cracking and groaning like a ship's rigging in its death throes. Snow came not as snow but as a horizontal white fury, and within twenty minutes the world outside our branch-curtain had ceased to be a world at all. Just white. Just noise. Just cold with ambitions.

"Well," I said, because someone had to say something, and I've never been able to bear a silence that might turn on me. "Cozy."

Nobody laughed. I hadn't really expected them to. But Quentin looked up from where she was digging in the rucksacks and gave me a look that said, as clearly as if she'd spoken it, *you're an idiot, and you're our idiot, and that's the only reason I'm not saying it out loud.* Twelve years old, and already fluent in a language most adults never learn. Her father had left her more than maps.

We took inventory, because inventory is a thing you do when the alternative is thinking about how long the storm might last. Odile laid it all out on her groundsheet with the precision of a surgeon: two stoves, fuel for perhaps five days of careful burning, food for six days of careful eating — and "careful" was doing heroic work in both sentences. A tent we wouldn't dare pitch in this wind. Rope. The keys, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, where they sat like two small cold hearts.

"Five days of fuel," Odile said. "If the storm runs longer, we burn the packs, the tent, and eventually our regrets."

"You'd burn the map?" Quentin asked, horrified.

"I'd burn the map," Odile said, "if it came down to the map or your toes. Maps grow back. Toes don't." A pause. "Most maps grow back."

Quentin clutched her father's folio to her chest with both arms, and I caught Odile's eye over the girl's head, and something passed between us — an apology, maybe, for a joke that wasn't quite a joke. Odile Ferran did not make jokes. She made statements with the safety off.

We set the watches — two hours on, four off, because two people couldn't keep the fire and the watch both, and three people meant someone was always sleeping — and I took the first shift, because I claimed it before Odile could assign it to herself, and because I wanted the quiet hours alone with the storm. Not for any noble reason. Because the storm was the first thing in weeks that was honest with me. It wanted to kill us. It said so, continuously, in the only language it had. There was a strange comfort in that. Halloway wanted to kill us too, but he smiled while he said otherwise. The navy wanted to erase us, and it didn't even bother with words. The mountain was the only enemy I had who never lied.

I fed the stove pinches of fuel. I listened to the spruce die by inches. I watched the branch-curtain and thought about the tide coming back into the cave beneath the cliffs, and how every trap I'd ever been in had the same architecture: a way in that was also the only way out, and a clock.

Somewhere in the third hour, Quentin crawled over and sat beside me, wrapped in both blankets, her chin on her knees.

"It's loud," she said.

"It's loud," I agreed.

"Pa would have loved it." She said it simply, the way she said most hard things, and it went through me like a fishhook. "He used to say weather was just the world thinking out loud. You could learn things if you listened." She tilted her head, listening. "It's thinking about the pass. I think it's saying we made the right call stopping."

"Or it's saying we're all going to die."

"Pa said you could tell the difference by whether it repeated itself." She frowned at the wall of white beyond the branches. "This one's repeating."

I put my arm around her shoulders, and she let me, which was still new enough that I noticed it, the way you notice a door left unlocked in a house you'd thought was sealed. "Then we live," I said.

"Then we live," she agreed, and went back to her blankets, and slept, which is the particular genius of twelve-year-olds. The rest of us have to earn it.

* * *

**Quentin**

The fever dreams started on the second night, but I didn't know they were fever dreams yet. That's the trick of them. They feel more real than the real.

I dreamed the cave was my father's study, and the storm outside was the sound of his pen, and if I could just get the branches open and walk into the white, I'd find him at his desk with his maps, and he'd look up and say *Quentin, you're late, we've a survey to finish,* and everything since — the harbor, the boat, the mountain, the keys, Cyrus — would turn out to be a story I'd made up to fill the time he was gone.

I woke up crying, quietly, the way I'd learned to do in the harbor town where crying loud got you robbed. The fire was down to a red eye. Odile was on watch, sitting with her back to the stone, and she heard me — of course she heard me, the woman heard everything, it was her curse and her trade.

"Bad dream," she said. Not a question.

"Bad dream," I agreed.

She didn't come over. That was the thing about Odile — she never came over, never touched, never did any of the soft things, but she did other things. She got up, fed the stove a careful pinch, and put the kettle on, and when the water was hot she made tea that was mostly hot water with ambitions of tea, and she handed it to me and sat down an arm's length away, which for Odile was practically an embrace.

"In the Karakoram," she said, "a client of mine dreamed his mother was calling him from the glacier. Walked out at two in the morning. We found him at dawn, frozen standing up, facing the way home." She looked at the fire. "Dreams up here aren't dreams. The mountain sends them. It's how it hunts things too smart for it to catch otherwise."

"Is that true?"

"It's true enough to keep you inside the cave." She glanced at me. "When you dream of him, and you will, and you want to walk into the white — you wake Cyrus instead. He's better at the waking part than I am."

That was the closest thing to kindness Odile Ferran had ever said in my hearing, and I turned it over in my head like one of Pa's brass instruments, looking for the mechanism. There it was, of course, where it always was: the client she'd lost. The man who'd walked into the white. She'd been telling me, in her sideways way, that she knew exactly what it was to lose someone to the mountain's voice, and that she would stand between me and that voice if it killed her, and that she'd never once say any of this in plain words because plain words were the one country she couldn't navigate.

"Okay," I said.

"Okay," she said, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

The storm kept thinking. I kept the folio under my blanket, and in the morning, in the gray firelight, I took it out and read Pa's letter again, the one that had started all of this, and I checked his shorthand against the map, and I did what Pa had taught me to do when the world got too loud: I worked. Survey the known. Fix your position. The storm could take the sky and the trail and the temperature, but it could not take the arithmetic.

Except it could take something else. I noticed it on the second day, when I went to write in my own notebook and my hand shook too hard to make a straight line. And when Cyrus touched my forehead, his face did a thing I didn't like at all.

* * *

**Odile**

Fever. On the second day, in a cave with five days of fuel and six days of food and a storm that showed no interest in ending, the child had a fever.

I have lost people to mountains before. I have carried them, in one case, eleven miles. I know the arithmetic of altitude and cold the way Cyrus knows tides. And the arithmetic said this: a fever in a warm lowland is an inconvenience. A fever at the tree line, in a storm, with limited fuel, is the beginning of a ledger that ends badly. The body burns fuel to fight infection. The fuel has to come from somewhere. Our stove could not warm a sick child and a healthy woman and a man who gave away half his rations when he thought no one was watching, and the snow outside was falling at a rate I had seen only twice before, and both times it had buried things that were never found.

So I did what I do. I made a plan, and then I made a second plan for when the first plan failed.

The first plan was simple: burn fuel warm, keep her wet with melted snow, aspirin if we had it — we had four tablets, which was nothing — and wait for the storm to break. The second plan required Cyrus to go out into it, and I did not write that plan down, and I did not say it aloud, because saying it aloud in front of the child would have been a cruelty, and saying it in front of Cyrus would have been an invitation, because that man had a talent for volunteering for suicide that I found frankly offensive.

He came to me on the second evening, while Quentin slept, and I knew before he opened his mouth. I have guided enough men to know the walk of a man who has already decided.

"The waystation," he said. "On her father's map. The one from the 1897 expedition, two days back along the ridge. It was stocked. It'll have fuel, food, medicine, maybe."

"Two days there. Two days back. In this." I gestured at the branch-curtain, behind which the storm was thinking its repeating thought.

"The storm's a wall, not a flood. Walls have edges. Her father marked the route. She can navigate it from in here — I take the map copy, I follow the ridge line, I dig for the waystation by the bearings."

"You'll die in a whiteout within the hour."

"Probably," he said, with that maddening half-smile, "but not within the first hour, and I've had a good run of first hours."

I should have refused. Every professional instinct I had said refuse, tie him to the wall if necessary. But I looked at the child, burning quietly under her blankets, and I looked at this man who had started as a stranger with a stolen key and had become — and I hated the word even as I reached for it — *family*, or the rough draft of one, and I understood that refusing him was not on the menu. The only question was how he went.

So I did the thing I could do. I spent an hour building the plan with him. Rope fixed between the cave and the first spruce, then spruce to spruce, a handline he could follow out and — this was the part that mattered, the part I made him repeat back to me twice — a handline he could follow *back*. We packed the lightest possible kit. We marked the bearings on his slate in Quentin's father's shorthand, which the child insisted on teaching him through chattering teeth, correcting his strokes with a teacher's patience, and if you had told me a month ago I would stand in a freezing cave watching a salvage diver learn a dead cartographer's notation from his orphaned daughter so he could go die politely in a blizzard for medicine — well.

At the cave mouth, with the branches pulled aside and the white fury leaning in, Cyrus stopped and turned around.

"Keep her warm," he said. "Keep the fire fed. If I'm not back in three days—"

"You'll be back in three days," I said, "because the alternative is explaining yourself to me, and I promise you the mountain is kinder."

He laughed — actually laughed, the idiot, into the teeth of it — and then he was gone, and the white took him, and I pulled the branches closed and sat down next to the child with her fever and her father's map, and I fed the fire, and I began the watch.

Outside, the storm went on thinking. Inside, we waited, and the fuel burned down, and the cold came in through the stone like a debt collector.

Three days, I had promised him. On the morning of the second day, with the fuel for one more day of warmth and the child's fever climbing, I looked at the last of the tea leaves and the last of the tablets and the branch-curtain, and I made a third plan, the one I hadn't written down even in my head.

Because if he didn't come back by tomorrow's dark —

— I was going out after him.
