## 28. Hollow Rations

The storm did not end. That was the first thing I had to accept about it. I had spent my whole life around weather that arrived and departed, weather with a shape and a schedule, and this was not that. This was a wall that had been built across the range by some patient, malicious mason, and we were on the wrong side of it.

Three days in the hollow beneath the tree line — a limestone overhang Odile had found in the last hour of light before the front hit, deep enough for three bodies and a stove, shallow enough that the wind found us anyway, curling in like a hand checking whether we were still there.

We had fuel for two days. We had food for one and a half, if you counted the pemmican honestly, which I had stopped doing because counting it honestly made me want to do arithmetic I couldn't afford. Odile stretched it the way she stretched everything — thin, silent, without complaint. She melted snow for water because melting snow costs fuel and drinking it cold costs body heat, and she chose the fuel every time, feeding our warmth into the pot like a woman paying rent she resented.

Quentin stopped complaining about being hungry on the second day, which frightened me more than any complaint would have. A hungry child complains. A child who goes quiet is spending her strength somewhere else.

And then, on the third night, I put my hand on her forehead to move the hair out of her eyes and my hand came away hot.

Not warm. Hot. The kind of heat that has nothing to do with blankets or proximity to the stove. I'd felt fever before — on boats, in myself, in men I'd fished out of cold water who were burning up from the inside while their skin went blue at the edges. I knew what it meant when a body started cooking itself.

"Odile."

She was already looking. She'd seen me touch the girl's forehead; she didn't need to be told. She crawled over, pressed the back of her own wrist to Quentin's brow — the professional's trick, wrist to forehead, skin to skin, more honest than a palm — and held it there a long moment.

"How long has she been shivering like that?" Odile asked.

"She wasn't shivering an hour ago."

"That's worse." Odile pulled back the blanket and looked at Quentin the way I'd seen her look at a route she didn't trust — reading it, weighing it, deciding how much of it would hold. "Her father's map," she said. "The waystation. How far?"

I dug out the map. It was soft as cloth now from handling, its folds gone furry at the creases, and I found the mark Quentin's father had left — a small square with a dot inside it, his shorthand for shelter, and beside it a distance estimate in his cramped surveyor's hand. Two hours' walk in good conditions. In these conditions—

"Four," Odile said, reading my face instead of the map. "Maybe five. Maybe never."

"Then I go tonight."

She looked at me for a long time. Outside, the wind made a sound I can only describe as a door trying to open in a house where all the doors are locked.

"You'll die," she said. Not cruelly. The way you'd say *the rope is frayed* or *the ice is rotten*. Information.

"Probably not."

"Probably." She almost smiled. "You've been navigating by probably since I met you."

"And it's gotten me this far."

"It's gotten you into a cave with a feverish child and half a day of food." She looked at Quentin, who was asleep or pretending to be, curled small under both blankets, her breath coming fast and shallow. "If you go and don't come back, I'm left here with her and no supplies and no story to tell her except that you tried."

"I'll come back."

"You said that about the syndicate contract too. You burned it."

"Burning it worked out."

"Did it?" But there was no edge in it. She was tired down to the bone, and her hands were still wrapped from the Red Stair, and she was asking the question she actually wanted answered: *can I trust you with the only thing left?*

I took the map, folded it into my inner pocket against my chest where my body heat could keep the paper from going brittle, and started pulling on the layers.

"Cyrus." Odile's voice stopped me with one boot half-laced. "The waystation. If the 1897 people stocked it, they stocked it for themselves. Take only what she needs. Leave the rest."

"I know."

"And if the storm turns—"

"It won't."

"You don't know that."

"No," I said. "But I know she doesn't have until morning to wait and find out."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about whiteout: it isn't darkness. Darkness at least has edges. Whiteout is a world with the edges sanded off, sky and ground merged into one seething pale nothing, so that your own feet become theoretical and the horizon becomes a rumor. I roped myself to the cave mouth with thirty meters of line — Odile's idea, delivered without sentiment: "So we can find your body" — and stepped out into it.

The wind hit me like a debt collector.

I've been cold before. Cold water at forty meters, cold that gets into the joints and starts making arguments. But cold water is honest; it takes your heat at a fixed rate and you know exactly what you're trading. This was different. This was cold with intent, cold that found the seams in my clothing and worked them like a pickpocket, patient and thorough, taking a little here and a little there and always coming back for more.

I navigated by the map in my head — Quentin's father's shorthand burned into memory during the hour I'd spent studying it: bearing east-northeast along the contour, past the split boulder he'd marked with a triangle, up through the stunted pines where the slope steepened, then the shoulder of the ridge and down the far side into the bowl where the waystation sat. Simple. Legible. Written by a man who expected someone might have to follow it in bad weather, which told me something about how Quentin's father had thought, and made my chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with the cold.

Two hundred meters out, I lost the ground entirely.

It happened between one step and the next. One moment my boots were crunching on wind-packed crust; the next, the crust was gone and I was floundering thigh-deep in powder that hadn't decided yet whether it was snow or air. I went down. The rope snapped taut behind me, and for a moment I just lay there, face full of snow, feeling the wind strip the heat off my back like a man peeling an orange.

Get up, Cyrus. Get up or lie here and become a landmark.

I got up. I checked my bearing against the little brass compass — the same compass, though Quentin didn't know it yet, whose twin hung on a cord around her neck — and corrected, and took another step, and another. This is diving, I thought. This is exactly diving. Zero visibility, a heading, a count of kicks, faith that the bottom is where the chart says it is. You don't look for the wreck. You swim the numbers and let the wreck come to you.

So I swam the numbers. Counted steps. Bore east-northeast. And somewhere in the white roar, the split boulder rose up in front of me like a whale surfacing — sudden, enormous, real — and I put my hand on it and laughed, a stupid bark of sound the wind ate instantly, and I could have kissed the frozen stone.

The triangle mark. On course.

* * *

The pines were the worst part, or I should say the best part, depending on how you like your lessons delivered. The trees broke the wind but they also held the snow, and every gust shook loose new avalanches of it down my collar, down my back, into my boots. The slope steepened. My thighs began to burn in a way that had nothing to do with youth, and my breath came in ragged pulls, and I noticed — distantly, clinically, the way you notice a leak in a hull — that I was no longer sweating. Which meant I was past sweating. Which meant the cold was winning the argument.

I gave myself rules. Divers' rules. *If you stop thinking clearly, stop moving.* *If you feel warm, you're dying.* *Count everything.*

I counted. Steps, breaths, the rhythm of my poles. And in the counting, because the mind is a treacherous crewmate that will fill any silence with mutiny, I started talking to Quentin in my head. Telling her things. Stupid things. That her father's shorthand had a symbol for *wind funnel* that I thought was elegant. That she navigated better than men twice her age and I'd never say it out loud because her head was big enough already. That when this was over — when the storm broke and the pass opened and we came down out of these mountains into whatever waited below — I intended to feed her until she complained about it. Hot bread. Butter. Honey if the town had honey. She'd earned honey.

That was the deal I struck, out there in the white. Not with God — I've never had much luck with God, and He's shown no particular interest in me. With the mountain. *Let me get there. Let me get back. I'll give up the fortune, the key, the whole cursed legend, I'll give it all away, just let me get back to that cave with medicine in my pocket.*

The mountain didn't answer. Mountains never do. But it let me keep walking, and I've learned to take silence for yes.

The ridge shoulder nearly ended me. The wind up there had cleared the snow off bare rock in places, and I crossed those patches on all fours, clipped to nothing, trusting friction and stubbornness, my fingers screaming every time they touched stone. Once a gust picked me up — actually lifted me, a grown man with a pack, like I weighed what Quentin weighs — and set me down three meters sideways, and I lay in the drift afterward and felt something crack inside me that wasn't bone. It was the last of the part of me that believed I was doing this for the Provision, for the fortune, for the legend. That part froze off right there, clean as a frostbitten fingertip, and I didn't miss it.

I was doing this for a twelve-year-old with a fever and her father's compass. Full stop. Everything else had been ballast, and I'd finally cut it loose.

* * *

I found the waystation the way you find a wreck in silt: by falling into it.

One moment the slope was dropping away beneath me blind; the next, my leading foot found emptiness and I pitched forward down a short drop into the lee of the structure — the 1897 expedition's stone hut, half-buried, its door hanging open on a hinge that had rusted generous. I lay in the snow inside the doorway for a moment, gasping, letting the sudden deadness of the air after the wind feel like a physical mercy.

Then I got up, because getting up is the whole job, and went inside.

The waystation was exactly as the journal had described it — preserved, stocked, eerie. The 1897 men had built it to last and then, apparently, simply left it, shelves lined with tinned goods, crates stacked against the walls, a stove with a chimney that drew. I'd stood in this room weeks ago, in another life, reading their journal and learning that the Provision was carried, not gold. Now I stood in it again with numb hands and one purpose.

Medicine. The journal had mentioned a medical crate. Aspirin, fever powders, iodine — the expedition's doctor had been thorough, because expedition doctors always are; they plan for the leg that breaks and the lung that fills.

I found the crate under a shelf, its lid nailed down, and I tore it open with my knife and my hands and not much dignity. Aspirin bottles, wax-sealed, decades old but sealed is sealed. Fever powders in paper envelopes, the ink faded but legible. A thermometer. Quinine. I loaded everything into my pack, then — because Odile's voice was in my head saying *take only what she needs*, and because I have never once in my life managed to follow good advice cleanly — I added tins of condensed milk, hardtack, a jar of rendered fat, sugar. Food is medicine too, when the alternative is starvation. I told myself that. It was even true.

At the last moment, standing in that cold room full of dead men's provisions, I saw the journal again on its shelf, and beside it an envelope I hadn't noticed before, wedged behind the crate. Paper, aged amber, addressed in that same cramped surveyor's hand I knew from the map.

Quentin's father's handwriting.

My hands stopped working for a moment. Then I took the letter, because I am not a man who leaves letters behind, and I stuffed it in my jacket unopened, because some things you don't read in a freezing hut alone at the edge of exhaustion. Some things you carry home first.

* * *

The way back was worse. It is always worse. The mountain knows you're leaving and takes it personally.

Somewhere on the ridge shoulder, in the worst of it, I fell again — a long slide this time, downslope, out of control, my pack saving me by catching on rock until I arrested myself against a pine trunk hard enough to see colors. I lay there in the tree shadow with the wind screaming above the canopy and took inventory. Fingers: present, agonizing, mine. Toes: same. Pack: intact. Letter: intact. Compass: gone — ripped from my neck by the fall, spinning away somewhere into the white, and I grieved it for exactly four seconds before I remembered that its twin was around the neck of a feverish girl in a cave, and that was the right place for it anyway.

I walked the rest by dead reckoning and pure spite.

When the rope found me — Odile hauling on the far end, having felt my tugs go wrong and started pulling, because of course she had, because she'd been sitting in that cave for five hours with her hands on that line like a woman holding a fishing rod over the abyss — when the rope found me, I didn't have the strength to be embarrassed. I just followed it in, hand over hand, into the hollow, into the yellow glow of the stove, into the smell of melted snow and wool and fear.

Odile took one look at me and said, "You look terrible."

"Felt worse," I said, which was a lie, and we both knew it, and it was the kindest lie either of us told that night.

I knelt by Quentin. Her cheeks were flushed dark, her lips cracked, her breath quick and hot against my ruined fingers. I got two aspirin tablets and meltwater into her, propped her up, wrapped her back down. Then I opened the condensed milk and fed her a spoonful, slow, watching her throat work.

Her eyes opened halfway. Found my face.

"You went out there," she whispered.

"Somebody had to eat the milk."

A ghost of a smile. Then her eyes closed again, and I sat back against the stone, shaking now that it was safe to shake, and felt the letter pressing against my chest like a second heartbeat.

In the morning, if the fever broke, I'd read it to her. Her father's last words, carried through a blizzard in a dead man's waystation, waiting in a pocket six inches from my skin.

Outside, the wind changed pitch. Lower. Slower. The sound of a wall coming down rather than holding up.

Odile heard it too. She lifted her head, listening, and her eyes met mine over the stove.

"The front's breaking," she said. "We move at first light."

I looked at Quentin's burning face, and at the letter over my heart, and at the map with its dotted squares leading upward toward a summit none of us had named aloud yet.

First light, then. Whatever it brought.
