## 29. The Medicine Run

There is a particular kind of silence that comes with a whiteout, and I want to be clear that it is not the peaceful kind. It is the silence of being erased. The storm doesn't howl so much as it swallows — every sound, every landmark, every certainty you've ever had about which way is up. You stand in it and the world simply declines to confirm that you exist.

I stepped out of the cave at what Odile swore was midmorning, and I could not have told you the difference between that and midnight. Snow came at us sideways, then upward, then in slow spirals that seemed to be considering their options. The temperature had fallen through the floor of what I'd believed possible and was still digging.

"Compass is useless this close to the magnetic rock," Odile said, pressing her goggles up onto her forehead so I could see her eyes. They were red-rimmed and steady. "So don't use it. Use the bearing. Two hundred and six, dead on, and count your steps. The map says eleven hundred paces to the saddle, then the waystation sits below the eastern lip. Quentin drew it for you?"

Quentin had drawn it. She'd copied her father's notation onto a scrap of waxed paper in her careful, cramped hand, and when she'd handed it to me her fever had been burning at a hundred and four and she'd still managed to make it look like a contract. *Don't lose it,* she'd said, or tried to say. It had come out as mostly consonants.

"She drew it," I said.

"Then follow it exactly. Not cleverly. Exactly." Odile took my arm, and for a woman who'd spent three weeks pretending she barely tolerated me, her grip was fierce. "Cyrus. Listen. If the storm turns, if you lose the bearing, if anything tells you this is wrong — you turn around. A dead man fetches no aspirin."

"That's the plan. Come back alive, deliver the drugs, be insufferable about it."

"Insufferable I can believe." She almost smiled. Almost. Then she pushed me out into the white and the wind took my name away before I'd finished turning around.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about walking through a whiteout: you cannot trust your own legs. The ground stops meaning anything. A slope of thirty degrees and a flat field look identical, because both are just white, and your inner ear — that smug little navigator you've relied on your whole life — starts making things up. Twice in the first hundred paces I stumbled because my body was certain I was walking uphill when I was walking down.

I counted steps. That was the discipline. Odile had made me practice it in the cave, pacing out a hundred, turning, pacing back, until I could hold a number in my head the way a drowning man holds air. Two hundred and six degrees. Eleven hundred paces. I said the bearing under my breath like a rosary, and every fifty steps I stopped, planted my ice axe, and checked the waxed paper against nothing at all, because there was nothing to check it against. Just the paper and my own stubbornness, agreeing with each other in the dark.

The wind found every gap in my clothing with the thoroughness of a customs inspector. By two hundred paces my left boot had developed a leak I hadn't known about, and by four hundred my left foot had stopped being a foot and become an idea of a foot, a rumor of sensation somewhere below my knee. I thought about the wreck, about the dives where the cold had come for me the same way, patient and professional, and how I'd always known the surface was a hundred feet up and all I had to do was kick.

There was no surface here. That was the difference. Out here the surface was a rumor too.

At six hundred paces the saddle was supposed to start. I couldn't see a saddle. I couldn't see my own boots. I stood with my axe planted and did the arithmetic of despair: if I'd drifted even ten degrees off the bearing, I was now walking parallel to the waystation, out into the high country, into the teeth of the range, and no one would ever find so much as my waxed paper.

But drift was a choice I hadn't made, and I'd learned something in the last month about not making choices out of fear. I put my head down and kept counting.

Seven hundred. Eight hundred. Somewhere in there the wind changed its mind about direction, which is the whiteout's cruelest trick, because your bearing is only as good as the air you're walking through. I adjusted by feel, by the way the snow now hit my right cheek instead of my left, and I prayed to whatever god supervises stubborn men that I'd guessed right.

At nine hundred and forty paces, my axe hit stone.

Not snow-packed-over-stone. Stone, bare and sudden, and I went down hard on my knees beside it and laughed, actually laughed, into the wind, because it was the saddle's eastern lip and I'd found it the way you find anything in a whiteout: by accident dressed up as skill. I followed the rock line south, one hand on it like a banister, counting again, and at sixty paces the rock dropped away and there — a shadow. A darker dark. A rectangle of not-storm where a door should be.

I fell through the doorway of the 1897 expedition's waystation and lay on the floor breathing like a landed fish.

* * *

The waystation was exactly as we'd left it three weeks and a lifetime ago: the bunks with their wool blankets still folded, the tinned goods on their shelves, the dry, dead air of a room that had been waiting a hundred years for someone to need it. My lantern made the shadows lean in. Frost had crept through the gaps in the doorframe and feathered the walls white, and in that light the room looked less like a shelter and more like a held breath.

I went for the supplies first, because sentiment is a luxury and Quentin was a hundred and four degrees. Aspirin — the expedition had carried it in glass bottles packed in sawdust, and I found three bottles intact in a crate marked with the crest I'd first seen in the sea cave, that crest Quentin had recognized on sight. I took all three. There was a thermometer, and I took that. There was a tin of condensed milk, and I took that too, and then I stood there in the lantern light being ashamed of myself, because I'd come for medicine and I was looting.

Then I saw the letter.

It lay in the station's writing desk, under a paperweight that had once been a chunk of the red rock from the Stair. It was not hidden. It was placed — squared to the desk's edge, addressed in a copperplate hand: *For whoever comes after.* I'd been in this room before. I'd searched it, Odile had searched it, and neither of us had found it, which meant either the storm had opened a gap the wind had been through, or the letter had decided it was done waiting.

I know how that sounds. A month ago I'd have laughed at it. But I'd taken a dead man's brass key out of a cave, and I'd learned that the dead leave things for the living on purpose, and that the least you can do is read them.

The letter was signed Elias Marchand, and dated the 14th of September, 1897. I knew the name by then. We all did — the expedition's cartographer, the one whose journals had named the Provision as something *carried*, not gold. This was his last writing, and his hand had deteriorated from copperplate to something scraped and desperate by the final paragraphs.

*We are nine now, of the thirty-one who walked out of the coast. The others are in the ice above the col, and I will not pretend I expect to join them anywhere but there. But the Provision has reached its place, and I set down here, in ink, what the world will insist on getting wrong, so that at least one man said it plainly.*

*The Provision is not a treasure. I want that written in a hand steadier than mine, so let it be this one. It is a testament — the record, the seeds, the medicines, the letters of thirty-one people who believed that what they carried was worth more than what they could keep. We were not a company. We were a town that decided to move itself, and the mountain has taken its tax, and what remains we have sealed at the Hollow Summit, under the door with two locks, so that it would wait for people who came for it and not for it alone.*

*The door wants two keys, made one. My colleague Aldous carried the first and I carry the second, and I will go up tomorrow and put mine in the ice where only a determined fool will find it, because a thing given freely should cost something. If you are reading this, you are the determined fool. Welcome. We were determined fools too, all thirty-one of us, and it was the finest year of my life.*

*Do not dig it up to sell it. Do not bury it to forget it. Carry it forward. That is the whole of the instruction, and it is harder than it sounds, and it is the only inheritance I have to give you, and I give it gladly.*

*E.M.*

I sat on the floor of that station for a long time with the letter in my hands, and the wind screamed against the door, and I did not move.

Here's the thing. I had come up the mountain for aspirin. I had come up the mountain, if I was honest, for the vault — for whatever the Provision turned out to be, because a man with my debts doesn't climb for the scenery. I had told myself the story where I was doing this for Quentin, and it was even mostly true, but underneath it there had always been the other story, the one with the numbers in it, the salvage-man's story, the one where the Provision was a thing with a value and I was a man with a claim.

Elias Marchand had taken that story out of my hands and burned it. *A town that decided to move itself.* Thirty-one people, and the mountain had taken its tax, and what was left they'd sealed up not as a fortune but as a letter — the longest letter ever written, addressed to whoever came after, which was me, standing in a waystation with stolen aspirin and a left foot I couldn't feel.

The legend was a man. That was what I understood, sitting there in the frost-feathered dark. The legend had been people all along, and every version of it that made men like Halloway climb and navies dig and salvage divers take keys from dead men's satchels — every version was wrong in the same way, wrong by exactly the width of a human heart.

I folded the letter into my coat, against my chest, where the waxed paper map had been. Then I packed the aspirin, the thermometer, the milk, and I looked around the station one more time and said, out loud, to no one, "Thank you." It echoed strangely, or the wind made it seem so, and I chose to believe the room heard me.

Then I went back out into the white.

* * *

I want to tell the return honestly, because the way out had been hard and the way back was worse, and a man who glosses over the worse half is a man writing fiction.

The storm had strengthened. The bearing was the same, but the air had thickened into something closer to water than weather, and within a hundred paces of the station I had lost all confidence that I was moving in any direction at all. I counted anyway. Two hundred and six degrees. Eleven hundred paces. My foot — the left one, the rumor — had progressed from numbness to a deep, grinding ache, which Odile would later tell me was the good sign, the sign of blood coming back, but at the time it felt like walking on a bruised bone wrapped in wool.

At three hundred paces I fell into a drift to my waist and it took me twenty minutes to get out, twenty minutes in which I did not count steps and could not afterward account for them, and that was the moment I understood I was in genuine trouble. Not dramatic trouble. Arithmetic trouble. My fuel was a chocolate bar and half a flask of brandy; my daylight was a lantern with maybe four hours in it; my margin, which had been thin on the way out, was now a knife's edge with me standing on it.

I did the thing that Odile had drilled into me without my noticing. I stopped. I planted the axe. I ate the chocolate, drank a mouthful of brandy, and refused — absolutely refused — to make a decision out of panic. Then I found the rock line. The saddle's lip ran east-west, and the cave lay west of the saddle, and if I kept the stone on my right hand and walked, the mountain itself would do my navigating for me.

It took hours. I have no honest count. The rock line vanished twice under drift and I crossed open ground on faith and bearing both times, and both times I found stone again, and the second time I actually kissed it, which I will deny to my grave. The cold stopped being a sensation and became a fact about the universe, like gravity. Somewhere in the middle of it I caught myself talking to Quentin — out loud, in the storm, telling her about the letter, telling her about Elias Marchand and his thirty-one fools, telling her that her father's map had led me true, that the shortcut through the karst and the Red Stair and all of it, it was real, her father had been right about everything — and I realized I wasn't talking to keep my spirits up.

I was talking because she was the reason I was still walking. Not the aspirin. Her.

There is a difference, and in a whiteout with four hours of lantern and one working foot, you find out exactly what it is.

The last hour is a smear. I remember the wind dropping, suddenly, the way it does, as if the storm had gotten bored of me. I remember stars — actual stars, torn open above me like a wound in the cloud, and me standing under them swaying, too stupid with cold to feel triumphant. I remember the cave's dark mouth and Odile's lantern and her hands on my collar, hauling me in, and her voice saying my name with a rawness I'd never heard from her, and I remember thinking, with the last coherent thought of the day, that Elias Marchand had it exactly right.

The finest year of his life. Determined fools, all of them.

I got the aspirin out of my coat with fingers that didn't work right, and pressed the bottles into Odile's hands, and said, "Milk too. She should eat something." And then, because I had earned the right to be insufferable, I pulled the letter from against my chest and held it up between two numb fingers like a winning card.

"And I know where the vault is," I said. "The Hollow Summit. It's real, Odile. It was never treasure."

She stared at me — at the frost in my beard, at the letter, at whatever was on my face that I couldn't feel. Behind her, on the bunk, Quentin stirred and murmured something in her fever, and Odile's eyes went to her and came back to me, and something in them settled, some long-standing account finally closed.

"Get warm first," she said. "Then tell me everything."

But as I sat down by the stove, peeling off a boot that had to be cut away from a foot I prayed was still attached, I looked at the letter in my lap — at *carry it forward*, in that failing hand — and I understood that the telling was going to have to wait.

Because through the storm's sudden silence, from far down the valley, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I knew from the coast, from the navy patrols, from every bad dream I'd had since the cave.

A whistle. Two notes, high and deliberate. A signal.

And it wasn't ours.
