## 30. Fever Night

I want to say I remember the whole night clearly, but that would be lying, and lying is Papa's trade, not mine. Cartographers tell the truth with lines and elevations. Everything else is guesswork.

What I remember is this: the cave breathing cold on my face, a fire so small it seemed embarrassed to be burning, and Cyrus Oyelaran sitting beside me with a wet cloth in his hand like he'd been doing it for years instead of three days. Odile on the other side, sharpening a knife she had no practical reason to sharpen. The storm outside sounding like the whole mountain was being shaken in a tin box.

And the fever. The fever was a country. I'd been living there.

"You're back," Cyrus said, when my eyes finally focused on something other than the inside of my own skull. "Welcome home."

"That's not home," I croaked. "Home is a harbor town with terrible fish."

"Fine. Welcome back to terrible fish country."

I laughed, and laughing hurt my throat, and I decided the pain was worth it. You have to ration your pleasures on a mountain. A laugh is a luxury item. So is water, which Odile pressed into my hands before I'd finished being amused, because she has the timing of a woman who has watched a lot of people die and decided to inconvenience death wherever possible.

"Drink all of it," she said. "Then drink the argument I'm about to have with Cyrus about whether you should drink more."

"He's against it," I guessed.

"He's for it. I'm against it. We've been at it for an hour. It's been very educational for you, since you slept through most of it."

I drank. The water tasted like snowmelt and iron and, faintly, of the tin cup, which had seen better centuries. Somewhere in the last four days — I'd lost count, which for a cartographer's daughter is a kind of blasphemy — Cyrus had gone out into a whiteout and come back with medicine and a letter that had rewritten everything we thought we were carrying up this mountain. He'd nearly died doing it. Odile told me this while I was too feverish to properly appreciate it, which she later said was a mercy, because if I'd been lucid I'd have cried, and crying costs salt, and salt was rationed along with everything else.

"How long was I—" I started.

"Four days," Cyrus said. "Five, if you count the part where you were talking to a marmot."

"I don't count that. The marmot and I had an understanding."

"He said you were very rude."

"The marmot started it."

* * *

Here is what a fever does, if you let it. It takes the things you've been carrying folded up small in your chest and unfolds them at full size. For four days I had been dreaming about my father. Not the way I usually do — the way where he's a shape at the edge of a map, always just leaving — but close up. His hands drawing contour lines. The way he hummed when he inked a ridge. His voice saying my name like it was a place he'd charted and was proud of.

And in the fever, the dreams had started including two other people. A big man with diver's shoulders and a laugh he tried to keep in a box. A sharp-eyed woman with ruined hands who walked like the mountain owed her money.

When I woke properly, I understood something I'd been refusing to understand for weeks: I hadn't been dreaming about my father. I'd been dreaming about the shape his leaving had left in me, and those two had moved into it.

That's the kind of thought you don't say out loud at four in the morning in a freezing cave. You hold it. You let it warm your hands.

But the fever had burned through, and dawn was coming up gray and clean against the cave mouth, and the storm had spent itself, and there was a silence in the cave that felt less like the absence of wind and more like the presence of a decision waiting to be made. So I said it.

"I dreamed about you two."

Odile's knife stopped. Cyrus's wet cloth stopped halfway to the fire.

"Not in a creepy way," I added. "Well. Slightly in a creepy way. But mostly not."

"Go on," Cyrus said carefully, the way you'd talk to a wild animal, or a twelve-year-old who has just said something true.

"When I was sick. I kept dreaming about Papa. And then near the end, you were both in the dreams with him. And I woke up and I realized—" My throat did something. I pushed through it, because a mapmaker pushes through the blank space; that's the whole job. "I realized I wasn't dreaming about what I lost. I was dreaming about what I've got."

The fire popped. Outside, a lump of snow let go of a pine somewhere below us and slid away with a long soft hiss, like the mountain exhaling.

Cyrus looked at Odile. Odile looked at Cyrus. It was the look of two people who had clearly been having this conversation in their heads for days and had each been waiting for the other to be brave enough to say it first, and now a feverish child had beaten them both to it, which was going to annoy them for years.

"Quentin," Cyrus started.

"Don't do the voice," I said.

"What voice?"

"The careful voice. The 'I'm about to say something kind but I'm going to build up to it like it's a diving bell' voice. Just say it."

He almost laughed. I watched him decide not to. "All right. Plainly, then." He set the cloth down. "When this is over — the summit, the vault, the navy, all of it — if we get down this mountain, I don't want it to be over. That's what I've been not saying since the waystation."

"There," Odile said. "He said it. Somebody write it down. Ink doesn't survive him well."

"Odile," Cyrus said.

"No, he's right, this is historic." But her voice had gone rough at the edges, and she didn't sharpen the joke the way she usually would. She turned her hands over in the firelight — the hands the Red Stair had taken payment from, still bandaged, still healing wrong, and she looked at them the way she'd been looking at them since the climb, like a ledger she was still reading. "I lost a client on this range. I lost the only person in the world that mattered to this girl. I have spent two years being a woman the mountain keeps anyway, out of spite. And somewhere on the moraine, when we pulled each other out of the fire, I stopped counting what I owed and started counting what I—" She stopped. Started again. Odile Ferran does not stumble on rock faces; words are her scree field. "What I have. I have you two. It's an absurd amount. I don't know what to do with it."

"Keep it," I said. "That's what you do with things. You keep them."

"Spoken like a girl who's never had a house burn down."

"I've had a house empty out. Same thing, slower."

* * *

The light came up while we talked, the gray going to white going, briefly, to a gold that touched the snowfield below the cave mouth and made the whole world look briefly like it was worth the trouble. The storm had passed. Behind it, the peaks stood out hard and blue and impossibly clear — the kind of air where you can see for fifty miles and every mile of it is honest.

Cyrus got the stove going properly and made the last of the oatmeal, which was less oatmeal than oatmeal-adjacent, and we ate it out of the one pot because we'd stopped pretending to have dishes. He talked while he stirred. That was new. Cyrus talks the way divers work — in controlled bursts, with long silences between, and you learn to read the silences.

"My mother left when I was nine," he said, to the pot. "Not died. Left. There's a difference and the difference is she chose it. My father lasted another four years and then the sea took him, which was at least honest. I've been on my own since I was thirteen, and I told myself that was a trade. Freedom for family. Good deal. Everyone I ever met who had a family seemed to be paying through the nose for it." He handed me the pot. "Turns out I was pricing it wrong."

"How so?" I asked, mouth full.

"Underpriced. Massively underpriced. I'd have paid anything." He looked at me across the fire, and his face did the thing it does when he's not performing being tough — the thing underneath, which is just a man, tired and fond and scared. "So. Plainly, since we're doing plainly. If there's an after — if we stand on the summit, and the navy doesn't shoot us, and the mountain lets us down — I'd like there to be an after-after. A harbor town with terrible fish. A shack with a better roof. A girl who needs someone to sign school forms."

"I don't go to school."

"I know. That was the pitch."

I looked down at the pot. The oatmeal had gone gluey and I stirred it anyway, because my hands needed a job. This was the thing, the actual thing, and it was being offered so plainly that it almost didn't seem real. People had been leaving my life in ones and twos since I was seven — Papa first, then the landlady, then the kindly woman at the mission who turned out to be less kindly than advertised, then everyone. And now a man I'd met by selling him a lie about a boat was asking to sign my school forms.

"You'd be bad at it," I said. "School forms. You'd write your name in the wrong box."

"Almost certainly."

"And you can't cook."

"Also true."

"And you snore. I heard it through the fever. The marmot complained."

"The marmot," Cyrus said, "is not part of this family."

The word landed in the cave like a coin dropped down a well. We all heard it. We all heard how long it took to hit bottom.

"Family," Odile repeated. She said it the way she'd test ice — weight on it slowly, ready to move. "That's the word, then."

"Unless someone's got a better one," Cyrus said.

"Company," Odile offered. "Crew. Party. A salvage syndicate, if we want to be funny about it."

"Crew's for work," I said. "This isn't work. This is—" I gestured with the pot at the cave, the fire, the two of them, the whole ridiculous arrangement of my life since a low tide had opened a door in the world. "This is the other thing."

"The other thing," Cyrus repeated.

"The thing people mean when they say the word and don't explain it. I've read the word in books. It never comes with a diagram." I set the pot down. My eyes were doing something embarrassing and I let them, because the fever had burned out my last supply of pretending. "Papa used to say a map is a promise you make to whoever comes after you. That the lines mean someone walked there and came back and drew it true. That's what I think family is. It's a map other people keep of you. So that when you're lost, somebody knows the ground you're standing on."

Silence. The good kind. The kind that has weight and doesn't need filling.

"Well," Odile said finally, wiping her eye with the back of a bandaged hand and pretending she hadn't. "That's the diagram. Someone draw it up."

"I will," I said. "When we get down. I'll draw all three of us. Proper survey. Elevations and everything. Cyrus, you'll come out looking like a lighthouse."

"I contain multitudes," he said, "most of them vertical."

And then — because we were doing plainly, and plainly is a country you can't visit halfway — Odile said what we were all not saying.

"Halloway is still on this mountain."

The gold light went out of the morning, or maybe it just moved. The peaks were still there, hard and blue, but the cold came back into the cave like a tenant who'd only stepped out for a smoke.

"Storm held him up same as us," Cyrus said. "Maybe longer. He was lower on the range when it hit."

"Lower means less exposed. Less exposed means he moves sooner." Odile fed a stick to the fire with her ruined fingers, carefully, like feeding a dangerous animal. "He's lost two keys' worth of pride and half his team to a rockslide. Men like Vess Halloway don't climb mountains for treasure after a loss like that. They climb them for the loss itself. For the settling of accounts."

"So we beat him to the summit," I said. "We're close. Papa's letter said the Hollow Summit, and by my map we're—"

"Half a day," Cyrus said. "Half a day in good conditions. The conditions are good. The snowpack above the col is not. And he doesn't have to reach the vault. He just has to reach us, on ground where he's got more guns than we do."

"Then we don't be on that ground," I said.

They both looked at me.

"I mean it," I said. "Papa's shortcut through the karst saved us a day. The decoy route saved us on the scree. Every time we've survived this mountain, it's been because we didn't fight the ground, we read it. So we read it. All three of us. That's the family business now — we're not salvagers, we're not guides, we're not syndicate. We're mapmakers. And Halloway's map of this mountain has a hole in it shaped like us."

Cyrus looked at Odile. Odile looked at Cyrus. And then Odile smiled, and it was the first full smile I'd ever seen her spend, and it was worth the rationing.

"God help us," she said. "She's the captain now."

"Absolutely not," said Cyrus.

"She's the captain."

"I'm the adult."

"Debatable on both counts," Odile said. "Captain, what are your orders?"

I stood up. My legs held, which felt like a personal miracle, and I walked to the cave mouth and looked out at the clear hard morning, at the Hollow Summit's shoulder showing white and terrible in the northeast, at the whole honest fifty miles of it.

"Orders," I said, "are we break camp in an hour, we take the high line along the ridge where the snow's wind-scoured and firm, and we get to that vault before a single man in a navy coat or a syndicate collar knows we've moved." I turned around. "And when it's over — the after-after — we go home to the terrible fish, and Cyrus signs my school forms in the wrong box, and Odile teaches me to guide, and nobody ever leaves."

The fire cracked. The mountain stood there, holding its breath.

Cyrus got up and put his hand on my head like a benediction, or like a man checking a fever, and maybe it was both. "Nobody ever leaves," he said.

"Nobody ever leaves," Odile said.

And that was when we heard it — faint, from the valley below, carried up on the clearing air with terrible clarity: the grind and whine of an engine that was not ours, moving up the trail we'd planned to take.

Odile was at the cave mouth before the sound finished, binoculars up. She was quiet for a long moment.

"Captain," she said. "Change of orders required. Halloway's not behind us."
