## 31. Halloway's Offer

The storm broke on the third morning the way a bad debt breaks — all at once, and with interest.

I woke in the tree-line cave to silence so complete it felt like an accusation. The wind that had screamed against our shelter for two days had simply stopped, and the light coming through the entrance had changed from the color of dishwater to something almost gold. I lay there a moment listening to Quentin breathe — slow and even, the fever finally gone from her — and thought: *this is either mercy or a trap.*

With mountains, it's usually both.

Odile was already up, standing at the cave mouth like a figurehead that had grown opinions. She didn't turn when I came up beside her. That was my first warning. Odile always turned; she'd spent thirty years reading slopes for a living and treated every human being who approached her as a slope worth reading.

"Look," she said.

I looked. Below us, the valley we'd have descended into today was a white sea, smooth as poured cream, beautiful in the way that things are beautiful right before they kill you. And across that whiteness, moving up toward us in a loose line of eight, were people.

Not navy. Navy moves in pairs and radios ahead. These moved like wolves who'd been promised dinner by someone they didn't trust.

"Halloway," I said.

"Since yesterday evening," Odile said. "They camped at the moraine. They waited out the storm same as us." She paused. "They're not hurrying."

That was the second warning. Halloway's men hurried everywhere; I'd watched them come up behind us on the trail like weather with grudges. Men don't stop hurrying unless they've already won.

"They know something," I said.

"They know we're here," Odile said. "There's smoke."

I turned. There was, faintly, a gray thread rising off our fire pit where Quentin had banked coals before dawn. In still air after a storm, smoke travels like gossip.

"Well," I said. "In my defense, I was going to mention it."

* * *

We had perhaps forty minutes, and we spent them badly, which is to say honestly. Odile wanted to run for the Red Stair's upper traverse — a route she'd scouted on the way up, exposed but fast if you didn't look down. Quentin, wrapped in both blankets and furious about it, pointed out that Cyrus Oyelaran could barely walk ten paces without his leg buckling, and that the wound in his side from the rescue on the moraine had gone hot and angry under its bandage.

"I can walk," I said.

"You can fall downhill with style," Quentin said. "It's different."

She was twelve years old and she'd been keeping me alive for a week through sheer contempt, and I loved her the way you love a lighthouse — fiercely, and mostly because you'd drown without it.

"We can't outrun eight," Odile said flatly. "Not him. Not in this snow. We can maybe hide, and they'll find us by tomorrow because of the smoke, and then we'll be found tired instead of found rested."

"So we don't hide," I said.

They both looked at me.

"We talk," I said. And then, because lying to these two had become harder than lying to anyone else alive, I told them the truth about what I intended, or most of it. "Halloway wants the keys and the route. He's got force enough to take them, but he's also got a mountain that's killed better men than his hires this week, and he knows it. A man like that will always trade when trading looks cheaper than bleeding. So I'll trade."

"With what?" Quentin said. "You're not giving them—"

"I'm going to give them exactly what they ask for," I said. "And then I'm going to cheat them so thoroughly they'll be finding pieces of the con until spring."

Odile studied me the way she studied cornices. "And if he doesn't trade? If he just takes?"

"Then I'll have lost nothing but dignity," I said, "and I've got very little of that left anyway. It's mostly scar tissue at this point."

Quentin grabbed my sleeve. Her hands were small and chapped and they gripped like someone twice her size. "Cyrus. What are you actually doing?"

"Trust me," I said.

"That's what Dad said," she said, "right before everything."

The words landed harder than any of Halloway's men could have hit. But she held on, and after a moment she said, "Fine. But I'm watching. I watch everything. Ask Odile."

"She does," Odile confirmed grimly. "It's unsettling."

* * *

Halloway came up the last stretch himself, which surprised me. I'd built him in my head as a man who sent other people up hills the way he sent other people into debt. But there he was, cresting the rise below our cave in a fur-collared coat that probably cost more than my boat, breathing hard and pretending he wasn't, six men fanned out behind him with rifles slung in the universal posture of *we could, but let's be civilized.*

Vess Halloway was smaller than I remembered from the syndicate office, and colder. Coldness reads bigger at altitude. He stopped a careful fifteen feet away — close enough to talk, far enough that even a wounded man couldn't reach him before the rifles spoke.

"Oyelaran," he said. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy," I said. "Actually, you should see your other guys. Two of them are still picking gravel out of their teeth from the rockslide."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Accounting. Anger is for people who haven't done the math yet.

"The girl," he said. "And Ferran. Inside?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters whether I need to worry about them." His breath plumed white between us. "Here's how this goes, Cyrus. You're going to give me both keys. You're going to give me the route — the pass, the summit, the vault location, all of it, drawn out clear enough that my guide can follow it drunk. And in exchange—" he tilted his head toward the cave, "—the girl walks away. Down the valley, into town, alive, rich, whatever sentimental arrangement you people have constructed back there. Ferran too, if she keeps her nose out."

"And you?" I asked. "What do I walk away with?"

He smiled. It was a banker's smile, the kind that arrives pre-notarized. "Your life. Which, given your debts, your wounds, and your current altitude, I'm told is worth more than you think."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out ragged and it hurt my ribs and it was absolutely genuine, and I saw it land on him wrong, the way genuine things always do around men like Vess Halloway.

"What's funny?"

"You are," I said. "You're offering to let a child live. Like it's a favor. Like her life is yours to sell back to me." I shook my head. "You've been chasing a dead expedition's leftovers for — what, years? Your whole career? And the whole time the thing you never learned is that some things aren't for sale because they were never inventory."

"Save the sermon," Halloway said. "I've read your file, Oyelaran. Diver. Debtor. Liar — you lie badly, by the way, the navy boys wrote that down themselves. You're not a philosopher. You're a man in a hole negotiating with the shovel." He spread his hands. "Both keys. The route. Or I take them, and I take the girl's chances with them. Snow's coming again tonight — real snow, the kind that closes passes till June. My men are fresh. Yours are a cripple, a child, and a blacklisted guide. Do the arithmetic."

I did do the arithmetic. That was the whole trick of it — I'd been doing it since dawn, since I'd seen his line crossing the valley and understood that he'd already won everything except the part he didn't know existed.

Because here's what Vess Halloway knew: that I carried two brass artifacts, halves of one mechanism, recovered from a crevasse and a dead man's satchel. Here's what he didn't know: that three days ago, in the hollow of a glacier, I'd sat with Quentin by lamplight and we'd copied the route onto three separate papers — one sewn into her coat lining, one in Odile's boot, one burned the same night along with the wax and the needle. Here's what he really didn't know: that the key I kept in my breast pocket was real, and the key in my pack's false bottom — the one his searchers would find first, the one I'd handled obsessively for days so it would smell of me and importance — was a forgery Quentin had cast from candle soot, pine resin, and her father's own mold-making notes, and it was good enough to fool a man who'd only ever seen mine through a spyglass.

And here was the crown of it, the piece I'd been carrying around like a hot coal waiting for exactly this morning: the forged key was flawed. Deliberately. Quentin had shaved the bit a hair too fine. Put it in the vault's mechanism and it would seat, would turn, would feel *right* — and then it would jam, and the spring-loaded ward inside the lock, per her father's notes on 1897 expedition hardware, would shear the teeth clean off and bind the mechanism for good.

One shot. One keyhole. One chance.

Halloway wouldn't just fail to open the vault. He'd weld it shut behind himself.

All I had to do was hand it over with a straight face, and keep him from searching me too closely, and get Quentin off this mountain before he ever learned the difference between brass and ambition.

"Arithmetic's done," I said. "I'll trade."

* * *

The handoff happened the way these things always happen in stories and never happen in life: slowly, awkwardly, everyone cold, no one comfortable.

Halloway made me empty my pockets first — compass, tin of matches, the folded route map I'd prepared for exactly this purpose, drawn in Quentin's father's shorthand so that it looked authentic and read, to anyone but the three of us, like elegant nonsense. His man patted me down thoroughly enough to find the real key in my breast pocket, and thoroughly enough to find the false bottom in my pack, and not thorough enough — because Odile, passing nearby with Quentin's bedroll, contrived to stumble against the searcher at precisely the moment his hands were on the pack's seam, and apologized so profusely and so profanely that the man forgot what he'd been feeling for.

Two keys into Halloway's gloved palm. He weighed them like a man weighing gold, which was the whole tragedy of Vess Halloway in a single gesture: he held the future of everything in his hand and checked it for carats.

"The girl comes down with us," he said. "To the treeline. Then she goes free."

"No," I said.

His eyes narrowed. "You're not in a position to add clauses."

"I'm adding nothing. I'm correcting your geography." I nodded down-valley. "Your men go east around the moraine. She goes west, with Odile, now, while there's light. You'll have the keys and the route in your hand and six guns at your back — what does a child matter to you anymore? Unless," I said, and let it hang, "she matters more than you're saying. Unless she's seen something. Heard something. Unless there's a reason you want her close that isn't in the contract."

I was guessing, and I hated myself for the shape of the guess — but I'd watched Halloway's eyes flick to Quentin once, early, when she'd stood in the cave mouth, and I'd seen recognition in it. Not of her. Of her *father*. The cartographer who'd vanished surveying the range, whose last letter named the trailhead. Halloway hadn't just been hunting the Provision. He'd been hunting the man who mapped it, and the man's daughter was a loose end wearing a coat.

For a long moment the wind filled the silence, and Halloway did his accounting behind those bank-teller eyes.

"West," he said finally. "Ferran takes her to the treeline and no further with you. Then they walk out. If I find either of them behind me on this mountain, the deal's ash." He pocketed the keys. "Pleasure doing business, Oyelaran. You always were the cheapest man in the syndicate."

"Cheapest," I agreed, "or the most expensive. Depends which side of the ledger you're standing on."

They went east. We went west. Quentin didn't speak until the moraine swallowed Halloway's line, and then she turned to me with her father's exact expression — the one I'd only ever seen in a faded photograph, the look of a man measuring a thing no one else could see yet.

"The false key," she said. "You gave him the false key."

"I gave him the false key."

"And the route map was gibberish."

"Gibberish with excellent penmanship."

"And when he puts that key in the door—" She stopped. She was smiling, and it scared me a little, how much she looked like her father in that moment, and how much she looked like someone I'd burn down a mountain to protect.

"When he puts that key in the door," I said, "it's going to break, and the lock is going to seal, and Vess Halloway is going to spend the rest of his natural life staring at a door he welded shut with his own two hands."

Odile made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "And the real key?"

I reached into my boot, where it had been since before dawn, strapped where a wounded man's limp would make any searcher's hands drift elsewhere. The brass was warm from my skin. Half a mechanism, half an inheritance, and — if Quentin's father's notes were right, and they had been right about everything else — the only key in the world that fit the Hollow Summit.

Behind us, far to the east, tiny against the white, Halloway's party was already climbing toward a door that would never open for them.

Above us, the peak waited, and the light was starting to go gold, and the snow held off — for now — like a creditor who hasn't yet checked the calendar.

"Come on," I said. "We've got a summit to beat a liar to."
