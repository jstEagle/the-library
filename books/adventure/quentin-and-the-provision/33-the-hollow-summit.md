## 33. The Hollow Summit

The storm broke at four in the morning, the way storms do when they've decided you've suffered enough — or when they're saving the rest of you for later. I woke in the cave with Quentin's fever-warm shoulder against my arm and Odile already standing at the mouth, watching the clouds peel back off the peak like a bandage coming off a wound.

"Clear," she said, which from Odile was a eulogy for the weather and a summons all at once.

I'd slept maybe two hours. My ribs still ached where Halloway's man had worked me over on the moraine, my left hand had gone stiff around the knuckles since the medicine run, and somewhere below us Vess Halloway was regrouping with whatever remained of her climbers after the rockslide. By every sensible measure we should have been dead three times over. Instead we were alive, underfed, half-frozen, and one day's climb from the Hollow Summit with two brass keys in my coat.

Sensible measures had stopped applying to this mountain weeks ago.

Quentin woke the way she always did now — instantly, completely, like a lamp turned up. Her eyes found mine in the dark and she didn't ask how I felt. She asked, "Is it today?"

"It's today."

She nodded once and started rolling her sleeping mat. Twelve years old. Her father had vanished into this range carrying a letter that said *the Provision is carried, not kept*, and she'd walked into the teeth of it without once asking to be carried herself. I've known grown men — I have *been* a grown man, mostly — who couldn't have done what she did between the waystation and here.

We moved at first light. The snow above the tree line lay deep and unbroken, blue in the shadowed hollows, blinding where the sun hit it, and the air had that thin, ringing quality it gets after a storm has scrubbed everything out of it. You could see forever. That was the problem. Forever also meant anyone could see *us*.

Odile set the pace and I let her. She'd taken the rope failure on the Red Stair personally, and her hands were still wrapped — the palms scalded raw where she'd held Quentin's line against the rock until the friction ate through her gloves and into her. She climbed with those hands anyway, testing holds with fingers that must have screamed at her, and never said a word about it. When I offered to take lead she looked at me the way you'd look at a man offering to carry your piano.

"You navigate," she said. "I climb. The girl maps. Everyone does their one thing well or we die doing three things badly."

It was hard to argue with her arithmetic.

The route Quentin's father had marked — the last leg of it, penciled in his cramped cartographer's shorthand that she read like other children read street signs — took us along a ridge spine barely wide enough for boots, then up a series of frozen chimneys that Odile led with a patience I recognized as fury in its work clothes. Twice the ice in the cracks rang hollow under her axe and she backed down and found another line without complaint, because complaint costs breath and breath was currency up here.

By midday the summit showed itself properly for the first time.

I'd been calling it the Hollow Summit in my head since the letter named it, and I'd assumed the name was poetry. It wasn't. The peak was split. Somewhere in its violent history the top of the thing had cracked open along a seam, and the two halves leaned apart just enough to leave a slot — a vertical fissure running down into the mountain's heart, maybe eight feet across, walled in red-and-black stone, breathing cold air upward like the throat of something asleep.

"That's not natural," Quentin said.

"Everything's natural," Odile said. "Some things are just natural and organized."

The 1897 expedition hadn't just found this place. They'd *finished* it. As we came around the eastern shoulder we saw the work: cut steps ascending the inside of the fissure, the same craft as the Red Stair but finer, older-looking, each tread fitted and pinned. Iron rings at intervals, still sound after a century. And carved above the first step, weathered but legible, the crest — the same crest that had been stamped on the chamber beneath the cliffs, the same crest on the satchel of a dead man whose brass key had ruined my quiet, indebted, solitary life.

Quentin put her hand flat against the carving. She stood there a long moment with her mittened fingers spread over the mark, and I watched her father's daughter say goodbye to him in the only language they'd shared.

"He got this far," she said quietly. "Or he knew someone who did."

"The letter said the vault was here," I said. "He didn't need to come. He needed to know."

"Knowing cost him anyway." She said it without bitterness, which frightened me more than bitterness would have. Then she turned, and the set of her jaw was pure Dunmore. "Let's finish his map."

* * *

The climb up the inside of the fissure took two hours and cost me everything I had left. The steps were true but narrow, the walls pressed close enough that my pack scraped stone on both sides, and the cold air rising from below had a mineral smell to it — old rock, old ice, old time. Odile went first, setting anchors where the iron rings looked doubtful. Quentin came second, counting steps aloud in a low voice, keeping her father's tally against her own count so nothing would surprise us. I came last, which suited me. Somebody had to be rear guard, and besides, I wanted to be the one looking back down.

Looking back down was a mistake I made every hundred feet. Below us the world fell away in white terraces — the glacier field where I'd fallen into the crevasse and found bones and a second keyhole-shaped artifact wedded to the ice beside them; the moraine where I'd bled; far beyond, the dark stitch of the tree line and, invisible but present, the whole drowned coast where a navy wreck sat on the seafloor holding manifests that shouldn't exist. Everything that had happened to me in two months was spread out beneath my boots like a chart. It looked smaller than it had felt. Most catastrophes do, from altitude.

Near the top the fissure narrowed, then opened — abruptly, like a held breath released — onto the summit platform.

I don't know what I expected. Gold, maybe. Even after the waystations, even after the journal that insisted the Provision was *carried*, some crooked salvage-diver corner of me had kept faith with gold. It's hard to kill hope entirely when your whole life you've been paid by the pound for what you can pull out of the water.

There was no gold.

There was a door.

It stood alone on the summit platform, freestanding, absurd, magnificent — a slab of bronze-green metal set into a frame of fitted stone that rose perhaps ten feet and supported nothing above it but sky. No wall. No structure. A doorway to nowhere, planted on the roof of the range, facing east into the sunrise. The metal was furred with a century of frost-lichen, but the lower third had been kept clear — kept clear by design, I realized, because the stone apron before it sloped just so, shedding meltwater across the face in a slow annual rinse. Someone had engineered the weather itself into the maintenance schedule.

Two keyholes. Side by side, chest height, each the size and shape of the artifacts we carried. And between them, cut deep into the metal, a single word in the expedition's angular lettering:

**PROVISION**

"One mechanism," Quentin breathed. "Both keys. Together." She was already pulling off her mitten with her teeth. "Dad's letter said the halves were separated so no one person could open it alone. So it had to be — it was built to require *trust*. Two people. Or—"

"Or one person who waited a century for company," Odile said.

I took the keys out of my coat. The first — the one from the dead man's satchel in the sea cave, the one that had started everything, the one the syndicate and the navy and Vess Halloway had chased across a mountain range — was heavy and warm from my body heat. The second, pried from the ice beside those old bones in the crevasse, was colder, lighter, its haft wrapped in rotted cord where someone had gripped it a very long time ago.

I stood there holding an inheritance in each hand and found, to my considerable surprise, that my eyes were stinging. Altitude, I told myself. Wind. The light off the snow. All the lies a man tells himself when his feelings arrive ahead of schedule.

"Cyrus." Quentin's voice was gentle. She'd seen. Of course she'd seen. "You don't have to do the speech."

"I wasn't going to do a speech."

"You get the look. Dad got the look."

"I'm not doing the look either."

"You're doing it right now."

Odile made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Open the door, Oyelaran. Before the weather changes its mind."

I fit both keys into both keyholes. They seated with a click I felt in my teeth — two tumblers, one lock, a mechanism designed by people who understood that anything worth protecting is worth protecting from loneliness. I looked at Quentin. She nodded. I looked at Odile. She gave me the smallest tilt of her chin, which from Odile was a parade.

I turned both keys.

The door did not swing. It *sank* — dropping smoothly into the stone on counterweights that still balanced perfectly after a hundred and twenty-eight years, sliding down and away until the frame stood empty and the interior lay open to the daylight.

Cold, clean air rose out of it. Not grave-air. Cellar-air. The smell of paper and oilcloth and cedar.

And inside, stacked in ordered rows down into the mountain's hollow: crates. Sealed tins. Glass jars packed in straw. Oilcloth bundles tied with waxed cord. Long wooden cases stenciled with the crest. Shelves cut into living rock, loaded floor to ceiling, descending beyond the reach of the light.

No gold. No jewels. No king's ransom.

Seeds. I saw the word stenciled on the nearest case — *WHEAT, HARDY STRAIN, N=400* — and beside it jars labeled in careful period handwriting: *penicillin mold cultures, method enclosed*. *Salt pork, 1896 pack*. *Textbooks, arithmetic & navigation, 30 copies*. *Letters, personal, DO NOT OPEN ALONE*.

"They weren't hiding treasure," Quentin whispered. She'd gone very still, the way she went still over a beautiful map. "They were stocking a larder. For whoever came next."

"A community," Odile said slowly. "They lost people — we've seen the graves, the bones — and the survivors didn't quit. They consolidated. Everything they had, everything they learned, everything they could carry forward..." She gestured at the shelves descending into the dark. "This is what they carried. This is the Provision."

A testament. A found family's testament, written in tin and seed and glass, addressed to the future, postage prepaid across a hundred and twenty-eight years.

And it had been waiting for us. For a disgraced guide with burned hands, an orphaned cartographer's daughter, and a salvage diver who'd come looking for a payout and was standing here instead with wet eyes and empty pockets, richer than he'd ever planned to be.

I reached for the nearest oilcloth bundle — the one marked *letters* — and had just broken the wax seal when Odile's head came up sharply, the way it did when her instincts outran her reasons.

"What?" I said.

She was looking east, past the empty doorframe, down the long white shoulder of the mountain. Her burned hands hung at her sides and her voice, when it came, was flat and cold as the ice we'd crossed.

"We left tracks," she said. "Three days of them. And the storm just cleared the sky for everyone."

Far below, small as ants, dark figures had begun to move up the glacier trail behind us.

Halloway had found our road. And we were standing on top of the world, inside the one room on earth she wanted most, with the door wide open.
