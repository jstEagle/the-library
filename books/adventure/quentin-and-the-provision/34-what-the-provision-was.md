## 34. What the Provision Was

The vault door opened the way my father's letters always described doors opening in his dreams: slowly, with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

I was the first one through. Nobody stopped me. Cyrus had both keys — well, one key and one artifact, which was really just the other key wearing a costume of ice and a century of patience — and he'd turned them together in the twin keyholes, and the mechanism had done something deep inside the rock, a series of heavy clicks like knuckles cracking, and then the door had swung inward on hinges that some 1897 genius had packed with grease that still worked. That was the part that got me, later. Not gold. Grease. Somebody had thought about the *hinges*. Somebody had stood in this cold a hundred years ago and thought, *someone is coming after us, someday, and they will need this to open.*

That's what I kept coming back to, standing there in the dark while Odile lit the lanterns they'd left for us. Somebody had thought about us before we existed.

"Steady," Odile said, but her voice wasn't steady. It hadn't been since the summit, since the storm cleared like a curtain pulled back and there it was — the Hollow Summit, a crater of blue ice with a stone house sitting in the middle of it like an egg in a nest. "Let it be real before you run at it."

I ran at it anyway. She knew I would.

* * *

The chamber inside was bigger than our whole house had been, back when we still had a house. Bigger than the chandlery, bigger than the harbor church. The walls were shelved floor to ceiling, and every shelf was full, and none of it was treasure.

I want to be honest about the first ten seconds, because everyone always lies about this part in the retelling. For ten seconds I looked at all those shelves and felt cheated. Ten seconds of *where is it, where's the gold, we climbed the Red Stair, Odile's hands are ruined, Cyrus got shot, we nearly died in a cave eating nothing* — ten seconds of that ugly arithmetic running in my head. Then I saw what was actually on the shelves, and I have never been so ashamed of ten seconds in my life.

Seeds. Thousands of them, in sealed glass jars, labeled in a careful copperplate hand: wheat, barley, beans, apples, things I didn't have names for. Medicines in brown bottles, rows and rows, the labels faded to ghosts. Books — actual books, farming books and medical books and a whole shelf of ledgers. And letters. Bundles and bundles of letters, tied with waxed string, each bundle addressed in the same copperplate: *To whoever comes next.*

"To whoever comes next," Cyrus read aloud behind me. His voice did something strange on the words. I didn't look at him because if I looked at him I was going to cry, and I had decided somewhere on the glacier that I wasn't going to cry until we were down off this mountain, and I am stubborn about my decisions even the bad ones.

"They called it the Provision," Odile said. She was reading a placard by the door, brass, green with age. "Not a treasure. A provision. Like you'd provision a ship." She laughed, one short bark with no humor in it. "A hundred years of men killing each other over a word, and it means *supplies.*"

"It doesn't mean supplies," I said. I don't know how I knew. I just did, the way I knew my father's shorthand, the way I knew weather before it arrived. "It means *provision for.* You provision someone. It's not the stuff. It's who it's for."

The lantern light moved across the shelves. Everything was exactly where they had left it. There was no dust — or almost none; the vault had been sealed too well — and the jars caught the light and threw it back warm, amber, like the whole room was holding its breath around a candle.

Cyrus picked up the nearest letter bundle and didn't open it. He just held it. I watched his thumb move over the waxed string the way he'd touched the brass key back in the cave, back when I first saw him and thought he was just another man carrying something that wasn't his.

"You should do it," he said to me.

"Why me?"

"Because you're the one who came looking." He said it simply, like it was obvious. Maybe it was. "Halloway wanted it. The navy wanted it buried. We wanted — I don't even know what I wanted, at the start. Money, maybe. To owe less. But you've been following your father up this mountain since the day he didn't come home. So."

So I untied the string.

* * *

There were forty-one letters. I know because I counted them twice, that night, by lantern, while the storm rebuilt itself outside and Cyrus slept with his bandaged shoulder propped against a seed shelf and Odile sat awake the way she always sat awake, listening to the mountain breathe.

The first letter was from a man named Aldous Wren — and yes, I checked my own name against it and no, probably not related, though I read his handwriting three times just to feel the coincidence. He wrote:

*We were nineteen when we set out and we are nine now, and the nine of us have argued for six months about what to leave here. Marta says leave the seeds, because a people without seed corn is a people waiting to die. Tomas says leave the medicines, because he has watched fevers take children and cannot bear to leave that watch unfinished. I say leave the letters, because everything else can be found again by someone willing to work, but a thing nobody writes down is lost forever the moment the last mouth that spoke it closes.*

*So we leave all three, and we call the whole of it the Provision, and we ask only one thing of whoever finds it: carry it onward. Do not guard it. Do not sell it. Do not build a wall around it and charge admission to your better nature. Take what your town needs and pass the rest to the next town, and the next, until the last jar is empty and the last letter is read aloud to somebody who needed to hear it.*

*A hoard dies with its keeper. A provision lives in the spending.*

I read that line out loud four times. *A hoard dies with its keeper. A provision lives in the spending.* Cyrus had gone very still when I read it, and later he told me why: it was the exact opposite of every rule he'd ever lived by. Salvage is hoarding. Salvage is *finders keepers* with a boat. He'd spent his whole adult life pulling things out of water and keeping them, and here were nineteen people — nine people — who'd carried everything they had up a mountain specifically to give it away.

The letters went on. One from Marta, the seed-keeper, describing each jar like she was introducing her children. One from Tomas the doctor, with instructions folded inside for making poultices from mountain plants, written in a hand that shook toward the end. One that was just a list of names — the nineteen who set out, with little notes beside each: *died of the fever, second winter. Died in the crevasse field. Went home in the spring; no shame in it. Died old, right here, happy.* Nine names had no note at all except *survived,* and beneath the list, in different ink, newer-looking:

*If you are reading this, then we made it out after all — out of the mountains, out of the silence, into whatever future we could not see. We could not carry all of this. We could carry the idea of it. That was the expedition's true cargo, and now it is yours.*

And then, near the bottom of the bundle, a letter I did not expect. The paper was different. Newer. Not a hundred years old — twenty years old at most. My hands knew before my head did. I know my father's paper the way I know his handwriting.

It was dated the spring before he vanished. It said:

*Q —*

*I found it. Or I found the trail to it, which may be the same thing. The waystation journals lead upward and the old man in the valley confirmed the crest. There is a vault in the Hollow Summit and I believe it holds the Provision entire.*

*I want you to understand why I'm going, because you'll be angry, and you have a right to be. Everyone who knows about this place wants to own it. The navy wants it buried — I've seen their charts, Q, they've been hunting it for decades, and what they're really hunting is whatever they themselves lost up here and cannot admit to. The syndicate wants to strip it and sell it jar by jar. If I tell anyone where it is, it becomes a hoard, and a hoard dies with its keeper.*

*So I'm going alone, and I'm going quietly, and I'm going to do the only thing that protects it: I'm going to become one more pair of hands carrying it onward. Copies. Notes. A map that a clever girl can read. If I don't come back, follow the map. Trust the guide who turns back — I mean that literally, Q, trust the one who turns back, because the ones who never turn back are the ones who get people killed.*

*Everything on that mountain is meant to be spent, not kept. Spend it well. Spend it loud. And when you find it, add your own letter to the bundle. That's the whole inheritance. That's the whole trick.*

*All my love, always,*
*Dad*

I sat with that letter for a long time. The lantern hissed. Outside, the wind worked at the crater rim like a patient carpenter.

He'd known. He'd known about Odile — *trust the one who turns back* — he'd known and he'd forgiven her before she ever confessed it, before the waystation, before any of it. He'd gone up this mountain knowing he might die carrying paper, and he'd gone anyway, because paper that gets carried is worth more than gold that gets guarded.

"He wasn't looking for treasure," I said. My voice came out cracked. I let it. "He was doing what they asked. Carrying it onward."

Cyrus put his hand on my head, awkward, the way he did everything tender — like a man handling something he was afraid of breaking and determined to handle anyway. "Then he finished the job," he said. "You're proof."

* * *

We spent two days in the vault, because the storm gave us no choice and because none of us wanted to leave. We worked by lantern light. Odile, whose hands were wrapped in bandages to the fingertips, directed the packing by pointing with her chin and swearing creatively when we guessed wrong. Cyrus copied documents until his eyes crossed, using my father's own shorthand once I taught it to him — he learned fast, faster than he admitted, and there was something in watching him write my father's symbols that closed a circle I hadn't known was open.

We took seeds — not many, a dozen jars, the hardy mountain varieties Marta's notes recommended for low valleys. We took medicines, the ones Tomas's instructions said would keep. We took copies of everything and photographs of the rest, because Cyrus owned a camera he'd salvaged years ago and never had a reason to use, and now he had the best reason in the world.

And I wrote my letter. Right there, on the vault's little writing desk, with the pen they'd left — the ink had dried to stone, but Cyrus had a pencil stub in his coat, because salvage divers apparently fear being without a pencil the way other men fear drowning. I wrote:

*To whoever comes next:*

*My name is Quentin Dunmore. My father followed these letters up the mountain and died carrying them forward, and I followed him, and I didn't die, because of a diver who owed money to terrible people and a guide who thought she deserved nothing. Neither of them thinks they're family. They're wrong. I've decided this unilaterally and I have it on good authority that unilateral decisions run in my family.*

*Take what you need. Pass on the rest. Read the letters aloud. And check the hinges — someone greased them a hundred years ago just for you.*

*— Q*

I put it on top of the bundle, under the waxed string, where the next pair of hands would find it first.

* * *

On the third morning the storm broke for good, and the world outside the vault was blinding white and utterly silent, and we stood at the crater rim with our packs full of other people's hope and looked down at the long descent.

That was when we heard it. Faint, below the wind, rising up the mountain's flank: engines. More than one. A rhythmic thrum I'd learned to hate on the trail — rotors.

Odile shaded her eyes. Cyrus's hand found my shoulder.

"Navy," he said. "Early. Faster than they should be."

Down in the white distance, small as insects, three shapes were moving up the glacier toward the Hollow Summit — and they were not climbing. They were riding machines, and they were coming straight for everything we'd promised to spend.

I tightened the strap on my pack and thought about my father's last instruction, the one he'd hidden in a letter for a girl he'd never see grow up.

*Spend it loud.*

"Well then," I said. "Let's go be loud."
