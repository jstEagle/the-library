## 36. Halloway's Last Climb

I have guided enough fools up enough mountains to know the difference between a man who is tired and a man who is coming apart. Tired men slow down. Coming-apart men speed up, because the mountain has stopped being a mountain to them and started being a jury, and they are trying to outrun the verdict.

Vess Halloway was coming apart, and I watched it happen from two hundred meters of scree below, and I did nothing, and I will be doing nothing about that for the rest of my life.

Let me start where it started, which was the morning after the rockslide, with my hands wrapped like a butcher's mistake and the three of us sitting in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, eating the last of the good chocolate because Cyrus insisted that victory rations were a tradition and I didn't have the strength to argue.

The rockslide had done its work. Two of Halloway's climbers had turned back down the mountain — I saw their tracks veering east at the col, sensible men, the kind I'd have been glad to guide in another life. The third, the one I recognized, the one whose name I will not put in this account because he has a mother somewhere, had limped off after them with his arm bound to his chest. That left Halloway and the big quiet one, Kessler, and a fourth man I'd only glimpsed through the dust, a lean climber with a scar through his eyebrow whom Halloway called Rook.

Four had become three, and three had become a problem with a different shape. A big team moves like weather — you can see it coming. Three desperate men move like weather's bad mood.

"He'll come up the Red Stair," Cyrus said, licking chocolate off his thumb with the serenity of a man who had recently lied to a crime syndicate so well that I'd briefly been proud of him. "He's got one key. He knows we've got the other. He can't open anything without us, so he'll follow."

"He'll follow *fast*," I said. "We cost him a day and two men and most of his dignity. Men like him don't forgive arithmetic like that."

Quentin was sketching. She'd been sketching since dawn — the summit ridge, the cornices along its northern shoulder, the wind plumes lifting off the crest like smoke from a fire nobody could see. Her father's shorthand crowded the margins. She was twelve years old and she read that ridge better than I did, and I have made my living reading ridges for twenty years, and I try not to think about what that says about either of us.

"The cornices are rotten," she said, not looking up. "Dad's notes say the north side loads heavy in a south wind. We've had south wind for three days." She looked up then, and her eyes were old in a way no child's eyes should be. "If Halloway takes the ridge line instead of the couloir, he'll be walking on a shelf with nothing under it."

"Then we hope he's stupid," Cyrus said.

"He's not stupid," I said. "He's worse. He's certain."

* * *

We climbed for the summit through the afternoon, and the mountain, for once, was generous. The storm that had nearly killed us in the tree-line cave had spent itself; the sky above three thousand meters was the hard clean blue that looks like mercy and is really just weather taking a breath. The snow under our boots squeaked and held. Quentin led on the steeper pitches, reading her father's route marks — a double dash here, a circle there — and every time her crampons bit and she pulled through, I saw Cyrus watching her with an expression I recognized because I'd been wearing a version of it for a week: the look of a man who has accidentally become a father and is trying to pretend it's a temporary condition.

My hands were the problem. Odile Ferran's hands, which had held a thousand ropes and once, in a better year, held a client's son above a crevasse for forty minutes — those hands were ruined for the season. The Red Stair had cost me the skin off eight fingers and most of the strength in my grip. I wrapped them, I flexed them, I lied about them. On the mountain you lie about your injuries the way you lie about your fears: constantly, competently, and in the hope that the lie becomes true through sheer repetition.

We reached the shoulder below the summit cone as the light went long and gold. And that was when we heard them.

Not voices. Crampons. The particular ringing bite of steel on ice, high and to our left, where the ridge line ran — the ridge line with the cornices.

Quentin heard it first. She always heard things first; grief had tuned her like an instrument. She held up one mittened hand and we froze, and there it was, faint and rhythmic, three climbers moving fast along the crest.

"He's on the ridge," Cyrus breathed. "Why is he on the ridge? The couloir is safer by half."

"Because the couloir is slower," I said, "and because he can see us from up there, and because he wants us to know he's coming. Fear is a tool. He's using it on us."

It was working. I will admit that. My heart was going like a fist on a door.

We had maybe forty minutes of light and a final pitch of mixed ground between us and the summit, where the vault door waited with its two keyholes and whatever truth the 1897 expedition had carried up here and left behind. Halloway had the ridge. We had the direct line. It was going to be a race, and I have been in mountain races before, and the mountain always collects its fee from somebody.

"Odile," Cyrus said quietly. "Can your hands take the pitch?"

"My hands," I said, "are the least of anyone's problems right now."

* * *

I want to be honest here, because this account will be published and because Quentin will read it and because she deserves the truth even when the truth is ugly. So: when I heard Halloway on that ridge, some part of me — the part that lost a client on a mountain because I turned back and he didn't — thought, *let the mountain have him.* Not kill him, exactly. Just... let the mountain conduct its interview. He had chased a grieving child across a range. He had put a rope around Cyrus's neck at the col. He had, I was almost sure, been the money behind the men who burned out a surveyor's camp two seasons back — the camp where Quentin's father had last been seen. The mountain interviewing Vess Halloway felt, in that golden light, like justice.

Then I looked at Quentin's face, and she wasn't thinking justice. She was thinking about the cornice, and about the men on top of it, and her expression was the expression of someone watching a disaster that hadn't happened yet and being powerless to stop it. Twelve years old, and her first instinct was pity.

That is the thing about that girl that I will spend the rest of my life failing to deserve. She has been failed by every adult who was supposed to protect her, and her instinct is still to warn people.

"Odile," she said. "The cornice. They're right on the edge of it. Can't we —" She didn't finish. She didn't know how to finish. Neither did I.

"Keep climbing," I said. "Fast as we can. If we're on the summit when they arrive, maybe this ends in a conversation instead of a catastrophe."

It was a stupid hope. Mountains don't end in conversations. But hope is the rope you tie when you've got nothing else, and I tied it, and we climbed.

* * *

The last pitch was ice over rock, forty meters of it, and my hands screamed through the wraps with every swing. Cyrus went first and set a running belay like a man possessed — and he is a salvage diver, not a climber, and watching him move up that pitch I understood something about him I should have understood in the harbor: he had spent his whole life going down into dark places alone and coming back out. This was just another dark place. He came back out, and he hauled the rope tight for Quentin, and she went up it like she'd been born on it, her father's route notes folded into her jacket over her heart.

I went last, because I always go last, because the guide goes last, because if the mountain takes anyone it should take the one whose job it was. My fingers closed on ice axes and reported their grievances and I overruled them. Forty meters. I counted them like a rosary.

We came over the summit lip into the last of the light, and there it was.

The vault.

I had heard them describe it — the door in the rock, the crest of the 1897 expedition, the two keyholes cut into one mechanism of bronze and iron, fused into the mountain as if the mountain had grown around it. Hearing about a thing and standing in front of it are different animals. The door was small, almost humble, set into a wall of granite at the summit's crown, and it had the patient look of something that had waited a hundred years and was prepared to wait a hundred more. The expedition crest — the mountain, the star, the seven names — was cut deep above it, and the wind had polished the letters but not erased them.

The wilderness forgets nothing. I thought that, standing there, and I didn't yet know how badly I was about to be proven right.

"Keys," Cyrus said. His voice had gone strange and soft.

Quentin produced hers — the artifact from the crevasse, the keyhole-shaped half. Cyrus drew the brass key from his coat. They knelt at the door together, side by side, and I stood back and kept watch, because that was my job, and because I wanted to give them the moment. Whatever was behind that door, they had earned it with blood and blizzards and a twelve-year-old's stubbornness, and I was just the hired help who'd finally shown up for the whole of her fee.

The two keys went in. They turned together with a sound like a held breath being released — a deep, mineral *clunk* that I felt through my boots.

And that was when Vess Halloway came over the ridge.

* * *

He came alone.

I saw it immediately and I knew what it meant. Kessler and Rook were somewhere behind him — dropped, refused, or abandoned. Halloway had come up the last section of ridge at a dead run, which no sane mountaineer does on a corniced crest at dusk, and he came over the crest line into the summit light with ice in his beard and his eyes burning like a man with a fever, and I understood that we were not looking at a rival anymore.

We were looking at a casualty.

"Stop," he said. His voice was wrecked — days of cold and shouting and defeat had ground it down to gravel. "Don't — don't open it. That's — you don't understand what's —"

"It's open, Vess," Cyrus said. He didn't stand up. He stayed kneeling by the door, between it and Halloway, and I saw what he was doing: putting his body between the madman and the vault, casually, like a man adjusting his position to get a better view. Cyrus Oyelaran, con man of the harbor, liar of the col, was doing the one honest thing of his life and disguising it as laziness. "It's open. And you should see what's inside."

"I know what's inside!" Halloway took another step, and the snow under him boomed — that hollow drum-sound that every mountaineer knows and every mountaineer dreads, the sound of a slab with air under it. I flinched. He didn't. That was the worst part. He didn't even hear it. "I know what's inside, Oyelaran. I've known for thirty years. My father was navy. He was *on* the recovery team in 'twenty-three, when they came to bury it. He told me what they carried down. He told me what they were ordered to leave." He was crying, I realized. Great frozen tracks down a face burned black by wind. "It isn't treasure. It was never treasure. It's a *list*. It's names and it's proof and it's everything they were ashamed of, and if it comes off this mountain —"

"Then the shame comes off too," Quentin said.

She said it simply, standing there with the door half-open and the wind pulling at her hood, and I watched the words go into Vess Halloway like an axe into a stump.

"You don't know," he whispered. "You're a *child*. You don't know what it does to a man to find out his whole life was built on a lie his father —" He stopped. He looked at the door. He looked at Cyrus. And I saw the last sane thing leave his face, the way a bird leaves a ledge.

"The keys," he said. "Give me the keys. I'll take it back down. I'll bury it properly. It's what my father couldn't — it's what he —"

"Vess." Cyrus rose, slow, hands open and empty. "The vault doesn't work that way. Come and look. Just look. Then decide."

And here is the thing I will carry to my grave: Halloway took a step *forward*. Toward the door. Toward looking. Whatever was left of the man — the son of a navy man, the heir to a shame, the syndicate boss who had chased three people across a mountain range — that remnant wanted, for one second, to *look*.

Then the mountain collected its fee.

* * *

I heard it before I saw it — a crack like a rifle shot, off to the east, along the crest he'd run in on. The south wind had been loading that cornice for three days, exactly as Quentin's father's notes had said, exactly as Quentin had said, and a hundred and forty pounds of half-mad syndicate boss running across it at dusk had been the last argument it needed.

"Cornice!" I screamed, and my voice cracked in the middle of the word like a schoolgirl's, and I was already moving, which was stupid, which was the guide's reflex, the reflex that had cost me everything once before — *go toward the falling client, go toward the sound* — and Cyrus caught my collar and hauled me back and I fought him, God help me, I fought him for half a second.

The cornice went with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

I have seen avalanches. I have seen seracs fall and whole faces of glacier let go. But a cornice collapse is its own horror — the sky itself peeling off the ridge, tons of wind-packed snow curling down and over, taking the edge of the world with it. Halloway was ten meters from the break line when it let go. I saw him turn. I saw him understand. And in the half-second before the white took him, he did not reach for his axe, and he did not reach for the slope.

He reached toward the vault door. Toward the light spilling out of it. Toward whatever his father had tried to tell him and whatever he had spent his whole rotten life refusing to hear.

Then the mountain closed its hand, and Vess Halloway was gone.

* * *

The powder cloud rolled over the summit and past us and out into the darkening east, and then there was silence, that enormous post-avalanche silence in which you can hear your own heart apologizing for still working.

Quentin was crying. Not loudly. She stood with her back against the vault door, inside the threshold, in the warm gold light of whatever burned or glowed in there — and I would learn soon enough what was in there, the letters, the seeds, the testament, the whole human inheritance of it — and she cried for Vess Halloway, of all the people on God's green and white earth, for the man who had hunted her across a mountain range.

"He was just somebody's son," she said. "Who got told the wrong story."

Cyrus put his hand on her head, on her hood, and left it there. He was looking east, down the fall line, at the smooth white scar where the cornice had been, and his face was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to do.

"Kessler and Rook," he said quietly. "They were behind him on the ridge. They would have seen it happen." He turned to me. "Odile. When they come up here — and they'll come, men like that always come to see the body — what are they going to do?"

I looked at my ruined hands. I looked at the door, and the two keys still standing in the lock, and the light coming out of the mountain like a held secret finally being told.

I thought about pity, and about absolution, and about how the first is something you can give anyone and the second is something nobody can give themselves, and how Vess Halloway had died reaching for the one thing that might have given it to him.

"They're going to see it," I said, "the same way we did. And then they're going to have to decide what kind of men they are. Same as all of us."

And then, from inside the vault, from the depths of a mountain that had kept a hundred years of silence, Quentin said, very softly:

"Cyrus. Odile. You need to come see this. There are letters in here — dozens of them. And the one on top is addressed to *whoever finally opens the door*."

The wind moved through the summit, and the light from the vault lay across the snow like a path.

We went in.
