## 37. Descent

**Cyrus**

The mountain let us go the way a fist lets go of something it's been holding too long — slowly, finger by finger, with no particular grace.

We left the Hollow Summit at first light, three days after the navy detachment marched back down into the weather they'd come out of, and I'll tell you what surprised me most: how little I wanted to look back. I'd spent my whole life diving toward things — wrecks, ledgers, other men's lost cargo — and every salvage had ended with me hauling something up and feeling lighter for having taken it. This was the first time in my life I'd walked away from a fortune on purpose, and instead of the old itch between my shoulder blades, the one that always said *you missed something, go back down*, there was just quiet.

The original testament stayed sealed behind its two-keyhole door, exactly as the keepers of 1897 had intended. We'd copied everything by lamplight over two nights — Quentin's hand steady as a draftsman's, Odile pressing each page flat with hands still bandaged from the Red Stair, me doing the one thing I'm genuinely good at besides holding my breath: keeping watch and staying out of the way.

"You could have taken it," Odile said to me that second night, not looking up from her copying. "The originals. Halloway would have paid for them. The navy would have paid more."

"The navy would have buried them again," I said. "And Halloway—" I stopped, because Halloway was somewhere below us in the white, or under it, and I didn't have the stomach for finishing sentences about him yet.

"Exactly," Odile said, and that was the whole conversation. Some inheritances you carry by leaving them where they lie.

Now we were descending through snow that deepened by the hour, and the copies rode in oilcloth against our chests like letters we'd been asked to deliver. Which, I suppose, is exactly what they were.

* * *

**Odile**

I have guided people down mountains for nineteen years, and I will tell you the truth that no guidebook prints: going down is harder than going up. Going up, a person has purpose. Going down, a person has only their own tired legs and all the time in the world to think about what they did.

My hands ached inside their bandages. On the Red Stair I had caught Quentin with rope burned through my palms, and the doctor in me — the part that used to be a client's last hope before I became a cautionary tale — kept cataloguing the damage. Two fingers on the right that might never grip a carabiner properly again. A left palm that would heal into a map of its own.

Worth it. That was the strange arithmetic I couldn't stop running. Nineteen years of guilt over one man who walked into the range alone because I turned back, and it took a twelve-year-old girl falling off a cut step in red rock to balance the ledger. Not fully. Ledgers like mine don't balance. But the debt had changed shape. It wasn't a weight anymore. It was a direction.

"Odile." Quentin had dropped back to walk beside me, which she'd been doing more often since the fever night, since Cyrus went out into the blizzard for her and came back looking like something the storm had chewed and reconsidered. "Your hands. Can I carry your pack?"

"I can carry my own pack."

"I know. I'm asking anyway."

I looked at her. She had her father's chin when she was being stubborn, which was most of the time now, and I understood suddenly that this was not charity. This was a girl learning how a family works — you notice things, you ask anyway, you accept being refused without taking it as a verdict.

"Left strap," I said finally. "Take the left strap."

She did, and we walked on, and neither of us said anything else, and it was the easiest silence we'd ever shared.

* * *

**Quentin**

Papa's maps had a shorthand for everything. A dotted line meant uncertain ground. A small triangle with a dot meant water you could trust. And there was one symbol I'd found only twice in all his notebooks: a circle with a line through it, drawn small, tucked into margins like an apology. It took me until the summit vault to understand it. It meant *something worth carrying forward*. He'd marked the trailhead with it. He'd marked the waystation. He'd marked the Hollow Summit itself, in his final letter, in handwriting gone shaky from cold.

He died carrying it forward. That's what the Provision was for — not gold, not glory, just a community's testament passed hand to hand down a century, waiting for someone to take up the delivery route.

So on the descent, while the men from the navy shrank to specks below us and the storm cleared into a blue so hard it rang, I started drawing. New map. My map. The pass, the Stair, the karst shortcut, the crevasse field where Cyrus fell and found the bones and the second key — all of it, in Papa's shorthand, plus a few symbols of my own invention. A circle with a line through it for the summit. A small square for the waystation where Odile told the truth and broke us and saved us, all in the same hour.

"What are you drawing?" Cyrus asked, leaning over my shoulder. He smelled like woodsmoke and iodine and he moved like a man whose ribs had opinions.

"A delivery route," I said.

"For what?"

"For us. For whoever comes next." I tapped the margin symbol. "Papa drew these for things worth carrying forward. Somebody has to draw the next ones."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "Your father would've liked you," which was such a ridiculous thing to say that I laughed, and he grinned, and above us the peak stood clean and white against the sky like a door closed gently from the inside.

* * *

**Cyrus**

By the fourth day we reached the tree line, and the snow turned from enemy to witness — soft underfoot, muffling everything, making the pines look like a congregation in gray coats. We were moving slower now, but it wasn't the slowness of exhaustion anymore. Or maybe it was, but exhaustion had been transfigured somewhere on the glacier, the way base metal is supposed to transfigure if you believe in that sort of thing. What I felt wasn't tired so much as *empty in a good way*, the way a hold feels after you've pumped out the water and found nothing rotten beneath.

I thought about the man whose satchel I'd robbed in the cave. I'd carried his brass key for months like a stolen thing, because it was one. Somewhere on the descent — I think it was the morning we crossed the scree where Odile had set her decoy route, back when Halloway's climbers were still a live threat — I stopped carrying it like theft and started carrying it like mail. He'd been a keeper. So was I now. The difference was just whether you delivered.

Quentin walked ahead of me, mapping. Odile walked behind, watching our backs out of habit even though the only thing hunting us now was winter. And I walked in the middle, which is where I'd apparently decided to live.

"You're smiling," Odile observed. "It's unsettling."

"Am I?"

"Like a man who's forgotten he owes money."

"I don't owe money," I said, and then remembered the syndicate, the burned contract, Vess Halloway lost somewhere on the mountain, and amended: "Not to anyone who can collect."

"Hm," said Odile, in the tone of a woman filing that away for later cross-examination.

* * *

**Odile**

On the fifth night we camped in the shelter of a granite overhang, and I took first watch because my body no longer believed in sleeping through the dark. The others slept — Cyrus curled like a man guarding something even unconscious, Quentin sprawled with the boneless abandon of the young, her notebook open on her chest.

I sat with my ruined hands in my lap and did the accounting I'd been avoiding since the summit.

Emeric Ferran would have called me a fool. Emeric, my brother, the one good thing in our family's ledger, dead two years now in a crevasse I should have been roped into. He used to say I guided mountains because I trusted rock more than people. He was right, and he was wrong, and both of those truths had cost me everything except the two people sleeping ten feet from me.

The client I lost was Quentin's father. I turned back at the tree line because the weather spoke sense and he wouldn't listen, and I have replayed that conversation every night for a year. But here is what I learned on this mountain, in the waystation, in the storm, on the Stair with my hands burning: turning back isn't always betrayal. Sometimes it's just the wrong answer to a question that had no right one. And sometimes the mountain gives you a second chance to answer differently, and the second chance looks like a sharp-eyed girl who needs teaching and a diver who lies badly and means well, and you say yes for reasons you won't share until you're ready.

I was ready now, mostly. There'd be time on the low ground to tell them the rest. Not tonight. Tonight I watched the stars wheel and the snow glimmer and let myself feel the thing I hadn't let myself feel since Emeric died:

Useful. Chosen. Home-ish.

* * *

**Quentin**

Day six, we hit the long moraine below the glacier, and Cyrus taught me the game he plays with clouds.

"Stratus means weather coming in slow and mean," he said, pointing with his chin because his hands were busy steadying me over a boulder field. "Cumulus building past noon means an argument with the sky. And those—" he nodded at thin high wisps like pulled wool— "those are the sky clearing its throat. Twenty-four hours, maybe less."

"Papa had a saying about mares' tails," I said. "*Mares' tails and mackerel scales make tall ships carry short sails.*"

"He steal that from a sailor?"

"Sailors stole it from somebody. That's how sayings work. They're cargo too."

Cyrus stopped walking and looked at me with an expression I was starting to know well — the one where he was surprised by me and trying to hide it and failing. "Cargo," he repeated.

"Everything true gets passed along. Maps. Sayings. Testaments." I shrugged, which was hard with a full pack, and did it anyway. "You just have to keep it moving."

That afternoon I taught him Papa's shorthand — the angle marks for slope, the wavy line for unreliable ice, the little crowned dot that meant *shelter, defensible, dry*. He copied them into the back of his dive log, which had survived the navy confiscation by virtue of being hidden inside a false bottom I built him back in the harbor town, and which he pretended not to be sentimental about.

"Why do you write so small?" he asked, watching me.

"So more fits," I said. "Papa said a map is a promise you make to someone you haven't met yet. You want to promise them plenty."

* * *

**Cyrus**

The last day above the valley, the snow began again — fat, unhurried flakes, the kind that mean the storm has spent its anger and is just tidying up. We descended through it in single file, and I found myself thinking about the cave where all this started. Low tide, open door, a chamber sealed since 1897, and me standing in it with greed and guilt in equal measure, taking a dead man's key.

If I could go back and stand beside that version of myself — soaked, broke, hunted by creditors, so lonely he didn't have a word for it — I knew exactly what I'd tell him. Not *don't take the key.* The key was never the point; keys never are. I'd tell him: *the Provision isn't in the cave. It's whatever you refuse to leave behind. Pay attention to the girl with the crest knowledge. Mind the guide with the guilty eyes. And when the choice comes — and it will come, on a summit, in a storm, with a gun at someone's throat — choose the person. Every time. Choose the person.*

He wouldn't have listened. Men like the man I was don't listen; they have to descend into it themselves.

But he'd have made it. That was the thing I understood, trudging down through the soft snow with two keepers flanking me and a testament against my chest: he'd have made it, because the Provision was never really treasure, and the curse was never really a curse. It was just a door, and doors only look like traps from the outside.

* * *

**Odile**

We came down out of the snow line at dusk on the seventh day, into a world of wet black rock and streaming pines and the distant, impossible sound of running water — meltwater, a stream, the ordinary music of a world that had never heard of the Hollow Summit. Below us, faint through the trees, a light burned. A farmhouse, maybe. A road beyond it. The harbor town a day's walk after that.

Quentin stood at the edge of the tree line with her new map rolled in her fist, looking down at the light like it was a creature she'd have to approach carefully.

"We publish it," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a delivery schedule. "All of it. Papa's letter, the expedition records, everything. The navy keeps its secret. We keep ours. And the testament goes to everyone."

"Newspapers," Cyrus said. "Printers. Maybe a lecture hall, if you can stand people asking questions."

"I can stand anything," Quentin said, "if Odile stands next to me."

I opened my mouth to deflect — nineteen years of practice — and closed it again. My bandaged hands ached. My legs ached. Something in my chest that had been clenched since a tree line in another year, another life, unclenched by degrees.

"Then I'll stand next to you," I said.

We started down toward the light, three tired keepers carrying paper through the dark, and behind us the mountain took the snow back and kept its counsel, the way wilderness does.

It forgets nothing. Neither do we.

And in Quentin's pocket, folded small, was a chart she'd been studying since the summit — a chart with a crest on it none of us recognized, inked fresh, pointing inland.

But that, as she said, was tomorrow's tide.
