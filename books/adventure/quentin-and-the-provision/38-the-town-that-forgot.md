## 38. The Town That Forgot

The harbor town had a name — Saltmere, though nobody used it except the ferry schedule and my father's old charts, where he'd written it in his small, patient hand: *Saltmere, pop. 1,140, one honest baker.* I'd lived here eleven years and it had never once looked at me twice. Now it couldn't stop looking.

It started with the printing.

Mr. Aldous at the *Gazette* had a press older than the expedition itself, a black iron beast that groaned like a ship in weather, and for six days straight it groaned without stopping. We'd brought down three copies of the testament — Cyrus insisted on that, even with his arm still in a sling from the moraine. "Paper burns," he said. "Three copies means somebody has to burn two houses." Odile had rolled her eyes, but she'd helped him carry them anyway, which was its own kind of agreement.

The testament ran to two hundred and forty pages once Mr. Aldous set it in type. Journals. Seed lists. Medicine inventories. Letters — dozens of letters, written by the eleven survivors of the 1897 expedition to people who were mostly dead by the time the ink dried. A schoolteacher named Ada Voss had written to her sister every week for four months in a cave below the Hollow Summit, describing how they'd divided their supplies so fairly it made your chest hurt to read it, how they'd voted on everything, even grief. The last letter wasn't to anyone. It was to whoever came next.

*We carried what we could,* it said. *Carry the rest.*

I read that line maybe forty times in the first week. I read it aloud once, standing in the chandlery while Odile pretended to inventory rope, and my voice cracked on *rest*, and Odile put down the rope and hugged me with hands still stiff from the Red Stair, and neither of us said anything, because we didn't have to. That was new for me. I was a girl who said things. I'd been saying things since my father vanished — sharp things, mostly, armor-shaped things. But Odile just held on, and I let her, and the bell over the chandlery door rang in the wind and nobody came in, and it was fine.

The town did not handle it fine.

* * *

By the second week, Saltmere had rearranged itself around us the way water rearranges around a dropped stone, all ripples and no dignity.

People who hadn't spoken to me since Father disappeared — since they'd decided, quietly and comfortably, that the cartographer's daughter was strange and probably cursed — now stopped me on the dock road to shake my hand. Mrs. Petraglia, who ran the boarding house and had once charged me double for a bath, pressed a tin of almond biscuits into my arms and said, "Your father would be proud," with such certainty that I almost believed she'd known him, which she hadn't. He'd stayed there exactly one night in 1899 and complained about the mattress.

Cyrus got it worse. Or better. Depending on how you felt about being famous, which Cyrus discovered he hated.

They wanted him to tell the story. All of it, always, and preferably with gestures. The fishmonger's son asked him about the crevasse eleven times. The harbormaster asked about the navy. A woman from the Methodist circle asked whether the cave had been "spiritually significant," and Cyrus, who could lie brilliantly when lives depended on it but apparently not otherwise, said, "It was cold and full of dead men's homework," and the woman wrote it down.

"You're terrible at this," I told him that evening. We were on the chandlery steps, sharing the last of Mrs. Petraglia's biscuits because Odile refused to eat them on principle — something about a client who'd paid her in bad baking once.

"I'm excellent at lying," Cyrus said. "This isn't lying. This is just true, and truth is worse."

"Say it with more gestures."

He gestured. It was somehow more insulting. I laughed until my ribs hurt, and he watched me laugh with an expression I'd learned to recognize on the mountain — the one where he looked surprised to be somewhere good, like a man checking a floor for traps before letting himself enjoy standing on it.

The navy, meanwhile, went quiet the way big things go quiet — completely, and with intent.

Lieutenant Marsh had come to the summit demanding reburial and left with a negotiated surrender: the testament public, the navy's own buried business staying buried. Whatever they'd been hunting under those waters for decades, whatever the sealed wreck held that Cyrus had found manifests for back when this whole thing was just debts and dive lights — that stayed theirs. In exchange, they withdrew. No more helicopters. No more men in navy coats asking questions on our doorstep. Just, one gray morning, a plain envelope on the chandlery counter containing Cyrus's confiscated dive logs, returned in order, unmarked, with a single typed card: *Matter closed.*

Cyrus read it twice and burned the card in the stove.

"That's it?" I asked. "No apology?"

"They don't apologize," he said. "They close matters." He fed the logs' rubber band to the flames too, thoughtfully, like it might contain evidence. "An apology would mean admitting there was something to apologize for. Closing a matter means nothing ever happened. It's very tidy."

"It's cowardly."

"It's both," he said. "Most tidy things are."

But I noticed he slept better after that. We all did. There's a particular exhaustion that comes from being watched, and it lifts all at once, like fog off the water, and you don't realize how heavy it was until your shoulders come back down where shoulders belong.

* * *

The debts dissolved slower than the navy's interest but more satisfyingly, because debt dissolving has witnesses.

Vess Halloway was gone — lost to the cornice on the summit ridge, and Odile had come down from that mountain carrying pity for her the way you carry a stone in your boot, present with every step, useful to no one. But Halloway's syndicate was not one woman; it was paperwork, and paperwork survives avalanches. Two weeks after we came down, a solicitor arrived from the coast with a leather folder and an expression of professional sorrow.

"The syndicate wishes to discuss your outstanding obligations," he told Cyrus.

Cyrus, who had burned his contract off a stove lid in chapter six of his life, as it were, folded his hands. "My outstanding obligation is that they sent climbers to kill a child on a mountain."

"The syndicate denies—"

"Show him," Cyrus said to me.

So I showed him. Not the testament — the *Gazette*. Six weeks of coverage. The editorial Mr. Aldous had titled "What We Owe the Dead," which was really about what the living owe each other, and which had been reprinted in three coastal papers. The photograph of the waystation. The letters, excerpted, including Ada Voss writing about fairness in a freezing dark. And beneath it all, the town's slow, total conversion of Cyrus Oyelaran from *that diver with the syndicate debts* into *the man who brought the Provision home.*

The solicitor looked at the papers for a long time. Then he looked at Cyrus, whose face was doing absolutely nothing, which I knew by then was his most dangerous expression. Then he closed the folder.

"The syndicate," he said, "has elected to consider the obligations settled."

"Generous," said Cyrus.

"They wish you well."

"I'm sure they do," said Cyrus, and showed him out, and stood in the doorway afterward watching the harbor like a man listening for a second wave. When he turned around, Odile was grinning at him from behind the rope shelf, and even he grinned back, briefly, before remembering he was a serious person.

"Notoriety," Odile said, "is worth more than money."

"Don't tell anyone," said Cyrus. "It only works if it's accidental."

* * *

Here is what I did not expect: that the hardest part would be the ordinary days.

On the mountain, everything had been simple. Cold, hunger, fear, keys, storms — you could hold any of it in your hands. Grief too. On the glacier, missing Father had been as clean as a blade. But back in Saltmere, with the stove lit and enough food and no one shooting at us, grief got complicated. Because the town kept handing me pieces of him.

The harbormaster produced Father's survey boat, impounded these four years, and offered it back with wet eyes. Mr. Aldous asked me to identify handwriting in a letter Father had mailed the *Gazette* the week he disappeared — a note promising maps of the range, *maps that will matter someday*, which the paper had never printed because nobody then cared about the range. Mrs. Petraglia remembered, suddenly and conveniently, that Father had paid his final boarding bill in advance, "such a careful man," and pressed the refund on me wrapped in more biscuits.

Each piece was a small kindness. Each piece landed like a stone.

I took to sitting on the survey boat in the evenings, going through his chart case, learning his shorthand properly now instead of in emergencies. *Dot-dash-circle* meant safe water. *Triangle-with-tail* meant scree that moved. There was a symbol I'd seen on the shortcut map, the karst one that had nearly eaten Cyrus — a square with a bite taken out of it — and I'd assumed it meant sinkhole, and it did, but underneath, in letters so small I needed the magnifier, Father had written: *trust the limestone, mind the edges, it holds if you ask politely.*

He'd written instructions for talking to rock. Of course he had. I sat in the boat and laughed and cried at the same time, which is a horrible thing to do alone in a harbor at dusk, and then a shadow fell across the deck and it was Cyrus, holding two mugs of tea because he'd counted on finding me there.

"He really wrote 'ask politely,'" I said.

"He did."

"To rock."

"To rock." Cyrus handed me a mug and sat down on the thwart with the care of a man whose ribs remembered the moraine. "You know what I keep thinking? That he marked that shortcut knowing someone he loved might need it someday. He wasn't mapping for himself. He was mapping ahead."

"For me," I said.

"For you." Cyrus blew on his tea. "That's what the Provision taught me, if it taught me anything. Everything worth keeping gets carried forward. Maps. Seeds. Letters. People." He said *people* looking at the water, not at me, which was how he said the biggest things.

I leaned against his good arm. He let me. On the mountain he'd have gone rigid; in Saltmere he was learning, slowly, the way you learn a language by drowning in it.

* * *

The guardianship hearing was held in the customs office because Saltmere doesn't have a courthouse, just a room where the customs officer also handled marriages and complaints about fences. There were four people present: the customs officer, a magistrate who'd come over on the ferry, Cyrus, and me. Odile waited outside because she said courtrooms made her itch, but I saw her through the window the entire time, arms crossed, watching like a hawk watching a henhouse she personally intended to defend.

The magistrate asked Cyrus why he should be appointed guardian of a twelve-year-old girl.

Cyrus had prepared, I later learned, three pages of careful answers. He used none of them. He looked at the magistrate for a moment and said:

"Four months ago I would have sold this town to pay a debt. Then I met her, and she navigated us through a karst field, a blizzard, and my own worst instincts. She reads weather better than I do, she forgave someone who didn't deserve it, and she carries her father forward better than I've ever carried anything. I'm not applying to raise her, sir. I'm applying to keep up."

The magistrate looked at me. "Is that accurate?"

"He undersold the karst field," I said. "I saved his life twice."

"Twice?"

"The sinkhole and the stew incident."

"There was a stew incident?"

"She's exaggerating," said Cyrus.

"I am not. He put salt cod in a dessert."

The magistrate wrote something down. I like to believe it was *stew incident*. The guardianship was granted that afternoon, and outside, Odile clapped Cyrus on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him and said, "Congratulations, old man," in a voice that wobbled only slightly, and none of us mentioned the wobbling, because that's the deal you make with people you love: you notice, and you don't say.

That night we ate at the chandlery — Odile's rooms above it now, permanent, her guide's license reinstated by a licensing board that had read the *Gazette* and decided redemption was fashionable. She'd hung her old client list on the wall, crossed out, and beneath it pinned a fresh sheet with three names already booked for spring parties into the range. She cooked. It was edible, which by her standards was a triumph, and Cyrus said so, and she threw a bread roll at him, and I sat between them eating stew — proper stew, no dessert fraud — and thought: *this is the inheritance.* Not the vault. Not the key. This table, these three, this exact Tuesday.

Father had written *carry the rest*. I finally understood that *the rest* wasn't cargo. It was this.

* * *

One more thing happened before winter closed the harbor, and it's the thing I can't stop turning over.

A letter came, forwarded through the *Gazette*, postmarked from a university two thousand miles south. A professor of history, name of Ellery Vance, who had read the serialized testament and written with a question no journalist had thought to ask: the 1897 expedition's journals referenced *a second crest*, a partner expedition that had split off at the coast and gone north along a different range entirely. Eleven survivors had carried the Provision inland. But the journals mention, in passing, a twelfth member — the cartographer's assistant — who left the main party at Saltmere harbor with "a portion of the record" and was never heard from again.

Professor Vance wanted to know: had anyone ever searched?

I read the letter four times on the chandlery steps. The wind came off the water cold and smelling of kelp and winter coming. Below me the tide was going out, further out than usual, drawing back from the cliff line the way it had that first dusk — the way it had shown Cyrus a door.

I carried the letter inside and laid it on the table between the tea mugs and the chart case, and I said, "You're both going to want to sit down."

Odile looked at the letter. Cyrus looked at me. Outside, the bell over the door rang in the wind, and far out past the breakwater, the sea kept pulling back from something it had hidden for a hundred years — and I swear, on my father's maps, it looked like it was smiling.
