## 40. Next Tide

**Cyrus**

The harbor town had a way of pretending winter hadn't happened. By April the ice had gone out of the moorings, the chandlery had restocked its barrels of tar and rope, and the fishermen were already lying to each other about the size of the season's first catch. I stood at the window of the shack — *my* shack, though the deed still felt like a borrowed coat — and watched Quentin come up the dock road with a chart tube under her arm and the particular walk she'd perfected, the one that said *I have found something and I intend to make you find it too.*

I'd learned to read that walk the way I used to read weather off the water. It meant trouble. It also meant she was happy, and in the Dunmore-Oyelaran household those two conditions had never once arrived separately.

"Breakfast first," I called through the glass, though she couldn't hear me. Old habit. Talking to her through windows, through doors, through the general noise of her being twelve and certain — it had become the background music of my life, and I'd stopped minding that I was mostly accompaniment.

She banged the door open with her hip. "There's a ship in the outer roads."

"There's always a ship in the outer roads."

"Not like this one." She unrolled the chart on my table, weighting the corners with a tin of tea, a fuse box, a stone she'd decided was lucky and I'd decided not to argue with. "I was copying Papa's coastal survey for Odile — she wants the updated soundings for her spring clients — and I cross-referenced the wreck marks. The navy-sealed one. Yours."

"The one the navy doesn't discuss."

"The one the navy doesn't discuss," she agreed, tapping a spot off the northern point, "has a new mark beside it. Look at the crest."

I looked. I'd spent enough months staring at expedition crests that they'd started to feel like faces — the 1897 crest with its crossed ice-axe and lantern, the navy's anchor-and-shackle, Halloway's syndicate mark that looked, fittingly, like a hook. This one I didn't know. A compass rose, but the needle pointed *down*. Beneath it, in a hand too fine to be a clerk's: a date. Last autumn. And a single word in Latin that Quentin translated before I could ask.

"*Sub unda*," she said. "Under the wave."

"Cheerful."

"It's not a warning, Cyrus. It's an *invitation*. Look at the ink weight — it's a stamp, a proper one, pressed hard. Somebody with a seal came out here, dove or sent divers to your wreck, and left their mark on the chart the same way the '97 expedition marked their trailheads." She looked up at me, and I saw her father in her face for a moment — the cartographer's delight, the hunger for the blank space and what might fill it. "Somebody else went looking. Somebody else found something. And somebody else is *telling us about it*."

I made coffee I didn't want and drank it anyway. That was another thing the winter had taught me: that the rituals mattered more than the reasons for them. "The navy will have seen that mark."

"The navy sees everything. That's not the same as understanding it." She rolled the chart with the crisp economy Odile had drilled into her — corners square, crest outward, always — and fixed me with a look I suspected I'd worn on my own face in a mirror more than once this year. "You're going to say we should wait."

"I'm going to say we should *think*."

"That's what you say when you've already decided yes."

She had me there. I'd been deciding yes since the tide first went out beneath those cliffs and showed me a door. I was, I was beginning to accept, the kind of man who walked through doors. The kind who took the key out of the dead man's satchel. The kind who, given a testament instead of a treasure, had carried it down the mountain and given it away — every letter, every seed record, every name — because that was apparently what the Provision had been *for*, and I'd been the fool stubborn enough to do what it asked.

The town had read the testament. The town had wept over the letters and argued about the seeds and, in the way of small places, had quietly rearranged itself around the three of us. My debts had dissolved — not forgiven, exactly, but rendered irrelevant, the way a debt to a man who's famous for the right reasons stops being collectible. Vess Halloway's syndicate had folded like wet paper once the navy publicly lost interest in us, and Halloway herself had gone to ground somewhere south, minus her best climbers and, Odile said darkly, minus her sense of proportion. The navy had sent one officer, polite as a customs man, to shake my hand and say nothing at length.

And I had a ward. A daughter, if I was brave enough to use the word, which I was getting braver about. That was the fortune. That was the Provision, carried onward.

"Odile's at the chandlery," Quentin said, already repacking the chart. "She'll want to see this."

"Odile will want to see this and then tell us all the reasons it will kill us."

"Obviously. Then she'll start packing."

She was right, of course. She usually was, which I found less charming than she found satisfying. I looked out the window at the water — flat and bright and innocent as a lie — and felt the old pull in my chest, the diver's pull, the door-walker's pull. The wilderness forgets nothing. I'd written that in the margin of the testament's copy, the one we kept. I hadn't known, then, how soon it would call in the debt.

* * *

**Quentin**

Odile read the crest the way she read weather: fast, twice, and with her jaw set.

"Compass pointing down," she said. "Latin. Recent stamp." She set the chart on the chandlery counter, next to the coils of rope she'd been inventorying for a client who paid late and complained early. "That's either a grave marker or a doorbell."

"It's a trailhead," I said.

"It's *trouble* with a trailhead's haircut." But her thumb was already moving along the coastline, measuring distances the way she did — by eye, by knuckle, by years of walking — and I watched her arrive, against her own better judgment, at the same place Cyrus and I had already been standing for an hour. She was easier to read now than she'd been in the foothills. The winter had done that. You can't share a storm-cave and a starving month with two people and come out of it a stranger.

"The wreck site's a two-day sail in decent weather," she said. "Three in bad. It's April, so: three."

"Papa's survey has a cove on the north point we could anchor in," I said. "Sheltered from the northwest. I've been over the soundings — there's a shelf at forty feet, right where the wreck mark sits."

Odile looked at me for a long moment. I knew what she was seeing: not a twelve-year-old with a chart tube, but the daughter of Étienne Vasseur, the cartographer she'd turned back on, the man she'd spent a year and a half trying to stop being. She'd told us the truth in the waystation with the storm hammering the door, and I'd hated her for it, and then the Red Stair had happened, and her hands had happened, and somewhere in the bleeding and the rope-burn and the sheer stubborn fact of her coming back for us, the hating had worn through to something else. Not forgiveness exactly. Forgiveness sounded finished. This was more like — a rope we'd both tied and both trusted.

"You've been over the soundings," she repeated. "Of course you have." She rubbed her scarred hands together — she did that when she was deciding — and then, because Odile Ferran had never once in her life done anything the easy way: "I'll need to close out my spring bookings."

"Both of them?" Cyrus asked, appearing in the chandlery doorway with the timing of a man who'd learned that the important conversations happened when he wasn't invited.

"Both of them. One's a bishop. He'll survive." She rolled the chart and handed it back to me, handle first, like passing a torch. "We go as a survey party. Legitimate papers, legitimate business, updating coastal charts for the hydrographic office — which, conveniently, now owes us favors. Nobody dives, nobody pries, nobody so much as *swims* near that wreck until I've looked at the approaches with my own eyes."

"And if the approaches look bad?" Cyrus asked.

"Then we look bad right along with them and come home." She said it flat, but her eyes went to me on the last word, and I understood what she was actually saying, which was: *I have lost one Vasseur on a mountain and I will not lose the other one to the sea.* Some things you don't say out loud in a chandlery. Some things you just let sit in the rope smell and the tar smell, between people, doing their quiet work.

"I'll get Papa's boat ready," I said. "The *Meridian*."

"The *Meridian*," Odile said, "hasn't floated in two years."

"She floated when it mattered." I slung the chart tube over my shoulder. "She'll manage."

* * *

**Cyrus**

Packing for a salvage run and packing for whatever this was turned out to be the same job, which told me something about my life that I chose not to examine too closely. Rope, lamps, the good knives. Quentin's father's field kit, which lived on a shelf in my shack now like a saint's bones — I'd offered it back to her a dozen times and she'd refused a dozen times, saying it was better kept at a base camp than in a girl's room, and I'd stopped arguing because I understood. It wasn't equipment to her. It was a way of being near him.

I was coiling line by the door when I noticed the brass key where I'd left it on the mantel, and stopped.

I'd meant to be rid of it. After the summit, after the vault, I'd genuinely believed the story was over — key returned to its mechanism, testament carried down, curse expired like a bad contract. But the two halves had come apart when the navy's people opened the door, and in the confusion of that last morning on the Hollow Summit, one half had come home with us in Quentin's pocket, and neither of us had ever quite gotten around to doing anything about it. It sat on the mantel between a candle stub and a piece of red Stair rock, and some nights I caught myself looking at it the way you look at an old scar. Not proud of it. Not sorry either. Just — aware of it. Aware that it had cost a dead man everything and me nearly the same, and that it had given me a daughter, which no honest ledger could balance.

Quentin came in while I was staring at it.

"We should leave it," she said. She'd followed my eyes without seeming to. She did that. "On the mantel. When we go."

"You think?"

"It's not ours anymore. It's the *story's*." She took it down anyway, turned it over in her small capable hands, and I watched her decide — the way her father must have decided, the way I'd decided in a flooding cave a year ago — and then she set it back exactly where it had been. "We'll know where it is. That's enough. You don't carry the last key into the next door. That's how curses get second lives."

"Where do you learn this stuff?"

"From a disgraced guide and a bad liar," she said, "who between them make one almost-responsible adult," and she went to help Odile with the boat, and I stood there in my shack with the key on the mantel and the tide going out in the harbor, and I laughed, alone, like a man who'd finally gotten the joke.

* * *

**Odile**

The *Meridian* floated. I will grant her that, and nothing more. Her seams wept a little, her compass had opinions, and her previous owner's rigging knots were the work of a man who trusted rope the way poets trust language — beautifully, and not enough. I re-tied every one of them while the other two loaded stores, and if my hands ached doing it, well. My hands had been aching since the Red Stair and had earned the right.

Étienne's daughter worked the foredeck like she'd been born there, which in every way that counted she had. She'd marked the chart with his shorthand — I'd learned to read it over the winter, letter by letter, the way you learn a dead man's handwriting — and the route north was annotated in that fine, certain script: soundings, hazards, the cove. He'd surveyed these waters the year he died. He'd stood on this deck. Some mornings I caught myself looking for him in her gestures and finding him, and I had stopped flinching from it. That was the winter's other gift, though I'd never say so out loud. You don't say soft things in a chandlery. You say them on a mountain, in a storm, when it's too late to be embarrassed — or you say them never, and let the rope do the talking.

Cyrus came down the dock with the last crate and the expression he wore when he was about to say something he thought was wise.

"We're a survey party," he said, "with legitimate papers."

"We are."

"Carrying diving gear."

"Legitimate survey parties carry diving gear. It's in the regulations. I wrote to the hydrographic office."

"You wrote to the — Odile, did you actually forge correspondence with the navy?"

"I wrote *to* them. Whether they wrote back is between me and my conscience." I threw him the mooring line, because the surest way to end one of his moral crises is to give his hands something to do. He caught it, naturally. He was, I'd finally admitted somewhere around the glacier, the best man I'd ever guided — not the most careful, not the wisest, but the one who, when the rope came taut, held. There were worse things to build a family out of.

Quentin took the tiller. The tide was on the turn, the harbor bar showing its teeth, and the wind stood fair out of the southwest. She brought us out through the moorings the way her father must have brought her out a hundred times — easy, exact, unhurried — and Odile's old grief and Odile's new hope sat down together in the stern sheets and, for once, didn't argue.

The town fell away behind us. The northern point stood up ahead, gray and patient, and somewhere beneath the water off its shoulder lay a wreck the navy wouldn't name, and beside it, on a chart in a twelve-year-old's careful hand, a crest with its needle pointing down.

* * *

**Quentin**

We made the open water by noon. Papa's compass settled, the *Meridian* found her stride, and Cyrus went forward to stand the first watch the way he did everything — like a man paying off a debt to no one in particular, cheerfully, forever.

I unrolled the chart across the cabin table and looked at the crest a long time. Compass pointing down. *Under the wave.* Somebody, somewhere, had gone looking the way we'd gone looking, had found something the way we'd found something, and had pressed their seal into a chart and left it where a cartographer's daughter would trip over it — because the wilderness forgets nothing, and apparently neither do the people who walk through its doors.

Papa had believed that. It was the last thing he'd written to me: *The country keeps its accounts, Quentin. Every trail walked is a trail owed.* I used to think it meant the mountain collects. Now I think it means the opposite — that the country *pays out*, patient as tide, to whoever keeps showing up. It had paid me a father's map, a mother's temper, two people who'd walked into my life through a wreck and a storm and stayed. It had paid Cyrus a family he'd stopped believing he was allowed. It had paid Odile a debt she'd have traded her other hands to settle.

I came up on deck. The wind was fair, the water was bright, and the northern point was a dark line on the horizon with our name on it — or somebody's name, some dead expedition's name, some door waiting to be a door.

"Two days," Odile said beside me, reading the water.

"Two days," I said, and marked it on the chart, in Papa's shorthand, in my own hand: the date, the heading, and the crest copied small and careful at the edge of the known world.

The needle pointed down. The tide was going out.

And somewhere under the wave, patient as everything, the next door was already opening.
