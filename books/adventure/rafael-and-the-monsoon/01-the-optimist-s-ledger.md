## 1. The Optimist's Ledger

The Bay of Bengal in June was a liar with a beautiful face. From the wheelhouse of the *Tern*, the sea looked like hammered pewter, calm as a church floor, and Wendell Ashgrove did not trust it for a second. He had learned the ocean's grammar the hard way, syllable by syllable, and it always said one thing before it said another.

He sat at the chart table with a pencil, a grease-stained notebook, and the ledger — the other ledger, the one that lived in his chest and never closed. The notebook was just where he wrote down what the one in his chest already knew.

Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six. That was the number this morning. Yesterday it had been forty-one thousand, one hundred and ninety. The difference was not work he had done or fuel he had burned. The difference was simply the sun rising, the calendar turning, the patient arithmetic of the Vane Syndicate doing what it did while men slept. Interest, the collectors called it, in the same warm voices they used to ask after your health.

He wrote the number at the top of a fresh page. Under it, he wrote what the *Tern* owed the world: Dov's deferred engine overhaul, four hundred liters of diesel on credit at the Chittagong fuel dock, Priya's wages three weeks late, Sefa's wages five. He wrote what the world owed the *Tern*, which was a shorter column, and then he closed the notebook because the second column was a form of self-harm.

"You're doing the thing again," Sefa said from the helm.

Sefa navigated the way other people prayed — quietly, constantly, and with total conviction that someone was listening. She stood with one hand on the wheel and one on the radar hood, a small woman with a face like a closed fist, and she could read a squall line at eighty kilometers the way illiterates read faces.

"I'm doing math," Wendell said.

"You're doing penance. Math is when the numbers change."

Wendell almost smiled. He looked out at the water instead. The *Tern* was fifty years old if she was a day, a converted survey trawler with a rusted shoulder line and a wheelhouse that whistled in crosswinds like a kettle deciding whether to sing. Her hull was patched in six places with plates Dov had welded himself, and every one of those plates was a promise Wendell had made and not yet kept. She deserved a yard. She deserved new plating and a hull survey and a crew paid on time. She deserved, in short, everything the Syndicate's number made impossible.

"Intercept window opens in ninety minutes," Sefa said. "Cell's building off the coast. The model says it'll make landfall south of Cox's Bazar, but the model has been wrong twice this season and the fishermen are already running for the shallows, which means the fishermen know something the model doesn't."

"Course?"

"Two-six-five. We'll cut across the cell's leading edge, drop the sensors on the north flank, and be drinking tea before it's over your head." She glanced at him. "Routine."

"Say it again."

"Routine," she said, and they both almost laughed, because on the *Tern* the word *routine* was a superstition you spoke aloud precisely so the sea could hear you being careless and decide to spare you.

Wendell went out on the deck to check the sensor array himself. This was not necessary — Dov had checked it twice — but his hands needed work the way other men's hands needed drink. The deck was hot and sticky under his boots, salt-glazed, humming faintly with the engines. The air smelled of brine and diesel and the green, metallic promise of distant rain. Out on the horizon, the sky had begun to assemble itself: a bruise-colored wall rising out of the southwest, tall as a cathedral, lit from inside by its own weather.

He had chased storms for eleven years. He had started because he was good at it, continued because it paid, and now did it because stopping meant standing still, and standing still meant the number in the notebook had nothing to hide behind. Motion was a kind of shelter. As long as the *Tern* was under way, as long as there was a cell to intercept, a client to invoice, a hull to patch, the debt was a thing that lived in a book instead of a thing that lived in his hands.

His brother had understood that about him. Tomas used to say, *You run toward weather because it's the only thing that chases you honestly.* Tomas had died three years ago off a research vessel in the closed corridor — the monsoon exclusion zone the shipping consortiums paid good money to keep empty of witnesses — and the sea had returned nothing of him but a life ring and a question Wendell still carried like ballast.

The radio crackled.

"*Tern*, *Tern*, this is Meridian Point Control, do you copy?"

Wendell knew the voice before he knew the call sign. Mr. Ovid Ren. The Syndicate's collector for this stretch of coast, a man who had never raised his voice on the air in four years of reminders, who spoke the way a tide comes in — without malice, without hurry, and with total confidence that the land would eventually surrender.

Wendell wiped his hands on his trousers and keyed the mic. "Copy, Meridian Point. Go ahead."

"Mr. Ashgrove. Good morning. Beautiful water today, isn't it? I was looking at your file this morning — I do that on Sundays, it's almost a hobby — and I noticed your balance has crossed the forty-one mark. Congratulations. That's persistence." A pause, warm as bathwater. "I also noticed you're running an intercept today. Storm work. Good, honest money. I hope you'll consider applying a meaningful portion toward principal this month. The interest, you understand, is not something I control. It's like your weather. It simply happens."

"Ren." Wendell kept his voice level. "You called at six in the morning to tell me it's raining somewhere."

"I called because I like you, Wendell. That's the truth, whatever you think of me. I like you, and I like your crew, and I would hate to see any of you have a very bad year." The warmth thinned by one degree, no more. "The Syndicate is patient. But patience is not the same as optimism. We are not optimistic about you. Do you understand the difference? A patient man waits. An optimistic man expects. We have stopped expecting."

The words sat in Wendell's chest like swallowed ice.

"I'll wire what I can," he said.

"You always do. That's why we're all still friends. Enjoy your storm, Mr. Ashgrove. Mind the squall line — the fishermen are nervous today, and they're usually right."

The channel clicked dead. Wendell stood holding the mic a moment longer, looking at the wall of weather on the horizon, and understood that Ren had just said, in the politest possible dialect, *we are considering whether you are worth more alive or settled.*

* * *

By noon the leading edge had found them. The wind arrived first as a rumor in the rigging, then as a fact — thirty knots, forty, the *Tern* leaning into it with a groan like an old man rising from a chair. Rain came in horizontal sheets that turned the world to gray glass. Sefa drove them along the squall's northern shoulder with the calm of a woman threading a needle in an earthquake, and Dov, who was built like a boiler and moved like one lighting, rode the pitching deck to the sensor mast and lashed down the last of the drop buoys with wire and fury and one hand.

Priya was in the wheelhouse doorway, soaked through, grinning like the storm was a carnival. She was twenty-four and the only person aboard who had chosen this life rather than been cornered by it. "Data's streaming clean!" she shouted over the wind. "Client's going to love this cell. It's a beauty."

"It's a monster," Wendell said.

"It's a paycheck," she said, and ducked out into the rain before he could explain the difference.

He watched her go and felt the old dread shift in its sleep. The intercept fee was four thousand. Four thousand against forty-one thousand was a bucket against a flood, but it was a bucket, and you bailed because bailing was what kept the boat afloat, and the boat was what kept you from finding out what the water wanted.

The cell passed over them in the early afternoon — a half hour of roaring dark, of rain like gravel, of the *Tern* climbing green water and sliding down the far side with her plates singing. Then it was behind them, stalking inland to frighten the coast, and the sun came out the way it does after monsoon weather, shameless and golden, as if nothing had happened.

Wendell stood in the wheelhouse with a towel around his neck and did the arithmetic of survival. Fuel burned: six hundred liters. Sensor fee, when the client paid: four thousand. Net against the number: a rounding error. He wrote it down anyway. The writing was the ritual. The ritual was the wall between him and the water.

Sefa appeared beside him with tea, strong and oversweetened, and put it in his hand without asking.

"Ren called," she said. It wasn't a question; she'd heard it on the open channel, they all had.

"Ren called."

"He said they've stopped expecting."

"He said that."

Sefa looked out the window at the bright, treacherous sea. "Wendell. There's a boat coming up our wake. Been holding station astern for an hour, just inside radar shadow of the cell. White hull, too clean for fishing. I don't like her lines."

He raised the binoculars. The vessel astern was maybe three kilometers out, running their wake with the easy confidence of something that had nowhere else to be. Too white. Too new. Too patient.

"Could be a patrol," Wendell said.

"Could be," Sefa said. "Or it could be an optimist."

The radio crackled before he could answer. Not Ren's warm tide-voice this time, but a woman's voice, clipped and dry and familiar in a way that made the hair on his forearms stand up — a voice he had last heard across a salvage auction floor in Colombo, five years and one dead brother ago.

"*Tern*, this is vessel *Kestrel*. Wendell Ashgrove, I know you're listening, because listening is the only thing you do for free. This is Imara Kess. I have a job for you. You're going to say no." A pause, precisely measured. "I'm going to dock beside you anyway."
