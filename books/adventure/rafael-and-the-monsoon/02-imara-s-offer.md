## 2. Imara's Offer

The harbor at Chittagong smelled of diesel and brine and the sweet rot of fish left too long in the sun, and Wendell Ashgrove had long ago stopped noticing any of it as anything but home. He sat in the Tern's wheelhouse with a ledger open on his knee — not a ship's log but the other kind, the kind that kept him awake at night — running numbers he already knew by heart.

Fourteen thousand, two hundred and six. That was what remained of his debt to the Vane Syndicate after three years of storm-chasing, salvage scraps, and one disastrous season hauling contraband mangoes up the Arakan coast that he preferred not to think about. Fourteen thousand, two hundred and six, compounding monthly at a rate no honest bank would dare print. The collector on the radio last week had said it almost fondly, the way you'd mention weather to a farmer: *Mr. Ashgrove, the interest this quarter came to more than your last two jobs combined. We'd hate for the arithmetic to outrun you.*

Wendell had thanked him. That was the worst part — thanking them. Gratitude as survival.

Outside, the monsoon season was still a rumor, a bruise gathering on the southern horizon, but the air had that thick, expectant weight it always carried before the Bay of Bengal made up its mind. Sefa was below deck re-plotting their intercept routes for the week. Dov had the engine apart again, swearing at it in three languages with genuine affection. Priya was on the foredeck coiling lines with the fierce precision of someone who needed her hands busy or her thoughts would wander somewhere expensive.

It was a good crew. It was a crew he could lose everything for. Which was why the numbers mattered, and why he hated them.

He was halfway through recalculating the fuel costs of a run out toward Sandwip when he heard the hull knock against theirs — a soft, deliberate bump, the nautical equivalent of a hand on the shoulder from someone who wanted you to know they could have hit harder.

He looked up. A sleek gray ketch had slid alongside the Tern without so much as an engine cough, silent as a shark cruising in to inspect a wounded thing. Her name was painted on the bow in white letters gone soft with salt: *Kestrel*. And standing at her rail, arms crossed, hair cropped short and silvering at the temples in defiance of every photograph Wendell had ever seen of her, was Imara Kess.

"Of all the harbors," Wendell said through the wheelhouse glass, "in all the Bay."

"You're predictable, Ashgrove." Her voice carried across the gap between the hulls the way it always had — level, unhurried, a voice that had never once needed to rise. "Cheap moorage, bad coffee, proximity to storms. You haven't changed."

"You have. The hair suits you."

"The hair is what happens when you spend nine years trying to beat me to wrecks." She stepped onto her gunwale, balanced there like she'd been born on rails, and swung across to his deck without waiting for an invitation. Imara Kess had never waited for an invitation in her life. "I need a word. Not on the radio. Not in front of your crew."

"They're my crew. They hear everything."

"Then say yes first, and let them hear something worth hearing."

* * *

They ended up in the wheelhouse anyway, because Wendell refused to leave his own boat, and because Priya drifted close enough to the door to be technically present while pretending to inventory flares. Imara unrolled a chart on the chart table and weighted its corners with a brass compass, a mug of Dov's terrible coffee, and Wendell's own ledger, which she picked up, glanced at, and set back down with an expression he chose not to interpret.

"Triple rate," she said. "Plus hazard pay. Plus I cover your fuel both ways."

"For what?"

"A salvage job. One wreck. In and out inside a week."

"Where?"

She turned the chart toward him, and Wendell felt the floor of the world tilt slightly, the way it did when a swell came from a direction the wind hadn't told you about.

The coordinates were inked in red, circled twice. Nineteen degrees, forty-one minutes north. Eighty-eight degrees, twelve minutes east. He knew those numbers. He knew them the way you know a scar — not by looking at it, but by the way it aches when the weather changes.

"That's inside the corridor," he said quietly. "The closed corridor. The exclusion zone has been up for six years. No commercial traffic, no salvage permits, nothing. The Navy boards anything that crosses that line."

"I'm aware of the line."

"Then you're aware I can't take this."

"I'm aware you haven't said no yet." She leaned against the bulkhead, watching him with those flat dark eyes that had unnerved him since they were both twenty-something and racing each other to a freighter on fire off Cox's Bazar. "You looked at those coordinates like a man reading his own obituary, Wendell. So before you say no, tell me why."

"There's nothing to tell."

"There's everything to tell. I've done my research. Six years ago a survey vessel went down near that position during an early cyclone. Eleven souls aboard. Ten recovered." She let the silence do its work. "One not."

Priya had stopped pretending to count flares. Wendell could feel her attention on his back like sunlight.

"The Meridian," he said. The name came out of him like water from a cracked hull. "Her name was the Meridian. Hydrographic survey ship. My brother Elias was her chief scientist." He straightened the chart, though it didn't need straightening. His hands needed something to do. "They lost her in a squall that came out of a clear sky. The Navy called it a rogue cell. Nobody dives there now because nobody's allowed to, and nobody's allowed to because the Navy says the currents are unsafe, which is true, and because the Syndicate asked them nicely, which is also true, though nobody says that part out loud."

Imara said nothing. She was good at that — letting you hang yourself with your own rope, then handing you more rope.

"Why do you want her?" Wendell asked. "There's no cargo worth triple rate down there. Survey ships don't carry treasure. Whatever instruments survived six years of saltwater aren't worth the fuel to lift them."

"What if I told you the Meridian wasn't sunk by weather?"

The wheelhouse seemed to get smaller. Outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"Elias radioed a mayday," Wendell said slowly. "I've heard the recording. Squall, flooding, going down by the stern. There were eleven people on that ship, Imara. Weather doesn't need a motive."

"No. But men do." She tapped the circled coordinates once, lightly, like knocking on a door she expected to be answered. "I've spent two years pulling threads on this, and every thread ends at the same place: the Vane Syndicate's shipping manifests from six years ago. Cargo that left Singapore and never arrived anywhere. Insurance claims paid on vessels that were never lost. And a survey ship that happened to be mapping the seafloor right where the Syndicate's ghost fleet liked to scuttle things — until she stopped mapping."

"You're saying they sank her."

"I'm saying the storm was real, and convenient, and possibly helped along. I'm saying there are people alive today who would very much prefer the Meridian stays where she is. And I'm saying I want to raise what she's holding, and I want the best storm crew on this coast to get me there and back — which means you, God help me, because you're reckless enough to cross that line and stubborn enough to come back."

"And if the Syndicate finds out—"

"They already know I've been asking questions. Why do you think I need a crew? I need witnesses who sail under a different flag than mine." She smiled, and it was the old smile, the rival's smile, sharp and fond at once. "Also, frankly, I need you. Don't make me say it twice; I'll deny it later."

Wendell looked down at the chart. The red circle sat there like a wound that had never been allowed to close. Six years he had stayed away from those coordinates — not because of the Navy's line, though he'd been grateful for the excuse, but because some part of him believed Elias was still down there, and going would mean finally knowing, and not going meant Elias could remain forever *lost*, which was a softer word than *dead*.

Triple rate. Fuel covered. Hazard pay. Enough to cut the debt nearly in half in a single run. Maybe enough, with a second job after, to end it entirely — to walk into the Vane office and slide the ledger across the desk and be, for the first time in three years, a free man.

And the price was sailing into the exact patch of ocean where his brother's voice had crackled out of a radio and then dissolved into static.

"No," he said.

Imara didn't move. "Wendell—"

"No. You can find another crew. Take the Kestrel in yourself if you're so sure."

"She draws too deep for the shallows near the wreck site, and I don't trust anyone else to handle a squall in that corridor, and neither do you." Her voice stayed level, but something underneath it shifted, tectonic. "You think I came here to play games? I came here because this matters. Because whatever's on that ship will prove what the Syndicate did — to my family, maybe to yours. You want to spend the rest of your life paying them interest? Or do you want to know what they actually took from you?"

"I want my crew alive. That's the whole of my ambition these days." He began rolling the chart closed. "I'm sorry, Imara. Genuinely. Find another madman."

For a long moment she just looked at him, and he saw her deciding something behind those eyes, some calculation she'd carried across the whole Bay and was now, reluctantly, abandoning.

"All right," she said. "No."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She stepped back from the table. "Because I'm leaving the chart."

"Imara—"

"It stays aboard your boat whether you take the job or not. That's not pressure, Ashgrove. That's just physics. Paper doesn't un-print itself." She moved to the wheelhouse door, then paused, one hand on the frame, and didn't turn around. "Elias was the smarter brother. Everyone always said so. But you're the one still breathing, and I've never figured out if that's luck or stubbornness, and honestly I don't care which. When you change your mind — and you will, because grief is patient and so am I — you know where the Kestrel moors."

She crossed the gap between the hulls without looking back, and the gray ketch slid away from the Tern as silently as she'd arrived, her wake barely wrinkling the harbor water.

Wendell stood over the chart table for a long time. The red circle gazed up at him. Nineteen forty-one north. Eighty-eight twelve east. He could feel the coordinates sinking into him like cold water into a boot, finding every low place.

Behind him, Priya cleared her throat. "Captain? What was that about?"

He should have rolled the chart up and locked it in the drawer. He should have thrown it overboard. Instead he stood there, and the harbor light fell across the paper, and somewhere south of them the horizon was quietly assembling a storm.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing at all."

But he didn't roll it up. And when Sefa came up the companionway an hour later with fresh intercept plots in her hand, she found the chart spread open on the table, and her captain staring at it, and she was smart enough — she was always smart enough — to say nothing at all.
