## 3. Crew of Small Mutinies

Sefa had learned to read a crew the way she read weather: by pressure, by the small telltale shifts that preceded something breaking. And tonight, in the galley of the *Tern*, the pressure was falling fast.

She stood at the chart table with her back to the bulkhead, pretending to correct the log, and watched the three of them orbit each other around the little table where Wendell usually sat. He'd gone up top after Imara Kess's boat pulled away — gone to stand at the rail like a man guarding a grave — and the moment his boots left the deck ladder, the galley changed. It filled. It hummed. It became, quietly and without anyone saying so, a mutiny.

Dov sat with his bad arm cradled against his chest, the way he always did now, as if he could hold the pain in place through sheer stubbornness. The shoulder had been coming apart for eight months — a torn rotator cuff from when the winch cable snapped off Chittagong, grinding bone on bone every time he reached above his heart. Sefa had watched him work one-handed through two intercepts, watched him wedge himself into the engine bay at angles no spine should make because he couldn't lift the panel cover anymore. Tonight there was a bottle of cheap analgesics on the table beside his elbow, half empty.

Priya stood at the stove even though nothing was cooking. She did that when she was working up to something — kept her hands busy so her mouth couldn't be accused of idleness. The steam from the kettle curled against the low ceiling and hung there, trapped, like everything else on this boat.

"So," Dov said. "Are we going to talk about it, or are we going to keep pretending the chart fell out of Kess's pocket by accident?"

Nobody answered. Sefa kept her pen moving over the log, writing numbers that meant nothing.

"Because I looked at it," Dov went on. "While you were all being very careful not to look at it. Triple rate. Triple, Priya. You know what that is? That's my shoulder. That's a surgeon in Colombo who doesn't laugh when I ask about payment plans."

"You don't know what it is," Priya said, without turning. "That's the point of it. Nobody offers triple rate for anything honest."

"It's salvage."

"It's salvage inside a closed corridor." She turned then, and Sefa saw the exhaustion on her face, the kind that sleep didn't touch. Two years running debt for the Syndicate's collectors, standing on deck while Wendell negotiated interest rates against storm windows — Priya had aged a decade in the time Sefa had known her. "It's salvage inside water the government closed for a reason, offered by a woman who docked beside us unannounced at midnight, and nobody thinks that's strange? Nobody wonders how she knew exactly where to find us?"

"She's known us ten years," Dov said.

"That's what worries me."

Sefa set down her pen. The sound of it clicking against the table was small, but all three heads turned toward it anyway, the way sailors turn toward any new noise in a quiet sea.

"You're both asking the wrong question," she said.

"Oh good," Dov muttered. "The navigator has an opinion."

"The wrong question is whether the job pays." Sefa came forward and laid Imara's chart on the table between them, smoothing it flat with both hands. The coordinates sat there in Imara's precise hand, circled twice: a position in the heart of the forbidden corridor, dead center of the monsoon's favorite hunting ground. "The right question is why those coordinates."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean I've been navigating these waters since before any of you owned sea legs." She tapped the circle once. "I know this stretch of ocean the way you know your own kitchen. And I'm telling you — I've seen that position before. Not on a chart. On a manifest. Eleven years ago, when I was still working ferry routes out of Cox's Bazar, we used to run cargo manifests past it for ballast records. There was a ship lost out there. The *Meridian*."

The name dropped into the galley and sank straight through the floor.

Dov went still. Even Priya stopped moving, kettle forgotten, steam rising past her face.

"You know whose ship that was," Sefa said quietly. It wasn't a question. Everyone aboard knew; it was the one thing Wendell never spoke of, the wound the whole crew navigated around the way they navigated around shoals. "You know who died near it. You know who *didn't* die near it, and what that did to him."

"Wendell will say no," Priya said.

"Wendell already said no," Dov corrected. "That's the problem. He said no, and Kess left the chart anyway, and now it's sitting on our table like a lit fuse."

"And you want to light it behind his back."

"I want my arm back," Dov said, and his voice cracked on it, just slightly — the first honest sound he'd made all night. "I want to be able to lift my daughter when I get home. I want to stop taking pills that make the engine room swim. Triple rate is six months' work in one run. One run, Sefa. In and out before the season peaks. Kess wouldn't have picked the window if the window weren't real."

"Kess picks whatever window the Syndicate tells her to pick," Priya shot back. "You think she found us by accident? You think she doesn't know exactly what that wreck means to him? She dangled his brother's grave in front of him and called it a job offer. That's not business. That's bait."

"Then it's good bait," Dov said. "Because it caught all of us, didn't it? Look at yourselves. We're arguing about a job our captain already refused, at one in the morning, instead of sleeping. If we were really going to let it go, we'd have thrown that chart overboard hours ago."

Silence again. The *Tern* creaked around them, her old hull talking in its sleep, and somewhere below, the bilge pump cycled on with its tired mechanical cough. Sefa looked down at the chart and felt the weight of it — paper, nothing but paper, and yet heavier than anything else aboard.

She thought about Wendell up on the rail. She thought about the ledger he kept in the cabin beneath the chart table, the one with the Syndicate's numbers in it, the debt that grew like coral in the dark whether he moved or stayed still. She thought about how he'd stood at that same table six months ago and told them, in his flat, tired voice, that if the debt ever endangered the crew, they should jump ship at the next friendly port and not look back. And how none of them had jumped. How none of them ever would.

That was the thing about a crew like theirs. They weren't bound by contract or flag or blood. They were bound by the accumulated weight of small mercies — Dov fixing the heater in Priya's bunk three winters running, Priya sitting up with Sefa through the fever in the Andamans, Wendell selling his own instruments to cover their wages the month the Syndicate froze their accounts. You didn't abandon people you owed like that. You just didn't.

And that, Sefa thought, was exactly what someone like Imara Kess understood about them. Whoever sent her — and Sefa no longer believed for a second that Kess acted alone — whoever sent her knew that the *Tern* wasn't a boat. She was a web. Pull one strand and the whole structure trembles.

"We should vote," Priya said suddenly.

Dov blinked. "Vote? On what?"

"On whether we take it to him. Whether we put it on the table in daylight and make him look at it with all of us standing there — or whether we drop it. Overboard. Tonight. Done." She wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture Sefa recognized as armor. "Because if we do this, we do it together or not at all. No half-mutinies. Half-mutinies sink ships faster than storms."

"A vote," Dov repeated, and laughed, short and bitter. "Fine. Fine. All in favor of giving the captain the chart and letting him decide like an adult."

He raised his good hand. His eyes went to Sefa.

Priya didn't raise hers. She folded her arms. "All in favor of dropping it overboard and staying alive."

Her eyes went to Sefa too.

And there it was. Sefa stood between them with the chart under her palms and felt the full, terrible arithmetic of the moment: two people she loved, split clean down the fault line of their own needs, both waiting on her the way sailors wait on a wind shift. Dov's hand hovering in the lamplight. Priya's arms crossed like a locked door. The *Tern* breathing around them. Somewhere overhead, Wendell's footsteps crossed the deck and paused — just paused, long enough to notice — before moving on.

Everyone compromised already, she thought. Everyone already bought and paid for, by debt or damage or devotion. The only question left was which way the fracture ran.

If she voted with Dov, they'd take the job — or at least force Wendell to look at it, and Sefa had watched Wendell Ashgrove look at things connected to his brother before, and she knew there was no force on that man stronger than grief wearing a work order. They'd sail into the corridor. They might die there. But Dov would get his surgery, and Priya might get her way out, and Wendell might get — God help them all — whatever it was he needed more than money.

If she voted with Priya, they'd stay safe and small and slowly drowning, running intercepts until the Syndicate's interest ate them alive, until Dov's arm froze solid and Priya's two years became twenty. Safe the way a fish in a closing net is safe.

Sefa looked at the chart. At the coordinates. At the small, neat circle Imara Kess had drawn around a place where a ship had gone down and a family had broken.

Then she lifted the chart off the table, rolled it tight, and held it against her chest.

"Dawn," she said. "We take it to him at dawn. All four of us, on deck, in the open, where he can't hide from it and neither can we." She met Dov's eyes, then Priya's. "That's my vote. Not yes, not no. Just — no more deciding things in the dark. Whatever we do next, we do it in the light, together, or we deserve whatever the sea does to us."

Dov exhaled slowly and lowered his hand. Priya unfolded her arms, and something in her shoulders loosened — not agreement, but surrender to the process, which on the *Tern* amounted to nearly the same thing.

"Dawn," Priya echoed. "And if he says no again?"

"Then we drop it overboard," Sefa said. "And I'll throw it myself."

But even as she said it, she was climbing the companionway steps ahead of them, out into the wet dark, where Wendell stood at the rail with his back to them — and where, far off across the black water, a single white light burned steady at the edge of sight, right where the corridor began.

It hadn't been there an hour ago.

It was getting closer.
