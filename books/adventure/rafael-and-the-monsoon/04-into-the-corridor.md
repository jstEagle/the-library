## 4. Into the Corridor

The exclusion line was a pencil stroke on Sefa's chart, thin as a hair pulled from a head, and crossing it felt like that too — a small snap, barely felt, that would ache for days.

Wendell stood at the Tern's bow rail at 04:40 with the engine's heartbeat in his boots and the sky the color of a bruise coming up through skin. Not storm-colored yet. Storm-colored was honest; it shouted what it meant. This was the color of something deciding whether to hurt you, and Wendell had spent enough years around weather to know that indecision in a sky was worse than any forecast. The clouds to the east were stacked like wet slate, layer on layer, and beneath them the sea ran flat and oily, holding its breath.

Behind him, the deck was quiet in the way he'd asked for. Dov had the engines humming at a pitch only she could love — low, steady, a little sulky. Priya coiled line at the rail with the exaggerated calm of someone who had voted for this and was now determined not to regret it out loud. Sefa had the helm and the chart and the small mutinous set of her jaw that meant she'd take them wherever the coordinates said, consequences be damned, because she'd cast the vote and she would not flinch from her own arithmetic.

Three against one, taken behind his back. He'd come up on deck at midnight and found them huddled over Imara Kess's chart like conspirators in a bad play, and instead of rage he'd felt something worse: relief. Someone else had made the decision. Someone else had chosen the corridor, the triple rate, the wreck his brother had died within sight of. He could be dragged into his own grief instead of walking there alone.

So here they were, forty miles past where any sane insurance policy ended, running east into the closed water at dawn.

"Line," Sefa said, without turning. "Crossing now."

Wendell looked down as if he might see it — some seam in the water, a stitch where the permitted world ended. There was nothing, of course. Just the same green-black swell sliding under the hull, the same salt smell, the same gulls wheeling off astern as if even they had read the notices. The Bay of Bengal did not care about exclusion corridors. Men had drawn those lines after the Meridian went down, after three salvage crews lost boats and one lost everything, and the Syndicate had been first in line to sign the petition closing it — which, Wendell understood now, standing at the rail with his brother's ghost riding shotgun, should have told him everything.

"You're doing the thing," Priya said beside him. She'd drifted to the rail without him noticing, coil of line over her shoulder like a soldier's bedroll. "The staring-at-the-horizon thing."

"I'm navigating."

"You're brooding with nautical intent."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Keep your eyes on the water, Priya."

"The water's fine. The water's the only thing out here that isn't lying to us." She said it lightly, but her knuckles were pale on the rail, and when the first squall band showed itself on the northern horizon — a gray curtain, soft-edged, deceptively gentle, like smoke from a fire far away — she saw it the same moment he did, and neither of them said anything, and that silence said plenty.

* * *

By seven the light came up wrong.

Not golden, not gray — a kind of bruised amber, as if the sun were rising through tea, and it turned the whole corridor the color of old paper. Wendell stood over the sonar display in the wheelhouse while Sefa held them on the bearing and Dov listened to her engines the way a doctor listens to a heart she doesn't trust.

"Bottom's coming up," Sefa said. "Shelf. Forty fathoms. Thirty-five."

"Slow to four knots," Wendell said. "Dov, keep her quiet."

"She's always quiet," Dov called from below. "It's the sea that won't shut up."

The sounder's trace crawled. Wendell had run sonar over wrecks before — dozens of them, broken-backed freighters, fishing boats folded like paper, the usual litter of the Bay. He knew what dead ships looked like on a screen: jagged, scattered, a signature like a scream frozen mid-note. The Meridian had gone down in the great cyclone eleven years ago, hull breached, decks blown open, cargo spilled across a mile of seabed. Every survey since said so. That was the story. That was the story his brother had been chasing when his boat went missing in these same waters two years later, looking for answers nobody wanted found.

The trace crawled. Crawled. And then rose — fast, clean, geometric.

"There," Sefa said quietly.

Wendell leaned in. The return was long, unbroken, symmetrical. A hull line. Not a broken back — a spine. Decks intact. Superstructure where the surveys said superstructure could not possibly still stand. The image resolved on the screen with terrible clarity: a ship lying upright on the shelf, sealed, whole, resting on the bottom the way a sleeping animal rests — deliberately, patiently, as if it had chosen to lie down and was waiting.

"That's not a wreck," Priya said from the doorway. Her voice had gone strange. "That's a grave somebody tidied up."

Wendell didn't answer. His mouth had gone dry. Eleven years of official record said the Meridian had been torn apart by the storm, its evidence scattered, its secrets dissolved into silt and current. And here it lay, intact, sealed — which meant the storm hadn't torn it open. Which meant someone had closed it. Sealed hatches didn't happen by accident. Whole decks didn't happen by accident. Somebody had gone down there, in the aftermath, in the closed water, and buttoned the ship up like a coat over a body.

And if someone had sealed it —

"Somebody's been diving here," Sefa said. She'd seen it too; of course she had. Sefa saw everything first. She tapped the screen, where the sonar return showed a faint irregularity near the Meridian's stern — a scar in the silt, a mooring shadow, the kind of mark a dive boat's anchor leaves. "Recent. Weeks, maybe less. Look at the sediment plume — it hasn't settled back."

Wendell stared at the screen until the numbers blurred. Awe had come first — that was the shameful part, the part he'd never admit aloud. For one full breath, seeing that impossible intact hull rising off the seabed, he had felt wonder. His brother's last obsession, real. Answers, real. Somewhere inside that ship, maybe, the reason Elias Ashgrove never came home.

Then the awe curdled, exactly as he'd known it would, into the other thing. Because if the Syndicate had lied about the wreck for eleven years, they had lied for a reason. And if someone else had been diving here recently — recently enough to leave fresh scars in the silt — then either they were ahead of Wendell, or behind him, or watching him right now, and all three possibilities ended badly.

"Nobody surfaces," he said. "Nobody radios. We go dark — Dov, I mean dark, no transponder, no lights above deck."

"You want me to kill my beautiful engines' voice?"

"I want you to make the Tern a rumor."

A pause from below. Then, grudgingly: "She'll hate it. Fine."

* * *

They anchored at eight hundred meters off the stern, in water deep enough to feel anonymous, and Wendell went out onto the deck and looked north.

The squall bands had grown teeth while they worked. What had been distant smoke at dawn was now a wall of rain-stained air marching down the corridor from the northwest, unhurried, enormous, the way glaciers are unhurried. Behind it, higher and darker, the real monsoon mass waited — a bruise the size of a country. The forecasts had given them a window. Windows close. He could already feel the pressure dropping, that subtle ear-pop change in the air, the sea flattening into that glassy, complicit calm that always preceded the worst of it.

"Sefa. How long is our window?"

She came out with the chart under her arm and studied the horizon the way she studied everything — without hope, without fear, just measurement. "Six hours. Maybe five. After that, that front closes the corridor behind us." She looked at him. "You understand what that means. Once it's shut, it's shut. No going back through it. Around means twelve hours south through open cyclone water."

"So we're already committed."

"We were committed when we crossed the line." She said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "I'm telling you the shape of the door."

The door. Wendell watched the squall wall crawl closer and thought of doors — the hatch on the Meridian, sealed; the door of his brother's cabin aboard the boat that never came home, which Wendell had opened a hundred times in memory and never once in fact; the Syndicate collector's voice on the radio last month, friendly as a handshake, saying *interest compounds faster than weather, Mr. Ashgrove*, meaning: we are patient, we are everywhere, and stopping means reckoning.

Every road he'd ever run down had a door like this at the end of it. He'd just never stood this close to one before it shut.

"Dive brief in ten," he said. "Priya, Dov topside. Sefa holds the boat. I'm diving with the new one."

"The new one has a name," Priya said. "Noor."

"Noor, then." He tested the name in his mouth and found it fit better than he liked. A stranger who'd surfaced beside their boat at the wreck site two nights ago with a dive light and a story about murdered family and Syndicate shipping crimes — a story that matched, detail for detail, the coordinates Imara Kess had left on his chart like a knife left in a door. Too many people knew about this wreck. Too many people wanted what was inside it. And every one of them, Wendell was beginning to suspect, had been pointed here by the same hand.

He looked north again. The squall band had reached the edge of visibility, close enough now that he could see rain falling inside it, silver curtains, and beyond the rain the deeper dark stacking up like debt.

Five hours. Enough time to go down, get inside, get the truth, and get back up before the sea changed its mind.

Enough time, if nothing went wrong.

The first drop of rain hit the deck beside his boot, fat and warm and deliberate, like a knock at a door.

"Get the case ready," Wendell said. "We're going down."
