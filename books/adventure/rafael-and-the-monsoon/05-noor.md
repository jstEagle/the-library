## 5. Noor

Priya felt the wreck before she saw it — a cold rising through the Tern's deck plates, as if the sea beneath them had changed its mind about them.

She was on the foredeck coiling line that didn't need coiling, because her hands needed something to do and her mind needed somewhere else to be. Three days inside the corridor now. The exclusion line lay behind them like a scar they'd chosen to cross, and the sky had gone the color of old pewter, low and heavy, pressing down on the water until the horizon was less a line than a suggestion. The air tasted of iron and kelp. Somewhere south, thunder spoke in a language Priya's grandmother would have called a warning and Wendell Ashgrove called weather.

"Sonar's holding," Sefa called from the wheelhouse, her voice pitched to carry over the idling engines. "Contact at forty-one meters. She's big. She's *whole*."

Whole. That word again. Priya had heard the crew say it in whispers since dawn, since Dov's screen had lit up with an outline no sunken ship had any right to keep after eleven years of monsoons. Wrecks collapsed. Wrecks silted over, broke their backs, opened like rotten fruit. The Meridian, according to every chart and every rumor, should have been a scatter of debris across the corridor floor.

Instead she lay below them like a held breath.

Wendell stood at the bow rail, and Priya watched him the way you watch a man standing too close to a fire. He hadn't spoken much since they'd crossed the line. He'd checked the dive gear twice, then a third time, handling his brother's old depth gauge like it might answer questions. Imara Kess's chart was folded in his jacket pocket; Priya had seen him take it out and put it back four times before breakfast.

"You're diving first," he said now, not turning around. He meant it for her and Sefa both. "Dov runs the winch. I go second."

"And if something's down there?" Priya asked.

"Then we'll know what."

* * *

The water swallowed her whole and went quiet.

Priya had dived wrecks before — honest wrecks, fishing boats and freighters dead of ordinary causes — but nothing like this. Forty meters down, the light failed by degrees, from green to gray to a blue so deep it felt like falling upward into night. Her lamp carved a cone through drifting sediment, and out of that cone the Meridian rose.

She was a coastal freighter, maybe ninety meters, lying on her keel with her superstructure intact, her funnel still standing, her railings furred white with eleven years of coral. Fish moved through her corridors like tenants who'd long since stopped paying rent. And there — Priya's breath caught hard enough to fog her mask — there were fresh marks on the hull. Cut marks. Clean, recent, deliberate. A salvage torch had been here within weeks. Maybe days.

Someone had been opening this ship like a letter.

She signaled up, and the reply came back as three sharp tugs on the line: *found something. Hold position.*

She held. The current pushed at her like a hand testing a door. Below, the Meridian's cargo hold gaped where the torch cuts had peeled back steel, and Priya told herself the shiver along her spine was just the thermocline.

That was when the other light found her.

It came out of the dark off the wreck's seaward side — a narrow, disciplined beam, nothing like the scattered glow of her own lamp. It swept past her fins, paused, and settled on her chest like a question. Priya froze, one hand going to the knife on her belt, absurdly, uselessly, forty meters down in someone else's ocean.

The beam tilted. Behind it, a figure resolved: a diver in a patched black wetsuit, gear older than hers, maintained with the fierce tenderness of someone who owned nothing else. Small. Compact. A rebreather, she realized — no bubbles. This person had been down here a long time, moving without a signature, and Priya understood with a lurch that they had been watched since the moment the Tern crossed the line.

The diver raised one gloved hand. Not a weapon. An open palm.

*Wait.*

* * *

They brought her up at dusk, under the guns of everyone's suspicion.

Noor — that was all the name she offered, and Wendell, arms crossed at the rail, did not offer his in return — sat dripping on an overturned crate while five people arranged themselves around her in a loose, hostile crescent. Rain had begun, fine and needling, ticking against the deck. The storm bands to the south had thickened into a wall the color of a bruise going bad.

"You've been diving our wreck," Dov said. His bandaged hand — the crush injury from the hold, three fingers wrapped thick — rested pointedly on the rail.

"Your wreck." Noor's voice was low and even, accented from somewhere east of the corridor. She looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, as if cataloguing them. "You bought a chart from Imara Kess two weeks ago. Before that, this ship was mine for six years. Before that, it belonged to thirty-one people who are at the bottom of it. So let's be careful with the word *your*."

Silence, except the rain and the sea working against the hull.

Sefa stepped forward, hands loose at her sides, navigator's calm stretched thin. "Thirty-one crew. The Meridian went down in the '19 cyclone. Official finding."

"The official finding," Noor said, "was written by the same company that scuttled her."

Priya saw it land on Wendell like a physical blow — not the words themselves but the shape of them, the way a man flinches at a voice he half-recognizes. She'd served on the Tern long enough to know the architecture of his silences. This one had his brother all over it.

"Say that again," Wendell said quietly.

Noor reached into the mesh bag beside her and drew out a waterproof pouch, salt-stained, its zipper replaced twice with wire. She held it up between them.

"My family ran cargo for the Vane Syndicate. Legitimate on paper. My father kept the manifests. In 2019 he was asked to carry something that wasn't on paper — Syndicate product moving through the corridor during the closed season, when no patrol boats sail. He refused. The Meridian sailed anyway, with a skeleton crew and a hull breach waiting to happen. The cyclone was real. The hole in her stern was made by men." Her voice never rose. That was the worst of it, Priya thought. Grief worn smooth as sea glass, all the edges ground away by years. "My father, my mother, my brother. Thirty-one people. The Syndicate called it weather. I have spent six years learning to dive so I could call it murder."

The rain ticked. Dov sat down slowly on the crate beside her, as if his legs had made the decision without him.

"You have proof?" Sefa asked, and Priya heard the careful hope in it, the same hope that had voted yes to Imara's chart.

"I have the ship." Noor tapped the pouch. "And in her strongroom, I have what my father hid there before he sailed — manifests, ledgers, payment records. Everything Vane paid, everything Vane buried. I've been cutting toward the strongroom for three months. Alone." For the first time, something moved behind her eyes — fatigue, vast and unglamorous. "A lone diver doesn't run a torch and haul steel at forty meters. I was going to die down there eventually. Slowly, or quickly. Then your boat shows up with a winch and an engineer and a captain who looks at that wreck like he's reading his own name on it."

All of them looked at Wendell.

He hadn't moved. Priya watched his face do the thing faces do when a locked room inside a person gets its door forced — pain first, then a terrible clarity. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough.

"There was a man aboard the Meridian. A clerk. Elias Ashgrove. He kept books for Vane's coastal operations." He stopped. Started again. "He was my brother. He died near these coordinates. They told us the storm took the boat he was on."

"They told you the truth the way the Syndicate tells everything," Noor said. "Sideways. Your brother wasn't cargo, Captain. He was the witness. He documented the shipments — dates, payments, signatures. That's why the Meridian couldn't be allowed to reach port. That's why the strongroom matters. His records are in there with my father's." She let that settle over the deck like the rain itself. "Your brother tried to sink the Syndicate with paper. They sank him with a ship. I think he'd want the paper to finish the job."

Priya's throat ached. She thought of her own reasons for being on this rust-bucket — a debt inherited from a drowned husband's business partner, years of running storms for a syndicate that smiled while it collected. Everyone on this deck was owned by somebody. It was the one thing they all had in common, and Noor had just shown them the deed.

"We should get below," Sefa said suddenly. She was looking south. "Barometer's falling like a stone. We're in for it tonight."

* * *

In the wheelhouse, later, with the rain gone from needling to hammering and the Tern rolling heavy on a confused swell, Priya found Wendell alone with the chart spread out and Noor's pouch sitting on top of it like a stone on a grave.

He was holding his brother's depth gauge. Turning it over. Not reading it — there was nothing to read — just turning it, the way you worry a tooth that's already lost.

"She's telling the truth," Priya said.

"I know." He said it fast, flat, the way you admit something you've known for hours. "The payments. She described the ledger format. Elias designed it himself — he was proud of it, God help him. Columns for the bribes, columns for the cargo, a code for the routes." He laughed once, a sound with no humor in it at all. "I owe Vane money, Priya. Every rupee of it traces back through their shipping. I've been paying interest on my brother's murder for six years."

Priya leaned against the doorframe. Through the salt-fogged glass, the southern sky pulsed — lightning inside the cloud, silent, like a heartbeat behind a wall.

"So what do we do?"

Wendell set the gauge down. He looked at the pouch, and at the chart, and at the black wall of weather coming up the corridor with the patience of something that had been sent for them specifically.

"Tomorrow we dive," he said. "We open that strongroom, and we take everything. Ledgers, manifests, all of it." He met her eyes, and she saw that the weary man who'd been calculating debts since the day she'd signed aboard was gone, and something harder stood in his place, something that had finally stopped running. "And then—"

The radio crackled. Not static — a voice. Calm, courteous, unmistakably Syndicate, cutting through the storm like a blade through wet canvas:

*"Tern, Tern, this is Vane maritime services. We see you've wandered somewhere interesting. Hold your position. Friends are on the way."*

Outside, far off through the rain, a searchlight swept the sea.
