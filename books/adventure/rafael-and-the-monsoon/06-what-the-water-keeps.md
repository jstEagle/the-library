## 6. What the Water Keeps

The sea at forty meters is a different country. Noor had learned its language young — her father's hands shaping the words in the green dark off Chittagong, her mother's laugh bubbling up through the regulator like something that could not drown. She had thought she'd forgotten it. But the moment she rolled backward off the Tern's gunwale and the monsoon corridor closed over her head like a held breath, every word came back.

Below her, the Meridian waited.

She had dreamed of this wreck for eleven years. In the dreams it was always whole, always lit from within, as if the ship itself refused to be a grave. Now, descending through curtains of silt and silver baitfish, the dream and the reality converged so exactly that for one vertiginous second Noor did not trust her own eyes. The hull rose out of the gloom intact — not broken, not scattered, but *sealed*, sitting upright on the shelf like a patient that had been tucked in rather than killed. Someone had wanted the Meridian preserved. Someone had wanted what was inside her kept fresh.

*Fresh.* The word turned in Noor's chest like a hook.

Wendell Ashgrove descended beside her, all economy of motion, his dive light cutting a clean cone through the murk. Even now, even here, he moved like a man settling accounts — measuring, weighing, deducting. She had watched him do it on the surface too, watch him tally the Tern's fuel against the storm's appetite, watch him price his own crew's safety in the currency of his debt. He thought no one noticed. Everyone noticed.

But down here, in the country where her parents still spoke, Wendell's face behind the mask looked different. Younger. Afraid in the way only the grieving are afraid — afraid of finding what they already know.

Noor touched the wreck first.

Her glove met the steel of the hull and the cold of it traveled up her arm into her teeth. Eleven years ago her father had stood on the Meridian's deck with a manifest in his hand and a conscience he couldn't afford, and eleven years ago someone had decided that manifest was worth more than two hundred and six lives. The Syndicate's accountants had called it a write-off. Her family had called it Tuesday. The sea had called it nothing at all; the sea keeps everything and names nothing.

She followed the guide line Dov had rigged toward the cargo hold breach — the *recently disturbed* breach Sefa's sonar sweep had flagged, the one that had made Wendell go quiet in the way men go quiet when a suspicion becomes a shape. Rivets pried, not corroded. Sealant smeared by modern hands. And stenciled beside the breach, half-erased but legible: a Syndicate lot number, the same prefix that appeared on the shipping documents Noor had spent a decade buying back one forgery at a time.

*They came back,* she thought. *They've been coming back.*

Ahead, Wendell's light swept across an open hatch, and inside it — stacked, strapped, absurdly orderly — were crates stamped with the Meridian's own registry. Cargo that should have been declared lost. Cargo that had been *declared* lost, signed off, insured, mourned. Except it hadn't drowned. It had been warehoused in the most secure vault on Earth: a forbidden corridor where no salvage license would ever reach.

Noor felt the vertigo then, the true kind, the kind that comes not from depth but from understanding. Not a shipwreck. A filing cabinet. Her parents hadn't died near evidence of a crime. They had died *inside* the crime's architecture, sunk deliberately atop the proof, a tomb built to be a lock.

Her breath rasped loud in her own ears. Somewhere above, thunder pressed down through thirty meters of water and arrived as a soft concussion, like a fist knocking politely on a door.

Then Dov screamed.

It came through the water as vibration before sound — a wrongness in the Tern's hull-line, transmitted down the guide line into Noor's bones. She turned. Above them, at the stage where the engineer had been cutting free a fouled mooring chain, a bloom of red unfurled like ink dropped in milk. His cutting torch — or the chain, or the wreck itself reaching out after eleven patient years — something had found him. Priya was on him instantly, small and ferocious, hauling him off the structure while the blood cloud widened and the current took it apart into threads.

*Surface,* Noor signaled. *Now. All of us.*

They ascended through weather. That was the only way to describe it — the corridor's squall bands had reached down into the water column itself, churned the light to brown soup, turned their own bubbles into a blizzard rising past their faces. Noor kept one hand on the line and one hand counting heads: Wendell, Priya dragging Dov, herself. Four lights in the murk. Four.

The Tern, when they broke surface, was arguing with the sea and losing.

She lay over hard in the swell, shipping water across decks that had been dry ten minutes ago, and the sound coming out of her was wrong — a low groaning from below the waterline, the voice of a hull flexing past its opinions. Sefa was shouting from the wheelhouse, rain flattening his words into shapes Noor had to read rather than hear. Imara Kess stood at the rail with a line coiled in her fists, and her face, when Noor climbed aboard, wore an expression Noor had seen once before — on the face of a person watching a plan arrive somewhere other than where they'd sold it.

"Get him below," Wendell was saying, tearing Dov's gear off in strips. "Priya, the kit — the real kit, not the show kit. Sefa, give me damage."

"Hull breach, aft hold," Sefa called back. "Slow, but it's not going to stay slow."

Noor knelt in the flooding scuppers and held Dov's head while Priya worked. The engineer's leg was opened from knee to shin, deep and ugly, the flesh flapped back like the page of a wet book. He was conscious, which was both mercy and cruelty; he kept apologizing, to the boat, to the crew, to God, in three languages. Priya stitched with hands that shook only at the start. Rain hammered the deck planks like a crowd demanding entry.

And through all of it, Wendell crouched at the open hatch where they'd stowed the recovery bag, and Noor watched him understand.

She had told him on the surface, before the dive — or tried to. That the case they sought held ledgers. That the ledgers held payments. That the payments bore signatures. But telling is weather forecasting; seeing is drowning. Now he lifted the waterproof case onto his knees in the flooded cockpit, cracked its seals with thumbs gone white, and read the first page by the strobing emergency lamp, and Noor watched eleven years of his grief get re-filed into a different cabinet entirely.

"It's my brother's hand," he said. Not to anyone. To the page.

"I know," Noor said.

"He wasn't just *near* it. He documented it." Wendell turned a leaf. Another. His voice stayed level the way a mast stays level — right up until the keel goes. "Manifests. Disbursement records. Payments routed through—"

He stopped.

Noor knew the name before he said it, because she had spent a decade learning to say it herself, in depositions that went nowhere, in letters that were never answered: **Corvane**. Aldous Corvane. The man whose signature sat at the bottom of the sink order, buried under three shell companies' worth of plausible deniability. The man who, four years later, had bought Wendell Ashgrove's debt from a dying lender the way one buys a distressed property — cheap, sight unseen, with plans for the land.

"Every payment I've made," Wendell said slowly, "every cent I've bled to that man — it's been *him*. The same hand. The same—" He laughed, one syllable, terrible. "I've been paying interest on my brother's murder."

The rain filled the silence that followed, and the sea filled the boat, inch by patient inch, and Noor put her hand flat on the ledger case between them because there was nothing else in the world to hold.

"This is why they sealed the wreck," she said. "Not to hide the cargo. The cargo was worthless. They hid the *accounting*. Corvane sank the proof inside the loss, and the loss became the insurance claim, and the claim became the capital, and the capital became—" she gestured, helplessly, at everything: the storm, the debt, the corridor, the two hundred and six names — "*this*. We are sailing inside his balance sheet."

Dov groaned on the deck below them. Priya looked up from the stitching, her face stripped to its foundations. "So we take it to the police," she said. "When we're out. We take it to—"

"No court touches the Syndicate," Noor said, and hated how practiced the sentence sounded. Eleven years of practice.

"Then what," Priya said, "do we *do* with it?"

Before anyone could answer, the Tern answered for them. The hull spoke again — louder, lower, a sound like a door giving up — and Sefa's voice came down from the wheelhouse stripped of everything but arithmetic: "Aft bulkhead's failing. We have hours. Maybe fewer."

Wendell closed the case. Lashed it. Held it a moment against his chest as if checking for a heartbeat.

And that was when Imara finally spoke, from the rail, rain streaming off her jaw, her voice carrying the particular flatness of a woman reading terms aloud: "Wendell. There's something you need to know about who hired me."

Lightning walked the horizon, and far off through the rain — a shape too deliberate for weather, too patient for coincidence — a running light turned toward them out of the dark.

* * *

Noor stood at the stern rail with the ledger lashed tight against her ribs and watched the light grow.

Behind her, the Tern groaned. Ahead, whatever Imara was about to confess came walking across the water wearing a searchlight. Eleven years ago, Noor had learned that the sea keeps everything. She was only now beginning to learn what it cost to make it give anything back — and that the Syndicate had sent something to collect before the storm could finish the job for free.
