## 7. The Syndicate Calls Collect

The storm found them before the Syndicate did, but only just.

Wendell stood at the Tern's helm with rain hammering the wheel like a fist on a door, watching a wall of black water march out of the southwest. The corridor had closed behind them hours ago — Sefa's charts showed squall bands stacked to the horizon, a lattice of storms with no gap wide enough for a boat their size. They were inside the monsoon now, in its throat, and there was no going back the way they'd come.

The waterproof case sat wedged under his arm where he'd lashed it against the chart table. He hadn't let go of it since Noor brought it up from the Meridian. It was heavier than it looked. Ledgers always were.

"Contact!" Priya's voice cracked through from the bow, half-swallowed by wind. "Two points off port — running lights, no transponder!"

Wendell raised his binoculars through the sheeting rain. A shape resolved out of the gray: long, low, dark-hulled, cutting the swells at an angle that said *military* even if the paint said nothing at all. The Vane Syndicate didn't put its name on anything it wanted deniable.

"They've been shadowing us since the corridor line," Sefa said, appearing at his shoulder with her hair plastered flat and her voice pitched low, for him alone. "I caught them on passive sonar twenty minutes ago. I thought it was a whale."

"Whales don't run searchlights."

The enforcer boat swung alongside, close enough that Wendell could see figures on its deck in yellow foul-weather gear, close enough that a loudhailer could carry across the gap without much effort. It carried anyway, booming and distorted, cheerful as a game-show host.

"*Tern, this is your friends at Vane Maritime Recovery. You're carrying property that doesn't belong to you. We'd like to discuss a settlement.*"

Dov limped up from below, one arm strapped tight against his ribs where the dive had gone wrong, his face gray with pain and salt spray. "That's a Type-Forty hull," he said. "Twin screws, reinforced bow. They can push through weather we can't survive."

"I know what it is," Wendell said.

"Then you know we can't outrun it."

"We're not going to outrun it."

* * *

The negotiation happened by loudhailer, because the sea between the two boats had become a churning alley of whitecaps too violent for lines or fenders. The Syndicate's spokesman — a voice like oiled hinges, patient as interest compounding — laid out the terms with the warmth of a man reading a menu.

"Mr. Ashgrove. Let me be direct, because the weather won't be. Your debt stands at a figure you already know. Our principals are prepared to forgive it entirely. Every rupee. Today. All you have to do is place the case you recovered overboard. That's it. One small splash, and you walk away a free man."

Wendell keyed the mic. "And my crew?"

"Your crew's debts are included. Generous, isn't it? We're not monsters, Mr. Ashgrove. We're accountants."

He looked down at the case. Through the salt-streaked plastic he could see the edge of a ledger page, a column of numbers in faded ink, and beside those numbers a signature he'd stared at for six hours in the lamplit hold of the Meridian until the letters stopped looking like letters: *R. Vane*. Payments authorized. Shipping lanes falsified. A crew of forty-one written off as cargo loss, including a man named Elias Ashgrove who had signed on as second engineer because the pay was good and the company seemed legitimate.

His brother had documented all of it. His brother had died keeping it dry.

"You know what's funny," Wendell said into the mic. "I spent three years thinking I owed these people money. Turns out they owe me a brother."

"The case, Mr. Ashgrove."

"Come take it."

A pause. Then the voice again, still pleasant, and underneath the pleasantness something that moved like cold water. "We'd prefer not to damage the boat. Boats are expensive. People, frankly, are not. But we will do what's necessary."

That was when Imara Kess stepped out of the wheelhouse doorway.

She'd been below since the dive, since Dov went down hard on the wreck's collapsed spar and Noor dragged him clear. Wendell had almost forgotten she was aboard. She came forward slowly, gripping the rail, and when she spoke it wasn't into the mic. It was to him, close, under the wind, and her face was doing something he'd never seen it do in fifteen years of rivalry: it was breaking.

"Wendell." Her voice was raw. "There's something you need to hear before they say it for me."

"Imara—"

"They hired me." She said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Vane. Three weeks ago. They gave me the coordinates, the salvage rights, the money for your rate. They told me you'd refuse the job, so I should leave the chart where your navigator would find it. They knew your crew better than I did. They knew Dov's debts, Priya's sister, everything. They built this whole voyage, Wendell. I was the hook. You were the fish. And the case—" she swallowed, "—the case was supposed to go down with you."

The rain filled the silence between them.

Wendell looked at her. Fifteen years of racing her across the Bay of Bengal, of stolen chart corrections and poached contracts, of a rivalry that had been the closest thing he had to friendship outside his own crew — and all of it, every mile of it, had been a leash someone else held.

"You could have told me at the dock," he said.

"I was paid half up front." She couldn't look at him. "And I told myself the rest was salvage. That nobody would get hurt. That's what they pay people like me to tell ourselves."

"And the other half?"

"Payable on delivery." She finally met his eyes. "Of your position."

Overhead, thunder rolled down off the storm wall like furniture falling downstairs. The enforcer boat adjusted its engines, sliding another meter closer, and Wendell understood suddenly, completely, with the clarity that comes only at the exact moment everything is lost: there was no deal here. There never had been. The Syndicate forgave nothing; it collected. And the collection today was not a case of ledgers. It was five witnesses and a boat, sunk in international water, in a storm no investigator would ever bother to reconstruct.

They weren't being offered freedom. They were being offered the courtesy of sinking themselves.

* * *

"Sefa," Wendell said quietly. "How's our steering?"

Her eyes flicked to the enforcer, then back. She understood immediately — she always had, that was the thing about Sefa, she read a helm the way she read weather. "Rudder's responsive. Hull's taking water in the forepeak but Dov's got the pump holding. Why?"

"Because I'm done running."

"Wendell." Priya had come aft, soaked through, Noor beside her with the ledger case clutched like a child. "That boat outweighs us four to one."

"It does."

"Ramming it is suicide."

"Staying alongside it is murder." He turned to face them all, and the strangest thing happened, which was that his hands stopped shaking. Three years of dread drained out of him in about four seconds, and what was left underneath was something simple and old and calm. "They want us to believe we owe them. That's been the whole trick, hasn't it? The ledger, the interest, the friendly reminders. Keep a man convinced he's in debt and he'll row himself to his own execution and thank you for the loan of the oars." He tapped the case under his arm. "But I've seen their books now. And I know exactly who owes whom."

The loudhailer crackled again, impatient now. "*Mr. Ashgrove. Our patience is not infinite, and neither, I'm afraid, is your freeboard.*"

Wendell keyed the mic one last time. "Tell Mr. Vane the Ashgrove account is closed."

"What did you—" the voice began, and Wendell threw the mic down, spun the wheel hard to starboard, and shoved the throttles forward until the Tern screamed.

The old boat answered the way she always had — badly, loyally, with everything she had. Her bow came around through a wall of spray, and the enforcer's helmsman, expecting surrender, expecting a man broken down to the last millimeter of compliance, reacted two seconds too slow. Two seconds is a long time in a rising sea.

The Tern hit the enforcer amidships with a sound like a cathedral door slamming.

It wasn't a killing blow — the Syndicate's hull was armored, and Wendell hadn't aimed to kill, only to cripple. But steel shrieked along steel, the enforcer's rail crumpled, one of its searchlights burst apart in a shower of sparks and glass, and the bigger boat lurched away with a fresh gash above its waterline and a sudden, furious chaos of shouting on its deck. On the Tern, everyone went down in a tangle of limbs and loose gear. Dov cried out as his ribs took the impact. Somewhere below, something structural let go with a deep groan, and the pumps changed pitch.

Wendell pulled himself up the wheelhouse ladder, spat seawater, and looked back.

The enforcer was turning. Slowly, ponderously, its damaged bow swinging toward them through the rain, and there was nothing hesitant about it anymore. The accountant's mask was off. What was coming after them now was just weight and speed and intent.

"How long do we have?" Noor shouted, hauling the case up as the deck tilted beneath them.

Wendell watched the storm wall ahead — the true heart of the monsoon, a black cathedral of cloud with lightning moving inside it like thought — and then at the failing enforcer, and made the only calculation left that mattered.

"Less than they'll like," he said, and put the Tern's bow straight into the storm.

Behind them, the Syndicate boat pursued, its engines howling, its gash bleeding rust into the sea. Ahead, the monsoon opened like a mouth.

Sefa gripped the chart table and said, very calmly, "Hold on."

The first wave hit them like the hand of God checking their papers.
