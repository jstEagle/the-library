## 8. Monsoon

The sky went the color of a bruise healing wrong.

Sefa had navigated through forty-one named storms in her eleven years on the water, and she had never seen anything like the wall that came off the southwest horizon at dusk — not a cloud line but a cliff of cloud, black at its base, green-black above, and somewhere inside it a sound like the sea clearing its throat before it swallowed. The barometer had been falling all afternoon, faster than Dov's gauges could plot, and by the time she called Wendell to the wheelhouse the needle had dropped below anything the instrument was built to read.

"We can't outrun it," she said. She didn't dress it up. Wendell hated dressing things up almost as much as he hated stopping. "The corridor's closed behind us. It's ahead of us. We're inside it now."

Wendell stood with his hands flat on the chart table, the waterproof case lashed to his chest under his foul-weather gear like a second heart. He'd refused to let it out of arm's reach since the dive. Sefa understood why. She'd read enough of those ledgers by lamplight to understand exactly what they were carrying, and whose names were in them, and how many men would sleep better tonight if the Tern and everyone aboard her simply ceased to exist.

"Meridian," Wendell said.

It wasn't a question. He'd been saying it for an hour, the way a man says a prayer he doesn't believe in but can't stop repeating. The wreck sat forty meters below them, intact, sealed, holding its dead and its secrets in the same iron grip. If the storm took the Tern apart — and Sefa's gut told her it would — the Meridian was the only thing in this whole drowned corridor big enough to hold onto.

"She'll tear us open on her superstructure," Priya said from the doorway, rain already streaming off her hood. "We'd be climbing down into that."

"We'd be alive to climb," Noor said. She stood at the back of the wheelhouse, half in shadow, and her voice had the flat calm of someone who had already survived the worst thing that would ever happen to her. "I've been on that hull. There's a cargo crane aft, collapsed against the deckhouse. It makes a pocket. Wind shadow, if you can call anything wind shadow tonight."

Dov said nothing. He was strapped into the engineer's chair with his burned arm cradled against him, gray-faced, watching the engine temperature climb because there was nothing else left to watch. The port shaft bearing had been screaming since the ramming — since Wendell had put the Tern's bow into the Syndicate enforcer's flank and changed all their lives with one act of magnificent stupidity — and no bearing in the world survived a monsoon at full throttle.

The first squall band hit at 19:40.

It came as a wall of rain so dense it erased the world. Sefa felt the Tern heel hard to starboard, felt the wheel go heavy in her hands, heard the halyards sing a note she'd only heard once before, the night the old skipper lost his boat off the Andamans. Rain hammered the wheelhouse windows until they might have been underwater. Somewhere below, metal shrieked.

"Dov?"

"Bearing's gone." His voice came up through the deck plates, thin and cracked. "Shaft's free-spinning. I've got maybe steerage from the starboard screw and nothing else."

They ran before it, or tried to. For two hours Sefa steered by instruments alone, blind in the rain, riding swells that came out of the dark like moving hills, and Wendell stood beside her without speaking, one hand on the case over his heart, the other braced on the overhead. Once, when the Tern surfed down a wave face so steep the bow buried itself in green water, Priya made a sound that wasn't quite a scream, and Noor reached across the darkness and gripped her wrist, and held on, and did not let go.

At 21:55 the sea changed.

Sefa felt it before she heard it — a long, wrong silence in the rhythm of the waves, the kind of pause a predator takes. Then the main body of the storm arrived, and the ocean stopped being water and became something geological.

The wave that broke over them carried the wheelhouse windows in. Sefa remembers it in fragments afterward: the glass going, the cold hitting like a fist, Wendell's arm around her harness hauling her clear as the sea poured through and tore the chart table loose and sent a hundred hours of her own hand-drawn charts spinning into the flood. The Tern rolled far past where any roll should stop. Everything not bolted down became projectiles. Somewhere in the chaos Dov's voice rose from below decks, steady as a metronome, calling numbers — *water in the engine room, water in the engine room* — and then the numbers stopped being steady, and then they stopped.

"Abandon ship!" Wendell's voice, raw, right against her ear. "To the rail! Ropes! Now!"

* * *

Getting off the Tern was the hardest thing Sefa ever did, harder than letting go of the wheel, harder than the wheelhouse flooding, because the Tern was hers — her charts, her coffee ring on the nav table, her six years of small accumulated life — and she had to step off her own deck into black water while her home died behind her.

They went down the lifeline in pairs, into seas that hit like falling buildings. Wendell went last. Of course he went last. Sefa saw him pause at the rail, one hand pressed flat against the wet steel of the Tern's hull the way you'd touch the shoulder of someone dying, and then the wave took him too.

She lost everyone in the first minute.

That was the part no one warned you about, in all the survival manuals she'd memorized: not the cold, not the exhaustion, but the loneliness. The strobe on her vest turned the world into green flashes every few seconds, and between flashes there was nothing — no boat, no faces, no horizon, just water with teeth. She swam because swimming was the only instruction left. Her arms moved. Her lungs burned. The storm screamed overhead in a voice that had no bottom.

Then a light found her.

A dive torch, clipped to a harness, swinging wild — and beneath it, a shape she knew: the collapsed crane arm of the Meridian, rising out of the sea like the rib of some drowned god, and clustered along its leeward side, five points of light. Five. Hers made six.

She got a hand on the rusted flange of the crane and someone — Priya, by the grip, small and ferocious — hauled her into the pocket Noor had promised. The wind shadow wasn't much. It was the difference between being flayed and merely being beaten, and after the open water it felt like mercy.

They were all there. All six. Dov was bleeding from a cut over his eye and shivering so hard his teeth chattered like a tool dropped on steel, but he was counting them aloud, over and over — *six, we're six, all six* — because counting was the engineer's way of praying.

Wendell got the ledger out.

Later, Sefa would try to reconstruct how he did it — how he'd gotten the case out of his jacket in the water, gotten the lashing cord out of the case, gotten the whole bundle cinched tight against his chest under his vest — and she would conclude that grief and stubbornness were simply stronger than physics. He worked by feel in the dark, tying the ledgers to himself with knots Sefa recognized from a hundred mooring lines, and when Priya shouted at him — *you'll drown, the weight will drag you down, give it to me* — he shook his head.

"It stays with me," he said. The storm tried to take the words; she caught them anyway. "It's the only thing I've ever carried that matters."

Noor wedged herself against him, back to back, sharing heat. "Then we keep you upright," she said. "All night. Whatever it costs."

Whatever it cost. Sefa closed her eyes and made herself stop calculating. That was the discipline of the worst nights: you stripped the future down past hope, past fear, past everything except the next breath and the next grip. Breath. Grip. Trust. Three words, three tasks, repeated until dawn or until the sea decided otherwise. She set the rhythm aloud, low, like a chant, and the others picked it up without being asked, and somewhere in the third hour she couldn't tell whether she was speaking or dreaming.

The Meridian held them the way the dead hold what they love.

Through the long black hours the storm tried every trick it knew. It sent waves that broke over the pocket and ripped at their harnesses. It sent debris — a hatch cover, a section of railing, a life ring stamped with a name none of them read aloud — and each piece struck the crane arm with a sound like a bell tolling. Twice the sea pulled someone half out of the pocket: Priya once, screaming, saved by Noor's fist locked around her harness strap; Dov once, saved by nothing anyone could explain, washed back against the flange by a wave that seemed almost apologetic.

And twice, in the gaps between the worst of it, people talked. Not plans. Not confessions. Small things. Dov described his daughter's drawing of the Tern, all wrong proportions, smokestack taller than the mast. Priya admitted she'd voted for Imara's job not for the money but because she couldn't stand watching Wendell age one debt at a time. Noor said her mother used to sing during storms, badly, on purpose, to make the children laugh at her instead of fear the sky.

Wendell said, near morning, in a voice scraped down to wire: "My brother would have liked all of you."

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. Sefa reached through the dark and found his sleeve and held it, and felt Noor's shoulder press harder against his back, and understood that this — this ragged chain of grips in a drowned ship's shadow — was the thing the ledgers were actually about. Not debts. Not crimes. Just people refusing, against all arithmetic, to let go of each other.

* * *

The storm began to die an hour before dawn, the way the great ones do — grudgingly, in stages, like a king abdicating province by province. The wind dropped from murder to menace. The waves shortened. And the east, behind the last ragged bands of cloud, turned the color of pewter, then pearl, then a gray-gold that Sefa watched with salt-crusted eyes until she realized her cheeks were wet and it wasn't spray.

They were alive. Six of six. On the spine of a murdered ship, in the middle of a forbidden sea, with nothing — no boat, no radio, no food, one raft canister jammed in the crane wreckage that Dov was already, trembling, trying to open.

Wendell looked down at the ledger lashed to his chest, then out at the emptying sea, and Sefa followed his gaze to the southern horizon, where a white shape was resolving out of the morning haze.

Too sleek for a fishing boat. Too deliberate for rescue.

Priya saw it a moment later and swore softly. "Syndicate hull. Same class as the enforcer."

"They're looking for wreckage," Noor said. "For bodies. For proof we're done."

Wendell's hand settled flat over the case, over his heart, and when he spoke his voice was hoarse but level, and there was something in it Sefa had never heard before — not dread, not defiance. Decision.

"Then let's not be wreckage," he said. "Get the raft ready. We're going to be very easy to find."
