## 9. Surface Tension

Dawn came to the Bay of Bengal like a rumor — gray at first, then a thin gold seam along the east, then the whole sky burning the color of an old bruise healing. Priya watched it happen from her corner of the raft, knees pulled to her chest, salt crusted in her eyebrows and her lips so swollen with cold she could barely shape words.

Five of them on a raft built for two. The Meridian's emergency float had been lashed together from wreckage in the last sane hour before the storm, and it was not designed for mercy. The deck flexed under every swell like something breathing. Their water was gone. Dov's shoulder was bound with strips of Priya's shirt, soaked through twice, and he lay across the center tubes shivering in a way that frightened her more than any weather had.

But they were alive. That was the miracle she kept returning to, turning it over like a coin she didn't trust. Alive, all five, after the Tern came apart beneath them like wet paper.

Wendell sat at the raft's edge with his feet in the water, which no one else would do, and the ledger case strapped across his chest with rope and belt and what looked like pieces of his own jacket. He hadn't let go of it since the storm. He hadn't slept either; Priya could see it in the geometry of his face, the hollows under his eyes deep as hull damage.

The patrol boat appeared out of the dawn haze at 6:40 by Sefa's watch — a white-hulled enforcement cutter with the Vane Syndicate's stylized wave mark on its flank, moving in a slow, patient circle around them the way a shark circles before deciding whether you're worth the trouble.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. The engine sound carried flat across the water, that low industrial thrum, and Priya found herself counting the seconds between swells because counting was better than thinking.

"They've been circling since first light," Sefa said finally. Her voice was scraped raw but steady. She'd kept them oriented through the worst night of all their lives, calling headings into wind that screamed loud enough to peel paint, and now her voice sounded like it had been dragged behind the boat for forty miles. "Three passes. They're waiting for orders."

"Or waiting for us to die on our own," Imara said. She sat hunched near Dov, one hand resting on his forehead, checking fever by touch. "Cheaper that way."

Noor said nothing. She'd barely spoken since dawn, sitting with her back against the air tank, her eyes fixed on the cutter with an expression Priya couldn't read — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of someone seeing an old debt walk up wearing a uniform.

It was Wendell who broke the silence, and when he did, Priya understood that whatever he was about to say, he'd been building toward it all night, through every wave that tried to tear the ledger from his chest.

"We're going to flag them down."

* * *

The words landed on the raft like ballast dropped from height.

Priya laughed first — one sharp, disbelieving bark that hurt her cracked lips. "Flag down the Syndicate. The people who sent a boat to kill us. You want to *wave at them.*"

"I want them to see us." Wendell turned, and there was something in his face she'd never seen there before, not in three years of running intercepts and dodging collectors. Not hope exactly. Something harder than hope. "Think about it. They can't just sink five witnesses in daylight with a patrol boat and a paper trail. Every hour we drift is an hour someone might be looking. They know it. That's why they're circling instead of closing."

"Or they're waiting for dark," Imara said quietly.

"Then we don't give them dark." He tapped the case on his chest. "This goes with us wherever we go. If they take us aboard, they take it aboard. If they put us in a cell, it's in a cell with us. And if they kill us—" he shrugged, and the gesture cost him something, Priya could see it, "—then we're dead anyway, and at least the water gets the story too."

"That's not a plan," Noor said. Her voice was low, roughened by salt and smoke and everything else. "That's a eulogy."

"Maybe." Wendell looked at her. "You told me last night, holding onto that wreck, that your family died because somebody needed them to. That nobody was ever punished. That nobody was ever even *named.*"

Noor's jaw tightened. "I told you the truth."

"And I'm telling you mine. My brother documented what the Syndicate did to your family's ship. He wrote it all down — payments, routes, signatures. Then he went into the water, and the ledgers went down with the Meridian, and for six years I thought that was the end of it. Six years I've been paying a debt to the men who killed him, and I didn't even know it." His voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it, Priya thought. It got quieter. "I am done paying."

"So what's the alternative?" Sefa asked. She'd produced her chart case somehow, waterproof, still slung across her back like a second spine. "We have no radio. We have no boat. We have a raft, a feverish engineer, and—" she nodded at the cutter, "—that."

"The alternative," Noor said, "is that we stay right here until someone who isn't Vane finds us. Fishing boats work these waters. Coast guard runs a sweep after major storms. We wait."

"We wait," Imara repeated, "with a Syndicate cutter watching, and Dov burning up, and no water. How long?"

Noor didn't answer. The silence answered for her.

Priya hugged her knees tighter. She thought about the night on the Meridian's spine, clinging to rusted steel while the monsoon tried to pry them loose one finger at a time. She thought about how Wendell had lashed the ledger to himself with shaking hands, how he'd tied Noor's harness knot himself because hers were slipping, how Sefa had sung — actually *sung*, some old navigation chant in a language Priya didn't know — to keep Dov conscious. She thought about how, somewhere in the black hours, she had stopped being a deckhand running from debt and started being part of something, and how much that terrified her, because things you were part of could be taken away.

"Wendell," she said, and everyone turned. She hadn't meant to speak. "If we go with them. If they take us aboard. What happens to the ledger?"

"It stays on my body until I can get it off my body onto something better."

"Like what?"

"Like a witness," Wendell said. "Like a camera. Like a name and a face and a story that's already halfway around the world before they can stop it." He paused, and Priya watched him choose his next words with the care of a man stepping between swells. "I'm not betting on courts. Noor's right — no court touches the Vane Syndicate. Half the judges in this region owe them money or fear them or both. I learned that the hard way, from inside the machine."

"Then what are you betting on?" Noor asked.

"People who write things down." A ghost of something crossed his face — not quite a smile. "My brother believed in documents. Ledgers, logs, manifests. He used to say paper doesn't drown. Well, it drowned once. But stories don't have to. There's a reporter — Ansel Roque, works out of Colombo, has been picking at the Syndicate's shipping lines for years, getting sued, getting threatened, never stopping. I met him once, years ago, when I still thought I was going to be somebody honest. If we surface anywhere with a phone signal, if we get one message out — one photograph of the first page — then killing us stops being cleanup and starts being a headline. Five survivors of the Meridian wreck, rescued by the very company that sank it, carrying proof of what it did. That's not a story they can bury. That's a story that buries *them.*"

"You're gambling lives on a journalist," Imara said.

"I'm gambling on witness," Wendell said. "There's a difference. Courts need jurisdiction. Journalists just need eyes. And the whole world has eyes now."

* * *

The cutter made its fourth pass closer. Close enough now that Priya could see figures on the deck — four, maybe five, in dark uniforms, one raising binoculars. Close enough that she could see the wake curling off the bow, white and patient.

A loudhailer crackled. The voice that came across the water was almost gentle, almost kind, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing Priya had heard in two days.

*"Vessel adrift. This is Vane Maritime Assistance. We're here to help. Do you require rescue?"*

*Maritime Assistance.* Priya almost laughed again. The same company whose collector had called the Tern every month with friendly reminders about compounding interest. The same company whose enforcer had found them mid-storm and offered forgiveness in exchange for evidence overboard. Now dressed up in the language of lifeboats and good samaritans.

"They're recording," Sefa murmured. "Everything. For the inquiry that will never happen, or for the story they'll tell if we vanish. 'Tragic loss at sea, all hands perished despite heroic rescue efforts.'"

"Then we give them a different story," Wendell said. He stood — stood, on a raft in open water, feet wide, one hand on the ledger case, and Priya saw the patrol boat's binoculars swing to him like compass needles. "Everyone listen. When they bring us aboard: no one signs anything, no one separates from the group, no one lets them take the case. We stay visible. We ask for the coast guard by name. We ask for medical attention for Dov loudly enough for their own cameras to hear it. We make ourselves expensive to disappear."

"And if they disappear us anyway?" Priya asked.

"Then Ansel Roque gets a package," Wendell said, "because Noor sewed a copy of the manifest index into the lining of her jacket two nights ago and none of you knew it, and neither did they."

Every head turned to Noor. She looked back at them with the smallest, driest expression Priya had ever seen on a human face.

"A salvager who doesn't keep backups," Noor said, "doesn't stay a salvager long."

For a moment — one strange, suspended moment on a raft in the middle of the sea — Priya felt something that wasn't fear. It was fragile as foam, ridiculous under the circumstances, but it was there: they were five people with nothing left except each other and the truth, and the truth was strapped to a dying man's chest and stitched into a stranger's jacket, and the Syndicate's whole white cutter suddenly looked smaller than it had a minute ago.

The loudhailer crackled again. *"Do you require assistance? Please respond."*

Wendell raised one arm. Not waving — pointing, straight at the cutter, straight at the figures on its deck, the way a man points at something he intends to hold accountable.

"Yes!" he called, and his voice carried clean across the water, stronger than it had any right to be. "We require assistance! And we require witnesses!"

The cutter slowed. Began its turn.

Priya took Sefa's hand. Somewhere beyond the horizon, she thought, a reporter in Colombo was drinking his morning tea without knowing yet that his life was about to change — and somewhere below them, thirty fathoms down, the Meridian held its secrets in the dark, and the tide was finally, finally turning.
