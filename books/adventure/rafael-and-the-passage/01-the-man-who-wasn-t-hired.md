## 1. The Man Who Wasn't Hired

The rain had been falling on Puerto Cabral for three days, and Isandro Halloran had been counting every drop of it the way other men counted money.

He sat in the back room of the Ferretería del Norte, where the smell of machine oil and damp cardboard hung in the air like weather that never broke, and he watched the water come down the window in ropes. Beyond the glass, the town went about its business bent double under tarps and umbrellas, and beyond the town, invisible behind the gray curtain, the Cordillera del Viento stood with its shoulders in the clouds. He could feel it there, the way a man feels an old fracture before a storm. Sixty kilometers of granite and ice, and somewhere on its flanks, six people who had walked up into that white silence four months ago and not walked down.

He hadn't been hired to find them. Not officially. Officially, he didn't exist.

The permit in his breast pocket said his name was Andrés Ferrer, licensed mountain guide, province of San Telmo, and the photograph—taken seven years ago by a man who owed him—showed a face two days of beard younger than the one he wore now. The laminate had gone soft at the corners from sweat. He'd stopped noticing the lie the way you stop noticing a stone in your boot: it still hurt, but only when you thought about it, so mostly you didn't think about it.

"You'll want coffee," said the intermediary. "Or something stronger."

"I'll want you to stop talking long enough for me to think."

The man's name was Quintana, and he was the kind of person who existed only in the margins of other people's business—a fixer of introductions, a mover of envelopes, a man whose hands were always slightly damp. He sat across the scarred wooden table wringing them now, and Isandro watched him the way he'd watch a client on a glacier who kept looking at the crevasse field instead of his feet. Nervous men made mistakes. Nervous men also told truths they didn't mean to tell.

"Six people," Quintana said anyway, because nervous men always talked. "Government survey team. Hydrology, geology, cartography—the whole alphabet. They went up the Río Blanco approach at the end of the dry season with sixty days of provisions and a satellite uplink, and they reported in every week like clockwork until week nine. Then nothing."

"People go quiet in the mountains."

"Not like this." Quintana leaned forward, and his voice dropped as though the walls might be listening, which in Puerto Cabral they sometimes were. "The uplink didn't fail. It was switched off. From inside the tent. The last transmission ended mid-sentence, and the log shows someone keyed the radio off manually afterward. You understand what I'm telling you? The equipment says these people decided to disappear."

"And the government says?"

"The government says the team is overdue and presumed delayed." Quintana's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Four months overdue. In monsoon country. With winter coming down off the passes."

"So the government has written them off, and yet here you are, hiring a guide off the books to go look for them." Isandro turned his cup slowly on the table, watching the coffee ring it left. "That's a lot of money being spent on people everyone official has already forgotten. Which means somebody unofficial hasn't forgotten them. And that somebody wants this handled quietly. Which means either they want the team found, or they want to know exactly how lost they are."

Quintana's damp hands went still.

Isandro had spent eleven years learning to read mountains, and five more learning to read men, and the second skill had paid better and cost more. There was a particular quality to a silence like the one that opened between them now—not the silence of a man deciding whether to answer, but the silence of a man deciding which lie will hold weight.

"You were recommended," Quintana said finally. "By people who say you're the best high-route man in three provinces."

"By people who also know I can't be seen asking questions in government offices. Convenient, isn't it? A guide with no name goes into the hills, and whatever he finds or doesn't find, no one official ever has to admit he was there." Isandro looked out at the rain. "I know how I'm being used, Señor Quintana. I just haven't decided if I mind."

* * *

He minded. That was the truth of it, and he carried the truth of it up three flights of stairs that evening to the room above the chichería where he lived under the name Ferrer, and he set it down beside the bed like a pack he couldn't take off.

The room was four meters square and held everything he owned: a sleeping bag rated for cold he rarely slept in, a rack of climbing hardware cleaned and oiled, a steel lockbox, and a photograph of a woman he no longer spoke to, face-down in a drawer. Eleven years ago he had been Isandro Halloran, the youngest lead guide ever certified on the northern cordillera, a man whose name appeared in alpine journals and whose rescue record was studied by students. Then came the Arrieta crossing, and the flood, and the testimony of a river pilot named Tomás Iriarte, and after that there was no Isandro Halloran anywhere except in the mouths of men telling the story of how six clients died because their guide pushed a crossing he should never have attempted.

Three of the six had died. The number in the story grew with each retelling, the way numbers do. But three funerals were enough. The license revoked, the blacklist in two countries, the lawsuits settled quietly by insurers who preferred quiet—and Isandro had done the only thing left to a man whose name was worth less than nothing. He had buried it. He had become Ferrer, and Ferrer took cash jobs from men like Quintana: recovery work, discreet surveys, the occasional retrieval of a body that a family wanted found but an insurance company wanted forgotten. Invisible work for an invisible man. He told himself it was a living. Some nights he even believed it.

He opened the lockbox and took out the map.

The Río Blanco approach was drawn in his own hand from a trip eight years back—contours inked in blue, hazards marked in red, the little annotations of a man who trusted terrain more than people. *Scree unstable above 3,400 m. Water source seasonal. Gorge narrows—no retreat route once committed.* He spread it on the floor and weighted the corners with his boots, and the Cordillera unrolled itself in his mind the way it always did, ridgelines rising off the paper, the white massif assembling itself out of memory and contour lines until he could almost feel the wind coming off it, dry and thin and smelling of stone.

Six people. A hydrologist, most likely, because the job was water—everything in this province was water now, the new dam project upstream on the Viento headwaters, the concrete cathedral the ministry was going to raise over the valley so the capital could have its electricity and the contractors their percentages. A team sent to measure what the dam would drown. Nine weeks of data, and then a radio switched off by hand.

He lay back on the sleeping bag and stared at the ceiling, where a water stain had mapped its own slow continent over the years, and he asked himself the question he asked himself before every job, the one that was supposed to be arithmetic and never was.

Was it worth it?

The money was good—half now, half on delivery, and Quintana's envelope had been fat enough to suggest real fear behind it. The work was simple on its face: walk the Río Blanco, find the camp or the grave or the empty ground, read the mountain's evidence, come home. Mountains kept their records honestly. Bodies stayed where they fell, gear stayed where it was dropped, and snow told the truth about dates. Whatever had happened up there, the mountain would still be holding it, patient as a ledger.

But the mountain would also be holding whoever had switched off that radio. Because equipment didn't turn itself off, and teams of six didn't all decide together to vanish, and somewhere in the arithmetic of this job was a variable Isandro couldn't see yet—a variable that had made a nervous man pay too much and talk too little.

He thought about staying invisible. He was good at it now; it was the one discipline he'd mastered since the fall. Don't ask questions in towns. Don't sign anything true. Take the job, do the work, deliver the report, move on before your face becomes familiar. For five years that doctrine had kept him alive and free, if you could call this living—this rented room, this false name, this life measured out in cash envelopes and seasons.

The rain kept falling. Somewhere below, the chichería crowd sang a song about a woman who waited, and the singer's voice cracked on the high note, and Isandro found himself thinking, absurdly, of Marisol—his half-sister, half a country away, half a stranger, the last person alive who shared his blood and almost none of his history. She'd written again last month. He hadn't answered. Answering was a footprint. Everything was a footprint. He had built his whole existence on the principle that the safest place in any landscape is the place where nothing marks your passage.

And yet.

And yet six people had gone up that mountain carrying instruments to measure the truth about a valley, and something up there had decided the truth was too heavy to bring back down. Isandro knew what it was to have the truth taken from you—to have your name rewritten by another man's testimony while you stood helpless in the flood of it. He knew what it was to be erased.

He got up in the dark, struck a match, and wrote two words on the back of Quintana's card:

*I'll go.*

Then he sat for a long time with the match burning down toward his fingers, watching the flame the way he'd learned to watch weather—as a thing that gives light and takes warmth, a thing you use carefully, knowing what it costs.

* * *

Quintana met him at dawn under the corrugated awning of the bus depot, where the rain had thinned to a mist that turned the world the color of old tin. The envelope changed hands inside a folded newspaper, the way such things always did, and Quintana's eyes flicked past Isandro's shoulder twice before settling, which told Isandro more than the briefing had.

"One condition," Quintana said. "You report to me alone. No intermediaries, no letters, nothing written. When you find something—or don't—you come back down the Río Blanco to the village of Santa Rosa and ask for me at the co-op. In person."

"In person. Of course. Nothing that leaves a trail." Isandro tucked the newspaper under his arm. "One question first. The team leader. What was her name?"

Something moved behind Quintana's eyes—quick, involuntary, like a bird flushing from cover.

"Bureaucratic lists don't matter to you," he said. "You're going to find a campsite, not a biography."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Quintana agreed softly. "It isn't." He stepped back into the mist, then paused, and for one moment the nervousness fell away and Isandro saw the thing underneath it, the thing that had been talking all along from behind the damp hands. "Guide. Whatever you find up there—find it slowly. Some answers are heavier than they look."

Then he was gone, swallowed by the depot crowd, and Isandro stood alone with the envelope against his ribs and the mountain waiting at the end of the road.

He bought his ticket north. At the last moment, checking his gear in the depot yard, he noticed the truck bed sat wrong—rode heavy at the rear axle, an inch low, the springs compressed in a way his eye caught before his mind did. Twenty years of loading expeditions had taught his hands the weight of things.

Someone, or something, was already hidden in his truck.

He stood in the tin-colored drizzle, looking at the sagging axle, and felt the first cold thread of the future run through him—the sense, familiar to any mountain man, that the route had already changed beneath his feet before he'd taken a single step.
