## 2. Stowaway

The truck smelled of diesel and rope and something older, like the inside of a church that had been closed a long time. Rafael had learned to read smells the way other boys read faces. This one said: nobody checks this truck. Nobody loves this truck. It belonged to a man who drove it the way he walked—shoulders forward, eyes on the middle distance, as if the world were a corridor he was passing through and not a place where anyone lived.

Rafael had climbed in at the co-op yard outside San Gabriel de las Nubes, when the driver went to argue about fuel receipts, and folded himself into the gap between stacked crates of dehydrated food and coils of climbing line, and pulled a tarp over himself, and became cargo.

He was good at being cargo. He had been practicing since he was eleven.

* * *

The road climbed all night. Rafael knew this because of his ears—they popped twice, small soft clicks, like knuckles cracking somewhere behind his eyes—and because of the cold. Cold came up through the truck bed first, through the metal, into his hips and spine, and then it found his hands, which he kept tucked into his armpits, and then it got into his breath so that each exhale was a small ghost he sent out and watched die. He counted the ghosts. At four hundred and six he stopped counting because the number had begun to feel like a debt.

He thought about the work camp the way you think about a tooth that's been pulled: you keep touching the space where it was. The dormitory with its forty bunks and its smell of wet wool and chlorine. The foreman Vásquez, who called the boys *los huérfanos útiles*—the useful orphans—as if orphanhood were a trade skill, like welding. The ledger on the office wall where their names were written in columns next to numbers, and Rafael had understood, even before he ran, that the numbers were what they were worth and the names were only there for the auditors.

His mother had died in the spring. His father had never existed anywhere except in a story she told badly, changing details each time—a fisherman, then a miner, then once, memorably, a bullfighter—until Rafael understood the story wasn't about a man at all. It was about her wanting him to believe he'd come from somewhere.

Now he came from a tarp in the back of a stranger's truck, going up into the Cordillera del Viento, where the wind had a name and the government had lost six men, and where, if the talk in the camp canteen was true, there was work that paid cash and asked no questions. Rafael had decided, with the cold clarity of someone who has nothing left to lose but the losing itself, that he would ask no questions either. He would arrive. He would be useful. Useful orphans survived.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the weave of the tarp, and the truck stopped, and Rafael heard voices.

"—permits are in order," a man was saying, a voice like gravel poured slowly. "You don't need to see them twice."

"I need to see them once properly." A woman's voice. Dry, unhurried, with an edge of amusement in it, the way a blade has an edge. "That's different from twice."

Rafael held his breath. Through a finger-width gap between crates he could see boots on gravel, two pairs, and beyond them a landscape that made his chest hurt: the trailhead of the Cordillera, where the forest gave up all at once and the mountain began, black-green falling away into cloud, and above the cloud, faint as a watermark, the ridgeline they called El Diente—the Tooth—white with old snow.

"Marisol." The gravel voice again, tired now. "We have a window. The weather will hold four days, maybe five. I don't want to spend them here."

"You spent twenty minutes arguing about a fuel receipt."

"That receipt is legal."

Something in the way he said it made the woman laugh, short and without much joy. "Everything you do is legal, Isandro. That's the saddest thing about you."

Then the tarp moved, and light fell in like water through a broken dam, and Rafael was looking up into the face of a man who had clearly expected everything in the world except a boy.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The man—Isandro, obviously; he had his half-sister's same dark watchfulness but none of her warmth, if the laugh had been warmth—looked at Rafael the way surveyors look at ground that has shifted. Recalculating. Then he reached in, took Rafael by the collar with a grip that was firm without being cruel, and set him on his feet on the gravel as though unloading a mislabeled crate.

"You were in Coquimbo," Isandro said. Not a question. "I bought supplies in Coquimbo. You weren't in the truck then."

"No, señor."

"So you got in at San Gabriel."

"Yes, señor."

"How."

"The fuel argument, señor. You were loud."

Beside Isandro, the woman put her hand over her mouth, but Rafael saw her shoulders shake. She was smaller than her brother, quick-moving, with gray already threading her black hair and eyes the color of river water over stone. She looked at Rafael the way no adult had looked at him in three years—not at the useful orphan, not at the ledger entry, but at him, at the dirt on his face and the hollows under his eyes, and her expression did something complicated and then settled on anger.

"He's fourteen," she said.

"Fifteen in March," Rafael offered.

"Nobody asked you." Marisol crouched to his level. Up close he could see she had been crying recently, or not sleeping, or both; the skin under her eyes had that bruised translucence. "Where is your family?"

"Gone, señora."

"Your mother?"

"Gone."

"Father?"

"Gone."

She waited. Rafael understood the test—he had taken versions of it before, from priests, from camp intake officers, from a kind woman at a bus station whose kindness had turned out to be a phone call to social services—and he gave the correct answer, which was silence. Silence said: don't dig here. Silence said: there is nothing down this hole but more hole.

Isandro had turned away and was looking at the mountain. That was the thing Rafael noticed first about him, and would keep noticing for weeks: he did his hardest looking away from people. "There's a bus back down at noon," he said to the gravel. "I'll pay for it. And for food until then."

"He'll die on that bus," Marisol said.

"He'll die on my mountain, and that will be my problem instead of somebody else's. That's the whole difference."

"Look at him, Isandro."

"I am looking at him."

"No, you're looking past him. There's a difference, and you of all people should know it." Her voice dropped, and Rafael felt, without understanding why, that he was hearing an argument much older than this morning. "You know exactly what happens to boys nobody comes back for. You've spent ten years making sure nobody has to come back for you. Well, congratulations. Here's one."

The wind came off the glacier fields above and moved through the stunted trees at the treeline, and the sound it made was like a crowd very far away deciding something. Isandro stood with his back to them both, hands loose at his sides, a big man gone lean the way tools go lean, and Rafael watched the muscles of his jaw work and thought: he is doing arithmetic. He is adding me up against the weather and the window and whatever it is he's actually driving toward, and I am coming out wrong in the sum.

"There's a sweep team working the lower valley," Isandro said finally, still not turning. "Officially they're looking for the survey crew. Officially." He glanced back then, one look, and his eyes were the flat gray of the sky. "If you come up with us and they find you, nobody will report you missing, because nobody knows you exist. Do you understand what that means? It means you can disappear completely. People do, up here. All the time."

Rafael considered this. Most adults, offering danger, dressed it up. This man had handed it to him raw, like meat.

"Yes, señor," he said. "So can I."

Something flickered across Isandro's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed—and Marisol made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost the opposite.

"He stays," she said. It was not a question either.

"I didn't say he couldn't stay. I said he should be on a bus." Isandro rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked less like a mountain guide than like a man standing in a doorway of a house he used to live in. Then the moment passed, and he was all business, crouching to rifle through a side crate, and he tossed Rafael a wool sweater, dark green, too big, smelling of camphor and cold storage. "Put that on. You'll carry your own weight or you'll wish you hadn't come. We move in twenty minutes, we walk till the light goes, and you keep between me and Marisol where I can see you. If I say run, you run. If I say hide, you get small and you get quiet. Can you do those things?"

"Yes, señor."

"Stop calling me señor. It makes me feel like the foreman at whatever place you ran from."

"Yes—" Rafael caught himself. "Yes. Okay."

"And eat." Marisol pressed a strip of dried meat into his hand, and her fingers were warm, and Rafael realized with a small internal shock that no one had fed him first and asked questions second in longer than he could remember. "Slowly. Your stomach will cramp if you've been hungry a while."

They broke camp around him with the wordless efficiency of people who had done this together before, before whatever had come between them, and Rafael ate his meat and pulled on the green sweater and watched the mountain. El Diente had come clear of the cloud now, and the morning sun caught the snowfields so they burned white, and below them the trail went up through the trees and out of sight, and the wind talked its far-off crowd-talk.

He should be afraid. He catalogued the reasons: the sweep team, the lost men, the cold, the man who did his feeling facing away from you. He held each reason up and examined it, the way he'd learned to examine everything, and found that underneath them all was a single strange warm fact, small as a coal:

Nobody had put him on the bus.

* * *

They walked. The trail rose in long switchbacks through *coigüe* forest, the trunks silver and dripping, the ground soft with centuries of fallen leaves, and Rafael learned the rhythm of the others: Isandro ahead setting a pace that was hard but honest, never showing off, never resting; Marisol behind him, and Rafael between, as ordered. Neither spoke much. But at the midday halt, when Rafael refilled the water bottles at a stream without being asked, he caught Marisol watching him with an expression he couldn't file anywhere, and at the afternoon rest Isandro adjusted the boy's pack straps in silence, shortening them two holes, and Rafael understood that he had been measured, and fitted, and neither of them had said a word about it.

Near dusk they came out of the trees onto a shoulder of bare rock, and the whole Cordillera opened above them, ridge after ridge going violet into the evening, and the wind hit them full in the face with the smell of snow and stone.

"Camp here," Isandro said. "Tomorrow we cross the high col."

And then he stopped. He was looking north along the ridgeline, into the last of the light, and his body had gone very still, the stillness of a man who has spent years learning to notice things and has just noticed one.

"What?" Marisol said.

"Smoke," Isandro said quietly. "Windward side. Somebody's burning something up there—and there shouldn't be anybody up there at all."
