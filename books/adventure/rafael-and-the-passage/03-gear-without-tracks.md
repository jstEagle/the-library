## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The ridge took the whole morning to give up.

Isandro climbed it the way he climbed everything now—slowly, deliberately, counting his breaths against his steps so that his mind had no room left for anything else. Four steps, inhale. Four steps, exhale. The Cordillera del Viento did not forgive hurry. It rose out of the cloud deck in long gray shoulders, wind-scoured and treeless above four thousand meters, and the wind that gave the range its name came off the icefields in gusts that could take a man's balance and keep it. He could hear it coming for minutes before it arrived, a low freight-train rumble up the valley, and he had learned to stop, plant his poles, and lean into it like a man leaning into a door held shut against something on the other side.

Behind him, Rafael climbed well. That surprised him more than he wanted to admit. The boy moved with the economy of someone who had learned young that wasted motion was wasted food—short strides, hands always finding the next hold before the weight committed—and he never complained, which Isandro distrusted on principle. Boys who didn't complain were either too proud or too frightened, and both kinds broke eventually. Marisol climbed between them, setting the pace she'd insisted on setting since the trailhead, her breathing ragged but steady, her jaw set in that way she'd inherited from their mother along with the dark eyes and the stubbornness.

They made the col at noon, in the lee of a fin of black rock, and ate cold maize cakes and hard cheese while the wind screamed over the top of them like something impatient to be elsewhere.

"Camp should be over that shoulder," Isandro said, nodding west. "Survey team's last coordinates put them on the bench below the second ridge. Flat ground, water from the meltstream, sheltered from the north wind." He said it flatly, professionally, the way he'd said a thousand such things to a thousand clients. "We'll be there by mid-afternoon."

"And if they're there?" Marisol asked.

He chewed, swallowed. "Then we ask them what happened to their radio schedule."

But he already knew they wouldn't be there. He'd known since the second day, when they'd found the cairn at the river fork rebuilt wrong—rebuilt *deliberately* wrong, stones stacked to point a following party toward the dead-end gorge instead of the high traverse. A survey team didn't misbuild cairns. Someone had wanted any searchers funneled away from the truth, whatever the truth was, and Isandro had spent the last two days climbing with the cold, specific attention of a man reading a sentence written in a language he wished he didn't speak.

* * *

They came over the shoulder at three o'clock, and the camp opened below them like an answer to a question nobody had asked aloud.

Three dome tents, pitched in a neat row on the gravel bench, guy-lines taut, rainflies staked. A cook fly strung between two boulders. The solar panels of a base station angled toward the afternoon sun on their aluminum frame, and beside them, on a folding table weighted with rocks, a theodolite under its canvas cover, a stack of aluminum instrument cases, a plastic crate of field rations with its lid still latched.

Everything in order. Everything *maintained*. The kind of camp a careful team leaves when they mean to come back to it.

No one had come back to it.

"Hello!" Marisol called, and her voice went out flat across the bench and died against the rock without an echo. "¡Hola! Anyone here?"

The wind answered. It was the only thing that ever answered up here.

Isandro stood at the edge of the bench for a long moment before he let himself walk in. This was the part of the job he hated—the threshold, the moment before knowing—and he felt Rafael come up close behind him, close enough that he could hear the boy's breathing, quick and shallow through his nose.

"Tents first," Isandro said quietly. "Don't touch anything until I've looked."

The nearest tent's zipper ran smooth as the day it left the factory. Inside: two sleeping bags rolled and strapped, foam pads stacked, a book—paperback, swollen with damp—with a pencil marking the page. A pair of spare boots, laces tucked inside, dry rot not yet begun. On the nylon wall, someone had taped a photograph, curling at the corners: six people squinting into the sun on some government office steps, arms slung over shoulders, a banner behind them half-readable. *Cordillera del Viento Hydrological Survey — Phase II.*

Six faces. Isandro counted them twice, then a third time, because counting was easier than thinking about what the counting meant.

The second tent held the same story. The third held the team leader's gear—a better grade of everything, a satellite phone case empty as a shed skin, and pinned to the inner wall, a hand-drawn map of the upper valley annotated in tight, precise script. Isandro photographed it with the small camera he carried for exactly this purpose, though he couldn't have said yet why he was documenting rather than searching. Some instinct older than reasoning told him to record everything exactly as found.

"No blood," Rafael said from the doorway of the second tent. His voice was strange—careful, flattened, the voice of a kid holding himself together with both hands. "There'd be blood. Or signs of a struggle. Something knocked over."

"You've thought about this," Isandro said.

"I've been around camps people left fast." The boy wouldn't meet his eyes. "This isn't fast. This is—" He searched for the word and didn't find it, and Isandro supplied it silently: *tidy.* This was tidy. This was the most frightening thing he had seen in twenty years of mountains, and it was how orderly it all was.

* * *

He found the radio at the bottom of the equipment cache, under the instrument cases, wrapped in a groundsheet as if someone had meant to protect it.

It had been smashed from inside the tent line—that much was legible in the wreckage. Not dropped down scree, not crushed under rockfall. The casing was stove in with a single focused blow, the circuit board cracked clean through, the antenna snapped at its root. One blow, delivered by someone standing over it, by someone who knew radios and knew that this model's guts were all clustered in the lower housing and that one strike there killed it beyond any field repair.

And then it had been wrapped up carefully and cached, like evidence. Like something to be kept and not found.

"Marisol," he called. She came around the cook fly, and he held the ruined radio up where she could see it. "Look at the break. One hit. From above, straight down."

She studied it, and he watched her arrive at the same place he'd already arrived, watched it land in her face like weather. "The team didn't do this."

"No."

"Somebody else was here."

"Somebody else was here," Isandro agreed, "and they were in no hurry, and they knew exactly what they were doing." He set the radio down gently, absurdly, as if it were a body. "You don't smash a radio unless you want the team cut off. You don't cut a team off unless you want them unreachable. And you don't want them unreachable unless whatever happens next can't be heard."

Rafael had gone very still by the cache. "What happens next?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Isandro walked the perimeter of the camp slowly, reading the ground the way other men read print. No tracks leaving toward the pass. No tracks toward the river. The bench's gravel surface held the wind-polished memory of boot prints—six sets, maybe seven, weeks old, blurred almost to nothing—but every trail faded *inward*, toward the tents, and none faded outward. As if six people had walked into this camp and dissolved into the thin bright air.

People didn't dissolve. He had spent a decade learning every way a mountain could take a person—fall, cold, water, whiteout, the simple treachery of cornices—and none of those ways left a camp this neat. Avalanche? Nothing above the bench to slide. Crevasse? No glacier within a day's walk. Panic, a night descent in storm? Then there would be dropped gear down the descent gullies, a glove, a stove, some small surrendered weight of a human being in flight. There was nothing. The mountain had been asked to swallow six surveyors and had declined, and that refusal sat in Isandro's chest like swallowed stone.

Because there was only one explanation left, and it didn't live in the mountain at all.

"They were taken," he said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. Marisol turned. Even the wind seemed to lean in. "Off the bench, alive or otherwise, by people who then cleaned the site. Packed nothing, burned nothing, left the camp looking lived-in because a camp that looks abandoned gets searched and a camp that looks occupied doesn't. They smashed the radio so no distress call ever went out, so the official record says *lost contact, presumed weather event,* and everyone shakes their heads about the Viento and goes home." He heard his own voice go quiet. "I've seen erasure work before. Never this clean."

"Who erases a government survey team?" Marisol asked, but her voice said she already suspected, the same way he did. Surveys measured things. Measurements became reports. Reports became obstacles to men who needed certain numbers never to exist. He thought of the intermediary in the lowland town, sweating through his shirt, paying cash, refusing receipts. *Find out what happened. Don't file anything. Don't tell anyone you were hired.*

"What do we do?" Rafael. Still at the cache, one hand resting on the crate lid without seeming to know it was there.

Isandro looked at the camp—at the curled photograph of six smiling faces, at the paperback with its pencil holding a page someone had meant to return to, at the theodolite waiting under its cover for hands that were somewhere else now, or nowhere at all. Invisibility, his doctrine, his religion for ten years: see it, log it, leave it, vanish. Report to the intermediary, take the money, become no one again.

"We document," he heard himself say. "Everything. Photographs, positions, dates. Then we go over that bench meter by meter before the light goes, because whoever did this was thorough, and thorough people miss things when the terrain is ugly enough." He paused. "And then we decide what we are carrying off this mountain besides ourselves."

Marisol's eyes met his, and something passed between them that had not passed between them in years—not forgiveness, not yet, but the recognition of two people standing on the same side of the same fire.

It was Rafael who found it, forty minutes later, in the jumble of collapsed slabs below the eastern edge of the bench where the meltwater had undercut the gravel: a flat seam of shadow behind a fallen plate of rock, and wedged into it, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax, a metal ammunition box stenciled with the government survey code.

The lock was intact. The wax was unbroken. Whoever had hidden it had done it fast and done it well, and had trusted the mountain to keep their secret better than any living soul.

Inside, beneath a folded map, lay a leather-bound field journal, and on its flyleaf, in the same tight, precise script as the map in the leader's tent, a name:

*Elena Vasquez-Roa. If found, do not return this to the Ministry.*

Isandro read it twice. The wind came up the valley, long and building, and somewhere below them, far down the windward slopes, he thought—for one uncertain moment he was nearly sure—he smelled smoke.
