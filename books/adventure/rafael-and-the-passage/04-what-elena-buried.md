## 4. What Elena Buried

The cache had been hidden the way Marisol's mother used to hide money from her father: not cleverly, but desperately, in a place only panic would choose. A shelf of stone beneath an overhang on the river side of the ridge, screened by a curtain of aliso branches that had grown up around it in the months since anyone last touched it. Isandro would have walked past it. Isandro, she was learning, saw the mountain as a series of problems—grades, exposures, water sources—and problems did not include things hidden by frightened people.

Marisol saw them everywhere.

She had seen them in the survey camp two days' climb below: the coffee cups washed and set upside down to dry, the way soldiers do before inspection; the logbook with its final entry written in a hand so controlled it might have been traced. She had seen it in Elena Vásquez's tent, where a hair ribbon was coiled on the sleeping bag with a precision that said *I will be back for this*, and no one had come back. Six adults did not vanish from a pitched camp leaving their tents zipped. Six adults were taken, or made to walk, or—

She stopped letting herself finish the sentence. It didn't help. The sentence finished itself anyway, usually at night, against the nylon of the tent wall while Rafael breathed in his careful, hoarded way three feet away, the way children breathe when they have learned that sleep is a vulnerability.

Now she crouched under the overhang with rain beginning to tick through the aliso leaves, and worked at the stones with her fingers like a woman digging out a buried animal.

Isandro and Rafael were fifty meters above, checking a col for sign of the sweep teams Rafael swore he'd smelled smoke from that morning. Marisol had volunteered to work the slope below, and Isandro had given her one of his long looks—the ones that weren't quite disapproval, weren't quite permission—and handed her the radio without comment. Her brother communicated chiefly in objects: a radio passed over meant *be careful*; a rope coil dropped at her feet meant *you go first, I'll watch*. He had been doing it since they were children, when their father still lived and words in their house were rationed like fuel in a bad winter.

The stones came away wet. Behind them, a cavity no deeper than her forearm, lined with a folded groundsheet gone green with mold. And inside the groundsheet, wrapped twice more in plastic and bound with surveyor's tape, a block the size of a brick.

Her first thought was ordnance. It had the density of it, the deliberate wrapping. Her second thought, as she peeled the tape and the plastic fell open in her lap, was worse.

A field journal. Waterproof pages, government issue, the kind Isandro carried and never let anyone touch. Stamped across the cover in faded ink: **DIRECCIÓN NACIONAL DE RECONOCIMIENTO HIDROLÓGICO — EQUIPO 3**. And beneath the stamp, in ballpoint, in small upright letters: *E. Vásquez, jefa de equipo.*

Elena. The woman with the ribbon on her sleeping bag.

Marisol sat back on her heels. Rain ticked louder on the leaves. Somewhere above her, faintly, Rafael's voice rose and flattened into Isandro's low answer, and the ordinary sound of it made her feel suddenly, absurdly protective of this moment—as if what she held were a bird with a broken wing that the world must not yet see.

She opened it.

The early pages were routine. Barometric readings, flow rates, transect notes in six different hands, jokes in the margins—a sketch of Tomás the cook drawn as a mule, a running argument about whether the condors over the gorge numbered nine or eleven. Marisol read faster. This was how it always began, she thought. With condors and jokes. No one wrote *today we discovered the thing that will kill us*; they wrote the weather, and then one day the weather entries got shorter, and then there were no more entries at all.

Day nineteen. Elena's hand again, tighter now, the letters pressed hard enough to emboss the next page.

*Resurveyed the reservoir basin per contract amendment 7-C. Figures do not reconcile. Modeled inundation at full pool exceeds the published projection by 11.4 meters at the confluence villages. Eleven point four meters. That is not error. That is design.*

Marisol's mouth went dry. She knew the confluence villages. Everyone in the province did—Puerto Ceniza, La Angostura, the string of settlements the government maps marked with such confident little clusters of squares. Four thousand people, give or take, between the river and the sea.

She turned pages. The data came in Elena's hand now almost exclusively, the other five voices dropping away as though they'd agreed to let her carry it alone. Tables. Cross-sections. A hand-drawn profile of the valley with the true flood line inked in red, climbing far above the blue line of the official projection, and beside it, in the margin, a single underlined sentence:

*They know. I sent copies. Now we document everything and stay alive until the hearing.*

*Stay alive until the hearing.* Marisol read the phrase four times. It had the tone of a woman reassuring her team, and underneath it, the tone of a woman who no longer believed herself.

There were names. Not just the corporate acronym—Consolidada del Norte, the dam's backers, whose logo she'd seen on trucks all her life—but men. A deputy minister. A project director with a name like a brand of tractor. An engineer who had signed off on the falsified figures, with a note beside his signature in Elena's writing: *signed twice, two different dates, two different numbers.* People. Men who ate lunch and had mothers and signed their names twice when the numbers changed.

And then, near the end, a page that stopped her breath.

*Day twenty-three. A helicopter yesterday, no markings. Rivas says he saw men on the far bank with equipment cases, too large for seismic gear. We moved the duplicate records tonight. If anything happens to us, the originals are with me and the duplicates are where only Marisol—*

The sentence broke off. Not crossed out. Broken, mid-word, as if the pen had lifted and never returned to it. The remaining pages were blank except for one entry, undated, in a different hand—hurried, slanting, unfamiliar:

*Jefa burned the map copy. We are going down to the crossing tomorrow. If we don't come back, the cache under the—*

Nothing. The paper after that was clean, and slightly warped, as if it had once been damp and dried again.

Marisol became aware that she was shaking. Not fear exactly—or not only fear—but something larger moving through her, the way floodwater moves through a channel, taking the shape of everything it passes. The team hadn't wandered into a storm. They hadn't fallen into a crevasse. They had walked down to the river crossing carrying the truth, and someone had been waiting, and every search party since, every official expression of concern, had been a hand smoothing sand over footprints.

And the cache—*the cache under the—* unfinished—was this one. It had to be. Which meant the duplicates, the second set of records Elena trusted more than her own originals, were somewhere else entirely, and the only person who knew where was a woman whose hair ribbon still lay coiled on a sleeping bag two days below.

"Marisol."

Isandro's voice on the radio, low, stripped of everything but function. She fumbled it up.

"Here."

"Come up. Now. Rafael found a burn pile—survey crates, recent." A pause, the length of a man deciding how much truth a radio can hold. "They're not looking for bodies, Mari. They're looking for paper."

She looked down at the journal in her lap, at Elena's red flood line climbing toward villages full of people who thought the dam would save them. She wrapped it in its plastic, wrapped the plastic in her spare shirt, and pressed it flat against her stomach beneath her jacket, against the warmth of her own body, the way you'd carry something alive.

"Coming," she said.

She climbed through the rain with the journal beating against her ribs like a second heart, and understood, with terrible clarity, that invisibility—the doctrine her brother had built his whole ruined life around—was already dead. You could not be invisible carrying this. You could only be hunted, or be heard.

Above her, at the lip of the col, two figures waited against the gray sky: her brother, motionless, reading her face the way he read weather; and Rafael, fourteen years old and soaked through, watching her with the eyes of someone who had learned very young to recognize when adults were carrying something dangerous.

She reached them, breath ragged, and put her hand flat over her jacket, over the hidden weight of it.

"It wasn't an accident," she said. "None of it. And they're going to want what we have."

Isandro looked north, where the smoke smell lived in the wind, and for the first time since she'd joined him, her brother looked afraid.
