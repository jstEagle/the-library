## 5. Smoke on the Windward Side

Rafael smelled it before he saw it—woodsmoke, but wrong. Campfire smoke was friendly, a smell that meant someone had made tea and was warming their hands against the morning. This smoke came in a long gray ribbon off the windward slope, straight and steady as a signal flag, and underneath it was something else: kerosene, and burning nylon, and the sweet chemical stink of synthetic insulation going up in flames.

He crouched at the edge of the scree field, one hand flat on cold stone, and counted.

Three men on the ridge above the old survey camp. Not walking like searchers walked. Rafael had spent two years watching adults move through places they didn't belong—foremen sweeping work camps for runaways, police sweeping markets for boys without papers—and he knew the difference between looking and hunting. Searchers moved fast and called out, their eyes jumping everywhere, hoping to find something. These men moved slow. They stopped at each cache site Isandro had marked on his map, knelt, did something with their hands, and then the smoke started. They were working down the route like men clearing a field, row by row, patient as weather.

"Rafa." Marisol's whisper came from behind him, low and careful. She'd taught him that word—*careful*—on the second day, when he'd grabbed her arm too hard pointing at condors. "What do you see?"

He didn't answer right away. He'd learned that too: never answer fast, because fast answers were usually wrong ones. He watched the nearest man pour something from a metal can onto a bundle of tarps, watched flame leap up orange and then flatten to almost nothing in the wind, watched the man stand and mark something on a clipboard before moving to the next site.

"They're not looking for anyone," he said finally. "They're erasing."

Isandro appeared at his shoulder, silent as always, moving through rock like he'd been poured into it. Rafael still couldn't predict him—one day cold as river stone, the next handing him an extra ration of dried meat without a word—but he'd learned to read the guide's silences, and this one went long and hard. When Isandro finally lowered the binoculars, his face had gone the color of the granite.

"That's the third cache line," he said quietly. "They're following our route backward. Which means they know we found the camp." He looked at Rafael, and there was something new in his eyes—not warmth, exactly, but recognition. The look you give someone who has proven useful. "Good eyes, chico. You saw them before I did."

Rafael felt the words land somewhere under his ribs and stay there. Nobody had ever told him his eyes were good. His eyes had only ever been good for running.

"We go down," Isandro said. "Now. Off the skyline, into the gorge. If they have glass on us—"

He didn't finish, because the wind shifted, carrying the smoke across the scree in a sour wave, and all three of them understood the rest at once.

* * *

The gorge took them in like a throat.

It was a crack in the mountain where the river ran high and white over brown stone, walled so steeply that the sky shrank to a bright ribbon overhead. Isandro led them along a goat track no wider than Rafael's boot, water thundering below, spray cold on his face even though the air had gone heavy and pre-monsoonal, thick enough to drink. Marisol carried the journal now, always, wrapped in oilskin inside her jacket, one hand pressed flat against it like she was holding a wound closed. She hadn't let Rafael carry it once. He understood why. He was the one who ran; things given to runners got dropped.

They made a cold camp in an overhang above the flood line, no fire, no talk, sharing hard biscuits and a strip of dried llama meat while the light died. Isandro sat facing the gorge mouth the whole time, motionless, listening. Rafael tried to copy him—to sit the way Isandro sat, spine loose, weight ready—and discovered it hurt muscles he didn't know he had. Staying still, he was learning, was harder than running. Running was easy. Running was what his legs did when his mind gave up.

Sometime past dark, Isandro's hand came up. *Stop chewing.*

Rafael froze mid-bite. Then he heard it too: voices, faint, threaded through the river's roar. Not up-slope. Downstream. Below them.

They crawled to the lip of the overhang and looked down into a bend of the gorge where it widened toward a hamlet of six houses clinging to the bank—a place so small it didn't have a name on any map Isandro owned. Lantern light moved between the houses. Men stood in the little plaza by the chapel, and their voices carried up the stone in broken pieces.

"—last seen Tuesday, maybe Wednesday—"

"—photographs, we need you to look at photographs—"

"—compensation for your cooperation, of course. The ministry is very grateful—"

Rafael pressed his cheek to wet rock and watched. There were five of them, dressed like hikers, clean gear, headlamps dimmed, and they moved among the villagers with a horrible gentleness. One of them held out photographs to an old woman who kept shaking her head. Another wrote in a notebook. A third stood apart, checking a radio, and when he lifted it to his mouth Rafael saw the logo stitched on his jacket sleeve—a blue diamond, sharp-edged, corporate, the same mark stamped on the survey instruments back at the abandoned camp.

"The HidroAndes mark," Marisol breathed beside him. "On their sleeves. They're not even hiding it."

"Why would they?" Isandro's voice was flat as slate. "Down here, who would believe a village woman over men with credentials? That's the whole design. They don't need to hide. They just need everyone else to be invisible." He said the last word like it tasted familiar, and Rafael, who had been invisible his whole life, felt it land strangely—like hearing your own name spoken by a stranger.

Below, the old woman was still shaking her head. One of the men put a hand on her shoulder, kindly, terribly kind, and steered her back toward her door. In the lantern light Rafael could see the village dogs had been shut away behind fences, quiet, and that every house had already been visited—the doors open, lamplight in each one, men inside doing whatever men like that did inside poor people's houses.

"They're asking if anyone saw us," Marisol whispered. "We bought eggs here. Two days ago. Doña Rosa fed us soup."

Rafael remembered the soup. Potato and wild mint, the first hot food in days, and the old woman had pinched his cheek and called him *flaco* and asked if his mother knew where he was. He'd lied and said yes. Now men with blue diamonds on their sleeves were standing in her plaza writing her name in notebooks.

Something happened in his chest then, something he didn't have words for yet. It wasn't courage—he'd heard about courage and knew better than to claim it. It was smaller and harder than that. It was the understanding that running had a direction now. All his life he'd run *away*, and away was everywhere and nowhere. But these people—Marisol with her hand pressed to the journal, Isandro sitting his silent watch, Doña Rosa with her soup—they weren't behind him anymore. They were ahead. And you couldn't run away from something that was ahead of you. You could only run toward it, or stand.

"I know her door," he whispered.

Both of them turned. In the dark, his voice sounded older than he was.

"The back way. Through the corral, past the drying racks. I went for water while you two argued about the route." He swallowed. "If they come back tomorrow—if they sweep up the gorge—we should warn her. Tell her to say she never saw us. Tell her what names not to say."

"It's too dangerous," Isandro said automatically, and Rafael heard it for what it was—not an order but a reflex, the reflex of a man who had survived ten years by never going back for anyone.

But Marisol was looking at Rafael in the starlight, and what was in her face made his ears burn. "He's right," she said. "Doña Rosa fed us. If those men decide she's lying, or decide she knows too much—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Everyone in the gorge knew how the survey team had ended, even if nobody had found the bodies.

Isandro was silent a long time. The river roared. Above them, far up the black wall of the cordillera, the fires of the sweep teams glowed faintly against the clouds like embers in a dying grate.

"All right," he said at last, and it cost him; Rafael could hear the cost in it. "Before first light. Rafa leads. We're ghosts—no noise, no tracks, twenty minutes, no more." He caught Rafael's chin with two fingers, rough and brief, the closest thing to a blessing the man knew how to give. "You watch the trail behind us. You see anything—any movement, any light—you say so. Your eyes are the best thing on this mountain."

Rafael nodded, because his throat had closed up.

* * *

They went down in the gray hour before dawn, when the cold is deepest and even sentries sleep. Rafael led them through the corral fence gap he'd memorized, past the sleeping llamas, along the ditch behind the drying racks where the mud held no prints because he made sure of it, placing each foot like a prayer. The village smelled of woodsmoke and animals and rain coming. Somewhere east, thunder muttered over the lowlands—the monsoon arriving early, Isandro had said, weeks early, which meant the passes would close and the rivers would rise and every safe way home would drown.

At Doña Rosa's back door, Rafael tapped the rhythm she'd used herself, three soft knocks, and when her face appeared in the gap, wrinkled and wary and then wide with fear, he put his finger to his lips.

"You never saw us," he whispered in her ear as she pulled him inside. "Not the boy, not the woman, not the guide. You fed some other travelers, weeks ago, you don't remember their faces. Say it boring. Boring people get forgotten."

She stared at him—at this flaco, this orphan, this child telling an old woman how to survive—and then, slowly, she nodded, and pressed a cloth bundle into his hands: bread, cheese, a handful of coca leaves. "Go with God, *flaco*," she murmured. "And don't come back."

Outside, the sky had begun to bruise. And as they slipped up the ditch toward the gorge, Rafael glanced back once and saw, far downstream around the riverbend, a boat with an outboard motor nosing against the current, five men in it, blue diamonds on their sleeves, coming to ask the village its questions a second time.

They had beaten them by minutes. Minutes.

Above, the first fat drops of monsoon rain struck the stone, hissing into dust, and Isandro looked up at the drowning sky and said, very quietly, the worst thing Rafael had ever heard him say:

"We're out of mountain. From here, there's only the river."
