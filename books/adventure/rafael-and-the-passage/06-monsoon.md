## 6. Monsoon

The rain did not arrive. It descended, all at once, like a verdict.

Isandro felt it before he heard it—a pressure change in his ears, a green smell rising off the valley floor, the birds going silent in a way that no bird ever went silent by accident. He had been reading the sky for thirty years, and the sky had been lying to him for two days: a high thin cirrus that should have meant another week of dry cold, a wind from the wrong quarter, warm air pushing up the gorge like breath through a keyhole. He had told himself it was nothing. He had told himself a lot of things lately.

It came at dusk, on the third day below the pass, and it came sideways.

The first drops hit the tarp over their camp like flung gravel, then the world simply turned to water. Not rain as he knew it—rain was weather, rain was rhythm, rain was something you waited out under stone with a fire and patience—but this was a standing column of water falling out of the dark, a sound so total it erased thought. The fire died in seconds. The river below them, which had been a silver thread forty meters down in its gorge, began to speak in a new voice.

By midnight it was shouting.

He lay awake in the wet dark listening to it, doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. The Quebrada Honda—the dry wash they'd followed down from the ridge, their road home—was filling. He could hear the boulders moving in the current, a deep grinding percussion like millstones turning underground. That sound meant the flood was carrying rock. Rock meant force. Force meant that everything he had planned on the walk down was now fiction.

His old plan had been simple, because his plans were always simple. Wait. Let the sweep teams burn what they wanted to burn and go home to their expense reports. Let the monsoon do what the monsoon always did, which was close the mountains for six weeks and make every trail a rumor. Nobody moved in monsoon season. Nobody found anybody in monsoon season. He would sit in the high country with Marisol and the boy and Elena's journal sealed in oilskin, and when the rains ended they would walk out over passes that were, by then, empty of everyone but condors. Invisibility through patience. It had worked for ten years. It had kept him alive when better men were dead.

He fell asleep telling himself it would work this time too.

* * *

Morning showed him the lie.

The Quebrada Honda was gone. Where there had been a wash of white cobbles and a trickle he could step across, there was now a brown ocean moving at the speed of a galloping horse, wide as a street, tearing at the banks with hands made of whole trees. Debris rafts rode the current—snagged trunks tangled with brush, spinning slowly, like wreckage from a drowned world. The spray came off the rapids downstream in cold clouds that drifted up and mingled with the actual rain until Isandro couldn't tell where the river ended and the sky began.

Rafael stood at the edge of the bank, small against the roar, watching it with an expression Isandro recognized because he'd worn it himself once, long ago, on another river: the look of someone recalculating everything they believed about how fast water could kill.

"It wasn't here yesterday," the boy said. Not fear, exactly. Accusation. As if the river had broken a promise.

"Rivers don't keep promises." Isandro crouched, touched the wet earth of the bank, watched it slump toward the water. Saturated. Unstable. Every hour the banks would retreat further, taking their footing with them. "This is early," he said, mostly to himself. "Monsoon doesn't peak for three weeks. This is the leading edge."

Marisol came up behind him with the journal strapped inside her jacket, oilskin wrapped twice around it. She hadn't let it out of her reach since the cache. "Then we're ahead of schedule or behind one."

"We're trapped." He said it flatly, because dressing it up would be worse. "The pass behind us is snowmelt and mudslides by now. The quebrada is a torrent. The only other way down is the gorge trail along the main river, and the main river is fed by everything you see falling out of that sky." He looked up at it—the gray ceiling of it, low enough to touch almost, raining with the steadiness of something that intended to continue forever. "In four days, maybe five, the gorge trail will be underwater too. Sections of it, anyway. After that we're not choosing routes anymore. We're choosing places to wait."

"Waiting was the plan," Rafael said.

"The plan is dead." He heard his own voice come out harder than he intended, and saw the boy flinch, and hated himself for it. He softened nothing else. They deserved the truth; softness now would be a kind of theft. "I've been waiting things out my whole life. Waiting is a skill. But waiting only works if the thing you're waiting out gets bored and leaves. Those men won't get bored. They're paid by the day, and the day they find us is the day they stop being paid, so every morning they wake up hoping today is the day." He turned to face them both, rain streaming off his jaw. "I built a doctrine around disappearing. It has failed me exactly once before, and I told myself never again, and here I am, and it's failing again."

Marisol studied him. She had their mother's eyes, though she'd gotten them from a different mother entirely—that was the strange arithmetic of half-siblings—and when she looked at him like that he felt read, thoroughly, like terrain.

"You knew the rains were coming early," she said. "You said the sky was lying."

"I said I hoped it was lying. Hoping isn't knowing." He wiped his face with a hand that wasn't quite steady, and pretended it was the cold. "There's a difference between a guide and a gambler, and I used to know which one I was."

* * *

They moved that afternoon, because staying put beside a rising river felt like standing in a grave that was being dug around them. The gorge trail clung to the south wall of the main valley, a shelf of packed earth and rotten log steps cut decades ago by mule trains and maintained by nobody since. In dry season it was a walk. Now it was a ledge above a drowning world, slick as soap, with water running down it in braided sheets that made every step a negotiation.

Isandro went first. He always went first; it was the one authority he had left. Behind him came Rafael with the coil of rope, then Marisol with the journal, and he had arranged them that way deliberately—if the slope took someone, it would take him alone, and the journal would survive him. He had done that math without telling anyone. Some math you carry privately.

The valley itself was becoming unrecognizable. The main river had swallowed its beaches, its gravel bars, the stands of aliso that lined its lower banks. Whole sections of forest slid past them in the current, upright trees sailing downstream like slow green ships. Once, a debris raft the size of a house roof ground against the far bank with a sound like a train derailing, held there ten minutes by the eddy, then tore free and vanished into the spray below the next rapid. Rafael watched it go with his mouth slightly open.

"Where does it all go?" the boy shouted over the water.

"Downhill," Isandro shouted back. "Everything goes downhill. The trick is going downhill slower than the water does."

That night they sheltered beneath an overhang of conglomerate rock, a shallow cave that dripped but did not pour, and Isandro sat at its mouth with his knees drawn up, watching the lightning walk along the ridgelines across the valley. Each flash printed the landscape on his eyes for half a second: the black teeth of the peaks, the brown flood, the ruin of the trail ahead. Then dark. Then the thunder arrived, rolling up the gorge like furniture being dragged across heaven.

He heard Marisol settle beside him. She didn't say anything for a while. Neither did he. There was a language of silence between them that had taken twenty years and one shared catastrophe to develop, and it was more fluent than anything they said aloud.

"You're doing the thing," she said finally.

"What thing."

"Where you carry all of it yourself and call it leadership." She pulled her jacket tighter. "Papá did it too. You both think worry is a private activity."

"Worry is useless. Planning is useful. I'm planning."

"You're grieving. You're sitting here mourning your own rules." She nodded out at the flood. "Your rule says wait. Your eyes say the men burning caches won't wait. So the rule dies, and you're treating it like a death instead of like information."

He almost laughed. Almost. "When did you get wise?"

"Raised by a stubborn man. Absorbed things." She leaned her head briefly against his shoulder, the first contact she'd initiated since the trailhead, since before the ridge, since years. "We're not invisible anymore, Isandro. We haven't been since the cache. Stop planning for invisibility. Start planning for arrival."

Arrival. The word landed in him and stayed there, glowing like an ember in wet grass.

Because she was right, and he had known it since the pass, and admitting it felt like pulling a splinter that had lodged against bone. Invisible people survived. Invisible people also accomplished nothing. He had spent a decade perfecting the art of mattering to no one, and it had brought him to a dripping cave with a stolen government secret in his sister's coat and a corporate death squad somewhere upstream, closing methodically, patient as erosion. His doctrine had one flaw, and the flaw was that it had no ending. You couldn't disappear your way into justice. You could only disappear your way into a longer life, and even that only sometimes.

Below them, the river roared on, eating the world.

* * *

It was Rafael who heard the engines.

Near dawn, in that gray hour when the rain thinned to a drifting mist and the gorge filled with a light like dirty milk, the boy's hand closed hard on Isandro's arm. Isandro came awake instantly, the way he'd trained himself to, all senses forward, and then he heard it too: motors. Two of them. Low gears, laboring. Coming up the valley from downstream—from the direction of the villages, the direction of home.

He bellied to the lip of the overhang and looked out. Through the mist, far below on the far bank, he picked them out: two trucks grinding along the old mining track, canvas-covered, moving with the unhurried confidence of vehicles whose drivers knew exactly where they were going. As he watched, the lead truck stopped. Men got out. Even at this distance he could read the choreography—not searching, sweeping. One man walked the bank. Another raised binoculars and panned them slowly along the south wall, along the very ledge Isandro lay on, and Isandro pressed himself flat against the cold stone and became, out of ten years' practice, part of the mountain.

The binoculars passed over him. Moved on. Came back.

Held.

For five heartbeats the lenses rested on the overhang, on the darkness beneath it where three bodies lay in shadow, and Isandro counted his pulse and did not breathe. Then a shout from below—one of the men waving, pointing upstream at something, a snag in the water perhaps, a flash of movement—and the lenses swung away, and the trucks ground on northward, hunting, and the moment closed over like water over a dropped stone.

But Isandro's heart kept its frantic count long after. Because he had seen, just before the lenses turned, the antenna on the lead truck's roof. A radio mast, tall and fresh, and beneath it painted on the cab door, half-erased but legible even through rain-blur, a logo he recognized from the dam documents in Elena's journal.

They weren't waiting for the monsoon to end. They were using it. Flooded trails, empty villages, washed-out roads—it was cover for them too. The season he had chosen as his shield was their shovel.

He crawled back beneath the overhang. Marisol was already awake, watching his face. Rafael sat up, eyes wide, saying nothing, learning silence from him the way he'd once learned everything from no one.

"They'll work the valley bottom for two days," Isandro said quietly. "Then the trail floods out completely and they pull back to the delta. Which means our window is the opposite of what I told you." He looked at each of them in turn, and heard his own doctrine crumble in his mouth, and spoke past its ruins. "We can't wait them out. We can't outrun them on foot. There's one way down this valley right now, and it's the river itself."

Marisol's jaw tightened. "Nobody runs the gorge in monsoon."

"Nobody sane does." He stared out at the brown flood, at the upright trees sailing past like the masts of a sunken fleet, and felt the last of his old self go quiet. "But there's a man at the delta with a boat. And he owes me a debt he's spent ten years pretending isn't there."

Rafael looked from him to the river and back. "Who?"

Isandro stood, and shouldered his pack, and gave the boy the name like a sentence being passed.

"Tomás Iriarte."
