## 7. The Pilot Who Owed Him

The river told them before any map did.

They came down out of the last forested bench at dawn, three days below the pass, and there it was: the Río Viento gone brown and swollen, carrying whole trees sideways in its current like a man shrugging off his coat. The monsoon had turned the water into something that no longer belonged to the valley. It belonged to the sky.

Isandro stood on the muddy shelf of the bank and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding for two days. Upstream was the pass—closed, drowned, a white wall of runoff where the trail used to be. Overland east meant the sweep teams, who would be working the tributaries now with the patience of men paid by the month. South was the gorge, forty kilometers of narrowed rock where the river ran fast and mean even in the dry season.

And boats. There were no boats. Except one.

"Tomás Iriarte," he said aloud, and the name tasted like river silt.

Marisol looked up from rewrapping the journal in its oilskin. "Who?"

"A pilot. Works—or worked—the lower gorge out of Puerto Ceniza." Isandro crouched, touched the water, let it push against his palm with the weight of a living thing. "Barge and skiff runs. Supply drops to the villages when the roads wash out. He's the only man fool enough to be on this water right now."

"And you know him how?"

Isandro wiped his hand on his trousers. "He testified against me."

* * *

It had been eleven years, and he could still see the hearing room: the government men in their pressed shirts, the rescue commission's report spread across the table like an autopsy, and Tomás Iriarte sitting in the witness chair with his hat crushed in both hands, saying the words that ended Isandro's career in two countries. *Halloran ordered us down the north chute. I told him the water was wrong. He said we'd lose the climbers if we waited.* Whether it was true no longer mattered; the inquiry had needed someone to blame, and Isandro had been the one whose name was on the permit. Two dead climbers. One blacklisted guide. And a pilot who had walked away clean, his own errors folded neatly into testimony against another man.

"He lied," Marisol said, when Isandro finished, in as few words as he could manage.

"Probably. Or half-lied. It doesn't matter anymore." He watched a half-submerged log grind past against the rocks. "What matters is that he owes me. Not money. Something worse than money."

Rafael, perched on a root above the bank, spat into the current. "You trust a man who betrayed you?"

"No," Isandro said. "But shame is more reliable than trust. A man who's ashamed will do almost anything to stop feeling it. For about a day. That's exactly long enough."

* * *

Puerto Ceniza was less a town than an apology—a scatter of tin roofs and drying nets on a mudflat where the gorge opened its throat. They reached it at dusk, coming down through the banana plots with Rafael in front because the boy moved quiet as smoke and saw everything first. The village was half-abandoned for the season; monsoon meant the river owned the place, and everyone with sense had moved uphill.

Everyone except Tomás Iriarte.

His barge sat moored at the rickety pier, riding low, its black hull streaked with rust like old tears. Beside it, tied off, was the skiff—aluminum, scarred, maybe seven meters, with an outboard that looked older than Rafael. And on the pier itself, under a tarp strung between posts, sat a broad-shouldered man drinking coffee from an enamel pot, watching them come down the path as if he'd been expecting them for eleven years.

"Halloran." Tomás set the pot down. His voice hadn't changed—gravel poured over honey. "I heard you were dead."

"You heard wrong."

"I hoped I'd heard wrong." He stood, slowly, joints cracking like green wood. He was heavier now, gray at the temples, but the hands were the same—huge, scarred, a pilot's hands. His eyes went over Isandro's shoulder to Marisol and Rafael, and something flickered there. "You brought children into the Cordillera."

"I brought people who chose to come. Same as your crew always did."

"That's not fair."

"No," Isandro agreed. "None of this is. That's rather the point."

The rain started then, soft at first, ticking on the tarp. Tomás looked up at it the way a farmer looks at locusts. "You want the gorge."

"I need the gorge."

"At this height?" Tomás laughed, but there was no humor in it, only exhaustion. "Nobody runs the gorge in monsoon. Nobody runs it in half this water. The eddies are gone. The read is gone. You can't see the rocks—you can only remember them, and memory lies."

"Then you'll run it from memory, and you'll correct for the lying."

For a long moment the only sound was rain on canvas and the river grinding past in the dark, patient as debt. Then Tomás picked up the enamel pot and refilled it, and his hands were not quite steady.

"Why?" he asked quietly. "Eleven years, Halloran. You could have come to me any time. Why now, soaked through, with a boy and a woman and whatever it is you're all pretending isn't in that oilskin roll?"

Isandro weighed the truth and found, to his surprise, that he had no appetite left for anything else. "A survey team went into those mountains. Six people. They found something upstream—something about the dam—and they didn't disappear. They were erased. We have what they died for. And there are men behind us burning every cache and questioning every villager between here and the pass, because they want it back." He held Tomás's eyes. "That's why now. Because if I wait for a better moment, there won't be one."

Tomás drank his coffee. Set it down. Looked at the black water for a long time.

"The hearing," he said finally. "You want to know if I lied."

"I stopped wanting to know years ago."

"I'll tell you anyway, then, since it's the only currency I have." His voice dropped low enough that the rain nearly took it. "I panicked. On the north chute. I froze at the stick for four seconds—maybe five—and when it was over and they were gone, I couldn't live with four seconds, so I made it your order instead. Made it your whole life instead of my four seconds." He turned and looked at Isandro directly, and his eyes were wet, though whether from rain or the other thing was impossible to say. "Two men died because I hesitated, and I let them bury you for it. Every year since, I've run this river telling myself the water would collect the debt someday. Well." He gestured at the flood, the dark, the three strangers on his pier. "Here it comes."

Isandro felt nothing he had prepared for. He had rehearsed rage for a decade—carried it like ballast—and now, offered the target, the rage wouldn't fire. There was only tiredness, and something underneath it that might have been pity, and the cold fact of the water rising.

"Four seconds," Isandro said. "I've thought about it every day for eleven years, and I never once considered four seconds. I built a whole life on being the man who got people killed on purpose." He shook his head slowly. "You should have told me. Not the inquiry. Me."

"I know."

"It doesn't fix anything."

"I know that too."

Isandro looked at the skiff, at the swollen river, at the boy shivering under the tarp with Marisol's arm around him. The arithmetic was ugly and simple. "Then here's the price. You take us down the gorge tonight, before first light, while the sweep teams are still thinking we're trapped above the pass. You get us to the delta landing alive. After that, we're square—not friends, not forgiven. Square."

Tomás nodded once, like a man accepting a sentence. "There's a leak in the skiff's stern seam. I've patched it twice. It'll hold if we bail."

"It had better."

"And Halloran—" Tomás paused, jaw working. "If we go in the water down there, nobody's coming. You understand that. Whatever happens in the gorge stays in the gorge."

"Then we'd best not go in."

* * *

They loaded in the dark. Marisol wedged herself amidships with the journal strapped inside her jacket, oilskin over oilskin, her arms crossed over it like a woman holding a door shut against wind. Rafael took the bail bucket without being told—he simply saw the stern seam weeping a thin silver thread of river and stationed himself there, and Isandro felt something turn over in his chest that he didn't examine.

Tomás stood at the tiller, testing the engine, muttering to it in the low voice men use for animals and machines they don't fully trust. The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the gorge like a barrel going downstairs.

"One thing," Tomás said, not looking at any of them. "When I say flat—everyone flat. When I say paddle—paddle like God himself is watching. And nobody touches the tiller but me. Agreed?"

Three voices. Even Rafael's.

Isandro cast off the line and felt the river take the skiff the way a fist takes a bird—gently at first, then completely. The black water slid them backward, turned them, and pointed them south into the gorge, where the walls rose on either side like the pages of a closed book.

Behind them, faint through the rain, an engine sound that wasn't theirs carried down the valley road toward Puerto Ceniza.

Tomás heard it too. His shoulders tightened.

"They're early," Isandro said.

"Then we're late," said Tomás, and gave the throttle everything it had.
