## 8. White Water, Black Boat

The river did not look like water anymore. It looked like the mountain had stood up and decided to leave.

Rafael crouched in the bow of Tomás Iriarte's boat—a flat-bottomed black thing patched with tar and prayer—and watched the gorge swallow the light. The walls rose on either side, wet black stone streaked with white mineral veins, so close in places that he could have touched both sides with outstretched arms. Between them, the river ran brown and swollen, carrying whole trees sideways, their roots clawing at the air like drowned men reaching for the bank.

"Sit down," Tomás said. He stood at the stern with one hand on the tiller and one on the engine's choke lever, his gray hair plastered to his skull by spray. "And whatever you do, boy, don't grab the gunwale when we hit. You'll lose the fingers."

Rafael sat. His hands wouldn't stay still. They kept opening and closing on his knees, on the bail bucket, on nothing.

* * *

They had put in before dawn, below the ruined ford where the trail home had vanished under floodwater. Isandro had stood on the mudbank for a long time, looking upriver as if the answer might come walking down it, and then he'd said, quietly, "There's no way over. There's only through." And Marisol had looked at Tomás, and Tomás had looked at his boat, and nobody had said the other name that hung between them—the name of the hearing six years ago, the testimony, the blacklist. It was there anyway. Rafael could feel it sitting in the boat with them like a fourth passenger who weighed nothing and everything.

Now the engine coughed against the current and the first rapid came up fast, a seam of white torn across the brown.

"Hold!" Tomás shouted, and the world tilted.

The boat climbed the standing wave like an animal rearing, hung there—Rafael's stomach floating free of him—and then slammed down into the trough and the river came aboard. Cold hit him like a slap. Water surged around his ankles, around his shins, swirling with grit and pine needles and something soft that might have been a fish or might not. Ahead, the gorge bent left and the sound changed, deepened, became less like rushing water and more like a crowd roaring in a stadium.

"Bail!" Isandro roared from midships, already bailing with the dented pot they'd brought for cooking. "Bail like your life is the water!"

It was. Rafael understood that with a clarity that felt almost peaceful. He snatched the bucket and threw himself into the rhythm—scoop, throw, scoop, throw—each haul of water over the side a small argument with the river, and losing any single argument meant losing all of them. The bilge was a hand's width deep when they entered the bend. By the third wave it was at his knees.

* * *

The helicopter found them at the narrows.

Rafael heard it before he saw it—that flat, patient chopping, like someone knocking on a door they intended to open no matter what. He looked up and there it was above the gorge wall, a dark shape skimming the ridgeline, close enough that he could see the sun flash off its glass. It didn't have search markings. Search helicopters flew grids. This one flew *them*, dropping low, tilting, keeping pace with the boat the way a hawk keeps pace with a rabbit it hasn't decided to strike yet.

"They're not allowed down here," Marisol shouted. She had the journal wrapped in oilskin now, three layers of it, tied with cord, strapped across her chest under her jacket like a second heart. "This airspace is closed in monsoon. They're breaking their own rules."

"That's what money buys," Tomás said through his teeth, and fed the engine more fuel.

The rotor wash reached them a moment later—a downdraft that flattened the river's surface into a shivering plate and shoved the boat sideways toward the rock wall. Rafael saw the black stone swing toward him and did the thing every instinct screamed against: he leaned *away*, dug the bail bucket into the water on the far side, and used the drag to pull the bow back around. It worked. It worked barely. The hull kissed the rock with a grinding scrape that he felt in his spine, and then they were through, and the helicopter lifted off over the ridge, unhurried, satisfied, a promise postponed.

"It'll be waiting downstream," Isandro said. He wasn't looking at the sky. He was looking at Rafael, and there was something in his face Rafael had never seen there before—not approval exactly, because approval came from above, and this was level, eye to eye. "Good hands."

Rafael bailed harder, so no one would see what that did to him.

* * *

The worst rapid came where the map said none should be.

Tomás knew it too. Rafael saw him mouth something—*shouldn't be here, shouldn't be here*—and then the old man did something extraordinary: he killed the engine. In the sudden silence the river's voice filled the gorge like a held breath finally released, and Rafael understood why. An engine could be flooded. An engine could be sheared off by a submerged log. What remained was Tomás's arms, the oars, and sixty years of a river running through his blood.

"Low brace," Tomás said, mostly to himself. "Everyone low. Boy—when I say *left*, you throw your weight left. Not before. Before kills us faster than after."

The rapid announced itself as a horizon problem: the river simply ended ahead, pouring over a lip into chaos. Logs turned in the foam below like the spokes of some vast slow wheel. Rafael got low, cheek nearly against the wet planking, the oilskin bundle pressing cold against Marisol's back where she crouched behind him, her arms locked around the journal and around him both.

"Now," Tomás breathed, more prayer than command. "*Left.*"

Rafael threw himself left. The boat obeyed him—it *obeyed him*—swinging its nose between two black fangs of rock with a hand's width to spare on either side, and then the world dissolved. For ten seconds, maybe twenty, there was no boat, no gorge, no Rafael; there was only brown thunder and cold and the animal certainty that he was going to die, and beneath that certainty, absurdly, a kind of joy, because he was dying *doing something*, as crew, as someone counted on, and not alone in a ditch behind a work camp where nobody would even look for the body.

Then the boat surfaced, half full, spinning, and Tomás was laughing—a cracked, wild, disbelieving laugh—and Isandro was bailing with both hands and shouting numbers of inches, and they were through.

* * *

They bailed and drifted and bailed through the long gray afternoon. The leak was worse now—the old patch below the waterline had begun to weep in a thin silver thread that no amount of tar seemed to shame into stopping. Rafael learned its rhythm the way he'd once learned the rhythms of guards and work-camp bells: when it quickened, when it lied, when it could be trusted. Scoop, throw. Scoop, throw. His shoulders burned. His palms were raw. He didn't stop.

Marisol unwrapped the journal once, briefly, in a stretch of calm water, just to check. The pages had held. Elena's tight survey figures stared up out of the lamplight-proof dark of the oilskin like seeds waiting underground. Marisol retied the cords with fingers gone clumsy from cold, and Rafael saw her look at Isandro, and saw Isandro shake his head once—*not yet, not here*—and saw her nod and wrap it away again. Whatever they were going to do with the truth, they would do it at the end of the river, not in the middle of it.

Twice more the helicopter came. Once it hovered so long that Tomás cut the engine and let them drift as flotsam among the storm debris, invisible by being unremarkable, and Rafael lay flat among the branches and sodden rope and pretended, with his whole body, to be garbage. The second time it only passed high, heading downriver, toward the delta.

Toward wherever they were going.

"They know where we land," Isandro said quietly, when the sound had faded. "There's one landing. We can't run the delta in this water and we can't portage it. So they'll be there."

Tomás said nothing. He rowed. But Rafael, watching the old man's back, saw the set of his shoulders change—the shoulders of a man rowing toward something he owed.

* * *

Dusk came down into the gorge like water filling a cup. The walls fell away at last, the current slackened, and the river spread wide and sullen toward a darkness ahead where Rafael could smell salt and diesel and woodsmoke—the delta, the landing, the end of the passage and the beginning of whatever came after.

He kept bailing anyway. The thread of the leak ran steady and silver in the last light, and his arms were beyond burning into something numb and obedient, and he realized he wasn't bailing because he was afraid anymore. He was bailing because the boat needed it, because these people needed it, because somewhere between the first wave and the narrows he had stopped being a boy hiding in a truck and become the reason a boat stayed above the waterline.

Marisol put her hand on his shoulder. "Rest a minute. We're almost there."

Rafael shook his head. "I've got it," he said, and heard, in his own voice, someone he hadn't met before this week.

Ahead, lights pricked the dark along the delta landing—more lights than a landing needed, arranged in a line, patient as the helicopter had been patient. A figure stood among them, motionless, watching the river come down to meet him.

Isandro went very still at the tiller. "That's him," he said. "That's the fixer."
