## 1. The River Gives Back

The river was low enough to lie about its age.

Isandro Dravenmoor noticed it first as a smell—the mineral breath of exposed mud, that old-coin stink the Serrit gave off when it dropped below its banks and showed the bones it had been hiding all season. Ten years of flying this stretch and he'd learned to read the water like weather. High water meant work: fog on the strips, current grabbing at the pontoon tips, cargo runs timed to the inch. Low water meant something else. Low water meant the river was talking.

He didn't like listening.

The Cessna came off the water at Kettle Bend with a half-load of medicine crates lashed in the back—penicillin for the mission at Two Sisters, insulin for old Marta Vey who'd outlasted three doctors and intended to outlast him too—and Isandro flew the way he always flew, low and unhurried, one eye on the channel and one on nothing at all. The morning was the color of hammered tin. Mist sat in the cuts between hills like something poured there overnight.

Below him, the Serrit ran narrow and brown-green, braiding around gravel bars that hadn't seen sun since spring. He knew every one of them by shape. The Horseshoe. The Widow's Teeth. The long gray spine they called the Deacon because it looked, from altitude, like a man kneeling.

It was at the Deacon that he saw the wreck.

He saw it before he understood it, which was how the river always got you. A dark suggestion under two feet of glassy water, longer than a log, straighter than a log, with a geometry no river made. His hands were already easing back on the yoke before his mind caught up, and the plane sank into a slow descending turn, floats kissing down on the flat water behind the gravel bar with a sound like a held breath let go.

He told himself it was driftwood. He told himself a lot of things in those thirty seconds, and none of them believed each other.

* * *

Up close, the thing under the water was an aircraft.

Not a bush plane, not anything that belonged here. The fuselage rose from the mud at a drunken angle, skin gone green-black with ten years—or twenty, or thirty—of silt and weed, but the lines were still clean under the grime. Military lines. Transport lines. He could see where a wing had sheared and been buried, could see the row of small square windows along the flank that no civilian airframe ever carried.

His boots hit the shallows before he'd decided to get out of the plane. Cold came up through the leather immediately, the kind of cold that had been sitting in the dark a long time. The water never really warmed in this stretch; it came down off the icefields and kept their temper all summer.

He stood hip-deep beside the hull and did not touch it.

There was a number stenciled on the flank, mostly eaten away. What remained was a letter and part of another—a **V**, and the ghost of an **S**—and Isandro's chest went tight in a way he recognized the way you recognize a face across a market crowd: instantly, unwillingly, completely.

VT-S. He didn't need the rest. He'd painted that prefix on three airframes himself, once, in a hangar that officially had never existed, in a country that officially had never existed either.

Vessania.

The word didn't make noise when he thought it. It made silence. It was a silence he'd carried for ten years, packed down under flight logs and fuel receipts and the careful, boring texture of an invisible life. He'd gotten good at it. You could bury a nation if you shoveled fast enough and never looked back at the hole.

The river had just looked back for him.

* * *

The chest was wedged under the broken wing root, half-exposed where the current had scoured a trench through the mud.

Rust-orange, iron-bound, small enough to carry with one arm if you were willing to hurt your back doing it—which Isandro was not, standing in moving water with a medical run waiting at Two Sisters. He crouched over it instead, water sliding past his thighs, and looked at the thing stamped into the lid.

A seal. Pressed into a plate bolted to the wood, tarnished almost black, but the relief still sharp underneath the corrosion: a mountain split by a river, crowned with seven stars. The arms of Vessania. He'd saluted that seal. He'd burned documents bearing it. He'd watched men die for it and then watched the world agree, politely and in writing, that it had never been anyone's arms at all.

The plate was loose. One bolt had rusted through; the other held by a thread of itself. When his fingers found the edge—purely to steady himself, he told himself, purely to keep his balance in the current—the plate shifted with a small grinding click, like a lock deciding.

He should leave it.

That was the whole discipline of his life, distilled: leave it. Leave the past where the water put it. Fly the medicine, drink the bad coffee at the trading post, fix the float strut Tam kept meaning to fix, be the man with no history who was useful precisely because he asked for nothing and offered less. Odile had never once asked where he came from. Pip, who asked everyone everything about everything, had somehow never asked him. That wasn't luck. That was a family quietly agreeing to protect a man from himself, and him letting them, and all of them pretending the arrangement wasn't an arrangement.

If he took the plate, he was answering a knock he'd spent a decade pretending not to hear.

The current pushed at his legs, patient, insistent, like a hand on the small of his back.

He thought about the row of small square windows. He thought about how many seats a transport that size would carry. Eleven, maybe twelve, plus crew. He thought about a night flight he'd been ordered to abandon, and a voice on a radio going from words to static to nothing, and the specific weight of flying home alone with a full fuel load and an empty heart.

He hadn't known where the transport went down. That had been the mercy of it, or the cruelty—he'd never been sure which. Somewhere in the Serrit system, command had said. Somewhere. The river keeps what the river keeps.

The river, apparently, was done keeping.

"Damn you," Isandro said quietly, to the water, to the seal, to whatever clerk in some drowned ministry had stamped this chest and sent it out onto a night flight ten years ago. His voice sounded strange over the river, thin, like it belonged to someone younger.

He worked the second bolt with his thumbnail and a stone until it shrieked loose. The plate came away into his palm—cold, heavier than it looked, the seven stars biting into his skin where he gripped it too hard.

He stood there a moment longer, water pulling at him, the wreck green and patient beneath the surface, the rest of the chest still buried in the mud with whatever else the river had decided to give back. Manifests. Orders. Names. Maybe tags. Maybe worse.

Then he waded back to the plane, climbed up on the float, and stowed the plate in the map case behind his seat, under the charts, under the pencils, under everything ordinary.

* * *

Two Sisters came and went. Marta Vey complained that the insulin vials rattled and that he flew like a man being chased, and Isandro laughed in the right places and signed the ledger in the right places and was, by every measurable standard, exactly himself.

But on the leg home, climbing over the pass with the evening light turning the snowfields pink, he caught himself reaching back to touch the map case. Three times. Four. Not checking that the plate was there—he knew it was there the way you know a splinter—but touching it anyway, the way you'd touch a bruise to confirm it still hurts.

By the time he set down at Poste Serrit, the lamps were lit along the dock and the smell of frying onions was coming off Odile's kitchen, and for one whole minute, walking up the boards with the crate straps over his shoulder, he felt almost normal. Almost invisible.

Then he saw the notice nailed to the post office door, fresh paper, ink still sharp against the fading bills around it, and stopped so abruptly that Pip nearly ran into his back.

Salvage license posted. Fleet inbound. Dredge rights, full recovery rights, the whole grammar of official greed laid out in tidy paragraphs.

And a name at the bottom, in a hand he'd have known anywhere, even after ten years, even drowned:

**M. Kolvek.**

The plate in the map case seemed suddenly very heavy against his spine.

Six tides, the notice said. The fleet arrived in six.
