## 2. Six Tides

The trading post at Graywater Bend smelled of diesel, tar, and the sweet rot of fruit nobody had ordered in a month. Isandro tied off at the dock with hands that wouldn't quite steady, and told himself it was the wind.

The seal plate sat in his jacket pocket like a coal that hadn't finished burning. He'd checked for it four times on the flight downriver—four times, in a plane he'd flown through worse weather half-asleep. That was what the thing did to you. It made you check your pockets like a man checking a wound.

Odile was on the porch of the post office, sorting mail into crates with the economy of someone who'd done it twenty years and resented every one of them. She was sixty if she was a day, built like driftwood, with forearms that could win an argument against most men's whole bodies. She didn't look up when he climbed the steps.

"You're late," she said.

"River's low."

"River's always low this time of year." She glanced at him then, quick and sharp as a fish hook, and whatever she saw made her set down the envelope she was holding. "What happened to your face?"

He'd forgotten about the scrape. The chest had given it to him when he hauled the lid aside—jagged rust, a shallow line along his cheekbone. "Slipped on the bank."

"On the bank." Odile repeated it the way a judge repeats testimony she doesn't believe but hasn't decided how to punish. "Sit down, Isandro."

"I've got cargo to—"

"Sit *down*."

He sat. That was the thing about Odile Marchetti: ten years ago she'd found him sleeping in the back of his own plane at her dock, broke and lying about why, and instead of running him off she'd fed him stew and asked no questions. In exchange, he'd never given her a reason to ask any. It had been a good bargain. It had held for a decade.

She poured coffee from the pot that lived on the stove even in summer, black enough to stand a spoon in, and set it in front of him without sitting herself. She stayed standing by the window, arms crossed, looking out at the river the way farmers look at clouds.

"Maren Kolvek," she said.

The name went through him like cold water through a wetsuit. "What about her?"

"Fleet of six barges out of Corvane Harbor. Licensed salvage, papers stamped by the Interior Ministry, which means stamped by whoever paid the Interior Ministry this quarter." Odile turned from the window. "They're coming upriver for the wreck field below the narrows. Everything the drought exposed, they're contracted to strip. Copper, brass, engines, hull plating. Anything with weight gets weighed and sold."

"How far out?"

"Six tides." She let that sit. "Three days, Isandro. And here you come down the river with rust on your hands and a story about slipping on the bank."

* * *

He made himself drink the coffee. Around them the post filled with its ordinary noises—Pip arguing with Tam about a carburetor somewhere behind the parts shed, the radio crackling a freight schedule, a dog scratching itself against the porch rail. Ordinary sounds. He'd spent ten years building a life out of ordinary sounds, and now every one of them seemed to be counting down.

"Kolvek," he said finally. "You know her?"

"Everybody knows her name. She salvages clean and pays fair and doesn't cheat the little posts, which around here makes her practically a saint." Odile's mouth twisted. "But I've heard things too. That she works for people who like their salvage quiet. That when her crews find something awkward, it goes overboard at night and the manifest says it never came up."

"That's gossip."

"That's arithmetic." Odile crossed the room and sat across from him, leaning forward on her forearms. "Isandro. Look at me."

He looked at her. Her eyes were brown and patient and absolutely unfooled.

"Whatever you pulled out of that mud—and don't tell me you didn't, because you flew in here flying like a man being chased by his own shadow—it needs to go back in the water or it needs to leave this river entirely. Tonight. And then you need to be gone before those barges round the bend."

"Gone where?"

"Gone. Anywhere. You've got a plane and a full tank and no ties worth the name—" She stopped. He watched her hear her own words, watch something flicker behind her face, regret maybe, or just the honesty she'd never been able to help. "That came out wrong."

"No," Isandro said quietly. "It came out exactly right. That's the whole point of me, isn't it? No ties worth the name. That was the arrangement."

"The arrangement was that you were safe here."

"And I was. Until the river gave something back."

* * *

Pip came in then, all elbows and eleven years old, carrying a coil of fuel line and a question about whether Mr. Dravenmoor would look at the tail strut because Tam said the rivets were singing. Pip talked the way rivers run—all at once, in every direction—and for two minutes Isandro got to live inside somebody else's urgency, and it was almost a mercy.

Then Pip spotted the scrape on his cheek and went quiet in a way children shouldn't know how to do, and said, "You're leaving, aren't you? Everyone's doing the face."

"We're not talking about that," Odile said.

"You're totally talking about that." But Pip set the fuel line down carefully, like an offering, and backed toward the door, and paused there. "If you're going, take me up first. Before you go. You promised."

"I remember what I promised."

Pip nodded once, solemn as a treaty, and was gone. Isandro stared at the closed door longer than the moment deserved.

"Ties worth the name," Odile said softly. "See what you did there anyway, without meaning to. Ten years of careful, and you went and got yourself adopted by a child."

"It wasn't on purpose."

"Nothing worth having ever is." She stood, restless now, and went to the window again. Down at the dock, Tam had emerged from the parts shed and was looking up at Isandro's plane with a mechanic's frown, and beyond Tam the river lay low and brown and patient, holding everything it had ever taken, giving back only what it chose.

"Here's what I know," Odile said. "Kolvek's divers are good. Best on the coast, some say. If there's anything down there worth finding, they'll find it in six tides, and whatever they find will end up in a warehouse in Corvane owned by men whose names we'll never learn. So either that chest of yours is junk—which it isn't, or you wouldn't have that look—or it's the kind of thing that ruins people. And I have watched you long enough to know you'd rather burn than be ruined slow."

"Odile—"

"So fly out tonight. Take the chest, take whatever else you took, and disappear the way you're good at. Let the fleet strip copper off dead boats. Let them dig up nothing that points at you. In a year you can come back and Pip will have forgiven you and I'll pretend I wasn't scared, and everything goes on being ordinary." She turned to face him fully. "Please, Isandro. I'm asking. I almost never ask."

* * *

And there it was—the crack, right down the middle of him, clean as ice splitting underfoot.

On one side: the life. The post, the dock, the medicine runs, the stew, the kid who wanted a ride before he left. Ten years of invisibility, purchased honestly, kept honestly. On the other side: a transport on the riverbed with a crew compartment still sealed, and names inside it that the world had been told never existed, and a man named Dravenmoor who had flown away from all of it while the water closed over the evidence.

He thought about the seal plate in his pocket. Vessania. A country that had drowned twice—once in fact, once in paperwork. He'd served it. He'd buried it. He'd built a new self on top of the grave and called it a life.

"If I fly out," he said slowly, "they dredge the wreck anyway. Kolvek's contract covers the whole field. My chest changes nothing except where I'm standing when they find the rest."

"Then find deeper water for it."

"There is no deeper water, Odile. There's just time. Six tides of it." He set the cup down. His hand was steady now, which frightened him more than the shaking had. "You want me invisible. I understand why. Invisible is safe—for me, for you, for everyone at this post. But invisible is also how it happened the first time. Men went into the water and the world agreed not to look, and I was one of the men who agreed."

Odile's jaw worked. "You don't owe the dead anything. The dead don't know."

"They know," Isandro said. "That's the worst part. They know exactly."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The radio muttered. Somewhere outside, Pip was telling Tam, in a voice pitched deliberately loud, that Mr. Dravenmoor was definitely leaving and Tam should say something about it, and Tam's low answer was lost beneath the wind.

"Six tides," Odile said at last, heavily, like a woman setting down something heavier than mail. "You're going to use them, aren't you. All six."

"I haven't decided."

"Liar." But there was no heat in it. She came around the table and put her hand on the back of his neck, brief and hard, the closest thing to an embrace she permitted herself. "Then hear me. Whatever you decide, you decide it as one of us. Not as a ghost passing through. You've been one of us since the stew, and I'll deny saying so."

He looked up at her, and for the first time in ten years he wanted—not safety, not silence, but to deserve the sentence she'd just handed him.

Outside, the light was going bronze over the narrows, and far downriver, past the last bend, six tides were already draining away, one after another, toward the sound of engines he couldn't yet hear.

By morning, he would learn they were closer than anyone thought.
