## 3. What the Water Keeps

The moon didn't rise until nearly eleven, and by then I was already standing on the mudflat in my wetsuit, looking at water that had dropped another hand's width since morning.

I told myself it was a survey. That was the word I used, the way a man uses *inventory* when he means *confession*. A survey of the wreck the river had decided to give back after ten years of keeping its mouth shut. I'd flown my medicine run to Kessik and back, delivered the insulin, signed the ledger, smiled at nobody, and come home to a night that felt like a held breath.

The chest was still there where the low water had exposed it, half-buried in silt, its lid furred with rust and zebra mussels. The seal plate on top caught the starlight—Vessania's triple wave inside a broken crown. A nation that never existed, stamped on metal that very much did.

I'd pocketed the plate that first afternoon like a thief, like a coward, like a man taking evidence out of his own crime scene. It sat now in the tin box under my cot, under the false bottom, under ten years of careful nothing.

Tonight I was going into the water.

* * *

The Vessian transport went down in the spring flood of the year I stopped being anyone. That was the official story anyway: lost with all hands during an unscheduled river crossing, cause undetermined, records sealed. Records were always sealed, in Vessania. Sealed was the national language.

What actually happened lived in me like shrapnel.

We'd been running the northern corridor—me flying lead in the Otter, Sefa Vane as my second, the Okonkwo twins on the radios, Dr. Ilmari Halden in the back with the medical crates that weren't medical crates. Nine of us. We'd been told the corridor was clear. We'd been told a lot of things.

The order came in at 0400, flat and calm over a channel that crackled like burning paper. *Dravenmoor. Break off. Return to base. Do not acknowledge.*

Sefa looked at me across the cockpit, her headset half off one ear, waiting for me to argue. That was my job. I argued about weather, about fuel loads, about the twins' card debts. I was supposed to argue about this.

"Isandro," she said. "They're still on the ground at the crossing."

"I heard the order."

"I heard it too. I'm asking if you heard *it*."

The river below us was rising. I could see the transport on the far bank, engines cold, crew visible as small dark shapes moving cargo in the rain. Halden was down there. Halden had taught me to suture a chest wound with fishing line and a bent needle.

I turned north.

I have replayed that turn maybe ten thousand times, and every time the yoke moves a little easier in my hands, which is the cruelest part. Memory is kind to pilots. It lets the turn happen faster each year until eventually you barely turned at all, until the whole thing is just weather you flew through.

The transport flooded two days later. All nine. The nation that never existed buried them in a cemetery that wasn't on any map, and I got a commendation for judgment under orders, and then I got out, and then I got here, to a river town where nobody asked questions because questions cost money and everybody was broke.

Ten years of ferrying insulin and pretending the weight in the crates was all I carried.

* * *

The water took me at the shoulders and then at the chin, and the cold closed over my head like a door.

Night diving alone is stupid. I knew that. Tam would've said it out loud, Pip would've said it louder, Odile would've said nothing and simply stood on the bank with her arms crossed until I came back up or didn't. Which is why none of them knew I was here.

I went down the anchor line I'd rigged at dusk—a length of chain off the old dock, weighted, dropped plumb onto the shadow of the hull. Visibility was maybe three meters by moonlight, less than that in the layers where the river kept its sediment like a secret. My light made a small yellow room around me and nothing else existed outside it. That was fine. Small rooms were what I was built for.

The hull rose out of the murk at eight meters, and my stomach dropped before my body did.

It wasn't a barge. It wasn't salvage scrap. It was a Vessian military transport, the K-11 airframe, twin-boom tail still mostly intact, one propeller blade bent back like a finger pointing at the surface. Ten years underwater had given it a skin of black silt and white bacterial lace, but the lines were unmistakable. I'd serviced that airframe class. I'd flown beside them. I knew the rivet pattern the way you know a face.

My heart was going hard enough that I could hear it through the water, through the regulator, through everything.

*Survey,* I told myself. *Just a survey.*

I swam the length of the fuselage with my glove trailing the plating, counting frame stations out of habit. At the wing root the silt had scoured away and I saw the stencil, faint but legible under my light: **K-11 / 44-VES / CORRIDOR AUTH**. Corridor Authority. The letters that had ordered me north while nine people drowned in a rising river.

Something in my chest did what the river had done—receded, all at once, exposing things I'd spent a decade keeping submerged.

* * *

The last time I'd seen Sefa Vane alive she was throwing a wrench at Tam's counterpart, a mechanic named Brull who'd cross-wired a magneto. She had a laugh like a door coming off its hinges. She kept her mother's dog tags on a cord inside her flight suit even though regs forbade it, and when the inspector flagged it once she'd said, "Arrest me, then," and he hadn't, because everyone was a little afraid of Sefa and a little in love with her, including me, including probably the inspector.

The twins, Ede and Rassan Okonkwo, finished each other's radio calls so seamlessly that command once accused them of sharing a single brain, and they'd taken it as a compliment. Halden drank tea from a dented tin cup and called everyone *youngster*, including the base commander, who was fifty.

Nine names. I could say them in my sleep, and often did, waking myself in the dark of my shack above the trading post with the taste of river water in my mouth.

*Do not acknowledge.* I'd acknowledged nothing for ten years. Not to Vessania, which no longer existed to acknowledge anything to. Not to Odile, who fed me stew and never pried. Not to the kid who painted my tail fins and thought I was just a pilot, just a man who hauled boxes.

The tags were down there. I knew it the way you know a scar is still there under your sleeve.

* * *

The crew compartment hatch was aft of the wing root, and it was open—not torn, not blown, just open, the way a crew leaves it when they're working and the water comes fast. That detail hit me harder than any wreckage. They hadn't panicked. They'd been loading, or unloading, right up until the river took the decision out of their hands.

I pulled myself through.

Inside, my light found the ordinary archaeology of a working crew: a ration tin, a coil of rope grown soft with rot, a playing card fused to the deck plating—the seven of something, suits long dissolved. And there, snagged in the frame of the aft bulkhead where the current had funneled everything small and heavy, a drift of personal effects. A watch with a cracked face. A comb. A boot sole.

And tags.

Three sets, tangled together on their chains, furred grey with ten years of river. I lifted them with both hands, slow, the way you lift a sleeping child, and wiped the silt away with my thumb.

**VANE, S.**

The stamp cut through the corrosion like it had been waiting for me. Her mother's tags would be on the same cord—I could feel the second set beneath the first, smaller, older stamping.

I couldn't read the others. My vision had gone wrong, swimming, and it took me a moment to understand that it wasn't the water. I was crying into my mask at eight meters in a black river at midnight, alone with the dead I'd been ordered to abandon, and the guilt that had lived in me as a shapeless weight finally had edges, had serial numbers, had a name stamped in steel.

I'd thought surfacing the wreck would be the hard part. It wasn't. The hard part was that touching those tags felt like being forgiven, and I had not earned forgiveness, and some traitorous animal part of me wanted it anyway.

Halden's cup was somewhere in this compartment. The twins' shared headset. I found a fourth set of tags near the radio station, and a fifth wedged under the deck grating, and I stopped searching after that because my hands were shaking too badly to be useful and because a diver who cries uses air twice as fast, and I am, whatever else, still a professional.

I put Sefa's tags inside my glove, against my palm, and swam for the line.

* * *

Breaking the surface was like being born into a colder world. The moon was up now, laying a silver road across the water, and the mudflats breathed around me, and my shack sat dark on the bank, and Odile's lamp burned in the trading post window the way it always did, patient as a lighthouse for a man who'd never told her there was a shipwreck.

I stood waist-deep in the shallows and opened my glove.

Sefa's tags lay in my palm, streaming river water, catching moonlight. Ten years I'd kept my past sealed tighter than any crate I ever hauled, and the river had undone it in one dry summer without lifting a finger.

In six tides, Maren Kolvek's dredgers would come up that channel with their licenses and their pumps and their appetite, and they would strip this hull to scrap and haul the rest away as slurry, and the tags, the names, the seven of hearts, the open hatch where good people worked calmly while the water rose—all of it would go into a crusher, and the nation that never existed would get to go on never having existed, and I would get to go on being nobody.

That had been the deal. That had been the whole architecture of my life.

I closed my fist around the tags.

Somewhere south, downriver, I could almost hear the fleet's engines already, idling, patient, hungry. Six tides. Maybe fewer. Kolvek had a reputation for arriving early.

I looked at the dark water covering the hull, at the moon-road leading south toward it, and understood that the choice I'd been avoiding for ten years had finally run out of river.

Tomorrow I'd have to tell Odile why I needed her boat.

Or lie again—and I didn't think I had one more lie in me that would float.
