## 4. The Family That Took Him In

Odile Ferre knew every sound the trading post made, the way a mother knows a sleeping child's breathing. The tick of the stove iron cooling. Tam's radio crackling through two stations and settling on neither. Pip's bare feet on the porch boards, always too fast for the rot that was coming for them. So when Isandro's plane lifted off the strip at dusk without a single word to anyone, she heard the silence it left behind, and she did not like it.

She stood in the doorway of the post with a ledger under her arm and watched the tail lights dwindle north over the black line of the river. Ten years, and he'd never once left without telling her. Not when the medicine runs were late. Not when the fuel was short. Not the night Tam's appendix went bad and Isandro flew him out through weather that should have killed them both, and came back three days later with his hands still shaking and nothing to say about where he'd been.

"He didn't even top off," Tam said, coming out beside her. The mechanic had grease worked so deep into the creases of his knuckles that Odile suspected it had become part of him. "Tank was at half. He always tops off."

"Maybe he'll come back and top off."

"Maybe." Tam spat off the porch into the dark. "Maybe he's been gone since before he left."

That was the thing about Tam. He said the quiet parts out loud, and once they were out, they stayed out, sitting in the air like flies.

Pip came around the corner of the fuel shed at a run, the way Pip did everything, and skidded to a stop when she saw their faces. "He left?"

"He left," Odile said.

"Without saying goodbye?"

"Without saying anything."

Pip's face did something complicated—hurt folding into anger folding into something stubborn—and then she pushed past them both into the post. "He'll be back," she announced to the empty room, to the shelves of tinned goods and the medicine cabinet Isandro stocked every month, to the chair by the stove that everyone pretended wasn't his. "He always comes back."

Nobody answered her. That was answer enough, and Odile watched the girl understand it, watched her jaw set the way it did when the supply boat was late or the school inspector came upriver. Pip was twelve going on a fortress.

* * *

The trouble was, Odile had been counting things for ten years, and tonight she finally let herself count them out loud.

She sat at the counter after Pip had been sent to bed—after Pip had pretended to go to bed—and laid it out for Tam the way she'd lay out a bad season's accounts. Not cruelly. Just honestly, because the ledger didn't lie and neither, she'd always believed, did she.

"His papers came with him already made," she said. "Nobody forges papers that good on their way downriver. He had money the first year, before the flying paid, and then he never talked about where it went. He knows things he shouldn't. He knew the Kessari customs men were dirty before the Kessari customs men knew it themselves. He reads three languages and he pretends he barely reads one. And tonight he flew out at dusk with half a tank, in the one week in ten years the river's low enough to show what's on its bottom."

Tam turned his mug in a slow circle on the counter. "You've been keeping that list a while."

"Ten years."

"Me too." He looked up, and there was no accusation in his face, which somehow made it worse. "I just never added it up, because the total didn't matter. He hauled our medicine. He pulled me out of the river when I fell through the ice that winter. You don't audit a man like that."

"And now?"

"Now the river's low," Tam said. "And a salvage fleet is coming, and he looked at that water like it owed him money." He rubbed his jaw. "Odile, I fixed his plane for ten years. I know what a man's hands look like when he's flying away from something and when he's flying toward it. Tonight was toward."

"Toward what?"

"That's the question, isn't it."

The stove ticked. Out in the back room, a floorboard creaked—Pip, listening—and neither of them said anything about it.

"I don't think he lied to us," Tam said finally. "I think he just never told us the truth, and there's a difference. A lie takes something from you. Silence just... keeps something back. Keeps it safe."

"From us, or from him?"

Tam didn't have an answer for that, and Odile hadn't expected one. She wasn't sure she had one herself. That was the worst of it—ten years of trust, and she couldn't say whether it had been misplaced or merely incomplete. Whether Isandro Dravenmoor was a man who had deceived them, or a man who had carried something so heavy he couldn't set it down without crushing someone.

She'd taken him in because he'd shown up half-starved with a plane full of penicillin and no destination, and because she'd recognized the look on him. She'd worn it herself once, after the fever year, after she'd buried what was left of her own family and come upriver to a place where nobody knew her name. She knew what it cost to start over. She'd never asked him what he was starting over from. That had seemed like a courtesy.

It occurred to her, sitting in the lamplight with her ledger closed, that it might also have been cowardice.

* * *

Pip didn't go to bed. Pip went out the back window and up to the roof of the fuel shed, which was where she went when the world was wrong, and Odile found her there an hour past midnight, knees drawn up, watching the river.

Odile climbed up beside her. The tin was cold through her dress. Down below, the water slid past, low and quiet, and somewhere out in that darkness was a wreck that had put this whole family sideways in a single afternoon.

"You couldn't sleep either," Pip said. Not a question.

"No."

"Is he a liar?"

Odile took her time. Pip deserved the truth, or as much of it as Odile had, which was to say: a shape with nothing inside it. "I don't know."

"He's not." Pip said it with the flat finality of a girl who had decided, and would not be deciding again. "I've known him since I was six. He taught me to read off his charts. He carries the medicine like it's eggs. When the Marlow kid broke his arm, Isandro flew him out in fog so thick you couldn't see the prop from the tail, and he didn't even charge the Marlows, and he told me it was because the fog owed him a favor." Her voice cracked on the last of it, and she pushed through anyway. "Liars don't do that. Liars don't remember everybody's birthdays. He remembered mine before you did, Odile."

"That's fair," Odile said, and meant it, and felt the sting of it anyway.

"Maybe he did something bad before." Pip's chin came up, defiant against the dark. "Everybody did something before. You did."

"I did," Odile agreed quietly. "And nobody upriver ever asked me about it, and that's why I stayed."

"Then that's the rule, isn't it?" Pip turned to face her, eyes bright and furious in the starlight. "Nobody asks. That's the whole rule of this place. We took him in under the rule. You can't change the rule just because the river got low."

And there it was—the girl, twelve years old, holding up the founding law of their little country of strays. *Nobody asks.* Odile had built her life on it. She'd built Pip's life on it too, or Pip had built her own, which amounted to the same thing. And now Isandro had gone and broken it himself, not by asking but by leaving, and the rule was cracking, and Pip was standing on the crack like she could hold it shut with her feet.

"Nobody's changing the rule," Odile said. "But Pip—rules keep people safe. They don't keep people *here*. If he's gone because he chose to go, no rule in the world—"

"He'll be back." Pip said it to the river, not to her. "You'll see. He just had to go look at whatever's down there. People have to look. That's not lying, that's just—" she searched for the word, twelve years old and furious with the limits of her vocabulary— "that's just being a person."

Odile put an arm around her, and Pip let her, stiffly, the way she did at twelve, and they sat on the cold tin roof watching the black water slide past the trading post like a courier carrying a message neither of them could read.

* * *

They came back the next afternoon.

Odile heard the engine before she saw the plane—she'd know that engine's stutter anywhere, the way it caught on the left bank of turns—and she was out on the porch before she'd decided to be, ledger forgotten, heart doing something she resented it for. Tam came out of the shed wiping his hands. Pip came out of nowhere, as always.

The plane came down the strip rough, too fast, and Isandro brought it to a stop looking like a man who'd been through a wringer—mud to the knees, a raw scrape along one forearm, and something in his face Odile had never seen in ten years. He looked, she thought, like a man carrying a weight he'd finally picked up on purpose.

He climbed down and stood there, and the three of them stood there, and nobody said anything, and the river said everything, low and murmuring behind the post.

"You left without topping off," Tam said.

"I'll top off."

"You left without a word," Odile said.

"I know." He looked at her, and then at Pip, and whatever he saw in the girl's face made him flinch. "I went to the wreck."

Pip's breath caught. "You found it."

"I found part of it." His hand went to his jacket pocket, an unconscious motion, guarding something. "There's a salvage fleet coming, Odile. Six tides. Kolvek's people. And what's down there—" He stopped. Started again. "What's down there is mine to deal with. Mine alone. I need you all to stay out of the water, stay out of it, and if anyone from the fleet comes asking questions—"

"Questions about what?" Odile said. "About who you are?"

The silence stretched. Tam looked at the ground. Pip looked at Isandro like she could hold him here by staring.

"About who I was," Isandro said finally.

And Odile Ferre, who had built a life on the rule of *nobody asks*, felt the rule crack straight down the middle—because now he'd said it, now it was out, now the silence they'd all agreed to was broken from his side, and there was no putting it back.

Before she could decide what to say, Tam pointed downriver.

Smoke. Thin, gray, wrong—too far out to be the post's own chimneys, too close to be weather. Anchored in the channel where the channel shouldn't have anything in it, riding at anchor with lights coming on as the dusk fell.

Tam counted under his breath, the way mechanics count. "That's not six tides early," he said. "That's two."

Isandro's face went the color of the river mud, and Odile understood, with a certainty that settled in her chest like a stone, that whatever he'd gone looking for in that water had found him first—and that it had brought company.
