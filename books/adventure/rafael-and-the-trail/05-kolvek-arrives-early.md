## 5. Kolvek Arrives Early

The tide was wrong.

Isandro felt it before he saw anything—felt it in the way the river lay against the bank, flat and patient, holding its breath. He'd spent ten years reading this water the way other men read faces, and it was telling him something had changed. The mudflats that should have been drowning were still exposed, glistening under a sky the color of old tin, and out past the channel marker where the current should have been running hard, the surface was dimpled by wakes that no fish made.

He set down his crate of vaccine vials on Odile's dock and stood very still.

Three barges came around the bend at first light, riding low, their decks bristling with cranes and coiled hose like insects that had grown metal shells. Behind them, a tug pushed a fourth barge stacked with dredge pipe, and above it all flew a survey drone, small and white and indifferent, mapping the riverbed in slow legal sweeps. Painted on the lead barge's hull, fresh and arrogant: KOLVEK SALVAGE & RECOVERY, LICENSE NO. 44-C.

Two tides early. Maybe three.

"Odile," he said quietly, not turning. She'd come out onto the porch behind him; he'd heard the screen door, heard her coffee cup stop halfway to her mouth.

"I see them." Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice she used when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "Pip counted six boats total. Tam's been down at the fuel shed since dawn watching through the binoculars."

"They're supposed to have two more tides."

"Laws are for people who can afford lawyers, Isandro. You know that."

He did know that. He'd built his whole life on knowing which laws applied to men like him and which ones simply didn't bother.

* * *

Captain Maren Kolvek came ashore at midmorning, and she did not come alone, but she might as well have. Two crewmen flanked her at a respectful distance while she walked the dock alone, hands clasped behind her back, surveying the trading post with the frank appraisal of a woman pricing real estate. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with gray shot through her cropped hair and a face weathered into something like kindness if you didn't look at the eyes. Isandro looked at the eyes.

"You're Dravenmoor." Not a question. "The pilot."

"I'm the pilot."

"Maren Kolvek." She didn't offer a hand; he didn't offer one back. Somewhere behind him he heard Pip's screen door bang shut—the boy had been ordered inside twice already and was ignoring it with the dedication of youth. "Your reputation precedes you. Ten years flying this stretch without a single insurance claim. That's either discipline or fear."

"It's weather," Isandro said. "You're early, Captain."

Something flickered across her face—not guilt, nothing so soft. Recalculation. "The river dropped faster than the survey predicted. My license runs from the moment water level hits datum, not from some clerk's tide table. You'd know about technicalities, I imagine."

"I wouldn't."

"No?" She smiled, and it was almost warm. "Vessania trained its pilots well. I served with men who could read a river like scripture. They said the same thing, always. *I wouldn't.* Right up until they needed to say otherwise."

The word landed in his chest like a dropped anchor. Vessania. Spoken aloud, here, on Odile's dock, in daylight, by a stranger. Ten years of silence and this woman had cracked it open in one sentence, casually, the way you'd crack an egg to check if it was still good.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Isandro said.

"Of course you don't." She turned to look out at the river, at the dark water covering the wreck he hadn't yet told anyone about. "Here's what I know, pilot. There's a transport down there. Vessian registry, military markings under the silt, forty years drowned—or thirty-eight, depending whose records you believe, since the nation that registered it officially never existed. Which makes salvage law interesting. Which makes my license interesting. And which makes whoever knows exactly where that wreck sits very valuable to me."

The river said nothing. It never did. That was its best quality and Isandro had leaned on it for a decade.

"I fly medicine," he said. "That's all I am to you."

"That's all you were to me an hour ago." Kolvek finally turned to face him fully, and now there was no warmth anywhere in her, just arithmetic. "Then I pulled the harbor manifests from the old service years. Cross-referenced pilot call signs with regional postings. And I found a Lieutenant Isandro Dravenmoor, Vessanian Air Transport Command, listed as sole survivor of an incident he was never debriefed on because the incident officially never happened. Records sealed. Name scrubbed. Man vanished." She let that sit. "And now here you are, standing over the wreck site like a man standing over a grave he dug himself."

Behind the porch glass, Isandro could see Odile's silhouette, motionless. Listening. God knew what she was hearing. God knew what she was making of it.

"What do you want, Captain?"

"To do my job efficiently." She said it as though efficiency were a virtue and not a blade. "My contract pays by volume recovered, and that riverbed is full of scrap worth pennies and silt worth less. But a Vessian military transport? Sealed compartments? Possible cargo manifest, possible state papers, possible—" she paused, choosing the word with care, "—*personnel effects*? That's a different pay grade entirely. All I need is a location. Compartment layout. Where to cut."

"And in exchange?"

"A cut. Ten percent of recovery value. Enough to disappear properly this time—south, maybe, somewhere the name Dravenmoor means nothing." She took a folded paper from her jacket, held it between two fingers without offering it. "Or refuse, and I dig the whole riverbed anyway. Every crate, every hull plate, every logbook that survived. Including flight records. Including service records. Including the full, unredacted story of how one lieutenant walked away from a crashed transport while his crew stayed aboard." Her voice didn't rise at all. That was the worst part. "I'll find your name whether you help me or not, Dravenmoor. The only question is whether you profit from it or just get buried by it."

There it was. The trap, laid out clean, no teeth hidden. She wasn't threatening violence. She was offering him complicity, dressed as mercy, and both doors led into the same room.

His hands were steady. He noticed that with distant surprise. Ten years ago they would have shaken. Grief had burned the tremor out of him somewhere along the way and left only the scar tissue.

"You think I don't know where the crew compartment is," he said.

"I think you've known for days. Since the chest surfaced. Since you pocketed that seal plate off the mudflat—I know about the plate, too. Harbor patrol logs a missing artifact report, and suddenly every collector from here to the coast gets interested. You've been busy, pilot."

"The dead aren't cargo, Captain."

"No," Kolvek agreed, and for one strange moment something honest moved behind her eyes. "They're not. But they're also not yours to hoard. You kept their grave secret for ten years. What did that protect? Them? Or you?"

The question went in deep and found bone. He thought of the dog tags in the flooded compartment, cold as coins. Thought of the sealed orders in his fist on the last night, the radio crackling *return to base, that is not a request*, and the transport going down behind him in the dark with everyone he'd ever trusted still aboard. He had told himself for a decade that obedience had been forced on him. Standing on this dock, hearing a stranger say it plainly, he understood at last that the seal on his silence had been his own thumbprint.

"I need until tomorrow," Isandro said.

Kolvek studied him for a long moment, weighing, and whatever she found in his face seemed to satisfy her—or at least to amuse her.

"One day. Then my divers go in blind, and blind divers break things." She tucked the paper away, unaccepted, and turned toward her launch. At the gangway she paused, not looking back. "For what it's worth, Lieutenant—the men who gave those orders are mostly dead now. The ones who signed the seals, the ones who buried the file. The river's the only witness left who remembers their names. Funny thing about rivers." She stepped down into the boat. "They give everything back eventually."

The engines coughed, caught, and carried her out to the lead barge, where cranes stood waiting like gallows.

* * *

Tam found him at the hangar an hour later, which meant Odile had sent him, which meant Odile had heard enough and chosen strategy over confrontation—for now. The mechanic leaned in the doorway, wiping grease off his hands with a rag gone gray from years of the same work, and looked at Isandro with those patient, unreadable eyes.

"So," Tam said. "Lieutenant."

"Don't."

"I'm not judging. I'm inventorying." He came in and sat on a crate of spare parts like a man settling in for a long repair. "Odile's furious. Pip's drawing pictures of the salvage fleet getting eaten by a river monster. And I'm sitting here wondering how long you were going to let us love a man who doesn't exist."

"Tam—"

"Kolvek offered you money to point at the graves." It wasn't a question either. The man missed nothing; he'd probably watched the whole conversation from the fuel shed through the binoculars, reading lips he couldn't hear. "What'd you tell her?"

Isandro looked at his hands. Steady hands. Coward's hands, maybe, all these years.

"I asked for time."

Tam nodded slowly, as if that answer confirmed something he'd already written in his mental ledger. Then he stood, dropped the rag on the bench, and said the last thing Isandro expected:

"Good. Because you're going to need more than time. You're going to need to tell them yourself—before she does it for you." He paused at the door, silhouetted against the afternoon light, the dredges humming faintly out on the water like a sound coming up through the floor of the world. "Low tide's tomorrow evening, Isandro. Mudflats'll be bare as a confession. Best stage there is."

And then he was gone, leaving Isandro alone in the hangar with the plane, the sealed past, and less than a single tide in which to decide what kind of man was going to stand on that mud when the water went out.
