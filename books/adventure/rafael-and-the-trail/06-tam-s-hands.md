## 6. Tam's Hands

Tam found the logs the way he found most things: by accident, elbow-deep in someone else's problem.

The Otter had come in two days ago with a shimmy in the left aileron cable that Isandro swore he'd already fixed, and Tam didn't argue, because arguing with Isandro Dravenmoor about his own airplane was like arguing with a river about which way it flowed. You just waited for it to change its mind. So Tam crawled into the tail with a flashlight clamped in his teeth, chasing a frayed fairlead, and that was where his knuckles knocked against something that wasn't airframe.

A panel behind the aft bulkhead, riveted shut from the inside. Factory rivets would have been flush. These were hand-set, slightly proud, the work of somebody patient and scared at the same time.

Tam sat in the narrow dark of the tail cone for a long moment, flashlight beam trembling on the metal, and thought about all the years he'd spent under this man's airplanes without ever once asking what they carried besides cargo.

He could have put the panel back. That was the easy version. He'd fixed plenty of things he wasn't supposed to look too hard at — Odile's books, Pip's school forms, the whole quiet arrangement of a trading post full of people who'd each arrived with a past they preferred not to discuss. The post ran on a kind of mutual blindness. You kept your hands on your own work and let other people keep theirs.

But Tam's hands had never learned how to stop working once they'd started.

He drilled the rivets out one at a time, slow, catching each one in his palm so it wouldn't drop into the belly of the fuselage where no one would ever find it again. Inside was a canvas tool roll, the kind pilots used for spares, except this one was heavy with paper instead of wrenches. He unrolled it across his knee.

Flight logs. Dozens of them. Not the official ones Isandro filed with the post — those lived in the office, neat and boring, fuel loads and cargo manifests and weather notes. These were different. Older paper, different ink, some of them water-stained, some written in a language Tam half-recognized from the crates that came through sometimes stamped with a seal nobody talked about: a coiled serpent around a broken crown.

Vessania. The nation that wasn't.

The handwriting was Isandro's, but younger — tighter, angrier, the letters pressed hard enough to emboss the page. Tam read by flashlight while the evening cooled around him and the river muttered against the pilings below, and what he read made his hands go still, which almost never happened.

Dates going back eleven years. Coordinates in the interior that matched nothing on any chart Tam had ever serviced. Cargo entries that said MEDICAL when the weights were wrong for medical, that said SPARES when the weights were wrong for spares. And then, near the middle of the oldest log, an entry that stopped being a log at all and became something else — a single page, written fast, the pen tearing through in three places:

*Ordered to withdraw. Told crew to hold position. I flew out alone. I flew out ALONE.*

Below it, over and over, in rows like a man doing penance in arithmetic: *I flew out alone. I flew out alone. I flew out alone.*

Tam rolled the logs up carefully, the way he'd handle a cracked cylinder head — like something that could still go off. He didn't put them back behind the panel. He wrapped them in a rag and carried them home, and he sat up half the night with a lamp and a cup of cold coffee, reading every page twice, because if he was going to do what he was already deciding to do, he needed to know exactly what he was vouching for.

What he decided, somewhere around the third hour, was this: Isandro hadn't been lying to them for ten years. Lying was a thing you did to get something. Isandro had been hiding, which was a thing you did because you believed you deserved nothing, and Tam knew the difference between those two conditions down to the bone, because he'd spent six years of his own life in a coastal town south of here with a warrant with his name on it and a workshop full of machines he'd rebuilt for people who never asked why he was so good at making things disappear.

He'd come north. He'd started over. Nobody at the post had ever made him say why.

That was the debt. And debts, in Tam's experience, were the only currency that appreciated.

* * *

He caught Isandro at dawn, down at the mudflat, where the low water had pulled back far enough to show the ribs of the wreck again — black wood and green copper just breaking the surface like something surfacing to breathe. Isandro stood at the edge of the flats with his boots sunk to the ankles in gray silt, staring at it, and he didn't hear Tam coming, which was also unlike him. A man who'd flown clandestine routes for a dead country should have heard everything.

"Your left aileron cable," Tam said. "It wasn't frayed."

Isandro turned. His face did something complicated and settled on tired. "No?"

"I replaced it anyway. It wanted replacing." Tam held up the canvas roll. "Found these behind your aft bulkhead. Riveted panel. Nice work, actually. Hand-set. Took me twenty minutes to drill out without marking anything."

For a moment Isandro looked at the roll the way a man looks at a snake he'd convinced himself was a stick. Then he looked past Tam, toward the post, toward the little cluster of buildings where Odile was probably already lighting the stove and Pip was probably already awake, because Pip was always already awake, and something in his expression said he was calculating distances. Runway headings. Escape math.

"Don't," Tam said. "Don't do the arithmetic. I've watched you do it for ten years. There's nowhere north of here you can reach before the fleet does, and nowhere south that doesn't know your face better than we do."

"You went through my private—"

"I went through your airplane. It's my airplane as much as yours. I keep it alive." Tam walked closer, boots sucking in the mud, and stopped an arm's length away. Close enough to see the salt-and-sleep damage on the man's face. "You want to be angry, be angry. But first listen, because I'm only going to say this once and I don't enjoy saying things twice."

Isandro said nothing. The river filled the silence, slapping the exposed hull of the wreck.

"Kolvek's fleet arrives in four tides now. Word came upriver last night — she bought another dredge off the coast outfit, running ahead of schedule." Tam let that land. "When her pumps start, they'll strip that wreck down to molecules and haul it out in barges, and whatever's in your crew compartment goes to a salvage court in a country that says Vessania never existed, which means it goes nowhere, forever. You know that. I know that. What I know that you don't is dredge pumps."

Isandro frowned. "What?"

"Dredge pumps are my favorite machines in the world," Tam said, "because they're arrogant. Big impellers, big appetite, and everybody assumes they're fine because they're big. But a pump like that has a gland seal, and a gland seal has tolerances, and tolerances can be adjusted. By a man with my hands. So that the pump runs beautifully for six hours, eight hours, long enough to pass any inspection — and then eats itself from the inside out, all four pumps, within a day of each other, in a way that will look like bad luck and cheap steel and Kolvek's own procurement officer's fault. Weeks to rebuild. By which time the tide cycle changes and the wreck silts back in, and she'll need a new survey license to dig again, and surveys take months."

Isandro stared at him. "You'd sabotage a licensed salvage operation."

"I'd adjust some tolerances," Tam said. "On machines that were maintained badly by their owners, which is true, because I've seen Kolvek's coast contracts and her maintenance records are a crime scene. But yes. Sabotage. If that's the word you need."

"Why?" It came out raw. "Why would you risk that? For me?"

And here was the part Tam had rehearsed in the dark, and it still came out rougher than he wanted, because grace was not a smooth thing; it had edges.

"Not for you," he said. "For us. For Odile, who took you in when you had frostbite and no name worth saying. For Pip, who thinks you hung the moon and would follow you into that wreck tomorrow if you let her. For the post. You've been carrying this alone so long you think alone is the same as strong. It isn't. Alone is just alone."

He pushed the canvas roll against Isandro's chest until the man took it.

"But there's a price, and the price isn't money. The price is this: tonight, at low tide, you tell them. Odile, Pip — all of it. The seal, the orders, the crew you left, why you flew out alone. You tell them yourself, standing on that mudflat, looking at them, before I touch a single one of Kolvek's pumps. Because if I rig those pumps and the truth comes out later — and it always comes out later, I know how that works — then it's not your lie anymore, it's ours. You'd make accomplices of people who love you without asking them first. That's worse than what you did eleven years ago. At least then you were following orders."

Isandro's jaw worked. He looked down at the logs in his arms, at the rag-wrapped weight of a decade, and Tam watched him stand at the edge of it — the old life pulling one way, the mudflat holding the other.

"They'll look at me different," Isandro said finally.

"Course they will," Tam said. "Pip already looks at you like you're ten feet tall. Wait till she finds out you're human. Might ruin her." He turned to go, then stopped, because there was one more thing and he'd promised himself he'd say it. "Isandro. I read the page where you wrote *I flew out alone* forty times. I want you to know — the men in that compartment, they wrote their names on their tags so somebody would find them. Somebody did. Eleven years late is late. It isn't never."

He walked back up the shingle toward the post, leaving Isandro alone with the wreck and the rising water, and behind him he heard nothing at all, which was how he knew the man was crying, or close to it, or doing whatever pilots did instead.

Tam spent the day in the shed sharpening tools that didn't need sharpening and laying out, on clean cloth in precise order, the small brass shims and feeler gauges he'd need for four pump glands — just in case. Just in case was most of his life.

At sunset, Isandro appeared in the doorway of the shed, silhouetted against a sky going the color of old copper, and he looked like a man who had made a decision the way rivers make decisions: completely, and all at once.

"Low tide's in an hour," Isandro said. "They're all at the post. Odile's got the stove going."

Tam set down his file. "And?"

"And I need you there," Isandro said. "Not to speak. Just — there. In case I stop."

"You won't stop," Tam said, reaching for his coat. "But I'll be there."

Out past the point, far downriver, faint on the evening horizon, Tam could just make out the running lights of a barge that shouldn't have been there for two more tides — riding high and empty, coming fast, hungry for the bottom of the river.

Four tides had just become three.
