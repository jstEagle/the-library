## 7. Confession at Low Tide

The tide went out farther than I'd ever seen it go. By late afternoon the river had pulled back past the old pilings, past the sandbar Pip called the Whale's Back, and left a mile of mudflat gleaming like hammered pewter under a sky the color of unpolished tin. The water had given up its secrets for the day. It seemed only fair that I give up mine.

I'd asked them to come without telling them why. Odile arrived first, arms crossed against the wind off the flats, her face already set in the expression she wore when she suspected someone was about to waste her time. Tam came with his hands jammed in his jacket pockets, and I knew he'd been into my flight logs again—knew it from the way he wouldn't quite meet my eyes, and from the way he stood slightly between me and Pip, like a man positioning himself for a fall he'd decided to catch.

Pip just ran out onto the mud ahead of everyone, boots squelching, arms wide, shouting something about how far you could see when the river got honest.

*When the river gets honest.* Out of the mouths of twelve-year-olds.

We walked out to the center of the flat, where a channel of leftover water cut a thin silver line through the silt, and there I stopped, because there was no further to go. No plane to climb into. No trading post noise to hide behind. Just four people and a mile of exposed ground and the light dying slowly over the hills of Vessania—the drowned nation, the nation that never existed—somewhere beyond them.

"Ten years ago," I said, "there was no such person as Isandro Dravenmoor."

Odile didn't move. Tam exhaled through his nose, slow, like he'd been holding that breath since he found the logs.

"The name came later. I built it out of spare parts—a dead man's surname, a border town's first name. It fit well enough that nobody ever checked." I looked down at the mud, because looking at them was going to be harder than anything else I did today, and I wanted to get the easy part done first. "Before that, I flew transport for a country you won't find on any map. Vessania. It existed for eleven years. Then the treaties dissolved it, the archives burned—or were said to have burned—and everyone who served it signed papers saying they hadn't. That was the price of walking away clean. You never existed either."

"And your crew," Odile said. Not a question. She'd had ten years to watch me flinch at certain words. *Crew* was one of them. *Transport* was another.

"My crew," I said.

* * *

The story came out of me in pieces, the way wreckage comes up—first the light debris, then the heavy things you have to dig for.

Vessania's air service ran medicine and mail and occasionally things that weren't medicine or mail into valleys that officially belonged to three other countries. I was twenty-six and I thought I was the best pilot in the fleet, which was true, and invincible, which was not. There were six of us on the last rotation: me, Captain Sef Aldous, navigators Wren and Ilka, loadmaster Brannoc, medic Yeva Corta. Yeva used to sing in the cargo bay when the turbulence got bad, some old hymn her grandmother taught her, and we all pretended it annoyed us because pretending was cheaper than admitting it was the only thing keeping us sane.

The last mission was an evacuation. The capital was falling—not to an army, to paperwork, which is faster—and command ordered us to lift out records and personnel before the borders sealed. We made two runs. On the third, loaded heavy with people instead of crates, we took fire crossing the Serrat narrows. An engine gone. Hydraulics failing. Aldous put us down on the river, and by some miracle nobody died in the landing.

That was where the orders found me. Sealed orders, priority cipher, in my pocket because I was junior officer of record. *Pilot Dravenmoor will proceed overland to relay station nine and transmit confirmation of cargo loss. Remaining crew will secure the aircraft pending recovery.*

Pending recovery. I read those words a hundred times in the years after. *Secure the aircraft.* Not *survive*. Not *hold position*. Secure the aircraft, as if the aircraft were the thing worth saving, as if the six people standing around it in rising water were inventory.

"I transmitted the confirmation," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's, flat and far away across the mud. "Then I started walking back toward the wreck site to tell them we'd be recovered within the week. Except there was no recovery. The country stopped existing while I was walking. The recovery fleet was decommissioned, the orders were voided retroactively, and every record of the transport *Meridian* —that was her name, the *Meridian* —was destroyed along with everything else. When I finally reached a border town, there was nothing left to report to. No fleet. No commission. No Vessania."

"So they're still there," Pip said quietly. "In the plane. In the river."

I nodded. I couldn't make my throat work around anything else for a moment.

"The river's moved since then. Shifted channels twice. I've flown over that stretch of water maybe four thousand times and told myself it could be anywhere under there. Told myself the current would have taken them downstream, scattered them, that there was nothing to find." I shook my head. "That was a lie I needed. Last week the water gave back the chest, and I knew exactly what was under the surface, and I still almost flew away."

"Why didn't you?" Odile asked. Her voice wasn't cruel. It was worse than cruel—it was genuinely curious, genuinely open, the voice of a woman who had fed me and employed me and vouched for me for a decade and simply wanted to understand what she'd been vouching for.

"Because Kolvek's dredgers would have found you anyway," I said. "And I'd rather you hear it from me than from a salvage manifest."

"That's not what I asked."

I looked at her. The wind pushed her gray hair across her face and she didn't bother moving it.

"Why didn't you fly away, Isandro? You're good at it. Ten years of practice."

* * *

I thought about lying to her one more time. It would have been easy. It was always easy; that was the whole disease of it.

"Because of Pip," I said.

Pip's head came up.

"Because when I landed here, broke and using a name that wasn't mine, a woman I'd known for six days gave me a cot in the back of a trading post and a job hauling freight, and a kid who couldn't have been more than two years old cried when my engine backfired, and I stayed anyway. Because somewhere in ten years of medicine runs and fuel runs and fixing Tam's generator in the rain, I stopped being a man hiding from a grave and started being a man with a life. And a life—" my voice cracked, and I let it, because hiding it felt like one more lie, "—a life is a thing you can lose. A secret can't be lost. It can only be carried. And I am so tired of carrying it."

The tide line glistened. Somewhere behind us a curlew called, long and descending, like the sound the world makes when it lets a breath out.

"You could have told us," Odile said. "Year one. Year five. Last month."

"Yes."

"You chose not to. For ten years."

"Yes."

"And now you're telling us because the dredgers are coming and you've run out of river to hide it under."

"That too," I said. "Both things are true. I'm not going to dress it up as nobler than it is. I should have told you when there was no fleet coming, no chest, no reason except that you deserved it. I didn't. That's mine to own, and I'm owning it here, in front of all of you, on the record, where I can't take it back."

Tam spoke for the first time. "The logs. The ones hidden in the wing locker. Those are real flight records? From the service?"

"Every hour I ever flew for them. Names, dates, tail numbers. Including the *Meridian*'s last flight plan and the crew manifest." I met his eyes. "You found them."

"I found them." He said it without apology. "I wasn't snooping. Well—I was snooping. After Kolvek's offer, I wanted to know who I was rigging pumps for." He glanced at Odile, and something passed between them, some conversation held in the space of a second, and I understood that Tam had already told her about the sabotage plan, or most of it, and that they had come out here having already decided something.

"What else did you find?" I asked him.

"A man who wrote *Y.C.—sang on the bad days* in the margin of a crew roster, and then kept flying for ten years like a ghost with a job." Tam shrugged, awkward, his big hands turning over each other. "I don't know what that makes you. But I know what you've been to us. Both things can be real."

* * *

Odile turned and walked away.

She walked fast, out across the mud toward the darkening tree line, and every instinct I had screamed to go after her, and I didn't move, because chasing her would have been for me, not for her. Some anger needs room. I watched her go until she was a smudge against the reeds, and the hurt in my chest was enormous and clean, like a wound that had finally been allowed to bleed.

"She'll come back," Pip said.

"Pip—"

"She always comes back. She came back when the fever took half the post. She came back when Tam blew up the still." Pip's hand found mine, small and certain and cold from the river wind, and closed around it. "You told the truth and she's mad and she's still here tomorrow. That's how family works. Tam told me."

Tam made a strangled noise that might have been protest and might have been agreement.

I stood there on the empty flat with a child's hand in mine and the names of six dead people sitting in my mouth like stones—Aldous, Wren, Ilka, Brannoc, Corta, and the sixth, Juno Pell, the trainee who'd begged to come along on her first evacuation run because I'd said yes when I should have said no—and I understood that whatever happened next, whatever Kolvek brought up the river in the dark, I had already survived the hardest part of this. The truth was out. It hadn't killed me. It hadn't even cost me everything.

Just enough. Truth always costs just enough.

Odile's silhouette stopped at the tree line. Stood there a long moment, facing the water, facing us. Then she turned—not toward the post, not away—but sat down on a fallen log at the edge of the flats, alone, her back to the last of the light, waiting out her anger where she could keep an eye on us.

Not leaving. Not yet forgiving. Both.

"We should get back before dark," Tam said. "Storm building in the west. And if Kolvek's divers work the night surge—"

I squeezed Pip's hand once and let go, and looked north, where the first bruise-colored clouds were stacking up over the hills, and felt the fear come back sharpened into something I could use.

"Then we'd better make sure the night doesn't go the way she plans it," I said.
