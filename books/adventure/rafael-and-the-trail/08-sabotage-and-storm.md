## 8. Sabotage and Storm

**TAM**

The pumps died at slack water, right on schedule, and Tam felt like a man who'd gotten away with something enormous and small at the same time.

He'd spent two nights inside the fleet's pump barge, a wrench in his pocket and a cover story about a coolant leak that Kolvek's chief engineer had accepted with the weary gratitude of a man drowning in broken equipment. The dredge pumps were beautiful machines—German-built, forty years old, still turning riverbed into slurry like it was nothing. Tam had grown up taking engines apart. He knew them the way he knew his own hands.

That was what made it hurt.

He hadn't blown anything up. Blown things got investigated. Instead he'd shimmed three impeller bearings with washers cut from tin, packed the seals with grease that would cook off under load, and loosened the intake screens just enough to let the river's sand chew the works from the inside. The pumps would run fine for six hours. Then they'd seize, one after another, in a way that would look like bad luck stacked on worse maintenance.

By the time anyone traced it to him—if anyone ever did—the review Isandro had set in motion would be done, and the fleet would be gone.

"You're smiling," Pip said. She was sitting on the trading post dock with her feet in the water, watching the salvage lights burn out on the barge line like a carnival that had come to the wrong town.

"I'm not."

"You are. You've got your engine face on."

Tam sat down beside her. Out on the water, a pump coughed, stuttered, and quit. A voice carried across the flat evening air, somebody swearing in three languages.

"Engine face," Tam said.

"That's the one. You get it when something you built is doing exactly what you wanted it to do."

He laughed despite himself. Then the laugh caught, because across the mudflats he could see Isandro standing alone by his plane, looking at the wreck site the way men look at graves. Two days ago Isandro had told them everything—the sealed orders, the crew he'd been made to abandon, ten years of running—and Tam had offered him sabotage on the condition that he tell the truth first. He had. It had cost him visibly, and he'd paid it anyway.

Tam had rebuilt carburetors for men who lied about smaller things than that. He figured Isandro had earned every washer he'd shimmed.

"Go tell him dinner's on," Pip said. "He forgets to eat when he gets that face."

* * *

**ISANDRO**

The first pump seized at dusk. By full dark, all three were dead, and the sound of the salvage operation changed from a grinding roar to the clank and shout of men improvising. Isandro stood at the edge of the mudflats and let himself feel one clean hour of hope.

It was the last hour he got.

The barometer had been falling all day—he'd felt it in his teeth, in the way the gulls went quiet, in the smell of the river going metallic. Odile had named the storm before the radio did: *Surge-runner*, the kind that came up the coast sideways and shoved the whole river backward. When it hit, past midnight, it didn't just bring rain. It brought water. Half a meter of surge rolled up the estuary and lifted the barges off their moorings like toys, and Kolvek—who Isandro realized now had been waiting for exactly this—had her divers in the water within the hour.

Storm diving was insane. Storm diving was also legal, because the surge gave her barges the depth to float directly over the wreck, and no regulation said a man couldn't drown himself if the captain signed the waiver.

Isandro watched their floodlights bloom green and ghostly through the churned water and understood, with terrible clarity, that his sabotage had bought him days and the storm had taken them back in a single night.

He ran for the plane.

* * *

**TAM**

Tam heard the Cessna's engine turn over at two in the morning and knew, before his brain finished the thought, that it was a mistake.

He crossed the yard at a dead sprint, rain hitting like gravel, and reached the wing just as Isandro ran through the preflight with his jaw set in a way Tam recognized from two days of confession and one day of silence: the face of a man who had already decided and was only waiting for the world to catch up.

"You can't fly in this," Tam shouted over the engine.

"I can fly *over* it." Isandro leaned out of the cockpit, rain streaming off his cap. "Kolvek's divers are on the compartment right now. If they bring anything up tonight, it's gone—logged, crated, buried in a warehouse before the review board ever sits. I can stop that."

"How?"

"Flares." He patted the rack Tam had helped him bolt to the strut two years ago for a forest-fire contract they'd never gotten. "A low pass over an active dive site with pyrotechnics is a maritime emergency signal. Her own safety officer will have to call a halt—it's regulation. She'll know exactly what I'm doing. She'll also have to do it, or explain to an inquiry why she didn't."

"And if she doesn't halt?"

"Then she explains it." Isandro's grin was thin and white. "Either way, it's on the record. That's all I've got left, Tam. Records."

Tam looked at the sky, which was the color of wet slate and moving like something alive, and then at the man in the cockpit who had spent ten years being invisible and two days learning to be seen.

"Hold the yoke with both hands on the turn," Tam said. "She'll want to weathercock. And Isandro—" he gripped the strut, "—come back. That's not a request. That's a maintenance directive."

Isandro laughed once, sharp, and pushed the throttle forward.

* * *

**ISANDRO**

The storm took the plane the moment the wheels left the mud strip.

It didn't hit him so much as pick him up—a wall of wind off the estuary that shoved the Cessna sideways thirty feet and dropped it into a hole of dead air, and for one long second Isandro fell with the river rushing up and the altimeter unwinding and every instinct screaming at him to turn back. He fed in full power instead, flew out of the sink on the ragged edge of a stall, and climbed into rain so hard it sounded like someone throwing stones at the airframe.

The barge lights appeared below him as smears of gold in the murk. Through a tear in the cloud he saw them clearly for three seconds: the crane swung out over black water, the dive platform crowded with figures, a recovery basket rising on the cable with something dark and rectangular in it.

His stomach dropped ahead of the plane.

They were bringing up the compartment.

He rolled in over the barge at two hundred feet, lower than he had any business flying in this weather, and triggered the flare rack. Six pyrotechnics arced out over the water in burning red stars, and the night turned the color of an emergency—which was, precisely, the point. Every man and woman on that deck looked up. Every one of them saw a small aircraft fighting for its life above their heads, dropping distress signals over an active dive operation, and every one of them knew what their regulations required next.

The crane froze mid-lift.

Isandro banked for a second pass and the storm hit him broadside.

The gust took the tail and slewed him ninety degrees; the Cessna snapped into a skid, half-spinning, and the horizon became a spinning blur of water and light. He fought it with both hands, boots hard on the rudders, feeling the airframe groan around him like a living thing being hurt. Somewhere below, faintly, he heard a siren start to wail on the barge—the dive halt, the recall horn, Kolvek's operation shutting down whether she liked it or not.

Good, he thought distantly. That part worked.

The part that wasn't working was the engine, which coughed as a slug of rain-choked air starved it, surged, coughed again. The mud strip was behind him and to the east, straight into the wind, which meant landing into a headwind that gusted across the runway like a hand slapping at the wheels.

He came in on the third approach. The first two he aborted—one for a downburst that shoved him at the treeline, one because the runway lights vanished entirely behind a curtain of rain. On the third, the Cessna slammed down hard enough to bend something in the gear, skipped, veered, and stopped fifty feet from the fuel drums with one wingtip quivering over the mud.

Isandro sat there in the shaking dark, hands welded to the yoke, heart slamming, and started laughing—wet, ragged, disbelieving laughter—because he was alive, and because somewhere out in that storm, on the deck of her barge, Maren Kolvek was standing in the rain reading a halt order she could not refuse.

* * *

**TAM**

Tam found him still in the cockpit, soaked through, one hand pressed flat against the instrument panel like he was taking its pulse.

"The gear's bent," Isandro said. "Left main. Maybe the spar fitting."

"I heard the landing from the post." Tam shone his light into the wheel well, winced, and said nothing for a moment. "You bent my airplane."

"*Our* airplane."

"Our airplane," Tam agreed, and then, because the truth of the last hour was sitting in his chest too heavy to hold, "the divers?"

"Halted. Mid-recovery. Whatever they pulled up is going back down or into a sealed hold—either way, it's documented now. There are witnesses, a siren log, six flares over an active site. Kolvek can't make tonight unhappen." Isandro exhaled, long and shaking. "I think it worked, Tam. God help me, I think it actually worked."

Tam put his hand on the man's shoulder and squeezed, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because the rain was coming down and the plane was shuddering around them and some things don't need words.

Then the radio in the post crackled to life across the yard, loud enough to carry through the storm, and Odile's voice came through tight and fast:

"Isandro. Get in here. Kolvek's on the channel—she wants to talk to you personally. She says—" a pause, and Tam heard Odile swallow, "—she says her divers already raised the tags. All of them. And she says if you want them, you'll come to her barge at dawn and take them out of her hand yourself."

In the cockpit, in the rain, Isandro Dravenmoor went very still.

"Dawn," he said finally. "Then dawn it is."
