## 9. The Wreck Belongs to the Dead

The storm had spent itself by dawn, and the river lay flat and gray as sheet metal. From the air I could see the whole fleet strung out along the channel like a row of knives: Kolvek's barge in the center, dredges flanking her, cranes folded against the morning like sleeping birds. And at the wreck site, three dive boats circling the spot where my crew had been sleeping for ten years.

I'd flown through the night on flares and fury and a fuel gauge that lied to me twice. The Cessna's left strut was bent from the crosswind landing at the trading post, and Tam had looked at it for exactly four seconds before saying, "It'll hold. Probably." Pip had stuffed a thermos of coffee into my jacket without a word, which was Pip's way of saying *come back*, and Odile had stood at the edge of the lantern light with her arms crossed, still angry, still there.

"Land on the barge," she'd said finally. "Not the water. Not the mud. Put yourself where they can't pretend you're not."

So that's what I did.

* * *

There's no graceful way to land a bush plane on a salvage barge. You pick your line, you kill your speed, you pray the deck crew has the sense to scatter. Kolvek's people scattered. I touched down hard enough to rattle my teeth, ground-looped the tail around in a spray of river water off the deck plates, and shut the engine down in the sudden ringing silence with the prop blades six feet from a coil of anchor chain.

Then I climbed out into the middle of Captain Maren Kolvek's operation, alone, visible, and completely out of moves.

She came across the deck unhurried, coffee mug in one hand, rain slicker open over a uniform shirt that hadn't been regulation anywhere since Vessania stopped existing. Up close she looked older than the last time I'd seen her—eleven years ago, across a briefing table in a hangar that was later demolished along with every record inside it. Her hair had gone iron at the temples. Her eyes hadn't changed at all. They were the eyes of someone who tallied things: cargo, hours, bodies, costs.

"Dravenmoor." She said it like a receipt. "You cost me two divers last night with those flares. One of them's got a busted eardrum."

"He'll hear fine," I said. "Better than my crew does."

Something flickered behind the tallying. "That's the play, then. Sentiment." She sipped her coffee. "I offered you a cut. Clean money, clean exit. Instead you burn flares over open water in a storm and now you land on my deck like this is a war you can win. It isn't. My license is stamped by three ministries. Yours is a piece of paper nobody honors anymore."

"I'm not here about licenses."

"No?"

"I'm here about registry." I reached into my jacket, slow, so every rifle on that deck could watch me do it, and brought out the seal plate.

The chest plate was the size of my palm, rusted orange along its edges, but the stamp in the center had survived ten years of river the way some truths survive everything thrown at them: stubbornly, and in relief. The crowned current of Vessania. Three interlocking waves beneath a star that no atlas had carried since before Pip was born.

Kolvek looked at it for a long moment. Around us, the fleet had gone quiet. Deckhands pretending to work, divers sitting on their fins at the rail, a crane operator leaning out of his cab. Word travels fast on water, and faster when it's about the dead.

"You know the statute," I said.

"I know a lot of statutes."

"Article Nine. Salvage Convention, Vessian registry law, whatever archive you dug your license out of—it'll be in there, because whoever licensed you pulled every scrap of Vessian maritime code they could find to make this dig legal." I held the plate up between us. "Wrecks carrying human remains belong to their nation of registry. Not to the salvor. Not to the finder. To the nation. Full stop. No dredge touches them. No crane lifts them. They wait for the flag they sailed under—or, if the flag's gone, for whatever successor body claims the file."

"The flag is ash," Kolvek said. "Vessania doesn't exist. There's no successor body. That's the entire reason this wreck is legally orphaned, and orphans get adopted by whoever holds the license."

"That's true," I said. "Except."

Her jaw tightened. She knew. I watched her know.

"Except Article Nine has a clause," I went on, "for vessels whose loss was caused by state action. A transport shot down over its own territory by its own government doesn't become an orphan. It becomes evidence. And evidence doesn't belong to salvage fleets, Maren. It belongs to the inquiry." I let that sit. "You didn't come all this way for copper ingots. Nobody strips a riverbed for copper. You came because somebody paid you to make sure nothing readable comes up out of that water. Which means somebody, somewhere, is still afraid of Article Nine. Still afraid of an inquiry. Which means the inquiry clause is live."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere aft, a diver whispered something and got elbowed quiet.

"And who invokes it?" Kolvek said quietly. "You? You're not a nation either, Dravenmoor. You're a ghost with a pilot's license. You spent eleven years making sure no file on earth connects Isandro Dravenmoor to that aircraft. I checked. That's how I found you—I looked for the man who didn't exist, and there was exactly one."

"Yes," I said. "There was."

I set the seal plate down on the deck between us, on top of her cargo manifest, and stepped back from it.

"So here's what happens now. Either you stand down pending review—and I will spend whatever years it takes getting a tribunal seated, starting with the ministries that stamped your license, who will be *delighted* to discover what else is in Article Nine—or you keep dredging, and I stand right here on your deck, named, documented, testifying, while your divers pull my crew's tags out of that compartment one at a time in front of witnesses. Every name they lift goes on the record with mine attached. Mine as pilot. Mine as the man who got the order to abandon them. Mine as the witness who can tell the inquiry exactly which officer gave the order, and which ministry buried it."

"You'd burn yourself," she said. "Eleven years invisible. You'd hand it all over."

"Yeah." My voice cracked on it, and I let it. "Turns out that was the price. I already paid it. Last night, on a mudflat, in front of the only family I've got. It turns out you survive it. You stand there afterward and the world keeps turning and the people who matter stay." I nodded at the plate. "Take it. Verify the stamp. Run your lawyers. But the pumps don't start again until review clears, because if they do, I'm not fighting you anymore—I'm just narrating. Loudly. To everyone."

* * *

She picked up the plate.

She turned it over twice, thumb moving across the crowned current the way you'd touch a scar, and I understood suddenly that Maren Kolvek had served too—that the iron in her hair wasn't all age, that somewhere under the salvage contracts there was a woman who'd also been told to forget a country. Maybe that was why she'd taken the contract. Easier to strip a grave than mourn it.

"Ten years ago," she said, "you'd have flown away."

"Ten years ago I did fly away."

"And now?"

"Now I landed on your deck."

She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever moved behind the tallying, she kept it off the books. Then she turned to her first mate—a broad woman with a clipboard and a face like a closed door—and said, "All stations, stand down. Secure the dredges. Nobody enters the water until review." Louder, to the deck: "You heard me. Stand. Down."

The sound that went through the fleet wasn't cheers. It was something quieter and heavier—the exhale of forty people who'd been dredging a graveyard and knew it. A diver at the rail took off his hood and sat down heavily. Somebody started coiling line that didn't need coiling.

Kolvek walked me to the plane. At the prop she stopped.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the order to abandon your crew. I've seen copies of orders like that. They never come from anyone brave." She handed the plate back to me. "This stays with you. Registry claims need a physical token of national character. Congratulations, Pilot. As of this morning, you're the closest thing Vessania has to a government."

"That's the worst thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It gets worse. Governments have to answer letters." Almost—almost—a smile. "Get off my barge, Dravenmoor. You bend my deck plates."

* * *

I flew home low over the channel, the river running gold under the climbing sun, the fleet shrinking in my mirror until it was just a rumor of steel. The strut held. Probably. The seal plate rode in my breast pocket, over the heartbeat, where I'd carried it since the day the river gave it back.

At the trading post, the whole town was on the bank. Odile, arms crossed. Tam, wiping grease off his hands on a rag that had been clean once, years ago. Pip standing on a crate so she could see over everybody, and when the wheels kissed the mudflat and I climbed down, she was the first one to reach me.

"Did it work?" she demanded.

"It worked."

"Did you tell them your name? On the record? Like, officially-officially?"

"Officially-officially."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a supreme court, then nodded once. "Good. Because I already started painting the tail."

Odile came last. She stopped an arm's length away, looked me over—the salt, the exhaustion, the plate-shaped bulge in my pocket—and said, "Tribunal's going to want statements. Months of them. Hearings. You'll have to fly to the capital. Repeatedly. In front of cameras."

"I know."

"They'll dig into everything. The orders. The crew. What you did after." Her voice caught, just barely, and steadied. "Everything, Isandro."

"I know," I said. "I'm done hiding things from people I love. It nearly cost me all of you. It won't happen twice."

She studied me a long moment, the way she studied weather coming in off the delta. Then she handed me the thermos Pip had sent with me, empty now, and said, "You'll need breakfast. Testifying hungry is how innocent men look guilty."

And that was Odile's way of saying *stay*.

I was halfway to the cookhouse, Pip chattering about paint colors for the tail—she wanted gold for the star, she was very firm on gold—when Tam fell into step beside me and lowered his voice.

"One thing," he said. "Before you eat. Kolvek's radio traffic last night, before the storm broke. I was still tapped into their band." He glanced back at the empty channel, then at me. "They weren't talking to a ministry, Isandro. They were talking to somebody *about* you. And whoever it was, they used your old service number."

The river glittered, indifferent and bright, all the way to the horizon.

"They knew you were alive," Tam said. "They've known the whole time."
