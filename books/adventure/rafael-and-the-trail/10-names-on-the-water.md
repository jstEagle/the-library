## 10. Names on the Water

The river took six weeks to decide what it thought of us.

It rose slow and sullen through the first frosts, swallowing the mudflats a hand's width at a time, until the only thing left above water was the marker we'd built on the highest shelf of gravel. I flew supply runs over it every third day, and every time I banked south I looked down at that small pale rectangle standing in the current like a tooth, and every time it looked less like a grave and more like an answer.

We built it ourselves. That mattered to me more than I could have explained to anyone who hadn't been there. No government crew, no salvage barge, no men with clipboards deciding what the dead were worth. Just Odile's post truck hauling stone, Tam's winch and his scarred hands setting the base true, Pip mixing concrete with the seriousness of a surgeon. The slab came from the old quarry upriver—granite, gray-green, shot through with quartz that caught the light like the river had put its own signature on it.

I carved the names myself. It took me nine days with a chisel and a mallet Tam weighted for me, and my shoulders screamed by the end of each one, and I would not let anyone else touch it. Eleven names. The seven from the transport—Vessanian crew, men and women whose nation had been erased from the maps so thoroughly that even their own families had been told they never served. And four more: my flight crew. The ones who died when I obeyed the order to turn back, or the ones who lived because I did—I still couldn't make the arithmetic come out clean, and I suspected I never would. So their names went on the same stone, side by side, no hierarchy of guilt. Just names.

* * *

Odile came back on a Tuesday.

She didn't announce it. She never announced anything; she arrived the way weather arrives, and you adjusted. I was in the hangar re-lacing a fuel line when I heard the post truck grind into the yard, and I came out wiping my hands and there she was, standing in the light with her duffel over one shoulder and her jaw set in the particular way that meant she'd rehearsed what she was going to say and then decided to say something else entirely.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been carving granite."

"That explains the hands. Not the face." She dropped the duffel. "Pip says you sleep in the hangar now."

"Pip talks too much."

"Pip is the only reason I came back, so be nice about Pip." She looked past me into the hangar, at the plane sitting on its jacks, tail toward the open doors. At the tail. I watched her see it—the fresh paint, still faintly tacky, the seal of Vessania rendered in gold and deep blue across the rudder, small and precise and utterly unmistakable.

Her breath went out slow. "That's new."

"Pip did it. Last week. Came in while I was asleep and wouldn't let me sand it off." I shrugged, which was a lie, because I would never have sanded it off. "Said if the dead get their names, the plane can wear its colors."

"It's good work," Odile said quietly. "The linework's clean."

"He traced it off the chest plate. Made me hold the lamp for two hours."

Something moved through her face then—not forgiveness exactly, not yet, maybe not ever in the shape I'd once wanted it—but something honest. She'd walked away angry the night I told them everything, walked the whole length of the mudflat and stood at the waterline until dark, and then she'd come back and sat down next to me in the cold without a word, and that had been its own kind of answer. But she'd left after that anyway. Gone upriver to her sister's, taking the anger with her to finish it properly, because Odile did nothing halfway, including grief.

"I'm not done being angry," she said now.

"I know."

"But I'm done doing it somewhere else." She picked up her duffel. "Kolvek sent a letter. Official channels. The review board confirmed the registry claim. The wreck and everything in it goes to the Vessian trust—they're calling it a cultural recovery operation now, which is a hell of a trick for a country that doesn't exist. They want you as a consultant. Paid."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"They assumed as much. That's why they're paying." Almost a smile. Almost. "There's a memorial service planned in the capital. Whoever's left of whoever's left. They asked if the plane could fly the seal over the ceremony."

I looked at the tail again. Gold and blue, drying in the cold air.

"Yeah," I said. "It can do that."

* * *

The morning we raised the marker proper, the river lay flat as poured tin, and the whole settlement came out—which, granted, was nineteen people, but nineteen people standing on a mudflat in the cold makes a crowd the way one candle makes a room brighter.

Tam read the names. He'd offered before I could ask, and I understood why: he wanted his voice attached to it, wanted the community's voice, not just mine. He read all eleven slowly, giving each one the full weight of its syllables, and when he reached the last—my mechanic Rell, who used to sing badly and constantly—his voice caught and he pushed through it, and nobody looked away, because looking away would have been the insult.

Pip laid wildflowers against the base, orange and stubborn, the kind that grow out of gravel and don't care about conditions. Then Pip turned around and faced me in front of everyone, fourteen years old and fearless in the way I remembered being once, before I learned what fear cost.

"Say something," Pip demanded.

"There's nothing to say that isn't on the rock."

"Then say that."

So I said it. I said: *Everything true is on the stone. Everything else is ours.* And Odile, standing at the edge of the group with her arms crossed against the cold, uncrossed them.

Afterward the crowd thinned the way crowds do, drifting back toward the post and the smell of Tam's coffee, and I stayed behind a while, the way I always would, and Odile stayed with me. We stood looking at the eleven names with the water sliding past below them.

"Ten years," she said. "You lived ten years forty feet from us and we never knew your name was even real."

"It's real."

"I know. That's the strange part. All this time I thought the mystery was hiding something worse." She shook her head. "It was just hiding a man."

"A man who lied to you for a decade."

"A man who carried medicine through weather that grounded everyone else, fixed our generator three winters running, and taught Pip to fly straighter than pilots twice his age." She finally looked at me. "The lying was the debt. The rest was the payment. I've decided the ledger balances."

"That's generous."

"That's arithmetic." She bumped my shoulder with hers, brief and hard, and walked back toward the trucks, and I stood alone with the stone a while longer, saying the names silently, the way I would every time I passed, for as long as I had passes left in me.

* * *

The paint dried hard that week, and on the first clear morning Pip and I took the plane up with the seal on its tail and flew the long slow circuit over the marker, low enough that our wake rippled the water across the graves, and Pip leaned forward in the second seat with both hands on the dash like a kid at a parade.

"They'll ask about it," Pip said. "At the service. In the capital. Everyone will want to know why a bush pilot has a dead nation on his tail."

"Because someone has to remember what the maps forgot."

"And you're okay with everyone knowing? The whole story? Kolvek knows, the board knows, soon the papers will—" Pip glanced sideways at me, and there was real worry under the excitement. "You spent ten years making sure nobody knew anything."

I banked gently south, bringing the marker into the windscreen one more time, the little granite tooth standing in the silver water with its eleven names facing the sky.

"For ten years I thought invisible was safe," I said. "Turns out it was just lonely. There's a difference between being hidden and being home, and I finally know which one this is."

Pip grinned and pointed north, toward the post, where the smoke was rising straight up from Tam's chimney and Odile was probably already reorganizing his toolshed out of pure love, and I turned the plane that way and started the descent.

Below us the river kept moving, carrying everything it carried, keeping what it kept. But it had given back what mattered. The rest—the letters, the hearings, the strangers in the capital who would want pieces of my story—I'd face them in daylight, in plain sight, with my name spoken out loud.

Not hidden anymore.

Just home.
