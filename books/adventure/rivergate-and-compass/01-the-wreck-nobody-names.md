## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The river at dawn was the color of old pewter, and Coral Voss loved it the way you love a thing that has taken everything from you — without apology and without illusion.

She stood on the stern of the *Magpie* in her dive skin, checking her gauges for the third time though she'd checked them twice already. Habit. Her father used to say a diver who trusted her memory over her instruments was a diver with an appointment at the bottom. She could still hear his voice saying it, that low river-gravel rasp, and she let herself hear it for exactly as long as it took to clip her regulator to her BC, and not one second longer.

"Bottom's at forty-one meters," Mercer said from the helm. He didn't look up from the depth sounder. He never looked up when they did this kind of work, as if the less he saw of it, the less of it stuck to him. "Silt's going to be thick down there. You'll have maybe a hand's width of visibility once you stir it."

"Then I won't stir it." Coral spat into her mask, rinsed it, tipped the water out. "I'll be polite."

Junie leaned over the rail beside her, sixteen years old and all elbows, watching the water with the particular hunger she got whenever Coral went down and she couldn't follow. "You see anything weird down there, you come straight up. You promise?"

"I never promise anything on a dive." Coral knocked her knuckles gently against the girl's shoulder. "But I'll be careful, if that counts."

"It doesn't."

"No," Coral agreed. "It doesn't."

Tam was below, in the engine room, keeping the compressor singing so the surface line would stay live. She could feel the *Magpie*'s heartbeat through the deck plates, that steady diesel thrum that had become, over six years, indistinguishable from her own pulse. The boat was mortgaged twice over. So was the compressor. So, if she was honest — and standing on the edge of cold water at first light was when she was most honest — so was she.

Forty-one meters. That was the number that mattered this morning, and not because of the physics.

Because of what sat at forty-one meters.

* * *

Everyone on the Rivergate knew about the hole in the shipping channel past Kiln Bend. It wasn't on any chart. It wasn't discussed at the dockside bars, or if it was, it was discussed in the way people discuss weather that killed someone — briefly, quietly, with a change of subject built into the last sentence. Divers called it the Transport, when they called it anything at all: a navy vessel, big as a chapel, that had gone down thirty-odd years ago with no wreck report, no salvage claim, no buoy marking it as a hazard to navigation. A ship that had sunk and been *forgotten*, which is a thing ships don't do unless someone is paid very well to forget them.

Coral had found it two years ago, running a side-scan survey for a bridge contractor, and had made the mistake of mentioning it out loud. Within a week, a man in a clean shirt she'd never seen before or since had come to the docks, bought her a coffee she didn't drink, and explained the situation to her in a voice like a man reading terms and conditions. The wreck was a navigation concern. It would be dealt with. In the meantime, divers were advised to stay clear, for their own safety.

Then he'd slid an envelope across the table. Five hundred dollars. Not payment — he'd been explicit about that, almost offended by the word. A goodwill gesture. An acknowledgment that she understood.

She'd taken it. That was the part that still sat wrong in her chest, worse than any debt. She'd taken the money, and she'd stayed clear, and every month since, another envelope had appeared — tucked under the *Magpie*'s wheel, taped inside the fuel shed — five hundred dollars, always cash, always unsigned. Hush money, paid out with the regularity of tide tables. She'd told herself it was compensation. Retainer. Whatever word made it bearable.

This morning's envelope had been different. This morning's envelope had come with a note, typed, four lines long:

*Item of interest in the forward cargo hold. Sealed case, olive drab, stenciled HYDRO-7. Recover it. Do not open it. Do not speak of it. Payment upon delivery doubles the monthly arrangement.*

No signature. No return address. And no way, she'd realized somewhere around three a.m., staring at the ceiling of her cabin while the river slapped the hull, to ever go back to being a person who hadn't read that note.

So here she was.

* * *

The water took her the way it always did — a cold hand closing over her whole body at once, erasing the sky, the boat, the year. Coral hung in the green murk for a moment and let her breathing settle, long slow draws, until the regulator's hiss matched her heartbeat. Then she tipped headfirst and followed the descent line down into the dark.

The thermocline hit at twelve meters, a wall of cold that raised gooseflesh under her hood. At twenty-five, the light began to fail in earnest, the gray-green dimming toward something older than night. She ran her light along the line, watched her bubbles climb away from her like a handful of released birds, and thought about nothing, which was the hardest work of any dive and the thing she was best at.

The bottom came up to meet her at thirty-eight meters of actual depth — the riverbed had silted above the true soundings, Mercer would want to know — and there, rising out of the ooze like a drowned cathedral, lay the Transport.

Even after two years, it stopped her breath.

It lay on its starboard side, half-buried, nearly ninety meters of navy steel furred with zebra mussels and trailing curtains of silt-fine algae. Its hull numbers had been ground off — not painted over, *ground off*, the steel scarred in long ugly strokes where someone had taken a grinder to identity marks. Its superstructure had collapsed inward, folded flat like a crushed tin cup. Around it, the riverbed was littered with debris that had drifted and settled in a wide apron: plating, cable, a cargo net fused into the mud, and — Coral's light caught it and moved on quickly — things she preferred not to name even in her own head. Boots. A helmet. The river kept what it was given.

She swam the length of the hull toward the bow, fins kicking in slow, measured strokes, disturbing as little of the silt as she could. Visibility held at maybe four meters, better than Mercer had promised, but she knew how fast that could die. One careless fin-beat against soft bottom and she'd be diving blind inside a wreck, which was how divers ended up as part of the debris field.

The forward cargo hold access was a hatch on the port side, now facing upward, its dogs already thrown back.

That was the detail that had lived in the back of her skull for two years like a splinter. The hatch was *open*. Not blown, not rusted through — opened, deliberately, from outside, sometime after the sinking. Someone had been in this hold before her. Someone with tools and time and a reason.

Her father had dove this river for thirty years. He'd mapped channels, recovered bodies for the sheriff, pulled cars and engines and once, famously, a grand piano out of the brown water. He'd taught her everything: buoyancy like breath, knots, the reading of current, the patience. And in all those years, all those stories told over engine repairs and cheap beer, he had never once mentioned the Transport. A man who talked about everything — who'd narrated his own near-drowning in '09 with such relish that Junie could perform it as a party piece — had said *nothing*.

Coral had spent a long time deciding what that silence meant. Lately, since the envelopes started, she'd begun to suspect it meant he'd known exactly what she now knew: that some wrecks aren't accidents, and some silences are purchased, and her father had either been offered the same envelope or had refused it, and she would never know which, because the river had taken him eight years ago on a routine job in good conditions, and had never given him back.

She clipped her line to the hatch coaming and dropped into the hold.

* * *

Inside, the dark was total. Her light carved a cone through water thick with suspended sediment, and the beam came back to her full of drifting motes, like swimming through a snow globe of ash. Cargo webbing hung in rotting loops from overhead rails. Crates sat stacked and slumped, their stenciling long since eaten away. The hold was bigger than it should have been — she'd guessed from the exterior that it was a standard transport configuration, but the interior had been modified, bulkheads added, compartments subdivided, a layout that made no sense for moving freight and perfect sense for moving something that needed to be organized, catalogued, contained.

Something had been carried in this ship that required architecture.

Her light swept across the compartment numbers still faintly legible on the added bulkheads — 1 through 9 — and stopped at compartment 7.

HYDRO-7, the note had said. She'd assumed it was a crate designation. Standing here, seeing the numbered compartments, she understood it differently. Compartment seven wasn't where the case was stored. Compartment seven was what the case was *from*.

She crossed the hold in slow pulls on the guide line, her gauge showing nineteen minutes of bottom time burned, and found the forward-most sub-compartment behind a jammed door she had to coax open with a pry bar from her kit. Inside, settled into the silt like it had grown there, sat a single olive-drab hard case, roughly the size of a footlocker, sealed with military latches and wrapped in a waterproof membrane gone brittle as autumn leaves.

No other cases. No other crates in this compartment. Just the one, alone, in a room built to hold many.

Deliberately buried. Deliberately left. The words arrived in her mind with the calm certainty of a depth reading, and she made herself look at what they meant: whoever had opened this hatch years ago had taken everything else and left this. Left it, or hidden it, or been unable to carry it out. A case in an empty room on a ship nobody named, at the bottom of a river everyone pretended was empty too.

Coral checked her gas. Fourteen minutes. Enough.

Getting the case out was the kind of problem she was genuinely good at, and she felt the small hot pleasure of competence cut through the dread as she worked — rigging a lift bag, flushing the silt with controlled bursts from her alternate, walking the case up off the bottom in stages rather than trying to haul it, keeping her trim, keeping her breathing shallow and even, doing everything right. The bag bloomed above her like a pale jellyfish, and the case rose with it, and she guided it through the hatch and out into the gray open water above the wreck.

At the top of her ascent hang, ten meters down, she paused as her training demanded, and looked back.

The Transport lay below her in the failing light, vast and patient and full of rooms. From this angle, with the silt hanging around it like breath held, it looked less like a wreck than like a body — something that had died holding its secrets and would keep them as long as the river allowed.

And on the hull, just visible through the mussel crust where her light grazed it, she saw something she had missed on her survey run two years ago, something too deep in the shadow of the collapsed superstructure to catch on side-scan.

Letters. Stenciled, faded, but there, in the standard navy lettering style, ahead of the ground-off numbers:

*RIVERGATE SURVEY UNIT.*

Not a transport. A survey vessel. A mapping ship, sent up a river to chart it, sunk with its identity scraped away and its holds reconfigured, carrying numbered compartments of hydrographic equipment to a place where hydrographic equipment had no business being.

Coral hung in the cold water and felt the whole shape of her life tilt a few degrees, the way a boat tilts when something heavy shifts in the hold.

Her father had been a survey diver. Her compass — his compass, the brass one she wore on a lanyard on every dive and hadn't yet looked at this morning — had been drifting wrong for months, tugging upstream toward the river's source no matter how she held it, and she'd been telling herself it was a broken pivot, a repairable thing, a small mechanical grief.

The case bumped gently against her hip, weightless underwater and heavier than lead.

She finished her hang. She went up.

* * *

The surface hit her like a slap of ordinary life — wind, gull noise, Junie's whoop, Tam's hands hauling the case over the gunwale with a grunt and then going very still.

"This weigh what it looks like?" Tam asked. Tam was broad and quiet and could fix anything with moving parts, and right now Tam was looking at the olive-drab case the way a mechanic looks at a noise they've heard before and hoped never to hear again.

"Heavier," Coral said, shedding her gear. "It's full."

Mercer stood at the helm with his back to them, which was answer enough. Old Mercer, who'd piloted supply boats upriver before the restrictions, who never talked about those years, whose face had done something complicated and closed when she'd shown him the note this morning — he stood with his hands on the wheel steering them home through water he'd steered his whole life, and he did not turn around, and he did not ask what she'd found.

Junie crouched next to the case, eyes huge. "Are you going to open it?"

"Not on this boat." Coral wrapped the lanyard of her father's compass around her fist, an old habit, a talisman. Under her palm, the needle trembled in its housing, restless, insistent, pointing not north but upriver — toward Kiln Bend, toward the high country, toward the source of the river that had given her everything and buried the rest.

The note had said do not open it. The note had not said what was inside. But she had held the case in her hands, and she had felt, through the membrane and the steel, the unmistakable flat slide of paper. Charts. Documents. Words.

Things that could be read.

Rivergate's docks rose out of the morning haze ahead, cranes and tarps and the smell of diesel and fish, her creditors waiting in it somewhere like rocks under brown water. Coral looked down at the case, then upriver, where the water ran silver toward mountains nobody was supposed to ask about.

"Take us in, Mercer," she said. "We're done pretending we don't know where this goes."
