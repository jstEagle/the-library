## 2. Debt and Salt Water

The Rivergate docks smelled the way they always did at low tide — diesel and rot and the mineral ghost of the river itself, that faint cold iron note that never quite washed out of the pilings no matter how many storms the water threw at them. Coral Voss came up her own ladder with the sealed case strapped to her back and the taste of the deep channel still in her throat, and she stood for a moment on the deck of the *Magpie* just breathing, letting the wind off the water scour her clean.

Forty-two minutes on the wreck. Thirty-one meters down. Her hands had only stopped shaking in the last hundred meters of ascent, and even now, gripping the rail, she could feel the tremor living somewhere in her forearms like a bird trying to get out.

The *Magpie* sat low in the water, which was nothing new. Everything about the boat sat low these days — the hull, the fuel gauge, the mood of the three people who called her home. She was a sixty-year-old salvage tug with a patched stern and an engine Tam described, with the tenderness of a parent describing a sick child, as "running on prayer and spite." Her paint had once been red. Coral remembered it red, from the photographs her father kept in a tin box under his bunk, the *Magpie* young and bright with a younger man standing at her wheel.

Now she was the color of old brick left out in the rain, and she was the only thing Coral owned outright that her creditors hadn't figured out how to take.

"You're back." Junie's head appeared over the wheelhouse rail, upside down, hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like an electrical cord. "You're back and you're vertical. I had a pool going."

"With who?"

"Mercer. He had you coming up at the two-hour mark with a crab attached to your face." Junie vaulted the rail and landed on deck with the grace of someone who had grown up climbing things she shouldn't have. Nineteen years old, all elbows and appetite, the best deckhand on the river and the worst gambler, mostly because she bet with her heart. "So? Was it there?"

Coral shrugged the case off her shoulder and set it down between her feet. It was the size of a footlocker, matte gray, unmarked, heavier than it had any right to be. The client's instructions had been precise to the point of insult: *Recover the case. Do not open the case. Deliver the case to the drop point at Marker 14 by Thursday. Payment on delivery.*

"Was what there?" Coral said.

Junie looked at her the way she looked at Tam when he claimed he'd *totally* checked the bilge. "Coral."

"It was there."

"And?"

"And it's Thursday's problem." Coral hoisted the case and stowed it in the forward hold, under the tarp, under the spare anchor chain, under the coil of rope Junie had been meaning to re-flake since spring. Out of sight. She told herself the tightness in her chest was just the residual pressure of the dive, the nitrogen still working its way out of her blood, and not the weight of a thing she'd been paid handsomely to pretend she hadn't seen.

She had seen it, though. She'd seen the way the case sat in the transport's cargo bay, strapped down with military webbing, surrounded by crates stamped with an insignia she'd been told a dozen times in her life didn't exist. She'd seen the way the wreck's hull had been *cut* — not torn, not blown, cut, with the clean surgical lines of people who had all the time in the world and no intention of ever being found. And she'd seen, in the silt beside the case, a dive boot. Just one. Navy issue. Sitting upright in the mud as neatly as if its owner had stepped out of it on the way to somewhere else.

Forty-two minutes. She'd spent thirty-eight of them telling herself it wasn't her problem.

The river didn't care what she told herself. The river just carried things downstream and waited to see who picked them up.

* * *

The *Salt Larder* was the dockside cantina where the river trade ate when they couldn't afford to eat anywhere else, which was to say: always. It was a low room with a tin roof that turned rain into applause and a proprietor named Odile who extended credit the way other people extended charity — reluctantly, with interest, and with a running tally she kept in her head because writing it down would have made it real.

Coral was three steps through the door when she saw them and understood that Thursday had come early.

There were two of them, seated at the corner table with the view of the docks, because creditors always took the table with the view of the docks. It was a kind of theater. The older one she knew: Hask Grier, who held paper on the *Magpie's* engine rebuild and on the mooring fees going back eight months, a soft-bodied man with a hard voice and a ledger's soul. The younger one she didn't — pressed shirt, river-polished shoes, a leather folder squared on the table in front of him like a man who had never once had to dry out a document.

"Voss." Hask didn't get up. He never got up. "Sit. Eat something. You look like the inside of a boot."

"Charming as ever, Hask."

"This is Mr. Pell. He's taken an interest in your outstanding obligations."

Mr. Pell smiled with exactly half his mouth. "An interest," he agreed. "Ms. Voss. Your debts, consolidated, come to a figure I suspect you already know. What you may not know is that my employer is in a position to be flexible. Restructure. Even forgive." He let the word sit there between them like bait in clear water. "Under the right circumstances."

"What circumstances?"

"Delivery." He tapped the folder once. "You've taken a private contract recently. Salvage, deep channel, north bend. Our employer would simply like to know what you found."

The room's noise — the clatter of the kitchen, Odile's voice, the tin roof ticking in the wind — seemed to arrange itself around the silence at their table.

"I found a wreck," Coral said. "I found a lot of mud. I found a boot."

"A boot."

"Navy issue. Size eleven. If your employer wants it, it's still down there. I can draw you a chart."

Pell's half-smile didn't waver, but something behind his eyes did — a small recalculation, a man updating a file. "Thursday," he said. "Marker 14. When the case is delivered, my employer considers the matter closed." He stood, squared the folder under his arm, and added, almost kindly, which was somehow worse: "You have a good crew, Ms. Voss. Be a shame if their livelihood had a lien on it."

They watched him go. Hask lingered long enough to say, "Eight months, Voss," and to leave coins on the table for his tea, because Hask Grier would cheat you on interest but never on tea.

Coral sat down across from the empty chairs and put her face in her hands.

* * *

Tam found her there an hour later, or maybe she found him; the sequence of that evening was never entirely clear afterward. The *Salt Larder* filled up with the river crowd as the light went copper and then gray — deckhands and pilots and the mudlarks who worked the shallows, and the crew of the *Magpie* at their usual table by the window, which Odile kept for them the way you keep a grave tended.

Tam Okafor was thirty-four and built like something the river had tried to drown several times and failed, broad and patient, with burn-scarred forearms and reading glasses he wore pushed up on his head like a crown he kept forgetting he was owed. He'd come to Rivergate eleven years ago off a dredging outfit that went under with two men drowned, and he'd never left, and he'd never said why, and nobody asked, because that was the etiquette of the docks: everyone was running from something, and the river was big enough to hold all of it.

He set a bowl in front of her — fish stew, heavy on the pepper, light on the fish — and sat down across from her without asking.

"Pell," he said. Not a question. The whole dock had watched the man in the pressed shirt walk the length of the pier, reading boat names like he was taking inventory for a seizure.

"You saw."

"Everyone saw." Tam broke bread, handed her half. "He stopped at the *Kestrel*, too. And the *Ferryman's Daughter*. He's not here for you specifically, Coral. He's here for the whole channel."

"Great. I'm part of a trend."

"Coral." Tam's voice did the thing it did, the low patient register he used on the engine when it shuddered in a way he didn't like. "What's in the case?"

"You know I can't—"

"I know you *won't*." He chewed, swallowed, looked out the window at the dark water. "Eleven years I've kept this boat alive with spit and stolen parts. Junie's been with us two years and she's the best hands I've worked with, and she's here because nowhere else would take a nineteen-year-old girl with a mouth. Mercer's been here since before any of us, and he drinks like a man paying off a debt nobody can see. We are not a crew, Coral. We are a lifeboat. And lifeboats have rules, and rule one is you don't take on water on purpose."

"I know."

"Do you? Because the way Junie tells it, you came up from that wreck moving like you'd seen a ghost, and now there's a man in pressed shirts asking after what you saw, and you're telling me it's Thursday's problem." He pushed his glasses down onto his nose and looked at her over them, and it was unbearable, that look, because it wasn't angry. It was *worried*. "I can rebuild any engine you put in front of me. I can't rebuild you."

Coral looked down at her stew. The pepper had made her eyes water, which was convenient.

"Three more days," she said. "I deliver the case Thursday, we get paid, we clear the engine note, we clear the mooring, we're clean through the winter. One contract, Tam. One more, and we're free."

"Nobody's free," Tam said, but he said it gently, and he refilled her bowl without being asked, and that was the thing about Tam, that was the whole of him: he would tell you the truth and then he would feed you anyway.

* * *

Mercer was on the *Magpie* when she got back, sitting in the wheelhouse with a lamp turned low and a bottle turned lower, and he didn't startle when she came up the ladder, because Mercer had stopped startling at anything sometime in the previous century.

Old boat pilot, old as the channel markers, with hands like braided rope and a face the river had been working on for seventy years. He'd run supply boats upriver back when upriver still had traffic, before the navy closed the upper reaches and the maps went blank past the bend. He knew every snag and sandbar from the mouth to the falls, and he knew, Coral suspected, a great many other things he kept in the flooded hold of himself, along with everything else he never talked about.

"Sit," he said, and she sat, and he poured her a finger of the raw cane spirit he called medicine and everyone else called paint thinner.

They drank. The lamp hissed. Out on the water, a barge horn moaned somewhere down toward the mouth, long and low, the river talking in its sleep.

"Man came by the boat this afternoon," Mercer said at last. "While you were out. Pressed shirt. Asked me questions about the north bend."

Coral went very still. "What did you tell him?"

"Asked me if I'd ever run cargo for the navy." Mercer turned the bottle in his hands, watching the lamplight swim in it. "Asked me if I knew anything about a transport that went down up there. Thirty years back, give or take." He took a drink. "I told him I'm an old man with a bad memory, and I poured him a drink, and he left."

"Mercer—"

"I know what you're going to ask."

"Then you know I have to."

"And you know I'll answer the same way I've answered for thirty years." He looked at her then, and his eyes in the lamplight were pale and flat and terribly tired, like water over deep mud. "Some water runs over things, girl, and some water runs *under* them, and the under-kind stays cold no matter how hot the summer gets. You dove that wreck. I can see it on you. Whatever you found down there — put it back. Bury it deeper. Some things the river keeps because the river knows better than we do."

"You don't believe that."

"No," Mercer said. "I don't. That's the worst part." He capped the bottle. "Go get some sleep. Tide turns at four."

She climbed down the wheelhouse stairs and stopped. "Mercer. The boot I found. In the silt by the cargo bay. Was it — did you ever—"

"I said tide turns at four," Mercer said, and turned the lamp down to nothing, and that was the end of the conversation, the way so many conversations with Mercer ended: with the door of him closing, gently, on a room she wasn't allowed to see.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She went forward instead, to the little cabin that had been her father's, that was hers now, with its single porthole and its shelf of salt-swollen paperbacks and the tin box she kept at the back of the lowest drawer.

The compass lived in the tin box. Brass-cased, glass scratched to frost, the kind of instrument they'd stopped making before she was born. Her father had carried it on every dive, every run, every crossing; the crew used to joke that he trusted it more than the depth gauge, more than the engine, more than God. When they'd pulled his boat out of the channel six years ago — empty, foundered, no sign of him, the third such loss the upper river had swallowed without a burp — the compass had been lashed to the wheel, pointing, as far as anyone could tell, at nothing.

Coral held it now in both hands, in the dark, and did the thing she did some nights and admitted to no one.

She turned it.

North. She turned it west, and the needle swung obediently west, and then — there, always there, the small stubborn drift — it eased back, a few degrees at a time, off true north, off any north at all, and settled pointing upriver. Up the channel. Past the bend. Into the blank space on the maps where the river went and the world's paperwork didn't.

She turned it south. The needle swung south, wobbled, and crept back upriver.

It had done it for six years. She'd had it checked twice, by instrument men who'd shaken their heads and said the magnetization was wrong, impossible, some local anomaly in the rock maybe, and she'd nodded and paid them and known, in the place where she kept the things she knew and never said, that it wasn't the rock.

Her father had marked his charts in pencil, in a small neat hand. She still had some of them. On the oldest one, the one of the upper reaches, there was a cross penciled at a bend in a tributary that wasn't on any chart printed before or since, and beside the cross, in that small neat hand, two words:

*It points.*

She put the compass back in the tin box. She put the tin box back in the drawer. She lay down on the bunk that still smelled faintly, impossibly, of his pipe tobacco, and stared at the porthole where the dock lights swam, and told herself, again, the story she'd been telling herself for six years: that her father had been a careful man, and the river was a careless one, and that was the whole of it.

Thursday. Deliver the case. Clear the debts. Keep the lifeboat afloat.

* * *

She was on deck at dawn, as she always was, when the river turned silver and the gulls started their arguments, and that was when she saw the boat coming up the channel.

It was a government survey launch — she knew the type, white hull, gray trim, the little radar mast, the insignia of the Department of Waterways — and it was coming straight for the *Magpie*, moving with the unhurried confidence of a vessel that had never once been boarded, fined, or asked to explain itself. A man stood at the rail in a clean field jacket, holding a leather folder squared against his chest, and behind him, in the launch's cabin, she could see the glint of instruments and the pale rectangles of paperwork stacked in a way that suggested everything had been filled out in triplicate by someone who had never gotten their hands wet in their life.

Tam came up on deck behind her, wiping his hands on a rag. Junie appeared at the wheelhouse window, hair still tangled from sleep. Down in the stern, the hatch of Mercer's cabin opened without a sound, and the old man came out and stood at the rail, and Coral watched him look at the launch, and watched the color go out of his face.

"That's a survey boat," Junie said. "What do they want with us?"

The launch throttled down. The man in the field jacket raised one hand in a greeting that was not a wave — it was a summons, Coral thought, a thing you'd hail a defendant with.

"Triple rate," Tam murmured, reading the folder before the man had even stepped aboard, because Tam could smell paperwork the way sharks smelled blood. "Mapping run, upriver. That's... Coral, that's three times the going rate for a charting job. Nobody pays triple for charting."

"No," Coral said. She watched the launch bump against the *Magpie's* hull, watched the fenders groan, watched the man in the field jacket prepare to step onto her deck with his clean folder and his clean boots and his clean, too-smooth paperwork. "Nobody does."

The gangway came across.
