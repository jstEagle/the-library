## 3. A Compass That Points Wrong

The compass lived in a tin that had once held cough drops, back when Coral's mother was still buying cough drops and still believing they would help. The tin was dented along one edge where it had taken a fall — Coral knew this because her father had told her the story a hundred times, how he'd dropped it off the rail of the *Marrow* in a squall off the lower bends and a deckhand had gone in after it without being asked, without even taking off his boots. That was the kind of thing worth knowing about a man, her father used to say. Not what he carried. What he'd dive for.

She had not opened the tin in four years.

Now she sat on the floor of her cabin aboard the *Marrow* — her cabin, her boat, her debt, all of it hers in the way that a stone tied around the neck belongs to the drowning — with the tin in her lap and the lamp turned low and the river talking to itself outside the hull in the low, patient voice it used at night, the one that never quite stopped, never quite said anything.

Her hands were still smelling of the wreck. She had scrubbed them twice at the dock and once more here, and still there was that faint mineral tang, the cold-room smell of deep water and old steel, the smell of the transport sitting on its side in the dark with its hatches sealed and its name scraped off its stern. She kept seeing the case in her mind. Not the case she'd been paid to bring up — that one was already gone, handed to the broker in a greased handshake at the fuel pier, its weight exchanged for a number that would cover maybe a third of what she owed and not a day of what she feared. She kept seeing the *other* thing. The way the transport's cargo deck had been arranged. The way things had been *placed*, not stowed. Someone had put that ship on the bottom like a hand closing over something it didn't want seen.

She shook her head. That was tomorrow's problem, or next week's, or never's. Tonight there was only the tin.

She thumbed the lid open.

The compass sat in its bed of faded red felt, and the first thing that hit her — before the brass, before the glass, before the needle — was the smell of it. Oil and river and something like iron left out in rain. Her father's smell, or the smell of his pockets, which for a child is the same thing. She had to put the tin down on the deck boards for a moment and breathe, one hand flat against her sternum, because grief is a diver's trouble too: it doesn't announce itself, it just takes you at depth and squeezes, and you either manage the ascent or you don't.

She managed it. She'd had practice.

* * *

The compass was a salvager's compass, not a sailor's. That distinction mattered, her father had explained, when she was nine or ten and all elbows and questions, sitting on his knee in this very cabin while he cleaned the instrument with a rag and a patience he never seemed to have for anything else. A sailor's compass tells you where north is. A salvager's compass tells you where *you* are — it's a bearing instrument, a thing you sight along, a thing you trust when the water takes your eyes and the mud takes your memory. He'd had it engraved, on the inside of the lid, with a line she had never fully understood:

**THE RIVER REMEMBERS WHAT WE FORGET.**

She turned the tin so the lamp caught the letters. The engraving was worn nearly smooth now. Everything about the compass was worn — the brass gone dark at the hinges, the glass scratched in an arc like something had raked across it, the felt beneath it bleached to the pink of an old scar. It had been used hard and carried long, and it had outlived the man who carried it, and now it sat in her hands doing the one thing it had never done in all the years she'd known it.

It was pointing wrong.

Not broken-wrong. She knew broken. A broken needle wanders, sulks, lies flat, sticks. This needle was alive and eager and *steady*, trembling only the way a good needle trembles, and it was pointing upriver. Not north. She checked it against the bulkhead seam, against the lamp bracket, against the little brass line her father had inlaid in the deck years ago to true the old ship's binnacle — the needle ignored all of it. It swung with a small, deliberate insistence until it lay along a bearing that ran straight up the Rivergate channel, past the fuel docks, past the last of the lit windows, into the dark upper reaches where the river narrowed and the maps went thin and the navy's charts, if you could ever get a look at them, went blank.

She turned the compass a quarter-turn left. The needle swung and settled, pointing upriver.

She turned it a quarter-turn right. The needle swung and settled, pointing upriver.

She stood, walked it to the porthole, held it against the window frame, against the horizon, against the distant red blink of the channel marker. Upriver. She held it upside down, which no manual recommends and every diver tries once. The needle fought through the inversion and came back to its bearing like a dog coming home.

"Well," she said to the empty cabin, because saying things out loud was a habit from diving, where silence can make you believe anything. "That's new."

It wasn't new. That was the trouble. Some part of her, some deep part that kept its own logbook, stirred and said: *you've seen this before.* She sat back down on the bunk with the compass open in her palm and let the memory come up slow, the way you let a body come up on a line — hand over hand, no rushing it, or it'll tear.

* * *

She was fourteen. It was the summer the dry season bit early, and the *Marrow* was running a contract to clear snags above the Bend, and her father had let her steer for the first time, which had made her so proud she'd barely slept for a week. She remembered the cabin at night, the same lamp, the same deck seams. And she remembered — suddenly, completely, the way a whole room comes back to you from a single smell — her father standing where she was standing now, holding this same compass, turning it the same way, quarter-turn left, quarter-turn right, his face doing something she had never seen it do before and would not see again until the day men came to tell her he was dead.

He had said, not to her, because he hadn't known she was awake in the forward bunk: "Every year it's a little further."

And then, after a long time, in a voice she'd had to strain for under the river-noise: "What did you *find*, old man?"

She had thought, at fourteen, half-asleep, that he was talking to the compass. It was only now, with the instrument warm in her hand and the needle lying patient along its impossible bearing, that she understood he had probably been talking to whoever had owned it before him. Because the compass was old. Older than her father's whole career, older than the *Marrow*'s engine, older than the felt it slept on. And her father had been a man who salvaged more than cargo. He'd salvaged stories, names, the odd grief of strangers off the bottom of the river. Somewhere, sometime, this compass had come up out of the water in someone's hand, and her father had known whose.

*What did you find, old man.*

She set the compass down and pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes until she saw sparks.

Four years since the funeral. Four years since the navy officer with the flat voice and the flat eyes had handed her a folded flag she had no idea what to do with, and a report that said *recovered remains, cause of death: drowning, circumstances: non-operational, location: withheld*, and she had been too gutted and too young and too broke to ask the questions that now, in the lamplight, lined themselves up like pilings in fog. *Location withheld.* Who withholds the location of a drowning? What kind of river kills a man with thirty years of water in his blood and then needs its coordinates kept secret?

And the compass had been in his effects, sealed in an evidence bag, and she had signed for it, and she had put it in the cough-drop tin, and she had not opened the tin in four years because some things you keep shut the way you keep a wound shut — not to heal it, but to keep from looking at it.

She opened the lid wider and looked.

* * *

There were other things in the tin. She had forgotten, or had made herself forget. Beneath the felt bed, which lifted out on two fingers, there was a flat space, and in the flat space: a stub of pencil, a folded scrap of oilcloth, and a strip of paper gone soft and brown at the edges, covered in her father's hand — the small, cramped, leftward-leaning script that she knew better than her own signature.

She unfolded the paper with the care you'd give a recovered document, because that's what it was. A dive note. She knew the format; she'd grown up on it. Depth, time down, bottom conditions, visibility. But the numbers were wrong for any site she knew. The depth was one no river in the valley could give you. The bottom conditions read: *limestone. cut stone. not natural.* And the last line, in handwriting that had pressed hard enough to nearly tear the paper, said:

*It breathes. The water comes UP. God forgive us, it comes up warm.*

She read it three times. The lamp hissed. Outside, a night heron worked the shallows with its ugly, wonderful patience, and the river went on saying nothing in particular, and Coral Voss sat very still with her father's last handwriting in her hands and felt the floor of her life tilt by a few degrees, the way a boat tilts when something heavy shifts below.

*It comes up warm.* Rivers didn't come up warm. Rivers came down from cold country, from snowmelt and shadow, and they arrived at Rivergate the temperature of stone. Everyone knew that. Everyone except, apparently, her father, and whoever had written the survey notes she'd glimpsed — no. She stopped herself. She hadn't glimpsed any survey notes. That was tomorrow. That was the broker and the sealed case and the scraped-off name and the navy that didn't have a wreck in this river, officially, and had paid her — through a broker, through a cutout, through two layers of greased hands — to bring up a piece of it anyway.

Her father's compass pointed at the same stretch of dark water.

She thought: *coincidence,* in the voice she used for junior divers who believed in luck. And then she thought about the way things had been *placed* on that transport's cargo deck, deliberate as a hand closing, and she did not believe in coincidence at that depth. Nobody does who has spent their life down where the light gives out. Down there you learn that everything you find was put there, or lost there, or taken there, and none of it is accidental, and the river keeps its ledger with terrible patience.

*The river remembers what we forget.*

She closed the compass lid on the worn engraving and held the tin against her chest for a moment, the way you'd hold a child, or a bomb. Then she got practical, because grief was a current and practicality was her line, and you didn't let go of the line.

* * *

She worked. That was the mercy of her hands: they always knew what to do even when the rest of her was somewhere cold and dark. She cleaned the compass with the same rag and the same patience, oiling the gimbals, checking the glass seat, and while she worked she talked to him. She never had when he was alive — Villalobos men and women kept their words for the water — but dead men are easier to talk to; they don't interrupt with advice.

"You knew," she said. "Whatever this is, whatever's up there, you knew, and you went back, and they gave me a flag and a blank spot on a map for it." The rag moved in small circles. "I'm not angry. I want that on the record. I'm not angry, I'm just — " She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I'm diving your last site, old man. That's all. I'm just finishing the job."

The needle, when she opened the lid again, had not moved. Upriver. Into the narrowing dark, past the last charted bend, toward whatever came up warm.

She was still holding it when the knock came — three raps, spaced wrong, the rhythm Tam used when he'd had bad news and too much coffee. She closed the tin, and for the first time in four years she did not put it away in the drawer beneath the charts. She left it on the table, open, the needle lying steady along its bearing, facing upriver like a dog that has caught a scent it will not lose.

"Come in," she said.

Tam filled the doorway, and behind him, out on the dock, under the sodium lights, stood a man in a government-issue rain jacket holding a leather folder, and even at this distance, even through the river-hum and the lamp-hiss, Coral felt the compass's pull against her palm like a second heartbeat, and thought: *there it is. The river's ledger, come to collect.*
