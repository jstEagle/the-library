## 4. The Survey Team

Tam Okafor woke before dawn the way he always did — not to an alarm but to a sound: the particular creak of the *Magpie's* hull breathing against her mooring lines as the river shifted beneath her. Most people heard nothing in that sound. Tam heard everything. Twenty years of engines and welds and water had tuned his ears like instruments, and this morning the *Magpie* was telling him something he didn't want to hear.

The starboard shaft bearing was going dry.

He lay in his bunk in the engine room's shadow — he'd converted the aft hold into a berth years ago because it put him within arm's reach of the machinery that kept them all alive — and listened to the bearing complain through two decks of steel. A faint, high whine on every rotation. Nothing catastrophic yet. Nothing that wouldn't wait. But bearings were honest in a way people weren't, and this one was telling him they had maybe six weeks of hard running left before it seized, and a seized shaft forty miles from Rivergate meant a dead boat, and a dead boat meant —

He got up. No point finishing the thought. The thought finished itself every night anyway.

*Six weeks.* He dressed in the dark, pulled the ledger from under his mattress — Coral would have mocked him for keeping paper records, but paper couldn't be hacked, frozen, or subpoenaed, and Tam trusted paper the way some men trusted God — and ran the numbers again even though he'd run them the night before and the night before that.

They were eleven hundred short on the mooring fees alone. Fourteen hundred behind on the fuel account at Calloway's dock. The insurance lapsed in nine days. And Coral's personal debts — the ones she pretended weren't the whole crew's problem, as if the crew hadn't watched the repo man measure the *Magpie* with his eyes last month — those were a number Tam refused to write down even in a private ledger, because writing them down made them real.

He climbed into the grey morning and found Junie already on deck, coiling lines that didn't need coiling, which was what Junie did when she was worried. Sixteen years old and built out of elbows and stubbornness, hair cropped short against the river damp, hands moving with the restless competence of someone who'd learned boats instead of childhoods.

"Bearing," Tam said.

"I know." She didn't look up. "I can hear it too."

"You shouldn't be able to hear it. That means it's worse than I thought."

"It means I listen." She flaked the coil over the cleat and finally looked at him, and her face did the thing it did when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed. "Tam. If we don't get a contract soon — a real one —"

"I know."

"— then maybe we should think about—"

"Don't finish that either."

She shut her mouth. Downriver, the sky was doing its pale gold trick over the grain elevators, and the smell of the morning market was drifting off the docks — fried dough and diesel and fish guts, the smell Tam had come to think of as home even though home was supposed to be a place with walls.

Coral came up the gangway at half past six with coffee in a dented thermos and an expression Tam had learned to read the way he read gauges. This one said: *bad news, delivered soon.*

"We got a job," she said.

Junie's head came up so fast Tam heard her neck pop. "A real one?"

"Mapping run. Upriver." Coral poured coffee into three tin cups without asking, which was how she did everything. "Government survey team. Hydrographic survey, channel depths, bank erosion, the usual. They want us starting tomorrow."

Tam felt the relief arrive first — warm, immediate, chemical — and then, right behind it, walking on its heels the way trouble always did, came the question. He made himself ask it, because somebody on this boat had to be the one who asked things out loud.

"How much?"

"Triple rate." Coral said it flatly, watching him. "Plus per diem. Plus hazard supplement, which is funny, because it's supposed to be routine."

The relief curdled. Triple rate for routine work was like a doctor charging triple for a checkup. Tam set down his cup. "Who's the client?"

"Papers say Ministry of Water Resources." Coral produced a folder — actual paper, actual stamps, the kind of documentation that usually took weeks to accumulate and arrived looking like it had survived a flood. This looked like it had been born yesterday, pressed and perfect. "Signed authorization, vessel charter agreement, indemnity waiver, cargo manifest. Everything notarized."

"Let me see."

He read it standing up, coffee going cold in his hand, and the longer he read the more the document bothered him, though it took him a while to understand why, because there was nothing wrong with it. That was the problem. Nothing wrong with it at all. Every clause where it should be. Every signature in the right place. The charter rate matched the ministry's published schedule exactly — not approximately, *exactly*, down to the cent, which no real contract ever managed, because real contracts were negotiated by tired humans who rounded numbers and split differences. This one had been generated by a machine that wanted very badly to look ordinary.

And the names. Five surveyors listed: team lead, hydrologist, two technicians, a logistics officer. Tam read them twice. Clean names. Names that sounded like names. But the logistics officer's listed address was a postal annex Tam happened to know had been closed for two years, because Mercer used to complain about it every time his pension checks went astray.

"Coral," he said carefully. "This paperwork is too clean."

"That's what I said."

"And you signed anyway?"

"I signed anyway." She met his eyes, and Tam saw the thing underneath the thing — the debt, the bearing, the nine days of insurance, all of it stacked behind her face like cargo. "We're eleven hundred short on the mooring, Tam. We're fourteen hundred at Calloway's. Junie hasn't been paid in—" 

"I've been paid," Junie said quickly, too quickly.

"You haven't," Tam and Coral said together.

Coral looked back at him. "Triple rate clears everything. The mooring, Calloway's, the insurance, the bearing if we can find a used one. It buys us the season. You tell me what part of that I'm supposed to turn down because the font on page two looks official."

Tam had no answer for that, which was its own kind of answer. He was the one who fixed things on this boat, and the truth he never said out loud was that he could fix anything except money, and money was the one thing broken.

"When do they board?" he asked.

"Eight o'clock tomorrow. They're bringing their own instruments. We provide boat, crew, and discretion." She tapped the folder. "There's a clause about discretion."

"There's always a clause about discretion."

"This one has a number attached to it."

* * *

Mercer arrived at noon, which was early for Mercer, which meant Mercer had heard.

Old boat pilot, sixty-some years of river in his joints, walked with a rolling gait like a man perpetually compensating for swell that wasn't there. He came aboard without asking, the way family does, lowered himself onto the hatch cover, and accepted coffee he immediately set aside untouched — which Tam noted, because Mercer never let coffee go cold.

"Heard you took a government run," Mercer said.

"News travels."

"News walks. Government news runs." Mercer's eyes went to the folder on the chart table, and something moved across his face — quick, gone, like a fish turning under the surface. "Ministry of Water Resources."

"So the stamp says."

"Upriver, is it?" Mercer asked, and his voice was casual in the way a rope goes casual just before it parts. "How far up?"

"Past the bend. Why?"

Mercer was quiet long enough that Junie stopped pretending to work and started listening openly. When he spoke again, his voice had changed registers, dropped into the low even tone he used for river hazards. "You'll want to take the eastern channel above Kiln Bend, not the western. Western's silted since the spring floods, whatever their charts say. And there's a stretch past the old quarry where the current does a thing it doesn't look like it's doing. Water lies, up there. Always has."

"You've run it," Tam said. Not a question.

"I ran everything once." Mercer picked up his coffee after all, drank it cold, grimaced. "Thirty years ago. Different river then. Or same river, different rules." He stood, rolling upright joint by joint, and paused at the gangway with his back to them. "Triple rate, is it?"

"Triple rate plus per diem."

"Money this easy," Mercer said to the water, "is never clean. Somebody told me that once. Took me twenty years to believe them." He stepped onto the dock and rolled away toward the market, and Tam watched him go and thought: *he knows something.* Thought it the way he heard the bearing — faint, high, impossible to unhear.

* * *

The survey team boarded at eight sharp the next morning, and they were wrong in ways Tam spent the whole day cataloguing.

Not obviously wrong. That was the unsettling part. They were five polite, competent-seeming professionals in field clothes with new-looking equipment cases, and they thanked Junie for showing them where to stow gear, and their team lead — a compact, grey-templed woman named Dr. Halloran who shook hands like she'd been trained to do it — complimented the *Magpie*'s upkeep in a way that sounded genuine.

But Tam lived belowdecks among sounds, and by the second hour he'd built himself a list.

Item: their equipment cases were new, but the equipment inside wasn't. He caught a glimpse when Dr. Halloran opened one to check a mounting bracket — sonar units with worn housings, serial plates filed or painted over, the kind of refit you did when you wanted gear that worked but couldn't be traced to an inventory. Government agencies bought new equipment. They bought it loudly, with procurement orders and press releases. They did not quietly refurbish anonymous instruments.

Item: none of them got seasick. Fine, they were professionals. But none of them got *bored*, either, and that was stranger. Real survey crews Tam had ferried spent the flat stretches napping, playing cards, complaining about the food. These five sat upright and watchful for ten straight hours, and twice Tam caught the logistics officer — a big soft-spoken man called Brandt — counting the crew. Not looking at them. Counting them, lips barely moving, the way you counted something you might need a number for later.

Item: Dr. Halloran's hands. She wore a plain steel band on her left ring finger, but the callus pattern on her palms — Tam noticed hands, hands were machinery — was wrong for a desk hydrologist. Rope calluses across the palm heel. Diver's calluses on the fingers. Whatever Dr. Halloran surveyed, she'd done some of it underwater.

Item, and this was the one that settled it: at midday, moored for a sounding station above Kiln Bend, Tam was in the engine room when he heard boots on his deck that didn't belong to his crew. He came up through the hatch quiet and found Brandt and one of the technicians standing at the bow rail, not taking readings, not talking. Just looking upstream. Looking upriver past the mapped channels, past the quarry, past the last marked buoy on any chart Tam owned, toward the grey line of hills where the river narrowed into country nobody local went into, and the technician said, low, "How far do you think it actually goes?" and Brandt said, "Far enough," and then both of them saw Tam and turned around smiling, and the smile was the most rehearsed thing Tam had seen since the contract.

That evening, moored at the edge of the mapped world, Tam found Coral at the stern rail and stood beside her without speaking for a while. The river ran dark and patient under the running lights. Somewhere upstream, a bird cried out once and quit.

"Their sonar's been reflashed," Tam said. "Serials scrubbed. Their lead has diver's hands. Two of them stand at the bow watching upstream like they're waiting for something to come down it. And the contract's a forgery so good it has to be official, because only an institution can afford to fake boring that well." He turned to look at her. "I fix engines, Coral. Engines tell you the truth or they stop. People are harder. But I'm telling you what my ears are telling me: this isn't a mapping run."

Coral didn't argue. That was the worst part. She just watched the black water slide past, and her hand drifted to her jacket pocket where she kept her father's compass, the way she did when she was thinking about him, which lately was more often than she'd admit.

"I know," she said.

"Then why are we still running upstream?"

"Because the bearing's dying, the insurance lapses Thursday, and triple rate is triple rate." She exhaled slowly. "One more day. We're already past Kiln Bend; turning back now costs us the return leg. One more day, and if nothing breaks, we finish the run, we take the money, and we don't ask what the maps were for."

Tam looked upstream, at the dark narrowing water and the hills beyond it swallowing the last light. He thought about Mercer's voice saying *water lies, up there.* He thought about the bearing whining below his feet, honest as a heartbeat, counting down.

"One more day," he agreed, because there was nothing else to agree to.

Three days later, they woke at the survey camp and the tents were still standing, the instruments were still running, and the five clean-named surveyors were gone — no tracks in the mud, no note, no wake on the water, as if the river had simply reached up in the night and taken them.
