## 5. Gear on the Bank, No Tracks

Junie woke to birdsong and knew, before she opened her eyes, that something was wrong.

It wasn't the song itself. The dawn chorus came up off the water the same as it had every morning since they'd left Rivergate — warblers in the willow scrub, a kingfisher rattling down its territory like a dropped chain. It was the *quality* of it. Uninterrupted. Nobody moving through camp, nobody clanking the mess tins, nobody cursing at the propane ring the way Dr. Okafor did every morning without fail, as if the stove had personally wronged him.

Three days of listening to that camp had taught Junie its rhythms the way she'd learned the engine room of the *Maggie's Fault* — by ear, in the dark, before she understood what any of it meant. The surveyors were early risers. Methodical. Reyes was always first up, her headlamp beam sweeping the bank like a lighthouse with somewhere to be. Then Okafor and his war with the stove. Then the others, one by one, until the whole sandbar hummed with the small industrious sounds of people who believed in their work.

This morning there was nothing but birds and the river, sliding past brown and patient.

She lay in her bunk on the boat for a long moment, listening, telling herself she was being silly. People slept in. People had late nights cataloguing whatever it was survey teams catalogue. Maybe they'd moved camp in the dark for some technical reason Junie couldn't imagine and didn't need to.

But the tents were still standing.

She saw them when she came up the companionway into the grey-pink light: five dome tents in their neat row above the high-water line, guy-lines taut, rainflies still on. The instrument tripod stood where it had stood at dusk, the sonic gauge blinking its little green eye at the current. A kettle sat on the cold stove. One of Okafor's boots lay on its side by his tent door, upright partner beside it, as if he'd stepped out of both in a hurry — or been lifted out of them.

"Mercer," Junie said, and her voice came out smaller than she meant it to.

He was already awake. Of course he was; Mercer slept like a cat that had once been a sailor, surfacing at every change in the river's mood. He stood at the rail with his coffee, looking at the camp, and Junie watched him do the thing he did — read water and ground the way she read gauges. His eyes went tent to tent to tripod to boot. Then to the mud.

The mud was what finished it.

There had been rain two nights back, a brief hard squall that had them all battening hatches, and the bank below the camp was still soft with it — a wide apron of chocolate-colored silt, smooth as poured batter, printed everywhere with their own tracks from yesterday, with bird tracks, with the elaborate calligraphy of raccoons who'd raided the rubbish pit overnight. Five people could not walk across that mud without writing themselves into it. Five people could not walk *anywhere* along this stretch of river without leaving a record.

There were no tracks from the camp. Not going to the boats. Not going to the latrine trench. Not going anywhere. The last prints belonged to yesterday evening — Reyes's boots, Okafor's, the younger ones', all crisp-edged in drying silt, walking the ordinary circuits of an ordinary evening. And then nothing. As if the whole team had evaporated between one set of footprints and the next morning.

"They didn't walk out," Mercer said quietly. It wasn't a question. He said it the way he said *water lies* — flat, certain, a man confirming something he'd already suspected and hated.

"Maybe they took the boats—"

"Both skiffs are on the trailer line." He nodded toward them, tied and beaded with dew. "And nobody swam out. Not five of them, not quiet, not in this current."

Junie looked at the tents again. At the kettle. At the boot.

"It's like they got called away," she said, and then wished she hadn't, because her mind immediately supplied the rest: *called away by whom, to where, and why would you leave your boots.*

* * *

By the time Coral and Tam were up, Junie had already walked the perimeter twice, because that was the thing about her — when fear arrived, she didn't freeze, she *tidied*. She checked the tents first, calling out politely at each flap before unzipping it, feeling ridiculous and terrified in equal measure. Sleeping bags rolled or half-rolled. Clothes folded. Reyes's field journal on her cot, weighted open with a rock — not packed, not taken, just left lying open to a page of dense figures and a sketch of the canyon walls upstream, annotated in a quick sure hand. Okafor's spectacles on his mess tin. A paperback novel face-down, spine cracked, holding someone's place forever.

Nobody packs out without their glasses. Nobody leaves a book mid-chapter unless the chapter stops mattering.

"The instruments are still logging," Tam reported, crouched at the tripod with the wary respect he gave all machinery. "Sonic gauge, sediment sampler on a timer, the met station. Everything's running on schedule. Battery banks are full." He straightened, pushing his hair out of his eyes, and Junie saw him doing the same arithmetic she was. "You don't leave a hundred thousand dollars of gear running on a timer if you're evacuating. You pack it. You *especially* pack it if you're government."

"So they meant to come back," Coral said. She stood at the edge of the trampled ground with her arms crossed, and Junie watched her look at the mud for a long time — longer than the rest of them, because Coral looked at things the way a diver looks at water, reading what was underneath. "They meant to come back within minutes. Kettle on. Boots off or half-off. This wasn't a decision, it was an interruption."

"Interrupted by what?" Junie asked.

Nobody answered. The river answered instead, sliding past with that soft continuous voice it had, which had sounded friendly for three days and now sounded like something humming to itself over a secret.

They searched properly after that. Mercer and Tam worked the bank downstream, quartering the mud, checking the strainer of fallen willows fifty yards below camp where anything floating would have hung up. Junie took the upstream side with Coral, and it was there, in the tangle of cane at the top of the bank, that she found the flashlight.

It was a good one — the heavy metal kind surveyors carry, scuffed, its lens starred with a crack. It lay in the cane as if dropped, pointing uphill. Toward the source country. Toward the hills that narrowed the river into that dark throat of water they'd all been pretending not to stare at.

"Coral," she said, and held it up.

Coral turned it over in her hands. Clicked it. Dead. She weighed it, frowning, and Junie saw her thumb find the crack in the lens and rest there.

"Dropped running?" Junie offered.

"Or dropped reaching." Coral looked up the slope, into the green dimness under the canopy, where the ground rose fast toward ridgelines no map in their cabin showed as anything but blank. "Or dropped being *taken*."

That word settled over the camp like weather. Nobody said it again out loud, but Junie carried it around all morning, felt its weight in her pockets alongside the flashlight's batteries, which she'd pocketed too, because you didn't leave useful things lying in a place like this anymore.

* * *

Midmorning, they found the one track.

Tam spotted it — of course Tam did, the man noticed everything mechanical and most things otherwise. Below the high-water line, at the very lip of the river where the silt stayed wet and held a print like wax holds a seal, there was a single impression. Boot-shaped. Larger than any of the surveyors' boots. Deep, as if whoever made it had been carrying weight, or standing a long time in one spot while the river licked at his heels.

One print. Facing the water.

"Not coming or going," Mercer said, crouched over it without touching. His face had gone the color of old rope. "Standing. Somebody stood right there, in the shallows, facing downstream. Looking at the camp." He paused. "Or waiting to be joined."

"And then?" Junie's mouth was dry. "Where's *his* second print?"

Mercer didn't answer. He straightened slowly, one hand pressed to the small of his back the way he did when the old injuries talked, and looked upstream at the narrowing water, at the hills, at the blank space on every map. When he finally spoke, his voice had the sound of a man reading from a page he'd hoped was burned decades ago.

"Water lies," he said. "I told you that. I told you the truth of it, near enough." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I never told you the worst part. The worst part is the water doesn't just lie. Sometimes it *collects*."

Junie looked at the single print, filling slowly with seep-water, blurring, erasing itself even as they watched — the river tidying up after whatever had happened here, the way it tidied everything, patiently, without malice, without memory. Except that somebody, somewhere upstream, clearly remembered. Clearly wanted the river's memory kept clean.

* * *

They argued about it in the cabin, voices low, as if loudness might summon something.

"We go back downriver," Mercer said. "Now. Today. We report it and let it become someone else's problem."

"With what story?" Tam asked. "'Sorry, Commander Whoever-You-Are, we lost five government employees'? You know what happens to the boat crew that loses a survey team? We're suspects. We're the last people who saw them. We'd be lucky to keep the boat."

"We'd be luckier than *this*," Mercer said, jerking his chin at the bank.

Coral hadn't spoken. She stood at the chart table with Reyes's journal — retrieved, with Junie's uneasy blessing, from the abandoned tent — turning pages with two fingers. Junie watched her stop at the sketch of the canyon, at the annotations, at a string of coordinates boxed twice in ink.

"These aren't mapping runs," Coral said quietly. "Look at the sample schedule. Sediment cores every four hours, day and night. Water chemistry at depths no mapping run needs. They weren't charting the river." She looked up. "They were watching it. Something specific. Upstream."

"Then they found it," Junie said, before she could stop herself, "and it collected them."

The silence that followed had texture. Junie thought about the kettle on the cold stove. About the book holding its place. About how three days ago this bank had just been a workplace — mud and instruments and Okafor's war with the stove, ordinary as breathing — and how overnight it had turned into a question mark, a big black curved shape drawn on the world, and they were all standing inside the hook of it.

Outside, the green eye of the sonic gauge blinked on, faithful as ever, logging a river that gave up nothing.

It was Coral who broke the silence, and she didn't decide so much as refuse to decide, which Junie recognized as its own kind of choice.

"We stay anchored here tonight," Coral said. "Nobody goes ashore alone. Tam, get the searchlight wired to the battery spare. If anyone comes down that bank — anyone — I want to know before they know we've seen them." She looked at the tents, at the boot, at the print filling with water. "And tomorrow, if they haven't come back, we go upstream far enough to see what's around the bend. Just to see. Because whatever took them didn't take them downriver."

"How do you know?" Junie asked.

Coral picked up the cracked flashlight and pointed it, dead lens and all, up the slope, into the hills, toward the source country where every map went blank.

"Because everything it left behind points that way."

That night Junie kept the middle watch, wrapped in a blanket in the wheelhouse with the searchlight charged and the river running black past the hull. Around two in the morning, far upstream, where the canyon pinched the stars down to a thread, she heard a sound she couldn't name — not a motor, not an animal, not rockfall. A deep, rhythmic pulse, like something enormous breathing under the water, three slow beats and then gone.

When she woke Coral to tell her, Coral listened to the silence for a long moment and then reached into her sea bag and brought out her father's compass, cupping it in both palms like something wounded.

The needle wasn't pointing north.

It was pointing upstream — steady now, trembling faintly, like a finger raised to its lips.
