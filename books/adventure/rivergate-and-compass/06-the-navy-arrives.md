## 6. The Navy Arrives

They came at dawn, the way trouble always does — not with sirens but with silence, three grey hulls sliding up the Rivergate channel without running lights, as if the river itself had agreed to keep their secret.

Coral was on the *Magpie*'s deck with a mug of burnt coffee when she saw them, and for a long moment she didn't move. She'd grown up learning to read boats the way other children learned to read faces, and these three said everything: low in the water, engines muffled, gunwales scrubbed of any markings that might survive a photograph. Patrol craft built to be forgotten the moment they passed.

"Junie," she said quietly.

Her deckhand was already awake — Junie was always already awake, some internal tide pulling her up before first light — and appeared at the rail with her hair still wet from the dockside pump. "I see 'em." A pause. "That's not customs."

"No."

"That's not fisheries either."

"No."

Below decks, Tam's hammering had stopped. The whole boat seemed to hold its breath with her.

The lead vessel came alongside with a precision that was almost insulting, fenders kissing the *Magpie*'s hull so gently Coral felt it more than heard it. A gangway went down. And then a man climbed aboard her boat as though he'd been invited, which he had not, and which he clearly considered a technicality beneath his attention.

He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, wearing a uniform without visible rank insignia — which told Coral more than insignia would have. Officers who wanted you to know their rank were performing authority. Officers who stripped it off expected you to understand authority was not the point. He carried nothing but a slim leather folder and an expression of patient, professional regret, like a doctor arriving to discuss results.

"Coral Voss," he said. Not a question.

"You're standing on my boat," Coral said. "You've got the advantage."

"Commander Ashe." He didn't offer a hand. He glanced around the deck — at the coiled dive lines, the compressor under its tarp, the patched plating Tam hadn't gotten to yet — and Coral watched him cataloguing it all, filing each detail somewhere behind his eyes. "Is there somewhere we can talk?"

"There's here."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Almost approval. "Here, then." He opened the folder. "Three days ago, a government hydrographic survey team failed to make scheduled contact from a camp sixty kilometers upstream. Four people. Dr. Imani Reyes, team lead. Three technicians." He let that sit. "Your boat is the last registered vessel to pass their position."

"We do a lot of passing," Coral said. "It's a river. Things go by."

"They hired you first, Ms. Voss. For a mapping run. You declined."

"I was booked."

"You weren't booked. Your mooring fees are ninety-one days in arrears and your salvage license is under review." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse — the flat recitation of a man reading someone else's ledger, a man who had done his homework and found it easy because her life was written in red ink anyone could read. "You declined because the paperwork was too clean. That's what your engineer told the harbormaster's clerk, who mentions such things to people who ask."

Coral kept her face still, but inside something cold turned over. They'd been watched. Or listened to. Or both.

"So now you're here," she said.

"Now I'm here." Ashe closed the folder and looked out at the river, at the grey light coming up over the salvage yards, the cranes standing against the sky like gallows that had found honest work. "Four people are missing, Ms. Voss. Possibly drowned. Possibly worse. The official search is proceeding from the north bank with two helicopters and a great deal of enthusiasm." His voice stayed level. "The unofficial search is me, and you, because you know that stretch of water better than any crew on this river, and because my superiors would very much prefer this handled quietly before the enthusiasm finds something it shouldn't."

"Such as?"

Ashe turned back to her. Up close, in the growing light, she could see the fatigue under his composure — real fatigue, weeks of it, worn like ballast. "Such as what they were actually doing up there."

* * *

By the time the sun cleared the yards, the whole crew was gathered in the *Magpie*'s cramped cabin, and Ashe stood wedged under the low beam with the unhappiness of a tall man in a small room, looking like he resented every centimeter of it.

Tam sat with his arms crossed, big hands wrapped around his own forearms as if to keep them from doing something. Junie perched on the chart locker, legs swinging, watching Ashe the way she watched weather. Mercer leaned in the doorway, half in and half out, and Coral noticed he hadn't come all the way inside. Hadn't, she realized, come fully into the conversation at all since the name *navy* entered it.

"The survey team wasn't mapping the channel," Ashe said. "That was cover. They were conducting a wreck survey. A specific wreck." He laid a photograph on the table between them: grainy, aerial, decades old by its palette. A river bend. A dark shape under the water, long and angular, like a coffin dropped by a giant. "A naval transport. Designation withheld. Lost with all hands thirty-two years ago. Officially, it doesn't exist. Unofficially—" he tapped the photo once, "—it does, and four civilians went upriver to find it, and now they're gone."

The cabin was quiet enough that Coral could hear the river slapping the hull.

"Why does a wreck nobody admits exists need finding by a team nobody will admit sent?" Tam asked.

"Because things were aboard it that shouldn't stay aboard it."

"What things?" Junie asked.

Ashe looked at her, and Coral saw him decide — deliberately, visibly — how much truth the moment required. It was like watching a man count out coins. "Documentation," he said. "Records. The kind that complicate careers when they surface. The kind that, in the wrong current, wash downstream." He straightened as much as the beam allowed. "My orders are to recover the team if they're alive and the material regardless. I have helicopters and I have men, and none of them know this river. The upper reaches eat helicopters' fuel margins alive, the channels shift every season, and the locals—" a fractional pause, "—are not inclined to cooperate with uniforms. You are. Or you can be."

"And if we're not?" Mercer said from the doorway. First words he'd offered. His voice came out gravelled.

Ashe didn't look surprised. Coral got the sense very little surprised him. "Then I invoke emergency maritime conscription, Mr.—"

"Mercer."

"—Mr. Mercer, and you search anyway, under my command, unpaid, with your licenses suspended pending review of your cooperation." He let that land, then softened it by exactly one degree, which was its own kind of technique. "Or. You take a contract. Triple rate. Hazard pay. All outstanding mooring debt, docking fees, and license penalties against this vessel settled in full upon completion." His eyes moved to Coral. "All of it, Ms. Voss. Every number in your ledger goes to zero."

There it was. Coral felt it settle over the cabin like fog — the smell of the trap dressed up as a door. Ninety-one days of arrears. Two seasons of bad luck. The compressor rebuild she'd financed against next year's earnings. The number she carried in her chest at night, the one that woke her at three a.m. doing arithmetic against the dark. Gone. Wiped clean by a signature.

She thought about the sealed case sitting in the bilge locker three feet from where she stood, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a sin. The one she'd been paid to ignore. The one whose client hadn't called again, which frightened her more than if he had.

"You knew we dove it," she said.

Ashe regarded her. "I'm sorry?"

"The wreck. You just described it. You knew we'd been near it, or on it, or you wouldn't be standing in my cabin offering to buy my debts." She held his gaze. "So say the rest of it, Commander. Say why you really came."

For a moment — brief, almost invisible — something moved behind Ashe's eyes that wasn't the commander at all. Something tired and human and cornered. Then it was gone, filed away with everything else.

"I came," he said, "because four people are missing, and because you're the best crew on this river, and because I'd rather have you willing than conscripted. Whatever else you think is true, Ms. Voss, those people have been out there for three days without shelter, and the nights upriver are dropping toward freezing." He picked up his folder. "The offer stands until noon. Think about it. Talk it through." At the gangway he paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back at her with that doctor's expression again. "One more thing. The team's last transmission mentioned a compass anomaly. Instruments drifting. Navigation readings that wouldn't settle." His gaze dropped, briefly, unmistakably, to the pocket of her jacket where the brass case sat against her ribs. "Rivers do strange things up there, I'm told. Old iron in the banks. Magnetic ore. Nothing to worry about."

He went down the gangway, and the grey hulls withdrew the way they'd come, silent, and the river closed behind them like water over a stone.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while after the engines faded. The *Magpie* creaked at her lines. Somewhere across the docks a gull complained about the morning.

"He knew about the compass," Junie said finally. "Coral. He *looked* at it."

"He looked at my pocket. He doesn't know what's in it."

"He knows *something's* in it."

"He knows everything," Tam said. He'd unfolded himself from the bench and was pacing the cabin's three usable steps, which was what he did instead of shouting. "Debt figures. Our conversations with the harbormaster. Where we dove. This isn't an officer, this is a man who's been reading our mail for weeks, and now he wants to strap us to the front of whatever's happening upriver so it eats us first." He stopped. "Triple rate. Debt clearance. Coral, money like that isn't payment, it's pricing. It's what you charge when you're not sure the buyer survives."

"It's also ninety-one days of arrears," Coral said quietly.

"It's also a wreck full of dead sailors and a missing team and a commander with no rank on his shoulders!" Tam's voice cracked up an octave and he reeled it back, hands flexing. "We barely made rent in spring. We take this contract, we're diving into somebody else's grave with their enemies holding the rope."

"We don't take it, he takes the boat, the licenses, and we're diving into our own grave." Mercer said it from the doorway, still not entering, and everyone turned. The old pilot's face was unreadable, which was unusual; Mercer's face usually broadcast everything, weather and all. Now it was shut like a locked shed. "Thirty years I've run this water," he went on, "and I'll tell you what I know about the navy coming quiet at dawn. It means somebody upstream is scared. And scared institutions don't hire crews, Coral. They spend them."

"Then we go in with our eyes open," Coral said.

"With our *debts* open," Tam muttered.

She looked around the cabin at them — at Tam with his anger that was really fear wearing work clothes, at Junie watching her with those clear unblinking eyes, at Mercer in his doorway like a man guarding a border only he could see. Her crew. Her family, though she'd never once said the word aloud where any of them could hear it. Broke, loyal, and now conscriptable.

And underneath it all, steady as a second heartbeat, the compass against her ribs, pointing upstream. Pointing, it seemed to her now, the same direction Ashe's whole machinery of coercion was pointing. Toward the bend in the old photograph. Toward the dark shape under the water.

Toward whatever her father had been looking at, in the last months of his life, when he'd started marking charts in pencil and burning others.

"Noon," she said. "We tell him yes at noon. But we go up as a contract crew, not a conscripted one — our boat, our rules, our route. And Mercer runs the helm."

Mercer's jaw worked. For a second she thought he'd refuse. "Aye," he said at last, and left the doorway, and went forward, and did not look at any of them.

Junie slid off the chart locker. "Coral? What's in the case? The one from the dive. Because I've been thinking, and that commander knew too much, and—"

"I know."

"If the survey team was hunting the wreck, and we're being paid to find the survey team, then everybody's looking for the same thing. Everybody except us. We're the only ones who already *have* it."

Coral looked at her — nineteen years old, sharp as a fishhook — and felt the day tilt.

"Yeah," she said. "I've been thinking that too."

Down in the bilge locker, wrapped in oilcloth, the sealed case waited. And above it, through the deck, the river ran north toward the source, carrying its secrets the way it carried everything — patiently, and always downhill, and never, ever, letting go.
