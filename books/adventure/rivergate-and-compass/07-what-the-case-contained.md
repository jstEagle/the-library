## 7. What the Case Contained

The case sat under her bunk like a sleeping animal.

Coral had carried it aboard the *Magpie* three days ago, had felt its weight in her hands as she'd climbed the ladder from the water, and had not once — not once in seventy-two hours — let herself be alone with it. There was always someone on the boat. Mercer at the tiller, Junie scrubbing the deck with a ferocity that suggested she was trying to scour the river itself off the wood, Tam below with the engine apart for the fourth time this month because a busy engine was a quiet mind. Coral had moved through those days like a woman walking around a hole in the floor, and now, past midnight, with the dock lamps burning low and the river muttering against the pilings, she was finally alone.

She'd told Junie she was going to check the mooring lines. Junie had looked at her the way Junie looked at everything — with those wide, unblinking eyes that missed nothing and forgave most things — and said, "Sure, Cap," and gone back to her cards.

Coral knelt on her bunk and dragged the case out into the lamplight.

It was navy gray, dented along one edge, and heavier than it looked. The seal was a strip of lead stamped with an insignia she'd never seen on any official document, which meant it was either very old or very secret, and the way the metal had been crimped — twice, deliberately, by someone with strong hands and no patience — meant it was meant to stay shut. The salvage contract had said *contents incidental, do not inventory, do not open, breach of clause voids payment.*

Payment. The word had teeth. Fourteen thousand owed to Harrow & Sons for the boat's refit, six to the fuel broker, eleven to a man named Dessel who smiled too much and whose patience was measured, Coral suspected, in knuckles. She owed more money than she'd earned in the last three years combined, and the navy's money — dirty, clean, it didn't matter — was the only money that had ever offered itself without a hook in it.

Except it did have a hook in it. She'd known that from the first moment she'd seen the wreck.

*The transport nobody names.* Forty meters down in the silt channel off Kestrel Bend, sitting on the bottom like a held breath. She'd dove it twice before this contract — once as a kid, hanging off her father's tether, and once two years ago, broke and curious, and both times she'd felt the same thing: that the wreck wasn't a wreck so much as a *burial*. Nothing had been stripped. No looters, no scrappers, no navy recovery teams. A transport that size, that intact, in that shallow a stretch of river, should have been gutted decades ago. Instead it sat there, whole, and everyone on the river pretended it wasn't there, the way you pretend not to see a fight happening in the next room.

And this time, when she'd gone down with the cutting torch and the manifest that said *recover one sealed case, aft hold*, she'd found the hold already swept. Clean. Organized. Someone had been down there after the sinking and taken everything — except the one thing they'd left behind, tucked into a rack bolted to the bulkhead, as if it had been placed rather than lost.

Placed. She'd thought about that word a lot in three days.

Coral set the case on the deck plating and worked the lead seal with a flathead screwdriver and her thumbnail. The metal gave with a small, obscene *tick*, and then the latches, and then the lid, and the smell came up out of it — old paper, machine oil, and something faintly chemical, like the inside of a camera.

She'd expected documents. Manifests, orders, maybe a pay ledger. She'd braced herself for the ordinary corruption of any large institution: money moved, names redacted.

What she found was a map.

No — charts. A sheaf of hydrographic charts, hand-inked on linen paper gone amber at the edges, the kind of survey work that took months and cost lives. The main sheet showed the Rivergate stretch she'd known her whole life, every shoal and channel marked with the confident precision of someone who'd sounded every meter of it. But the river didn't end where the charts in the public survey office ended. These continued. Up past the falls at Harrow's Teeth, past the old mining claims that had been abandoned before she was born, into a tributary that appeared on no map she had ever seen — a blue thread threading north and east into canyon country, drawn in a hand that had grown shakier as it went, as if the surveyor were running out of time or nerve or both.

The tributary had a name. Someone had inked it in the corner in small, careful letters, and then someone else had crossed it out — once, twice, three times, pressing so hard the linen had nearly torn — and written instead, in a bureaucratic hand: *UNNAMED. DO NOT SURVEY. DO NOT CHART. DO NOT ENTER.*

Three do-nots. Coral had grown up on the river, and she knew that when an institution told you three times not to do a thing, the thing was the whole point.

She turned the sheets over, one by one. Depth soundings in a tributary so narrow the walls would close over a diver like a hand. Flow rates, dated across four different years. And on the last sheet, a cross-section of something that made her go still — a cavern system, sketched in pencil, the river rising out of it like a vein out of a heart, and around the vein, drawn in red ink that had faded to rust: containment structures. Walls. Grates. A grid.

Someone had built a cage around the source of the river.

She sat back on her heels. The dock lamp hissed. Out on the water, a night heron cried once and got no answer.

*Do not open.* She'd opened it. *Do not inventory.* She was inventorying. And the strange thing, she thought, the thing she'd examine later when her hands stopped shaking, was that she didn't feel like a thief. She felt like a daughter going through a desk drawer she'd always been told was locked for her own good.

Because — and here was the vertigo, here was the floor tilting — the hand that had inked the main chart was familiar.

She knew it from the margins of her childhood. From tide tables annotated over breakfast. From the inside cover of every one of her father's dive logs, where he'd printed depths and times in that same upright, slightly left-leaning script, the *R*s with their kicked-out legs, the numbers with the little serif on the seven that everyone had teased him about. Her father, Elias Villalobos — no. Not Villalobos. Her father, Elias Voss, who had died in a dive accident on the upper river when Coral was nineteen, who had been officially recorded as having suffered a regulator failure at a depth of eleven meters in open water, which every diver on the Rivergate had privately agreed was nonsense, because Elias Voss had maintained his regulators the way priests maintained altars.

His hand was on the chart. Her dead father's hand had drawn the river that didn't exist.

Coral's mouth had gone dry. She set the charts down with exaggerated care, the way you set down a sleeping child, and that was when she saw the photograph.

It had been tucked into the back of the chart sheaf, inside a fold of oilcloth, and it was a proper photograph — the old kind, printed on paper with a tooth to it, gone soft at the corners. Eight people standing on the deck of a workboat she recognized down to the rust streaks on its gunwale, because the workboat was the *Magpie* — or the *Magpie's* previous hull, refitted, repainted, but the same boat, the same boat her father had left her, the same boat she was sitting in right now.

Eight people. Her father stood at the center, younger than she had ever known him, thirty maybe, lean and sunburned, grinning like a man with a secret he couldn't wait to spend. And around him, seven others. She recognized two on sight: a much-younger Mercer, dark-haired and already heavy through the shoulders, standing at the tiller with the particular set of his jaw that meant he was the one responsible for whatever came next. And beside him, a woman — tall, sharp-faced, holding a water sampling kit the way other people held weapons.

The other five she didn't know. But they were all wearing the same thing: navy dive tags on lanyards around their necks, and the tags were stamped with an insignia she'd just seen an hour ago, crimped into the lead seal of the case.

Her father's dive crew. Her father's *navy* dive crew. Standing on her boat, in front of the river that didn't exist, and someone had put this photograph in a sealed case and sunk it in a wreck that everyone was paid not to name, and the case had been left in a swept hold like a message in a bottle, like a thing placed rather than lost.

*Placed for whom?*

Coral turned the photograph over. On the back, in her father's hand — that upright, left-leaning script, the serifed sevens — was a single line:

*If you're reading this, the river finally gave it back.*

She read it four times. The words did not change. The lamp hissed; the river muttered; somewhere down the dock a mooring line creaked like a joint.

Her father had been in the navy. Her father had surveyed the forbidden tributary. Her father had stood in front of a caged source with seven other divers, three of whom — she counted the faces again, counted, counted — three of whom were not in any photograph she'd ever found in his things, not in the house, not in the boathouse, not in the shoebox of his life she'd gone through a hundred times in the nineteen years since the river took him. Five remained, and she knew only two, and her father had died in eleven meters of open water with a regulator that could not have failed, and the navy had paid her — *her*, his daughter, his only family — to dive a wreck and recover a case and not look inside, and the case contained the one thing in the world that could tell her why.

Betrayal was not the right word, she thought. Betrayal was what happened between people who knew each other. This was something older and colder: the discovery that the shape of your whole life — the orphaned daughter, the drowned father, the debts, the boat, the river you'd never left because leaving it felt like abandoning him — that all of it had been arranged by hands you'd never seen, that your grief had a floor plan, and the floor plan had been drawn by the same institution that stamped the seal she'd just broken.

She was still holding the photograph when the deck creaked.

Coral's whole body went to stone. The footsteps on the dock were slow and heavy and she knew them the way she knew the tide — Mercer, coming back from wherever he went on the nights he couldn't sleep, which was most nights, which she had never asked about because some doors you don't knock on until they open themselves.

She had perhaps forty seconds. The case open on the deck. The charts fanned out. The photograph in her hand with her father's handwriting facing the ceiling.

She looked at the case. She looked at the photograph. And she understood, with the cold clarity of a diver watching her gauge needle drop, that Mercer had been on that boat in the photograph. That Mercer had known. That whatever her father had died for, Mercer had walked away from it and spent thirty years piloting her around the river's safe stretches and never once — not once — said *your father drew that chart, Coral, your father stood where you're standing*.

The footsteps stopped at the gangway.

Coral closed the case. She swept the charts inside, dropped the photograph in last, threw the latches, and slid the whole thing back under her bunk just as Mercer's shadow filled the companionway — a big, blocky shadow, smelling of river air and the one whiskey he allowed himself at the dockside bar, a man who had carried her on his shoulders at her father's funeral and had never, in nineteen years, given her a single reason to doubt him, which now felt less like loyalty and more like the most elaborate patience she had ever witnessed.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he said, from the top of the companionway.

"No," Coral said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "Checking the mooring lines."

Mercer looked at her for a long moment. The dock lamp behind him put his face in shadow, but she could feel his eyes, the way you feel a depth change in your ears before your gauge confirms it. He knew. She'd have bet the boat — the boat in the photograph, the boat under her feet — that he knew, that he'd known since the day she'd hauled the case aboard, that maybe he'd been waiting three days for her to open it, or maybe he'd been waiting thirty years for someone to.

"Lines look fine," he said finally. "Get some sleep, Cap. Long run tomorrow."

"We're not scheduled for a run tomorrow."

"No," Mercer said, and there was something in his voice she'd never heard before, something old and tired and braced, like a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for three decades. "We're not. But there's a survey team came into the office this afternoon asking after a boat that knows the upper river. Triple rate. Clean paperwork." He turned to go, then stopped, one hand on the companionway frame, and said, without looking back:

"Your father used to say the river gives back what it takes. Eventually. Whether you want it or not."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the dock, and Coral sat alone in the lamplight with a sealed case under her bunk that was no longer sealed, a photograph of a crew that included her father and her pilot and five strangers, and the growing, vertiginous certainty that the survey team with the clean paperwork and the triple rate was not coming to map the river at all.

They were coming for the same thing her father had died holding.
