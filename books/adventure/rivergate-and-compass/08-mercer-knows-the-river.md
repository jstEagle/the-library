## 8. Mercer Knows the River

Mercer had not slept in the boat's cabin since the night the survey team vanished, and he told himself it was because his back was too old for the narrow bunk. It was a lie he'd been telling for thirty years, in one form or another. The truth was that sleep had a way of opening doors he'd nailed shut, and behind those doors there was always water — black water, moving fast, and two men's faces looking up at him through it.

He sat on the stern bench with a mug of coffee gone cold an hour ago, watching the river do what it always did: carry things away and pretend it hadn't. The *Rivergate and Compass* rode at anchor in a slough off the main channel, her hull ticking as the current tugged at her lines. Upstream, somewhere past the last charted bend, five government surveyors had walked out of their own camp without leaving footprints, and a naval officer with a soft voice and hard eyes had turned Coral's debt into a leash.

Mercer knew why they'd vanished. He'd known from the moment Ashe said the words *upper reaches* and watched everyone's face but Mercer's.

Because Mercer had been there before.

* * *

The others were asleep below — Tam snoring like a failing generator, Junie curled in the wheelhouse with a paperback open on her chest, Coral in the aft cabin with the door cracked so she could hear the river. She always slept with the door cracked. Her father had done the same thing; Mercer remembered that from the first week he'd taken her out, a girl of nine with salt-crusted braids, lying awake in the old man's boat listening to the current like it was reading her a story.

That was the trouble with Coral Voss. She was her father's daughter down to the marrow, and her father had died asking questions that Mercer had spent three decades refusing to answer.

The compass was the reason he couldn't hold his tongue any longer.

He'd seen it on the chart table that evening — the old brass thing with the dented case, the needle swinging lazy and wrong, dragging itself toward the upper river no matter how Coral held it. He'd seen her frown at it, tap the glass, mutter about magnetized housing. And he'd felt something in his chest turn over like an engine flooding, because he knew that compass wasn't broken. It had never been broken. It had been *adjusted*, back when there were men whose job was adjusting such things, and it pointed where it pointed because it had been made to point there — made to lead divers to a place the navy swore didn't exist.

Coral thought it was grief playing tricks. Mercer knew better, and every day he kept quiet, the lie got heavier, and the river got closer, and the odds got worse that he'd be able to live with what happened next.

So when her bare feet padded up onto the deck near midnight — he heard her before he saw her, the way you hear family — Mercer set down the cold coffee and said, before she could speak:

"I ran supply boats to the source."

* * *

She stopped halfway across the deck. The moon was half-full and low, laying a silver seam along the water, and in that light her face did something complicated — surprise, then suspicion, then a kind of terrible patience, like she'd been waiting years for him to say it and had promised herself she wouldn't ask.

"Sit down," Mercer said. "It's a long story, and my knees don't like standing through long stories anymore."

She sat at the far end of the bench. Not close. He deserved that.

"Thirty-one years ago," he began, "I was twenty-six, and I thought I knew everything worth knowing about this river. I'd piloted supply runs since I was fifteen. Fuel drums, chain, canned peaches, dynamite — you name it, I hauled it up and down the Rivergate for whoever paid. And then a man came to the dock with money that smelled different."

"What do you mean, smelled different?"

"Clean." Mercer laughed, and it came out bitter. "River money's dirty money, Coral. It's greasy, it's wet, it's short-counted by men missing fingers. This was crisp. Bundled. A navy paymaster's allotment, though I didn't know that word yet. The man wore civilian clothes but stood like a fence post, and he offered me triple rate to run cargo up past the falls country. No manifests. No questions. Deliver to a marked bank, take on nothing, tell nobody."

"And you said yes."

"I said yes before he finished the sentence." Mercer looked at his hands. They were wrecked hands, rope-scarred and knuckle-swollen, honest hands for dishonest work. "You have to understand what I was, back then. I wasn't loyal to anything but the next payday. Your daddy used to say I had a compass in my chest that only pointed at money. He meant it as a joke. Mostly."

Coral flinched at the name — her father's name, which Mercer still carried carefully, like a full cup. Elias Voss. Diver. The best the river ever produced, and the stubbornest.

"First season, it was easy," Mercer went on. "Concrete in bags. Steel plate. Pumps, generators, hose. I told myself it was a mining operation — the canyon country upstream had played out everywhere else, but there was always some fool willing to dig deeper. Then they started bringing in dive gear. Recompression chamber, the big kind, the kind you bolt to a barge. And divers. Quiet men. Navy haircuts growing out. They'd come aboard my boat at the transfer bank and ride upriver staring at the water like it owed them something."

"The source," Coral said quietly. "They were diving the source."

"They were diving *into* it." Mercer's voice dropped, though there was no one awake to hear. "You've seen where the river rises — nobody has, that's the point of it. The maps go blank above the narrows. But the water comes out of that canyon cold and steady year-round, even in drought, even in fire season. Cold and steady means deep, and deep means caverns. Somebody in the government figured that out a long time before I did, and they wanted what was down there. Minerals, maybe. Or just to know. Doesn't matter now. What matters is they needed boats, and I had one, and I didn't ask."

"You must have asked eventually."

Mercer was silent a while. A night bird called from the willows, one note, twice, and the river answered with its endless low voice.

"Third season," he said finally. "Two divers went down and didn't come up."

* * *

He told it the way he'd rehearsed telling it a thousand times and never once told it — flat, chronological, holding the details at arm's length like hot iron.

Their names had been Okonkwo and Brandt. First names lost to him now, which was its own indictment. Okonkwo was the senior diver, a broad, careful man who checked his gauges twice and laughed easily. Brandt was young, all elbows and ambition, the kind who volunteered for the deep runs because the deep runs paid a bonus. They went into the source caverns on a Tuesday morning in October, on a line-and-bell operation run off a converted landing craft, and the plan was simple: extend the guide line through the second constriction, survey the gallery beyond, surface by fourteen hundred hours.

At eleven-twenty, the topside crew lost communication. At eleven-forty, the guide line went slack — cut or parted, nobody ever established which. At noon, with the bell crew frantic and the site supervisor screaming into a radio that had no business working that far upriver, the order came down from somewhere above the supervisor: seal the site. Pull the equipment. Nobody goes in after them.

"And they listened," Coral said. It wasn't a question. Her hands were fists in her lap.

"The supervisor listened. I listened." Mercer's throat worked. "I was tied up fifty yards downstream, watching the water where they'd gone under, and a lieutenant I'd never seen before told me to get my boat ready to load, and I — I got my boat ready to load. That's what I did, Coral. Two men were drowning in the dark a hundred meters under my keel, and I coiled my mooring lines."

"Someone could have—"

"No." He said it hard enough that it surprised them both. "No one could have. That's the lie they fed us and the lie I swallowed, because swallowing it was easier than drowning beside them. Maybe the caverns were unsurvivable. Maybe the line was cut on purpose. I don't know. I've had thirty years to wonder and I still don't know, and that not-knowing is the punishment. But here's what I do know: nobody tried. The whole apparatus — the navy, the contractors, the supervisors, me — we all decided those two men were cheaper dead than rescued, and quieter dead than talked about. There was a widow in Rivergate within the month, and a mother upstate, and both of them got letters saying their boys were lost in a training exercise off the coast. Off the *coast*. A hundred miles from any ocean."

The moon had climbed. The silver seam on the water had widened into a road, running upstream, straight as if laid by hand, toward the place Mercer had spent his whole life not looking at.

"After that," he said, "they sat us all down in a tent. Eleven of us — boatmen, riggers, a cook. A man in a suit explained our obligations under statutes I'd never heard of. He wasn't threatening, exactly. He was worse than threatening. He was reasonable. He explained that the operation was vital, that the deaths were tragic, that disclosure would endanger the project and, by extension, the region. Then he shook each of our hands, and we each took an envelope, and we each swore silence. And here's the part that shames me most, Coral — not the swearing. The relief. I was *relieved*. Somebody had finally told me what to do with the thing sitting on my chest, and the thing was: nothing. Say nothing. Go home. Get paid."

"So you came back to Rivergate and piloted tourists."

"Cargo, mostly. Tourists when cargo was thin." He managed something like a smile. "And I told myself the story all good cowards tell: it was bigger than me, it was already done, my silence changed nothing. And maybe that's true. Maybe my silence changed nothing. But your father never believed that."

Coral's head came up.

"Elias figured it out," Mercer said. "Took him years, and he never had the pieces I had, but he was smarter than all of us put together. He noticed the same things I'd noticed — the cold water, the blank maps, the navy barges that filed false float plans. He started diving wrecks along the upper channel, pulling records, asking questions in that polite bulldozer way of his. And I watched him do it, and I said nothing, because helping him meant breaking my oath and stopping him meant breaking something else, so I did what I do best. I steered the middle of the channel and kept my mouth shut."

"He died up there."

"He died up there." Mercer's voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "Officially, a regulator failure on a routine salvage. I've had thirty years to turn that over too. I don't believe it. I can't prove otherwise, and I won't insult you by pretending certainty. But I'll tell you what I can prove." He reached into his coat, slow, and set something on the bench between them: a photograph, creased white with folding, older than Coral. "This came inside my last pay envelope. No note. I've kept it hidden ever since, and I've hated myself for keeping it, and I've hated myself more for being too afraid to give it to you."

Coral picked it up. In the moonlight she had to hold it close, and when she understood what she was looking at, Mercer watched her whole body change — watched the breath go out of her and the stillness come in, the diver's stillness, the kind that precedes descent.

It was a crew photo. Eight men and one woman on the deck of a navy utility boat, squinting into sun, arms slung over shoulders. Third from the left, young and grinning with his arm around a much younger Mercer: Elias Voss. And at the far right, barely more than a boy, jaw set like he was already bracing for the world to disappoint him — a face Coral would know from briefings and newsprint and the gunmetal eyes that had conscripted her boat three days ago.

"Ashe," she whispered.

"Commander Ashe," Mercer said. "Your father's dive partner, once. Before either of them knew what the source was for." He looked out at the water, at the road of moonlight running upstream. "I don't know what happened between those two. I don't know if Ashe sold out or was broken or is still, in whatever's left of his soul, trying to finish what your daddy started. But I know this: I kept my oath for thirty years, and it bought two widows a lie, and it bought your father an unmarked grave in a navy file, and it bought me a bunk I can't sleep in. I'm done steering the middle of the channel, girl. Whatever you decide to do with what's in that sealed case — whatever's waiting upriver — I'm done pretending I don't know the way."

Coral was quiet for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough but level, and she didn't look at him — she looked upstream, at the dark water, the way her father used to.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "you're going to show me exactly how far upriver you can take us before the charts run out."

Above the narrows, faint as a memory, thunder rolled — though there wasn't a cloud in the sky, and the dry season had not broken in forty days.
