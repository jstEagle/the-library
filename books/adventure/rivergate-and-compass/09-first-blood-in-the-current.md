## 9. First Blood in the Current

Tam Okafor had never trusted a night river, and the Kestrel Reach after midnight was the river at its most dishonest. By day the water told you what it meant — a boil here, a seam of slack current there, the pale grammar of rocks showing through the shallows. At night it lied. It flattened into a single black ribbon, and every sound became something else: wind in the cottonwoods sounded like motors, motors sounded like wind, and the slap of a fish against the hull sounded, once, exactly like a hand grabbing the gunwale.

He sat in the engine bay of the *Magpie* with a headlamp off and a screwdriver in his hand he had no use for, listening to the raw-water pump the way other men listened to music. The pump had a rhythm. *Chk-chk-hiss. Chk-chk-hiss.* As long as it kept that rhythm, the world was more or less intact.

Three days out from the drowned camp. Three days since Junie had stood on the muddy bank with her arms wrapped around herself and said, in the small voice she only used when she was badly frightened, *there's nothing out here but us.* Three days since Commander Ashe's launch had peeled away downriver with its running lights doused, taking the crew's paperwork, their contract, and — Tam was increasingly certain — any pretense that any of this was legal.

Coral was at the wheel. He could hear her through the bulkhead, the particular way she stood a helm at night: weight on the balls of her feet, small corrections, never big ones. Mercer was up in the bow on watch, a shape against the stars, his old .303 across his knees like a sleeping cat. Junie was asleep in the pilothouse chair, folded up small, one hand still holding the thermos.

Tam checked the pump rhythm again. *Chk-chk-hiss.*

Then it changed.

It wasn't the pump. It was underneath the pump, underneath the engine, underneath the river itself — a low, broad hum, the sound of a displacement hull pushing water at speed, coming up from downstream. From behind them.

He was moving before he finished deciding to move, out of the engine bay and up the ladder and into the pilothouse, and the words came out of him flat and fast, the engineer's version of a scream: "Something's coming up the channel. Big. Fast. No lights."

Coral didn't ask how he knew. She'd hired him eight years ago because he could hear a bearing going bad from across a dock, and she'd never once wasted time doubting the ear. "Kill the cabin lights," she said. "Junie. Junie, wake up, I need you on the searchlight but *not* on it — hand on the switch, nothing else."

Junie came up out of the chair like she'd never been asleep at all. That was the thing about Junie that Tam had stopped finding surprising and started finding quietly terrifying: the girl slept like a stone and woke like a struck match.

"Mercer," Coral called down the deck. "Company."

The old man didn't turn around. "Heard it two minutes ago," he said. "Thought you might want to sleep through it."

"Which side?"

"Starboard quarter. Hugging the far bank, using the bluff for cover." Mercer finally turned, and even in the dark Tam could see the set of his jaw. "That's a navy approach. That's how *we* used to do it."

The hum became a sound, and the sound became an engine, and then there it was — a long black shape sliding out from the shadow of the bluff, maybe four hundred meters back and closing, a patrol skiff riding low and mean, its bow throwing a wedge of phosphorescent wake that was the only part of it visible. No running lights. No hail. No searchlight sweeping them, no voice on the radio asking them to heave to.

That was the part that made Tam's stomach drop through the bottom of the boat. You could talk your way out of a hail. There was no version of this you could talk your way out of.

"Coral," he said. "They're not stopping us. They're stopping *us*."

"I know what a ramming course looks like, Tam."

"Sorry. Sorry. I just —"

"Go be sorry at the engine. Give me everything she has and then give me the thing past everything she has."

He went down the ladder two rungs at a time.

* * *

The *Magpie* was forty-one feet of river salvage boat that Tam had rebuilt twice and rebuilt a third time in his head, every night, the way a monk says prayers. He knew her the way you know a body you've repaired: the soft place below the port fuel line, the weld on the transom knee that Mercer had done drunk and that Tam had never had the heart to grind out because it held. She was not a fast boat. She was a strong boat, which was a different religion.

He opened the fuel rack to its stop, cracked the waste gate, and did the thing he'd sworn he'd never do outside of a fire — pulled the emergency governor override, the one that let the old Detroit scream past every red line on every gauge he'd installed himself. The engine note climbed from a growl into something like a howl, and the deck plates began to buzz against his boots, and above him he heard Coral put the wheel over and the *Magpie* lean into the current like a dog taking up slack in a leash.

"Come on, old girl," he whispered, both hands flat on the engine block, feeling the heat of her through his palms. "Not tonight. Not for these bastards. Tonight you're a young boat."

The skiff was two hundred meters back. Then a hundred and fifty. It was faster — of course it was faster, it was a military hull with a matched pair of diesels and probably forty years less rust — but the *Magpie* knew the channel and the skiff didn't, and Coral was threading the reach with a confidence that Tam recognized and feared: she was driving from memory of the *day* channel, from the survey charts, from the mapping run they'd done with the vanished team, and the river at night was not the river by day, and every one of those assumptions was a debt the river intended to collect.

The first impact, when it came, wasn't an impact at all. It was a *swash* — the skiff's bow wave reaching them first, a rolling wall of water that hit the *Magpie*'s quarter and slewed her ten degrees off line. Tam heard Coral's boot heel skid on the wheel deck, heard her correct, heard Mercer say something low and filthy in river Spanish.

Then the skiff's bow came out of the dark like a door swinging open.

"STARBOARD—" Tam got half the word out.

The collision was not the movie version. It wasn't a crash. It was a grinding, shuddering *argument* — the skiff's bow shoulder hitting the *Magpie*'s quarter, steel finding steel, the whole boat lurching sideways with a sound like a cathedral being dragged. Tam went down on one knee in the engine bay and caught the fuel rack with his hand before the vibration could back it off. Above him, Junie was screaming something, and the searchlight came on by accident and strobed across the water — one white flash of the other boat, and Tam saw, for half a second, faces. Helmets. Young faces. Men doing a job.

Then the searchlight died, Junie killed it, and it was dark again, and the dark was better.

"They're coming around!" Mercer bellowed. "Coral, they're going to hit us again, they're going to put us into the bank!"

"Then we stop being in the channel," Coral shouted back, and there was something in her voice Tam had heard exactly twice in eight years — once running a storm out of the Delta, once the day they'd pulled a body out of a wreck and she'd refused to let go of the line. It wasn't fear. It was the thing on the far side of fear, the flat calm place where she did arithmetic with people's lives and always included her own in the sum. "Tam! How much more does she have?"

"She has everything I have," Tam yelled back. "What she doesn't have is a hull that likes rocks!"

"Neither do they." And then, louder, to the whole boat, in the voice she used for orders that were also confessions: "Hang on. We're going off the chart."

* * *

Off the chart meant the north side of the reach, where the survey maps — the clean, triple-rate, too-good paperwork maps — simply stopped drawing the river and started drawing blank space and a dotted line marked *RAPIDS, EXTENT UNKNOWN, NOT SURVEYED.*

It meant the water that made the Kestrel Reach a place the navy officially denied existing.

The *Magpie* crossed the channel seam with a shudder, and the river changed under her the way a sleeping animal changes when it wakes. The current took the hull and *pulled*, a long muscular grab that spun the bow downstream, and the sound of the water went from river-sound to something with syllables in it, something with intent. Ahead — not far ahead, two hundred meters, maybe less — the black ribbon of the river simply ended, and where it ended there was a line of pale foam, and beyond the foam there was nothing visible at all except a lower darkness that was somehow louder.

"They'll never follow us in there," Junie said. Her voice shook, but her hands didn't; she'd gotten the bow floodlight on and was sweeping it across the drop ahead, and what it showed was white water stacked in three long steps, each step worse than the last, and a channel that split around a rock island and came back together in a chute the width of a truck.

"They'll follow us anywhere Ashe tells them to," Mercer said. He'd come aft off the bow, and he stood at the pilothouse door with the .303 slung now, both hands on the frame, reading the water with an expression Tam had never seen on him. It took him a moment to name it. It was *recognition.* "Left of the island. There's a tongue — you can't see it, but it's there, the water tells you. Smooth water means deep water. Coral. Left of the island. Trust the smooth."

"Left of the island," Coral repeated. "Mercer, have you run this?"

"No." A pause, long enough to be an entire confession. "But I've run water like it."

The skiff came around behind them, its engines flaring, and for one long moment it hung there at the top of the channel, and Tam understood — with the strange clarity of a man watching his own life narrow to a single decision made by someone else — that the boat behind them was choosing too. Choosing to follow four civilians into unsurveyed rapids in the dark, because a commander's voice on a radio had told it to.

Then the *Magpie* hit the first step and the world went white.

* * *

There was no running rapids. That was the lie everyone believed. You didn't run rapids; you were *run* — picked up by the river and carried through a gauntlet of rock while you made perhaps six decisions, of which four were wrong and one was irrelevant and one, if the river was kind, was the one that mattered.

Tam rode it out in the engine bay with his arms wrapped around the block and his shoulder braced against the bulkhead, and he did the only useful thing a man could do down there, which was listen. The first step hit them like a dropped building. Water came over the transom in a cold black fist and found every seam he'd ever caulked. Something in the lazarette broke loose and traveled the length of the boat with a shriek. The engine screamed at a pitch he'd never let it reach sober, and he screamed back at it, wordless, an argument between old friends.

"RIGHT — RIGHT — *LEFT* —" Coral's voice from above, ragged, the wheel fighting her, the *Magpie* slewing through the chute with the rock island's black teeth sliding past close enough to touch. Mercer was shouting depths and tongues and seams in a language that was half English and half thirty years of river, and Junie was bailing — actually bailing, a bucket appearing from nowhere, the girl throwing water over the side in the middle of a rapid because someone had to and because Junie had never once in her life waited to be told.

The second step was worse. The boat went nearly vertical — Tam felt the propeller actually *cavitate*, felt the engine's load vanish for one horrible weightless second, the *Magpie* airborne in the dark — and then the river caught them again and slammed them down and the world was foam and roar and the searchlight beam cartwheeling across the sky.

And through all of it, the thing Tam would carry for the rest of his life: nobody panicked. Coral drove. Mercer read. Junie bailed. And Tam — Tam kept the engine alive, one hand on the fuel rack, one on the throttle linkage, holding the governor override against the vibration with his thumb like a man holding shut a door with a storm behind it, and he realized, somewhere in the third step, in the long grinding roar of the final chute, that he was not afraid anymore. Not because the danger had passed. Because it hadn't, and wouldn't, and the four of them were inside it together, and there was no version of the next sixty seconds that did not include all four of them, and some animal part of his brain had looked at that fact and *settled*.

Fear, it turned out, was mostly the fear of being alone in it.

The *Magpie* came out of the chute sideways, half-swamped, spinning in the long dark pool below the rapids, and the silence that followed was so total that Tam could hear the water dripping off the pilothouse roof. The engine was still running. He checked. Still running. Rough, hot, wounded — he'd pay for tonight in bearings and gaskets for a month — but running.

Above him, Coral's voice came down through the deck, small and hoarse and shaking now that it could afford to shake:

"Tam. Report."

"She's alive," he called back. "Barely. Like the rest of us."

* * *

They drifted the pool in the dark and bailed her out by bucket and by hand, and nobody spoke for a long time. The skiff had not followed. Tam kept listening for it — the engineer in him unable to stop listening — but there was nothing behind them but the roar of the rapids, which was now not a threat but a wall, a door slammed between them and everything downstream. Everything downstream. Ashe. The contract. The debts. The whole legal, charted, lit world.

Junie was the first to laugh. It started as a shiver and came out the other side as a laugh, high and slightly hysterical, and then Mercer laughed too, a great rolling bark, and then Tam found he was laughing as well, sitting in six inches of river water on the engine room deck, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the fact of being alive.

Coral didn't laugh. She stood at the wheel with the floodlight off and the stars coming out over the canyon rim, and Tam watched her turn her father's compass over in her hand — she'd taken to carrying it on the helm — and watched her frown at it, and tap it, and hold it flat and turn in a slow circle.

"Coral?"

"Tam." Her voice had gone very quiet. "How long have we been running downstream?"

"Since the camp. Three days. Why?"

She held the compass out to him without looking away from it. The needle didn't waver. It didn't point north. It pointed upriver — dead upriver, straight back the way they'd come, straight toward the rapids and the blank space on the chart and the forbidden source — steady as a nail.

"Compasses don't do that," Tam said.

"I know," Coral said. "My father's does."

Behind them, far off through the canyon dark, so faint it might have been the river, Tam heard it anyway: engines. More than one. Coming around the long way — or already ahead of them.

Mercer heard it too. He'd gone still at the pilothouse door, head cocked, and when he spoke his voice had thirty years of guilt in it and no laughter at all.

"That's not a patrol boat," he said. "That's two. And that's not behind us."
