## 10. The Village at the Bend

Junie smelled woodsmoke before she saw the village.

She was up in the bow with the sounding pole, reading the shallows the way Mercer had taught her — watch the water's color, not its surface; brown means sandbar, green means snag, and black means deep enough to drown a horse. The river had been running low and mean all day, dropping like a held breath being let out slowly, and her arms ached from pushing off snags. Then the smoke came threading down through the cypress on the left bank, sweet and resinous, and beneath it something else: fish roasting over coals, and under that, faintly, the mineral smell of wet stone that Junie had been noticing more and more as they pushed upstream. Like the river itself had a body and they were getting close to its lungs.

"Smoke," she said, and then, louder, over the engine's mutter: "Mercer. Smoke, left bank."

Mercer didn't answer right away. He stood at the wheel with his hands loose on the spokes, and Junie watched him go still in a way that made her stomach tighten. In three weeks on this river she'd learned his silences the way she'd learned Coral's tempers and Tam's humming — each one meant something different. This one meant *trouble I remember*.

"I know," he said finally. "I see it."

"You know it?"

"I know *of* it." He eased the throttle back until the engine dropped to a low idle. "Bend Village. Wasn't much of anything when I ran supply thirty years ago. A dozen houses, a drying rack, a woman who sold the best smoked catfish between here and the canyon mouth." His jaw worked. "It wasn't on any chart then either."

"It's not on ours now," Tam said from the engine well, where he'd been nursing their overheating impeller for two days with wire, epoxy, and profanity. "I checked. This whole stretch is blank paper. Whoever drew our charts just... stopped drawing."

"That's because whoever drew them was paid to stop," Coral said.

She came forward from midships where she'd been lying under the tarp with the case, and Junie watched her take in the smoke, the bend, the dark line of houses emerging now through the trees — stilt houses, raised high above the bank on pilings gray as old bone, connected by walkways that swayed in no wind at all. Behind them, terraced gardens climbed the slope in green steps. A drying rack stood at the waterline hung with fish that flashed silver when the light moved. And people. Maybe forty of them, standing along the bank and the near walkway, watching the boat come around the bend with an expression Junie couldn't name at first.

Then she could. It was the way you watched weather come.

"They don't look surprised to see us," Tam said quietly.

"No," Coral said. "They look like they've been waiting."

* * *

Mercer brought them in slow, hands visible on the wheel, and cut the engine fifty yards off the bank so the last approach was drift and pole-work, quiet, unthreatening — or trying to be. Junie poled them the rest of the way in, feeling every eye on her back like sunburn. The bottom here was gravel and clean sand, no silt, and the water over it was so clear she could count the stones. She'd never seen water like that in the lower river. Downstream the Rivergate ran the color of strong tea, carrying the silt of a hundred miles of farms and logging cuts and one very large secret. Up here the river was young. It hadn't learned to hide things yet.

A man waded out to meet them before the hull touched. He was old, broad through the shoulders in the way of people who'd spent a lifetime hauling nets, with white hair cut short and a scar that split one eyebrow. He wore rubber boots and nothing else that looked bought new. He planted himself in knee-deep water directly in their path and put one hand flat against the bow, and the boat stopped, and that was that.

"This is Renn's water," he said. Not hostile. Just stating a fact, the way you'd say *this is my fence* or *this is fire*.

"We're not here to take anything," Coral said from the bow. She kept her hands where he could see them too. "We need shelter. Our boat needs work. One of us is hurt." She tipped her head toward Reyes, who lay wrapped in blankets under the canopy, shivering even in the afternoon heat, her lips still gray from the river she'd nearly died in. "We'll pay what we can. We'll leave when you say."

The old man looked at Coral for a long moment. Then past her, at the others, counting them the way you count rifles. His gaze caught on Mercer and stayed there, and something moved across his face — recognition, but old recognition, worn smooth like a stone in a current.

"You," he said. "You're the supply runner. The one who quit coming."

Junie felt Mercer flinch. It was small — a tightening around the eyes, a half-inch drop of the chin — but she'd spent enough years reading grown men's faces across a card table to catch it.

"A long time ago," Mercer said.

"The divers' boats stopped too." The old man said it flat, no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. "After that, nobody came. Nobody went. We thought the government had finished with whatever it wanted finished." He took his hand off the bow. "Now you come upstream with a drowned woman in your boat and navy men behind you. So maybe they're not finished."

"How do you know about navy men?" Tam asked sharply.

The old man almost smiled. "Because we can hear their engines at night, boy. Because their helicopters fly the canyon at dawn, low, like they think the canyon is deaf." He stepped back, splashing, and jerked his chin toward the bank. "Beach your boat by the rack. You eat with us tonight. You sleep in the long house. Tomorrow we talk about whether you stay longer." His eyes went to Reyes again, and softened by a degree. "Bring the drowned one first. My sister will want to look at her."

* * *

The long house sat at the top of the bank on the tallest stilts, a single great room of blackened timber and woven panels, big enough for the whole village to gather in bad weather. They carried Reyes up the switchback stairs between them, Junie taking her weight under the arms while Tam steadied the stretcher board, and Junie used the climb to look.

She was good at looking. It was the deckhand's whole job, really — read the load, read the weather, read the water. So she read the village.

She read: no children's toys in sight, though she could hear children, high voices somewhere behind the garden terraces, deliberately kept away from strangers. She read: the drying racks full, the smokehouses full, the gardens tended with a precision that spoke of people who planned to be here a long time and who did not trust anyone else's food. She read: a watchman posted on the highest walkway, a teenager with a pair of binoculars older than he was, who had been watching the river since before they rounded the bend and would be watching it after they left. She read: no radio antenna. No generator. Wires, yes, strung house to house, but dead ones, or carrying nothing she could hear.

And she read, carved into the lintel post of the long house, worn nearly smooth by decades of touching hands: a spiral. A single line coiling inward, tight at the center, and at the center a small drilled hole where something might once have been set — a stone, a bead, a piece of the mountain itself.

"That's the source," Sela said quietly beside her.

Junie jumped. She kept forgetting how silently the hydrologist moved. Sela stood with her head tilted at the carving, and her expression was one Junie had seen on Tam's face over a broken engine: recognition, and grief, and the beginning of a plan.

"The spiral," Sela said. "The river rising out of the mountain. Every village on the upper water carves it. It's older than any map." Her fingers hovered near the groove without touching it. "They aren't superstitions, Junie. They're records. Communities that have lived on this water for four hundred years keep their history in marks and songs because paper burns and governments confiscate paper." She lowered her hand. "Ask yourself why a people would build their whole iconography around a spring."

"Because the spring feeds them?"

"Because the spring *is* them," Sela said. "And someone poisoned it. And they've known longer than we have."

* * *

Reyes woke properly for the first time in two days that evening, in a dry bed in the long house, under the care of the old man's sister — a tiny, iron-backed woman called Maren who smelled of smoke and river clay and who handled the surveyor's wounds with a competence that made Tam raise his eyebrows and mouth *where did she train?* to nobody in particular.

The village fed them. That was the thing Junie kept circling back to later, lying awake on a woven mat with the river talking to itself below the floorboards. They fed them like kin. Smoked fish and tuber stew and a flat bread cooked on hot stones, and tea made from something juniper-bright that Junie drank three cups of before anyone told her what was in it. The whole community ate together in the long house, forty-odd people plus six strangers, and the strangers were given places in the circle without ceremony, and the food was passed hand to hand, and nobody asked them a single question for the better part of an hour.

That was the hospitality. Junie understood hospitality; she'd grown up on a dock where feeding whoever showed up was just what you did, because tomorrow you might be the one showing up. But underneath the welcome ran the other thing, the suspicion, and it wasn't rude and it wasn't hidden. It was structural. The watchman never came down from his post. The children stayed away. When the old man — whose name was Doss, and who turned out to be the closest thing the village had to a headman — finally began to question them, he did it in front of everyone, so that everything the strangers said belonged to the whole village at once.

He started with Mercer, naturally. The two of them had a history Junie could feel but not see, two old men circling a debt thirty years wide.

"You ran the government boats," Doss said. It wasn't a question.

"I ran supplies," Mercer said. "Where they told me to run them. For three years. Then I stopped."

"Why did you stop?"

Mercer looked into his tea. The firelight moved on his face and Junie saw him age ten years in ten seconds. "Because two men I knew went into the water upriver and didn't come out. Because the people who sent them in told me to haul their gear back down and say nothing, and I did it, and I've been doing it ever since — saying nothing." He set the cup down. "I'm done saying nothing. That's part of why we're here."

Doss studied him for a long time. Around the circle, the villagers watched, and Junie realized with a small cold shock that they weren't deciding whether to believe Mercer. They were deciding what to do with the truth if it was true — a different arithmetic entirely.

"And these others," Doss said, turning. "The diver. The engineer. The girl." His eyes landed on Junie and she fought the urge to look at her own boots. "Why does a salvage crew come upstream into water nobody maps, in the dry season, with the navy burning fuel behind them?"

Coral answered. She gave him most of it — the wreck, the contract, the case, the vanished survey team, Ashe and his leverage. She left out the compass and she left out the photograph and Junie noticed both omissions and noticed that Sela noticed too. When Coral finished, the long house was quiet except for the fire and the river.

Doss nodded slowly. "You want the surveyors."

"We want to find them alive if we can," Coral said. "And we want to know what they found that got them taken."

"They found graves," said a voice from the edge of the firelight.

Everyone turned. A young woman stood there — not much older than Junie herself, with a baby asleep against her shoulder in a sling, and the firelight catching the same spiral carved into a wooden pendant at her throat. Junie hadn't seen her before. She must have come in from one of the outer houses.

"My grandfather was one of them," the young woman said, to the room, to the strangers, to the fire. "Navy gear. In the caverns. We have known this since before I was born. We bury our dead facing downstream so their spirits can find the sea, and we have always known that some of our dead are upriver, facing the wrong way, wearing the uniforms of the men who put them there." She shifted the baby gently. "Your surveyors came with instruments and permits and took rubbings of the burial walls. We told them to stop. They smiled and kept working. Then the water rose and took their camp, and we mourned them anyway, because drowning is drowning, whatever you came to steal."

"The water rose," Tam repeated carefully. "You're saying the flood that hit their camp—"

"Was sent," the young woman said simply. "The source gives and the source takes. But lately—" and here her voice changed, flattened, went careful, "—lately it is men who decide which. There are gates in the canyon. Old ones. Rusty ones. Men come sometimes to work on them, always at night, always by air. The river has been... managed. Like livestock." She looked at Coral. "You asked what your surveyors found. Ask instead: who has the power to open and close the throat of a river? And what are they afraid will happen if it breathes freely?"

Nobody answered. The fire cracked. Below the floorboards the river ran on, clear and young and, Junie thought suddenly, *managed*, and she would never again hear moving water the same way.

* * *

Later, when the long house had gone quiet and the villagers had drifted home to their stilt houses, Junie couldn't sleep. She slipped out onto the walkway and stood in the dark listening to the river, and that was where Doss found her.

He came up the stairs slow, favoring one knee, and stood beside her at the rail without speaking for a while. Two people watching black water move.

"You're the youngest," he said at last. "Of the crew."

"By a year. Tam's twenty-six."

"You're the one who watches, though." He glanced at her. "You've been reading us since you tied off. Don't apologize. It's a good habit on a river."

Junie shrugged, embarrassed. "I grew up on docks. You learn to read a place fast or you lose your wallet."

"What did you read?"

She considered lying, decided it would be pointless, and gave him the inventory. "You feed strangers like family and you post a watch like you're at war. Your kids are hidden. Your gardens could feed eighty people but there's forty of you. You've got no radio and dead wires, which means you *had* power once and pulled it out yourselves." She paused. "And everybody here knew about the surveyors' camp flooding before we told you, and nobody seemed surprised about the bodies either. Which means the graves aren't a rumor to you. They're a memory."

Doss was quiet a moment. "The dock taught you well."

"Who pays the cost?" Junie asked, and heard her own voice come out younger than she meant it to. "Of the secret, I mean. Downstream nobody knows any of this exists. The navy spends its money, the government spends its paper, the divers die, the surveyors die — and you all just live here, on the water, knowing. Forever. Why don't you leave?"

"Leave." Doss said the word like he was tasting something spoiled. "Girl, the river is not where we live. The river is what we are. Where would we carry it?" He gripped the rail, and the old scar on his eyebrow caught the starlight. "But I'll tell you what the cost looks like, since you asked. The cost looks like my grandmother teaching the burial songs only in whispers. It looks like good young people going downstream for work and not coming back, because they can't stand living next to a locked door. It looks like me, standing in the water today, ready to turn away six hungry strangers because the last strangers who came through here were measuring our dead for theft." He shook his head slowly. "The secret doesn't cost the men in the capital anything. It costs the people who live on top of it. It always has. Remember that, when your crew decides what to do with what you find. Whatever you choose, somebody on this water will pay the bill. The only question is whether it's chosen for them again — or whether, this once, they get a vote."

Junie looked at him. "You want us to keep going upstream."

"I want the door opened," Doss said. "What comes through it after that — truth, soldiers, flood, justice — at least it would be *air*. Forty years of holding your breath is enough." He pushed off the rail and started toward the stairs, then stopped. "One more thing, since you read so well. Read this: at dawn, a boat will come down from the canyon. One of ours. It carries fish to the hermit who lives below the gates — the only soul who watches that stretch of water day and night, and who owes me a debt older than you. If your crew means to go on, that boat is your road past the gates. And if the navy asks me tomorrow whether strangers passed through here—" the ghost of a smile crossed his face, "—I am an old man. My memory is not what it was."

He went down the stairs. Junie stayed at the rail a while longer, watching the river slide past below her feet, clear and quick and secretly governed, and thought about bills and votes and doors.

Then she heard footsteps behind her again — lighter ones — and turned to find Coral standing in the doorway of the long house, wrapped in a blanket, awake. Behind her, inside, Sela sat cross-legged by the banked fire with the compass open in her palm, its needle trembling, and she was looking up at Coral with an expression Junie had never seen on a grown person's face before: like someone about to hand over the worst news of their life and hoping it turns out to be a gift.

"Coral," Sela was saying softly. "There's something you need to know about your father. Sit down."

Junie stayed at the rail, unseen, and listened.
