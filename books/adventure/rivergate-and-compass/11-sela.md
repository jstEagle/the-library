## 11. Sela

The woman came to the boat at dusk, when the river had gone the color of hammered tin and the village fires along the bend threw long orange fingers across the water. Coral was sitting on the *Kestrel's* rail with her father's compass open in her palm, not reading it so much as holding it, the way you'd hold a hand that couldn't hold yours back. She heard footsteps on the mud-slick bank — unhurried, deliberate, the walk of someone who had decided long ago that nothing on this river could surprise her.

"You keep opening it," the woman said. "Every evening since you arrived. You look at it like it owes you an answer."

Coral closed her fist around the compass before she thought about it. Old reflex. On the docks at Rivergate, anything valuable in your hand and a stranger talking to you meant one of them was about to lose something.

"It doesn't work," Coral said. "If that's what you're wondering."

"I wasn't wondering." The woman stopped at the foot of the gangplank. She was perhaps fifty, gray threading dark hair she'd tied back with a strip of cloth, and she carried herself with the particular stillness of people who had spent years being watched. Her hands were stained faintly green at the knuckles — ink, or algae, or both. "I was noticing. There's a difference."

Junie appeared in the galley doorway behind Coral, dish towel over one shoulder, wearing the expression she wore whenever strangers approached the boat: friendly on the surface, a cleat hitch already tied somewhere underneath. Tam looked up from the engine hatch where he'd been elbow-deep for an hour, cursing softly at a fuel line.

"We're not selling anything," Junie said pleasantly.

"Good," the woman said. "I'm not buying." She looked past them all, upriver, toward the black seam of canyon where the water came from. Then back to Coral's closed fist. "My name is Sela. I was told you pulled Dr. Reyes from the strainer two days ago. I'd like to see her, if she's well enough. And I'd like to talk to you about that compass."

The name landed in Coral's chest like a dropped anchor. *Reyes.* They hadn't told anyone in the village they had her. Mercer had argued — was still arguing, in his low grinding way — that handing her to Commander Ashe was the only play that got them home with their debts cleared and their skins intact. Keeping her hidden aboard, in the forward hold, feverish and whispering about bodies in navy gear, was a decision Coral had made alone and hadn't yet found the courage to defend out loud.

"How do you know about Reyes?" Coral asked.

"The river told me," Sela said, and didn't smile. "A strainer that size, at that bend, after the release upstream — anything caught in it is caught there because the water wanted it kept. I've been walking the banks since the flood pulse came through. Looking for what the water wanted kept." She tilted her head. "You brought her here. To this bend. That tells me something too."

"What does it tell you?"

"That you don't trust the uniform either."

* * *

They let Sela aboard — Coral told herself it was because a hydrologist might help Reyes, and only partly because the woman had said *compass* in a voice that made Coral's skin prickle. Sela moved through the boat like someone who understood vessels, stepping over the coiled lines without looking, ducking the low beam by the companionway from memory of a thousand boats that weren't this one. She knelt beside Reyes's bunk in the hold, pressed two fingers to the surveyor's wrist, studied the gray of her lips, and did something with a rehydration sachet and a strip of boiled cloth that brought color back to the woman's face within the hour. She spoke to Reyes in a low, clipped cadence — questions mostly, and Reyes answered them in fragments, and Coral stood at the top of the hold ladder listening to words like *cavern* and *containment* and *the old ones, the ones in the gear* drift up like cold air off deep water.

When Sela climbed out, she found Coral waiting at the rail with the compass open between them like a card laid face-up.

"All right," Coral said. "Talk."

Sela didn't reach for it. That was the first thing. Everyone else who had ever shown interest in the compass — dockside appraisers, a customs officer once, Junie with her idle curiosity — had reached for it. Sela put her hands flat on the rail instead, as if the instrument were something wild that spooked easily.

"Where did you get it?"

"My father. He was a diver." Coral turned the brass housing so the firelight caught the hairline crack across the crystal. "He went out on a contract run when I was nine. The river gave back his tank but not him. This came back with the tank."

"And it points wrong," Sela said. Not a question.

Coral's throat tightened. "It points upriver. No matter how I hold it. North, south, sideways — it wants the source. Twenty-five years it's been doing that. I used to think it was broken."

"It isn't broken." Sela finally looked at her, and her eyes were the flat brown-green of river water over silt — calm on top, everything moving underneath. "There's iron in the rock up there. Magnetite seams running through the canyon walls, thick enough to drag any needle in the valley. Every compass on this river lies about north. But that one—" she nodded at it, "—that one was compensated. Rebalanced. Someone built it to find the seams instead of fight them. Do you know how I know?"

"Because you're a hydrologist."

"Because I held it before." Sela said it simply, the way you'd report a depth sounding. "Twenty-two years ago. It belonged to a diver named Elio Villalobos."

The world went narrow and loud and very far away, all at once. Coral heard the village fires popping. Heard Junie laughing at something with the kids on the bank, the sound thin and distant as radio static. Heard her own blood.

"That's my father's name," she said.

"I know," Sela said. "I watched you come down the bend yesterday and I saw his jaw on your face and I nearly walked into the river." She exhaled, long and controlled, a diver managing air. "Elio Villalobos came to this village in the year you were probably learning to swim. He came with charts he shouldn't have had and questions nobody would answer, because answering them had gotten two of my neighbors disappeared and one of them drowned with his lungs full of water that smelled like rust. Elio sat where you're sitting. He asked to borrow our boats. We told him no. He went anyway."

Coral's hands were shaking. She set the compass down on the rail so she wouldn't drop it. "He died out there. In the canyon."

"He died trying to finish something," Sela said. "And the thing he was trying to finish — I know what it was, Coral, because I spent eleven years of my life measuring it, and when I published what I measured, they took my position, my papers, and my right to live within sixty kilometers of this water. I have been exile and guest in this village ever since. They feed me because the river feeds them and I taught them to read the river. But I am not one of them. And neither was your father. And neither, whatever you tell yourself, are you."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the fires had burned to red eyes and Tam had gone below and even Mercer's pipe had gone out on the pilothouse bench — Coral sat with Sela on the foredeck, a lantern between them turned low, the compass lying open on the deck planking between their feet like a sleeping animal.

"Why would he compensate a compass?" Coral asked. "He was a salvage man. He bought his instruments used and trusted his hands more."

"Because he wasn't salvaging anymore, by the end." Sela fed a twig into the lantern and watched it catch. "The last time I saw him, he told me the compass was the only honest thing left on the river. Everything else — the charts, the permits, the navy's maps with their blank spaces where blank spaces shouldn't be — all of it lied. So he rebuilt the needle himself. Took him a season. He said if the river was going to be weaponized against the people drinking it, then the least one instrument could do was point at the wound."

"The wound," Coral repeated.

"The source isn't just water rising out of stone, Coral. It's water rising through thirty years of everything they buried above it. Tailings. Solvents. The runoff from an operation the government says never existed, feeding an aquifer every village on this river draws from." Sela's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse — the flatness of a woman who had screamed herself empty years ago and now had only precision left. "I published the numbers. Contamination gradients, flow rates, isotope signatures. Enough to close the valley's wells. And the response was not to fix it. The response was to make me unemployable, unpublishable, and eventually unwelcome anywhere the paper trail could find me. Your father read my data and didn't flinch. Most people flinch. He started planning a dive."

"To do what? Get samples?"

"To get proof that would survive being buried. Paper burns, Coral. Hard drives drown. But he told me — the last night, on this same deck, maybe this same plank — he said the one thing they can't disappear is testimony delivered straight into the current. Water carries everything downstream eventually. Even truth. Especially truth, if you weight it right." She looked at Coral then, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "He sealed something. I don't know what or where. He went up the canyon with a dive plan I begged him not to run alone, and the river came back without him, and I have spent twenty-two years not knowing whether he failed or succeeded or simply drowned like divers do."

Coral picked up the compass. It lay in her palm, light as a bird's bone, heavy as a headstone. All those years she'd carried it as grief — a broken thing from a broken man, a souvenir of absence. She'd worn the groove of it into her pocket lining. She'd held it in the dark of a hundred dives and pretended its wrongness was company.

It had never been pointing wrong. It had been pointing the whole time.

"He marked a site," she heard herself say. "On his old charts — the ones I found in the case from the wreck. A dive site in the canyon slot. I thought it was a work note."

"It's where he planned to seal it," Sela said quietly. "Or where he sealed it. Or where he died before he could. The compass will take you to the seam lines; the chart will take you to the mark. Between the two of them—" she spread her hands, "—there's a place on this river that has been waiting for you longer than you've been alive."

From the pilothouse came the creak of Mercer shifting his weight, and Coral realized with a cold drop in her stomach that the old pilot had been awake the whole time, sitting in the dark, listening to every word. When she looked over, he didn't pretend otherwise. He just met her eyes across the deck, and his face — weathered down to its last honest grain — said what his voice wouldn't say in front of Sela:

*I ran those supply boats. I knew your father was going up there. I said nothing then, and I've been saying nothing for thirty years.*

And beneath it, older and worse, the question he'd been carrying since the wreck, since the case, since the first night:

*What else did I stay quiet about that was yours?*

Sela rose, stiff-kneed, and gathered her things. At the gangplank she paused.

"Tomorrow I'll show you the seam lines on my old field maps. If you're going up the canyon — and you are, we both know it, you have his jaw and his inability to leave a question in the water — you'll need someone who knows where the magnetite turns the currents treacherous. Where the eddies can hold a body for twenty years." She stepped down onto the mud. "I'm coming with you, Coral. Not because I owe your father. Because I owe the river. And because—" the ghost of something crossed her face, quick as a fish under ice, "—twenty-two years is a long time to talk to a ghost. I'd like to meet what he left behind."

She walked back up the bank toward the fires, and Coral stood at the rail until the dark took her.

Then she opened the compass one more time. The needle swung, settled, and held — steady as a struck bell — pointing up the black throat of the canyon, toward the source, toward whatever her father had weighted and sealed and trusted to the current.

Toward home, or toward the thing that had killed him. With this compass, Coral was beginning to understand, those had always been the same direction.

Behind her, Mercer's pipe scraped alight in the dark, and the old man's voice came low across the deck: "Girl. We need to talk. Before you go another mile up that river — there's things I should've told you the day you hired this boat, and I'm done drowning them."
