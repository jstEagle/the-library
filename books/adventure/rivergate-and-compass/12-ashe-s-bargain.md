## 12. Ashe's Bargain

The summons came at dawn, delivered not by a runner or a radio call but by the simple, unmistakable fact of a navy launch idling off the *Rivergate and Compass*'s stern, its wake rolling against their hull like a hand shaking them awake.

Coral was on deck before Junie finished shouting. She'd slept badly — she always slept badly now, with the sealed case stowed beneath her bunk like a second heartbeat — and she came up the companionway with her hair still wet from a dream of dark water, squinting into the gray light where the launch sat waiting. Two sailors aboard. A third figure standing at the rail in a coat too good for the river.

Commander Ashe.

He'd given them three days to search the stretch where the survey team had vanished. Three days of dragging sonar over water that gave up nothing but old snags and the sunken ghost of a logging barge. Three days of Tam muttering about instrument drift and Junie scanning the banks for footprints that weren't there, and Mercer saying nothing at all, which from Mercer was louder than anything he could have said.

Now the three days were up, and the navy had come to collect.

"Captain Voss." Ashe's voice carried across the water without effort, the way voices did when men were used to being obeyed on open decks. "I'd like a word. Alone."

Tam appeared at her shoulder, wrench in hand out of pure habit. "We're under contract to—"

"You're under contract to me," Ashe said mildly. "Which is why I'm offering to renegotiate it. Below decks on my launch, Captain. Your crew can watch the whole time through the windows if it makes you feel better."

It didn't make her feel better. Nothing about Commander Ashe made her feel better. He had the particular stillness of a man who had learned early that motion was a concession, and his eyes moved over the boat — over Tam, over Junie, over Mercer standing silent at the wheel — cataloguing them the way a salvage diver catalogues a wreck: what's valuable, what's dangerous, what can be cut loose without loss.

But there was nowhere else to go. The launch's cabin was warm and smelled of coffee and laminated charts, and Ashe poured her a cup without asking, sliding it across the table along with a folder stamped in red.

"Do you know what your debts total, Captain Voss?" he asked.

The question landed like cold water down the back of her collar. "That's not—"

"One hundred and fourteen thousand, six hundred and twelve marks," he said, as though reading it off the inside of his eyelids. "Salvage equipment liens held by Corven Marine Supply. Dock fees accrued at Rivergate over nineteen months. A personal loan from Halloway & Sons at an interest rate I'd describe as predatory if I thought describing it would help. And the refit loan on this vessel, co-signed—" he glanced up, and something almost gentle crossed his face, "—by one Mercer Ondu, who put his own boat up as collateral alongside yours."

Coral's hands had gone still around the coffee cup. She hadn't known about the co-signature. Mercer had never said. The old man let her believe the refit money had come from his savings, and all this time his own hull had been chained to hers, sinking or floating together.

"You've done your homework," she managed.

"It's my job to know what things cost." Ashe opened the folder. "Including silence."

* * *

The document inside was two pages, typed on heavy stock, with a signature block already bearing Ashe's name. She read it twice because the first time she was certain she'd misunderstood.

In exchange for delivery of all materials recovered from the wreck site designated **TRANSPORT 7-KILO** — including but not limited to sealed cases, documents, instruments, and personal effects — the undersigned authority agrees to: (1) full extinguishment of all listed debts and liens; (2) release of the vessel *Rivergate and Compass* and all attached collateral; (3) termination of the current conscription order upon completion; (4) no further inquiry into the contracting party's prior recovery of said materials.

Prior recovery. He knew. Of course he knew — the case had been logged somewhere, some manifest, some record of what Transport 7-Kilo had been carrying when it went down, and when the survey team started asking questions about the same stretch of river, someone had pulled the thread and found her name at the end of it.

"You want me to hand over the case," she said. "And lie about having it."

"I want you to hand over the case," Ashe corrected, "and stop carrying it. Those are different things." He leaned back, folding his hands. "You've been diving that wreck against every unwritten rule this river has, Captain. Somebody paid you to ignore what you found, and you took the money anyway because you're drowning — I don't say that as an insult, I say it as a man who reads balance sheets. Now the same river has swallowed six government surveyors, and I have admirals asking me questions I can't answer, and families asking questions I won't answer. You are standing on the smallest piece of solid ground in this entire affair. I'm offering to buy it."

"And if I don't sell?"

Ashe's expression didn't change, which was itself an answer. "Then the conscription continues. Indefinitely. Your boat stays requisitioned, your crew stays conscripted, and your creditors stay exactly where they are — except that Corven Marine has expressed interest in recovering its equipment through legal seizure, and seized dive gear doesn't fetch much at auction. You'd keep the boat and lose everything that makes it worth keeping." He paused. "I'm told Mr. Ondu's health isn't what it was. Old men sleep badly on requisitioned decks."

The coffee had gone cold in her hands. She set it down carefully, because her other option was setting it down otherwise.

"You talk about the survey team like they're a paperwork problem," she said. "Six people are missing. There were no tracks, Ashe. No footprints. Their instruments were still running. Whatever took them didn't walk out of that camp."

Something flickered behind his eyes then — fast, quickly banked, but real. Not indifference. Recognition.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I know."

* * *

He stood then and moved to the cabin window, looking out at her boat, at her crew. Tam had stopped pretending to work and stood at the rail with his arms folded. Junie sat on the hatch coaming, knees drawn up, watching the launch with the flat unblinking stare she usually saved for storms. Mercer hadn't moved from the wheel at all, but Coral could see his hands resting on the spokes, and she could see how still they were.

"Thirty-one years ago," Ashe said, without turning around, "the navy ran a hydrographic operation on the upper river. It was classified, it was necessary, and it ended badly. Men died. Records were sealed. I have spent eleven years as the officer responsible for making sure those records stay sealed, and in eleven years I have learned exactly two things." He turned back to her. "First: the river keeps everything. Second: everyone who goes looking for what it keeps either comes back changed or doesn't come back. Your father was one of the ones who came back changed. You should ask yourself what he brought home before you decide what you're willing to carry."

The words went through her like a hook through soft tissue. She kept her face still — years of poker with dockside lenders had taught her that much — but inside, everything tilted.

"You knew my father."

"I knew of him." Ashe resumed his seat, and just like that the window was closed again, the man behind it smooth and official. "The offer stands until noon tomorrow. One hundred fourteen thousand, six hundred twelve marks, erased. Your boat free. Your crew released. All you have to do is give me a metal box and forget you ever opened the river's mail." He slid a pen across the table. "Temptation is only a sin if you sign it, Captain. Until then it's just arithmetic."

She didn't touch the pen. She stood, and her legs felt strange, lighter than they should, as if the weight she'd carried for nineteen months had briefly been lifted and her body didn't know how to stand without it.

"Noon tomorrow," she repeated.

"Noon tomorrow." He walked her to the launch's rail himself, and as she stepped across to her own deck, he added, low enough that only she could hear: "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I hope you take the deal. The alternative endings to this story are all worse."

* * *

She called no meeting. She simply sat in the wheelhouse after the launch had gone, the unsigned contract in her lap, and watched the river move.

One hundred fourteen thousand, six hundred twelve marks. She said the number to herself the way you'd test a bad tooth. It was freedom. It was the dockmaster's smile instead of his invoice. It was new regulators for Tam and a proper winch and Junie paid a wage that didn't evaporate the week it arrived. It was Mercer's boat unchained, Mercer's nights easy, Mercer sleeping like a man whose debts weren't nailed to someone else's hull.

All it cost was a metal box.

Except it wasn't a metal box, and she knew it, and Ashe knew she knew it. It was charts of an unmapped tributary. It was a photograph of her father's dive crew — five faces grinning into a camera thirty years dead, one of them her father, young and sunburned and whole. It was whatever the navy had buried so deep it needed a transport sunk in forbidden water to hide it. It was the reason six surveyors had walked into the wilderness and left no footprints behind.

And it was, she understood suddenly, sitting alone in the wheelhouse with the contract warming under her hands — it was the only thing standing between her and becoming the kind of person who could live with herself afterward.

Her father had gone upriver chasing this. She'd spent nine years telling herself he'd died in an accident, a routine dive gone wrong, the river doing what rivers do. But Ashe had said *changed*, and Mercer had gone silent at the mention of the source, and the compass on the shelf above the wheel was pointing — she checked, out of habit, out of grief — not north, not toward any magnetic pole she could name, but upstream. Toward the headwaters. Toward whatever waited there with its hand out.

Below her, she heard Tam and Junie arguing softly about fuel reserves, and Mercer's low voice cutting through, steady as ballast. Her crew. Her family, assembled by accident and held together by poverty and stubbornness. If she signed, they were free. If she didn't, she was gambling all of them on a truth she couldn't yet see the shape of.

Coral looked at the pen lying on the chart table where Ashe had left it — no, not left it; she realized with a small cold shock that he hadn't left it at all. She'd brought it back aboard without noticing, wrapped in the contract's fold. Even his stationery conscripted people.

She set the contract down on the shelf beside the compass, side by side, the debt and the direction, and stood between them for a long moment in the failing light.

Then the wheelhouse door banged open and Junie burst in, breathless, holding a scrap of oilcloth like it might bite.

"Coral — Mercer wants you at the stern. Now." Her eyes were wide, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "He says he's ready to tell you what happened at the source. He says he's been ready for thirty years."

The contract lay on the shelf, unsigned, its ink drying in the river air. Coral picked up neither it nor the pen.

She followed Junie aft.
