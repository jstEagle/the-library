## 13. Upstream Against the Season

Tam Okafor had a rule about rivers: they never lied, but they never hurried to tell the truth either. You had to stand still long enough and watch, and most people couldn't do that. Most people looked at moving water and saw only movement.

Tam saw arithmetic.

She'd been awake since before dawn, sitting on the *Rivergate and Compass*'s bow deck with her knees drawn up and her notebook open against them, watching the light come gray into the gorge. The river ran below her in the dark like something poured rather than flowing — thick, slow, deliberate — and she had been measuring it against the same stone for three mornings now. A shelf of granite that jutted from the eastern bank, streaked white with old mineral stain, its lip cutting the current at a fixed point. Every morning she marked where the water's edge sat relative to that lip. Every morning it was lower.

Three fingers yesterday. Four this morning.

"Say it out loud," Junie said behind her, voice sleep-rough, a tin mug steaming in both hands. "You've been doing your face. The one where you're counting something terrible."

"I'm always counting something terrible."

"Yeah, but this is the special face. The one you did when the bilge pump died in the flood season." Junie lowered herself onto the deck beside her and offered the mug. Tam took it. Chicory, scorched, made with river water boiled twice. It tasted like being poor in a beautiful place, which was accurate.

"The river's dropped four fingers overnight," Tam said. "It dropped three the night before. Two before that. That's not seasonal fluctuation, Junie. That's a curve. And curves steepen."

Junie squinted at the granite shelf as if she could see the math on it. "How bad?"

Tam turned back through her notebook — pages of soundings, flow estimates, the crude profile of the gorge she'd been building since they passed the last village at the bend. She'd charted this stretch of river more honestly in six days than the government survey team had managed before they vanished, mostly because Tam charted with fear instead of funding.

"We came up on spring melt tailwater," she said. "The whole upper system was still draining snowmelt off the high country. That's what carried us — we were riding the last of it. But the dry season's taken hold upstream. No rain for eleven days by Sela's count, and the tributaries feeding this reach are down to seepage." She tapped the page. "I estimate we're losing between eight and twelve centimeters of depth per day, accelerating. In ten days, maybe twelve, the rapids below us — the ones Mercer called the Staircase, the ones we barely ran with full water — become a boulder garden. No channel. No line through. Nothing a boat our draft can float."

"And going forward?"

"Forward gets shallower too, but slower. The source country is canyon-bound; the gradient steepens but the walls narrow, so the water concentrates. We can probably push the boat another thirty, forty kilometers before she starts grinding gravel. After that—" Tam shrugged. "After that we're portaging, and every kilometer of portage costs a day, and every day costs water depth behind us."

Junie was quiet for a moment. Downriver, then. Behind them. The way home filling up with stones.

"So the river's closing the door," she said.

"The river's closing the door," Tam agreed, "and it isn't going to tell us when."

* * *

By midmorning the whole crew had gathered around the chart table under the awning, and Tam laid it out the way she laid out everything — flatly, in order, without decoration. Coral stood at the head of the table with her arms crossed, the compass in its leather case beside her left hand like it always was these days, like an organ she'd grown. Mercer sat heavily on the bench, his shoulder bandaged from the narrows, his color better than it had been but his breathing still carrying a wet rattle he pretended wasn't there. Sela leaned against the rail, arms folded, listening the way scientists listen — already three objections ahead. Imani Reyes lay propped in the stern cabin doorway, wrapped in two blankets, hollow-eyed and upright by sheer professional discipline, because she had refused to miss a briefing about her own rescue.

"Ten days," Tam finished. "Maybe twelve if we're lucky and the upstream gauges are wrong. In that window we have to find whatever's left to find, get Reyes stable enough to travel, and run back down through the Staircase with enough water under our keel to survive it. Miss the window, and we winter here. No boat, no radio relay past the bend, no resupply. The valley below us has maybe two hundred people who'd share what they have, and they don't have much in a dry year."

Nobody spoke immediately. The river filled the silence, that deep throaty sound it made against the hull, patient as anything.

Coral looked at the chart. Tam watched her look — watched her finger trace the penciled line of the gorge, stop at the mark Tam had labeled CAMP 2 (FLOODED), continue up to the blank space beyond it where the paper just gave up. The blank space was where the survey team had been going. The blank space was where her father's compass wanted to point.

"How far to the source?" Coral asked.

Sela answered before Tam could. "By water, perhaps fifty kilometers. By foot through the canyon country, less distance but three times the time. The walls close in above the confluence — there's no bank to walk, only benches and rubble slopes." She said it evenly, but Tam heard the undertow in it: Sela knew this country, had walked some of it once, and had not come back until now.

"Then we split the difference," Coral said. "We take the boat as far as she'll float — Tam, give me your honest number, not your scared number."

"My honest number is my scared number. That's what makes it honest." Tam held Coral's gaze. "Thirty-five kilometers. Forty if we lighten her and lift the engine. Past the second confluence the channel braids over gravel bars, and I won't ground her in moving water — grounding is how boats die and crews after them."

"Thirty-five," Coral said. "Then cache and walk."

Mercer grunted. Everyone turned to him. He'd been quiet all morning in the way that meant pain or memory, and with Mercer those had always shared a root. "You're talking about Bone Country," he said. "Above the second confluence. I told you what's up there."

"You told us there was mining equipment," Coral said. "Abandoned."

"I told you what I saw from the water thirty years ago, at night, running supplies I shouldn't have been running." His hand went unconsciously to his bandaged shoulder. "What's on the banks, I only ever glimpsed. Derricks. Adits cut into the canyon walls. Cableways. Enough iron rusting in that gorge to build a town. Nobody walks through Bone Country fast, girl. The ground itself is against you — slag heaps, shafts, tailings that look solid and aren't. Men drowned in tailings ponds up there that weren't on any map." He looked at Tam. "Your numbers assume walking pace. Halve them."

Tam wrote it down because writing things down was how she kept her hands steady. The math got worse and she let everyone watch it get worse, because they deserved to see it.

"Then here's the shape of it," she said finally. "Every day we spend finding answers is a day subtracted from our exit. There is no version of this where we're not racing. The only question is whether we race intelligently or whether we stand around having meetings while the river makes the decision for us." She flipped to a fresh page. "So: decisions today. What we carry, what we cache, who goes forward and who stays with the boat. Because someone has to stay with the boat, and bring her down alone if it comes to that, and I want to know now who that is so I can start teaching them the Staircase lines tonight."

"I'll stay," Junie said, too fast.

"You'll stay because you want to protect us or because you think you're the least useful?" Tam asked, not unkindly.

Junie's jaw set. "Because somebody small and quick should be on a light boat in skinny water, and that's not you, you're built like a dock winch."

Despite everything, Tam felt her mouth twitch. "Noted. Motion carries pending objection from the captain."

"No objection," Coral said quietly. Her eyes hadn't left the blank space on the chart. "Thank you, Junie."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and Tam lost herself in the work the way she always did — the good kind of losing, the kind that kept the fear in a box with a lid on it. They stripped the *Rivergate and Compass* down to bones: spare canvas ashore, half the water supply cached in sealed drums buried in the shade of a rock overhang and marked on Tam's chart with three separate redundancies, tools sorted into go-bags by weight and desperation. Tam pulled the engine's cowling and serviced it by feel, cleaning the carburetor with a rag and prayer, because salt-free river water or not, grit got everywhere in dry season and a fouled plug forty kilometers up a dying river was a death sentence with extra steps.

Imani came and sat on an overturned crate to watch her work, blankets still around her shoulders despite the heat.

"You count everything," Imani observed. Her voice was still scraped thin from the strainer, from the water, from whatever she'd seen that she hadn't yet told them all of. "The engineer does. I noticed on the boat. You touch things and you're measuring them."

"Everything's measurable," Tam said. "That's either the most comforting fact in the world or the most horrifying one. I haven't decided."

"Which is it today?"

Tam tightened a fitting, considered. "Today it's comforting. Today the river is the thing I can't measure precisely enough, and I hate it for that, so I'm taking comfort in bolts." She glanced up. "You're a hydrologist. You knew this system was dropping before I said it. Why didn't you say anything at the briefing?"

Imani was quiet a moment. Somewhere up-gorge a bird cried twice and quit.

"Because my team didn't listen to measurements either," she said. "We had gauges. We had the seasonal models. Our own logistics officer flagged the window in week one — said we had twenty working days before the drawdown closed the lower reaches, and the mission lead extended anyway. Pressure from above. There was always pressure from above." Her hands worked at the blanket fringe. "The river tried to warn everyone, Ms. Okafor. It warns everybody. It's the only honest party in this entire business. People just keep deciding they have more time than it says they do."

Tam set down her wrench. "Then help me be the exception. Tomorrow, when we're underway — I want you at my elbow reading water. You know this basin's behavior from data; I know it from scar tissue. Between us we might actually beat the curve."

Imani looked at her, and something in her exhausted face steadied, the way a person steadies when handed a job, a role, a reason to sit upright. "All right," she said. "But you should know what I know about where we're going first. Tonight. All of you. There are things my team recorded that I haven't—" She stopped. Started again. "There are bodies in the numbers, Ms. Okafor. Old ones. And the water levels are part of why."

"I know," Tam said gently. "Junie found the drowned camp. We know someone released water on purpose."

Imani shook her head slowly, and Tam saw that the horror in her went deeper than the flood, deeper than the strainer, down somewhere the rest of them hadn't dug yet. "That wasn't the first release," Imani said. "And it wasn't done by hand."

* * *

Evening came down the gorge in layers — gold on the western rim, violet in the depths, and the river turning the color of gunmetal, still dropping, always dropping. Tam walked the bank one last time with her lantern and checked her gauge stone. Five fingers below the morning mark. The curve was steepening exactly as she'd feared, the river falling away beneath them like a tide going out on a shore with no sea behind it.

She stood there a while, letting the arithmetic settle in her chest where it lived, cold and clean and heavy. Ten days. Less, now. Maybe nine.

Behind her, voices drifted from the boat — Coral and Sela talking low over the chart, Mercer's cough, Junie laughing at something, the ordinary music of a crew that had become, without anyone signing anything, the family Tam had never planned on. Ahead of her the gorge ran north into darkness, toward the blank space on the map, toward whatever Imani had to tell them tonight, toward the source that fed a poisoned aquifer and a thirty-year lie.

The river would not wait. Fine. Tam had never once in her life waited for anything either.

She turned back toward the firelight — and stopped.

Downstream, far off in the blue dusk, where the gorge bent toward the Staircase, she saw it: a single point of light, moving. Not a star. Stars didn't travel along the river's line at boat speed, didn't blink green-to-white as they rounded the bend.

A running light. Someone was coming upriver, against the dying current, in the dark.

Tam snuffed her lantern and stood very still in the shadow of the rocks, counting — heartbeats, seconds, the closing distance — and ran for the boat.
