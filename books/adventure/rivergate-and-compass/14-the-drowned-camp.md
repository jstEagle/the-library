## 14. The Drowned Camp

Junie smelled the water before she saw it.

That wasn't right, and she knew it wasn't right, and the knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Rivers had a smell — green rot and mineral cold, silt and sun on stone — and she'd grown up breathing that smell the way other kids grew up breathing the sea. But this was different. This was the smell of water that had been *still*. Water that had sat somewhere dark for a long time and then been let go all at once, the way a cellar smells when the door's been shut for a season. Old water. Stale water. Water that had been waiting somewhere and had been told, by somebody, to come out.

"Tam," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. "Tam, come smell this."

Tam was at the rail with the depth sounder, because Tam was always at the rail with something that beeped, and she came over with the easy, rolling walk she had, one hand on the gunwale like the boat might try something. Junie watched her lean out, watched her nostrils flare, and watched the easy walk stop being easy.

"That's not river," Tam said.

"No."

"That's reservoir water. That's — Junie, that's water that's been sitting behind something." Tam straightened up and looked upstream, and Junie looked with her. The river ran narrow here, canyon-walled, the morning light coming down in slats through the pines on the rims. The current had gone strange overnight. Mercer had said so at first light, in his flat pilot's way — *she's running heavy for the season, and she's running cold* — and had spent the whole morning with his hand in the water like he was taking its pulse. Now Junie understood what the pulse had been telling him.

The water was two feet higher than yesterday. It had come up in the night, quiet as a thief, and it had brought the smell of somewhere else with it.

"Mercer!" Tam called aft. "Get up here."

Mercer came the way Mercer always came, unhurried, which was how Junie knew he was worried, because Mercer's hurry and Mercer's calm both meant the same thing and she'd learned to read which one was which. He dipped his hand, sniffed his fingers, and spat over the side.

"Somebody's been holding water back up there," he said. "And somebody let it go."

"Let it go for what?" Junie asked.

Mercer looked at her, and then he looked upstream, and he didn't answer, and the not-answer was worse than any answer.

* * *

They found the forward camp an hour on, at a bend where the canyon opened into a bowl of floodplain and the survey team's maps had promised a terrace above the highwater line. The boat came around the bend slow, Mercer holding her against the current with the engine just breathing, and Junie saw it first because Junie always saw things first — it was the one thing she was good at, the one thing she'd ever been praised for, seeing the small wrong thing at the edge of a big right scene.

The tents were still there. That was the first wrong thing. They stood in a row on the terrace like they'd been set up that morning, three dome tents and the big mess fly, guy-lines taut, and for one whole second Junie's heart lifted — *they're fine, they're here, they walked out and left camp standing* — and then the second passed and she saw the rest.

The terrace was underwater.

Not deep. Not the dramatic drowning of a movie, no roofs poking out of a lake. Just a foot and a half of water lying over the whole flat, perfectly still, perfectly clear, so clear that Junie could see the ground beneath it as if through glass — the trampled grass, the fire ring, the boxes of instruments, the folding table with its papers pinned under stones. Everything exactly where people had left it. Everything under water that had no business being there.

"Stop the boat," Coral said quietly. Mercer was already stopping the boat.

They came in to the edge of the flood in silence, and Junie stepped off into water that soaked her boots to the ankle and then stopped, because the water had stopped, held at some invisible line as if a giant had drawn a border in the mud and told it not to cross. Behind the boat, the river flowed on. Here, the flood lay flat as a table. She stood on the boundary with one foot in moving water and one foot in still, and the still water was cold in a way the river wasn't. Cold like a cave. Cold like something kept.

"Junie." Tam's voice. "Look at the mud."

Junie looked. The line where the flood ended was a clean edge, a razor line of wet silt on dry, and above it — nothing. No tracks going out. No tracks coming in. The ground above the waterline was printed with the surveyors' boot-traffic, days of it, a whole archaeology of a working camp, and it all simply *ended* at the water. As if the people had walked into the flood and dissolved.

"Nobody walked out of here," Tam said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the engineer's voice, the voice she used when a load was about to fail and everyone needed to stay calm. "Nobody walked out, and nobody walked in. This water came up over them while they were — "

"While they were asleep," Junie said.

Nobody told her she was wrong. That was how she knew she wasn't.

* * *

They waded in. Coral went first, of course — Coral always went first, it was a law of the universe like gravity — with the case from the wreck strapped tight to her back, because she never let that thing out of her sight anymore, and Junie had stopped asking about it because the asking only made Coral's face do the thing it did, the shut thing, the door-closing thing. Reyes's data case was in there too, or the memory of it; they'd found nothing of the team but this camp, and the camp was a museum of interruption.

Junie waded behind Tam and tried to make herself useful and tried not to look at the tents.

She looked at the tents.

The nearest one was zipped open from the inside. She could see it clearly through the clear water — the zipper pulled back in a long smooth curve, unhurried, the flap folded over the way you fold it when you're getting up in the night to pee and you don't want to wake anybody. The sleeping bag inside was unrolled and empty and *dry*. That was the detail that got her, that sat down in her stomach and made a home: the sleeping bag was dry. The water had come and gone over it — or come and *stopped*, come and stayed — and the bag in its hollow was dry as bone, because the water had risen so gently, so perfectly, that it hadn't even disturbed the hollow where a body had lain.

The water had risen like it was being poured by hand.

"Junie." Coral was at the mess fly, lifting a box out of the flood. "Give me a hand with these. We get the instruments up high, we get the papers, and then we go. We don't stay here."

Junie waded over. The water was shin-deep here, thigh-deep there, following some contour of the old ground she couldn't see, and everything in it was preserved with a terrible tenderness — a mug sitting upright on the flooded table, a pencil resting on an open notebook, the notebook's pages fanned and *dry* under a skin of water. She reached for it and stopped.

"Coral. The pages are dry."

"What?"

"The notebook. Under the water. The pages are dry." She lifted it out, and it was true — the cover soaked, the edges dark, but the pages inside dry and legible, the water never having found its way in, as if the flood had risen around everything with the care of a curator. As if the water had been *told* what to touch and what to leave.

Coral took the notebook and looked at it for a long time, and Junie watched her face do something new, something past the shut-door thing, something Junie didn't have a name for. Fear, maybe. But a specific kind. The kind you feel when the world shows you it's been arranged.

"Water doesn't do this," Coral said. "Water doesn't *choose*."

"Then somebody chose for it," Mercer said from the edge of the flood, where he stood hip-deep with his arms crossed, refusing to come further in, and his voice had a sound Junie had never heard from him, and she'd heard Mercer scared, she'd heard Mercer angry, she'd heard Mercer talk about the war years and the bad wrecks. This was none of those. This was Mercer sounding *old*. "Thirty years ago we had a word for this. When the water came up wrong, when the river did something a river can't do. We called it *the release*. And you did not ask who gave the order. You did not ever, ever ask."

"Mercer," Coral said. "Who released it?"

He looked at her, and Junie saw him decide not to answer, and she saw Coral see it too, and the space between them filled up with all the things the old man had carried since before Junie was born.

* * *

They worked fast after that. Nobody said *let's work fast*; the working fast just happened, the way it does when everyone in a crew understands the same thing without saying it. They pulled the instruments up onto the boat's deck — theodolites, a laser level still blinking its patient green eye, a data logger in its waterproof case that Tam cracked open and went quiet over. They hauled the boxes of core samples. Junie gathered papers, dozens of them, maps and printouts and handwritten sheets, and she pinned them under a cargo net on deck because the wind off the water kept trying to take them, and she noticed, because Junie noticed things, that every single sheet was about the same thing.

Elevation. Flow rate. Containment integrity.

The survey team hadn't been mapping the river. The survey team had been mapping *structures*. Every page had the same header, the same project code, and every page had numbers that meant nothing to Junie except that they were numbers about things built in the water, things holding water back, things that were — she read it in the margin of one sheet, in a hurried hand — *failing*.

"Tam," she said. "What's a containment grid?"

Tam took the sheet. Read it. Read it again. And Junie watched her friend's face, which was the most expressive face on the boat, do the thing it never did, which was go completely still.

"Tam?"

"It's a dam," Tam said. "Sort of. It's — Junie, this is a grid of structures upstream, holding water in the source reaches. Old ones, by the dates. And this — " her finger tapped a column of figures — "is a log of controlled releases. Scheduled. Documented. Somebody's been opening and closing this river like a tap for *decades*." She looked up, and her eyes went past Junie, upstream, into the slatted canyon light. "And three days ago, somebody opened it over a sleeping camp."

"Maybe it was an accident," Junie said, and heard how thin it sounded even as it left her mouth.

"An accident," Tam repeated. She handed the sheet back. "An accident that rises eighteen inches, in the night, at a walking pace, and stops at the exact edge of the terrace. An accident that leaves the sleeping bags dry and the notebooks readable." Her voice cracked on the last word, not with tears, with something hotter. "That's not an accident. That's a *message*. That's somebody saying: we can do this whenever we want. We can do this to you."

The wind came down the canyon and moved the water. All of it, the whole flooded terrace, moved together, one slow glassy shiver, like something under the surface turning over in its sleep. Junie's arms came up in gooseflesh and she hated that they did it, hated being the youngest, hated being the one whose body told the truth.

Coral stood in the middle of the drowned camp with the water at her knees, and Junie looked at her and understood, suddenly, completely, why the crew followed her. Not because she was brave. Because she was *facing* it. Whatever it was — the wreck, the case, the compass that pointed wrong, and now this, this water that behaved like a weapon — Coral stood in the middle of it with her chin up and her eyes moving, cataloguing, refusing to look away, and the looking-away was the only surrender that mattered.

"We take everything," Coral said. "Every page, every instrument, every sample. If they flooded this camp to bury something, then the something is what we carry out of here. And Junie — " Coral's eyes found hers, and they were steady, and that steadiness was a rope Junie grabbed with both hands — "you saw the waterline. You saw the edge. When this is over, when we're somewhere safe, I need you to tell it exactly how it was. Can you do that?"

Junie nodded. Her throat was too tight for words.

"Because somebody drowned a camp to keep a secret," Coral said, "and the only thing they couldn't flood was what we saw."

* * *

They were loading the last of it when Junie saw the thing she would tell, later, exactly how it was, to investigators and journalists and anyone who would sit still long enough — the thing she would dream about for years after, the small wrong detail at the edge of the big wrong scene.

She was wading back from the tents with an armful of sample tubes when the light shifted on the far side of the flood, where the water lay deepest against the cut bank, and she saw a shape in the water. A dark rectangle, man-made, lying on the drowned ground near the base of the bank. She thought, for one merciful second, that it was a case. A toolbox. A door.

Then she saw the boots.

The boots were navy boots, government-issue, laced, and they were attached to legs, and the legs were attached to a body lying face-down in eighteen inches of perfectly clear water, arms out, in the posture of a man who had been running when the water took him — or who had been running *from* the water, toward the high ground, and had been one step short. The water lay over him with the same terrible tenderness it had lain over everything else, so clear she could see the mud printed with the marks of his knees, his hands, his final scramble, preserved like a fossil. Preserved like a message.

Junie made a sound she didn't decide to make.

They were all around her in seconds — Coral first, always first, wading hard, and Mercer at the edge going gray, and Tam with her hand fisted in Junie's collar keeping her upright, keeping her from going under herself. Coral reached the body and turned it over with the gentleness of someone handling something sacred, and the face came up out of the water streaming, and it was a young man, younger than Tam, with a government ID lanyard still around his neck and the card face-down against his chest, and his eyes were open, and the water had not closed them.

Coral lifted the card, turned it over, read it. Her lips moved around the name without sound. Then she looked up — not at the crew, not at the body, but upstream, up the canyon, into the darkening slats where the river came from, where the water came from, where the *orders* came from. And Junie watched her jaw set in a way she'd only ever seen it set once before, the day the repo men came for the boat.

"Someone opened a gate," Coral said, very quietly, to the canyon, to the water, to whoever was listening. "And someone's going to answer for it."

Above the bend, far upstream, so faint it might have been wind, they heard it: a long, low, grinding groan of metal under strain, rolling down the water like a bell.

The river shivered. The flood at their feet began, very slowly, to move.
