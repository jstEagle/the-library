## 15. One Survivor

The water had a voice, and it was saying her name.

Imani Reyes knew that wasn't true. She knew it the way she knew her own credentials — coldly, academically, from a great remove — because knowing things had always been the one skill she could rely on when everything else failed. The water was not saying *Imani*. The water was saying nothing. It was simply moving, with the patient indifference of something that had been moving for ten thousand years and would move for ten thousand more, and she happened to be caught in the middle of its sentence.

She was in a strainer. That was the technical term Tam had taught her three days ago, pointing at a fallen tree wedged between two boulders downstream of their camp, its branches combing the current like the ribs of some enormous drowned animal. *If you ever end up in the water,* he'd said, in his flat engineer's way that made every warning sound like a maintenance bulletin, *you do not want to be in that.* She had nodded. She had taken notes. She was a hydrologist; she understood flow dynamics better than anyone on the boat, arguably better than anyone in the survey program. She understood exactly how water moved through a lattice of branches, and exactly what happened to objects denser than water when they met such a lattice at speed.

Understanding had not saved her. Understanding never saved anyone. It only let you narrate your own disaster with terrible precision as it happened.

The flood had come at 3:14 in the morning. She knew that because her watch had been glowing on her wrist when the first wall of it hit the forward camp, and she had looked at it — actually looked at it, some reflex of the researcher that would have been funny if she lived — before the tent collapsed around her. Three hours later, or maybe four, maybe five, time having gone soft and unreliable as wet paper, the river had carried her two kilometers down the gorge and deposited her against the strainer like a piece of driftwood, and now here she was: arms hooked through the branches, boots gone, one sock gone, the other full of gravel, holding on to a dead tree while the living water tried to take her apart.

Her team was upstream. Her team was —

She made herself not think about it. Not yet. Not while her hands were still working.

The sun came up over the canyon rim the way it always did, gold and indifferent, painting the mist off the water. Imani hung in the strainer and watched it happen and did an inventory, because inventories were what she did. Two fingers broken on the left hand, probably. Ribs bruised where a log had clubbed her during the second rapid. A gash along her scalp that had stopped bleeding but had taken a payment of hair. Hypothermia circling her like a patient shark. And below her, perhaps a meter and a half of churning brown water, and below that, stones that would break her like an egg if she let go wrong.

Above her, footsteps.

She heard them before she saw anything — the particular crunch of boots on wet stone, someone moving carefully along the bank. Then a face appeared over the edge of the cutbank, backlit, all shadow and halo, and a voice called out in a tone that was half hope and half dread, the way people call out when they've been looking for bodies and are afraid of finding either result.

"Hello? Hello! Is someone there?"

"I'm here," Imani said, and discovered her voice was a ruin, scraped raw by river water and screaming. "I'm here. I'm alive."

The face resolved. A young woman, dark-haired, mud-smeared, with the wide startled eyes of someone who had not expected the answer. Junie — the deckhand, the smallest one, the one who hummed while she coiled rope. Imani had catalogued the crew during those first too-clean days at the dock: the engineer with his careful hands, the old pilot with his thousand-yard river eyes, the captain with her father's compass and her father's jaw, and this girl, this humming girl, who was now looking down at Imani like she was a miracle or a problem.

"Oh my god," Junie said. "Oh my god. Okay. Okay, don't move. Don't move, I'll — Coral! CORAL! She's here! Someone's in the tree!"

* * *

They got her out badly and then well, which is to say they got her out the way river people get people out: first attempt clumsy, second attempt practiced, third attempt unnecessary because the second worked. Coral Voss went into the water on a line, which Imani noted even through the shivering was a thing you only did if you trusted your crew absolutely, and hooked an arm under hers, and the two of them swung together across the current like a pendulum until hands pulled them both onto the stones.

Then there was a fire, and a blanket that smelled of diesel and wool, and a cup of something hot and sweet pressed into her ruined hand, and Imani Reyes sat on the bank of the river that had killed her team and drank it and shook.

"Dr. Reyes." Coral crouched in front of her. Up close her face was all planes and weather, younger than her authority, older than her years. "It's Coral. We were your boat crew. Do you remember us?"

"Voss," Imani said. "Coral Voss. Salvage contract, vessel *Rivergate and Compass*, registered out of—" She stopped, because her teeth were chattering so hard the words fell apart. She tried again. "I remember. I do the paperwork."

Something flickered across Coral's face at that — the paperwork, always the paperwork, the clean clean paperwork — but she didn't press it. "Where are the others?"

And there it was. The question. Imani had known it was coming since the moment she'd seen the firelight, had been building a wall against it, and now it arrived and took the wall down with one hand.

She looked upstream. The gorge narrowed up there into a slot of gray stone, and the river ran out of it fast and high, higher than it should have run, higher than any snowmelt schedule explained. Somewhere beyond that bend was the forward camp. Somewhere beyond that bend was Dr. Okafor's spectrometer still running on its battery pack, logging data to no one. Somewhere beyond that bend were five people she had led into this canyon because a government agency with immaculate paperwork had paid triple rate and asked polite questions.

"The water," Imani said. "It came at night. There was no rain. You understand? There was *no rain*. I've reviewed the catchment data for this entire basin. There was nothing upstream to make that kind of surge except—" Her voice cracked. She drank. Started again, quieter. "Except a release. Except someone opened something."

Nobody spoke. Mercer, the old pilot, stood a little apart with his arms folded, and Imani watched him watch the upstream water with an expression she couldn't read yet but would come to understand later, much later, when he told them what he knew. Tam had stopped fiddling with the fire. Even Junie had gone still.

"We ran," Imani said. "When the surge hit, we ran for the high bench above camp. Okafor went first, then me, then the others. The water took the camp, and then it kept rising, and we climbed, and there's a place where the bench narrows against the cliff, and—" She closed her eyes. The river filled the silence for her, talking its endless indifferent talk. "Mbeki slipped. I had his wrist. I had his wrist and then I didn't. The rest of it is — pieces. I went into the water at some point. I don't remember deciding to. I remember the strainer. I remember holding on and doing math to stay awake. Flow rates. Calorie expenditure. Anything with numbers in it."

"You held on all night," Junie said softly. It wasn't a question.

"It was that or find out what's downstream," Imani said, and was surprised to hear a ghost of dryness in her own voice. Grief did that sometimes. It came out sideways as humor, like blood from a wound you hadn't noticed.

Coral sat down beside her on the stone, close enough that her shoulder was a small warm weight. "You said *except a release*. You think someone flooded your camp on purpose."

"I think," Imani said, "that I am a scientist, and I don't say things like that lightly, and I have been saying them since dawn to myself, alone, in a tree." She turned to look at Coral directly. Her whole body hurt. Her left hand had swollen to twice its size inside the blanket. None of it mattered as much as what she had to say next, and she found, with a strange clarity, that the near-drowning had burned away whatever caution had brought her this far upriver in the first place. Caution had gotten her team killed. What remained was just the truth, and the truth wanted out.

"Captain Voss. Before the flood — before we even hired your boat — my team found something. In the source caverns. That's why we were really up here. Not mapping. The mapping was the cover story, and I suspect you already know that, because nobody pays triple rate for bathymetry."

Tam made a small sound. Mercer shifted his weight.

"We got into the upper caverns eleven days ago," Imani said. "Through a fissure in the east wall of the gorge, past a containment structure that isn't on any survey, any map, any record I've ever seen. Rust everywhere. Concrete spalling. Whatever it was built to hold, it's been failing for decades. And inside — past the containment grid, in the dry galleries where the water doesn't reach —"

She stopped. Not because she couldn't go on, but because she was choosing, deliberately, to make them wait for it, the way you make a student wait for the result of an experiment. Old habit. Teaching habit. She hated herself a little for it.

"There were bodies," she said. "Nine that we counted. Maybe more deeper in. Old ones. Decades old. Mummified in the dry air. And they were wearing navy dive gear."

* * *

The fire popped. Downstream, the river ran on, carrying its cargo of silt and secrets toward Rivergate and every mouth it fed.

"Navy gear," Coral repeated. Her voice had changed. Imani watched it happen — watched the salvage captain's practical surface develop a crack in it, and something older and more personal show through. "You're certain."

"I photographed them," Imani said. "Every one. I logged position, depth of deposit, associated artifacts. Diving helmets of a pattern that went out of service thirty-five years ago. Weight belts stamped with naval inventory numbers. One of them had a tag on his suit — a name tag, laminated, still legible." She swallowed. "Hollis. R. Hollis. Does that mean anything to you?"

Coral had gone very still. It was Mercer who answered, and his voice came out low and rough, like something dragged over gravel.

"Hollis ran point on the source dives," he said. "Back when. Before my time on the river, mostly. He was — " He stopped. Unfolded his arms. Folded them again. "He was the best diver anybody'd ever seen. They gave him a commendation. Posthumous. Said he drowned in a training exercise down at the coast base."

"He didn't drown at the coast base," Imani said. "He drowned forty meters inside a cavern the navy says doesn't exist, next to eight other men the navy also says don't exist, and the reason the cavern has a containment structure around it is that whatever the navy was doing at the source was worth nine lives to keep quiet, and still is. Because someone released that water last night, Captain. Someone watched our instruments, or watched our lights, or simply decided we'd seen enough, and they opened a gate and erased my team." Her voice finally broke properly, and she let it. "Mbeki was twenty-six. He was twenty-six years old and he was going to defend his dissertation in the spring and he liked terrible pop music and he slipped out of my hand because someone upstream turned a valve."

No one moved. The blanket had stopped helping; the shaking was coming from somewhere deeper than cold now.

It was Coral who reached out and took Imani's good hand — the right one, the unbroken one — and held it, hard, her grip steady as a cleat. Her eyes were bright and furious and wet all at once, and Imani thought: *she knows. She doesn't know yet, but she knows. Something in her life already touched this, and she can feel the shape of it.*

"Your data," Coral said. "The photographs. Where are they?"

"Gone," Imani said, and the word was a stone dropping. "The camera was in the camp. Everything was in the camp. The samples, the logs, the backup drives. The water took all of it. That's what a release does, Captain — it doesn't just kill you, it destroys the evidence of you. I've been trying to figure out all morning whether I survived because they missed me or because I'm not worth another surge." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "A controlled flood event timed to erase an unauthorized survey. It's elegant. It's deniable. It reads as seasonal flooding in every model. If I reported it, I'd sound insane. If I hadn't lived it, I wouldn't believe me."

"But you're going to report it anyway," Junie said. Not a question either. The girl was watching Imani with an expression of fierce, uncomplicated certainty, as though the answer were obvious.

"I'm going to do better than report it," Imani said. She sat up straighter, and her ribs screamed, and she ignored them. "Everything I put in the camera, I also put in here." She tapped her temple with her swollen left hand, wincing, not caring. "Nine bodies. Grid coordinates. The fissure entrance. The containment structure's construction style — postwar military concrete, I'd stake my career on it. The name Hollis. The timing of the release. Every number I have, I have twice, once in the water and once in my head, and the copy in my head doesn't drown." She looked around the circle of faces — the engineer, the deckhand, the old pilot, the captain — strangers ten days ago, the only people in the world now who knew she existed. "But data needs a witness to become testimony, and testimony needs someone to carry it somewhere it matters. My agency signed the cover story. I can't go back through channels. Channels are what killed my team."

"So where does it matter?" Tam asked quietly.

Imani looked upstream, into the gray slot of the gorge, where the water ran too fast and too full and the mist was climbing the canyon walls like something searching.

"Upstream," she said. "It matters upstream. At the source. That's where the proof lives — in the ground itself, in the rust and the bones and the chemistry of the water. You can flood a camp. You can't flood a cavern system without showing everyone what you're protecting." She turned back to Coral, and took a breath that cost her more than the climb out of the strainer had. "Your boat goes upstream anyway. I watched your compass man argue about the channel depths. I know you're not here for me."

Coral's jaw tightened. For a long moment she said nothing, and Imani watched her weigh it — watched the arithmetic run behind her eyes, debts and dangers and something else, something inherited, something that pointed wrong the way her father's compass pointed wrong.

"The water that took your team," Coral said at last, slowly, "came from upstream."

"Yes."

"And whoever released it will release it again."

"If we're on the river when they do," Imani said, "yes."

Mercer spat into the water, an old pilot's verdict on the whole conversation. "Then we'd best not be on the river," he said, "when they do."

But he was already turning toward the boat. They all were. That was the thing about rivers, Imani thought, as they lifted her — gently, competently, these strangers who owed her nothing — and carried her toward the *Rivergate and Compass*: rivers only ran one way, and so, once you were in the current, did everything else.

Behind them, far upstream, past the bend, past the slot, past the rusted gates of the world's most patient lie, the water kept rising.

Someone, somewhere, was still opening valves.
