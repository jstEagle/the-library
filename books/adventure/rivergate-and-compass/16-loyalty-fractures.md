## 16. Loyalty Fractures

Tam Voss had spent twenty years learning to read machines, and in all that time he had never once heard an engine lie. Engines told you the truth in vibration, in temperature, in the particular whine a bearing made three days before it seized. People were harder. People said *fine* when they meant *falling apart*, and they said *we should turn back* when what they meant was something else entirely.

Mercer had been saying *we should turn back* since dawn.

He stood at the stern now with his arms folded over his barrel chest, watching the river slide past the transom the way a man watches a door he's decided to stop trusting. The *Magpie* ran shallow and true through the braided channels below the drowned camp, her patched hull holding, her engine — Tam's engine, rebuilt twice on this trip alone — ticking along at the steady rhythm he'd coaxed out of her. Behind them, somewhere downriver, Commander Ashe's patrol boats were regrouping. Ahead of them, upstream, lay the source of everything: the wreck, the missing surveyors, the sealed case, Coral's father's compass, and now Dr. Imani Reyes, wrapped in every blanket Junie owned and shivering herself inside out in the wheelhouse.

"We could still make it," Mercer said. He didn't look at Tam. He looked at the water. "Downriver by dark tomorrow if we push. Hand the doctor to Ashe at the bend, tell him we found her floating, and go home."

"Tell him we found her floating," Tam repeated. Not arguing. Testing the weight of it, the way he'd test a weld.

"She was floating. Near enough." Mercer's jaw worked under his white stubble. "She was in a strainer, Tam. Half dead. Nobody would question it."

"Except she'd question it," Tam said. "And then she'd tell him about us. About the boat, the route, the village at the bend. You want to hand that woman to the same navy that's been running skiffs up a river they swear doesn't have anything on it?"

"I want us alive." Mercer turned then, and Tam saw something in the old pilot's face he'd only seen once before — the night two years ago when the *Magpie* had nearly gone down in the spring flood and Mercer had grabbed Tam by the collar and hauled him off a deck that was already underwater. Fear. Real fear, worn without apology. "I've been down this river my whole life and I've never seen water moved like that. Somebody opened a gate above the drowned camp. Deliberate. That's not a river anymore, boy. That's a weapon, and somebody's holding it, and we are sitting in a tin can in the middle of their firing range."

Tam didn't answer right away. He put his hand flat against the engine housing instead, feeling for the truth in the vibration. Number two cylinder running a hair hot. Nothing fatal. He let the silence stretch because he'd learned from Mercer himself that silence was a tool, and because he genuinely did not know which side of this he stood on yet.

* * *

The trouble with being caught between people, Tam thought later, was that both sides assumed your silence was agreement.

Coral found him in the engine bay an hour after they'd tied up in a willow-choked slough, deep enough off the main channel that the current barely whispered against the hull. She came down the ladder the way she always did — fast, like gravity owed her money — and crouched beside him where he sat with the cooling system open across his knees.

"You heard him," she said. It wasn't a question. The whole boat had heard him; Mercer's voice carried like weather.

"Heard him."

"And?"

Tam set down the wrench. This was the moment, and he knew it, and he hated that he knew it. There were conversations you could drift through and conversations that were load-bearing, and this one was going to hold up the whole rest of the trip no matter what he said.

"Coral, I'm the one who keeps this boat running. That's my whole job description. Keep the boat running so everybody else can do whatever brave thing they're doing." He wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than rag. "So I need you to tell me straight. If we turn back — if we take Reyes downriver and hand her over — what happens to her?"

Coral sat back on her heels. In the lamplight her face looked older than it had six days ago, before the tents standing empty and the instruments still recording and the mud with no footprints in it. Before Reyes, coughing black water into Junie's cupped hands.

"You know what happens," Coral said quietly. "Ashe doesn't want a witness. He wants a report. And reports get edited."

"That's a guess."

"It's a pattern. My father guessed the same way, thirty years ago, and look how that ended." She tapped the deck plating with one knuckle — the gesture she used when she meant the compass, stowed in the wheelhouse, pointing its wrong-pointing point toward the upper river. "Tam. I'm not asking you to be brave. I'm asking you to tell me if the boat can make the run upstream. Because if she can't, then this argument is over and Mercer wins by default."

That, at least, was a question he could answer honestly, and answering it honestly felt like the first solid thing he'd done all day.

"She can make it. Barely. I've got maybe forty hours of good running left in the impeller before I need parts I don't have, and the dry season's pulling the level down faster than the charts say it should." He held her eyes. "But Coral — barely isn't nothing. Barely means if anything else goes wrong, we're walking out. And Reyes can't walk."

"I know."

"And Mercer's not wrong about the gates. Whoever's moving that water knows we're here."

"I know that too." Coral exhaled through her nose, slow, controlled — the breath of a woman managing herself rather than the situation. "Then we're agreed. We go up."

"We're agreed that *you're* going up," Tam said. "Mercer isn't. And I haven't voted yet."

* * *

Junie found them both at supper, or rather supper found them — rice and tinned fish eaten in a silence so complete that the river itself seemed loud. Reyes slept in the wheelhouse bench, propped upright because lying flat started her coughing again. Every few minutes the cough came, wet and rattling, and every few minutes everyone pretended not to count the seconds between coughs.

It was Junie who broke first. She always broke first; it was practically her function on the crew, the way a fuse was the functional part of a circuit.

"This is stupid," she said. "We're all sitting here acting like nobody said the thing. Mercer said hand her over. Coral said no. And now we're eating fish like it's a normal Tuesday."

"Eat your fish," Mercer said.

"No." Junie put her fork down. She was the youngest of them, nineteen and all elbows and stubbornness, and she had the particular courage of someone who hadn't yet learned which fears were supposed to be bigger than her. "I pulled that woman out of the water. I felt her heart going like a bird's. And you want to hand her to the people who flooded her camp?"

"The navy didn't flood her camp," Mercer said. "Somebody upstream did. Could be the navy, could be the mining company, could be God punishing surveyors for trespassing. I don't know. That's exactly my point, girl. We don't know. And every hour we sit here not knowing, the water drops and Ashe gets closer and our options get smaller." He pushed his plate away. "I ran boats to the source when I was younger than you. I watched two men go into that water and not come out, and I kept my mouth shut about it for thirty years because keeping quiet was the price of coming home. I paid that price. I'm telling you the exchange rate hasn't changed."

"But you *did* come home," Junie said. "Those two didn't. Because somebody decided their lives were the price of something else. And now you're doing the deciding."

The words landed hard enough that Tam flinched. Mercer didn't. He just looked at Junie for a long moment, and something in his face went very old and very tired, and Tam understood suddenly that the old man wasn't angry — he was grieving in advance. Grieving the way engineers grieved: calculating the failure before it happened, mourning the machine while it still ran.

"You think I don't know that?" Mercer said softly. "You think I haven't done the arithmetic every night for thirty years? Two names, Junie. I say them to myself like a prayer I never finished. And I will carry any number of names it takes to keep the four of you off that list. That's the whole of it. That's all the loyalty I've got left, and I'm spending it on you."

Nobody answered that. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the willows a bird made a sound like a hinge.

* * *

Tam couldn't sleep, so he did what he always did when he couldn't sleep: he worked. By lantern light in the engine bay he stripped the raw-water intake and cleared the silt that the drowned camp's flood surge had packed into it, and he let his hands think while his head refused to.

The problem was that Mercer was right. That was the rotten core of it. Everything the old man said was true — the water was dropping, Ashe was closing, the options were shrinking, and turning back was the only plan on the table that ended with all four of them breathing. Tam had run the numbers a dozen times in his head and the numbers loved Mercer's plan. Fuel margin: adequate. Downstream transit: eighteen hours. Risk profile: bad but bounded.

And Coral was right too, which was worse. Because the numbers didn't include Reyes, and the numbers didn't include the two divers whose names Mercer prayed with, and the numbers didn't include the compass in the wheelhouse pointing upstream like a finger raised in accusation. Some things didn't fit in a fuel calculation. Tam had built his whole life around machines precisely because machines didn't ask him to hold those two truths at once.

Footsteps on the deck above. Then Mercer's boots on the ladder, and the old man folded himself into the cramped space across from him, knees up near his ears, too big for the room the way he was too big for most rooms.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Tam asked.

"Sleep's for men with clean hands." Mercer watched him work for a while. "You're going with her. Upstream."

"I haven't—"

"Don't insult me. I taught you engines, not poker." Mercer's voice wasn't bitter. It was resigned, which was somehow heavier. "I've known since supper. You've got the look. Same look your captain gets."

"She's not my captain because I follow her orders. She's my captain because—" Tam stopped, hands still in the machinery. "Because when my shop burned and my debts ate me alive, she took me onto a leaking boat and called it a job. You were there. You vouched for me. So whatever I owe her, I owe half of it to you, and I know that, and it's making this the worst week of my life."

Mercer grunted. For a long time the only sound was the lantern's faint hiss and the river moving past the hull, patient as erosion.

"Here's what I'll give you," the old man said finally. "I'll take us upstream. One more day, maybe two, as far as the canyon mouth. After that the channel gets too skinny for the *Magpie* anyway — dry season or no. So we're not really choosing to go forward. The river's choosing for us." He levered himself up with a groan, joints cracking like small-arms fire. "But hear me, Tam. If it comes to it — if it comes down to that woman's testimony or these four lives — I will put her off this boat myself. And I need to know you won't stand in front of me when I do."

Tam looked up at him. Lantern light, grease-blackened hands, an old man carved out of guilt and love in equal measure.

"I won't stand in front of you," Tam said. "But I'll be honest with you, Mercer — I don't know which way I'll move until it happens. That's the best I've got."

For the first time all day, Mercer smiled. It was a terrible smile, thin and sad, but real.

"Honest," he said. "Good. That's rarer than bravery on this river." He climbed the ladder, paused at the top. "Get some sleep, boy. Tomorrow we run the canyon."

* * *

They cast off before first light, and the river met them gray and low, the sandbars rising out of the current like the backs of sleeping animals. Tam stood his watch in the engine bay with the hatch cracked so he could hear the wheelhouse, and through that crack he listened to Coral and Mercer navigate together — depth calls and channel calls, terse and precise, two people who disagreed about everything except the work. It was almost worse than shouting. Shouting would have meant the fracture was clean. This was a fracture under load, hairline, invisible, waiting for the one impact too many.

Reyes surfaced from her fever long enough to drink broth and say, in a voice like gravel underwater: "There were bodies. In the caverns. Old ones. Navy gear." Then she went under again, and nobody spoke, and the compass in the wheelhouse kept its patient, impossible pointing.

Near midday the banks began to close in. Sandstone rose on either side, striped red and cream, and the channel narrowed until Tam could have spat from the gunwale to either shore. The engine note changed — shallower water, colder air off the stone — and he adjusted the mixture by feel, eyes closed, listening.

That was why he heard it first. Not the sound itself, but the absence: the river ahead going quiet, the particular hush of water backing up behind something.

He stuck his head up through the hatch. "Coral. Kill the engine."

She was already reaching for the throttle. The *Magpie* glided to a stop in water so still it reflected the canyon walls perfectly, and ahead of them, wedged across the entire channel, lay a boom of logs and cable — fresh-cut timber, bright wounds of sawdust still pale on the ends, dragged into place within the last day. A barrier. A roadblock. Someone had known exactly where the river pinched, and exactly when they'd arrive.

From the rim of the canyon, high above, a voice came down through a megaphone, distorted and cheerful:

"*Magpie*. This is Commander Ashe. We should talk."
