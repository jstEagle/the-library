## 17. The Compass True North

The canyon swallowed them by degrees.

First the walls narrowed enough that Tam could touch both sides from the gunwales, his fingertips trailing the stone like a man reading braille. Then the sky became a ribbon, blue-white and indifferent, and the light went the color of old glass — green-gold, filtered through water that had been falling for a thousand years in some seep above them they couldn't see, only hear: a thin continuous whisper, like the canyon breathing.

Coral stood at the bow with the compass open in her palm.

It had been doing it again. She'd felt it before she'd looked — that faint tug at her attention, the way you feel someone watching you across a crowded dock. For three days since leaving the village at the bend, since Sela had come aboard with her rolled charts and her careful silences, the needle had been drifting. Not spinning, not trembling the way cheap compasses do when the world's iron gets into them. Drifting. Deliberate. As if it were being *led*.

She held it flat now, level as a spirit gauge, and watched the needle swing slowly off north — off true north, off magnetic north, off every north she'd ever steered by — and settle toward the canyon wall to port, toward the black seam of shadow where the rock folded inward on itself.

"Still doing it?" Sela came up beside her, quiet-footed even on a deck littered with line and chain. She carried two mugs of the terrible coffee Junie brewed, and handed one over without asking. That was another thing Coral was still getting used to — being handed things without having to ask.

"Still doing it." Coral didn't look up. "It's been pointing at that wall for an hour."

"Rocks don't have north."

"No," Coral said. "But my father's compass never cared much about what things were supposed to have."

Sela drank her coffee and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. She'd been doing that more lately — filling silence instead of breaking it, standing close instead of apart. Coral found she didn't mind. Found, in fact, that she minded the opposite: the hours Sela spent below with Reyes's data, or up on the rocks taking readings, left a shape in the day that nothing else filled. It was inconvenient. It was also, she suspected, the truest instrument reading she'd gotten in years.

* * *

They made camp that night in a slot canyon so narrow the stars came down in single file.

Tam rigged the boat against a shelf of stone with lines doubled and tripled; Mercer would have called it belaying a boat, and Mercer would have hated how much sense it made. The fire was small — everything here was small except the walls — and Junie cooked river fish wrapped in clay from the bank, the way the villagers at the bend had shown her. The smell of it rose into the slot and got trapped there, woodsmoke and hot earth, and for a little while nobody talked about Ashe, or helicopters, or the fact that Mercer's blood was still dried into the deck planking no matter how many times Junie scrubbed.

Reyes slept propped against the transom, feverish but past the worst of it, Imani's decoded pages spread over her knees even in sleep, as if the numbers might keep her warm.

Coral took first watch because she wanted it. Because there was something she needed to do alone.

She climbed out of the camp, up the talus slope where the slot widened into a chamber — not quite a room, not quite a throat of the canyon, but a place where the walls fell back and the river pooled deep and still, black water holding the last gray light like a cup holds wine. Her father had brought her to places like this once. Before Rivergate, before the debts, before he went upstream one season and the river gave him back as a name on a memorial plaque she couldn't afford to visit. He'd taught her to read water in pools like this one. *The river tells you what it's hiding,* he used to say. *You just have to stand still long enough to be told.*

She sat on a ledge above the pool and opened the compass.

The needle swung. Settled. Not north. Not toward the source they were walking toward, either — and that was the thing that had been working at her for days, the splinter under the skin of it. If the compass pointed at the forbidden place, at the caverns, at the heart of whatever the navy had drowned thirty years of men to protect, that would be one kind of story. A story about the mission. About the case, the charts, the cover-up, all the machinery of other people's sins.

But the needle wasn't pointing upstream.

It was pointing *across* the river. Into the wall.

Coral turned slowly, holding the compass level, letting the needle speak. And the needle kept speaking — steady now, steadier than she had ever seen it, no drift, no tremble, fixed on a point in the rock face across the pool where the waterline cut a horizontal scar into the stone. An old waterline. A high one, from some season decades gone when this canyon ran full and violent.

And below that scar, half-drowned in shadow, she could see it: a ring of rusted iron set into the rock. An eye bolt. A mooring point.

Divers' hardware.

Her breath went strange in her chest. She stood, and the needle didn't waver — she turned full circle around the chamber and it stayed locked on that bolt like a hound on a scent, and when she moved toward it, it stayed true, and when she stopped, it stopped, and there was no magnetism in the world she knew that behaved like this. This wasn't finding north. This was remembering.

*He marked it,* she thought. *He marked it somehow, and the compass has been trying to bring me here ever since.*

All those years on the mantel in Mercer's keeping, then in hers — all those years she'd assumed the thing was simply broken, sentimentally broken, the way grief breaks people. Pointing wrong. And it had never been wrong. It had been waiting. Every degree of drift, every wrong turn it had begged her to take, had been a sentence in a language she hadn't learned until now: *come here. Come here. Come here.*

"Coral." Sela's voice, low, from the base of the talus. "Your watch ended twenty minutes ago. I was going to let you have it, but—" She stopped. She'd followed Coral's gaze to the bolt in the wall, and Coral watched her face change, the hydrologist coming forward behind her eyes. "That's a dive anchor. That's pre-war salvage pattern. What is this place?"

"I think," Coral said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, "it's a dive site. My father's."

* * *

They woke Tam at midnight, which told him everything before anyone said a word.

He came up the talus with his tool roll already in hand, because Tam understood that when Coral woke people at midnight it was never for conversation. Junie followed, bleary and silent, and Imani stirred but Reyes slept on, and the four of them stood at the lip of the black pool while Coral showed them the compass and the bolt and the scar of the old waterline.

"There's a cave behind that wall," Tam said. He said it flatly, the way he stated facts about engines. "Feel the air. There's a draft coming off that seam — cold, steady. Moving air means moving water means a way through."

"You can tell all that from a draft?"

"I can tell all that from thirty years of listening to Mercer complain about drafts." Tam knelt at the water's edge, laid his palm flat on the stone near the seam. "There. You feel it? The whole wall is breathing. Somewhere behind there is a space, and somewhere behind that is the river's real channel, and I would bet the boat that your father knew it."

Coral crouched beside him. The draft touched her fingers like something alive — cool, patient, ancient. The breath of a place that had been holding its secret longer than any of them had been alive.

"He dove here," she said. "Before the source. Before whatever happened to him. This was practice, or preparation, or—" She stopped, because the word arrived and she had to say it. "*Or a message.*"

Junie hugged her knees. "A message in a wall?"

"People leave messages in the last places they can reach," Sela said quietly. Everyone looked at her. She was staring at the bolt, at the rust bleeding down the stone beneath it like old tears. "I've read enough expedition logs to know. When you can't carry the truth out, you put it where only someone who understands will find it. Where only someone with your training, your gear, your—" she glanced at Coral, "—your blood will come looking."

"The compass," Coral said. "It's the key. He keyed it to this site somehow — magnetized it, anchored it to something in the rock, I don't know how, I don't care how. He built me a trail. Thirty years long. And it starts here."

Nobody spoke. The draft breathed. Somewhere far above, the ribbon of stars turned.

Then Tam, practical as gravity: "Water's cold enough to kill in forty minutes if you cramp. We've got two working regulators, three tanks, and one of them's suspect. Who's diving?"

"Me," Coral said.

"Obviously," Tam said. "I meant who else."

"I am," Sela said.

Coral turned. "You're a hydrologist."

"I'm a hydrologist who grew up on these rivers and did my field certification in cold-water systems." Sela met her eyes, and in the firelight Coral saw again the thing she kept seeing — the exile's steadiness, the woman who had lost everything once already for the truth and had come back anyway. "You shouldn't dive alone into an uncharted space your dead father marked. And I'm not going to sit on a rock and wait to find out what happened to you. So. I'm diving."

There was nothing to say to that that wouldn't take too long, so Coral just nodded, and Sela nodded back, and that was the whole conversation, and it was the most important one Coral had had in years.

* * *

Dawn came down the slot in pieces.

By the time the light reached the pool, the gear was staged: tanks lined along the ledge, regulators checked twice, Tam's dive light lashed to Coral's harness and a spare clipped to Sela's belt. Junie had braided Coral's hair back tight without being asked, the way you prepare someone you might lose, and had pressed something small into her palm — a river stone from the bend, smooth and white. "For your pocket," Junie said. "Village rule. You carry a piece of home so the water knows where to give you back."

Coral put it in her pocket next to the compass, which she would not leave behind, which she wore now on a lanyard inside her suit like a second heartbeat.

At the edge, she paused. The pool lay black and perfect, and in its surface she caught her own reflection, and behind it, ghosted in the dawn-gray stone, the rusted bolt, the scar of the old waterline, the seam in the world.

*Dad,* she thought. *Thirty years, and you're still teaching me to read water.*

She spat in her mask, rinsed it, settled it on her face. The world went green and framed. Beside her, Sela's regulator hissed once, twice, testing.

"On three," Coral said around her mouthpiece, though neither of them needed counting. Some descents you make together or not at all.

They went down.

The cold hit first — a fist closing around her chest, squeezing the breath she'd brought with her into something small and precious. Then the dark, total, until Tam's light cut it open and revealed the underwater seam: a fissure in the canyon wall, wide enough for a body, breathing its slow cold current outward. The compass inside her suit pressed against her sternum, and through neoprene and flesh she would have sworn she felt it — the needle swinging, settling, pointing *in*.

They swam into the mountain.

The fissure narrowed, widened, narrowed again. The light played over stone scored with tool marks — old ones, deliberate ones — and then, forty feet in, where the passage kinked and rose, the beam found air: a pocket, a chamber above the waterline, dry, black, untouched. Coral broke the surface gasping, hauled herself onto a shelf of rock, and pulled the mask up, and Sela surfaced beside her coughing, laughing, alive.

Tam's light swept the chamber.

And stopped.

Along the far wall, sealed under a slab of stone and coated in river wax, waterproofed with a diver's meticulous patience, was a bundle. And on the slab, scratched deep into the rock with a dive knife, in a hand Coral knew better than her own — she'd seen it on every scrap of paper Mercer had saved, every margin note, every chart annotation —

*FOR C.V. WHEN THE COMPASS COMES HOME.*

Coral's father's initials for her. Coral Voss. Written thirty years ago, in the dark, by a man who knew he might never surface, addressed to a daughter who was eleven years old when he wrote it.

The compass hung against her chest, needle steady, pointing not north, not anymore, never again north — pointing here, pointing home, pointing at the words of a dead man waiting in the dark for his daughter to arrive exactly on time.

Coral reached out with shaking hands and began, gently, to break the seal.

Behind them, far down the passage, the water shivered — a deep, rolling concussion, felt more than heard, like the mountain clearing its throat. Sela's head came up sharply.

"That wasn't us," she whispered. "Something's moving upstream."

Coral's hands kept working. Whatever was coming, whoever was coming — her father had waited thirty years.

He could hold five more minutes.
