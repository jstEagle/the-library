## 18. Sela's Confession

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time the village went quiet. Sela sat apart from it, the way she always did now — close enough to feel its warmth on her left side, far enough that the light didn't reach her face. Old habit. In another life she had stood in lecture halls and conference rooms and government briefing chambers with every light in the room on her, and she had learned that visibility was a currency, and currencies could be seized.

Tonight the light belonged to Coral Voss.

The diver sat cross-legged near the flames with her father's compass open in her palm, turning it over and over as though the answer were engraved somewhere on the brass housing she hadn't yet found. She'd been doing that for three nights. Sela recognized the gesture the way you recognize a limp — because you've walked with one yourself. It was the posture of a woman holding an inheritance she didn't yet understand, asking it questions in the only language grief allowed: repetition.

Across the compound, Junie was asleep sitting up against a stack of net crates, mouth open, one hand still curled around a coil of line she'd refused to put down even unconscious. Tam had gone to check the boat's seams for the fourth time since dusk. Mercer's absence was a shape in the firelight — he was upriver with the village elders, trading stories Sela suspected were also confessions, the two being nearly the same thing among people who lived off water.

And Coral was awake. Of course she was awake.

Sela rose, wrapped her shawl against the river damp, and crossed the packed earth between the cook shelter and the fire. Coral looked up when her shadow fell across the flames, and something in her face — wary, tired, hopeful in a way she clearly resented — made Sela's chest tighten in a manner she had not budgeted for.

"You keep looking at me like I'm going to disappear," Coral said.

"I keep looking at you like someone who is about to make a very large mistake," Sela said, "and I'm trying to decide whether to let you."

"That's not ominous at all." But Coral shifted, making room on the log beside her, and Sela sat.

For a while neither of them spoke. The river spoke instead — it never stopped speaking here, this close to the bend where the current gathered itself for the run into the narrows. Sela had spent eleven years listening to rivers talk, first as a student, then as a scientist, then as a woman reading contaminated sample results by lamplight in a house that would be searched before the month was out. Rivers said everything if you knew their grammar. This one was saying *hurry*.

"Your compass," Sela said. "May I?"

Coral hesitated — Sela watched the hesitation move through her like weather — and then set it in her open hand.

It was lighter than it looked. Brass worn to gold at the pivot points, the crystal scratched in long arcs from years inside a dive bag. The needle swung as Sela tilted it, and there it was again, that wrongness she'd felt the first night she'd seen it: not north. Not magnetic anything. The needle leaned upstream, patient and insistent, toward the canyon country where the river came from, toward the source, as if the instrument had somewhere to be and had been waiting thirty years for someone to follow it.

"Where did he get it?" Sela asked, though she knew. She had known since the moment Coral first unwrapped it on the dock at the bend and Sela's whole body had gone cold.

"My father. It was his. He was a salvage diver on this stretch of river my whole childhood." Coral's voice flattened carefully, the way voices do around wounds they've decided not to look at directly. "He drowned eight years ago. Officially. A routine dive, a snagged line, bad luck. That's what the report said." Her jaw worked. "You've seen it before. Haven't you. You knew him."

Sela turned the compass so the firelight caught the back of the housing. There, beneath decades of tarnish, a small stamped mark: a stylized wave over three vertical bars. The insignia of the Rivergate Hydrographic Cooperative — the civilian survey outfit that had contracted with her institute, once upon a time, before everything.

"His name was Elias," Sela said. "Elias Voss. He dove for the cooperative in the years when I was publishing what I shouldn't have published. And yes, Coral. I knew him. He carried this compass on every dive I ever saw him make."

Coral had gone very still. Stillness, Sela was learning, was this woman's version of screaming.

"Tell me," Coral said quietly. "All of it. Whatever it is you've been not-telling me since we got here."

* * *

Sela looked at the fire for a long time. Then she began, because there was no version of the coming days in which Coral survived them without knowing, and no version of Sela herself that could live with sending her into the dark unlit.

"I was a hydrologist with the Regional Water Institute. Twenty-two years ago, I was twenty-six, and I believed in institutions the way you believe in a rope — you don't think about it until it holds or it doesn't. My assignment was baseline water quality for the upper basin. Routine. Nobody was supposed to care about the headwaters; the money and the attention were all downstream, in the cities, in the ports." She paused. "Except somebody did care. Somebody cared enough that my access requests to the source tributary were denied four times, each denial citing a different agency, each citation false."

"The navy," Coral said.

"The navy, the minerals directorate, the interior ministry — whoever owned the secret that month. So I did what young scientists do. I got creative. I sampled tributaries downstream of the confluence and ran backward models. And the numbers made no sense. Heavy metals in concentrations that couldn't come from any geology in the basin. Solvents that hadn't been manufactured in this country in forty years. An aquifer under the whole eastern valley carrying a chemical signature like a fingerprint — and the fingerprint matched nothing on any map, because whatever was producing it wasn't supposed to exist."

"And it did exist."

"There was an operation at the source." Sela heard her own voice go flat and distant, the voice of testimony rather than memory. "Mining. Not small-scale. Containment ponds, processing runoff, the whole apparatus of extraction hidden inside a canyon the maps showed as blank. They'd been running it since before I was born. The divers — your father's generation of divers — weren't salvaging wrecks, Coral. Some of them were maintaining the containment structures. Sealing them. Keeping the poison in its grave. And when the structures failed, as structures do, they sent men down to fix them without telling the men what they were fixing, or why, or what the water would do to them over ten years, fifteen, twenty."

Coral's hand had closed around her own wrist, hard, white-knuckled. Sela wanted to reach over and loosen it and didn't dare.

"I published," Sela said simply. "A full paper. Data, methodology, maps. I thought that was how it worked — you found the truth, you gave it to the light, and the light did the rest. The journal pulled it in nine days. My institute revoked my field access in eleven. By the end of the season I was formally expelled, my credentials flagged, my name attached to a file that followed me to two countries before I stopped running and came here, to the people who already knew, who had always known, who drank the water and buried their dead and were called superstitious for it." She gestured at the darkened village around them, the low roofs, the drying nets. "Exile isn't dramatic, Coral. It's just a door closing behind you that doesn't open again. You stop being a scientist and become a rumor with a sampling kit."

"What happened to the operation? Did it shut down?"

"No. It went deeper. Quieter. And the containment started failing faster, because nothing built in secret gets maintained in daylight." Sela finally turned to face her fully. "Which brings us to your father."

* * *

"Elias came to find me seven months after the expulsion," Sela said. "He tracked me through three villages, which should have been impossible, and when I asked him how, he held up that compass and said, *'It doesn't point north anymore. Started pointing somewhere else the year after my worst dive. I figured whatever it's pointing at, somebody wants it found.'*" She smiled despite herself, despite everything. "He was like that. He treated mysteries as invitations. It was infuriating. It was why everyone loved him."

Coral made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been broken on this particular subject. "That sounds like him. That's — yeah. That's him."

"He'd read my paper before it vanished. Kept a copy. Divers keep copies of things; paper survives water better than reputations do. He told me his crew had been diving maintenance runs on structures they'd been told were old bridge footings. Told me about the seals, the rust, the water taste that lingered in your mouth for days. Told me two of his crewmates had died on those runs — officially drownings, officially accidents — and that both men had been sick for months beforehand, sick in ways drowning doesn't explain." Sela's voice caught, and she let it. "He wanted to finish my work. Finish it properly. Get inside the source, document the containment grid, bring out samples and photographs that no ministry could unpublish. He had the skills. He had the compass, which by then he suspected was more than brass and bad bearings. What he didn't have was a map of the upper tributary, because I'd never gotten one either."

"So you made one together."

"We tried. For two years. Every approach failed — permits, patrols, the river itself. The dry seasons kept coming early; the water levels dropped past anything navigable. And then—" Sela stopped. The fire popped. Somewhere across the compound a dog stirred and settled.

"Then he dove anyway," Coral said. Not a question. Her eyes were fixed on the compass in Sela's hands, and her voice had the terrible steadiness of someone walking toward a cliff edge on purpose. "Eight years ago. The routine dive. The snagged line."

"He went alone," Sela said. "I begged him not to. I told him we needed a team, a season, a plan. He said the containment wouldn't wait for our plan — he'd seen the seepage numbers, he'd taken the samples himself from the outflow, and the curve was bending the wrong way. He said if he waited, there'd be nothing left to document but the flood." She swallowed. "He marked a site on his charts. A place where he believed the original survey records were cached — physical records, sealed against water, the kind of evidence that can't be deleted or discredited. He told me if he didn't come back, someone with the compass would come looking eventually. He said the compass would know the way home."

"Home," Coral repeated. "Not north. Home."

"Your father had a theory about it. That he'd magnetized it himself, deliberately, on his last refit — that he'd bent the needle toward the thing that mattered most, so that no matter how lost he got, it would pull him back to the work." Sela closed her hands around the instrument and held it out. "I think he was right. I think he made sure that whoever inherited his grief would also inherit his direction."

Coral took the compass back. She held it the way you hold a letter from the dead, and for a long moment the firelight moved on her face and she didn't blink, and Sela understood that she was watching a woman's entire understanding of her own history rearrange itself — the drowning reclassified from accident to cost, the orphanhood reclassified from bad luck to consequence, the debt and the drifting and the desperate salvage contracts all revealed as the wake of a single deliberate act committed by a man who chose the river's truth over coming home.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Coral asked finally. Her voice was rough. "You've had days."

"Because tomorrow you'll be close enough to see the containment towers from the water," Sela said. "Because Commander Ashe's patrol skiffs were spotted at the narrows this afternoon, which means the window for choosing freely is closing. And because—" she paused, and the honest thing arrived in her mouth before the prudent thing could stop it, "—because I have watched you for six days, Coral Voss, and I am beginning to care what happens to you, and caring makes me a coward about the things you need to hear. So I said them while I could still say them plainly."

Coral looked at her. The fire was low; the shadows did most of the talking. When Coral spoke, her voice was quieter than the river.

"You loved him."

"I loved what he was trying to do, and I loved the man who wouldn't stop trying. Whether that was love—" Sela shook her head. "It was twenty-two years ago and it was mostly fear and purpose wearing love's clothes. What I feel watching you argue with a compass at midnight is a different animal entirely, and I'm too old to pretend otherwise and too sensible to name it tonight."

"Don't name it," Coral said. But she didn't move away. Their shoulders were almost touching, and the heat between the fire and the shawl was warmer than either alone.

* * *

They sat until the coals grayed. At some point Coral's head tipped sideways, not quite resting on Sela's shoulder but occupying the same airspace, and Sela let the contact happen without acknowledging it, the way you hold a wild bird — stillness first, meaning later.

"Coral," she said at last. "There's something else. The part I've been circling."

"I figured."

"Your father's last message to me — a note passed through a fish trader, six days before he dove — said he'd found the cache location. He was certain. He said the records were in the containment grid itself, in the oldest structure, the one the builders used as a reference vault. And he said one more thing." Sela made herself say it. "He wrote: *'If the water starts moving before I'm back, it means they're flushing the system. Don't come upstream. Come downstream, and warn the bend.'*"

Coral lifted her head. "Flushing. Like the flooded camp. Like Reyes's team."

"Like a system being purged ahead of an inspection nobody official will ever admit is happening," Sela said. "Whoever controls the release gates has known for weeks that Ashe's search would push this far. Your surveyors weren't lost, Coral. They were flushed. And if the pattern holds, the next release will be bigger, timed to the dry-season lows, and it won't stop at a camp." She stood, pulling the shawl tight, and looked upriver into the blackness where the canyon walls would be invisible even by day. "Everything your father tried to warn me about is happening on schedule. Which means the choice you came here to make — sell the evidence, leak it, give it away — may already be smaller than you think. Because there may be no evidence left to bargain with unless we reach the source before the water does."

Down by the bank, a lantern came alive. Tam's silhouette, moving fast along the deck of the boat, and then Junie's voice cutting sharp across the compound — *"Sela! Coral! You need to see this"* — and beyond her, out on the black water, the sound every river child learned before they learned letters: engines. More than one. Running quiet, running slow, coming around the bend from the narrows with their lights killed.

Coral was on her feet with the compass in her fist, and Sela saw the grief in her face fold itself away into something harder and older, something that looked, for one uncanny second, exactly like her father.

"They're early," Coral said.

"They're not early," Sela said, reaching for her kit. "We're late. Run."
