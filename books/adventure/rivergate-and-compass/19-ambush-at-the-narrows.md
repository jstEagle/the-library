## 19. Ambush at the Narrows

Mercer had run the Narrows forty years ago with a hold full of diesel and a mouth full of lies, and he had promised himself then that if the river ever gave him back to this stretch of water, it would be as a different man. The river, being a river, had waited him out. It was patient in the way only old things are patient — stone patient, silt patient — and now here he came again, up the throat of the canyon with three young people and a dying surveyor aboard, running toward the one place on earth he'd sworn never to see twice.

He stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Grace* with his hands light on the spokes and let the boat tell him what it thought. She was a good hull. He'd built her frame by frame the winter after his wife passed, when grief needed somewhere to go that wasn't a bottle, and she had never once lied to him about the water. Boats didn't lie. Men did. That was the whole trouble with men.

The canyon closed around them like a held breath. Walls of red sandstone rose sheer on either side, streaked black where thirty years of seep had painted them, and the river ran narrow and fast between them, green-black in the shadowed stretches, glittering where the morning found gaps in the rimrock overhead. The dry season had dropped her maybe two feet since they'd left Rivergate, and he could read the drop in every eddy line, every exposed root, every rock that should have been drowned and wasn't. The river was getting thinner and meaner by the day, like an old dog guarding a bone it no longer had strength for.

"Water's down another six inches since yesterday," Tam said, crouched at the depth sounder with his sleeves rolled and his forearms scarred from a lifetime of engines. "We're drawing close on four feet loaded."

"I know what we're drawing." Mercer didn't mean to snap. He did it anyway, and saw the boy take it without flinching, which was worse somehow. Tam had learned not to argue with him on the water, and Mercer hated that he'd taught him that. "Keep your ears on the engine. I want to know if she so much as clears her throat."

Behind them, under the canvas aft, Coral sat with Sela bent over the charts, and Junie kept watch with binoculars, and Dr. Reyes lay wrapped in every blanket they owned, breathing shallow but steady. Five souls. Six counting the boat, and Mercer counted the boat. You always counted the boat.

The Narrows proper began where the walls pinched to less than a boat-length apart, and the current through there ran better than eight knots even in a fat year. In this year, with the water low and the channel braided around hidden rock, it would be a threading job — throttle, rudder, and prayer, in that order. He'd done it once at nineteen with his father hollering at him from the bow, and he remembered every second of it, the way you remember the moments that nearly killed you: the smell of wet stone, the white knuckles, the way the whole world narrowed to the next ten feet of moving water.

He was reading the approach current, looking for the seam that would carry them clean into the slot, when he felt it before he heard it — a wrongness in the air, the particular silence of a place where birdsong has stopped.

He'd felt that same silence once before, thirty years ago, two miles below the source, the morning the divers went down and didn't come up.

"Junie," he said quietly. "Kill the binoculars and get low. Tell the others."

She looked at him, started to ask, and then — good girl — didn't. She slid back under the canvas, and Mercer heard the low murmur of voices, Coral's rising sharp and then going flat and controlled, the voice she used when fear got converted into orders.

Then the sound came. An engine. Not one — two, high and hard, the whine of outboards run wide open, coming from behind the downstream bend they'd already cleared, which meant they'd been waiting in the side cut below the Narrows and had doubled back, which meant planning, which meant Ashe hadn't sent a patrol skiff after them at all.

Ashe had sent a trap.

* * *

The first bullet took the rail six inches from Junie's hand.

Mercer heard the crack of it roll down the canyon like a door slamming in a cathedral, and then the second shot came before the echo died, punching through the cabin wall and out the other side, and glass sprayed across the deck where the chart window used to be. The skiffs came around the bend stacked wide, gunwale to gunwale, three of them, navy gray with the numbers painted over, men in the bows with rifles braced against the windshields.

There was nowhere to go. That was the arithmetic of the Narrows, and the men in those boats knew it the way Mercer knew it: the canyon ahead was a quarter mile of unrunnable slot, the canyon behind was full of guns, and the banks were vertical stone. They had built the box and driven their quarry into it, and all that remained was the closing.

"Coral!" Mercer bellowed, and his voice surprised him with its strength, the old river-holler coming up out of his chest like something alive. "Side channel! Starboard bank, two hundred yards — there's a cut behind the fallen slab!"

"There's nothing charted there—" Tam shouted back.

"There's nothing *charted* anywhere up this river, boy, that's the entire point!" Mercer hauled the wheel over and the *Grace* leaned into the turn, her hull groaning, the current grabbing her stern and trying to swing her broadside into the main channel where the skiffs could rake her length. Bullets walked across the water toward them, little geysers stitching closer, and Mercer felt each impact in his teeth before he heard it.

Coral came forward at a crouch, Sela behind her with Reyes half in her arms, and Coral's face was the face he'd seen on her father thirty years ago standing on this same water — set, furious, unafraid in the specific way of people who have decided that fear is a luxury. "That cut is a dead end, Mercer."

"It's a dead end they don't know about." He threw the wheel hard starboard as a round clipped the exhaust stack with a sound like a struck bell. "My father showed me. Nobody else alive knows it's there. Not even your compass knows it's there."

"That's not comforting—"

"It wasn't meant to be. Get everyone down behind the engine block. Tam — when I say, you give me everything she has."

The cut appeared exactly as memory had drawn it: a seam in the south wall where a house-sized slab of sandstone had calved off centuries ago and come to rest leaning against the cliff, leaving behind it a gap — not a channel, really, more a suggestion of one, screened by deadfall and willow and the accumulated litter of forty floods. At normal water it would have been suicide. At this water, with the river two feet down and the gravel bar exposed, it was merely impossible.

Which made it, Mercer reflected, the best option on the table.

"They're fanning out," Junie called, voice cracking but level. "Two coming along the north wall, one holding the middle."

"Hold on to something," Mercer said, and gave Tam the word.

Tam opened the throttle and the *Grace* surged, and Mercer steered her straight at the willows.

* * *

There is a moment in every salvage man's life — and Mercer had been a salvage man longer than he'd been anything, longer than husband, longer than pilot, longer than honest — when the thing you committed to stops being a decision and becomes simply the next fact of the world. He hit the willows in that moment. Branches raked the cabin roof like fingernails, the hull ground over submerged gravel with a shriek he felt in his spine, and then the gap swallowed them and the gunfire went suddenly strange and echoic, bouncing off the slab above, and they were through.

The cut behind the slab was everything his father had promised and less: sixty yards of slow backwater, deep enough, barely, walled in by stone and deadfall, dark as a cellar. Mercer brought the boat in dead slow and let her drift to rest against the inner bank, engine ticking, everyone flat on the deck, breath held like children playing at hiding.

Outside, the river went on talking. The skiffs circled. Voices carried over the water, distorted by the canyon — orders, short and clipped. A rifle shot, deliberate, into the deadfall near the entrance. Then another. Probing. Patient.

They were going to search the cut. Of course they were. It might take twenty minutes or it might take two hours, but men who had built a box this careful were not going to leave one corner of it unexamined, and when they found the *Grace* wedged in this stone pocket like a coin in a couch, they would find five people with nowhere left to run.

Mercer sat down slowly on the engine housing and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Rivergate.

Somebody had to be on the outside of this when the searching started. Somebody had to draw them — make noise, make wake, make themselves a target worth chasing — so the others could slip out the far end of the cut, where the deadfall thinned and a scramble of scree led up and over the shoulder of the canyon to the water beyond the Narrows. It was a bad plan. It was the only plan. And it required a decoy who knew these waters well enough to run a skiff alone through broken channels at speed, because a decoy who wrecked in the first hundred yards bought nobody anything.

There was exactly one person aboard who fit.

He looked at them while the voices outside grew methodical. Coral, kneeling by Reyes, one hand on the doctor's forehead, listening to Sela's low report with her jaw tight. Tam, hands moving over the engine without being asked, keeping her ready, because that was how Tam loved things — with a wrench. Junie, sixteen years old and watching the entrance with a boathook gripped like a spear, too young for this, too brave for it, both at once. Sela, steady, foreign to this river and loyal to it anyway. And Coral's eyes came up and met his, and he knew — God help him, he knew she was doing the same arithmetic, and arriving at the same answer, and preparing to argue him out of it, and he had perhaps ninety seconds before she won.

Old sins, he thought. Thirty years ago he had run supply boats up this river for men in pressed shirts, and he had carried crates he didn't ask about, and when the divers drowned he had signed the paper they put in front of him and taken the money and gone home to Rivergate and told himself for three decades that a man can't be guilty of what he didn't look at. Two men. Divers, like him. Like Coral's father. Their names were in his logbook and their blood was on his silence, and every year the interest compounded, and here, at last, was the payment notice.

"Coral," he said. "Come here a minute. Bring the logbook."

She came. Suspicious — she was her father's daughter — but she came, and he put his hand on the worn leather cover and felt the weight of everything inside it, thirty years of coordinates and confessions, the map to every grave this river held.

"When we get clear," he said, "you read it. All of it. Even the parts that shame me. Especially those."

"Mercer—"

"The far end of this cut, the deadfall thins and there's scree going up to the bench above the Narrows. Take the boat through on foot lines, haul her over, launch below. Tam can rig the lines. Junie knows the hand signals." He was talking fast now, laying it all down like ballast, and her eyes had gone wide and hard because she was ahead of him, she was already ahead of him. "I'm going to take the skiff they left us no choice about — the dinghy, with the little outboard — and I'm going to go make a great deal of noise heading downstream."

"No." Flat. Immediate. "Absolutely not. We do this together or—"

"Together is how they catch all of us." He smiled at her, and was startled to find the smile cost nothing; it had been waiting a long time to be spent. "Girl, I'm seventy-one years old. I've been running from this river my whole life. Let me finally run *toward* it."

"You are not—" Her voice broke, and he saw her do the thing she hated most, which was feel something in front of witnesses. "You're the only one who knows these waters. You're the only one who can get us to the source."

"And I'll get you to the head of the Narrows first, and after that the river does the navigating, and the river and I have an understanding." He took her hand and pressed the logbook into it and closed her fingers over it, and his hands were shaking and hers were shaking and neither of them mentioned it. "Your daddy trusted me with his life once, and I failed the trust. I don't get to fail it twice. That's not heroism, Coral. That's bookkeeping."

Outside, a skiff's engine revved, close, turning toward the cut. A voice called out — *"Check the side water"* — and time collapsed to nothing.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way the important things always did, leaving no room for ceremony.

Mercer got the dinghy over the side with Tam's help, and Tam understood — Tam always understood, that was his curse — and said nothing, only gripped Mercer's forearm hard enough to bruise and then turned away to work, because grief with a wrench in its hand is still useful grief. Junie kissed his cheek, quick and fierce, salt on his skin. Sela squeezed his shoulder and said something in her own language that he didn't know but understood completely.

Coral held on longest. "You come downriver to us," she said into his ear. "You hear me? You come downriver. We'll be at the bend village. You come downriver, old man, or I swear I'll dive down and drag you myself."

"Wouldn't put it past you," he said, and pushed the little boat off, and started the outboard, and opened it up.

He went downstream loud. Louder than any seventy-one-year-old man had a right to be — throttle wide, cutting wakes across the main channel, kicking up spray in the morning sun like a boy showing off, and it worked, it worked beautifully, both skiffs swung toward him at once, engines screaming, and the rifles opened up, and Mercer Villalobos ran the broken water below the Narrows the way he had at nineteen, reading seams his father had taught him, jinking around rock gardens that existed only in his memory, laughing — actually laughing — at the pure animal joy of it, the last clean joy of a long compromised life.

They chased him into the lower reach where the canyon kinked and the current piled against the outside wall, and that was where he made his mistake, or where the river made it for him, or where thirty years of debt finally came due — the accounts differ, and Mercer, feeling the first bullet tear through his shoulder, did not stop to audit them.

The second one caught him higher, and the wheel spun loose in his hands, and the dinghy swung broadside into the current, and the last thing he saw clearly was the canyon wall sliding past, red stone veined with black water-stains, beautiful, unbearably beautiful, the most beautiful thing he had ever betrayed and the only thing that had ever forgiven him.

He heard them stop shooting. He heard the water. He thought, with the strange clarity of the drowning: *the logbook is safe, the girl has the logbook, make the river tell the truth.*

The current took him the way it takes everything eventually — patiently, without malice, without mercy — and carried him down through the kink and out of sight of the guns, into the long green quiet where the river keeps what it is owed.

* * *

They found the dinghy at dusk, hung up on a gravel bar two miles below the Narrows, holed at the waterline, oars gone, outboard flooded. There was blood on the thwart, dried brown, not much of it. No Mercer.

Coral stood in the shallows beside it for a long time without speaking, the logbook clutched against her chest, while Tam scanned the banks and Junie called his name until her voice gave out, and the canyon gave back nothing but its own enormous indifference.

Sela touched Coral's arm. "The current runs fast here. If he went in—"

"He went in." Coral's voice was raw. "And this river doesn't give things back. Except—" She stopped. Looked down at the logbook. Opened it with hands that barely worked, to the first page, to her father's name in Mercer's handwriting, dated thirty years ago, and beneath it a single line:

*If anything happens to me, this book goes to his kid. She'll know what to do with it.*

"He knew," Coral whispered. "He's known since Rivergate. He knew this was the price."

Downstream, the evening wind came up the canyon carrying the smell of rain that wouldn't fall, and far away — or perhaps only imagined — a bell buoy tolled once against the current, the way it always did at the bend below the old dive site, marking the water over the drowned.

"We make camp," Coral said at last, and her voice had gone somewhere past grief into the flat bright country beyond it, the country where debts get collected. "Tonight we read every page. Tomorrow we go upstream. All the way."

She looked up at the blackening canyon walls, at the stars kindling in the slot of sky above the Narrows, and her hand closed tighter around the book.

"He bought us the truth with his life. I intend to spend every cent of it."
