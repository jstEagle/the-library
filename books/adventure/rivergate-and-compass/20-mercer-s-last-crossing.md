## 20. Mercer's Last Crossing

The side channel swallowed them whole.

Tam ran the boat with the engine half-flooded and the throttle cracked just enough to keep steerage, threading between deadfalls that rose out of the brown water like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down in the river a long time ago and never gotten up. Junie knelt on the bow deck with a boat hook, pushing off snags before they could find the hull. Nobody spoke above a whisper. Behind them, back at the narrows, the echo of the shot was still living in the canyon walls, bouncing and thinning but not gone.

Mercer lay in the stern with his head in Coral's lap.

She had done what she could, which was almost nothing. The wound was high in his chest, under the collarbone, and it bled the way old men bleed — slow and stubborn and dark, as if his body had decided to be economical about it. She'd packed it with the shirt off her own back and pressed until her arm shook, and Mercer had watched her do it with an expression she'd seen him wear a hundred times at the wheel: patient, faintly amused, waiting for the river to finish making its point.

"Save your strength," he said, when she shifted to press harder again.

"Shut up," Coral said. Her voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "Just — shut up and save yours."

The channel bent north. The light through the canopy went green and underwater-colored, and the air smelled of rot and mineral and the cold breath of stone. Somewhere behind them, muffled by miles of winding rock, an engine idled — Ashe's men deciding whether to follow into water they didn't know. Tam listened to it the way a deer listens, one ear cocked, and then eased the throttle down further still, letting the current carry them.

Coral looked down at Mercer's face. It was gray in the green light, gray as river clay, and there was blood at the corner of his mouth that he kept wiping away with two fingers, fastidious even now, like a man brushing crumbs from a table he meant to leave tidy.

"You took a bullet for us," she said. It wasn't what she meant to say. Nothing she said was what she meant to say.

"I took a bullet because I stood up in an open boat like a damn fool." He coughed, wet and long, and when it passed he smiled at her with bloody teeth. "Thirty years ago I'd have ducked."

"Don't." Her hands were shaking against his chest. "Don't do the thing where you make it a joke. Not this one."

"All right." He was quiet a moment. The boat ticked against a snag; Junie pushed them free without a word. "Not this one."

* * *

They beached in a cutbank where an old flood had carved a shelf of gravel under overhanging stone — defensible, invisible from the main channel, dry. Tam killed the engine and the silence that followed was so complete Coral could hear the river breathing in the dark behind them, a low continuous exhale through the canyon.

They carried Mercer up onto the gravel on a stretcher of strapping and a hatch cover, Junie and Tam taking the corners, Coral cradling his head. He weighed less than she remembered him weighing. When had that happened? He'd been iron all her life, iron and salt and rope, and now he came up out of the boat like driftwood, like something the river had already begun reclaiming.

Imani Reyes did what she could too. The surveyor sat propped against the stone with her own bandaged leg stretched out, and she worked with steady, clinical hands — checking pupils, counting pulse, listening to his chest with an ear pressed flat because they had no stethoscope. When she sat back, she caught Coral's eye and gave a small shake of her head. Not now. Not yet. But soon, and nothing anyone could do about it here.

Coral nodded as if accepting a weather report.

They made him comfortable with life vests under his head and Junie's coat over his legs. Junie fussed — water, more water, a folded towel, the angle of the coats — and Mercer endured it with his eyes closed, and Coral understood that Junie was doing what Coral herself wanted to do and couldn't: keep moving, keep tending, keep the grief out ahead of her instead of on top.

Tam built a small fire in a hollow of stone, screened so no light would show from the channel. The flame was the first warm color any of them had seen in hours. It painted Mercer's face amber and made him look, for a moment, like himself again — like the man who'd taught her to read water at nine years old, standing behind her small hands on the wheel of the *Maren V*, saying *the river tells you everything, girl, if you stop trying to talk over it.*

"Coral." His eyes were open now, fixed on her. "Sit down. You're hovering like a gull."

She sat.

He reached inside his jacket — slowly, wincing, each movement a negotiation — and brought out a book.

It was a logbook. Real paper, real binding, the kind nobody used anymore except old rivermen who didn't trust anything that needed charging. It was swollen with water damage long since dried, its cover warped into a shallow bowl, its edges soft as cloth. The navy stencil on the spine had been scratched out with a knife, deliberately, thoroughly — but not deeply enough to erase. She could still read it if she let her eyes go soft: *U.S.N.* and beneath it a hull number she didn't recognize.

Her heart began to beat somewhere up under her jaw.

"I know what you found in that case," Mercer said. "Don't look surprised. You've been carrying it like a stone in your cheek for two hundred miles." He turned the book over in his hands, once, twice, a man handling something both precious and hated. "I've been carrying mine thirty years."

"Mercer—"

"Let me talk." His voice was quiet but it had the old wheelhouse authority in it, the voice that had steered her through her first flood season. "I don't have the wind for interruptions."

She folded her hands and waited. Around the fire, the others had gone still. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"I ran supply boats to the source," he said. "You know that much. I told you on the water, the night you asked me straight out, and I'll not repeat the whole confession — my chest hasn't got the room. But there's a part I left out, because I was a coward, and I'm not going to die a coward."

Junie made a small sound and pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"There were four of us who knew the run," Mercer went on. "Four pilots, rotating, so no one man saw the whole picture. That was deliberate. They were careful people, the ones who built that operation. Careful the way rot is careful — working in the dark, eating things out from the inside." He tapped the book's warped cover. "Two divers drowned in the containment pool the summer of the big drawdown. Officially it was equipment failure. I was the pilot who brought their bodies downriver. I carried them past Rivergate at night with canvas over the rails, and I signed a piece of paper that said I'd seen nothing, and they paid off my boat's mortgage, and I told myself for thirty years that a man with a crew to feed does what he has to do."

His breath caught. Coral leaned forward, but he waved her back with two fingers.

"The part I left out," he said, "is your father."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, water fell over stone in a long soft rush.

"He dove with them. Not for them — with them, at first, same as me, a hired hand who didn't ask questions. But your father asked questions, Coral. It's the first thing I loved about him and the last thing too. He started keeping his own notes. Soundings where there were no official soundings. Water samples in jars hidden in the bilge. And when the drownings happened — when he understood what the containment grid actually was, what it had been leaking into the aquifer since before you were born — he came to me and asked me to help him take it all downstream. To Sela's people. To newspapers, if it came to that. To anybody who'd listen."

Coral's throat had closed. She got the words out anyway. "And you said no."

"I said worse than no." Mercer's eyes were wet in the firelight, and he didn't wipe them. "I told him the river was bigger than the truth and we were smaller than both, and that a dead hero feeds nobody. I told him if he went poking at that grid again I wouldn't run him up there, and I'd tell the navy he was coming if they asked me — which they did ask me, later, and God forgive me, I answered." He closed his eyes. "Three weeks later he went up alone. And the river kept him."

Nobody moved. Coral heard her own pulse in her ears, loud as oars.

"They called it an accident," Mercer said. "Same as the divers. Same words, same paperwork, same careful rot. And I signed the second piece of paper too, and I took my boat home, and I raised his daughter's friend's generation on that river and never once said his name above a whisper." He opened his eyes and held the book out to her across the space between them, across the firelight, across thirty years. "This is his work and mine both. My logs from every supply run — dates, water levels, gate positions, what was loaded, what was buried. And in the back, his pages. What he salvaged before the end. It's not everything. It's not enough. But it's true, and it's written down, and truth written down is the only kind that survives a drowning."

Coral took it.

It was heavier than it should have been, the way all such things are. The warped cover fit her two hands as if it had been shaped for them. She opened it to the first page and saw her father's handwriting — narrow, slanted, unhurried — and the sound she made was small and involuntary, and Junie put a hand on her shoulder and left it there.

"Make the river tell the truth," Mercer said.

She looked up at him.

"That's the job now. Not revenge — I've no use for it, and neither have you, though you'll want it tonight. Not justice either; that word belongs to courts and we both know whose courts those are. Truth. Just that. Get it downstream. Get it into daylight where it can't be stuffed back in a case or a cavern or a grave. Your father tried to carry it alone and the river took him. Don't you dare try to carry it alone." His gaze moved, slow with pain, around the fire — Tam, Junie, Reyes, the dark shape of the boat at the bank. "None of you. That's the whole trick of it. A river is a thousand tributaries agreeing to go the same way."

"And you?" Coral managed. "What do we do with you?"

"What sailors have always done." He smiled, tired and unafraid, and it broke her heart clean through. "You put me in the current and you go on."

"No." She heard herself say it, hard and flat. "No. We're getting you downriver. Tam can run the channels, we can—"

"Coral." Gentle. Terrible. "Look at my color, girl. Listen to my chest if you doubt me — the doctor there already has. Every hour you spend going slow for my sake is an hour the dry season takes off your water, and an hour Ashe gains on you, and I will not spend my last day being ballast." He drew a breath that whistled faintly at the bottom of it. "Besides. This is my crossing, not my wrecking. A man gets to choose his water. I'm choosing this one — high ground, good fire, my crew around me, and the river talking to itself ten feet away. There are worse places to lie to. I've lain in most of them."

* * *

He lasted until the small hours.

They spent them the way river people spend vigils — quietly, close together, the fire fed small. Tam checked the perimeter twice and came back each time reporting nothing, and the second time he stayed sitting near Mercer's feet, elbows on knees, saying nothing, which from Tam was a eulogy. Reyes dozed sitting up and woke at every change in Mercer's breathing with her ear already tilting toward him. Junie held his hand for a long while and told him, in a low rush, about every small kindness he'd ever done her that he probably didn't remember — the winter he'd quietly covered her dock fees, the time he'd lied to a customs officer about her age, the way he'd taught her to tie a bowline by calling it *a rabbit comes out of the hole, round the tree, back down the hole* — and Mercer listened with his eyes closed and his mouth curving, correcting her technique once, softly, out of habit.

Coral read the logbook by firelight.

Page by page, the operation assembled itself out of his blunt pilot's shorthand. Dates. Gate openings. Barge tonnages listed as *aggregate* that no aggregate quarry on earth would need armed escort for. Water levels at the source pool recorded against rainfall that couldn't account for them — the pool rising when the season said it should fall, falling when it should rise, the signature of something upstream being opened and shut by human hands. And in the back, in her father's hand, the deeper record: sketches of the containment grid, corrosion notes, a list of compounds she didn't recognize and one she did, because Sela had spoken it in the village at the bend like a curse.

On the last page of her father's section, dated eleven days before the river kept him, a single line:

*If I don't come back down, it's in the grid. Third cell from the intake, below the false floor. Tell Lior's girl the compass knows.*

Coral read it three times. The firelight swam. *Tell Lior's girl the compass knows.* He had known — somehow, in whatever way fathers know things they never say aloud — that the compass would end up in her hands, drifting wrong on some dock, pointing its stubborn secret toward the headwaters. He had left her a message the way he'd left her everything else: submerged, sealed, waiting for a diver.

She hadn't cried at his funeral. She'd been nineteen and hard as a cleat, and she'd stood at the rail of Mercer's boat and scattered the urn and felt nothing but the wind. Now, with the old man himself breathing his last ten feet away and her father's handwriting trembling in her hands, the debt came due all at once. She turned her face away from the fire and let it happen, silently, the way you cry on watch — shoulders shaking, hands steady on the book.

When she looked up, Mercer was watching her.

"Now you know why I stayed silent," he said, "and why I can't anymore. Silence was my sin, girl. Don't make it your inheritance."

"It won't be," she said. Her voice was wrecked and she didn't care. "I promise you. On the book. On the boat. On both of them."

"Good." He settled back against the life vests. "Then I'm square with the river."

Near dawn — the sky above the canyon rim going from black to iron to a thin bruised silver — he asked her to help him sit up a little, facing the water. She propped him against her, his weight feather-light now, his breathing a tide going out. The river talked to itself in the dark, low and constant, the oldest voice there was.

"Listen," he whispered. "Hear that? Been talking the whole time." A pause. Long. Then, barely: "*Rivergate and Compass.* That's what your father used to call us, you know. Him and me. Said a riverman's only ever got two things worth naming." His hand found hers and gripped it with the last of the old iron. "Told him he was a sentimental fool."

The grip loosened. Didn't return.

The river went on talking. It didn't raise its voice, and it didn't fall silent, and Coral understood, sitting there holding the weight of him in the gray first light, that this was how the river said everything — steadily, whether anyone listened or not.

She laid him back down and closed his eyes with two fingers, the way he'd taught her to close a fish's eyes, gently, like finishing a sentence.

* * *

They buried him at first light, above the flood line, in the gravel shelf under the overhang. Tam cut a marker from a piece of broken oar and burned the letters in with the fire iron: **MERCER VANE — PILOT — HE MADE THE CROSSING**. Junie laid her coat over the grave because it was the warmest thing she owned and she said he'd have complained about the cold otherwise, complaining being how he'd loved people. Reyes stood at attention, surveyor-straight on her bad leg, and saluted with two fingers off her brow, and Coral thought Mercer would have laughed at that and then done it back.

No one suggested leaving quickly. No one suggested staying. They simply took the time the grave required, and when it was taken, Coral shouldered the pack with the logbook in it, feeling the warped cover press against her spine like a hand.

"We're two days' walk from the mining country," Tam said quietly, crouched over the chart they'd salvaged from the drowned camp. "Sela marked the portage route. If the dry season holds its pace, we've got maybe five days of water left in the canyon stretches. After that, nothing runnable, nothing drinkable." He looked up. "And Ashe will assume we're running downstream. Which means upstream is the one direction he isn't watching."

Coral stood at the edge of the shelf and looked up the channel, where the canyon narrowed to a slot of white water and beyond it, unseen, the bone country waited — the rusted machines, the abandoned adits, the graves of everyone who had ever been paid to keep quiet. Her father's line was folded into her pocket now, copied out in her own hand: *it's in the grid. Third cell from the intake.*

The compass hung at her neck. She didn't check it. She didn't need to. For the first time in her life, she knew exactly which way it pointed.

"Break camp," she said. "We're going up."
