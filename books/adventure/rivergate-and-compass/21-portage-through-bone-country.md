## 21. Portage Through Bone Country

Junie had carried a lot of things in her nineteen years — firewood, water jugs, her mother's anger, Tam's tool bags when his shoulder went bad — but she had never carried a boat.

The *Rivergate and Compass* wasn't really a boat anymore. She was forty feet of stubborn oak and patched steel that Mercer had bought used before Junie was born, and now she was a patient on a litter of peeled logs and rope lashings, being dragged uphill by four people who should have known better.

"Again," Tam said, hands flat against the hull's flank like he was pushing a sleeping animal. "On three. One—"

"I hate three," Coral said, and set her shoulder anyway.

They moved the boat maybe ten feet. The runners groaned over stone. Somewhere behind them, the river they'd abandoned ran shallow and mean through its bed, too low to float anything bigger than a coffin.

Junie wiped her palms on her thighs and looked up the canyon instead of at the work, because looking at the work made her want to cry, and crying wasted salt she'd need later.

The canyon didn't care about any of them. It rose on both sides in walls of red and gray stone, close enough in places that she could have thrown a rock from one rim to the other, and the light came down in a single narrow ribbon that turned everything the color of old rust. The air smelled of dust and hot metal and something else underneath it all — something chemical and wrong, like a struck match that never quite went out.

That smell had started two miles back.

* * *

They'd found the first machine an hour after dawn, if dawn meant anything in a slot of country where the sun took until midmorning to find you.

It was a dredge. Or it had been. Junie knew dredges the way river kids know them — the big floating ones that chewed gravel out of bars downstream, legal ones, loud ones, the kind men worked in shifts and cursed in two languages. This one was nothing like those. This one sat on skids halfway up the canyon wall, tilted like a drunk leaning on a friend, and it was the size of a chapel. Its boom arm lay folded against the rock. Its cables had rusted into orange lace. Painted on its flank, mostly eaten by weather but still legible, were letters that had once been white:

**H.A.C. — PROPERTY OF THE CROWN MINERALS AUTHORITY — DO NOT APPROACH**

"Nobody approaches," Junie read aloud, "and nobody leaves."

Nobody laughed. That was how she knew everyone was scared.

Tam circled it with his hands in his pockets, not touching, reading it the way he read engines — whole, then parts. "Hydraulic extraction," he said finally. "Look at the intake diameter. That's not a gravel rig. That thing was built to move water by the ton."

"To move water," Coral said, "or take it?"

Neither of them answered, because the answer was lying all around them, going up the canyon as far as the light reached.

* * *

By noon they understood why the maps stopped.

Mercer's logbook — Coral carried it now, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like something newborn — had described this stretch in a code they'd only half cracked: *the yards*, he'd called it, *where we offloaded*. Junie had imagined a dock, some crates, maybe a shed.

It was a city.

Not a living one. A swallowed one. Every bend of the canyon opened onto more of it: rail spurs running into dead ends, their ties rotted to black sponge; ore carts sitting full on a siding, their loads fused into a single mass of gray slag; a row of bunkhouses with their roofs caved in, bedsprings pushing up through the floors like pale grass. There was a mess hall with a piano still standing in it, swollen and split, its keys furred green. There was a water tower on legs so rusted Junie wouldn't walk under it. There was a machine shop with a lathe big enough to turn a tree trunk, and a wall of hand tools hung in perfect order, waiting thirty years for men who had never come back.

And everywhere, everywhere, the pipes.

They came down the canyon walls in bundles, thick as a man's waist, wrapped in insulation gone to powder, and they all ran the same direction: upstream. Toward the source. Some were burst, split along their seams, and around each rupture the rock itself had changed color — stained in fans of white and yellow and a bruised, oily purple that no mineral Junie knew made.

She touched one stain, just once, with a gloved fingertip. The glove came away smelling of pennies and rot.

"Don't," Reyes said quietly, from the travois they'd rigged for her. She hadn't walked more than fifty yards since the strainer; her voice was still thin as thread. But her eyes tracked everything, cataloguing, and Junie was starting to understand that Dr. Imani Reyes saw the world the way Tam saw engines — as systems, all of them either working or failing. "Don't touch anything here. Please."

"What is it?" Junie asked, though she already suspected.

Reyes looked up the canyon, toward the source none of them could see yet, and her face did something complicated. "It's the reason my team came. It's the reason your river runs strange above the bend. It's—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "It's thirty years of runoff, and it never stopped. They just stopped writing it down."

* * *

They camped that night in the machine shop, because it had a roof that mostly held and walls that blocked the wind, and because Coral said the alternative was sleeping in the open under the eyes of all that rusted machinery, and she'd rather not.

Junie drew first watch, which suited her. She liked the hours when the others slept — Tam snoring in short efficient bursts, Coral curled around the logbook like a dog around a bone, Reyes breathing shallow but steady, Sela awake somewhere in the dark, always awake, watching the canyon mouth. Junie sat on an overturned crate with the lantern turned low and listened to the canyon tick and settle around her.

That was when she found the tags.

They were in a drawer beneath the crate — she'd felt for a handle to move it and the drawer slid out instead, silent on runners someone had oiled within the last decade, which was its own small horror. Inside, packed in rows like coins, were brass diving tags. Dozens of them. Each stamped with a number and a name, and each punched through with a hole where a lanyard had once run.

Junie knew what dive tags were. Everyone on the river did. You wore yours on every working dive, and when you came up, you clipped it to the board, and the board told the world who was under the water. A tag left unpunched on a board at day's end meant a man who hadn't come up.

These tags had never been clipped anywhere. They were new-old, pristine in their rows, issued and never worn. Names she didn't know. Numbers running into the hundreds.

Hundreds of divers. For a mine that officially did not exist, on a river that officially had no operation, in a canyon that officially ended twenty miles south of here.

She sat with the drawer open for a long time, the lantern flame bending in the draft, and thought about Mercer. About what he'd confessed on the deck in the dark — *two drowned, and we swore silence* — and how he'd said it like it was the whole weight. Two. He'd carried two for thirty years, and the true number had been sitting up here the whole time, waiting in a drawer, wearing brass.

She didn't wake anyone. But she took one tag — number 214, a name worn nearly smooth, *E. VASQUEZ* — and put it in her pocket, and closed the drawer, and sat watch until the sky through the roof holes went from black to iron to the color of a healing bruise.

* * *

Morning brought the bone yard.

Sela named it that, or maybe just confirmed what everyone was already thinking. It lay in the widest part of the canyon, where the walls fell back and the sun finally got honest, and it was acres of equipment laid out in rows like a graveyard with very orderly graves. Pumps the size of trucks. Generators on sleds. Crates stenciled in three languages. And scaffolding — miles of it, it seemed, galvanized steel towers that had once marched upstream toward the source, section by section, carrying whatever those pipes carried.

In the middle of it all stood a crane, and hanging from the crane, frozen mid-swing, was a containment sphere the size of a house.

"It was leaving," Tam said. He'd climbed partway up a scaffold to look, and his voice came down flat and strange. "Or trying to. Look at the rigging — they were loading it onto transport when they stopped. Everything stopped at once. Not wound down. Stopped."

"An order," Sela said. She stood with her arms crossed, and the wind coming down the canyon moved her hair without moving her at all. "One morning, someone with enough rank said *stop*, and thousands of people put down their tools and walked out, and the canyon doors closed behind them, and the maps were redrawn." Her voice had an edge Junie had never heard from her, thin and bright as wire. "I published contamination data from the outflow, numbers a fraction of this, and they exiled me. This—" she gestured at the whole silent acreage, "—this they erased. Whole careers. Whole crews. Probably whole villages' worth of health, downstream, for decades. Erased."

"The divers," Junie said. It came out smaller than she meant it to. When they all looked at her, she made herself go on, because Reyes needed to know and Coral needed to know and keeping it in her pocket felt suddenly like complicity. "There's a drawer. In the machine shop. Dive tags — hundreds of them, never used. They were bringing divers up here. A lot of divers."

Coral had gone very still. Junie watched her hand drift, without her seeming to notice, to the oilcloth bundle at her chest — her father's logbook, her father's compass, her father's whole buried life.

"He was one of them," Coral said. Not a question.

"He was one of them," Sela agreed gently. "And he was one of the few who tried to say so."

* * *

They hauled past the bone yard through the heat of the afternoon, and the boat's runners screamed on the stone, and nobody talked much, because there was nothing to say that the canyon wasn't already saying better.

It was Junie, on the tow line, who saw the thing that changed the shape of the day.

She almost missed it — a glint in a side gully, half-buried in talus, the kind of shine that in this canyon meant rust nine times out of ten. But this was the tenth time. She called a halt, and they climbed the slope together, and it was a truck. An army truck, navy gray, its canvas rotted through, its tires melted to tar. Its bed was full of crates, and the crates were full of paper.

Records. Shipping manifests, personnel rosters, maintenance logs — waterlogged, foxed, but legible. Reyes went through them with shaking hands while the others stood guard out of habit, though there was no one to guard against except the past.

"These are originals," Reyes whispered. "Do you understand? Everything official was destroyed — I've seen the destruction certificates, they're meticulous. But somebody loaded a truck to haul these out and it never left." She looked up, and her eyes were wet, and she was smiling, which frightened Junie more than the sphere on the crane had. "This is the chain. This is the proof that holds together. My data tells you *what*. This tells you *who*."

Coral crouched beside the truck a long moment, saying nothing. Then she reached into the cab and came out with a clipboard, and on the clipboard, under a sheet of plastic gone yellow, was a duty roster dated thirty-one years ago, and third from the top, written in a hand Junie knew from the front of the logbook — knew from birthday cards, from the flyleaf of her own first chart book — was a name.

*C. Voss.*

Her father had signed for a load of pipe. Destination line blank. Signature steady, unhurried, a man doing a job on an ordinary day, weeks or months or years before the water took him.

Coral put the clipboard down with exaggerated care, the way you put down something that might go off. She stood. She looked up the canyon, toward the source, toward whatever cathedral of rust and rising water waited at the end of all this, and Junie watched her captain's face do the thing it did before bad weather — go calm, go simple, go dangerous.

"They're going to want this burned," Coral said. "Ashe. Whoever sent him. The moment they understand what's in this canyon, every order that comes down will be about erasure. Again." She turned, and her eyes found each of them — Tam, Sela, Reyes, Junie last, always Junie last, because Junie was the youngest and Coral still believed that protected her from anything. "So we don't give them the chance. We photograph everything. We take the roster, the manifests, a crate of the best documents. And then we keep moving, faster than we've ever moved, because the dry season isn't waiting and neither are they."

"And the boat?" Tam asked, nodding down the slope at the *Compass*, tiny and absurd on her log litter, forty feet of home stranded a thousand feet from any water deep enough to hold her.

Coral followed his gaze. For a moment something crossed her face — grief, plain and unguarded, for the boat, for Mercer, for the whole easy life at the docks that had died somewhere back in the rapids.

"We cache her," Coral said. "Tonight, at the next flat ground. She's carried us as far as she can." She put her hand flat on the truck's cold fender, as if steadying herself against it. "From here, we walk."

Junie looked back at the boat and felt her throat tighten. But then her fingers brushed the brass tag in her pocket — *E. VASQUEZ, 214* — and she thought about drawers full of names, canyons full of erased men, a river fed by a lie, and she found that her feet, at least, were already pointed upstream.

They made two more miles before dark, hauling the boat through the bones of the hidden industry, and it was Junie on point who topped the last rise and stopped so abruptly that Tam walked into her back.

Ahead, the canyon forked. And in the fork, planted in the middle of the trail like a flag, stood a fresh wooden post with a strip of new-painted metal nailed to it, catching the last of the light.

Military stencil. Fresh paint. A week old, at most.

It read: **RESTRICTED — TURN BACK — LAST WARNING.**

Behind them, miles back, an engine sound drifted down the canyon on the evening air. Small. Patient. Getting closer.
