## 22. The Dry Season Bites

Tam woke to the sound of the river dying.

It wasn't a dramatic sound. That was the worst of it. No roar, no crack of collapsing bank — just a thinning, a hush where there had been a voice. For eleven days he'd slept to the river's mutter against the hull, and now that voice had dropped to something like an old man breathing through his teeth. He lay in the narrow bunk beneath the wheelhouse for a full minute before he got up, because he already knew what he'd find, and there was a small mercy in not knowing for one more minute.

The gauge told him anyway. He'd painted the marks himself back at Rivergate, neat white ticks on the transom with dates beside them, an engineer's habit he couldn't shake. The waterline sat four fingers below yesterday's mark. Six below the day before that. He did the arithmetic without wanting to: at this rate they had maybe three days of navigable water in this stretch, two if the heat held, and the heat was holding like a grudge.

He climbed out into a morning the color of hammered tin. The canyon walls rose sheer on either side, red rock streaked white with old mineral stains, and the sky above them was a hard pale blue with no mercy in it anywhere. The *Maren Voss* sat in six inches of what had been a channel, canted slightly to port, her propeller exposed and furred with dried silt. Junie was already awake, sitting on the bow rail with her knees drawn up, watching the shallows downstream where a heron stood in water barely deep enough to cover its feet.

"Don't say it," she said when she saw him.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were doing your face. Your math face."

Tam looked at the gauge instead of answering, which was answer enough. Junie unfolded herself from the rail and came to stand beside him, and together they regarded the numbers like two people regarding a diagnosis.

"Three days," Tam said.

"Two," said Junie. "I've been counting since before you got up."

* * *

By the time Coral came out of the cabin, the argument had already been had twice — once between Tam and Junie, once between Tam and himself, the second one longer and less productive. Mercer's logbook lay open on the chart table, weighted down with a wrench, its pages soft as cloth from thirty years of river damp. Tam had been through it three times since the ambush, since the blood, since they'd buried what was left of the old pilot in a side-channel grave marked with a piece of driftwood because the ground was too rocky for anything deeper. The logbook was the only map they had of the upper reaches, and Mercer's handwriting grew more cryptic the farther upstream the entries went, as though the man had been writing in code even to himself.

"Portage," Coral said. She didn't make it a question. She'd been making fewer things questions lately, and Tam understood why, and grieved it a little. Grief either softened a person or sanded them down to load-bearing structure, and Coral was sanding.

"The boat can't go another fifty meters," Tam said. "Let alone the twelve klicks of dead water Mercer logged before the next pool." He spread the relevant page flat. "Look. 'Dry season, year of the big fire. Walked the boat light over shale, lost the rudder to a crack at the third bend.' Even Mercer portaged, and Mercer knew this river like his own hands."

"And Bone Country's past the third bend," Junie said quietly. "Where the mining equipment is."

Nobody wanted to say the rest of it, so Tam said it, because somebody had to and he was the one who thought in loads and leverage. "We cache the *Maren*. We take what we can carry. Water, Reyes if she can walk, the data, the testimony if we find it. Everything else stays with the boat." He paused. "Including most of our food."

Coral looked at him across the chart table, and behind her eyes he could see the same ledger everyone aboard was keeping — debts, distances, days. "How much water?"

"Forty liters in the tanks, call it. Another twenty in odds and ends. Five people, Reyes barely walking, canyon country, high sun." He let the numbers sit. "If we're disciplined, ten days. If we're not, five."

"And the source is how far?"

"Mercer's marks put the cavern mouth at maybe forty klicks overland. But overland in this terrain isn't distance, it's vertical. Up and down every ridge, around every slot canyon. Call it sixty of actual walking. Maybe seventy."

Junie whistled low. "Seventy klicks on five liters a day per person."

"Less than that. We'll be lucky to carry three each after weight."

Coral nodded slowly, and Tam watched her arrive at the conclusion he'd already reached in the dark hours before dawn: the calendar was no longer their enemy's weapon. It was simply the enemy. The dry season didn't care about navy patrols or cover-ups or fathers' ghosts. It would kill them with the impersonal patience of arithmetic.

"Then we don't stop," Coral said. "We cache the boat today, we walk tomorrow at first light, and we don't stop until we find water Mercer didn't mark."

* * *

Caching the boat took the whole day and most of what Tam had left in his shoulders.

They found a slot in the canyon wall half a klick upstream — a collapsed overhang forming a shallow cave, screened by deadfall, invisible from the water. They ran the *Maren* aground on the last of the channel with her engine coughing silt, and then the real work began: unloading everything, deciding everything, burying everything.

An engineer's whole life is decisions about what matters, and Tam made them the way he always did, aloud and without sentiment, while the others carried. Tools went into waterproof bags and then into a steel drum they'd salvaged from the drowned camp, sealed with gasket compound and rolled into the deepest corner of the cave. Spare parts. The good rope. Half the food, vacuum-sealed and double-bagged, on the chance anyone ever came back this way alive. The radio's spare battery, because Imani had insisted, and Imani's insistence had a way of being right.

Reyes sat on a rock above the work, wrapped in every layer they could spare, directing the packing of her own equipment with the fierce precision of a woman who had watched her colleagues die and would not watch their data die too. Her lips were still gray at the edges. The river had nearly kept her, and some part of her stayed cold ever since; Tam saw it in how she held her hands, always close to her body, always conserving.

"The samples stay with me," she said, for the fourth time. "Not the drum. With me."

"With you," Coral agreed, for the fourth time, and Tam caught the look that passed between them — the scientist trusting the diver with the thing that mattered more than her life, and the diver accepting the weight without flinching.

By late afternoon the *Maren Voss* sat empty as a picked bone, high-centered on the gravel bar, and Tam walked her length one last time with his palm trailing along the rail. Eleven years he'd kept her running — patched boilers, rewired panels, coaxed power from an engine older than he was. She was the first thing that had ever felt like his, in a life of borrowed rooms and other people's boats. Leaving her felt like leaving a door open behind him in winter.

"She'll hold," Junie said, appearing at his elbow. She'd gotten good at that, appearing. "Steel doesn't rot. Worst case, she waits for us."

"Worst case," Tam said, "nobody comes back to argue about it."

Junie bumped her shoulder against his arm. "Then best case, we come back and you get to be smug about your sealing job."

He almost smiled. It was the currency they traded in now, these small counterfeits of cheer, passed hand to hand because the real thing was too expensive.

* * *

They walked out at first light, five of them in a line, and the canyon swallowed the sound of their footsteps so completely that Tam felt deafened.

He carried the most, because he was built for it and because carrying was easier than thinking. Forty kilos on a frame pack he'd rigged from a cargo net and the boat's old awning stays: twenty liters of water in scavenged containers, the tools they might need to breach whatever containment grid waited at the source, rope, the medical kit, his own share of the food. Coral carried Mercer's logbook against her chest in a plastic sleeve, and the compass on a lanyard, and something else Tam had stopped asking about — the sealed case, or what remained of it after she'd opened it, as though the paper inside were a passenger she couldn't set down. Sela carried the hydrology instruments and moved through the canyon with the ease of someone returning to a house she'd been exiled from. Junie carried the lightest load and scouted ahead, because she was smallest and quickest and because someone had to go first into country where the map was a dead man's handwriting.

Imani walked between Coral and Sela, one arm over each of their shoulders, her boots leaving a drag-mark in the dust that Tam found himself tracking obsessively — measuring it, hour by hour, the way he measured everything now. In the morning her drag was eight centimeters long. By noon it was fifteen. By mid-afternoon they were moving at the speed of her worst step, and the arithmetic in Tam's head had turned ugly.

Water discipline started civil. Everyone drank on schedule, measured sips, no arguments. By the second day the schedule became a ritual and the ritual became a religion, and Tam, who administered it, understood that he had become the keeper of the only sacrament that mattered. He doled out water in the blue-gray hour before dawn and again at dusk, and between those hours the canyon cooked them and nobody drank and everybody thought about drinking.

The land did not want them there. That was the truth of Bone Country that no one had said plainly yet: the place itself seemed to have opinions. The canyon narrows forced them into single file over ground polished slick by flash floods. The ridges between drainages rose steep as stairs and dropped away just as fast. Rust-colored stone shed heat all night, so that sleeping was a negotiation with a warm corpse. And everywhere, everywhere, the abandoned machinery — a winch housing grown into the rock like a fossil, a cableway stanchion leaning against the sky, the ribs of a tram line marching up a slope toward a mine adit that breathed cold air like a throat. The secret operation hadn't just worked here. It had colonized the canyon, and then died here, and the canyon had kept the body.

On the third morning, Junie came back down the trail at a jog, which was bad, because Junie didn't waste motion.

"Spring," she said. "Mercer's mark. Or where it should be."

Tam heard the shape of the sentence and felt his stomach drop before his mind caught up. "Should be?"

The spring was exactly where the logbook promised, and exactly as dead. A dark stain on the rock face, a basin of cracked mud, a single cottonwood skeleton standing over it like a mourner. Tam knelt at the stain and touched it — damp, barely, a ghost of seepage somewhere deep in the stone — and did the recalculation he'd been dreading since the boat went aground.

"It's dropping faster than the river," he said. "Whatever feeds it is being drawn down upstream. Or cut off."

"Containment structures," Sela said quietly. She'd gone very still, the way she did when the data and the dread arrived together. "If the grid at the source controls flow — if they built it to manage water levels — then the drought down here isn't weather. It's policy."

Nobody answered that. There was nothing to answer it with. Tam marked the spring on his copy of the route anyway, because a dead spring was still a landmark, and landmarks were the only wealth they had left.

* * *

That evening, camped in the lee of a fallen slab with the wind combing grit over them, Tam sat apart with the water containers and did what he did every evening: counted.

Twenty-two liters. Four and a half days at strict ration, less if the heat broke records, more if they found anything. Behind him he could hear the low sounds of the camp — Junie laughing softly at something Imani had whispered, Sela's voice explaining the geology of the ridge they'd climb tomorrow, Coral silent in the way that had become her native language. He listened to them the way he listened to engines, cataloguing the sounds, checking for the knock that meant failure.

Coral came and sat beside him. She didn't look at the water containers, which he appreciated. Everyone else pretended not to look at them; Coral simply didn't look, which was its own kind of respect.

"Give it to me straight," she said.

"Four days. Five if we're lucky and disciplined, and we haven't been lucky yet."

"And the source?"

"Two days' hard walking, minimum, if the route holds. So we arrive with a day of water in hand, maybe less." He turned a container in his hands, watching the last light move through it. "There's supposed to be water at the source. The river rises there. But if the containment grid controls the flow, and if whoever's running that grid knows we're coming—"

"They control the tap," Coral finished.

"They control the tap."

She was quiet a moment. Down the canyon, the first stars were coming out over the rim, hard and white and indifferent. "My father came through here," she said at last. "Thirty years ago, with Mercer, with the others. Same season, probably. Same thirst." Her thumb moved over the compass hanging at her collarbone, an unconscious gesture Tam had seen a hundred times and never mentioned. "What I can't figure is whether he knew what he was walking into, or whether he found out the way we're finding out. One liter at a time."

"Does it change what you'll do when you get there?"

"No," she said. "But it changes what I'll feel. And I'm trying to decide if I have room for that yet."

Tam almost laughed. "You're asking me? I'm the guy who talks to boilers."

"You're the guy who keeps us alive," Coral said. "Same skill set."

She got up and went back toward the fire's last coals, toward Sela's voice, toward the small gravity of the people she'd collected and refused to lose. Tam stayed with the containers a while longer, turning them, listening to the water shift inside like a slow tide.

* * *

The fourth day, the canyon played its trick.

They'd climbed since dawn — a brutal switchbacking ascent out of the main drainage, following Mercer's note that the route crossed "the spine and dropped to hidden water." Tam's legs had gone past burning into a kind of numb obedience. Imani had to be hauled up two of the pitches, Junie and Coral taking turns at the rope, and each haul cost water they didn't have, sweat they couldn't replace. By early afternoon they stood on the spine of the world, a knife-edge ridge with the whole broken country laid out below them, and Tam allowed himself thirty seconds of looking before he bent back to the business of staying alive.

That was when Junie, out front, shouted.

The drop off the far side led into a slot canyon, green-shaded, deeply cut — and at its bottom, unmistakable even from two hundred meters up, the glint of standing water. A pool. Real water, dark and still, ringed with living reeds.

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Imani made a sound that was half laugh and half sob, and even Coral's iron composure cracked into something young and astonished, and Tam felt the arithmetic in his chest loosen its grip for the first time in four days.

"Mercer," Coral said. "Hidden water. He wrote it down thirty years ago so someone could find it."

"He wrote it down so *you* could find it," Sela said.

They descended fast, too fast, careening down the talus with thirst riding them like spurs. Tam reached the pool first — habit, or suspicion, or just the weight of the pack driving him downhill — and slid the last few meters on loose stone to the water's edge.

And stopped.

The pool was real. The water was real. But the reeds around it were dead, brown and collapsed, and along the near shore lay a scatter of things that did not belong to any wilderness: a rusted pump housing, half-buried. A coil of hose fused to the rock by decades of sun. And a metal sign, shot through with bullet holes, its stencil lettering still legible under the corrosion:

**RESTRICTED WATERS — AUTHORIZED OPERATIONS ONLY — U.S. NAVY**

Behind him, the others came down the slope and fell quiet, one by one, the way water goes still.

Tam knelt and dipped his hand into the pool. The water was cool. It was clean-looking. It was everything they needed, four days from the end of their strength, sitting in the palm of a canyon that had been forbidden before any of them were born.

"Coral," he said quietly. "It's fresh. But I want to test it before anyone drinks."

From upstream, around the slot's blind bend, came a sound none of them expected and all of them understood at once: the distant, rhythmic thrum of machinery, running smooth and well-maintained, drawing the water of the hidden spring through pipes toward the source — and someone, somewhere ahead of them in the green dark, keeping the machines company.
