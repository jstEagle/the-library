## 23. Reyes's Data

The fever broke on the third morning, and Imani Reyes woke to the smell of juniper smoke and the sound of someone — the engineer, Tam — arguing gently with a kettle.

She lay still for a long time before she opened her eyes. The shelter was a hollowed alcove beneath a shelf of sandstone, half-cave, half-camp, walled off from the canyon wind by tarps and river cane. Her body felt like something that had been dredged up from a great depth and left on the bank to dry. In a way, she supposed, it had been. She remembered the water. She remembered the strainer of flood-tangled cottonwoods, the way the current had held her under like a hand pressing down on the back of her neck, and she remembered — this was the part she kept returning to, turning over like a stone in her palm — Coral Voss's face above her, framed by gray sky, saying *I've got you* in a voice that did not sound like a stranger's voice at all.

"You're awake." Tam crouched beside her with a tin mug. Steam rose from it in a thin, honest line. "Broth. Don't argue. You've been talking to people who aren't here for two days."

"Have I?" Imani's voice came out as gravel.

"Mostly a man named Okafor. And once, at length, about sediment cores."

Imani almost smiled. "Okafor was real. He was our team lead." The smile died where it started. "Was."

Tam said nothing. That was its own kind of answer. The engineer set the mug in her hands and stayed crouched there, watching her drink with the particular attention of someone who fixed things for a living and could not fix this.

"The others," Imani said. "My team. Did they—"

"We found four," Tam said quietly. "You're the fifth. There were nine of you."

Five out of nine. Imani closed her eyes and let the arithmetic sit in her chest like cold water. Dr. Okafor. Chen. The two field techs whose names she could still see printed on their gear tags. She had crawled downstream through willow scrub for six hours with a cracked rib and a data drive sealed inside a waterproof bag strapped against her skin, and she had told herself the whole way: *the data survives, the data survives, the data survives.* She had not let herself think the other sentence. Now the other sentence arrived anyway, patient as the river.

"I need my pack," she said.

"It's soaked through and full of silt, but it's here."

"And the drive?"

Tam hesitated. "Voss has it. She wouldn't let anyone else touch it after we pulled you out. She said—" Tam paused, choosing the words carefully, the way engineers chose load-bearing ones. "She said she knew what it was to carry something sealed."

* * *

Coral sat at the mouth of the alcove with the data drive in her lap and her father's compass open beside her, and if anyone had asked her what she was doing, she would not have had an honest answer. Guarding it, maybe. Or keeping vigil. The compass needle trembled in its housing, restless, tugging southwest toward the high country, toward the source, and Coral found herself watching it the way she watched weather.

When Imani emerged into the light — moving slowly, one arm pressed to her ribs, squinting against the morning glare off the canyon walls — Coral stood without being asked and held out the drive.

"It never left my sight," Coral said. "But it's yours."

Imani took it with both hands, the way you take something holy or something explosive, which are often the same thing. "You didn't open it."

"No encryption key. No reason to." Coral paused. "And no right."

Something moved across the surveyor's face — surprise, then a tired kind of gratitude, then something harder underneath, something that looked like resolve reassembling itself out of wreckage. "There's a passphrase. It's long. It's also written on the inside of my left boot insole, because Okafor made all of us memorize contingency protocols and I never trusted my memory under stress." She looked down at her ruined boots, one missing, and laughed — a small, cracked sound. "The one I'm wearing. If it survived the river, the code survived."

The boot had survived. Of course it had; survey-issue boots were built for worse than drowning. Junie pried the insole loose while Imani sat propped against a stone, and read out a string of characters that meant nothing to any of them except Imani, who recited them back from memory anyway, twice, just to be sure the river hadn't taken that too.

They gathered around Tam's battered field laptop in the deepest shade of the alcove, away from sightlines up- and down-canyon. Mercer's absence was a shape in the group, a missing tooth the tongue kept finding. Sela stood slightly apart, arms crossed, watching the screen with the expression of a woman who had seen this exact story before and knew how it ended. Coral knelt closest, the compass closed in her fist.

The drive spun up. Files bloomed across the screen in tidy, bureaucratic order — folders labeled by grid coordinates, by date, by instrument. Hydroacoustic surveys. Water chemistry panels. Ground-penetrating radar returns. And at the top level, a single document titled **SUMMARY — DO NOT SYNC**, which Imani opened with a breath that seemed to come from somewhere below her broken ribs.

"Okafor wrote this the night before the flood," she said. "He sent it nowhere. He said he wanted it in writing, in his own words, in case—" She stopped. Started again. "In case the official channel wasn't safe. He'd stopped trusting the official channel three weeks in."

She scrolled. Read silently first, her lips moving. Then she looked up at the circle of faces and said, "You should hear it in his words. He was better at this than I'll ever be." And she began to read aloud.

* * *

*"We came to map a tributary."*

*That was how Okafor had begun, and Imani read it plainly, without performance, letting the plainness do the work.*

*"The contract described a routine hydrographic survey of an unmapped distributary system feeding the main stem near the upper gorge. Triple rate. We should have asked why triple rate. We asked instead why the region had no prior survey data at all — forty years of modern instrumentation on the main stem and nothing, literally nothing, upstream of the narrows. The answer we were given was 'security restrictions.' We accepted it. Note that down as the first failure: we accepted it."*

*"Findings, in order of discovery:"*

*"One. The tributary is not natural in its lower course. It is channeled. Cut, lined, and graded — the work is old, pre-dating modern survey standards, but the engineering is professional. Someone built a waterway here and then erased it from every record we can access."*

*"Two. The source cavern system discharges into an aquifer that underlies — conservatively — eleven hundred square kilometers. Every well, every spring, every farm drawdown between the bend settlements and the lowland towns draws from it. This aquifer is the region's drinking water. There is no alternative source. There is no backup. There is only this."*

*"Three. The aquifer is contaminated. Heavy metals consistent with decades of mining runoff — arsenic, cadmium, mercury in concentrations that exceed every public health threshold we have on file. The contamination plume is not recent. Isotope dating of sediment cores puts the earliest deposition at thirty-eight years ago, with continuous input since. Thirty-eight years. Someone has been poisoning the regional water supply for thirty-eight years, and the survey that would have caught it was suppressed, defunded, or buried each time it was proposed. We found references. Four separate proposals, 1989 through 2011, all cancelled by the same oversight office."*

*"Four. The containment structures at the source are failing. Whoever built them intended to intercept and hold the runoff — treat it, dilute it, or simply hide it — and the holding works are corroding through. Our flow measurements show seepage increasing year over year. The plume is growing. Within a decade, at current rates, the contamination reaches the main-stem intakes at Rivergate. Not the villages first. Rivergate. Forty thousand people."*

*"Five. We were not the first team here. See attached photographs from the cavern approach. Remains, equipment, dive tags. Navy issue. Old navy issue. I am a hydrologist, not an archaeologist, but I count at least six individuals, and the state of the gear suggests they have been there a very long time. Somebody sent divers into that water knowing what was in it. Somebody sent them more than once."*

*"Conclusion: this is not an accident, an abandoned site, or a legacy problem. It is an operating concealment. The water is being managed — released, withheld, flooded — to control access and evidence. We observed an engineered release from upstream structures during our occupation of the forward camp. That release killed four of my people. I am recording this summary in the expectation that it may be the only record that survives us."*

*"Recommendation: none. I don't know who to recommend anything to anymore. I only know that eleven hundred square kilometers of people are drinking this water, and that the people who know are the same people who built the fences."*

*End of summary.*

* * *

No one spoke for a long time. The canyon wind moved through the junipers above the alcove with a sound like slow breathing.

Junie sat down first — not collapsed, exactly, but lowered, the way you sit when your legs make the decision independently. "Eleven hundred square kilometers," she said. "That's — that's everything. That's the bend, that's the lowlands, that's—" She looked up. "That's my mother's well."

"That's Rivergate," Tam said. Their voice had gone flat and precise, which those who knew Tam understood to be the sound of an engineer doing emergency arithmetic. "Forty thousand people on main-stem intakes. Ten years to plume arrival at measured seepage rates. Less if the containment fails faster than modeled, and corroded steel in saturated conditions always fails faster than modeled. I'm telling you that as an engineer. Those structures are a clock, and nobody's winding it down. They're just letting it run."

"They're not letting it run," Sela said.

Every head turned. She had not moved from her place apart, but now she stepped into the circle, into the laptop's glow, and pointed at the screen — at Okafor's fourth finding, at the phrase *engineered release*.

"They're managing it," she said. "Actively. The releases, the flooding of the camps, the patrol skiffs, the denial that the wreck even exists — that's not neglect. Neglect doesn't post guards. They know the containment is failing. They've known for years. And their entire strategy is to keep the failure quiet until—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "Until it's someone else's administration. Someone else's problem. Until the plume arrives at Rivergate and can be called an act of God."

"You've seen the data before," Coral said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"I published a fraction of it. A fraction, from samples I carried out by hand, years ago." Sela's voice was steady, but her hands were not. "They exiled me for it. Called it fabrication. And I thought — God help me, I thought *my* data was the whole story. Contamination at the springs, elevated metals in the village wells. I thought I'd found the crime." She shook her head slowly, looking at the screen. "I found a symptom. This—" she gestured at the drive, at Okafor's careful, doomed prose, "—this is the body. The whole body. Thirty-eight years."

Imani watched them take it in, and understood that she was watching something she had already lived through once — the moment the abstract became geographic, the moment *eleven hundred square kilometers* turned into *my mother's well*, *my town's intake*, *my father's grave*. She had done her own version of it three weeks ago, standing in the cavern approach with Okafor, looking at dive tags green with age, and feeling the floor of her profession drop away. Science was supposed to be neutral. Science was supposed to be numbers. But some numbers were graves.

"There's something else," she said. "In the attachments. Okafor cross-referenced the old cancelled survey proposals. Four of them, all killed by the same oversight office." She pulled up the file, turned the laptop so Coral could see. "The signature block on the cancellation orders isn't a name. It's a department designation. But the authorization chain—" she tapped the screen, "—traces back through naval infrastructure command. The same command structure that owns the wreck you dove. The same command that sent divers into the source thirty-eight years ago and, apparently, several times since."

Coral had gone very still. In her fist, the compass had gone still too — needle settled, pointing southwest, unwavering, as though it had been listening.

"My father," Coral said. It came out barely above a whisper. "His crew dove up here. Before I was born. He drowned on a contract nobody would name." She looked down at the compass in her hand, then at the drive, and Imani saw the two objects become, in her expression, the same object — grief and evidence fused into a single weight. "He wasn't lost. He was *sent*. And then he was spent."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing to answer it with. Some truths you could soften. This one you could only hold.

It was Tam, finally, who said what everyone was thinking, in the flat voice of a load calculation: "So let's be clear about what we're carrying. We're not carrying proof of a crime. We're carrying proof of *thirty-eight years* of crimes, committed by an institution that still exists, that is currently hunting us with helicopters, and that has already killed — by the most conservative count — four surveyors, six divers, and one old man who ran boats for them when he was young enough to believe silence was loyalty." Tam looked around the circle. "And the victims aren't just the dead. The victims are everyone downstream who's been drinking it. Eleven hundred square kilometers of them. Which means every day we spend hiding in this canyon, the clock runs, and every option we choose has to buy more time for *them*, not just for us."

"That's why Ashe wants the drive," Imani said. "Not the boat. Not us. The drive. He offered you debt forgiveness for the survey data and your silence — that was before he knew what Okafor put on it. Now?" She shook her head. "Now the drive is the single most dangerous object in this watershed. It's the only complete record in existence. My backups went down with the camp. Okafor's went into the flood. This is it. One copy."

"One copy," Coral repeated softly.

And Imani, watching her, saw the thought arrive — saw it move behind Coral Voss's eyes like a fish rising under still water. One copy meant leverage. One copy meant a target on their backs until it was delivered or destroyed. One copy meant that whatever they decided next, they could not decide it twice.

"What are the options?" Junie asked. "Actually. Name them."

"Deliver it to Ashe," Tam said. "Debts die, we go home, the plume keeps growing, and we live with it."

"Leak it," Junie said. "Press, regulators, anyone with a printer."

"To whom? With what authentication? Half the institutions we'd send it to are in the authorization chain." Imani heard her own voice harden. "Okafor tried channels. The channels are the crime."

"Then the villages," Sela said. "The people drinking the water get the truth first. All of it. Every well, every number, every name."

"And Ashe burns the valley looking for us, and the data dies with us in some canyon shoot-out," Tam said. "None of these options survive contact. We need a fourth thing. Something that can't be seized, bought, or buried."

The wind gusted through the alcove, flapping the tarps, and somewhere far upstream — so faint it might have been imagination — came a sound none of them could name and all of them felt in their teeth: a deep, structural groan, metal under strain, like a ship's hull complaining in heavy seas.

Sela's head came up. She stared upstream into the blue shadow of the gorge, and her face did something Imani had not seen it do before — something older than fear.

"You hear that?" Sela said. "That's the containment grid. I grew up hearing that sound in dreams." She turned back to them, and her voice dropped to something urgent and certain. "It's failing faster than Okafor modeled. Faster than any of us thought. Which means we don't have ten years, and we may not have ten days — and it means there *is* a fourth option. One nobody in this canyon is going to like."

Coral looked at her. "Tell us anyway."

Sela's eyes went to the compass in Coral's hand, then upstream, toward the cathedral of rust and water waiting at the end of the gorge.

"Not here," she said. "And not all of us. Come walk with me, Coral. The rest of you — start packing. Whatever we decide, we decide it moving."
