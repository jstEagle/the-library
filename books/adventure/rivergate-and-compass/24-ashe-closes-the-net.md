## 24. Ashe Closes the Net

The sound came before the shadow did — a flat, rhythmic thudding that Coral felt in her back teeth before she understood it with her ears.

She was standing hip-deep in the shallows where the river pooled below the slot canyon, rinsing silt from the last of the dive gear, when the first helicopter crested the eastern rim. It hung there a moment against the white glare of morning, black and insectile, its rotor wash flattening the water into rings. Then it swung downstream and began to fly the canyon in slow, methodical sweeps, the way a man runs his hand along a shelf looking for something he knows is there.

"Reyes!" she shouted, already moving. "Tam! Get off the bank!"

Imani Reyes came up from the gravel bar where she'd been working, her face gray with exhaustion but her hands steady as they gathered the data case to her chest. Tam appeared from behind the boat cache, dragging Junie by the sleeve, and for one absurd second the five of them stood in the open like startled animals, blinking up at the noise.

Then the second helicopter came around the bend.

"There," Mercer would have said, if Mercer were alive to say it. *There's the door you didn't know you were knocking on.* But Mercer was three days dead, wrapped in canvas and sunk beneath a cairn of stones at the river bend, and Coral had to find the door herself.

* * *

She'd marked it two days earlier without thinking much of it — a dark seam in the canyon wall upstream of their camp, half-hidden behind a curtain of tamarisk, breathing cold air into the heat of the morning. An adit. Old mining country; the whole gorge was stitched with them, according to the maps in Reyes's recovered data. The men who'd worked this river in secret had left their holes behind like the burrows of some animal that had been hunted to extinction.

They reached it in four minutes of hard running over slick stone, Reyes stumbling twice, Tam hauling her upright both times without breaking stride. The seam opened into a throat of timbered rock, and then darkness, and then a chamber wide enough that their headlamps found no far wall.

Coral went in last. She pulled the tamarisk branches across the entrance until only a knife-thin line of daylight remained, and then even that seemed too loud, too visible, and she crouched in the gloom listening to her own heart and wondering how a machine in the sky could possibly hear it too.

Outside, the helicopters kept working the canyon. She could track them by sound alone now — the doppler swell as one passed the adit's mouth, the long silence, the return. They were flying low. Low enough that grit sifted down from the ceiling and ticked against the rocks around them like rain on a tin roof.

Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that wouldn't echo.

Junie sat with her knees drawn up, arms locked around them, and Coral could see the girl's jaw working — not fear exactly, or not only fear, but the specific fury of someone small being made to feel small. Tam knelt beside Reyes, checking the sutures along her ribs, and the surveyor bore it without complaint, though her breath came shallow and quick. Sela pressed close to Coral in the dark, her shoulder warm against Coral's arm, and neither of them moved away.

An hour passed. Maybe two. Time went strange in the mine, the way it did underwater — measured in sounds instead of minutes. The helicopters refueled somewhere out of earshot and came back. A third joined them. Once, something heavy hit the water downstream — a sonobuoy or a supply drop, Coral couldn't tell — and the whole company of them flinched like a single animal.

It was Reyes who finally broke the silence, her voice a thread: "They're not searching the river anymore. Listen. They're quartering the banks." A pause while the rotor wash swelled and faded. "Grid pattern. They know we're in this section."

"They don't know where," Tam whispered.

"They will," Reyes said. "That's what grids are for."

* * *

Coral crawled deeper into the mine because sitting still had become unbearable, and because the cold air flowing out of the dark carried a smell she couldn't name — wet iron, old timber, something mineral and ancient, like the inside of the earth holding its breath.

Her lamp found rails first. Narrow-gauge ore tracks, rusted to lace, running back into the mountain and vanishing where a roof fall had choked the tunnel decades ago. Then timber cribs, stacked and rotting. Then, scattered along the walls, the sediment of a vanished workforce: a boot sole gone stiff as bark. A lantern with its glass fogged white. Tin plates fused together by corrosion into a single lump. And everywhere the orange bloom of rust bleeding down the rock like the mine itself was wounded and weeping.

This was Bone Country — the portage route they'd hauled the boat through two days ago, all those canyons littered with dredges and hoists and the sun-bleached bones of an industry that officially had never existed. But here, underground, the evidence was different. Down there the machines had been abandoned. In here, it looked more like a retreat. Like men had walked away from their own tools mid-shift and never come back.

Her lamp caught a notice board nailed to a crib near the tunnel mouth, and she crossed to it despite every instinct telling her to stay low and stay quiet.

Most of the paper had rotted to pulp. One sheet survived, laminated somehow by its own chemical decay, and she leaned close to read the ghost of the type:

**SAFETY BULLETIN 14 — RIVERGATE EXTRACTION SITE**
*All diving personnel are reminded that descent beyond the marked grid requires Level Three authorization. Divers are further reminded that discussion of site conditions, depths, or recoveries with any person outside the operation constitutes—*

The rest was eaten away. But the words *diving personnel* sat in Coral's chest like a swallowed stone.

Level Three authorization. Her father had been a navy diver, then a salvage man, then — for one unexplained year that her mother never spoke of and Coral had never been able to stop wondering about — employed by someone whose name appeared on none of his papers. That missing year had ended with him going into the upper river on a contract job and not coming out. She'd been nine years old. The compass had arrived by mail six months later, forwarded through three addresses, wrapped in oilcloth, with no note.

She had spent her whole life assuming the river had simply taken him, the way rivers take people — carelessly, without malice, without meaning anything at all.

Standing in the rust-dark of the mine, reading a safety bulletin from an operation that had never officially existed, Coral felt that comfortable assumption begin to dissolve. Rivers took people. Operations *lost* them. And operations that lost divers wrote bulletins about authorization levels first and grief notices never.

"Coral." Sela's voice, very soft, very close. "You need to see this. Back here. Bring your light."

* * *

Sela had gone deeper while Coral read the board — deeper than prudence allowed, drawn by whatever instinct made her a hydrologist, the compulsion to follow water wherever it led. She stood twenty meters back in a side chamber where the ore body had been cut away in great scalloped bites, and her lamp was fixed on the far wall.

On the wall, someone had painted numbers. Dozens of them, in white and yellow paint, in a careful column that ran floor to ceiling: dates. Dates spanning eleven years, each one annotated with a depth figure and a single letter code. And beside the column, at shoulder height, someone had stenciled a heading so faded Coral had to wet her thumb and rub the dust away to read it:

**DIVE LOG — PERSONNEL ROTATION**

Eleven years of dives into the source caverns. Logged, tracked, scheduled — like shifts in a factory. Except that Reyes had told them what waited in those caverns, and it wasn't ore. It was bodies. Old ones, in navy gear, arranged with a care that suggested somebody, once, had still believed in dignity.

"Somebody kept records," Sela said quietly. "Right here, where nobody was ever supposed to look. Why would you keep records of a thing you're burying?"

"Because somebody above them wanted proof it was being done," Coral said. The answer came out of her flat and certain, the way truths do when they've been waiting a long time. "Burying a thing is work. Work gets audited."

Sela looked at her. In the lamplight her face was stripped of everything but attention. "Your father's year," she said. "The year nobody talks about."

"I'm starting to talk about it."

Before Sela could answer, a sound rolled down the tunnel from outside — not rotor wash this time but voices, faint and distorted by stone, and beneath them the crunch of boots on gravel. Many boots.

Coral killed her lamp so fast the dark slammed down like a lid.

* * *

They flattened themselves against the cut wall in the side chamber, all five of them, lamps dead, breathing through their mouths. The boots came closer. Through the tunnel mouth Coral could see a sliver of canyon light, and shadows crossing it — men in tactical gear moving with the unhurried confidence of people searching ground they expected to clear easily.

A flashlight beam swept the tamarisk screen at the entrance. Held. Swept again.

Coral's hand found Sela's in the dark and gripped it, and Sela gripped back, and Coral thought with strange clarity: *this is what my father's last minutes must have been like. Hiding in the dark from the people he worked for, listening to boots, understanding at last what he'd signed on to.* She had imagined his death a thousand ways across twenty years — the cramp, the snagged line, the cold — but she had never imagined this version, and now that she held it, it fit her hands like a tool she'd always owned.

The beam withdrew. Voices conferred, low. Then, maddeningly, the boots started up the canyon — past the adit, onward, toward the boat cache.

"The cache," Tam breathed against her ear. "If they find the boat—"

"They'll find the boat," Reyes whispered. "It doesn't matter. What matters is whether they find us with it."

The boots faded. The light at the tunnel mouth dimmed as the searchers moved on. But nobody relaxed, because everyone understood the arithmetic: the grid was tightening, the day was burning down, and sooner or later a flashlight beam would hold on tamarisk and not move on.

"We can't stay," Coral said at last. "And we can't go out the front."

"So we go out the back," Tam said.

"There is no back. There's a roof fall."

"There's a roof fall *from this side*," Tam said, and Coral could hear the engineer's mind already turning over, weighing stone, calculating. "Falls loosen things. On the other side of that choke there might be another working — these mines interconnected, they always did, for ventilation. Give me an hour and a bar, and I'll give you a hole."

"And if there's no other working?" Junie asked.

"Then I'll have made a very nice hole," Tam said, "and we'll all die in it together, which is at least efficient."

Someone laughed — a thin, shocked sound, maybe Junie, maybe all of them — and the laughter broke the paralysis better than courage would have. Tam got the pry bar from the gear bag. Coral took point with her lamp turned low, and they moved toward the roof fall together, five people and a dead man's compass, into the deepest dark any of them had ever walked into willingly.

* * *

It was while Tam worked the choke — the ring of the bar against stone muffled under jackets, each blow a small controlled crime against silence — that Coral drifted to the edge of the side chamber and saw, wedged in a crack of the cribbing at knee height, a small canvas roll.

She almost didn't touch it. Twenty years of salvage had taught her that mines were full of other people's ghosts and most of them bit. But her hand was moving before her caution finished objecting, and the canvas was dry — sheltered, preserved — and it unrolled across her palm with the soft whisper of something that had been waiting.

Dog tags. Four sets.

Navy-issue, dull with age but not corroded, wrapped in greasecloth by someone who had cared enough to protect them. She tilted her lamp and read the stamps one at a time, and the names went through her like current through water:

VOSS, E.R.
DELACROIX, M.
OKONKWO, T.
HALE, J.

E.R. Voss. Edmund Rafael Voss. Her father.

The world narrowed to the size of a stamping. She was aware of Sela's hand on her shoulder, of Tam's bar gone silent, of the others gathering without a word, but all of it happened at a great distance, across a great cold depth, the way surface light looks to a diver hanging at thirty meters with the wreck below and the air far above.

His tags. His *final* tags — the ones a diver surrendered when he left the rotation, or the ones a crewmate collected when he didn't come back and couldn't be brought back. Wrapped in greasecloth. Hidden in the wall of the mine where the operation was run from. Not lost. *Kept.* Someone had kept them, hidden them, the way you hide evidence, the way you hide a message you're not yet brave enough to send.

Or the way you hide something until the right person comes looking.

Coral closed her fist around the tags and felt the stamped edges bite her palm, and the pain was good, was clarifying, was hers. Grief she had carried since she was nine rearranged itself around a new spine. This wasn't an accident she'd inherited. It was a debt. And debts, at least, she knew how to collect.

"He was here," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "He stood in this room. He logged dives on that wall. And when it ended, someone rolled up his tags and hid them in the dark and waited."

"Waited for what?" Junie asked softly.

"For whoever came next," Coral said. "For me, maybe. For anyone." She looked at the dive log column on the wall, at the eleven years of dates and depths, at the bulletin downstairs with its eaten-away warning about silence. "Ashe thinks he's closing a net around five people in a canyon. He's not. He's closing it around the truth, and the truth has been down here the whole time, waiting for us to pick it up."

Behind them, deep in the roof fall, Tam's bar rang out — once, twice — and a slab shifted with a grinding sigh, and a thread of moving air stirred against Coral's face. Cool. Clean. Coming from somewhere beyond the choke.

"There's a way through," Tam called, wonder cracking his voice. "There's air moving. There's *another working* back there."

Coral slid the tags around her neck, next to the compass, next to the weight of everything her father had left her. The metal was cold against her sternum and growing warmer by the second, warming to her skin, to her pulse, as if it had only been waiting to be worn again.

"Then let's not keep the dead waiting either," she said, and turned her lamp toward the hole in the world Tam was making, as outside, high over the canyon, the helicopters began their next sweep.
