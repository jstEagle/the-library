## 25. The Choice Offered Again

They took her at first light, while the mist still lay on the canyon like a held breath.

Coral had heard the helicopter before she saw it — that flat, patient thudding that carried through stone the way sound carries through water, arriving in her chest before her ears. She'd had time to wake Tam, time to douse the fire, time to press the logbook and Reyes's decoded data into the dry bag they kept lashed beneath the thwart. She'd even had time to think about running, and to understand that running was a word for a slower kind of being caught.

So when the soldiers came down the game trail in single file, rifles slung but hands near, she was standing at the water's edge with her hands visible and her face arranged into something she hoped read as boredom.

It didn't work on the one who mattered.

"Voss." The sergeant was young, sandy, and nervous in a way that made her more afraid than competence would have. Nervous men with rifles did arithmetic badly. "Commander Ashe wants a word."

"Tell him to make an appointment."

The sergeant's mouth twitched — not quite a smile, not quite a threat. "Ma'am, he said you'd say something like that. He said to tell you the appointment is the word."

They zip-tied her wrists in front of her, which was either courtesy or theater, and walked her up the trail away from the boat, away from Tam's low voice and Junie's wide, furious eyes and Sela standing very still among the willows with the compass in her pocket, because Coral had made her promise — *if they come for anyone, they come for me* — and Sela had made her own promise back, and Coral had known even then that promises made at knife-point were just knives with better manners.

The advance team had set up in a bend of the canyon where a side wash opened into a gravel flat: two tents, a field radio, a folding table that looked obscene in its tidiness, like a dinner party staged in a graveyard. A generator hummed. A man in a wet suit sat on a rock drying his hair, and the sight of navy neoprene this far from any water they were supposed to know about made Coral's stomach tighten.

Ashe stood at the table with a mug of coffee and a folder, and when he looked up at her, she saw what she had been refusing to see since Rivergate: the man was tired. Not soft. Tired the way load-bearing stone is tired, holding up a weight it had never chosen.

"Cut her loose," he said, and someone did, and he nodded at the folding chair across from him. "Sit. Coffee?"

"I'd rather stand."

"Suit yourself." He poured a second mug anyway, set it where she could take it if she changed her mind, and opened the folder. "Your crew is being fed. Nobody's been touched. I want you to understand that before anything else, because I know how this looks."

"How this looks," Coral said, "is a man with soldiers and a table and a folder, and me with zip-tie marks on my wrists. I don't need help understanding how it looks, Commander. I grew up poor. I'm fluent in how things look."

Something moved behind his eyes — not amusement, exactly. Recognition, maybe. He closed the folder.

"Dr. Reyes is alive," he said. "I know because we've been monitoring your radio traffic, and because my people found her boot prints at the drowned camp. I also know she's carrying data my government spent eleven years and four dead surveyors trying to bury, and I know your father spent the last year of his life trying to surface the same thing." He turned the mug a slow quarter-turn on the table, a small precise motion, a man who controlled what he could control. "So let's not do the part where we pretend either of us is confused about what's on the table."

"And what's on the table?"

"The same thing that was on the table in Rivergate." He slid a single sheet of paper across the metal surface. It was typed. It was signed. It had a signature block with more stars and letters after a name than Coral had ever seen on a document she was meant to read. "Full discharge of your debts. The Voss salvage contract closed as *completed*, which matters, because right now it's closed as *breached*, and the bond holders have a claim against the boat. Your boat becomes yours, clean title, this afternoon. Your crew walks away with no file, no flag, no follow-up. You hand over Reyes and everything she recovered, and you go back to Rivergate and dive honest wrecks until your hands give out."

Coral looked at the paper and did not touch it.

The numbers were right there, in the language of her whole adult life: *discharge, satisfaction, release*. Words that meant waking up without the weight. Words that meant Tam's hands steady on tools he owned instead of pawned. Words that meant Junie eating breakfast that wasn't rationed from a jar. She could feel the paper's pull the way you feel current under still water — invisible, total, patient.

"You've made this offer three times," she said. "That's not generosity. That's a man who knows his offer doesn't hold."

"It holds."

"It doesn't. Because if it held, you'd have taken the data off us by force already, and you haven't, and I've been trying to work out why." She let that sit. Ashe's face did nothing, which was itself an answer. "It's because you can't. Because if the data comes out of this canyon through your hands — seized, logged, chain-of-custody — then somebody above you has to sign for what's *in* it. And nobody above you wants to sign for that. You need it to disappear quietly, or not at all. Preferably not at all. Preferably it was never found by anyone who could testify it existed." She nodded at the paper. "That's why the deal is so clean. Clean deals leave no fingerprints."

The sergeant by the tents shifted his weight. Somewhere up-canyon a raven said something ugly and dropped away into silence.

"You think I like this," Ashe said quietly. "You think I'm on the wrong side of it because it's comfortable."

"I think you're on the wrong side of it because you're *in* it. Same as Mercer was. Same as the divers in the containment grid. Everybody in this story is somebody's diver, Commander. The only question is who's holding the rope."

For a moment — one long breath — she thought he might actually answer that. His jaw worked. His eyes went up-canyon, toward the source, toward whatever he'd been ordered to protect and had spent years protecting without ever being allowed to say so, and Coral watched a man stand at the edge of his own confession and step back from it.

"Take the deal, Voss."

"No."

"Take it, and I'll tell you where your father's body was recovered. The coordinates. The date. What he was carrying." The words came out level, almost gentle, and that was the cruelest thing about them. "You've spent fifteen years not knowing. I can end that in a sentence."

The canyon tilted.

Coral had prepared for threats. She had prepared for the paper, for the numbers, for the arithmetic of debt that had ruled her life since she was nineteen years old and signing things she didn't understand. She had not prepared for this — for the offer not of money but of *him*, of the one fact the river had kept, the answer to the question she asked the water every time she went under. Her father, face-down in the dark somewhere, with a date and a place and a weight in his hands, and this man knew, this man had *read the file*, and he was holding her grief out like a lure over deep water.

"That's not a deal," she said, and her voice came out lower than she meant it to. "That's a ransom."

"It's the only currency I have left." For the first time, something cracked in the level voice. "You think I want this? I've got orders that don't make sense and a body count I can't account for and a commander's pension that evaporates if I file the wrong report. I am as trapped as you are, Voss. The difference is I signed my name to the trap. Take the paper. Take your boat. Take the coordinates and go home and let this canyon keep what it's kept for thirty years. Everybody lives. Isn't that what you people want? Everybody lives?"

"Everybody *forgets*," Coral said. "That's different. That's the whole trick of it. Everybody lives, and everybody forgets, and thirty years from now there's another commander standing at another table offering another diver the coordinates of somebody else's father."

She pushed the paper back across the table with two fingers, slow, so her hand didn't shake. It mostly didn't.

Ashe looked at the paper for a long time. Then he looked past her, up the trail, and Coral turned to follow his gaze, and the bottom dropped out of the morning.

Sela was coming down the trail.

She had her hands up, palms out, walking the way you walk toward armed men who don't know you — slow, deliberate, no sudden moves. Her hair was loose, her jacket open to show she carried nothing, and her voice carried ahead of her, calm and clear in the cold air: "I'm coming in. Nobody needs to do anything. I'm coming in."

"*Sela.*" Coral's whole body turned toward her before her mind caught up. "Sela, stay back—"

"Commander." Sela reached the edge of the gravel flat and stopped, hands still raised, and looked at Ashe with an expression Coral had seen on her only once before — in the village, over the contamination tables, the face of a woman doing arithmetic with lives. "Before you waste more time on her, you should know who I am. Sela Marchetti. Formerly senior hydrologist, Regional Water Authority. You've read my file. I know you have, because my file is thicker than hers."

Ashe had gone very still. "Dr. Marchetti."

"I published the source data eleven years ago. The contamination plume, the aquifer mapping, the runoff chronology — all of it, all of it was mine. They burned my career for it and exiled me to the bend, and I have spent eleven years waiting for someone to come up this river and finish what I started." Her hands came down slowly. Her eyes found Coral's, and what was in them made Coral's chest hurt — apology and resolve braided together, love wearing the mask of strategy. "The data Reyes recovered is built on my work. The interpretation is mine. If you want someone to charge, someone whose testimony actually *matters*, someone whose name on a report makes it real — you want me. She's a salvage diver. I'm the crime."

"Sela, don't." Coral stepped forward and a soldier's hand closed on her shoulder and she shook it off without looking at him. "Don't you dare. You don't get to buy me out. That's not— we don't *do* that. Nobody on this river does that."

"Someone has to be the one who falls," Sela said, still looking at Ashe, still steady, and her voice only broke a little on the next words. "It was always going to be one of us, Coral. Let it be the one who's already lost everything. You still have a boat. You still have them." A flick of her eyes toward the trail, toward Tam and Junie somewhere behind the soldiers. "I have a file and eleven years of practice at being ruined. I'm the cheaper loss."

"That's not—" Coral's voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, hated the tears standing in her eyes in front of soldiers, hated that love had made her stupid enough to think she could keep it out of the line of fire. "That is not how this works. That is *not* how we work."

"Commander." Sela turned to Ashe and lifted her chin. "Arrest me. Charge me with the unauthorized disclosure. Everything she did, she did because I recruited her, misled her, manipulated her — I'll sign whatever you want. And when the investigation comes, my testimony stands, because unlike a debt-ridden diver, I can't be discredited. I already have been. It's the one thing they can't take twice."

The gravel flat was silent except for the generator's hum. Ashe looked at her for a long, long moment, and Coral watched him do the terrible math she'd watched him do all along — the math where every column balanced and the sum was a lie.

And then Ashe did something she had not expected. He laughed. One short, bitter, airless sound, like a man coughing up a stone.

"Eleven years," he said, "and they send me the *hydrologist* and the *diver's daughter* and a surveyor who won't stay drowned. You know what my orders actually say, Dr. Marchetti? My orders say *contain*. Not arrest. Not prosecute. Contain. You know why? Because an arrest is a document, and a document is a record, and a record is a thing that surfaces. Thirty years of men smarter than me learning that lesson in this canyon, and the institution's one surviving skill is making sure nothing ever gets written down." He looked at Coral, and the tiredness in his face had gone all the way through to the bone. "She's offering to confess. Do you understand that there is *nowhere* to confess *to*? There's no court that has jurisdiction over a river that doesn't exist. There's no docket for a crime the file says never happened. If I take her statement, I have to file it, and if I file it, I'm the one who surfaces it, and then *I'm* the diver on the rope."

"Then cut the rope," Coral said.

Ashe closed his eyes.

For three seconds — she counted them, the way you count the seconds of a breath-hold, the way her father had taught her, *the panic lives in the counting, so count anyway* — the commander of the navy's advance element stood on the gravel flat with his eyes closed and his hands flat on the folding table, and Coral would swear for the rest of her life that in those three seconds he was somewhere else, some office, some earlier year, some version of himself who had signed on to serve the water instead of bury it.

When he opened them, the radio man called from the tent. "Commander. Base wants a sitrep. They're asking about the Marchetti woman. They know she's on the river."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Coral felt it the way she felt current change before she saw it — pressure shifting, the whole river deciding something.

Ashe straightened. When he turned back to them, the tired man was gone behind the uniform again, buttoned up, and his voice was flat and official and worse than any threat he'd made.

"Here's what happens now," he said. "I report that I have the hydrologist in custody. I report that the Voss woman and her crew are assisting with the search. And in the next forty-eight hours, before the main element arrives, this ends — one way or the other, up at the source, with whatever's there staying there." He looked at Coral, and for just a moment the tired man looked out through the uniform's eyeholes. "Last chance, Voss. Take her and your crew and walk downriver. I will log you as uncooperative and lose the paperwork. That's the last kindness I have in me, and it's not much of one, because in forty-eight hours the men who come up this canyon won't be tired old men with coffee. They'll be the ones the institution sends when it stops pretending."

"And if we don't walk?"

"Then you'd better be faster than they are." He nodded at the soldiers. "Let them go."

They let her go. They let Sela go — Sela, who stood there stunned for a heartbeat, having offered her whole ruined self on the altar and been refused, having watched Coral refuse to let her fall and not known whether that was love or lunacy. They walked back up the trail together without speaking, past the willows, past Tam's white-faced fury and Junie's shaking hands, all the way to the boat before Coral finally stopped and turned and took Sela's face in both her cold hands.

"Never do that again," Coral said.

"You'd have taken the deal for me."

"I'd have taken the deal for *any of you*. That's the problem with me. That's the whole problem with me, Sela, I'd drown myself to keep you afloat and I'd call it salvage." Her forehead came down against Sela's, breath fogging between them. "So we don't trade. Not me for you, not you for me, not any of us for any of the rest. We go up that river together or we don't go. Those are the only terms."

Sela's eyes were wet and furious and, under it, something that had been waiting eleven years to be looked at like this. "The main element comes in forty-eight hours."

"Then we've got forty-seven hours to reach a cathedral at the bottom of the world and make the river tell the truth." Coral pulled back, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and turned to her crew — Tam already untying the dry bag, Junie already at the engine, Mercer's logbook waiting in her mind like a hand on her shoulder.

"Get us upstream," she said. "All the way."

The engine caught on the second try, and the canyon took them in.
