## 26. Escape Down the Adit

Tam Okafor had spent eleven years learning how things fell apart.

It was the family trade, in a way. His mother had been a bridge inspector on the coast roads, a woman who could put her palm flat against a piling and tell you, within the year, when it would fail. She used to say that everything built by human hands carried its own death inside it — a date written into the welds and the mortar and the grain of the timber — and that her job was not to prevent it but to read it honestly. Tam had inherited the reading. He'd turned it toward engines instead of bridges, toward pumps and winches and the cranky diesel heart of the *Rivergate and Compass*, but the principle held: nothing collapsed without warning. Everything whispered before it screamed.

The mine adit above them was whispering now.

He stood at the mouth of it with his head tipped back, one hand braced against the cold rock of the portal frame, listening the way other men prayed. The adit was old — older than the navy's denials, older than Mercer's silence, maybe older than Coral's grief — a horizontal tunnel bored into the canyon wall by some company whose name had rusted off the sign decades ago. Behind them, down the slope of scree, the canyon floor waited with its helicopters and its sweep lines and Commander Ashe's patient, patient net. Ahead of them, the adit ran back into the mountain like a swallowed word, dark and dry and smelling of iron and old dust and, faintly, impossibly, water.

"Talk to me, Tam." Coral stood beside him, arms crossed against the canyon chill, her voice pitched low so it wouldn't carry. Behind her, Junie hovered with Reyes leaning heavily on his shoulder, the surveyor gray-faced and swaying, running on will and half a canteen. Sela was farther back with Mercer's logbook wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, watching the sky the way you watch a wound for infection.

Tam didn't answer right away. He was reading the portal.

The frame was timber, black with age, and it had shifted — he could see it in the way the lintel no longer sat square with the jambs, in the fan-shaped crack that spread up from the upper left corner into the rock face itself. That crack was the important thing. Cracks in rock told you where the mountain wanted to let go; they were the seams of an unfinished argument between gravity and stone. This one said the roof of the adit, somewhere back in the first twenty meters, was already failing. Already sagging toward collapse. All it needed was permission.

"Roof's rotten through the first chamber," he said finally. "Timber props are powder. There's a fault line running across the ceiling about fifteen meters in — see how the crack fans? That whole section is hanging on by habit." He turned and looked at Coral, and made himself say the rest plainly, because she deserved plainness and because saying it out loud would make him own it. "I can bring it down. Deliberately. But once I do, there's no un-bringing it. No going back out this way. Not for us, not for Ashe, not for anyone."

Coral was quiet for a long moment. In the gray light of the canyon she looked carved rather than drawn, all planes and shadow, and Tam thought — not for the first time — that she carried her father's death the way other people carried knives: close, worn smooth from handling, always within reach.

"Ashe's men reached the lower switchback," Sela called softly from behind them. "Twenty minutes. Maybe less if they're motivated."

"They're motivated," Junie said grimly. "They've been motivated since dawn."

Reyes made a small sound — not quite a laugh. "You're discussing sealing yourselves inside a mountain," she rasped. Her voice was still wrecked from the strainer, from the river, from whatever she'd seen in the source caverns that she hadn't finished telling them. "With me. I can't climb fast. I can barely walk."

"That's why we're not climbing," Tam said. "We're walking. Twenty meters in, past the collapse zone, then the tunnel levels out — feel that air?" He gestured at the adit mouth, where the darkness breathed outward, cool and steady. "Moving air means another opening somewhere. Mines always have two mouths. The company that dug this needed ventilation or their workers died, and dead workers don't dig. It comes out somewhere higher, or on the far side, or into one of these side canyons. We find it."

"And if it doesn't come out?" Reyes asked.

"Then we've bought ourselves a very quiet tomb instead of a very loud one," Tam said. "I prefer quiet."

That got the ghost of a smile out of Coral, which had been the point. But when she looked back at the adit, the smile went away, and Tam understood what she was really weighing. It wasn't the risk. Coral Voss had never once in the time he'd known her chosen the safe option when a dangerous one paid better — in truth, in loyalty, in forward motion. What she was weighing was the door itself. The way home. The *Rivergate and Compass* cached downriver with her father's compass pointing at it, the whole familiar geography of their lives sitting on the wrong side of a wall of rock he was about to build.

"You're sure about the charge?" she asked him. "No second-guessing later?"

"I'm never sure about anything," Tam said. "I'm sure enough. That's the best engineering offers anybody."

* * *

They worked fast and mostly silent, because fear makes people efficient or it makes them useless, and Tam had long ago decided which kind of crew he was going to run with.

The adit's first chamber had been a staging area once — ore carts, a winch mount, the skeletal remains of a compressor bolted to the floor. Debris lay everywhere in drifts: rotted planking, coils of wire gone to red lace, drums split open and bleached pale. And everywhere, half-buried, the machinery of the secret operation — hoists stamped with a company name that matched nothing in any registry Sela had ever found, rail sections too heavy for anything legal, a crane arm long enough to lift things the size of small boats. The scale of it sat in Tam's stomach like swallowed stone. Somebody had moved mountains here. Somebody had moved *water* here. And then somebody had walked away and let the mountain keep the receipt.

He gathered what he needed from the debris while the others hauled Reyes deeper along the tunnel wall, past the worst of the sagging roof, to a bend in the adit where the rock closed around them like a cupped hand. Junie came back twice to help him carry — the kid complained the entire time, quietly, constantly, in the low muttering register that Tam had learned to read as affection — and together they stacked the charges.

There wasn't much. A salvage boat carries blasting supplies the way a church carries snakes: carefully, rarely, and only because the work sometimes demands it. Tam had three sticks of gelignite left from a stump-clearing job two seasons ago, sweating slightly in their waxed paper, plus a spool of det cord and a handful of caps he'd kept dry in a tin because keeping things dry was half of who he was. Not enough to blast a proper collapse. Plenty to *ask* the mountain to finish one.

"The trick," he told Junie, as they tamped the gelignite into the crack at the base of the fault line, packing rubble over it with the flat of a shovel, "is that I'm not blowing anything up. I'm introducing a suggestion. The roof wants to fall. It has wanted to fall for forty years. I'm just being persuasive."

"You're being insane," Junie said, tamping harder. "This is insane. We are sealing ourselves in a hole in the ground with a dying man's logbook and a woman who can't run, and the plan is *geology*."

"The plan has always been geology," Tam said. "Rivers are geology. Canyons are geology. The whole reason we're in this mess is that somebody forgot the river was geology and thought it was property." He seated the last cap, checked the wire twice, then a third time because his mother had raised him that way, and unspooled the det cord back around the bend to where the others waited. "Geology doesn't take sides, Junie. That's the only mercy in it."

Sela had gotten a small fire going in a hollow of the rock — smokeless, nearly, a handful of dry scrub — and Reyes lay beside it with her eyes open, watching the ceiling. Coral stood apart, holding the compass. Even here, even in the dark, Tam could see it in her hands: the brass case catching the firelight, the needle doing its slow wrong thing, drifting upstream toward the source. Toward her father. She wasn't looking at it. She was looking at the tunnel ahead, into the black, and Tam recognized the expression because he'd seen it on divers' faces his whole life — the look of someone standing at the edge of water they can't see the bottom of, deciding to go anyway.

"How long?" Coral asked him.

"Fuse burns thirty seconds after I trigger it. Collapse takes another ten, fifteen. If the mountain cooperates." He held up the firing spool — a simple magneto detonator, thumb-triggered, salvaged off the boat's emergency kit. "When I squeeze this, everybody gets flat against the outside wall, mouths open, hands over ears. Dust will be biblical. Then we wait, and we listen, and if the air keeps moving, we go deeper."

"And if the air stops?"

"Then we start digging with our hands and praying with our lungs."

Junie laughed — high, cracked, real. "You should write greeting cards, Tam."

"I contain multitudes," Tam said. "Get down."

* * *

The last thing he saw of the canyon, before he thumbed the trigger, was the light.

Late afternoon came slanting down the gorge in bars of gold, laying stripes across the scree slope, touching the tops of the twisted pines on the rim. Somewhere out in that light were men with rifles and a commander with a signed offer and a debt ledger, and somewhere beyond them, days downstream, was a boat named for a story nobody had ever actually told him, and a dock, and creditors, and the whole small warm fragile life they'd built out of broke-ness and stubbornness. Tam looked at it for exactly as long as it took to memorize it. Then he looked at his crew — Coral kneeling with the compass, Sela's hand resting light between Reyes's shoulder blades, Junie flat on the ground with his fists clenched like a kid on a rollercoaster — and he squeezed.

The sound arrived in two parts.

First the *crack* — sharp, obscene, a slap of pressure that hit his chest and his eardrums and the wet backs of his eyes all at once. Then the mountain's answer: a deep, rolling groan that came up through the floor and into his bones, followed by a rumble like the throat of something enormous clearing itself after centuries of silence. Dust exploded past them in a hot gray wall, choking, tasting of sulfur and stone, and the firelight died, and the world became a single color and a single sound — the grinding, settling, final weight of rock finding the shape it had always intended to return to.

Tam pressed his face into the crook of his arm and counted. Ten. Twenty. Thirty. The rumble diminished by degrees, breaking into smaller voices — a crack here, a slide there, the tick and whisper of settling — until at last there was only the ringing in his ears and the sound of six people breathing hard in absolute blackness.

"Well," Junie's voice said, out of the dark, muffled and shaking. "Is anybody else's entire body made of static, or just mine?"

"Mine too," Sela said.

"Light," Coral said. "Somebody give me light."

Tam fumbled the storm lantern off his belt and lit it with hands he was proud to notice were almost steady. The flame took, wavered, steadied, and threw its circle out against the dust-thick air.

Where the adit had run open toward the day, there was now a wall.

Not a neat wall — a *true* one, the kind no engineer could draft and no crew could argue with. Rubble had fallen in a great natural fan, boulders the size of carts interlocked with shattered timbers and finer crush, packed tight by the mountain's own weight, sealed at every seam with dust. Here and there thin threads of daylight still threaded through, needle-fine, already dimming as the dust settled into the cracks. In an hour there would be nothing. In an hour the door would not even be a memory.

Reyes started to laugh. It was a terrible sound, ragged and disbelieving, and then it turned into coughing, and Sela held her shoulders until it passed. "You did it," the surveyor wheezed. "You actually — you blew up the way home."

"No," Tam said quietly. He got to his feet, lifted the lantern, and turned it toward the tunnel ahead, where the darkness ran back into the mountain, deep and level and breathing. He could feel the faint cool pull of moving air against his cheek. Moving air meant another mouth. Moving air meant the mountain had kept a door open for them, somewhere, and all they had to do was find it.

"I closed a door," he said. "Now we go find the one that's still open."

Coral rose beside him. She looked at the wall of rubble for a long moment — at the last gray thread of daylight dying in the cracks — and Tam watched her do the same arithmetic he had done, the same farewell, the same turning of the key. When she spoke, her voice was steady as bedrock.

"Mercer's logbook says the old workings connect to the caverns below the containment grid." She tapped the oilcloth bundle under Sela's arm. "If he's right, this tunnel doesn't just lead out. It leads *down*. To the source. To my father's last dive."

"Then we'd better hope your father was good at leaving directions," Junie said, hauling himself upright and brushing dust from his hair, which only made him grayer.

"He was good at leaving everything," Coral said. "Directions most of all."

Tam lifted the lantern higher and led them into the dark, and behind them the mountain settled one final inch, closing the last seam, and the sound it made was soft as a door shutting gently in an empty house.
