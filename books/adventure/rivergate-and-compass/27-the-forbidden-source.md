## 27. The Forbidden Source

The river ended before the canyon did.

Coral felt it first as a wrongness in her boots — the ground trembling faintly, a vibration like something enormous breathing beneath the stone. Then she heard it: not the chatter of rapids or the slap of current against rock, but a deep, continuous roar, the sound of water being born somewhere ahead in the dark.

They had come the last three miles on foot, hauling Reyes between them on a litter Tam had lashed from pack frames and rope, Sela walking point with the compass held flat in her palm like an offering. The dry season had stripped the river to its bones behind them — gravel bars exposed like ribs, pools gone stagnant and warm — but here, where the canyon walls pinched together into a slot barely wider than the boat they'd abandoned two days ago, the air was wet. Cool. It smelled of limestone and iron and something older, mineral and vast, like the inside of the earth holding its breath.

Sela stopped at the mouth of the slot and raised one hand.

"Listen," she said, though there was no need. The sound filled everything.

Beyond her shoulder, Coral could see it: light — not sunlight, but a pale, sourceless glow rising out of the rock ahead, blue-green as deep water, as if the canyon itself had swallowed a piece of the sky and never given it back.

"Thirty years," Mercer would have said. *Thirty years I ran boats past this bend and never knew what was singing.*

She pushed the grief down where it lived now, a cold stone behind her sternum, and followed Sela into the dark.

* * *

The passage opened all at once, the way a cathedral opens when you come through the narthex — no gradual widening, just sudden impossible space, and Coral's breath left her in a single involuntary syllable that echoed up and up and did not come back.

The cavern was a hollowed mountain. Her headlamp couldn't find the ceiling; the beam simply dissolved into blackness above, and the glow that lit the chamber rose from below — from the water itself. A pool occupied the cavern floor, wide as a harbor, unnaturally still except at its center, where the river came up.

It came *up*. That was the thing her mind kept refusing and refusing again. A column of water surged from some vent in the bedrock, boiling silently at this distance, a standing wave of white and green that fed the pool and spilled over a lip of natural stone at the far end to become — impossibly, quietly — the Rivergate itself, the same river she'd dived beneath since she was nine years old, the same brown patient water that carried barges and drowned drunks and paid her rent. All of it. All of it started here, in a room no map admitted existed.

"It's an artesian rise," Sela said softly. She'd gone still in the way she went still when data was moving through her, Coral had learned — a kind of reverence that looked like paralysis. "A spring fed under pressure from the aquifer. The whole valley drinks from this. Every well, every field, every child." Her voice caught. "And look what they built around it."

Coral looked.

Ringed around the pool, bolted to the living rock, stood the containment structures. They were rusted now, decades of mineral breath flaking them orange and brown, but their geometry was unmistakable — industrial, deliberate, military. Concrete plinths anchored steel gantries that arched out over the water. Pipes as wide as road culverts ran from the pool's edge into the walls, capped with valves the size of dinner plates. A catwalk system spiderwebbed along the eastern wall, and hanging from it, swaying gently in the cavern's slow respiration, were chains — long ones, ending in hooks, ending in nothing, or in things too corroded to name.

It was a machine for holding down a river. And it was dying.

"Tam," Coral said. Her voice sounded small and flattened by the space. "Tell me what you see."

Tam crouched at the nearest plinth, running a gloved thumb through the rust, and when he spoke he didn't look up. "I see a containment grid that was engineered for maybe twenty years of service and has run thirty-five. I see stress fractures in those gantry legs. I see—" He stopped. Started again. "I see why the navy keeps survey teams away from here. Not because of what's in the water. Because of what happens when this fails."

"Which is when?" Junie asked. She had Reyes's head cradled against her shoulder; the surveyor slept in fevered fragments, waking to murmur coordinates and names.

"Years," Tam said. "Maybe less. Maybe more. But it's failing. Look at the seep lines." He pointed, and Coral saw them — dark streaks weeping from the base of the concrete, staining the rock below in fan-shaped shadows, like the cavern was crying rust-colored tears into its own pool.

Sela knelt and touched one of the stains, then held her fingers to her headlamp. The residue gleamed faintly, oily, iridescent.

"Runoff," she said. "From the mines upstream of the aquifer. It's been bleeding into the rise for decades. This is what my data showed. This is what got me exiled for saying." She wiped her hand slowly on her thigh, leaving a gray smear. "The river isn't sick yet. But it's carrying the sickness up from below, and every year the dose gets bigger."

The roar of the rising water filled the silence none of them wanted to fill.

* * *

It was Junie who found the graves.

She'd wandered along the catwalk while Tam and Sela argued in low voices about valves and pressure heads, and Coral realized she'd lost sight of her only when Junie's voice came back to them across the water — thin, strange, stripped of everything that made it Junie.

"Coral. Coral, you need to come see this."

The catwalk ran along the eastern wall to a shelf of natural rock behind the last of the gantries, sheltered from the mineral spray, and there the navy had done its housekeeping. There were eleven graves.

They were marked with dive tags — brass tags, the kind stamped with serial numbers and blood types, nailed to wooden crosses that had long since silvered and split. Some of the crosses had rotted to stubs; the tags hung from new nails, replaced at intervals, which meant someone still came. Someone still maintained this room of the dead the way you'd maintain any other piece of equipment.

Coral walked the row slowly. Eleven tags. Eleven serial numbers. She didn't touch them. Her hands stayed at her sides, curled, as if touching them would make the row longer.

"Divers," Sela said quietly, beside her. She'd followed without Coral hearing, which frightened her a little. "Sent down to work the grid. To keep it patched. It's the only explanation that fits — someone had to maintain this place, and you can't put soldiers underwater for thirty years without records, without rotation, without questions."

"So they used divers who wouldn't be missed."

"They used divers whose deaths could be explained." Sela's voice was very level, the way it went flat when she was holding something down. "Drowning is a generous accident. It leaves no marks, requires no investigation anyone can sustain, and rivers are full of them anyway. Especially rivers full of salvage divers with debts."

Coral stopped walking.

The tag at the end of the row was newer than the others. The cross beneath it was younger wood, unweathered, and the stamping on the brass was crisp enough to read by lamplight. She read it twice because the first time her eyes slid off it like water off wax.

V-1147. VILLALOBOS, E.

Her father's middle initial was wrong. Everything else was right.

The cavern roared. The water rose and rose and rose out of the heart of the earth, and Coral stood in front of a wooden cross in a room the government denied existed, and found that she had known — had known somewhere below thought, below grief, in the diver's part of her that reads pressure and current without being told — that this was how the story ended. That the compass hadn't been drifting all these years. It had been pointing at his grave.

"Coral." Sela's hand hovered near her elbow, not quite touching. "Coral, breathe."

"He didn't drown in the shipping channel." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, which felt like watching someone else speak. "They told my mother he drowned in the shipping channel. They gave us a box. There wasn't anything in it. I was twelve and I thought that was normal, that was just what happened to divers, the river keeps what it takes." She laughed, one dry note that bounced off the unseen ceiling. "The river doesn't keep anything. The river gives everything back. It's the only honest thing in this whole valley. *They're* the ones who keep."

Junie had come up the catwalk behind them and gone very still. Tam stood at the far end of the row of crosses, hat in his hands, an old-country gesture he probably didn't know he was making.

"I'm sorry," Sela said. "I suspected. When I saw the compass, I suspected. Your father came to me eight years ago with hydrographic readings from this reach. He said he'd found something the charts lied about. I published what he gave me and they destroyed me for it, and he—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He said he needed to go back down. One more dive. He said he'd found a way to seal testimony inside the grid itself, somewhere no one would purge it, because purging it would mean admitting the grid existed. I told him it was suicide. I told him the truth could wait until it could be survived."

"And?"

"And he smiled at me the way you smile, and he went." Sela's eyes were bright and unblinking in the lamplight. "I have spent eight years telling myself the river took him. Standing here, I understand that I sent him."

The words landed in Coral like ballast dropping. She thought of chapter after chapter of her own anger — at the navy, at the creditors, at the faceless *they* who had taken her father and buried him in paperwork — and understood, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with heights, that the story had no villains clean enough to hate. There was Sela, who had loved the truth more than the man carrying it. There was Mercer, who had run the supply boats and sworn silence. There was herself, who had dove the wreck for money and sat on the sealed case for weeks because opening it might cost her the contract. Everyone in this valley had a hand on the lid. Some hands pressed harder than others.

"We should go back," Tam said gently, from the end of the row. "Reyes needs rest. We need to cache our gear, set watches, figure out water. We've got maybe—"

"I'm not leaving him here." Coral heard her own voice and didn't recognize it — not angry, exactly. Decided. "Not in this room. Not under their chains."

"No one's asking you to," Tam said. "But Coral — look around. This is the heart of it. Everything Reyes decoded, everything in your father's logbook, everything Sela bled for — it all points here. Whatever we do next, we do it from this room. So let's do it awake, and fed, and together. He's waited thirty years. He'll wait one more night."

The terrible thing was that he was right, and the worse thing was that Coral knew it, and the worst thing of all was that when she looked down the row of crosses one final time, she found herself doing what divers do — counting. Eleven tags. Eleven men sent down to hold the river's mouth shut.

And somewhere in the grid above the pool, sealed against corrosion and time and the indifference of institutions, her father's voice was waiting in the dark, patient as water, for someone to finally come and listen.

* * *

They made camp on a shelf of rock high above the pool, back from the spray line, where the cavern's glow turned everything the color of shallow sea. Tam rigged the stove behind a windbreak of packs. Junie fed Reyes broth spoonful by spoonful, murmuring to her like the surveyor was a spooked animal, which, Coral reflected, they all were. Sela sat apart with her instruments, logging readings by lamplight, recording the seep concentrations, the flow rate, the fracture patterns — building the case even now, even here, because building the case was the only prayer she knew.

Coral sat at the shelf's edge with the compass open in her palm.

Down here, in the presence of the source, it had gone quiet. No drift, no tremble, none of the restless hunting that had plagued it since the day she'd pulled it from her father's footlocker. The needle lay steady, pointing not north but inward — toward the pool, toward the rising column of water, toward the grid of rusted steel where eleven crosses kept their vigil.

"You knew," she whispered to it. "All these years. You weren't broken. You were just pointing at the one place nobody would look."

Below her, the river rose out of the earth in its endless silent boil, feeding the water that fed the valley that fed her, and the chains hung swaying over it, and the pipes ran into the dark, and somewhere in the tangle of steel her father's last words waited like a sealed case, like a wreck, like everything else in her life worth salvaging — sunk, denied, and stubbornly, patiently real.

Tam came and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. Neither of them spoke for a long time. The cavern breathed around them.

"Tomorrow," he said finally. "We go down tomorrow."

Coral closed the compass. In the glow of the rising river, her father's grave markers stood their watch, and the water sang its one long note, and she understood that whatever they found in the grid — testimony, evidence, absolution — there would be no putting the lid back on any of it.

"Tomorrow," she said.
