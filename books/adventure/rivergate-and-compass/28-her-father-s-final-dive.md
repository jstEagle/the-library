## 28. Her Father's Final Dive

The cathedral held its breath.

Coral stood at the water's edge where the river rose out of the earth — the true source, the place every map in Rivergate pretended ended at a blank space and a printed warning — and she felt the way she always felt at the top of a dive ladder: that suspended moment between who she was on the surface and who she would become below.

Except there was no ladder here. There was only the containment grid, a lattice of rusted steel and concrete rising from the water like the ribs of something enormous that had swallowed the river whole and never digested it. Decades of mineral stain ran down its pillars in orange and black curtains. Somewhere above, filtered through the cavern's high throat of stone, daylight came down thin as thread.

Tam had spent two hours with the logbook open on a flat rock, cross-referencing Mercer's cramped handwriting against the grid itself, counting bays, checking weld seams. Junie had rigged three work lights off the boat's battery bank, and their glow turned the water a strange, holy green, the color of bottle glass held up to sun.

"It's here," Tam said finally, not looking up. "Bay eleven. He marked it — see? The little triangle. Same symbol he used on the wreck charts." She tapped the page. "But Coral — the bay is flooded. Has to be. Look at the waterline staining. It's been under since before you were born."

"Then I dive it."

"Coral." Sela's voice, quiet, from behind her. "Let me say the thing nobody else will. If your father sealed testimony down there, and the navy has been guarding this place for thirty years, they may have already found it. They may have taken it. You could go down and come back with nothing but cold water in your lungs and—"

"Then I'll know," Coral said. "That's more than I've had since I was nine years old."

Sela didn't argue again. That was one of the things Coral was learning about her — she argued like the river moved, persistent, finding every gap, but when she met bedrock she went around it and waited.

* * *

They suited up in the green light, and it felt like a ritual none of them had planned. Tam checked Coral's valves twice, then a third time without being asked, her hands steady in the way they only got when she was frightened and refusing to show it. Junie held the regulator mouthpiece while Coral pulled her hood on, an old dock habit, and squeezed her shoulder through the neoprene hard enough to mean something.

"Bottom time's whatever the logbook says he had," Junie said. "Not a minute more. You hear me? He had thirty years of experience and the river took him anyway. You get one hour and then you come up or I come down after you, and I'm a terrible diver."

"You're a terrible swimmer," Coral said around the mouthpiece, voice muffled and strange to her own ears.

"Both things are true."

Mercer's logbook lay open on the rock, weighted with a wrench, its pages warped and soft as old skin. Coral had read the final entries so many times she could recite them. *Ran the grid survey with E. Voss and D. Okafor. Bay eleven holds. Eli says if anything happens, the truth is in the walls.* And beneath that, in a different hand — her father's hand, the handwriting she'd inherited along with his jaw and his stubbornness — a single line:

*If I don't surface, tell my daughter the compass was never broken.*

She had been carrying that sentence for twenty-two years without knowing what it meant. Tonight, standing at the edge of the source with her father's compass strapped to her wrist inside a waterproof housing Sela had improvised from a sample canister, she thought she finally understood. The compass had never pointed north. It had pointed here. It had been pointing here the whole time, through every harbor and every dead-end job and every dive for other people's sunken property, patient as geology, waiting for her to stop fighting it.

"Compass check," Tam said.

Coral raised her wrist. Inside the housing, the needle swung once, twice, and settled — not north, not toward the entrance, but straight into the flooded heart of bay eleven, quivering slightly, like a finger pointing at a name written on a wall.

"That's not magnetic anomaly," Sela said softly. She'd explained it on the portage, in her careful hydrologist's way: the compass card was weighted, modified, someone had doctored it decades ago to seek a specific bearing — probably a steel marker, a rebar stake, something driven into the grid itself. Her father hadn't just hidden his testimony. He'd built a key and given it to her before she knew there was a lock.

"He knew I'd come," Coral said. Her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it.

"He hoped," Sela corrected gently. "Hoping is braver. Hoping means he did it knowing you might never have to."

Coral looked at her — this woman who had lost her career, her village, her whole life to this same secret, and who still chose precision over comfort even now — and nodded. Then she spat her regulator back in, gave Tam the thumbs-down, and stepped off the ledge into the river's first breath.

* * *

The water closed over her and the world went green and silent.

It was warmer than she expected. The source water came up from deep aquifer, and it carried a faint mineral sweetness, a taste she registered through her teeth more than her tongue. Her lights carved cones through water so clear it seemed embarrassed to be touched, and the containment grid rose around her like architecture — not wreckage, not ruin, but *built*, deliberate, column after column marching away into the dark, each one stenciled with numbers that thirty years of rust had half-erased.

BAY 7. BAY 8. BAY 9.

Her father had swum here. That thought arrived with physical force, a pressure behind her sternum greater than any depth. Elias Voss, twenty-nine years old, in gear cruder than hers by two generations, had kicked through this same green silence with Mercer beside him and a secret in his teeth. Had he been afraid? Of course he'd been afraid. But he'd done it anyway, and then he'd done it again, and the last time the river had kept him.

Coral had spent her whole life angry at that river. Swimming through it now, she felt the anger change shape. This wasn't the thing that had taken him. This was the thing he'd tried to give her.

BAY 10. BAY 11.

The needle on her wrist trembled and swung left, and there it was.

Bay eleven looked like all the others from a distance — a square cage of steel and concrete, silt drifting across its floor in slow galaxies. But as her light swept the near face, she saw it: a rebar stake driven into a seam, wrapped in faded cloth that might once have been red. And tied to the stake, a length of braided wire running down into a recessed panel near the base of the column, where the concrete had been poured around a steel box the size of a ship's strongbox.

Someone had tried to open it before. She saw the marks immediately — chisel scars, fresh-ish, maybe years old rather than decades, the rust squeezed out of the metal in bright blooms around each strike. The navy had found the box. The navy had not opened it. Her chest went tight with something between triumph and terror: they'd known it was here and they couldn't crack it, which meant her father had built it better than they could break it, which meant —

Which meant it needed the compass.

She hovered in front of the panel, breathing hard enough now that her bubbles broke upward in ragged strings, and studied it the way Tam had taught her to study engines: not what it was, but what it wanted. There was a slot. Narrow, horizontal, machined edges still bright under the rust bloom. And stamped above it, so small she had to press her mask almost to the steel to read it:

*BEARING.*

Her heart slammed. Of course. Of course. Not a lock that took a key — a lock that took a *number*. The compass wasn't pointing at the box; the compass was the combination. Her father had set the card to seek a bearing, and that bearing, read to whatever precision the instrument allowed, was the code.

She backed off, steadied herself with one glove on the column, and raised her wrist. The needle settled. She read it against the housing's etched degree ring, then read it again, then a third time, because her eyes were burning and she refused to trust them.

Two hundred and sixty-one degrees. Two-six-one.

She rotated the bezel ring on the compass housing — Sela had wondered aloud why it was knurled, why it clicked in detents, why a simple sighting compass needed a ratcheted bezel at all — and stopped it at 261. Then she slid the whole assembly into the slot.

For one long heartbeat, nothing. The green silence pressed in. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of water and stone, she imagined she could hear Junie pacing the ledge.

Then the mechanism woke. A sound traveled up through her gloves, through her bones — heavy tumblers falling, one, two, three, a language of iron she felt more than heard — and the panel exhaled a cloud of rust as bolts sheathed in thirty years of corrosion drew back into the column.

The box door swung open six inches and stopped, hinged, obedient.

Inside, dry as the day it was sealed — packed in waxed canvas, nested in grease-blackened cloth — lay a waterproof case, military grade, with a brass clasp and a strip of tape across the lid. On the tape, in her father's handwriting, letters slightly blurred by the adhesive bleeding through the ink:

*FOR CORAL. WHEN SHE'S READY.*

Coral Voss had been diving since she was twelve. She had worked wrecks in flood current, cut herself free of strainers, watched Mercer take a bullet for her crew and kept her hands on the wheel. She had not cried at his funeral because she was nine and someone told her strong girls didn't, and she had not cried since because the habit calcified.

She cried now, alone, forty feet down in the river that had taken him, laughing bubbles and sobbing in the same breath, holding a waterproof case against her chest plate while her tears vanished instantly into the water that surrounded her, as if the river were drinking them, accepting them, forgiving her for taking twenty-two years.

When she could breathe evenly again, she tucked the case into her harness bag, clipped it twice, checked it twice more, and looked back once at the open box — at the empty slot where the compass had been, at the dark interior that smelled, even through the water, faintly of machine grease and old paper and, impossibly, of him.

*Tell my daughter the compass was never broken.*

"I'm here, Dad," she said. The words came out as a burst of silver, tumbling upward through the green. "I'm ready."

She kicked for the surface, and the cathedral's columns slid past her like the pillars of a church after the last hymn, and the river carried her up the way rivers carry everything eventually — patiently, and toward the light.

* * *

They opened the case on the flat rock under the work lights, all four of them kneeling around it like children at a fire, and Sela insisted on photographing every stage with the survey camera before anyone touched the contents — chain of custody, she called it, though whose custody, and before whom, none of them could yet say.

Inside: a sealed dive slate, the old ceramic kind, wrapped in oilcloth. A roll of film in a lead pouch. And a notebook — a real paper notebook, waterproof stock, its cover stamped with navy property numbers that someone had deliberately defaced with a chisel, the same chisel marks as the box, the same hand.

Elias Voss had stolen government property to tell the truth. Coral felt a fierce, unexpected pride bloom in her chest.

The slate held one message, scratched deep with a carbide pencil so it would survive anything short of fire:

*THEY KNEW IN '61. THE RUNOFF WAS NEVER AN ACCIDENT. THE CONTAINMENT WAS BUILT TO FAIL SLOWLY SO NO ONE COULD PIN A DATE ON IT. NAMES AND NUMBERS IN THE BOOK. IF I DIE, IT'S NOT THE RIVER.*

Below that, smaller, the pencil pressing lighter, the handwriting hurrying:

*Mercer knows. Trust Mercer. Forgive Mercer. He stayed so someone would remember where I'm buried.*

Coral looked up. Mercer's logbook sat closed on the rock beside her, and beyond it, upstream, somewhere past the narrows they'd run under fire, lay a grave at the river bend where an old pilot had asked to be buried facing the water. *He stayed so someone would remember.* Thirty years Mercer had carried that — the location, the guilt, the silence — and he had paid for it in blood in the Narrows, and he had put the logbook in her hands with his last strength and said *make the river tell the truth.*

She had thought that was a request. Reading her father's slate, she understood it had been a delivery. Mercer hadn't been passing her a tool. He'd been completing a relay that began the night Elias Voss failed to surface, passed hand to hand across three decades of complicity and grief, and ended — was ending right now — with her.

"The film," Tam said quietly. "And the book. We should see—"

"No." Coral closed the notebook and kept her hand flat on it. "Not tonight. Not by work light in a cave with Ashe's helicopters sweeping the canyon." She looked at each of them — Tam with her engine-grease hands, Junie with her chin trembling and her jaw set against it, Sela with her eyes bright and wet and absolutely steady. "Tomorrow we start south with Reyes's data and this, and we finish what he started. All of it. Every name, every number, every grave."

"And Ashe?" Junie asked.

Coral picked up the compass. The needle, freed from its slot, unweighted, unmodified — no, not unmodified; she could feel the difference now, understand at last what her father had built — swung lazily, honestly, and settled pointing south. Downriver. Toward Rivergate. Toward the choice that waited at the end of the water like a lit window.

"Ashe gets the same thing the river gets," she said. "The truth. Whether he wants it or not."

Above them, far up the cavern throat, a sound drifted down through the stone — the distant, rhythmic thrum of rotors, sweeping the canyon in the dark, closing like a hand.

Tam killed the work lights.
