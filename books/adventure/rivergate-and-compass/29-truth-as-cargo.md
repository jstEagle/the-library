## 29. Truth as Cargo

Junie had never thought of herself as a person who carried things. She was the smallest on the crew, the one who got handed the light loads and the lookout shifts, the one who fit through hatches and crawlspaces and the narrow gaps between cargo crates where nobody else bothered to look. Carrying was other people's work. Tam carried the engines. Coral carried her father's ghost. Mercer had carried his guilt thirty years downriver until it killed him.

But standing in the mouth of the source cavern with a waterproof satchel heavy against her hip, Junie understood that she was carrying something now, and that it weighed more than any crate she'd ever hauled across a deck.

Inside the satchel: Mercer's logbook, its pages soft as cloth from decades of damp. Imani Reyes's decoded data drives, wrapped in a sock to keep them from rattling. Coral's father's testimony, pried from the containment grid two days ago, still smelling faintly of rust and old rubber. And printed out in Tam's careful block capitals on waterproof paper — because Tam trusted paper more than electronics, and after everything, who could blame him — the full accounting of what the river's source actually was.

Not a spring. A wound. Thirty years of concealed runoff from a mining operation the navy had never officially acknowledged, bleeding into the aquifer that fed every village from the bend to Rivergate itself. Slow poison, measured in parts per million, documented by divers who had been sent down to keep it quiet and had stayed down there instead.

Junie turned the satchel over in her hands and felt the weight shift, and thought: *this is a cargo like any other.* It had a destination. It had handlers. It had a value, and values could be argued about, and people could die over them. She'd grown up on the docks watching men fight over cargoes worth a fraction of this.

The difference was that this cargo was arguing back.

* * *

They'd made camp on the high ledge above the cavern mouth, where the rock shelved out over the water like a brow over an eye. The source pooled below them, impossibly clear in the failing light, so clear you could see forty feet down to where the rusted bones of the containment structures spread across the riverbed like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down there to die. The graves were upriver, marked with dive tags strung on wire. Coral had spent an hour there at dusk, saying nothing, touching nothing, just reading names.

Now the fire was small — Tam insisted on small fires now, small fires and cold food, because smoke drew helicopters — and the five of them sat around it in the order they'd fallen into over the weeks: Coral with her back to the stone, Sela close enough that their shoulders touched, Tam cross-legged with a wrench he wasn't using, turning it over like worry beads. Imani lay propped against a pack, her color better than it had been, her hands steady enough to hold tea. And Junie sat with the satchel in her lap because nobody else wanted to touch it.

"So let's say it plain," Tam said, because Tam always said it plain eventually, usually when the silence had gone on long enough to hurt. "Three doors. We sell it to Ashe. We leak it wide. Or we hand it to the villages." He looked around the fire. "And every door has somebody standing behind it with their hand out or their fist up. So we'd best decide who we're willing to pay."

"Nobody's selling anything," Coral said. Flat. Final. The voice she used on the boat when a decision was already made.

But Junie had been watching Coral for three years, through storms and salvage runs and the bad winter when they'd eaten rice for a month, and she knew that voice wasn't final. That voice was Coral nailing a door shut so she wouldn't have to look at what was behind it. Junie lifted the satchel a little. "Then why are we all sitting here like it's settled? If it's settled, why is the cargo still in my lap?"

"Because it isn't settled," Sela said quietly. "And it shouldn't be. Not yet."

* * *

Sela laid it out the way she laid out everything — like water finding its level, patient, unstoppable, indifferent to whether you liked where it went.

"Ashe's offer first," she said. "Debt relief. Immunity. Probably money, if Coral pushed. In exchange, the evidence goes into a navy archive and stays there until everyone who remembers the river before the contamination is dead." She held up one finger. "What it costs: the aquifer keeps poisoning. Every village downstream drinks a little more of it every year. The children at the bend — you saw them, Junie, the ones with the rashes — they keep getting them. And in twenty years some other diver finds another sealed case, and we start over."

"That's not a door," Tam muttered. "That's a trap painted to look like a door."

"It's a door someone will walk through," Sela said. "So we name it honestly." Second finger. "The leak. Everything to every news outlet, every agency, every inbox we can reach. Maximum noise. What it costs: the moment it goes public, the evidence becomes a legal battlefield. Lawyers will spend years arguing chain of custody while the company lawyers argue contamination thresholds. And Ashe's institution doesn't go down quietly — it goes down swinging, and the swing lands on whoever's closest. On us. On the bend village, which took us in. On Sela—" her voice didn't waver but Junie saw her hand close slightly, "—who has already been exiled once for exactly this."

"And the third," Coral said, though her voice had gone rough, like she was paying for each word.

"The villages." Sela opened her third finger slowly. "We give the data to the river communities first. They get the truth before anyone else, they get time to organize, to test their own wells, to demand remediation with the evidence in their own hands. What it costs—" she paused, and Junie realized Sela was choosing her words with a care that frightened her, "—is speed. Villages move at the speed of trust. By the time the story reaches the outside world, months could pass. Months in which Ashe's people can find us, take the evidence, and bury it properly this time. Months in which the flood Sela says is coming off that failing containment grid—" she nodded toward the dark water below, "—arrives without anyone downstream warned."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the river moved through its cathedral with a sound like slow breathing.

Junie looked down at the satchel. "So it's fast or fair," she said. "Sell it and it's neither. Leak it and it's fast but it's chaos. Give it to the villages and it's fair but it might be too late." She laughed, and hated how thin the laugh came out. "Mercer used to say there's no cargo so valuable you can't afford to lose it, and no cargo so cheap you can afford to sink with it. He never told me which one this was."

"He'd have known," Tam said. His voice came out strange, and Junie looked up and saw that Tam's eyes were wet in the firelight, and that he wasn't ashamed of it. "He'd have known because he stood at this exact fire thirty years ago, metaphorically speaking, and he chose the archive. He chose silence. He spent his whole life paying for it, and then he spent his blood on these rocks paying some more." Tam set the wrench down. "I loved that old man. So let's not pretend his vote isn't in the room. His vote is: don't do what I did."

Nobody spoke for a while after that.

* * *

It was Imani who broke the silence, and she did it the way she seemed to do everything now — slowly, deliberately, like someone relearning how much force the world required.

"You're framing it wrong," she said. All four of them turned. She shifted against the pack, wincing, and continued anyway. "You're framing it as one choice about one cargo. But evidence isn't cargo. Cargo sits still. Evidence *moves* — it moves through people. The question isn't where it goes first. The question is who carries it next, and whether they can be trusted to keep carrying."

"Meaning what?" Junie asked.

"Meaning you don't have to pick a single door." Imani wrapped both hands around her tin cup. "My team mapped this tributary for six weeks before they—" she stopped, and Junie watched her swallow whatever lived in that pause, six colleagues and a flooded camp and bodies in navy gear in the source caverns, "—before the water took the camp. And here's what six weeks taught me: the navy doesn't want this evidence destroyed. Destroyed evidence can be rediscovered. They want it *owned*. Owned evidence is dead evidence. It sits in a classified annex and does nothing forever." She looked at Coral. "Ashe knows that. I think Ashe is more trapped than dangerous, but his institution isn't trapped, it's *functioning*, and this is what it functions to do."

"So we don't give it to him," Coral said.

"We don't give it to anyone," Imani said. "We split it."

Junie felt the idea land in her chest before she understood it fully, the way you feel a change in current before you see the water move.

"Copies," Imani said. "Tam can make copies — you've got the equipment, you've made copies already, don't look surprised, I counted the drives. One set goes downstream to the bend village, to their council, to the elders who've been drinking this water for thirty years without being told. That's the debt owed first. Another set goes to Sela's old colleagues, the hydrologists, the people who can read this data and make it scream in a language institutions understand. And a third set—" she paused, "—goes somewhere Ashe can't ever fully own. Broadcast fragments. Scattered. Enough that even if he takes the rest, taking it all becomes impossible."

"And the original?" Sela asked softly.

Imani looked at the dark water below the ledge. "The original goes where your father put his testimony. Into the river's keeping. Let the thing they poisoned hold the proof against them."

* * *

It should have ended there. Junie would remember afterward that it almost did — that for one whole minute the fire crackled and the plan hung in the air like something nearly finished, and Tam was already reaching for his tool roll, and Coral's face had done something complicated and loosening, like a knot working itself free.

Then Sela said, "There's a problem with the timing," and the minute died.

"The containment grid," Sela said. "I've been studying it since we got here. It was built to fail — that's not cynicism, that's engineering, the maintenance logs Mercer copied show inspections stopped eleven years ago. When it fails, and it will fail soon, maybe within days, the impounded runoff behind it releases all at once. A black flood, moving down this canyon and into the main channel at however many cubic meters per second the breach allows." She met each of their eyes in turn. "The bend village is twelve hours downstream. If the grid fails at night, unannounced—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Junie had seen the flood line at the drowned camp, the brown stain on the rocks four meters up, and she had done the arithmetic in her head every night since, and the arithmetic always came out the same way: *not enough warning, not enough height, not enough time.*

"So the evidence plan and the flood plan are the same plan," Coral said slowly. "The villages need the truth *and* the warning, together, or neither saves them."

"And Ashe is upstream of both problems," Tam said grimly, "with helicopters, and a signed offer sitting in Coral's pocket that solves all of his problems at once if she just—"

"I'm not taking it." Coral's voice cracked across the ledge like a whip. Then, quieter, wrecked: "I'm not taking it. Stop offering me ways to take it."

"I'm not offering," Tam said. "I'm telling you what he thinks. Because he's going to come ask you himself, probably tomorrow, probably with guns, and you need to know your own answer cold before he asks. That's all. That's all I'm doing."

Junie hugged the satchel to her chest and felt the logbook's spine press through the canvas, and thought about Mercer's last crossing, the bullet, the water, the way he'd pressed the book into her hands specifically — not Coral's, hers — and gasped, *you're quick, girl, quicker than the rest of them, keep it moving.* She'd thought he meant the book. Maybe he'd meant all of it. Keep the truth moving. Don't let it sit still long enough to be owned.

"Then we run it tonight," Junie heard herself say.

Every head turned. Her own voice sounded foreign in her ears, small and certain at once.

"The copies. Tonight, before Ashe's next sweep. Tam makes the sets, I carry the village copy downriver at first light — I'm the fastest portager, I've run that bank twice already, I know where the skiff is cached. Twelve hours to the bend, six hours to convince the council, and I'm back before the grid fails or I'm not back at all." She swallowed. "Somebody has to be quick. He said so."

Coral was looking at her with an expression Junie couldn't read — pride and fear braided together so tight she couldn't tell which strand was which. "It's not safe."

"Nothing's safe. That's the whole lesson of this river." Junie stood, the satchel heavy and right against her hip. "Vote. All in favor of splitting the cargo?"

Tam raised his hand immediately. Sela raised hers. Imani raised hers with both arms like a child in a schoolroom, and despite everything, despite the dark and the dying fire and the poisoned cathedral breathing below them, Junie laughed.

Which left Coral.

Coral looked into the fire for a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket, pulled out Ashe's signed offer — the paper that would have erased every debt, bought every silence — and fed it to the flames. It curled, blackened, and was gone in three seconds, faster than thirty years of cover-up, faster than a career, faster than a life.

"All in favor," Coral said.

* * *

They worked through the dark hours. Tam hunched over his equipment by shielded lamplight, duplicating drives, printing charts, sealing sets in oilcloth with the methodical tenderness he usually reserved for engine parts. Sela wrote the letter to her colleagues, crossing out and rewriting until the words were exact. Imani dictated annotations from her bedroll, her voice gaining strength with every technical term, and Junie listened and packed and repacked and tried not to think about the twelve hours of river between here and the bend, or what moved on that river at night besides her.

Near dawn, Coral found her at the ledge's edge, checking the lines on the cached skiff below.

"You don't have to be the one," Coral said.

"Yeah, I do." Junie tightened a cleat hitch and grinned up at her, the grin she used for hiding things. "Besides, you've got the hard job. Holding the cavern. Reading your dad's words over the radio if it comes to that. I just have to paddle fast and talk straight."

"Those aren't small things."

"No," Junie agreed. "Turns out none of the things are small anymore."

She swung down the rope toward the skiff, the first gray light coming up the canyon, the village copy of the truth riding snug against her ribs. Behind her, above her, the source breathed its slow breath. And it was exactly then — halfway down the rope, the whole crew silhouetted on the ledge, the morning still untouched — that Tam's head snapped up toward the southern rim, and his hand closed hard around Coral's arm, and Junie heard it too:

Rotors. More than one. Coming fast, and low, and straight for the cathedral.
