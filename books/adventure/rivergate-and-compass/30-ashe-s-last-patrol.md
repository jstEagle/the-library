## 30. Ashe's Last Patrol

They heard the boats before they saw them — not engines, which would have been swallowed by the canyon's echo, but the flat slap of paddles and the low, disciplined murmur of men who had been trained to move quietly and had forgotten how.

Coral was on watch. She'd taken the last shift without being asked; nobody slept well anymore, and sleep came easier to her when she was doing something with her hands, even if that something was just holding a rifle she barely knew how to use and watching the black water slide past the cavern mouth like a tongue testing its cage. The source murmured behind her, thirty meters back into the cathedral dark, where Tam's flood charges sat wired to the containment grid like a heart waiting for a defibrillator. Ahead of her, the river ran shallow and silver under a half moon, and downriver — where the water went to the villages, to Rivergate, to everything that had a name — the night lay open.

The patrol came around the bend in three inflatable skiffs, riding low, paddles dipping in unison. No running lights. No challenge broadcast over any frequency. Just men in dark gear moving upriver toward them with the patience of people who already knew how the story ended.

Coral didn't shout a warning. She turned and put two fingers in her mouth and whistled — Mercer's signal, the one he'd used for thirty years to call his crews off the water before weather broke. It carried through the cavern like a knife through silk.

Behind her, the camp woke in stages: Junie first, always, rolling out of her bedroll with her boots already half-laced; then Tam, swearing softly at the radio he'd been trying to coax back to life for two days; then Sela, who didn't startle at all, who simply opened her eyes as if she'd been listening to the river the whole time and it had finally said something worth hearing. Imani Reyes stirred against the far wall, still gray-faced from her ordeal, her bandaged hands curled in her lap like sleeping birds.

"Three skiffs," Coral said. "Coming slow. They know we're here."

"They've known since the adit," Tam said. He stood, joints cracking, and looked at the charges wired into the containment grid with an expression Coral couldn't read — pride or dread or both wearing each other's clothes. "Question is whether they know what we're sitting on."

"They know," Sela said quietly. "They've always known. That's why the patrol comes now, and not a week ago."

Coral looked at her. In the lantern light Sela's face was calm in a way that frightened Coral more than the skiffs did — the calm of someone who had done this arithmetic before, alone, in exile, and had never stopped carrying the answer.

"What do we do?" Junie asked. She'd picked up the rifle and held it wrong, muzzle down, like a tool she didn't trust. "Fight?"

"No." Coral slung the rifle across her back where it would be visible and useless. "We talk. That's what he wants. He's come all this way to talk."

* * *

Commander Elias Ashe stepped out of the lead skiff onto the gravel shelf below the cavern mouth, and Coral saw immediately that something had changed him.

She'd met him twice before — once on the dock at Rivergate, all pressed uniform and leverage, holding her debt like a hook through her jaw; once at the drowned camp, colder, more careful, when the bodies started complicating his paperwork. Both times he had been a man performing certainty. This man who climbed the bank toward them moved like the certainty had been surgically removed and the wound left open. His uniform was rumpled. He hadn't shaved. And he came alone — his men stayed at the waterline, ten of them, rifles slung but hands near, forming a crescent that was either courtesy or a noose depending on how you read it.

He stopped six paces from Coral and looked past her, into the cavern, at the glow of their lanterns deep inside where the source rose from the earth.

"You found it," he said. Not a question. Something almost like grief.

"We found what you sent divers into thirty years ago," Coral said. "We found what my father died in."

Ashe's jaw worked. For a long moment he said nothing, and the river filled the silence, talking to itself in the shallows, patient as everything else about it.

"May I sit?" he asked, and gestured at a boulder worn smooth by centuries of flood season. It was such an ordinary request, so human, that Coral heard herself say yes before she'd decided anything.

He sat. He looked older than his file photograph by twenty years. Up close she could see the tremor in his left hand, the one he kept pressing flat against his thigh as if to hold himself down.

"I'm going to tell you things I shouldn't," Ashe said. "Not because I've grown a conscience. Because you deserve to make your choice knowing what it actually costs. And because—" he exhaled through his nose, a short bitter laugh— "because I've spent four months discovering that the institution I gave my life to doesn't protect the men who carry its secrets. It consumes them. Slowly. On a schedule."

Tam had drifted closer, arms crossed. Junie stood at Coral's shoulder. Sela remained where she was, apart, watching Ashe with the expression of a woman reading a document she'd seen before in draft form.

"Talk," Coral said.

* * *

"The source isn't a spring," Ashe began. "You know that much. It's a pressure system — an aquifer the size of a small sea, capped by rock, feeding every tributary in this watershed. Thirty-eight years ago the navy found it while surveying for a deep-water installation. And eleven months after they found it, they found the contamination. Runoff from the mining operation upstream — the one you walked through, the bone country. Heavy metals, processing chemicals, decades of it, leaching straight into the caprock. Into the water table. Into every village downstream."

"We know," Sela said. "I published it. You people burned my career for it."

Ashe looked at her, and Coral watched something pass across his face that might have been shame, if shame were allowed to survive long in a man like him.

"Yes," he said simply. "I read your paper. I was a lieutenant then. I was told it was fabricated. I believed what I was told, because believing was the job." He turned back to Coral. "Here is what you don't know. The containment grid isn't failing because of age. It's failing because it was built to fail — on a delay. The original engineers knew the caprock couldn't hold forever. They built the grid to buy time and wrote reports saying so, and the reports went into the same archive as everything else, and the time they bought got spent pretending. Every year the budget line for the source gets smaller. Every year the inspection gets shorter. There are men in the capital who have signed off on this site annually for three decades without ever looking at it, because looking at it makes it real, and real requires money, and money requires admitting what's down there."

"And the divers," Coral said. Her voice came out flat. "My father. The crew in the photographs. The graves in the containment ring."

"The divers were maintenance," Ashe said, and flinched at his own word. "That's what the program called them. Maintenance divers. Men who went into the grid to patch welds and clear intakes in water that was eating their suits and their lungs, on rotations that weren't logged, under contracts that never existed. Your father was one of the best. He lasted longer than most. And when he started asking questions — when he started collecting evidence instead of welding seams — the program ended his contract the way it ends everything. Quietly. With water."

Junie made a small sound. Coral didn't look at her. She kept her eyes on Ashe and felt the old cold place in her chest, the one that had opened the day the navy boat came to her mother's door, widen by another degree.

"So why are you here?" she asked. "Why not just send your men in and take us? You've done worse."

And there it was — the thing he'd rowed upriver to say. Ashe looked down at his trembling hand, pressed it flatter, and spoke to the gravel between his boots.

"Because three days ago I received orders to seal this canyon. Not the site — the canyon. Demolition charges on the upper narrows, a controlled collapse of the aquifer vent, permanent. The engineering assessment calls it 'hydrological risk mitigation.' What it means is that when the grid fails — and it will fail, within two years, maybe five — the failure happens underground, contained, deniable. It also means the water table dies. Every spring, every well, every bend village from here to the delta. Tens of thousands of people lose their water so that several hundred very comfortable people keep their secrets." He raised his eyes to hers. "I was ordered to make sure you weren't here when it happened. Any of you. The survey team was written off months ago. You four, Dr. Reyes, Ms. Anaya — you're listed as casualties of a flash flood that hasn't occurred yet. The paperwork is already filed. I signed part of it myself."

The words landed in the group like stones into still water. Tam sat down slowly on a crate. Junie's knuckles went white on the rifle sling.

"You're telling us they're going to blow the mountain," Tam said, "with us in it."

"I'm telling you the order exists, and that I have not yet executed it, and that my window for not executing it is closing by the hour." Ashe stood. The tremor in his hand had spread to his voice, and he seemed to stop fighting it. "So here is my offer. Final. The same offer, enlarged, because the stakes have enlarged. You give me the testimony — the father's recording, Reyes's data, the logbook, all of it. My men escort you downriver tonight. Debts erased — all of them, formally, every lien on your boat and your name. Immunity for everyone present, including Ms. Anaya. New identities for anyone who wants one. And in exchange, the evidence goes into the archive. Sealed. Classified. Where it will be safe, and preserved, and completely useless, until everyone who could act on it is dead of old age."

"That's not an offer," Coral said. "That's a burial. You're asking us to dig the graves ourselves."

"I'm asking you to live." For the first time Ashe's composure cracked clean through, and what was underneath was not cruelty but exhaustion so total it looked like illness. "You think I don't understand what you're carrying? I've carried it for twenty years. I have a daughter in Corvane who thinks I survey bridges. I have a drawer full of medals for operations that never happened. I am *inside*, Ms. Voss — deeper than you will ever be — and I am telling you that from the inside, this looks different. You want to burn it all down. Fine. Burn it down. And then stand in the ashes and count what the fire takes with it. The archive sealed is a tragedy. The archive burned is a massacre — because the moment that evidence surfaces outside these walls, the demolition order stops being contingency and becomes necessity. They won't risk a leak. They'll drop the narrows tomorrow, and they'll do it with you downstream of the blast wave unless you're already in custody, and the only version of you that survives their custody is the version that handed everything over."

"You could refuse the order," Junie said. Her voice was thin but steady. "You have rank."

Ashe smiled, and it was the saddest thing Coral had seen in a year of sad things.

"I refused once before," he said. "Eleven years ago. A similar situation, a smaller valley. I drafted a dissent, routed it through channels, followed every rule I'd sworn to. Six weeks later I was reassigned to a hydrographic desk in the capital, my dissent was reclassified as a personnel grievance, and the operation proceeded on schedule. Fourteen people died in a flood the official report attributed to seasonal rainfall." He looked at his hands. "I attended none of the funerals. I wasn't permitted to know where they were held. That is what refusal costs, deckhand. Not martyrdom. Erasure. And then they hand your erasure to the next officer, and he carries it forward, and that is how an institution stays alive while everything it touches drowns."

The river said nothing. It never did. It just kept moving, taking everything downstream, keeping nothing.

* * *

Coral walked to the edge of the gravel shelf and looked down at the water.

She thought about her debts — the numbers that had lived in her head so long they'd become furniture, the liens on the *Rivergate and Compass*, the interest accruing like silt. One signature and they were gone. One signature and Junie never had to eat rice again because it was cheap. One signature and Tam's hands, those brilliant ruined hands, could buy parts instead of improvising them from scrap.

She thought about her father's voice on the waterproof tape, recorded in the dark under this mountain, steady as a man reading coordinates: *If anyone finds this, the water is the witness. Make the river tell it.*

And she thought about Ashe — really looked at him, standing there in his rumpled uniform with his shaking hand, and understood suddenly that he hadn't come to threaten them or save them. He'd come because he was drowning too, and had been for twenty years, and some drowned part of him hoped that if he laid the whole weight down in front of strangers, one of them might find a way to carry it that he never had.

"You're trapped," she said aloud. Not cruelly. Just true.

Ashe closed his eyes. "Yes."

"And you can't get out. And you can't stay. So you're trying to buy the only exit you can see, and the price is us."

"Yes."

Coral turned back to her crew. Tam was watching her with the look of a man who had already run the engineering on every option and hated all the outputs. Junie's eyes were wet and furious. Sela had come closer without Coral noticing, close enough that Coral could feel the warmth of her at her shoulder, and Sela's gaze was fixed not on Ashe but on the containment grid glowing faintly in the cavern dark — on the welds, the rust, the geometry of a structure built to fail slowly, on schedule, by design.

"Sela," Coral said quietly. "The grid. If it fails naturally — the way they built it to — what happens to the water?"

Sela answered without hesitation, the way she answered everything scientific, as if the facts had been waiting in her mouth for years.

"Slow poisoning. Years of it. Invisible, deniable, exactly like the last thirty years. Villages sickening one household at a time. No single disaster large enough to point at." She paused. "But if the grid fails *fast* — if it fails all at once, visibly, catastrophically — the contamination arrives as an event. Undeniable. Unhideable. A flood of black water through the canyon that every journalist, every investigator, every grieving family downstream can trace to one source." Her voice dropped. "It also means warning the valley first. All of it. Before the water comes."

Ashe had gone very still. "You're talking about breaching it yourselves."

"I'm talking about choosing the manner of the truth," Sela said, "instead of letting them choose the manner of the lie."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, in the dark throat of the canyon, wind moved through stone with a sound like breathing.

Coral looked at Ashe one last time. "Tell your men to go back downriver, Commander. Tell them whatever you have to tell them. Because we're not selling you the river, and we're not burying my father twice." She felt Sela's hand find hers, and gripped it. "But I'll tell you something for free. You said the demolition order needs you not to execute it to stay dead. So don't. Stand where you're standing. Refuse one more time — not through channels this time. Right here. With witnesses."

Ashe stared at her. Behind him, at the waterline, his ten soldiers waited in their crescent, and the current pulled at their skiffs, patient, insistent, already carrying the night toward morning.

Then, from deep inside the cathedral dark, Tam called out — his voice cracked and strange:

"Coral. You need to see this. The grid's leaking. It's starting *now*."
