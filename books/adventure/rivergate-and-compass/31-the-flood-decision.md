## 31. The Flood Decision

Sela had spent eleven years learning to read water the way other people read faces — the small lies in its surface, the grief it carried downstream, the things it refused to say out loud. She had published a paper once, in another life, with a title so dry that only seven people ever opened it: *Hydrochemical Anomalies in the Upper Vell Tributary: A Preliminary Assessment*. Seven readers. One of them had been Coral's father. The others, she suspected, had been the men who ended her career.

Now she stood at the lip of the source cavern with the same data living in her head instead of on paper, and the arithmetic she had been avoiding for three days finally demanded to be done.

The containment grid filled the far half of the cathedral like the ribs of something enormous that had died standing up. Rusted steel pilings rose out of the clear water in ranks, braced with crossbeams gone orange and furred with mineral bloom, and between them the river came up from the dark of the earth in columns so pure they were almost invisible — the source itself, rising clean, meeting the structures built to strangle it, and passing through changed. Where the water exited the grid on the downstream side, it ran black-veined, threaded with plumes of iron and worse, the color of tea left too long, of old wounds. Forty years of concealed runoff from the mining operation, forty years of the navy's quiet engineering funneling contamination into the aquifer that fed every well, every field, every child downstream from here to Rivergate.

Sela crouched at the waterline and dipped two fingers in, out of habit more than need. Cold. Clean where it rose. Poisoned where it left.

"Tell me again," Coral said behind her. "Slowly this time."

Sela turned. Coral stood with her arms crossed against the cavern chill, the compass hanging open in one hand like a question she'd stopped expecting answers to. Tam was at the mouth of the adit, keeping watch down the canyon; Junie sat on a supply crate with Reyes's decoded charts spread across her knees, not reading them, just holding them down against the draft as if the wind might steal the truth back. Imani lay propped against her pack, feverish but lucid, watching everything with the flat attention of someone who had already done her own arithmetic and hadn't liked the sum.

"The containment is failing," Sela said. "It's been failing for years. That's not my theory — you can see it. Look at the stress fractures in the third piling row. Look at how the black water is finding new channels around the seals instead of through them." She pointed, and the others followed her finger into the rusted geometry. "Someone upstream has been making emergency releases to relieve pressure — that's what drowned the survey camp. They're managing a collapse, not preventing one. And when it comes, it won't come as a slow leak. It'll come all at once, a wall of contaminated water moving down a canyon with nothing in its path."

"And your plan is to make it come *now*," Coral said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — understanding.

"On our terms. Yes." Sela made herself hold Coral's eyes. "We breach the lower seals deliberately. Controlled failure. The flood goes down the canyon while we know it's coming, while the valley below has hours of warning instead of minutes — or none."

"And the evidence?" Imani's voice was rough from the strainer, from the river that had nearly kept her. "My team's data. Your father's testimony—" she caught herself, corrected, "*your* father's testimony. The dive logs. Photographs of the graves. Everything we came here carrying. A flood doesn't sort documents, Dr. Villalobos. It takes all of it."

"I know what a flood takes." Sela heard her own voice go flat and old, the voice of the woman who had watched her career dissolve in a hearing room while men in uniforms explained that her numbers were inconvenient and therefore wrong. "I've spent eleven years being careful with evidence. I built chain-of-custody protocols nobody asked me to build. I backed up my files three ways and hid copies in places I'm still not sure I remember. And do you know what happened? Nothing. My paper vanished into an archive. My name became a warning told to young hydrologists. The river kept dying quietly, and everyone downstream kept drinking it, because the evidence existed and the evidence did *nothing*."

She stood, wiping her fingers on her trousers, and gestured at the black-veined water.

"You can't prosecute a poison that stays hidden. That's the whole design. That's why there are divers' graves in navy gear fifty meters from a cathedral of clean water. Not because the dead knew too much — because the *water* knows too much, and it tells anyone who drinks it, and the only defense against that is to keep the story buried deeper than the source." She looked at each of them in turn. "So we stop trying to carry the proof out. We make the river carry it instead. We breach the grid, the contamination becomes visible in every household tap from here to the delta, and no committee, no commander, no archive can unsee it. You cannot classify a thousand sick villages. You can't redact a dead aquifer."

* * *

They argued for two hours, and Sela let them, because the argument was not really about the plan. It was about grief, and each of them grieved a different thing.

Tam grieved the engineer's faith. He climbed partway into the grid — carefully, testing every rung, because he was Tam and would test a ladder even at the end of the world — and came back shaking his head with something close to admiration. "Whoever built this understood load paths," he said. "It's elegant. Ugly, but elegant. It was never meant to last forever, though. These expansion joints are shot. Honestly, I think we could breach it with what's left of the blasting supplies, or without them — give me six hours and a pry bar and the lower row lets go on its own schedule." Then his voice dropped. "But if we do it, we're not witnesses anymore. We're the cause. Every consequence downstream lands on us. Legally. Morally. I need everyone to understand that difference, because Ashe will certainly understand it, and he will use it."

Junie grieved the village at the bend. She'd run rivers her whole short life and she knew, better than any of them, what a flash flood did to a bend community — the low fields flooded first, the livestock, the elders who wouldn't leave their houses, the children who thought high water meant holiday until it didn't. "Warning them isn't a phone call," she said. "There's no signal past the narrows. Someone has to *go*. Downriver, alone, ahead of the water, in whatever boat we've got left, through channels that are about to change shape permanently." She said it looking at her own hands, and Sela understood before Junie said it aloud: Junie was volunteering, and Junie was terrified, and Junie was going anyway.

Imani grieved the science. She made them spread her charts on the crate again and walked them through it with a fever-bright patience, the survivor's stubbornness that had carried her miles downstream inside a log jam. "The aquifer contamination is regional. My team proved it before they—" she paused, steadied. "Before. Wells in four districts are already showing markers. People are being poisoned *now*, slowly, invisibly, in a way that will take decades to trace back if it ever gets traced. A flood is terrible. But a flood is *legible*. A flood has a date and a cause and a body of water everyone can point to. Slow poisoning has neither. If you ask me — and I know none of you asked me — the hidden version of this disaster is worse than the visible one. We're not choosing between harm and safety. We're choosing which harm, and who gets to see it."

And Coral grieved her father. Sela saw it in the way she held the compass, thumb moving over the cracked crystal, and when Coral finally spoke it was quiet enough that the river nearly covered it.

"He came up here to finish your work, Sela. He died trying to get the testimony out. His whole last dive was about *carrying the proof*." She looked up. "If we flood it, we drown his words. Again. After everything, we finish the job the navy started."

Sela had prepared for this argument the way she prepared for all arguments — with data — but standing in front of Coral she found the data wasn't the point, and she was tired of pretending otherwise.

"Coral. Your father sealed his testimony in the containment grid because he believed the structure would stand. He believed someone would come back for it — him, or us, or the courts, someday. He trusted the future to be patient." She took a breath. "The structure is not going to stand. That's not pessimism, that's metallurgy. The choice isn't between preserving his words and destroying them. The choice is between drowning them in a collapse nobody warned anyone about — or spending them. Deliberately. As the price of the warning." She stepped closer. "Your father didn't die to protect a document. He died trying to tell the truth. We have one chance to tell it in a way that can't be buried, and it costs us the document. I think — I *have* to think — he'd pay that price. He paid a bigger one already."

Coral said nothing for a long time. The river rose and rose beneath the grid, clean into poisoned, patient as geology. Finally she closed the compass.

"How long do the villages have?"

* * *

That was the question that mattered, and it sent Sela and Tam back into the grid with a measuring line, a stopwatch, and the last of Reyes's functioning instruments, doing hydrology by lantern light in a cathedral built to hide a crime.

The answer, when Sela finished the calculations on the back of a chart margin, was worse than she'd hoped and better than she'd feared.

"If we breach the lower seals in sequence — Tam collapses them upstream-to-downstream, so the initial surge spends itself against the inner rows — the peak arrives at the narrows in roughly nine hours. The village at the bend is further. Fourteen hours, maybe sixteen, depending on how much the dry-season channel absorbs." She looked at Junie. "You can beat it. You'll have to run water nobody's ever run, at night, with the channel dropping under you — but you can beat it if you leave within two hours."

"Two hours," Junie repeated, and started inventorying the boat's remaining fuel without another word, because that was who Junie was.

"Fourteen hours," Tam said slowly. "To evacuate a village, move the livestock off the low fields, get word to the settlements below the bend..." He rubbed his face. "It's possible. Barely. If they believe the messenger."

"They'll believe her," Coral said. "Mercer ran supply for them thirty years. Junie's got his blood and his logbook." Her voice caught on Mercer's name and went on anyway, and Sela watched her choose, visibly, to fold her grief into the plan rather than let it stop the plan. "Okay. Okay. We do it. But I want it on record — all of it. Before we touch anything, we document the grid. Photographs of the fractures, the seals, the graves, the testimony housing. Copies of Reyes's data and my father's pages, wrapped, sealed, split between whoever leaves and whoever stays. If the flood takes the originals, the copies survive somewhere. That's the best chain of custody we can build, and we build it in ninety minutes."

"That's not enough time to—"

"It's what we have," Coral said. "Ashe's helicopters found the canyon yesterday. His advance team is close enough that Tam heard engines this morning. We don't get a comfortable version of this, Sela. We get this one."

Sela nodded, and felt something in her chest loosen that had been tight for eleven years — not relief, exactly. Permission. All those years of hiding data in triplicate, of writing truths in language so cautious it barely qualified as speech, of surviving by burying herself — and here, at the top of the river, the strategy that had failed her completely was finally, obviously, done. There was nothing left to protect the evidence *from*. There was only the valley, and the clock, and the work.

* * *

They worked through the last of the daylight.

Sela photographed the containment grid until her hands stopped shaking, frame after frame of rusted steel and black-veined water, and then photographed the graves on the ledge above the cavern — the divers, the ones in navy gear, the ones whose tags Coral had found in the mine adit — and made Tam witness the log entries with his signature and the date and the words *taken at the source, prior to deliberate breach, freely and knowingly*. Imani dictated her findings aloud while Junie wrote them out twice, in case one copy soaked. Coral knelt at the testimony housing her father had sealed into the grid and copied his pages by lantern light, and when she finished she pressed her forehead briefly against the cold steel of the housing, and Sela looked away to give her that moment, and then Coral stood up and was crew again.

At full dark they gathered at the boat. Junie had the fuel cached, the copies sealed in oilcloth lashed inside her jacket, Mercer's logbook wrapped in plastic against her chest. She hugged each of them fast and hard, the way deckhands hug — quickly, because lingering is how you find reasons to stay.

"Nine hours to the narrows," Tam said to her, gripping her shoulders. "The peak. Don't be at the narrows at hour nine."

"I'm never anywhere I don't mean to be," Junie said, which was a lie, delivered perfectly, and they all let her have it.

Then she pushed off into the dark and the sound of the engine faded down the canyon, and Sela stood listening until there was nothing to hear but the river rising under the grid, and the wind coming down-canyon ahead of the weather, and — faint, very faint, from the direction of the lower gorge — the thrum of helicopter rotors, or engines, or both.

Coral appeared at her shoulder, lantern in hand, face lit from below.

"So," Coral said. "How do we blow it?"

Sela looked at the failing structure that had hidden a poison for forty years, at the water rising clean and endless out of the earth, at the people beside her who had followed her arithmetic all the way to the edge of it.

"We start with the third piling row," she said, "and we work fast. Because whatever's making that noise down-canyon just heard Junie's engine too — and it's going to reach us before the water reaches the bend."

Above them, at the canyon rim, a searchlight swept once across the stars and began to descend.
