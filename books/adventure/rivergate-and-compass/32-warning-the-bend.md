## 32. Warning the Bend

The boat was called *Kestrel's Mercy*, and she had never once been alone on it.

Junie had been the smallest thing on the *Rivergate and Compass* since the day Coral fished her off a dock at fourteen, all elbows and hunger, and the boat had always been full — Tam swearing at an engine, Mercer humming something off-key at the wheel, Coral's voice carrying over the water like a hand on your shoulder. Even in the worst nights, running the rapids with a navy skiff chewing at their wake, she had never been alone. There had always been someone to hand her a line, someone to shout the depth, someone to be afraid with.

Now the wheel was hers and the river was dying and there was no one at all.

She looked back once, only once, at the mouth of the cathedral cavern. Tam stood at the waterline with a coil of detonation wire over his shoulder like a bandolier, and Coral was already turning away, already walking into the dark with her father's compass in her fist, and Sela — Sela raised one hand, not waving, just open, the way you hold your palm up to say *go, go now*. The light behind them was the last light Junie would see for hours, maybe the last light there would ever be in that canyon if they got the arithmetic wrong.

*Go,* Sela had said. *You're the only one who can. The river knows you.*

The river did not know her. The river was trying to kill her. But Junie had learned a long time ago that the difference between those two things was mostly a matter of how you held the wheel.

She opened the throttle and the *Kestrel's Mercy* came up on plane and the canyon walls began to run backward like pages tearing out of a book.

* * *

The river was lower than she had ever seen a river be. That was the thing nobody said out loud back at the bend, the thing the whole village seemed to understand in its bones without naming: the water was leaving them. The dry season had come down hard and mean this year, and every mile she ran the banks rose higher above her, striped with old waterlines like the rings on a drowned man's lungs, and the channel narrowed and braided and shallowed until the boat's hull sang a constant low note of *sand, sand, sand* beneath her feet.

She had the chart Mercer's logbook had given them — Coral had copied the pages in pencil, quick and careful, the night before — and she had Sela's hydrology, the flow numbers scrawled on the back of an old survey map, and she had her own eyes, which had grown up reading water the way other children learned to read street signs. What she saw was a river being strangled from the top. What she knew, because Sela had made her repeat it twice with her hands on Junie's shoulders, was that all of that was about to change.

"When the grid goes," Sela had said, "it doesn't trickle. The containment has been holding back thirty years of poisoned water under pressure. When Tam blows it, the source is going to come down this canyon like a fist. The flood front will move faster than a man can run, faster than a truck on those switchback roads. The only warning the village gets is the one you carry."

"How long do I have?"

Sela had done the numbers in her head, lips moving, and Junie had watched her arrive at the answer the way you watch someone read a death notice. "Four hours. Maybe five if the breach goes unevenly. Less if the old channels fail early."

Four hours to run sixty miles of a dying river in the dark, through rapids she'd never seen at water levels no one had ever charted, and then get a whole village of people who had every reason not to trust her up and out of the flood path.

Junie was nineteen years old and five foot two and she had never been the one. She had always been the hands, the extra pair, the kid. And she had stood there in the mouth of the cavern with her heart going like a drum in a parade and heard herself say, "Then I'd better not stop for anything."

* * *

The first rapids nearly ended it in the first ten minutes.

Mercer's pencil marks said *ledge drop, run left of the rock tooth, easy water at summer level.* There was no summer level anymore. The left channel was a boneyard of exposed rock, and the right channel — the one that shouldn't have had enough water to float a coffin — was the only way through, and it was boiling, narrow, and loud enough that she couldn't hear her own shouting.

She did what Mercer had taught her without ever teaching her, all those years of standing behind him at the wheel pretending to be bored. She read the water. The V of the current pointed true. The dark water was deep, the green water was shallow, the white water was a lie that only looked like danger. She lined up the V, committed the boat, and drove it in.

The *Kestrel's Mercy* dropped four feet in a single sickening instant, slammed, wallowed, shipped half a ton of freezing water over the bow, and came out the bottom of the chute spinning like a coin. Junie got it straight, got the bilge pump going, and laughed — a wild, cracked, terrified laugh that she couldn't stop, because she was alive and the boat was alive and the river had just told her the only secret that mattered: *you can do this.*

"Okay," she said out loud, to the boat, to Mercer, to nobody. "Okay. Next one."

* * *

The moon rose fat and white over the canyon rim and she ran by it, cutting the engine on the long flats to save fuel and listen, running it hard through the drops, learning the dying river mile by mile. She passed things she would remember for the rest of her life. A herd of bighorn standing on a gravel bar that should have been under six feet of water, staring at her with flat yellow eyes. An old ferry cable sagging slack across a channel, its anchor tree dead on the bank. The rusted hulk of mining equipment half-buried in a cutbank — more of it, always more of it, the secret operation's bones surfacing as the water fell away, the river giving up the evidence it had been made to keep.

At the halfway mark she hit the strainers.

A flood two years back — she remembered it, remembered the village talking about it — had piled a raft of dead trees against a canyon narrows, and at normal water the current would have run clean underneath. Now the mat of timber sat right at the surface, a wall of branches and snags, and the whole river was funneling into it.

She killed the engine and drifted, and the silence was worse than the noise. The boat slid toward the tangle with the terrible patience of water, and Junie stood at the wheel doing math with her whole body — the current, the wind, the eddy line she could just barely see kissing the rock on river right. There was an eddy. A tiny one, a lie of an eddy, a pocket of slack water the size of a kitchen.

She rowed for it. There was no motor assist, no second set of hands, no Mercer's voice saying *easy, girl, easy.* Just her, and two oars, and the boat, and the black glass water. The eddy took the bow, spun them, and held — held — held — the strainer wall sliding past close enough to touch, close enough that a single dead branch trailed across the deck like a finger checking her pulse.

She sat down on the deck plates when it was over and shook for a full minute, and then she got up and went on, because four hours was not a number you could afford to spend shaking.

* * *

She smelled the village before she saw it. Woodsmoke and roasting maize and the green smell of the terraced gardens they kept on the high benches — the gardens that would be under ten feet of brown water in three hours.

The bend opened out around the last bluff and there it was, the whole living world of the village at night: lantern light in the windows, the mill wheel turning slow in what was left of the current, dogs barking at the sound of her engine long before she reached the landing. People came down to the bank with torches, and Junie saw the moment the torchlight reached her face and the murmur went through the crowd — *that's the salvage boat's girl, that's the outsider, that's one of the ones who brought the soldiers' war up the river.*

She ran the boat against the landing stones and stood up and her legs nearly folded under her, and she made them not fold, because she had one job and she had about two hundred and forty minutes to do it.

"My name is Junie," she said, and her voice came out raw and small, and she hated it, and she planted her feet and made it bigger. "My name is Junie Okafor, I crew for Coral Voss on the *Rivergate and Compass*, and I'm here to tell you the water is coming."

An old man with a miller's shoulders pushed to the front. "The water is leaving, girl. It's been leaving all season."

"It's coming back all at once." She held up the copied pages, Mercer's logbook charts, Sela's numbers, and her hands were shaking now and she let them shake, let them see it. "Up at the source there's a containment structure, old, rusted, holding back thirty years of poisoned water. My friends are going to breach it. Tonight. Within the hour. When it goes, the flood front comes down this canyon faster than anything you can outrun on foot. The low terraces, the mill, everything below the high bench — it's all going under."

She saw it move through the crowd the way wind moves through maize — disbelief first, then fear, then the hard ugly face of suspicion. A woman's voice: "This is the soldiers' trick. Get us off our land so they can have it."

"I know," Junie said. "I know why you'd think that. Soldiers came to your river and lied to you and took from you, and I can't fix that tonight. But I'm not them. I'm the smallest person on my crew and they gave me the job nobody else could do, and I ran sixty miles of dead river in the dark to stand here and tell you this, and every word of it is true, and you can check it — send your fastest runner to the bluff above the narrows. When the grid blows you'll hear it. You'll hear it like the mountain clearing its throat. And then you'll have minutes. Only minutes."

The old miller looked at her for a long time. Then he looked at the river — at the low, starved, shrunken river that had fed his family for six generations — and Junie watched him read it the way she read water, the way river people read everything, and saw him find the truth in it. A river this low didn't come back on its own. Something upstream was holding more than it should.

"Rings," he said. "Sound the rings. Everyone to the high benches with what they can carry. Livestock to the cutbank pasture. And you—" he turned to Junie, and his voice was not kind, but it was not cruel, and after the week she'd had it sounded like mercy. "You'll stand on the bluff with us, girl. If the water comes as you say, you'll watch it come. And if it doesn't—"

"If it doesn't," Junie said, "you can put me off your land yourself."

* * *

The rings were old bronze, hung from a frame at the village heart since before anyone living was born, meant for flood warnings from the days when the river ran wild and honest. The sound they made was enormous, a bronze voice rolling down the terraces and out over the water, and Junie stood beside the miller's granddaughter and pulled the rope until her palms bled and the village woke and rose and moved.

And oh, they moved. That was the thing that undid her, standing there ringing the bell with her arms burning — how fast they moved, how sure, families gathering children and grandmothers and seed grain and the small holy things of their houses, no panic, no stampede, just the deep practiced choreography of people who had always known, in the back of their bones, that the river could turn on them. They had evacuation paths worn into the hillside like deer trails. They had meeting stones and count-keepers and a woman whose whole job, whose inherited office, was to call every name twice and mark it on a slate.

They had known how to survive a flood their whole lives. What they had never been given was the warning.

Junie rang the rings until the miller's hand closed over hers and took the rope. "Enough," he said. "They're all up. Come and watch."

She climbed to the bluff with the whole village, two hundred souls on the high benches in the moonlight, and she looked up the canyon toward the source, toward Coral and Tam and Sela and the dead men's graves and the poisoned cathedral of the river's birth, and she waited.

The village was quiet. The dogs had gone still. Even the mill wheel had stopped, the race run dry, and the silence was the silence of a held breath.

Then the mountain cleared its throat.

It came up the canyon as a sound before it came as a sight — a deep rolling boom that Junie felt in her teeth and her sternum and the soles of her feet, and then a second boom, and then a long grinding roar like the world's largest door being dragged open, and the miller's granddaughter grabbed her arm and the whole village made one sound, a single low exhalation of two hundred people understanding at once.

And then the river came back.

They saw it first as a brown gleam far up the canyon, a seam of moving light where no light had been, and then it was a wall, and then it was a flood, and it came down the narrows with a sound like the end of the world being born. It hit the first bend and exploded into spray. It filled the channel Junie had run through on fumes, filled the boneyards and the gravel bars and the dead ferry cable's slack, and it came down on the village terraces black and roaring and full of thirty years of everything the navy had buried.

It rose to the mill and took the mill. It rose to the low gardens and took the gardens. It stopped — the water stopped — two feet below the high bench, two feet below two hundred pairs of feet, exactly, precisely, where Sela's numbers had said it would stop, and Junie sat down on the cold stone and put her face in her bleeding hands and cried.

The miller's granddaughter sat down beside her, and after a moment the old man did too, and none of them said anything for a long time. Below them the river ran full and brown and honest, carrying its secret out into the world where no one could ever stuff it back.

"Your friends," the miller said finally. "Up there. At the source."

Junie lifted her head. The flood had come down the canyon and the canyon was behind her now, dark, and somewhere up in that dark her people were standing on a ledge in a flooded cavern with soldiers closing in, and the last thing she had heard Coral say was *hold the cavern until Junie's clear, then get to high ground,* and she had no way to know, no way at all, whether they'd made it.

She got to her feet. Her legs held this time.

"There's a radio skiff in my boat," she said. "And there's a navy frequency Coral gave me, and a channel the whole valley can hear. Somebody down here has a transmitter?"

The miller smiled, and it was not a warm smile, but there was iron in it. "We have the mill's battery house. What do you want to say, girl?"

Junie looked up the dark canyon one more time, at the place where her family was holding a door against the dark, and she wiped her face and said:

"Everything. I want to say everything."
