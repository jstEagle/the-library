## 33. Standoff in the Cathedral

The light came down in columns.

That was the thing no photograph could have prepared her for — the way the source cavern caught the sun through its broken ceiling and turned it into pillars, pale gold shafts standing in the black water like the masts of drowned ships. The river rose here out of the floor of the world, upwelling through fissures so deep that Sela's soundings had never found their bottom, and it rose *clear*, impossibly clear, water that had spent a century underground being filtered through limestone until it came up clean as glass. In those columns of light, dust motes drifted like slow snow. It should have been holy.

It was not holy. It was a crime scene the size of a cathedral, and they had all just walked into the nave.

Coral stood on the rusted catwalk above the containment grid, her father's logbook open against her forearm, and looked at what thirty years of silence had built. The containment structures ringed the upwelling in a great corroded circle — steel bulkheads bolted to the living rock, pump housings furred with orange rust, pipes as wide as road tunnels running off into the dark toward the old mine workings. And between the bulkheads, on a shelf of stone above the flood line where the water could never reach them, the graves.

Nine of them. She had counted twice, because the first time she'd hoped she'd miscounted. Navy markers, most of them, stenciled aluminum plates gone green with age. A few were only cairns, stones stacked by hands that must have been shaking. One had a dive mask hung on a length of rebar driven into the rock, the silicone cracked to powder, the glass fogged from the inside with thirty years of river damp.

Her father's name was on none of them. His tags were in her pocket, pulled from the mine adit two days ago, and she had not yet decided whether that meant he'd been buried somewhere else or never buried at all. She wasn't sure which answer she wanted. Both of them were unbearable in different directions.

"Coral." Tam's voice came low across the catwalk, pitched under the sound of the water. "Movement at the south adit. Two, maybe three. They're not hurrying."

They never hurried. That was the thing about Ashe's men — they moved with the patience of people who knew the geography was on their side, who knew there was nowhere left to run. The crew had come down off Bone Country on foot after caching the boat, hauling Reyes on a litter Tam had lashed together from pack frames, and every mile of it Coral had known they were being herded. The helicopters hadn't fired. The patrols hadn't closed. Ashe wanted them here, at the source, where everything could be gathered up in one place and made to disappear in one place. He'd been driving them upstream like cattle toward a chute this whole time.

She'd let herself be driven. Because the chute ended here, and here was where the truth lived.

"Positions," Mercer would have said. Mercer wasn't there. The thought arrived the way it always did now, a door opening onto an empty room, and Coral shut it again because there would be time to grieve properly later, if any of them lived to have later.

Junie took the high ledge above the north bulkhead with the flare gun and the last of the signal cartridges. Tam crouched at the junction of two pipe runs where the catwalk narrowed, wrench in hand, already eyeing the containment valves with the expression he wore when he was calculating how many seconds it would take to make something fail catastrophically. Sela knelt beside Imani Reyes, who sat propped against a bulkhead with her bandaged ribs and her data drive taped inside her shirt, grey-faced but awake, awake and watching the south adit like a woman who had already seen what came out of it once.

And Coral stood over the graves with her father's handwriting under her thumb, and waited.

* * *

Ashe came himself.

She heard him before she saw him — boots on steel grating, unhurried, the rhythm of a man who had walked this catwalk before and expected to walk it long after everyone else was gone. Then he rounded the pump housing into one of the columns of light, and the gold fell across his uniform and turned him briefly, absurdly, beautiful: a tall man in navy grey with dust on his sleeves and tiredness carved deep around his eyes, holding his sidearm pointed at the deck plates rather than at anyone. Behind him came four of his men, rifles up, spreading along the catwalk with drilled economy.

"Dr. Voss." His voice carried strangely in the cavern, flattened by stone, swallowed by water-sound. "You've made good time."

"You've been following us since the Narrows," Coral said. "You didn't need me to make good time. You needed me to lead you here."

"I needed you alive." He stopped ten feet away, close enough that she could see the sweat at his temple despite the cavern's chill. "There's a difference, and you've spent the entire journey refusing to learn it."

"Mercer learned it."

Something moved behind his eyes — not quite flinch, not quite regret. "That was not my order. I want that understood, whatever else happens today. The patrol commander exceeded—"

"He shot an old man in the back on your river, Commander, on your operation, and you've spent four days telling yourself the paperwork will hold." Coral kept her voice level. Her hands were not level; she pressed them flat against the logbook. "Save it. Save all of it. We're past the part where the words matter."

"Then let me be plain." He holstered the pistol — deliberately, letting her watch him do it — and spread his hands. "You have Dr. Reyes's data. You have the logbook. You have—" his gaze flicked, involuntary, to her jacket pocket, to the shape of the compass, "—the rest of it. Hand it over, walk out with my team, and by Thursday you're debt-free, your boat is yours clear of lien, and Rivergate never hears the word *source* again. That offer has not changed. It will not be offered again."

"It was never really an offer," Coral said. "An offer means I could say yes."

Behind Ashe, one of his men shifted, and the rifle barrel tracked a half-degree toward Tam, and Tam went very still with his hand still resting on the valve wheel, and the whole cathedral held its breath — the water rising and falling against the bulkheads, the light standing in its columns, nine dead divers keeping their silence on their shelf of stone.

"You read the file," Coral said quietly. "The real file, not the one they gave the survey team. You know what's in the aquifer. You know what comes out of the taps in the villages downstream. You've known long enough to stop sleeping well."

"I know what happens if it gets out *badly*," Ashe said. "Panic. Litigation that dies in committee. A cleanup contract awarded to the same company that caused it, sealed for another forty years. I have watched this institution eat the truth before, Dr. Voss. I am offering you the version where nobody else drowns."

"And I'm telling you the truth doesn't drown." She lifted the logbook. "It floats. Ask the river."

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way bad things do — not building but breaking.

One of the soldiers, young, jumpy, saw Tam's hand move on the valve and shouted a warning, and Ashe turned, and in turning his shoulder opened, and Coral was already moving — not toward cover, toward the radio. They'd salvaged it from the survey camp three days ago, a field transceiver with a hand crank and a whip antenna, and Tam had spent a night in the dark rigging it to the cavern's old mine-phone line where it punched up through the canyon walls toward the repeater on Mount Halden. Junie had asked her once, back at the bend, why bother carrying the weight.

"Because a secret is only a secret while it stays in one place," Coral had said. "I'm going to put it in every place."

She reached the transceiver as the first rifle shot cracked off the ceiling and went screaming away into the dark overhead, stone chips raining into the water. She didn't look to see who'd fired or who'd returned it. She cranked. The flywheel spun up under her palm, humming, and the needle swung into the red, and she keyed the transmit switch and started to read.

"*Dive log, final entry. Elias Voss, diving for the record, because the record is the only witness they can't drown.*"

Her father's voice came out of her mouth in her own voice, and that was right, somehow — that was the inheritance working exactly as designed. She read fast and clear, pitching it over the gunfire, over the shouting, over the groan of the bulkheads:

"*Third dive on the grid. The seals are failing. They built these bulkheads in '61 to keep the runoff out of the main channel and they have not maintained them since. The company pumped tailings into the sinkholes for eleven years and capped it with concrete and called it remediation. It is not remediated. It is a wound with a bandage, and the bandage is rotting.*"

"Coral!" Tam's voice, raw. Something heavy went over — metal on stone — and then a flare burst above the north bulkhead and turned the whole cathedral red, shadows lunging enormous across the walls, and still she read.

"*I have documented seepage at six points. I have samples. If anything happens to me, the case is in the grid, third seal, north face, behind the inspection plate. Tell my daughter the compass points home. Tell her the river keeps what matters.*"

A soldier reached the catwalk junction twenty feet away. Sela stepped out of the column of light to meet him — unarmed, hands open, planting herself in his sightline like a wall made of a person — and Coral heard her voice, steady and furious: "Look around you. Look at the graves. Are you going to be the tenth?"

The soldier hesitated. It was only a second, but it was enough, because Ashe shouted *hold*, and — incredibly — they held. Not out of mercy. Out of arithmetic. Every shot fired now was a shot that would have to be explained later, and the radio was already singing, and Ashe knew better than anyone in that cavern what happened to explanations that contradicted a broadcast.

He crossed the distance to her in five strides and put his hand over the transmit key.

Coral let go of the switch. There was no point gripping it; the words were out. She looked up at him, and in the fading red light of the flare she saw the thing she'd suspected since the Narrows finally surface in his face — not cruelty. Trapping. A man standing in a chute of his own, staring down at the place where it ended.

"It's transmitting," she said. "Every channel the repeater touches. Coast guard, river authority, the ham bands, the weather net. Half the valley listens to the weather net, Commander. Fishermen. Ferry pilots. Children doing their homework next to the set." She tapped the logbook against his chest, gently, almost kindly. "You can take it. Take the pages, take the drive, take my voice. It won't matter. Truth released cannot be recalled. Your own institution taught me that — it's the only lesson it ever taught anybody."

For a long moment Ashe said nothing. The flare died. The ordinary gold light came back, standing in its columns, and the water rose and fell against the rotten bulkheads with a sound like slow breathing, and somewhere below them, behind the third seal on the north face, a waterproof case sat in the dark holding the rest of her father's voice.

"The grid is failing," Ashe said at last, very quietly. "You understand that. It's been failing for years. What you're proposing — what she's proposing—" his eyes cut to Sela, "—isn't exposure. It's detonation. When those bulkheads go, everything the company buried comes down the valley at flood speed. Every village on the bend. Every landing. Everything."

"Which is why we're going to warn them first," Coral said. "Which is why we needed the radio. Which is why, Commander, you are going to help us — because your boats are faster than our feet, and your men know the channel markers, and in about"— she checked her watch, an old habit, her father's watch, stopped at some hour she'd never reset —"no time at all, the only question left in this valley is going to be who got warned in time. You can be the man who saved the bend, or the man who stood in the cathedral guarding a lie while it drowned people. Choose fast."

Above them, on the high ledge, Junie's voice came down thin and urgent: "Coral — the water. Look at the water."

Coral looked.

The upwelling had changed. All morning it had risen clear, glass-clear, the famous impossible clarity of the source. Now a thread of darkness moved in the deepest of the light-columns — a bloom of black unfurling upward from the fissures like ink dropped in a glass, spreading, thickening, staining the gold.

The grid wasn't just failing. The grid was failing *now*.

Tam was already at the valves, wrench in hand, looking back at her across the ruined light with a question in his face that was really an answer: *how much time do we have?*

Coral looked at the black bloom, at the graves, at Sela, at Ashe — and felt the whole long river of her life narrow to a single point ahead.

"Not long enough," she said. "Tam — tell me exactly how big the charge needs to be."
