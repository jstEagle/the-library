## 34. Breaching the Grid

Tam had wired charges before. He'd blown stumps off riverbanks for dock builders, cracked a beached hull loose from a sandbar once when Mercer swore it was the only way. He knew the mathematics of it — the charge weight, the standoff distance, the way water carried shock like a fist through a wall of glass. He knew what it did to flesh, which was why his hands were steady and his mouth was dry.

This was different. This wasn't demolition. This was surgery on something that had been bleeding for forty years.

The containment grid rose out of the source cavern like the ribs of a drowned cathedral — rusted steel lattice bolted into living rock, furred with mineral bloom the color of old bruises, its cells clogged with decades of silt that should never have been there. Behind it, visible through gaps in the corroded mesh, the water ran black. Not shadow-dark. Black the way oil is black, black with a shine to it, black that moved with a sluggish confidence, as if it had all the time in the world.

And around it, the source itself: clear water rising from the throat of the cavern in columns so pure they were almost invisible, catching the light of their lamps and throwing it back broken into drifting rainbows. The place where the Rivergate was born. The place the villages downstream drank from, baptized in, named themselves after.

Tam knelt at the base of the grid with a headlamp and a roll of det cord and tried not to think about how much he loved these people.

"You're sure about the placement?" Coral crouched beside him, her voice low. It didn't need to be low. Ashe's men had pulled back to the far ledge after the broadcast — after her father's voice came crackling out of every radio they carried, reading testimony into the dark like a man dictating from the bottom of a grave — but old habits held. You didn't shout near a charge.

"Sure as I can be." Tam pressed a primer into the cord's end and checked it twice. "We're not trying to drop the whole grid. Just the lower panels. Sela said the pressure behind it is already doing most of the work — the structure's failing anyway. We're just helping it fail honestly."

"Honestly," Coral repeated. She almost smiled. "That's one word for it."

"It's the only word for it." He fed the cord along a crossbeam, taping it at intervals. "Everything else about this place has been a lie since before I could swim. The maps lie. The surveys lie. That man over there—" he tipped his chin toward Commander Ashe, who stood apart from his soldiers at the cavern's edge with his cap in his hands like a man at a funeral he'd been dreading for years, "—his whole career is a lie with medals on it. We're not blowing up a dam, Coral. We're blowing up the alibi."

Above them, on a shelf of rock near the cavern's high arch, Sela worked by lamplight with Imani Reyes, the two of them bent over the surveyor's salvaged instruments and the waterproof pages of Mercer's logbook. Their voices drifted down in fragments — *flow rate*, *dilution curve*, *downstream travel time* — the vocabulary of people doing arithmetic with lives. Every few minutes Sela would glance at the radio handset beside her, still warm from the broadcast, as if she couldn't quite believe it had worked. As if she expected the sky to reach down into the cavern and take the words back.

It wouldn't. That was the whole point. Words out were words gone. Tam had heard his own heartbeat in the silence after Coral finished reading, heard Junie's ragged breathing over the open channel from somewhere far downstream, running the dying river toward the village at the bend with nothing but a boat and a promise. The transmission had died mid-sentence, the battery finally giving out, and the silence afterward had been the loudest thing Tam had ever heard.

Then Ashe had lowered his sidearm. Tam had watched him do it — watched the commander look at his own men, at the crew, at the black water seeping through the grid, and say, quietly, in a voice that carried in the stone hollow of the cavern: *"Stand down."*

One of his sergeants had started to object. Ashe had turned and looked at him, and whatever passed between them had ended the conversation before it began. Now the soldiers stood along the ledge with their weapons slung, watching the water like everyone else, and Tam understood that something had broken in the commander that no order could mend. He'd spent his whole life inside the lie. Hearing it read aloud in a dead diver's voice had taken the walls down.

"He's going to catch hell for this," Coral murmured, following Tam's gaze.

"He's going to catch worse than hell if we don't finish." Tam tied off the cord's run and unspooled it back toward the rocks, measuring the distance in paces. Water carried blast shock farther than air — double, nearly triple. He wanted eighty meters between himself and those lower panels, and he wanted it in a straight line, and the cavern floor offered him a crooked sixty-five. "Sela! How long do we have before the village needs the warning window closed?"

Sela's voice came down from the shelf, steady but stripped thin. "Junie signaled twenty minutes ago. She's past the narrows. If we breach now, the front takes four hours to reach the bend — she'll have the village moving long before." A pause. "The aquifer won't forgive us a second delay. Every hour that water sits behind this grid is another hour of plume spreading into the clean channels."

Tam looked at Coral. She looked at the grid. Somewhere in the rusted lattice, in the dark behind the mesh, divers were buried — men in navy gear who had been sent down thirty years ago to keep a secret and had been kept instead. Her father among them, or near enough. His dive tags sat against Tam's friend's sternum right now, on a cord under her shirt, and Tam thought: *she's about to blow up their headstone.*

"Coral," he said quietly. "You don't have to throw the switch."

"Yes," she said, "I do." And then, softer, with the ghost of her father's river-flat accent surfacing in it: "He waited thirty years for someone to make some noise down here. I'm not going to be quiet about it."

* * *

They retreated along the crooked path Tam had measured, past the graves — the row of stacked stones the villagers' grandparents had raised at the cavern's edge, each one marked with a rusted dive tag lashed to a stick, a little cemetery of men the navy had listed as lost in training accidents in waters that appeared on no chart. Reyes paused at the stones on her way to the firing point, and Tam saw her touch one, briefly, the way you'd touch a stranger's shoulder at a funeral.

Ashe stood alone at the far end of the ledge. His radioman had rigged the surviving handsets together on a crate — every channel still open, the broadcast dead but the frequencies live, waiting. Whatever happened next would happen on the record. Tam realized that was deliberate. The commander had decided, sometime in the last hour, that everything from here forward would be witnessed.

"Firing wire's good," Tam said, settling at the clacker. His hands were steady. His voice wasn't quite. "Coral. On your count."

She stood beside him, close enough that he could feel her breathing. Sela had come down from the shelf and stood at her other side, and for a moment none of them spoke. The source rose behind the grid, clear and patient, the oldest thing any of them had ever seen.

"For the record," Ashe said from the end of the ledge, formal as a court proceeding, "Commander Elias Ashe, commanding, authorizes and witnesses the controlled breach of containment structure seven, Rivergate headwaters. The structure was constructed without congressional authorization in—" his voice caught, just barely, "—in nineteen seventy-one. Proceed."

Coral squeezed Tam's shoulder. "Do it."

Tam counted down from three, mostly for himself, and threw the clacker.

The world went white and then went loud. The blast came through the rock first, up through his boots and into his teeth, and then the sound arrived — a flat, enormous concussion that slapped the cavern walls and came back doubled, tripled, an avalanche of echo with nowhere to go. Dust fell from the high arch in silver threads through the lamplight. Somewhere above, a chunk of the ceiling calved off and hit the water with a boom like a door slamming on a century.

And the grid opened.

Not dramatically — not the Hollywood flower of fire Tam half-expected. The lower panels simply folded outward, peeled back like petals, and the black water behind them leaned into the gap and *poured*. It came in ropes and sheets, glossy and wrong, blooming into the clear rising current the way ink blooms into a glass — slow at first, then all at once, a great dark flower opening in the heart of the light. The rainbow drift of the source dimmed, browned, curdled. Where the plume met the rising columns, the water churned and smoked faintly, giving up a smell that reached them even at sixty-five meters — chemical and mineral and old, the smell of a wound that had been packed shut for forty years finally allowed to bleed.

Nobody cheered. Tam hadn't expected cheering. But he watched faces — Coral's, Sela's, even the soldiers on the ledge — and what he saw wasn't horror, exactly. It was recognition. Forty years of quiet sickness in the valley below, the cancers nobody had counted, the fish that had thinned year by year while the official reports said *stable* — all of it made visible at last, flowing, undeniable, wearing its true color in the brightest room in the world.

"There it is," Sela whispered. Her hand had found Coral's. "There's all of it. Every drop they said wasn't there."

Reyes was already moving down toward the waterline with her sample kit, professional to the bone, kneeling at the flood's edge with vials in her frostbitten-steady hands. "Plume's mixing faster than modeled," she called back, half-shouting over the roar of the breach. "The aquifer's drawing it straight down-channel — Sela, your numbers were conservative. Downstream concentration at the bend could peak inside three hours, not four."

"Junie's ahead of it," Coral said. It wasn't hope in her voice. It was arithmetic, and she was choosing to believe it.

Tam let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding since the night the patrol skiff rammed them in the dark. His ears rang. His legs felt strange, weightless, the way they always did after a shot went off — the body's dumb confusion at having survived its own intentions. He looked at the ruined grid, at the black water pouring through it into the clear, and thought about his father, who had taught him engines and honesty in equal measure and died owing money on both. *Some jobs,* the old man used to say, *you don't get paid for. You get paid BY.* Tam had never known what he meant until now.

Then the radios crackled.

All of them at once — the crate of handsets, the soldiers' units, the spare on Reyes's belt — a burst of static and then a voice, clipped and furious and very far away, calling a callsign that made every soldier on the ledge stand up straighter.

Ashe picked up the nearest handset. Listened. Tam watched the man's face do something complicated — watched the last of the institutional man and the newly honest one fight for the same ground.

"They know," Ashe said, lowering the handset. He didn't raise his voice; the cavern did it for him. "Command's monitoring the channels. They heard the broadcast, they've got seismic on the breach, and they are asking me to report the status of 'hostile elements' at the site." His eyes moved across the crew — Coral, Sela, Tam, Reyes, the graves, the black bloom. "Meaning us."

"What are you going to tell them?" Coral asked.

Ashe was quiet for a moment. Out beyond the cavern mouth, far upstream of the flood's slow advance, the daylight was changing — Tam could see it at the entrance, a flicker of rotor-shadow crossing the canyon light, there and gone. Then another. Helicopters. Coming fast.

"I'm going to tell them the truth," Ashe said. "All of it. In writing. With my name on it." He set the handset down on the crate, deliberately, like a man laying down a weapon. "And then I'm going to ask them to come down here and explain to me why a navy transport full of divers sank in a river that doesn't exist, and why I've spent eleven years guarding the answer."

The rotor-shadow crossed the light again, closer now, and the sound arrived a beat later — a heavy thrum rolling down the canyon, bouncing off the walls, drowning the rush of the breach. Every soldier turned toward the cavern mouth. One of them, the young sergeant who'd objected earlier, looked at Ashe, and his hand drifted toward his rifle sling — not raising it, just finding it, the way a man finds a railing in rough water.

"Sir," the sergeant said. "Those aren't ours."

Ashe looked up at the flickering light at the mouth of the cavern, and Tam saw him arrive, all at once, at the understanding that surrendering to one institution did not mean the other institutions would let you.

"No," Ashe said quietly. "They're not."

The thrum grew. The black water poured. And at the far downstream edge of Tam's hearing, faint beneath the rotors and the flood, the radio on the crate spat a single burst of signal — Junie's callsign, three short clicks, the prearranged mark meaning *village warned, people moving, I'm coming back upstream.*

Into whatever was coming down that canyon to meet them.
