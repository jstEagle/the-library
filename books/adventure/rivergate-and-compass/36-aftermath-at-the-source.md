## 36. Aftermath at the Source

Imani Reyes had spent eleven days believing she would die in the water, so when the water finally came for everyone else instead, she did not know what to feel.

She stood on the high shelf of rock above the cathedral cavern with Tam's coat around her shoulders — it was too big for her, smelling of engine oil and river salt — and watched the black water bloom outward from the breached containment grid like ink dropped into milk. The sound was wrong for something so enormous. She had expected thunder. What she got was a long, low exhalation, the sound of a held breath released after thirty years, and beneath it a grinding that she felt in her back teeth rather than heard.

The flood took the lower galleries first. Then it found the old service tunnel, the one Mercer's logbook had mapped, and it went through the mine adits like water through a throat. Imani had been a hydrologist for fourteen years. She had modeled dam failures, tailings breaches, aquifer collapses. She knew exactly what she was watching, and knowing made it worse: every gallon of that black water carried decades of concealed runoff — heavy metals, processing chemicals, the slow poison of an industry that had never officially existed — out of the dark and into the light of day, where no one could pretend anymore.

"That's it," Tam said quietly beside her. The engineer's hands were still shaking. They had not stopped since the breach charge went off. "That's the whole lie, going downstream."

"It's not a lie anymore," Imani said. "It's evidence."

Coral Voss stood at the edge of the shelf, radio in her hand, though the radio had gone silent twenty minutes ago. Whatever she had broadcast on every open channel before the transmission died, it was done. You could not recall a voice once it had gone out over the airwaves. Imani had learned that lesson in a flooded camp with her team dead around her, and Coral Voss had apparently learned it standing in a cavern full of graves, reading a dead man's testimony to anyone who would listen.

The commander's soldiers stood apart from them on the ledge, weapons slung, saying nothing. Commander Ashe stood farthest out, closest to the drop, watching the black water rise up the walls of the cavern below. He had ordered his men to stand down rather than fire. Imani kept waiting to understand why, and kept failing, and eventually decided that some arithmetic was not meant to be understood from the outside.

* * *

They came down off the mountain two days later, following the flood's wake, because there was nothing left to guard and nowhere left to hide.

Imani walked because walking was possible now, and for eleven days it had not been. Her legs worked badly. Her lungs ached where the strainer had crushed them. But the trail downriver was soft with new mud, and the river beside it ran brown and loud and honest, carrying its confession toward the valley at a pace no one could stop.

Junie met them at the bend.

The deckhand came poling a battered skiff around the last curve of the gorge, and Imani saw the moment the crew saw her — saw Junie, who had run the dying river alone, downstream, ahead of the flood, to warn a village of people who owed them nothing. The kid looked like they'd been dragged behind the boat for forty miles. One arm was wrapped in a filthy bandage. Their eyes were red-rimmed and hollowed out.

But they were grinning.

"You're alive," Junie said, and then, as if remembering protocol, "all of you," and then, abandoning protocol entirely, they let the pole fall and grabbed the gunwale as the boat bumped the bank, and Tam waded out into knee-deep water to haul them ashore, and Coral held onto the kid's shoulders and pressed her forehead against theirs and said nothing at all, which Imani understood was the loudest thing she'd said in weeks.

"The village?" Sela asked. It was the question under all the other questions. Imani had watched the hydrologist-in-exile carry it down the whole mountain like a stone sewn into her coat.

"High ground," Junie said. "We got them moving the night before. Old Mercer's channel markers — the ones he told me about, the ones nobody else uses anymore — they still hold. We ran the whole way in the dark." Junie's grin flickered, and something older moved underneath it. "The water came through the bend at dawn. Took the docks. Took the fish racks. Took the church roof." A breath. "Took nobody."

Sela sat down right there on the muddy bank, in front of everyone, soldiers included, and put her face in her hands, and no one pretended not to see.

* * *

The village at the bend received them the way the river received rain: without surprise, without warmth, without refusal.

Imani had expected anger. She had prepared speeches on the walk down — apologies, explanations, the whole architecture of institutional contrition she'd rehearsed in hospital corridors after her rescue. She was Dr. Imani Reyes, government survey team, sole survivor, and she had come up this river with instruments and permits to measure a place her own agency had spent three decades pretending did not exist. If anyone had a right to hate her, it was these people, whose water had been poisoned in secret while their grandparents were told the source was sacred and off-limits and none of their business.

But the villagers did not perform hatred. They fed them. That was the thing that undid her. Fish stew and flatbread, set down in front of navy soldiers and salvage divers and a half-drowned scientist alike, as if hunger were the only rank that mattered.

An old woman — Imani never learned her name; she seemed to be the kind of person whose name was simply the village — looked at Imani's government-issue jacket for a long time across the fire.

"You measured our river," the woman said.

"Yes," Imani said. "And we found what was killing it. And my team died finding it, and I don't know yet if that means anything to anyone, but I'm going to make sure it does."

The woman considered this. "The river already knew," she said. "But it is good that someone wrote it down. The river can't testify. People only listen to paper." She refilled Imani's bowl. "Eat. Paper people faint if they don't eat."

* * *

The journalists arrived with the third day, riding the floodwater down from the headwaters in rented boats, and they arrived the way weather arrives — everywhere at once, impossible to argue with.

Imani watched them come ashore at the bend: six boats, a dozen reporters, two drone operators whose machines rose into the canyon air like nervous birds. Someone upstream had heard Coral's broadcast. Someone had pulled files. Someone had talked — a records clerk, a retired hydrologist, a widow of one of the drowned divers, Imani would learn later, a whole chain of people who had waited thirty years for permission to speak and had taken Coral Voss's desperate voice on an open channel as exactly that.

The first reporter to reach Imani was young, wet to the waist, holding a recorder out like a torch.

"Dr. Reyes — you were with the survey team. Can you confirm—"

"I can confirm everything," Imani said, and heard her own voice come out flat and steady, the voice she used for data. "I can confirm the tributary exists. I can confirm the containment structures at the source, and their age, and their failure history. I can confirm the mining operation that fed them, and the runoff schedule, and the aquifer contamination plume my team mapped before we were flooded out of our camp by a deliberate release from upstream." She paused. "I can confirm bodies. In navy gear. In the caverns. Old ones. My team found them. That's why there was a release."

The recorder trembled slightly in the young reporter's hand. "Who gave the order to flood your camp?"

"That," Imani said, "is the question I have been asking since I was pulled out of a strainer half-drowned. Ask it loudly. Ask it for a long time. Don't let anyone answer it with a retirement."

Behind her, she heard Ashe shift his weight in the mud.

* * *

She found him that evening at the edge of the village, alone, looking downriver.

He had surrendered his sidearm to the senior naval investigator who'd arrived with the press — a procedure Imani suspected was entirely theater, since the man had ordered his soldiers to stand down in a cavern and had not resisted since — but the surrender of the weapon was nothing compared to what he did next, and Imani only saw it because she had come looking for him.

He carried a waterproof case. Standard navy issue, scuffed, old. He walked down to the investigators' boat, where a lieutenant with a clipboard was cataloguing recovered materials, and he set the case on the gunwale and opened it.

Even from ten meters, even in the failing light, Imani could see what was inside: notebooks. Dozens of them. Decades of them. Field logs, water-quality readings, incident reports stamped and countersigned and buried, correspondence with initials she recognized from her own agency's org charts, and photographs — photographs of divers, of equipment, of the source itself, taken years before any of them had ever seen it.

Ashe handed the case to the lieutenant and said, in a voice pitched not to carry but carrying anyway, "Everything. Chain of custody starts now. I'll sign whatever you need me to sign."

"You understand what this means, Commander," the lieutenant said carefully.

"I've understood what it means for about nine years," Ashe said. "I just didn't have the courage to say it out loud until a salvage diver read a dead man's testimony over an open radio channel in front of my own men." He closed his empty hands. "I'm not drowning alone in this. Take it all."

Imani stayed hidden in the shadow of the boat shed and did not go to him. She wanted to — wanted to ask him about the release order, about her team, about whether he had signed it or merely obeyed it or merely known and done nothing, and whether those three things were different crimes or the same crime wearing different uniforms. But she had been a scientist long enough to know when to let the record speak first. His notebooks would answer more honestly than he would. They always did.

Still, as she turned away, she heard him say one more thing to the lieutenant, quiet enough that she almost missed it:

"There's a diver listed in the 1994 logs. Villalobos. Make sure his file goes to his family before it goes to the press. They've waited long enough to be lied to politely."

* * *

The investigation swallowed the valley the way the flood had — thoroughly, impersonally, leaving changed terrain behind.

Investigators came in helicopters. Then lawyers. Then more lawyers. Imani gave testimony four times in six days: to the naval board, to the environmental authority, to a parliamentary subcommittee via satellite link from the village's single working phone, and once, strangely, to a woman from the historical records office who asked almost nothing about the crime and everything about the divers — their names, their gear, their families, whether any of them had kept journals.

"They did," Imani said. "One of them kept a logbook for thirty years. His name was Mercer. He died getting us here. His book is with his crew."

"Then his book will matter," the woman said, writing it down as if a logbook were a legal instrument, which, Imani was beginning to understand, it was.

Justice, when it came, came the way Imani had always privately known it would: imperfectly, partially, with gaps. Two mid-level officials resigned into comfortable obscurity. A contracting company dissolved and reconstituted under a new name within the month. The order for the release that killed her team traveled upward through signatures until it dissolved into institutional fog, and the fog, as fogs do, declined to identify itself.

But the aquifer remediation funding passed. The source was designated a protected site pending full decontamination — the first such designation in the region's history. The villages along the bend received clean-water infrastructure with their names, not a corporation's, on the contracts. And the names of the drowned divers — all of them, the old ones in the caverns and the recent ones in the current — were read aloud at a hearing into the public record, on the transcript, permanent, unspillable, unfloodable.

Imani attended that hearing and thought: *this is not enough.* And also: *this is not nothing.* Both thoughts were true. She was learning to hold both.

* * *

On her last evening at the bend, before the helicopters took her back downriver to testify again, she found Coral Voss sitting on the rebuilt dock — the third dock that season, the villagers joked grimly, each one better than the last — with her feet hanging over the water.

Imani sat down beside her. For a while neither of them spoke. The river ran past, brown still, but lighter at the edges, the silt beginning to settle. Somewhere downstream, the flood was meeting the sea.

"My team," Imani said finally. "Okafor. Lindqvist. Bright. They came up here to map a tributary and they found a graveyard, and the graveyard is the reason they're dead, and I need you to know that I'm going to spend whatever's left of my career making sure their findings are the foundation everything gets built on. Not a footnote. The foundation."

Coral nodded slowly. "Mercer used to say the river keeps its own ledger," she said. "I used to think that was just old man talk. A way of letting the water off the hook." She turned the compass over in her hands — her father's compass, the one that pointed wrong, the one that had pointed true at the end. "Now I think he meant it as a warning. To us. Keep your own ledger too, because the river's version takes decades and drowns people."

"Is that what you'll do? Keep the ledger?"

"I'm a salvage diver, Dr. Reyes. I recover what's lost and I hand it to whoever it belongs to." Coral looked out at the water. "Turns out most of what was lost belonged to everybody."

Imani smiled despite herself, despite the exhaustion, despite the four funerals she had not yet been able to attend because the hearings would not release her. "That's a terrible business model."

"So I'm told. Repeatedly. By creditors." Coral's mouth quirked. Then it sobered. "There's a wreck downriver. Navy transport. Officially it doesn't exist. I dove it before any of this started, on a contract I shouldn't have taken, and I pulled a sealed case off it that turned out to be stitched into all of—" she gestured vaguely at the river, the mountains, the whole poisoned inheritance, "—this. There's more down there. I can feel it. And now that nobody's shooting at us, I keep thinking somebody ought to go back down and bring up the rest."

"You're going to dive a wreck the navy denies exists, right after helping bury the navy's biggest cover-up."

"I'm going to dive it with journalists' phone numbers written on my arm," Coral said, "and a witness on the boat. Times change."

Imani laughed — really laughed, the first time since the camp — and the sound went out over the water, and the water did not care, and somehow that made it better.

Downriver, past the bend, past the shallows where the village children were already swimming again in water that tested clean for the first time in a generation, the old transport lay in the cold dark with its secrets rusting quietly into the mud. And somewhere between the dock and the wreck, between the testimony and the tide, the rest of the story waited — patient as sediment, certain as current — for whoever was brave enough, or broke enough, or stubborn enough to come back for it.

Coral Voss turned the compass over once more, watched the needle swing and settle, and began, quietly, to plan the dive.
