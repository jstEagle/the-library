## 37. Burying Mercer

The river bend where they buried Mercer was the same bend where he had first told Coral about the two divers who drowned, thirty years and one lifetime ago. She recognized it by the leaning cottonwood — a tree split nearly in half by some old lightning strike but stubbornly alive, both halves still leafing out every spring like a man who refused to stop working with half his body gone. Mercer had pointed at it from the wheelhouse of the *Magpie* and said, *That's what a river does to you, girl. Splits you down the middle and expects you to keep growing anyway.*

Now they dug his grave beneath that tree, in ground that was soft from the flood's retreat, and Coral could not stop thinking about how much he would have hated the ceremony of it.

He'd have hated the shovels most of all. Tam had brought three, good ones, from the boat's tool locker, and Junie had protested that they were for work, not for this, and Tam had said quietly, "This is work," and nobody had argued after that.

The grave went down four feet before Junie hit clay and called it deep enough. Coral climbed out, mud to her knees, her hands blistered in the way hands blister when grief makes you grip too hard, and looked at the hole, and thought: *too small*. Not physically — physically it was exactly right, six foot two if you accounted for the way old men fold. But it was too small for forty years of river knowledge, for the logbook in her pack with its water-swollen pages, for the supply runs he'd made in the dark and the silence he'd kept and the bullet he'd taken so that three people and a half-drowned scientist could disappear into side channels while soldiers' headlights swept the narrows behind them.

Too small for all of that. Graves always were.

* * *

They buried him at morning, because Mercer had been a morning man. He used to say the river lied to you after noon — went smooth and pretty and hid its teeth — but at dawn it told you the truth about itself, cold and gray and honest. So at dawn they carried him up from the boat on a stretcher lashed from oars and tarp, and the whole village at the bend came out to stand along the bank.

Coral hadn't expected that. She'd expected the five of them — herself, Tam, Junie, Sela, Imani Reyes walking carefully with her ribs still strapped — and maybe a handful of curious villagers. Instead there were dozens. Old men who remembered supply boats running upstream in the years before the navy closed the reach. Women who had bought flour and lamp oil off Mercer's deck without ever asking where he'd gotten it or why he never met their eyes. Children too young to know his name, standing solemn because everyone else was.

An elder woman — Coral had learned her name was Odile, and that she ran the village the way a keel runs a boat, unseen and absolutely necessary — stepped forward as they lowered him. She spoke in the river dialect first, low and rhythmic, and then she switched to trade speech so that the strangers could understand.

"This man fed us in the hungry year," she said. "We did not know then why he came only in darkness, or why he wept once at our fire and would not say why. Now we know. Now we know what it cost him to keep coming." She looked at Coral. "You bury your dead facing which way?"

"Downriver," Coral said. Her voice came out rough. "So they can go home."

Odile considered this. "Then turn him around."

Coral blinked. "Ma'am?"

"He spent thirty years trying to go home and not being able to find the door." The old woman's face was seamed like driftwood. "Bury him facing upstream. Let him look at the source. He earned the view."

So they turned him. It took all of them, and it was awkward and undignified and Mercer would have complained the entire time, and Coral found that she was laughing while she cried, which she supposed was the correct way to do anything involving Mercer Okafor.

They filled the grave by hand. Nobody suggested the winch. Tam shoveled until his engineer's shoulders shook; Junie shoveled with the furious efficiency of someone who had been a child when Mercer taught her to tie a bowline and could not reconcile that child-knowledge with this hole in the ground. Sela worked steadily, silently, her dark hair tied back, and when Coral glanced at her she saw that Sela was mouthing something — a prayer, or a name, or perhaps just *thank you*, over and over, to a man she had known for less than a week and owed more than a lifetime.

Imani Reyes stood apart, one hand pressed to her strapped ribs, and saluted. A real salute, crisp, military-trained, held too long. When Coral caught her eye, the surveyor said simply: "He died so my team's data would live. My government will forget me before it forgets him. That's backwards, but it's something."

* * *

Afterward, when the mound was dressed with river stones and Junie had planted a cutting from the cottonwood at its head — "It'll take," she said defiantly, daring anyone to tell her a tree couldn't grow from a stick in clay — Coral opened the logbook.

She had read it twice since the narrows. Once in the immediate aftermath, by lamplight, hunting for anything that might help them survive Ashe's pursuit. And once later, alone at the bow watch, slowly, the way you drink something expensive — because she'd realized somewhere in the second reading that this was the last new thing Mercer would ever give her, and she wanted to make it last.

It was a shipwright's ledger originally, its stamped cover half worn away: *PROPERTY OF RIVERGATE MARINE WORKS*. Inside, in Mercer's small, precise hand — the hand of a man who'd had schooling once and never let anyone forget it — thirty years of the river.

The early entries were ordinary. Depths, currents, fuel burns, the small economies of a working pilot. Then, thirty-one years back, the entries changed. They started appearing at night, dated wrong on purpose, locations given as numbers instead of names. *Ran cargo to marker 9. Two passengers, no conversation.* *Water high, ran the slot at first light, don't ask me how.* And then, in an entry Coral had read eleven times now:

*Divers went down at the source today. Ruiz and Calloway. Navy men, good ones, or they said they were. Only Ruiz came up. Calloway's line went slack and they wouldn't let anyone go after him. Told us it was a cave-in. Told us the water did it. The water didn't do it. I've run this river twenty years and the water doesn't do things. People do things.*

And below that, in handwriting gone suddenly large and unsteady:

*I am done pretending I don't know what's under this valley. But I have a daughter in Rivergate and a crew who eats because I keep quiet, and so I will keep quiet, and God forgive me, I will keep running these boats.*

He had a daughter. Coral had read that line and sat very still for a long time. She'd known Mercer twenty years — he'd been on her father's crew, then hers, then just *hers*, the way family is yours without paperwork — and he had never once mentioned a daughter. She'd asked Sela, who'd asked Odile, and the answer had come back soft and sad: a woman in a downriver town, estranged these fifteen years, sent money anonymously through a freight office every quarter. Junie had already gone to find her. That was Junie's errand, and she'd left at dawn yesterday with the village's fastest skiff and a face like flint, and Coral missed her fiercely and knew she was exactly where she should be.

Today, though, at the graveside, Coral wasn't going to read the secret entries. Today she was going to read the other kind — the ones that showed who he was when he wasn't carrying the weight.

She cleared her throat. The crowd along the bank quieted.

"Mercer Okafor kept a logbook for forty years," she said. "Most of it's depths and diesel and complaints about my steering. I'm going to read some of the complaints. I think he'd hate that, which is why I'm doing it."

A ripple of laughter moved through the villagers. Good. He'd have wanted them comfortable. He'd have wanted them fed — she'd already ordered the village stores stocked with the last of their trade goods, and Tam was drawing up plans to leave the *Magpie*'s spare pump with them, and none of it was charity, it was all debt, all of it, everything they had was debt now and Coral was finally at peace with that.

She read:

*"March 14th. The girl Voss steered us into a wing dam again. Third time. Told her a river is a sentence and you have to read it before you speak it. She told me I talk too much. She's not wrong but neither am I."*

More laughter. Tam's hand found Coral's shoulder and squeezed.

*"June 2nd. Taught the little one — Junie — to tie a bowline. She tied it left-handed and faster than I can right-handed. Told her she'll be running boats someday. She said she'd rather fix them. Fine. Fixers are rarer than runners."*

Junie wasn't there to hear it, and Coral's throat closed on the next entry, and Sela stepped forward without being asked and read it for her, her voice low and clear across the water:

*"September 9th. Voss's father dove the wreck today. Came up with that compass of his acting strange, pointing upriver. He laughed about it. Said the river was calling him home. I didn't laugh. I've seen where it calls people."*

Coral took the book back. The last entry she read, she read slowly, because it was the last entry, written in the hospital at Rivergate with a hand that shook, two days before the ambush, two days before the narrows, two days before:

*"If anyone reads this: I ran supplies to a place I should have burned down. I kept a silence I should have broken. I told myself it was for my crew. It was partly for my crew. Mostly I was afraid, and fear wears duty's clothes so well you can't tell them apart in the mirror. The girl — Coral — she isn't afraid, or she is and goes anyway, which is the same thing and better. Make the river tell the truth. That's the whole job. That was always the whole job."*

She closed the book. For a moment nobody moved, and the river made its long gray sound against the bank, honest, dawn-honest, telling the truth about itself.

Then Odile began to sing.

It was a working song, Coral learned later — a hauling song, the kind sung when boats came through the shallows and everyone pulled together. The villagers sang it in their own tongue and then, verse by verse, in trade speech, and the words were simple and old and about water carrying what hands cannot hold. Tam sang. Imani sang, badly. Sela sang with her eyes closed. Coral didn't sing. She stood with her hand flat on the fresh-turned earth and felt, absurdly, irrationally, certain — the way you feel certain about current and load and weather, the certainties of a working riverwoman — that somewhere upstream, in the cathedral cavern where the river rises out of the dark earth, her father and Ruiz and Calloway and all the others whose bones the containment grid had hidden were hearing it.

*Make the river tell the truth.* Well. The river was telling it now. Black water blooming into the source, investigators coming downstream with the flood, Ashe surrendering his records rather than drown alone in the lie. The truth was loose in the world the way silt is loose after a flood — everywhere, undeniable, settling into everything.

But Mercer wasn't here to see it. Mercer was here, under the stones, facing upstream at last.

* * *

They stayed at the bend three days, because the *Magpie* needed work and grief needed time and the flood had left both in similar condition.

Tam rebuilt the raw-water intake that had clogged with silt during the breach surge, cursing cheerfully the whole time, and Coral noticed that he'd stopped flinching at loud noises, which meant the ambush was loosening its grip on him, which meant he'd be all right, eventually, the way Tam was always all right — slowly, thoroughly, with diagrams.

Sela spent the days with Odile and the village's elders, mapping what they knew of the aquifer against what Imani's data showed, and the two women emerged each evening looking like conspirators, which Coral supposed they were — conspirators on behalf of clean water. There was talk of a monitoring station at the bend. Talk of the villages hiring their own surveyors. Talk, Sela said one night with a wonder she didn't bother hiding, of people who lived along the river deciding for themselves what happened to it. "Do you understand what we did?" she asked Coral, in the dark of the bow, her shoulder warm against Coral's. "Not the breach. Anyone can break a thing. We made it so they can't put it back."

"I know what we did," Coral said. "I also know what it cost."

"Yes." Sela didn't soften it. She never softened things, and Coral loved her for it. "Mercer. Your salvage. Your father's whole life. It cost all of it. That's the arithmetic. I won't pretend it balances."

"It doesn't have to balance," Coral said. "It just has to be paid."

On the third evening Junie came back.

Coral saw the skiff rounding the bend at dusk and was moving before she'd decided to move, down the bank, into the shallows, catching the painter as Junie threw it. Junie's face was blotchy and set, and she jumped down onto the mud beside Coral and stood there a second, breathing, before she said:

"She came."

Coral's heart did something complicated. "Mercer's daughter."

"Nadia." Junie wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a streak of river mud. "She's — she's coming up on the mail boat tomorrow. I told her where he is. I told her about the logbook. She said—" Junie's voice cracked and steadied. "She said she got the money orders for fifteen years and never once wrote back, because she was angry, because he chose the river over them, and she wanted him to come crawling. And now he can't, and she said—" Junie stopped. Started again. "She said, *tell whoever buried him that I want to see where he's keeping himself.*"

Coral looked at the fresh mound under the young cottonwood cutting, black against the last light, facing upstream toward the source.

"She can stay in my cabin," Coral said. "As long as she wants. As long as she needs."

"And the debts?" Junie asked, and Coral knew what she meant — not the creditors, those were dead letters now, drowned with the contract, but the other kind. The debts of the heart, the ones Mercer's logbook had itemized without knowing it: a daughter unforgiven, a silence unbroken, a truth delivered late.

"We pay those slow," Coral said. "That's the deal with those. You pay them slow, and you pay them in person, and you don't get to decide when you're done."

Junie nodded, and leaned into her, briefly, hard, the way she'd leaned into the wheel running the rapids — total commitment, no apology — and then pulled back and became practical again, because that was Junie's whole genius, grief and gear-checks occupying the same pair of hands.

"Mail boat docks at nine," she said. "I'll meet her. And Coral — Tam wants to cast off day after. Says there's survey work waiting downriver. Honest work. Village contracts." A pause, and then, with the ghost of her old grin: "He says somebody's got to map the tributary properly. Says it ought to be people the river trusts."

Coral looked upstream, where the water came down cold and gray and honest out of the forbidden country, past the cavern, past the graves, past the containment ruins that investigators were even now picking over like crows, all the way down to this bend, this tree, this stone-dressed mound where an old pilot lay finally facing the thing he'd feared and served and, in the end, helped to free.

"Yeah," she said. "Day after."

Behind them, the river kept talking, and for the first time in thirty years, everything it said was true.
