## 38. What the Salvage Was Worth

Tam Okafor had a theory about money, and it went like this: money was like ballast. You needed some of it — enough to keep the keel down, the fuel tanks wet, the crew fed — but past that point it just shifted around in the hold with every swell, throwing off your trim, making you list toward whatever you were chasing instead of whatever mattered.

He'd developed this theory over thirty years of being broke in interesting ways, and he'd never once had occasion to test its corollary: that sudden wealth was worse than no wealth at all. Until now. Because for about eleven days back in the spring, Tam had been rich.

Not rich-rich. Not yacht rich. But Commander Ashe's office had wired a "goodwill settlement" into Coral's account — the price of silence, dressed up as compensation for their conscripted search, for Mercer's bullet, for the season they'd lost — and for eleven days the *Rivergate and Compass* had been the best-provisioned boat on the waterway. New winch cable. A second bilge pump, backup, still in its box. Diesel topped to the brim. Junie had bought actual coffee, the good stuff from the roaster up on Mill Street, not the sawdust-in-a-tin they usually drank. Tam himself had ordered a lathe bit he'd been coveting for four years, the one that would let him true a propeller shaft without begging harbor time off some other yard.

And then Coral had walked into the harbormaster's office with a manila folder full of Ashe's paperwork and fed every page of it into the shredder while the clerk watched, and Tam had stood on the dock watching her do it, and something in his chest had done a strange thing — half grief, half relief, like a splinter coming out.

The wire reversal came through two days later. The bank took it all back. Every cent. The lathe bit order canceled itself. The good coffee ran out on a Tuesday morning and Junie made the switch back to tin-sawdust without comment, which was its own kind of grace.

Tam had expected to feel robbed. What he felt instead, standing in the drizzle outside the harbormaster's office with his hands in his pockets and nothing to show for the worst season of his life except a dead friend buried at the bend and a story nobody would print for another three weeks — what he felt was *lighter*.

That was the part nobody warned you about. That poverty could be light, if everyone carried it together.

* * *

It was October now. Six months since the source. The river had come back up with the autumn rains, fat and brown and moving, carrying silt from the breached containment grid forty miles upstream the way rivers carry everything eventually — patiently, thoroughly, without caring who objected. The water below Rivergate ran tea-dark some mornings, and twice the county had posted advisories, and both times the village at the bend had sent word downstream before the county did, because Sela's old hydrology network had never actually stopped existing; it had just gone quiet, waiting for someone to give it permission to speak again.

The investigation had teeth now. That was the phrase Imani Reyes used, teeth, and she said it with the particular satisfaction of a woman who'd spent three years being told her data was speculative. There were subpoenas. There was a subcommittee. There was, as of last month, an actual federal marshal assigned to the file, a tall patient woman named Okonkwo who came down to the docks sometimes just to drink bad coffee on the *Compass*'s aft deck and ask questions nobody had asked before, like *who signed the dredge permits in '94*, which was exactly the right question, and Tam liked her enormously.

But none of that put diesel in the tank.

That was the thing about the aftermath of a moral victory that nobody put in the songs. Truth was heavy cargo and it paid nothing. The boat still needed paint. The engine still had the knock — the knock had survived a patrol skiff ramming, rapids, a portage through bone country, and a flash flood, because Tam's engines were built by people who understood that machinery should outlive its owners' worst decisions — but the knock wanted parts, and parts wanted money, and the money was gone, shredded, symbolically, along with the contract.

Coral had taken it hardest, or claimed to. She'd spent the first week after the reversal doing that thing she did where she stared at the water like it owed her an apology. But Tam had watched her closely, because that was his job on this crew — not just the engines, but the people, the way you watched gauges — and what he'd actually seen, underneath the staring, was a woman who slept better than she had in two years. Debt didn't sleep well. Debt lay awake at 3 a.m. doing arithmetic. Coral hadn't done 3 a.m. arithmetic since spring. Whatever the salvage contract would have cleared, it would have cost more than it paid, and she knew it now the way you know things after they've stopped being theoretical.

"You're smiling," Junie had told her once, mid-September, over the rail. "You're broke and you're smiling."

"I'm broke," Coral had said, "and I'm *clean*."

Tam had thought about that for a long time afterward. Clean. He'd spent most of his adult life around boats and the men who owned them, and he could count on one hand the owners he'd met who were clean, and most of those had inherited rather than earned. Clean was expensive. Clean was the one luxury commodity on the river. And Coral Voss, who had started this whole catastrophe by diving a wreck nobody names for money she desperately needed, had ended it owning the only thing she couldn't have bought at any price.

* * *

The morning it happened — the morning Tam found the refit already underway — he'd come down to the dock early, earlier than anyone, because that was also his job. First light, low fog, the kind of morning where the river and the sky were the same color and the pilings went down into gray forever. He liked being first. He liked the boat asleep, the deck beaded with condensation, the whole world holding its breath before the day committed to anything.

Except the boat wasn't asleep.

There was a tarp off the engine hatch. There was a work light rigged to the dock box, burning yellow through the fog. There was Junie, seventeen years old and built like a whippet, halfway up the mast in a bosun's chair she'd rigged herself, feeding new halyard through the sheave box, humming something tuneless and defiant. There was Coral in the engine room, coveralls zipped to the throat, hair tied back, lying on the creeper under the main with her boots sticking out into the walkway, and there was — this was the part that made Tam stop in the gangway and just stand there for a second, fog beading on his beard —

There was Sela, sleeves rolled, holding the trouble light steady and reading torque specs off a tablet like she'd been born in an engine room, calling numbers down to Coral in a voice that was calm and precise and slightly amused, the way she always sounded when she was learning something fast, which was always.

Nobody had asked him. Nobody had waited. They'd just *started*.

"Morning, chief," Junie called down from aloft, cheerful as a pirate. "We decided not to wait for you. Hope that's okay."

"It is not okay," Tam said, automatically, and then, because it was six in the morning and the fog was pretty and his crew was already working, "it is extremely not okay, and also, whose idea was the halyard?"

"Coral's. She says the old one chafed at the spreaders and we've been ignoring it since June."

"We've been ignoring it since June because halyard costs money," Tam said, descending into the engine room, where Coral's boots were tapping slightly in rhythm with something — she worked to a beat in her head, always had, some internal percussion that never matched any song he knew. "What are we using for money?"

"Nothing," Coral said, muffled, from under the engine. "We're using nothing. Pass me the fourteen millimeter."

"There is no fourteen millimeter. The fourteen millimeter was in the socket set we sold in July."

"The twelve will do if you shim it."

"The twelve will strip the bolt head and then we'll be using nothing *and* have a stripped bolt."

"Then don't strip it," Coral said, sliding out on the creeper with a smudge of grease across her cheekbone like warpaint, looking up at him with that expression she got — chin tilted, eyes bright, absolutely certain of herself in a way that had gotten them shot at, flooded, and subpoenaed, and had also kept them alive. "Come on, Tam. Look at this boat. Look at her. She carried us through all of it. She deserves better than us waiting around for money that isn't coming."

He looked at the boat. Or rather, he looked at what the boat was becoming, because apparently it had been becoming it for hours while he slept. The rust streaks below the rub rail had been attacked with a wire brush and primer — cheap primer, the wrong color, a pale industrial gray that clashed magnificently with the hull's faded blue, so that the *Compass* currently looked like a quilt made by someone with strong opinions and no fabric budget. The chart table had been cleared for the first time since the survey run, and on it, weighted down with a wrench, sat a piece of paper that made Tam's chest go tight and warm at the same time.

It was a list. In Junie's handwriting, which was loopy and enthusiastic and slanted hard to starboard, like everything else about Junie. At the top it said: **HONEST WORK THE BOAT COULD DO**. And beneath it, numbered:

1. Water sampling runs for Sela's network (pays in diesel + prestige)
2. Ferry service, bend village ↔ Rivergate, market days
3. Dive support for the county bridge inspection (Okonkwo knows the engineer!)
4. Teaching Junie's navigation class?? (Junie wrote this one) (Coral circled it twice)
5. Salvage — *legitimate*, permitted, boring salvage

Under item five, in Coral's hand, smaller: *boring is fine. Boring might be the point.*

Tam read the list twice. Then he laughed — really laughed, the deep involuntary kind, the kind that comes up from somewhere below the lungs — and Coral looked at him from the deck plates and said, "What?"

"Item one pays in prestige," he said. "You put prestige on a budget line."

"Sela insisted. She says scientific prestige is a real currency and I believe her, because she once traded a dataset for a roof repair, so."

"And item four. Your deckhand wants to teach navigation."

"Junie ran point coming downriver during the flood while the rest of us were busy being terrified," Coral said. "Junie read that water better than I did. Better than Mercer would've, maybe. Mercer would've hated hearing me say that, and then secretly agreed, and then denied agreeing." Her voice caught for half a second on Mercer's name — it still did, sometimes, a small hitch like a rope over a rough fairlead — and then smoothed out. "Anyway, the village asked. There are three kids up at the bend who want their river licenses and nobody to teach them. Junie volunteered us."

"*Us*."

"I'm teaching the diving portion. You're teaching engines. Obviously."

Obviously. As though any of this were obvious. As though the natural endpoint of a season of gunfire and drowned camps and a broadcast that had ended three careers and opened forty investigations was a navigation class for teenagers at a bend in the river. Tam lay down on the second creeper — the good creeper, the one with wheels that worked — and slid in next to her, shoulder to shoulder under the block of the main, and picked up the twelve millimeter and the shim stock, and began, carefully, to not strip a bolt.

"So we're poor," he said, working. "Genuinely, structurally, long-term poor."

"Yep."

"And the plan is ferry runs and water samples and teaching children."

"Yep."

"And you're okay with that."

Coral was quiet for a moment. Under the engine, in the dark and the oil smell, quiet came easy. Then: "You know what I keep thinking about? The case. The original case. The one I dove the wreck for. Hydrographic charts of a tributary that wasn't supposed to exist, sealed, stamped, classified, worth — I don't know, whatever they were paying. Worth my debts, anyway. Worth a clean slate." She turned her head against the deck plate. "And I keep thinking about how the whole time I had it, I couldn't sleep. Not because of the danger. Because of the weight of it. Knowing what it was and holding it shut. That's what debt feels like too, actually. Same feeling exactly. Something sealed that should be open, and you carrying it around, and it getting heavier every mile."

"That's your theory?"

"That's my theory. Poverty's lighter. You carry it together." She passed him the shim. "Besides — look around. Junie's up the mast. Sela's holding the light. You're here at dawn for free, again, like always. If we'd taken Ashe's money, half this crew wouldn't be on this boat. Mercer would—" she stopped. Started again, gentler. "Mercer would've taken the money. You know he would've. He'd have called it practical. And he'd have been on the boat alone, or not on it at all, and either way it would've killed him faster than the bullet did."

Tam turned that over. It was true, and it was sad, and it was the kind of true-and-sad that Mercer himself would have appreciated, the old man had loved a hard truth delivered dry. *Make the river tell the truth.* His last request. His logbook sat in Coral's cabin now, in a waterproof bag, next to the compass that pointed home. Some nights Tam heard her in there reading it aloud to herself, quietly, the way you'd talk to someone sleeping.

"Okay," Tam said finally. "Poor it is. But I want it noted for the record that I had a lathe bit."

"Noted. Mourned. We'll get you another lathe bit when item three pays out."

"When the *bridge inspection* pays out."

"County contracts pay slow but they pay *real*," Coral said. "Ask Okonkwo. She checked the engineer's funding herself. She said, quote, 'this one's actually clean,' which from a federal marshal is basically a love letter."

* * *

By nine the fog had burned off and the dock had woken up, and the *Compass*'s strange gray-patched hull had drawn its usual audience. Word traveled on a waterfront the way current traveled in a channel — everywhere, invisibly, and fastest around obstacles. Everyone on the river knew the *Compass* now. Knew the story, or a version of it, versions multiplying like tributaries: that they'd found bodies in navy gear at the source; that a commander had cried when the broadcast went out (unverified); that the little deckhand had rowed forty miles through a dying river to warn a village (true, and understated — Junie had rowed through the night with a broken oar lock, rigging a spare with zip ties and pure fury, and had beaten the flood by ninety minutes, and had never once mentioned the oar lock until Tam found the ties still on the spare and asked).

People came by. That was the new thing. Before, the *Compass* had been just another working boat, ignored the way working boats get ignored. Now fishermen slowed to look. Old women from the bend village brought bread wrapped in cloth and stayed an hour. A kid — maybe ten, rubber boots two sizes too big — came down the dock on a Saturday and stood at the gangway and asked, with the directness of the young, "Is this the boat from the flood?" and Junie had crouched down to his level and said, "This is the boat from *everything*, wanna see the engine room?" and Tam had watched that exchange and understood that something had changed in the fundamental nature of the vessel. Boats were tools. This one had become a story with a hull around it. Stories drew people. People, occasionally, drew money — small amounts, honest amounts, ferry fares and sampling stipends and the occasional envelope from the bend village marked BOAT FUND in careful letters, which Coral refused twice and then accepted on the condition that it be logged publicly on the chart table, every dollar, visible to anyone who walked by.

Transparency as ballast. Tam approved. It was, he reflected, the exact opposite of a sealed case.

The creditors, meanwhile, had entered a strange new phase. Coral's largest creditor was a man named Denny Halloway, who held paper on the boat itself — second lien, the reason the salvage contract had existed in the first place, the reason she'd been diving wrecks nobody names at midnight. Denny was a dockside fixture, gold chain, laugh lines around a mouth that hadn't laughed sincerely in years. For six months after the source, Denny had kept his distance, watching the investigations with the expression of a man recalculating. And then, in late September, he'd come down the dock with two coffees and an offer.

Tam had been aboard for it. He'd wished he hadn't been, and he'd been glad he was, both at once.

"Voss," Denny had said, setting a cup on the chart table next to the public ledger, "look, everybody knows what you did. Everybody also knows what your testimony's worth. There are people — lawyers, journalists, one very interested documentary outfit out of the capital — who'd pay real money for access. Exclusive interviews, the logbook, the dive footage if any of it survived. I'm not saying sell the *story*. I'm saying license it. Structured. Tasteful. Everybody wins. I'd take my cut off the top, sure, but you'd clear your paper with me and walk free, and honestly, hon, the way this town works, somebody's going to monetize this whether you do or not. Better it's you."

Coral had listened all the way through, which was her discipline — she listened all the way through even to offers she intended to burn, especially to those. Then she'd picked up Denny's coffee, drunk a sip of it, set it back down, and said:

"Denny. Six months ago I would've taken that deal in a heartbeat. You know why I won't take it now?" She'd leaned forward, elbows on the chart table, next to the public ledger. "Because the river already told the truth. Free. To everyone. That was the whole point. If I start charging admission to it, I'm just building a new containment grid, and I've seen what those look like from the inside. They're rusted, they leak, and there are graves around them." A pause. "Also, and I say this with love: your interest rate is criminal and I'm contesting it with the state attorney general, who returns my calls now. So."

Denny had left without his coffee. The rate dispute was ongoing. Tam had framed nothing, but he'd thought about framing something.

* * *

October deepened. The refit went on in the evenings and on market-day gaps, funded by nothing, powered by spite and affection in roughly equal measure. The knock in the main got diagnosed — worn thrust bearing, of course, the one part that required the lathe — and Tam solved it the old way, the poor way: he machined a bronze bushing on the harbor cooperative's ancient lathe, trading three hours of labor on the co-op's own cranky compressor for the machine time, and the bearing came out beautiful, and the knock went away, and he stood in the engine room afterward listening to the idle with his eyes closed like other men listened to music.

Sela came and went between the boat and the bend village, where she was rebuilding her monitoring stations with county grant money that had materialized, astonishingly, because investigators needed baseline data and Sela was the only person alive who'd collected any before the breach. Her stations. Her exile. Her twenty years of being called a crank, vindicated by black water. She talked about it without triumph, which Tam found more impressive than triumph would have been. She talked about it like bookkeeping. Like the river keeping its own ledger, and everyone just finally reading it.

One evening in the third week of October, with the light going copper on the water and the smell of woodsmoke starting up along the shore, Tam climbed up to check Junie's halyard work — she'd spliced the thimble herself, and he wanted to inspect it before he trusted it, because trust on a boat is earned in millimeters — and from up there, forty feet above the dock, he looked down at the whole picture at once.

Coral and Sela at the rail, heads close, arguing gently about station placement, Coral's hand resting on Sela's forearm between points, the gesture so habitual now that neither of them noticed it anymore. Junie below them re-rolling hose with a technique she'd invented and defended in writing. The patched hull, ugly and honest. The chart table ledger, updated in three hands. Mercer's name painted small now inside the wheelhouse door frame, under the door latch where your fingers touched it every single time you came in from the cold — Junie's idea, done without asking, in gold paint pen, so that Mercer was literally part of the boat's handshake forever.

And the compass. Sitting on the shelf above the chart table, brass case polished, needle steady, pointing downriver. Toward Rivergate. Toward all of it.

Tam had spent his life around instruments, and he knew what a healthy instrument looked like, and this one was healthy, and it occurred to him — up there in the rigging, with the splice holding and the light failing — that the compass had probably been healthy for a long time. That maybe it had only ever pointed wrong when the person holding it was pointed wrong. That instruments didn't drift. People drifted, and blamed the needle.

Below, Coral laughed at something Sela said — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind that used to be rare on this boat and was now common enough that nobody remarked on it — and Junie yelled up, "Chief! Is the splice good or not? Stop having feelings up there and answer!"

"The splice is good," Tam called down. "The splice is better than good. Whoever taught you to taper a strand should be proud."

"You taught me!"

"I know," he said. "I'm proud."

He came down the rigging slowly, hand over hand, taking his time, because there was nowhere to hurry to and no one paying him to arrive. Down on deck, the evening settled around them like a tide. Somewhere upriver, at the source, rangers were building a visitor platform over the cathedral cavern — he'd heard it from Okonkwo, who'd heard it from the permit file — and school groups would stand there someday behind a rail, looking at clear water running over clean gravel, and most of them would never know what it had cost, and that was fine. That was the point, maybe. Containment grids were for hiding things. Rails were for showing them.

The debts were gone — not paid, *dissolved*, contested into abeyance, swallowed by the investigations like silt into current. The salvage that would have cleared them sat at the bottom of a shredder in a harbormaster's office, and Tam missed the lathe bit, and he'd miss it tomorrow, and it was still, by any accounting he trusted, the cheapest trade he'd ever watched a person make.

Poverty shared was lighter. He'd have staked the boat on it. He had, effectively, staked the boat on it.

They'd finish the hull paint by November, if the weather held. The ferry route started the week after. And it was Junie, naturally, Junie with her loop-slanted handwriting, who taped the final touch to the wheelhouse bulkhead that Friday — a fresh copy of the honest-work list, items one through five, with a sixth added at the bottom in fresh ink:

6. Whatever the river needs next.

Coral read it, and didn't cross it out, and didn't laugh, and didn't explain it. She just tapped it once with two fingers, the way you'd tap a gauge that was reading true.

Which was when the phone rang — the boat's old handset, mounted by the wheel, the number almost nobody had — and Tam answered it, because he was closest, and it was Okonkwo, the marshal, and her voice was different tonight. Careful. Measured. The voice of a woman choosing words the way you choose footing on scree.

"Tam. Is Coral there? And before you get her — I need you to hear this first, because it concerns the logbook. All of it. The pages Mercer never showed anyone."
