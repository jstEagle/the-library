## 39. Compass Points Home

The pages Okonkwo read aloud that night were not what anyone expected, and they were not, in the end, anything that changed the shape of the truth. They changed only its weight.

Mercer had written them in the winter before he died — Coral could tell by the ink, the way it went thin and gray when the cabin of the *Rivergate and Compass* dropped below freezing and his hands shook too badly for a full pen. He had written about the two divers who drowned at the source thirty years ago. He had written their names, which no report ever carried: Odile Ferrant, twenty-six. Basilio Reyes — no relation to Imani, though the coincidence had made the young surveyor go quiet for a long moment when she heard it. He had written how the navy's containment crew had ordered the dive anyway, how the current in the lower gallery was running three times its seasonal norm, how Mercer had begged them to wait and been told that waiting was not among his options.

And then, on the last page, in handwriting steadier than all the rest, as if he had saved his strength for exactly this:

*If anyone reads this, tell Coral she doesn't owe the river anything. She never did. The debt was always mine.*

Coral had sat with that page open on her knees until the lamp burned low and Sela put her hand over hers and closed the logbook gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

That had been eleven days ago. Now the morning came up gold and cold over Rivergate, and the river ran high with the first melt of the new season, loud enough to hear from the docks even over the hammers and grinders Tam had going aboard the boat.

---

The *Rivergate and Compass* looked better than she had any right to. Junie had scraped her hull down to bare wood along the port side where the patrol skiff had gouged her, and Tam had welded a new strake over the wound, and now Junie was painting it — not navy gray, not salvage-yard rust-red, but a deep river green that matched the water in spring. She worked with a brush in each fist and paint on her cheekbone and was singing something tuneless and triumphant about a woman who outswam a flood.

"You're getting it on the cleats," Tam called down from the wheelhouse without looking up from the engine manifold he had spread across the deck like surgery.

"I'm getting it *on purpose*," Junie called back. "It's called style."

Coral stood on the dock with the compass in her palm and let the noise wash over her. This was the sound she had decided to keep. Not the silence of money in an account she'd never see, not the clean paperwork of a contract paid in full by people who wanted her quiet — but this. Hammers. Bad singing. The river saying its one long word.

Her debts had not vanished. That was the strange arithmetic of it. The salvage contract had died unsigned somewhere in a navy office, and the creditors still sent their letters, and the letters still used words like *outstanding* and *final notice*. But Okonkwo had come down to the dock last week in her marshal's coat and stood very straight and told Coral, in front of Tam and Junie and half the fish market, that the recovery of Dr. Reyes and the evidence from the source constituted services rendered to the territory, and that certain filings had been made, and that Coral Voss should expect some of her creditors to become suddenly, bureaucratically reasonable. It wasn't forgiveness. It was something better: it was *on the record*. The river had testified, and the river's testimony counted.

Imani Reyes had gone back to the capital with a crate of data and a subpoena list as long as her arm. She'd hugged Coral hard at the rail station and said, "You know they'll want you to testify. Months of it. Rooms with bad lighting." And Coral had said, "I've dived worse," and Imani had laughed until she cried, and then just cried a little, and that had been right too.

Now there was only the compass.

---

Sela found her at the end of the dock, past the last piling, where the boards gave way to gravel and the gravel gave way to water. She came quietly — she always did, a habit from years of being a woman whose name was on lists — but Coral heard her anyway. You learned a person's footsteps the way you learned a river's currents: not by studying them, but by living inside them long enough.

"He's been staring at you all morning," Sela said, nodding at the compass.

"He's been staring at everything all morning." Coral turned it over in her hand. Her father's initials were worn nearly smooth on the case back. The glass had a hairline crack across the upper quadrant that had been there longer than Coral had been alive. "He pointed wrong for forty years, Sela. Upstream. Every day of my childhood, every dive, every crossing — upstream, toward the source, toward—" She stopped. Toward the containment grid. Toward the graves. Toward the waterproof testimony sealed in steel that she had carried out of the dark in both hands while the black water bloomed behind her.

"Toward the truth," Sela said. "He just didn't live long enough to deliver it. Neither did Mercer. You did." She crouched beside Coral, sleeves pushed up, forearms freckled from the weeks in the canyon. In her hand she carried a small cloth roll, and she unrolled it on the gravel with the care of someone laying out instruments: a jeweler's loupe, a fine screwdriver, a magnetized calibration weight, a strip of soft chamois. "So. Do we fix him, or do we let him keep pointing wherever grief points?"

Coral looked at her. "You've been thinking about this."

"Since the cavern." Sela's voice was steady, but her thumb moved once across the chamois, back and forth, the only tell she had. "A compass is a simple thing, Coral. A needle, a pivot, a field. It doesn't choose north. It just stops lying about where north is. Everything else — the drift, the pull, the wandering — that's damage. Interference. Something near it that shouldn't be near it." She met Coral's eyes. "People are the same way. I spent six years drifting after the exile, and everyone assumed I'd lost my bearing. I hadn't. There was just a mountain of iron sitting between me and it."

"And now?"

"Now the mountain has a hole blown in it and journalists crawling through it." A small, dry smile. "So. Let's see what your father does when we take the interference away."

They worked together on the end of the dock, in the sun, with the boat-sounds behind them. Sela opened the case — the hinge gave with a crack like a knuckle — and lifted the dial assembly free, and Coral held the loupe while Sela examined the pivot under magnification. Inside, the mechanism was beautiful and old-fashioned: a jeweled bearing, a card of paper-thin mica, the needle itself a sliver of magnetized steel no heavier than a thought.

"There," Sela said softly. "Look."

Coral looked. Wedged against the underside of the card, so small she'd have missed it if Sela's fingers hadn't coaxed it free, was a sliver of iron — a filing, a fragment, maybe a shaving from a drill bit, lodged there decades ago. It clung to Sela's forceps with a faint, stubborn grip.

"That's it?" Coral said. "Forty years of pointing upstream, and it's a speck of metal?"

"It's never just a speck of metal." Sela held it up to the light between finger and thumb. "Someone put this here. Or something did. Iron filings migrate — vibration, water, time. But this one's wedged. Deliberately." She was quiet a moment. "Your father dove navy wrecks. Navy wrecks are full of shavings. He might have picked it up in a hold somewhere and never known. Or—" she turned the fragment over, "—someone who didn't want him finding the source put it there, and he found it anyway, because he found everything anyway, and then he kept the compass because the flaw had become the message."

Coral thought about that. About a man diving forbidden water with a broken compass that pointed at the one place he wasn't allowed to go, and choosing, somehow, to keep it. To carry the wrongness like a lantern.

"Take it out," she said.

"You're sure? Once it's true, it's true forever. No more mystery in it."

"Sela." Coral took her hand — the one holding the forceps — and lowered it gently until the iron sliver rested on the chamois, and then she kept the hand. "I have had enough mystery to last the rest of my life. What I want now is direction."

Sela looked at her for a long moment, the sun full on her face, and whatever passed between them needed no radio channel and no broadcast tower. Then she drew the sliver away, blew the housing clean, and seated the card back on its jewel with the tenderness of a surgeon closing.

She set the compass flat on the dock boards between them.

The needle swung — one wide arc, one smaller, a tremble — and settled.

South. Downriver. Steady as a heartbeat, steady as the current sliding past the pilings, the needle laying itself along the river's spine toward Rivergate, toward the bend, toward the village that had survived the flood because Junie had run ahead of it screaming warnings through the night. It did not waver. It did not wander. It pointed home with the dumb, perfect honesty of a thing that had finally been allowed to speak.

Coral found she couldn't say anything at all.

"There," Sela said quietly. "True north. Well — true *south*, in his case. Your father would have appreciated the contrariness."

Coral laughed, and it came out wet, and she didn't bother hiding it. She picked up the compass and held it level and walked it slowly around in a circle, watching the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, each time finding the same line. Downriver. Home. Forty years of pointing at a grave, and now pointing at a life.

Junie appeared at the rail of the boat, saw them, and cupped her hands. "Is it fixed? Did you fix it?"

"It's fixed," Coral called back.

"Does it point at treasure?"

"It points at the dock, which currently contains zero treasure."

"Then it's broken," Junie said cheerfully, and went back to her painting.

Tam leaned out of the wheelhouse window, wiping grease off his hands with a rag that was mostly grease itself. "Voss. When you're done having feelings on my dock — the survey contracts came through. Three villages, two mapping runs, one wreck assessment for the historical society. All legitimate. All boring. All paid in advance." He paused, and his voice lost its bark. "Mercer would've hated how clean the paperwork is."

"No he wouldn't," Coral said. "He'd have read every line twice and then taken the job."

Tam grinned and disappeared back into the engine.

---

They stayed on the dock until the light went long and amber. Sela sat with her shoulder against Coral's shoulder, and the compass lay open on Coral's knee, and neither of them moved much. Below them the river ran green and strong, carrying the season downstream, carrying nothing hidden anymore. Upstream, days away, the source cavern stood open to investigators and daylight, its containment structures cut apart section by section, its graves marked with real names at last — Odile Ferrant, Basilio Reyes, and eleven others, and beneath the newest marker, the one the villagers had carved themselves, the name of a supply-boat pilot who had run the truth downriver at the cost of everything except the truth.

"I keep waiting for it to feel finished," Coral said. "The whole thing. Like a dive where you surface and break the water and it's done. It doesn't feel done. It feels like — the first stroke of a long crossing."

"That's because it is one." Sela laced her fingers through Coral's. "Finished is for cargo. We're not cargo."

"No," Coral agreed. "We're crew."

The compass ticked faintly against her knee as the dock shifted with the current — the needle trembling, settling, trembling, settling — and always coming back to the same heading. South. Downstream. Toward the town where the lamps were coming on one by one along the waterfront, toward the boat with fresh green paint, toward the work that was honest and the people who were hers.

She was still looking at it when Junie's voice came down from the wheelhouse again, and this time there was no laughter in it.

"Coral. Radio's picking something up. Channel sixteen." A pause. "It's the navy band. And they're asking for you by name."

Coral closed the compass over the needle pointing home, and stood up.
