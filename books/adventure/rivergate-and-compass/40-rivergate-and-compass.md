## 40. Rivergate and Compass

The river ran low and clear in the autumn light, the way it always did now that the season had a name again — not just dry season, but *the* season, the one the villages marked on their own calendars, the one they taught their children to read by the color of the water. Coral Voss stood at the rail of the *Mercer's Wake* and watched the current carry leaves past the hull, each one a small gold boat going somewhere she didn't have to follow.

Three years. Long enough for the scar tissue on her palm to fade from angry pink to pale silver. Long enough for the containment breach site upstream to grow its first stubborn fringe of reeds along the banks where the flood had scoured everything bare. Long enough for the word *Rivergate* to mean something other than debt.

"Captain." Junie's voice came up from the deck below, warm with the particular amusement she reserved for catching Coral thinking too hard. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The staring-at-the-water-like-it-owes-you-money thing. Except now it doesn't, so I don't know what we call it."

Coral laughed despite herself. Below, Junie was coiling line with the quick economy of someone who'd grown two inches and four years of competence since that first terrified morning when the survey team's tents stood empty on the bank. She was nineteen now, or claimed to be — she'd never produced papers and nobody pressed — and she ran the forward deck like she'd been born to it. Which, Coral supposed, she had. River children all were. They'd just needed someone to give them back their river.

"Where's Tam?" Coral called down.

"Engine room. Where Tam is always. He says if you ask him to come look at the water one more time he's going to install a periscope so you can do it from the galley."

"Tell him the starboard intake is making a sound."

"It's always making a sound. That's how you know it's working."

* * *

They were running a survey run for the village at Second Bend — depth soundings for a new ferry crossing, paid for in smoked fish, mended nets, and the kind of gratitude that didn't come with liens attached. It was honest work, slow work, work that would never make any of them rich, and Coral had learned that this was not the same as being poor. Poor was a condition. Broke was an arithmetic. What they had now was neither. It was something without a word in the salvage trade, because the salvage trade had no use for it: enough.

She found Tam in the engine room, of course, lying on his back under the port engine with a wrench across his chest like a knight's effigy, grease to his elbows and a headlamp throwing his shadow huge against the bulkhead.

"The intake," Coral said.

"I heard it. It's fine."

"You haven't looked."

"I don't have to look. I built that intake out of three wrecks and a prayer, and the prayer is holding." He slid out, sat up, pushed his goggles onto his forehead, and looked at her with the expression he saved for moments when engineering failed him and he had to fall back on being a person. "You're diving today."

It wasn't a question. Word traveled fast on a boat forty meters long.

"Yeah," Coral said. "Today."

Tam nodded slowly. He didn't say *it's been three years, why now*, and he didn't say *you said last time was the last time*, because Tam understood machines and grief well enough to know both ran on their own schedules and neither responded well to being rushed. Instead he said, "I'll check your regs myself. And I'm putting a second tank on the line whether you want it or not."

"Deal."

"And Coral?" He stood, wiped his hands on a rag that was already beyond saving. "He'd be proud of you. Your dad. If you were waiting for me to say it out loud sometime when it wasn't a funeral or a flood — there. Said."

Coral's throat did something complicated. "Grease monkey," she managed.

"Debt-ridden captain," he said fondly, and went back under his engine.

* * *

The wreck sat in thirty-one meters of green-brown water, six kilometers downstream of Rivergate, exactly where it had always been, exactly where the navy had spent forty years insisting it wasn't. The old transport lay on its port side in the silt of the riverbed, its hull furred now with zebra mussels and trailing curtains of milfoil, its superstructure softened by seasons into something less like a ship than a reef. Fish moved through the open cargo doors in slow silver schools. A snapping turtle the size of a wagon wheel regarded Coral from the collapsed bridge wing with ancient indifference.

She'd named the site herself, quietly, on the charts the crew kept for the villages: *Voss Shoal*. Not after her. After the case she'd pulled from it, the first domino, the sealed box of hydrographic charts and a photograph of five young divers grinning on a rail very much like this one. The name had stuck. Divers from three villages used it now. Somewhere above, on paper she'd never see, some government cartographer had probably inked it into the official record too, which struck Coral as the funniest and most fitting revenge imaginable: the cover-up's own maps, amended in her family's handwriting.

Her father had dove this wreck. She knew that now the way she knew the shape of her own hands — through Mercer's logbook, through Sela's archives, through the testimony she'd broadcast over every working radio channel on a night when the cathedral cavern roared around them. He'd come here hunting the same truth she had, years before she was born into the hunt, and the navy's silence had swallowed him whole. His dive tags hung on a hook in the *Wake*'s galley, next to Mercer's, next to a photograph of the crew of the *Wake* taken the day the source became a protected site — everyone squinting into the sun, Junie mid-laugh, Sela's hand on Coral's shoulder, Reyes holding up a water sample like a trophy.

She swam the wreck slowly. There was no hurry. That was the luxury of this dive, the one she'd promised herself: no contract clock, no creditor's arithmetic, no patrol skiff on the night water. Just the river, holding her, the way it held everything eventually.

At the cargo hold she paused. The space where the sealed case had rested was a shallow depression in the silt, long since filled and refilled by seasons of sediment. She'd returned the case itself years ago — its contents copied, catalogued, entered into evidence, entered into history. But her body remembered the weight of it. Forty kilos of deliberate burial. Forty kilos of somebody's decision that a river could be owned.

Not anymore. The source was a protected site now, co-managed by the village federation and a hydrological trust that Sela directed with the terrifying efficiency of a woman who'd been exiled once and intended never to be exiled again. The aquifer remediation was in its second year. The black water that had bloomed into the clear source the day Tam blew the grid had faded to a stain, then a memory, then a monitoring station with a solar panel and a plaque. Children from the Bend came up to see it on school trips. They touched the rusted containment structures the way kids touch dinosaur bones — with fascination untangled from fear, because fear required having lived with the secret, and the secret belonged to the dead now.

Coral checked her gauge. Fourteen minutes. Plenty.

She rose along the starboard rail, hand trailing over the metal, and stopped where the rail stood proudest of the silt, near the bow, where the current ran cleanest. This was the spot. In the photograph — her father's photograph, five divers on a rail — the man on the far left leaned against a stanchion almost exactly like this one, laughing at something outside the frame. She liked to think it was Mercer who'd made him laugh. Mercer had that effect on people right up until the morning he didn't, until the narrows, until the bullet, until the logbook pressed into her hands with fingers already going cold: *Make the river tell the truth, girl. It's the only witness that never lies.*

The river had told it. All of it. And the world had done what worlds do with truths — absorbed them unevenly, punished some guilty men and promoted others, rebuilt what it could, forgotten what it preferred to. Commander Ashe had surrendered his records rather than drown alone in the lie; he'd testified for eleven days, gone to prison for four, and now lived, according to Reyes, in a town two hundred kilometers downstream, volunteering with a flood-warning program. Coral had decided a long time ago that she wasn't going to have feelings about that. Mostly she succeeded.

From the mesh bag at her hip she brought out the compass.

Her father's compass. Brass-cased, glass scratched, the needle housing clouded with age. For thirty years it had pointed wrong — drifting upstream, toward the source, toward the truth, no matter how she held it, a dead man's finger crooked toward the thing that killed him. Then in the canyon slot it had steadied, and pointed not north but home, to the dive site he'd marked, and Coral had finally understood that it had never been broken. It had been *pointing*. Every wrong reading was a correct one, if you understood what it was measuring. Not magnetic north. The weight of what waited upstream.

Sela had helped her recalibrate it, two winters ago, at the galley table, with the patience of a scientist and the tenderness of — of whatever Sela was, which had stopped needing a noun somewhere in the second year and simply became the weather of Coral's life. They'd cleaned the pivot, replaced the damping fluid, trued the card. And the compass had settled into ordinary, obedient northward honesty, like a watchman relieved of duty after the war.

Except it hadn't stayed ordinary. On quiet nights, moored at the Bend, Coral would catch it drifting again — a degree, two degrees, always upstream, always toward the source. Sela insisted it was residual magnetization in the case, a flaw in the metal, nothing more. Coral had stopped arguing. Some instruments measured magnetism. Her father's measured unfinished business, and the business had been finished, and still it drifted, and she had come to believe it drifted the way old dogs walk the fence line: out of habit, out of love, checking on the thing it had guarded.

Today she was going to let it rest.

She turned the compass in her gloved hands one last time. Through the scratched glass the needle swung, wavered, and settled — pointing, unmistakably, upstream. Toward the source. Toward the cathedral cavern where the river was born and where her father's waterproof testimony had waited thirty years in the dark for a daughter he'd never met to read it aloud.

"I got there," she said into the regulator's bubble-hush, knowing the words wouldn't carry anywhere but inward. "Took me long enough. You'd have teased me about that."

The needle trembled, held.

She wedged the compass into the junction of the rail and the stanchion, seating it firmly against the current, facing upstream — the way a sentry faces, the way a helmsman faces, the way you face the thing you're watching over rather than the thing behind you. From here it would look upriver forever, toward the place the truth came from, keeping its post on a wreck the navy had sworn didn't exist, on a river that now belonged to the people who drank it.

She let go.

The compass sat solid in the current, brass catching the filtered light, and for a moment — Coral would swear this later, and Sela would smile the way she smiled at things she attributed to science — the needle swung gently off upstream-north and pointed, briefly, directly at Coral's chest. Then it settled back, contented, and the milfoil swayed, and the fish closed over the spot like water closing over a stone.

Coral hung in the green light a moment longer, breathing, letting the river take her weight. Grief didn't leave, she'd learned. It just changed vessels. It had started as a hole in her shaped like a father, then a hole shaped like Mercer, then a hole shaped like the money and the life she'd lost — and somewhere in the flooding and the aftermath and the long slow work of rebuilding, the holes had filled with something else. Not replacement. Ballast. The kind that keeps a boat upright in weather.

She kicked for the surface, and the light opened above her like a door.

* * *

Junie hauled her ladder up and wrapped her in a towel before Coral's feet were fully on deck, the way she'd done since Coral was half-drowned and shaking on a ledge shared with enemy soldiers, and had never quite stopped doing.

"Well?" Junie demanded. "Did you do it? Is it done?"

"It's done."

"Facing upstream?"

"Facing upstream."

Junie nodded with tremendous gravity, then ruined it by grinning. "Good. Because Tam says lunch is ready and he made too much again, and Sela radioed from the Bend — the ferry crossing soundings are approved, they want us back for the dedication next month, and Dr. Reyes says the aquifer numbers are the best she's seen since monitoring started. Also there's pie. Riverberry. Don't ask whose tree."

Coral toweled her hair and looked downriver. Rivergate sat in the middle distance, its docks rebuilt, its cranes busy with honest cargo, its name no longer a shorthand for what had been buried there. Behind her, upstream, the river ran clear toward the source, toward the protected dark where the water began, watched over by a brass compass on a sunken rail.

The debts were gone. The salvage was gone. The wreck would keep.

"Full ahead," she said. "Let's go home."

And the *Mercer's Wake*, true to her name and her crew, turned her bow downriver and ran with the current — carrying survey gear, smoked fish, a tin of riverberry pie, and four people who had once been broke together and had never since been poor — toward Rivergate, toward the bend, toward the ordinary miraculous future, while behind them the old transport slept in the silt with its new sentinel on the rail, pointing faithfully upstream, at peace at last with the direction of the truth.
