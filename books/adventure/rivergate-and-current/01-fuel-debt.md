## 1. Fuel Debt

The de Havilland Beaver came off the water at Moose Narrows like an old dog hauling itself out of a pond—reluctant, heavy, and making noises it had no business making.

Astrid Ravensworth held her breath through the whole takeoff run, the way she always did now, counting the seconds until the hull stopped slapping and the floats bit air. Four seconds. Five. The narrows unspooled beneath her, brown water flecked with the first scum of spring, and the engine note climbed from a cough to a growl to something almost like trust. Six seconds. Airborne.

"Come on, girl," she murmured, tapping the gauge cluster with one knuckle, a habit she'd told herself a hundred times to quit. "Come on."

The fuel gauge for the port tank read a hair past a quarter. She knew it was lying. She'd known since Tuesday, when she'd tapped the sender unit and it had given up a dribble of watered avgas and the truth, which was that she was flying on arithmetic and prayer. The starboard gauge was honest but cruel. Together they told the same story her bank manager had been telling her since November: there wasn't enough, and what there was wouldn't last.

She climbed out of the narrows on a heading that wasn't really a heading, just north and a little west, toward the smoke of Current, and let the plane settle into the rhythm that had become her whole life—engine, water, debt, engine, water, debt.

Two hundred and eleven days since the license suspension. Not that she was counting. She was counting. She kept the letter folded in the chart case behind her seat, soft as cloth now from handling, Transport Canada's letterhead gone grey at the creases. *Pending review of the incident of 14 August.* The incident. As if the mountain had filed the complaint. As if the weather that had come down off the divide like a dropped ceiling hadn't been the worst she'd seen in nine years of bush flying, and as if the man who'd told her to push through it anyway—her charter client, her *only* charter client that week—hadn't been the first one to step off the wreck and start talking to the investigators about pilot error.

She'd walked away from that one. Her passenger hadn't. But the inquiry had taken its time about the distinction, and in the meantime the bills hadn't.

The fuel account at Current Air Services was frozen—no, that wasn't right, it was *open*, technically, which was worse, because every drum she bought went on the ledger, and the ledger went to a man in Vancouver named Hargreaves, and Hargreaves held the paper on the Beaver. Fourteen thousand dollars outstanding. She'd stopped opening his letters. They had a shape to them now, those envelopes, a weight, and she could read her future through the paper without the trouble of the words: *default, repossession, sale of assets.* Her assets. Her one asset, sitting under her in the morning light, carrying her north on fumes and stubbornness.

Below, the river bent east around a bluff of black spruce, and there it was—the mine road, or what passed for one, a scar of orange gravel cut through the trees, and the first buildings of the Rivergate operation. She'd been avoiding that stretch of river all spring. Today she couldn't.

The radio crackled. "Beaver on the narrows, this is Rivergate camp, do you copy?"

She knew the voice. Everyone in the territory knew the voice, or would soon enough. Corliss. Rivergate Mining's man for everything that wasn't in the brochures. She'd met him twice, both times in the Current hotel dining room, both times over coffee she hadn't drunk because her hands had been unsteady and she hadn't wanted him to see.

"Beaver copies," she said. "Go ahead."

"Ms. Ravensworth. You're a hard woman to raise on the radio." A pause, pleasant as a held door. "We've got freight for the upper camp. Drums, mostly. Some crates. Weather window looks like it closes tomorrow. Can you lift it?"

She looked at the fuel gauge. A quarter. She looked at the river, at the orange road, at the camp buildings with their new tin roofs catching the sun.

"How many drums?" she asked, and hated herself for how fast she asked it.

"Six. Plus crates, maybe four hundred pounds all in. We'd want it at the upper site by dark." Another pause. "Name your figure."

She named a figure that made her ears hot. He didn't haggle. That was the part she'd remember later, lying awake in the bunk at Current—the way he hadn't haggled, hadn't even breathed at the number, the way the silence on the line had lasted one beat too long, like a man letting a fish run before setting the hook.

"Upper camp by dark," she said. "I'll need fuel on your pump before I lift the load."

"Fuel's on us," Corliss said. "Consider it a signing bonus."

The line went quiet in that way radios do, and Astrid flew north with the taste of the word in her mouth. *Signing bonus.* Like she'd signed something. Like there was a paper somewhere already with her name on it, waiting for her at the camp office.

* * *

Rivergate's lower camp sat on a gravel flat where the river widened, and it had the look of a place built in a hurry by men who expected to be gone before anyone asked questions. Two Quonset huts, a fuel farm of maybe twenty drums, a dock of fresh-cut lumber already greying, and a D4 dozer parked with its blade half-raised, like a dog interrupted mid-dig. The whole thing smelled of diesel and cut spruce and money moving too fast.

Astrid set the Beaver down on the flat water below the dock with the kind of landing she could have done asleep—glassy, no wind, floats kissing—and taxied in feeling the old pride come up in her chest and the new shame arrive right behind it, the way they always did now, travelling together like a married couple who'd stopped speaking.

Three men unloaded her while she stood at the pump and watched the fuel drum drain into her tanks, doing the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing. Full tanks. Six drums of freight, four hundred pounds of crates. Fuel to the upper camp, forty minutes north, and back. She could make it. She could make it and still have enough to reach Current and maybe, if the wind was kind and the engine was merciful, enough to do one more charter this week before the ledger caught up with her again.

"You're Ravensworth." One of the men had come over. Not Corliss—she'd have known Corliss's voice anywhere now, that pleasant held-door voice—but one of the hired hands, a big man with a sunburn peeling off his nose and the flat, incurious eyes of a man paid not to be curious. "Corliss says you take the freight straight up. No stops."

"No stops," she agreed. "That's the plan."

"He says some of the crates are survey equipment. Sensitive." The man shrugged, a big slow shrug. "He says don't open them, don't drop them, don't ask."

"I fly freight," Astrid said. "I don't read it."

Something moved through the man's face—not a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might have gone on a different man. "Corliss said you'd say that. He said, *tell her she's the first pilot this season who didn't ask what's in the crates.*"

"Tell him it's not a compliment."

The man laughed, one short bark, and went back to the drums. Astrid finished fuelling, capped the tanks, and walked the dock to look at her load. Six drums, stencilled RIVERGATE MINERALS LTD., lashed down in the hull. Four wooden crates, heavier than survey equipment had any right to be, roped and tarped. She put her palm flat on the nearest one, just for a second. It was cold through the tarp, colder than the morning, and she felt—she was almost sure she felt—a faint shifting inside, like sand settling.

She took her hand away.

*Don't ask,* she told herself, and the words had a ring of the litany to them, the prayer she'd been saying all winter. *You'll fly anything except questions. That's the deal. That's how you keep the airplane. That's how you keep flying.*

Because that was the truth under everything, the truth she kept in the same pocket as the folded suspension letter: she was not a good person doing a bad thing. She was a desperate person doing an ordinary thing. Bush pilots hauled freight for mining companies the way rivers hauled silt. It was the oldest trade in the north. Nobody asked the river what was in the silt.

She climbed back into the cockpit, ran the checks, and lifted off the flat with six drums and four cold crates and a full tank of Rivergate's fuel, and if there was a hook in that, she told herself, she hadn't felt it set.

* * *

The upper camp was forty minutes north through country that got wilder with every mile, the river narrowing, the hills closing in, the spruce running right down to the water like a crowd pressing toward a stage. Astrid had flown this stretch a dozen times in other years, for other outfits, back when the territory was just country and not a claim map. She knew the bend where the current split around the gravel bar. She knew the cliff with the peregrines. She knew, or she'd known, the little creek that came in from the west just past the narrows—she'd landed on it once, years ago, for a fly-fishing outfit out of Vancouver, and the water had been so clear you could count the stones on the bottom from two hundred feet.

She looked down for it now, out of habit, and it took her a moment to find it, because it wasn't clear anymore.

The creek ran orange.

Not muddy—orange. A bright, mineral, unnatural orange, like rust stirred into milk, and it spread out where it met the river in a fan of stained water that she could see from eight hundred feet, a wound in the brown current. Below the fan, on the gravel bars, something pale caught the sun. She was too high to make it out and too busy to circle, and she told herself it was rocks, bleached driftwood, the light doing what northern light does.

She flew on. But her eyes kept going back to the mirror, and the fan of orange stayed in it a long time, and the pale things on the bar stayed pale, and somewhere under the engine noise a voice she didn't want to hear started up, the way it always did, the voice of every question she'd spent all winter refusing to answer.

*What's in the crates, Astrid?*

*What's in the water?*

*What are you carrying, and for whom?*

The upper camp appeared around a bluff—a drill platform, a cookhouse, a scatter of tents and trailers, a helicopter pad with no helicopter. She brought the Beaver in over the trees, set it down on the wide brown water, and taxied to their dock while men came out of the cookhouse wiping their hands on their thighs, moving with the unhurried urgency of men who'd been told to expect her.

They unloaded fast. Too fast, she thought. The drums went one way, the crates another, and a foreman with a clipboard counted everything twice and signed her waybill without reading it, which she'd never once seen a mine foreman do in nine years of flying. He signed it the way you sign something you've been told to sign.

"Corliss says you're paid," the foreman said, folding the waybill into her hand along with a bank draft. "He says there's more work if you want it. Regular runs. Weekly."

"How regular?"

"Regular enough." He looked past her at the Beaver, at the floats, at the engine cowl with the patch on it she'd done herself in Current with a rivet gun and bad light. "He says a plane like that, a pilot like you—you could be flying for Rivergate full time by June. Steady pay. Fuel account. Debt cleared by fall, he says. He says a lot of things." The foreman shrugged. "I just fly the clipboard."

"You don't fly anything," Astrid said, "you count it."

"Same thing up here." He handed her the last of it, the bank draft, and she looked at the number and felt the ground shift under her the way it does in dreams, everything familiar suddenly tilted. It was three times her asking. Three times. She stood on the dock holding it, and the men went back to the cookhouse, and the Beaver rocked on the brown water beside her, and the orange creek came down out of the hills behind the camp and ran into the river a quarter mile south, and she could see it from the dock, the fan of stained water spreading, and the pale things on the bar.

She looked at the pale things for a long time.

Then she folded the bank draft into her breast pocket, where it sat against her heart like a second letter, and climbed into the cockpit, and flew south with the sun going down and the fuel gauges honest for once, both of them, because Rivergate's fuel was good fuel, and full tanks were full tanks, and a full tank is a kind of answer, and she had been living all winter on questions.

She was twenty minutes south of the camp, running the river in the long gold light, when she saw the smoke.

Not camp smoke—camp smoke rises straight and thin in the evening calm. This was a smudge, a dirty grey column, off the river to the west, in the country where there was nothing, where the maps showed nothing but the contour lines of the divide and the little orange creek and, if you knew where to look, the ghost of an old prospecting trail. A fire where no camp should be. A fire where no one should be.

Astrid flew on for another mile, because she was tired, and the draft was in her pocket, and the light was going, and every mile west was a mile of fuel she'd have to explain to a ledger.

Then she swore, banked the Beaver west, and climbed.

She told herself it was to have a look. Just a look. Just enough to say she'd checked, so that if some trapper's cabin was burning down out there and somebody's name ended up in the newspaper, she'd know she hadn't flown past it with full tanks and a clear sky.

She was three miles out, down to five hundred feet, the smoke rising ahead of her in the windless evening, when she saw the roof of the old camp through the trees—the Crane camp, the prospectors called it, though she'd never met the man—and beside the roof, in the trampled snow of the dooryard, a shape that was either a fallen log or a man, and did not move.

She put the Beaver down on the last open water below the camp with the light nearly gone, and the floats touched, and the hull came to rest, and the silence after the engine was the kind of silence that makes you listen.

The shape in the snow did not move.
