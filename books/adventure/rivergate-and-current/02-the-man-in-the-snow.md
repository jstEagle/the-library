## 2. The Man in the Snow

The weather turned ugly an hour north of the Kettle, the way it always did—no drama, no warning shot, just a gray wall sliding up the valley like something poured. Astrid watched it come through the windshield of the old Beaver and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since she was nineteen years old: fuel remaining, distance to Current, ceiling dropping, wind quartering out of the northwest at a speed that made the wings talk to her.

She could push it. Pilots pushed it all the time up here, and most of them got away with it, and the ones who didn't were mostly names on memorial cairns now.

"Come on," she told the airplane. "Not today."

The Beaver answered with a shudder through the airframe, ice starting to feather along the leading edges. That settled it. She'd flown this river so many times she knew its bends like the lines of her own palm, and three miles ahead, tucked into a cutbank below a ridge of black spruce, there was an abandoned survey camp—Rivergate's own, from the first season, before they'd moved operations downstream and left the buildings to rot. She'd ducked into worse places.

She dropped the flaps, killed her altitude in a long descending spiral, and put the plane down on the frozen slough beside the camp with more luck than skill, the skis hissing across drift-glazed ice until the tail swung around and she was stopped, engine ticking, alone in a silence so complete she could hear her own blood.

Astrid sat for a moment with her hands still on the yoke, letting the adrenaline drain out through her fingers. Then she looked at the fuel gauge, because looking at it had become a kind of prayer she didn't believe in. A quarter tank. Enough to get to Current if the wind behaved. Not enough to loiter.

*You'll fly anything except questions,* she reminded herself, and climbed out into the cold.

The camp was two tar-paper shacks and a collapsed cache stand, snow drifted against the walls in sculpted curves. One roof had partially failed; the other held. Smoke—thin, gray, wrong-smelling—rose from a stovepipe bent at a desperate angle.

Smoke meant someone.

Astrid stood in the prop wash fading behind her and felt the old wariness settle over her shoulders like a coat. In the interior, smoke could mean a trapper, a prospector, a fugitive, or a trap. She'd been burned before by people who needed things from pilots. People who needed things from pilots usually needed them badly, and badly-needing people didn't always ask nicely.

But it was twenty below and falling, and whoever was burning that wet, green wood was burning it because they had nothing else, and Astrid Ravensworth had spent enough winters hungry herself to know what that stovepipe smell meant.

She crossed the yard, boots crunching, and pushed open the door of the standing shack without knocking. Up here you knocked and nobody answered, or you knocked and somebody shot through the door. Better to be seen coming.

Inside, the light came through one cracked window in a pale blade, and in that blade of light, half on and half off a bunk built from shipping crates, lay an old man.

He was propped against the wall like something set there by hand rather than having lain down himself. His parka was good quality once, now greasy and patched with duct tape gone brittle. His beard was white and full of frost where his breath had frozen in it. His eyes were open, which surprised her—they were clear blue and furious, fixed on her face with an intensity that had nothing foggy about it, even as the rest of him shook with cold so deep it had gone past shivering into tremor.

"You're not Rivergate," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"No," Astrid said. "I'm not Rivergate."

"Then either you're lost or you're mine." He tried to laugh and it turned into a cough that bent him double, and when he straightened, there was blood on his lips, bright against the white beard, and his hand pressed against his side where the parka showed a dark stain the size of a dinner plate.

Astrid's stomach dropped. She'd seen gut-shot animals. She'd seen men hurt by machinery and weather and their own stubbornness. She knew what that stain meant, and she knew what the color of his lips meant, and neither of those things was anything she could fix with what was in her first-aid kit.

"What happened to you?" she asked, already moving, shrugging off her gloves, pulling the kit from her jacket.

"Horse fell on me." He said it with contempt, as if the horse had done it deliberately. "Three days ago. Down at the lower crossing. Dragged myself here after." He waved a shaking hand at the shack. "Knew this camp was here. Knew the stove worked. Figured I'd either mend or freeze, and I've been arguing with both ever since."

"Where's your plane? Your boat? Anyone?"

"Sold the boat in spring. Never had a plane. And anyone—" he smiled, and it was a terrible smile, all teeth and no warmth, "—anyone who'd come looking for Elias Crane is either dead or works for the company that wants him dead."

The name landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. *Crane.* She knew the name. Everyone on the river knew the name. Twenty years of staking claims, twenty years of showing up in Current every spring with ore samples and stories too big to swallow, twenty years of being the last man in the valley who still believed there was gold in the headwaters—and the last man who believed Rivergate Mining had come to take it honestly.

"I'm Astrid Ravensworth," she said. "I fly out of Current."

"I know who you are." His eyes narrowed, and for a moment the delirium she'd expected wasn't there at all—just a sharp, assessing intelligence, cataloguing her the way she'd catalogued his camp. "Ravensworth. You fly for wages now. Flew a load of drill casing up to Camp Nine last month. I watched you go over."

Something cold walked up Astrid's spine. Camp Nine was a Rivergate site, remote and quiet, the kind of charter that paid well and asked nothing. She hadn't known anyone was watching.

"I fly for whoever pays," she said evenly. "That's not a crime yet."

"It will be," Crane said, "before this is over. Help me sit up."

She helped him sit up, and the effort cost him—he went gray, and his breathing went shallow and fast, and she felt the heat coming off him even through her gloves. Fever. Blood loss. Three days on a dirt floor in an unheated shack. By any honest measure, Elias Crane should have been dead already, and the only thing keeping him on the far side of that line was whatever was burning inside him that had nothing to do with fever.

"I need to get you to Current," she said. "There's a nurse there. Marta—"

"Marta's my wife." Something moved across his face, quick as a fish under ice. "She doesn't know where I am. Nobody knows where I am. That was the point."

"Why? What are you running from?"

Crane looked at her for a long time. Outside, the wind found the loose corner of the tar paper and worried it, a slow tearing rhythm like something chewing. The blade of window-light had thickened, gone amber, then gray. The storm was settling in for the night.

"I'm dying," he said simply. "Horse saw to that. So the question isn't whether I get out of this valley alive. It's whether I get out at all, or whether Rivergate gets to write my ending for me." He lifted his chin toward a shelf above the bunk. "Hand me that."

On the shelf, wrapped in oilskin and tied with waxed cord, sat a package the size of a large book. Astrid took it down. It was heavier than it looked, dense, and something inside it shifted with a dry rustle.

"That's my life's work," Crane said. "That's the reason there are men on the river right now who'd pay you more than you owe to put me in the ground unburied."

Astrid's hands went still on the oilskin. "How do you know what I owe?"

"Current is a small town, girl. Debt travels faster than news." He coughed again, wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, studied the blood on it with clinical disinterest. "You think I don't know why you flew that casing to Camp Nine? A pilot with your hours doesn't haul freight for pennies unless the bank owns her wings. Rivergate holds your paper. Am I close?"

Astrid said nothing. The silence was answer enough, and Crane nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd already written down.

"So here's my proposition," he said. "And I'll only make it once, so listen. You fly me out of here. You get me to Current, to Marta, alive—or as close to alive as this body's going to let me be. You do that, and this map is yours. Free and clear. No debt attached. The only thing I ask is the one thing you're already going to do."

"And if I just take it?" Astrid heard herself say it, and hated how it sounded—how desperate it sounded—but she was tired and cold and the gauge in her head that measured her own decency had been running near empty for two years. "If I fly out tomorrow and leave you?"

"Then you'll be a thief," Crane said, "and a fool, because you'll have stolen from a dead man something he wanted to give you, and that kind of theft follows a person." His voice dropped, roughened. "Besides. You won't. I've been watching pilots on this river for thirty years. Most of them are broke and half of them are liars, but the ones who land in a storm to check a stranger's smoke—" he tapped his chest, twice, weakly, "—those ones are all right. You're all right, Ravensworth. God help us both."

Astrid looked down at the oilskin package in her hands. Through the window, the storm had erased the world entirely; the plane was a shadow among shadows on the slough, and the thought of taking off into that gray churn made her palms sweat even in the cold.

"I can't fly tonight," she said. "Weather's down. We'd both die on the ridges."

"Then we wait," Crane said. "I've waited three days. I can wait for morning." He paused, and for the first time something uncertain flickered across his face—the first honest fear she'd seen in him. "Assuming I'm still here in the morning."

Astrid looked at the man, at the bloodstain, at the frost in his beard, and felt the wary compassion in her chest tip over into something harder and more dangerous: responsibility. She'd promised herself, after the license trouble, after the creditors started circling, that she would never again be responsible for anything she couldn't carry out of a situation under her own power. No passengers. No partners. No promises.

"You need water," she said instead. "And food, if you have any. And I need to look at that side."

"There's beans in the tin by the stove. Water in the barrel, if it's not frozen through. And you can look all you like, but you can't fix it, so don't waste the bandages."

She fixed what she could anyway. The wound was as bad as she'd guessed—a deep crush injury, ribs likely broken, internal bleeding she couldn't see and couldn't stop. She cleaned it, packed it, gave him the last of her own painkillers from the kit, and heated beans and snow-water on the stove while the storm threw itself against the shack like something locked out.

They ate in silence. Or rather, she ate; Crane managed four spoonfuls and then rested, his breathing a wet, labored sound that she tried not to count the rhythm of, and failed.

Near midnight, with the fire burned down to coals and the wind screaming steadily now, he spoke again from the bunk, his voice thinner.

"Ravensworth. You want to know what's in the map?"

"I figured you'd tell me if I asked."

"It's not gold." A pause. The wind filled it. "Twenty-two years I chased gold in these hills. And last summer I found something better, or worse—depends on the day. I found where Rivergate buried its mistakes." Another pause, longer. "I photographed them. Documented them. Every poisoned creek, every falsified log, every drum of tailings they sunk in the muskeg and pretended never existed. There's a valley upriver, girl, where the water kills everything that drinks it, and Rivergate has spent two years making sure nobody asks why."

Astrid sat very still. The coals ticked. Somewhere in the walls, the tar paper tore free altogether and flapped loose.

"The company that holds my debt," she said quietly.

"The company that holds everyone's debt, from here to the coast. That's how they work. They don't need guns. They need ledgers." Crane's eyes found hers in the dark, and they were clear and terrible. "So. Still want the map?"

Astrid thought about the freight runs, the drill casing, the quiet sites. About Corliss—she'd met the fixer once, briefly, a handshake like a closed door. About the quarter tank of fuel and the storm and the widow named Marta waiting in Current who didn't know where her husband was.

She thought about flying home safe.

"Yes," she said.

Crane smiled, and this time it reached his eyes. "Good girl. Then here's your promise, and I'll hear you say it back: you fly me out of this valley alive. Whatever the weather does, whatever the company does, whatever you find when you open that oilskin—you get me to Marta. Say it."

The wind hammered the shack. The coals settled. Astrid Ravensworth, who had sworn off promises the way other people swore off drink, looked at a dying man in a blade of moonlight and heard herself speak clearly, without hesitation, as if some part of her had already decided days ago and had only been waiting for her to catch up.

"I promise," she said. "I'll fly you out alive."

"Then we're partners," Elias Crane said, and closed his eyes, and slept—for a little while—with the map between them on the crate-table, wrapped in oilskin, patient as a loaded gun.

Outside, the storm raged on, and somewhere south of it, in the warm offices of Rivergate Mining, men who did not yet know their secret was walking were already beginning to wonder where Elias Crane had gone.

* * *

Dawn came late and grudging, the sky a bruised pearl with the storm's tail still dragging east. Astrid was awake before it, having slept maybe two hours in the chair by the stove, and the first thing she did was check Crane's breathing.

It was still there. Thinner. Wet. But there.

She dug the plane out of its drift, swept the wings, checked the control surfaces for ice with numb fingers, and calculated—again, and again—the weight and balance of a quarter tank, a headwind, and a passenger who might die at altitude. If he died in the air, she decided, she'd put down on the river wherever the river allowed it and let the chips fall. She would not break the promise by arithmetic.

By the time the light was strong enough to fly legal—if she'd cared about legal, which these days was a flexible word—Crane was conscious, gray-faced, and furious about being alive.

"Took you long enough," he rasped, as she bundled him into the passenger seat with blankets and the last of the dignity he had left. "In my day, pilots were punctual."

"In your day, planes had two wings and a propeller you spun by hand."

"They did, and we liked it." He gripped her wrist as she buckled the harness, sudden strength in the grip, sudden clarity in the eyes. "Map goes in your jacket. Not the baggage. Your jacket. If we go down, it goes down with you, and if we don't, you keep it where your heart is until you understand what you're carrying."

Astrid slid the oilskin package inside her flight jacket, against her chest. It was warm from the shack, oddly heavy, and it beat against her ribs with each heartbeat like a second pulse.

She climbed in, primed the engine, and took the Beaver off the slough into a sky the color of dirty pewter, climbing hard to clear the ridge, turning south toward Current with a dying man beside her and a buried secret against her heart—and forty miles out, on the river bend below, she caught the glint of something that made her jaw tighten: a boat, moving fast upstream, riding low in the water, too many men aboard for fishing.

Crane followed her gaze down, and laughed once, a dry, bloody sound.

"Well," he said. "They found my horse."
