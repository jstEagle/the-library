## 3. A Promise Made at Altitude

The storm built itself out of the western horizon like something being assembled by a spiteful god—first a bruise of cloud over the divide, then a gray wall, then the anvil-topped thunderhead that made Astrid's hands go tight on the yoke.

"Come on," she muttered to the sky. "Not now."

The de Havilland Beaver answered with its usual indifference, droning north on one good engine and a prayer she hadn't paid for. Behind her, in the passenger seat Elias Crane had occupied for barely forty minutes, the old man was dying, and he was doing it loudly—not screaming, but breathing, each breath a wet tearing sound like canvas ripping slow.

"You still with me, Crane?"

A pause. Then, faint: "Where's that."

"Fifty miles south of Current. Storm's coming down the valley behind us." She glanced at the mirror. His face had gone the color of tallow, blood seeping through the field dressing she'd strapped across his belly back at the camp—a gash from a fall, he'd said, though the way he'd flinched when she touched his ribs suggested the fall had company. "I can't outrun it. I'm going to have to go through the edge of it."

"Then go through." A ghost of a smile. "You're the pilot."

"I'm the *pilot*," she agreed, and pushed the throttle forward until the old engine grumbled its objection.

She'd made him a promise an hour ago, kneeling in the snow outside a camp that had been abandoned so long the stovepipe had rusted to lace: *I'll get you out alive.* He'd pressed the oilskin-wrapped map into her hands as collateral, as if she'd needed convincing, as if she were the kind of woman who haggled with a freezing man. She'd taken it anyway. Pride, maybe. Or the animal instinct to hold something valuable when everything else in your life is held by someone else—by creditors, by Rivergate Mining, by a license suspension that hung over her like weather of its own making.

Now the map rode in her jacket pocket against her ribs, and Crane rode behind her, bleeding into the seat cushions, and the storm came down the valley like a debt collector.

*Everything comes due,* she thought. *Everything.*

* * *

The radio had been dead since takeoff—static, then nothing, then static again, the set sulking the way it always did in cold. So there would be no one at Current expecting them, no doctor waiting on the dock, no ambulance idling at the strip. There would be only the settlement itself, small and gray and huddled at the river bend, and whatever luck Astrid could land on.

"Crane. Talk to me. Keep talking."

"What do you want me to say."

"I don't know. Anything. Where you from, before all this."

A long silence, filled with wind and engine and the wet rasp of his breathing. Then: "Saskatchewan. Wheat farm. Hated it." Another breath, worse than the last. "Came north in 'sixty-one. Thought I'd strike it rich." A cough that sounded like it cost him a year. "Forty years chasing gold, Miss Ravensworth. Forty years."

"And did you?"

"Did I what."

"Strike it rich."

He laughed then, or tried to—it turned into a wet rattle halfway up. "Found something better. Found something they'll kill for." His hand found the back of her seat, gripped it. "That map. You looked at it yet?"

"Looked at the first marker. Creek fork, burned spruce, some coordinates I don't recognize."

"That's not where the gold is." His voice dropped, went strange and urgent, the voice of a man spending his last coins. "There's no gold on that map, girl. Never was."

Astrid kept her eyes on the wall of cloud ahead, but her stomach did something complicated. "Then what am I flying you out for?"

"The truth." The word came out cracked. "Rivergate didn't just poison that valley. They buried it. Buried the proof. Buried—" A breath. "Buried men. My partner. Tom Wexler. He took samples off their drill sites. Water, soil, tissue from the game. Sent them to a lab in Whitehorse under a false name because he knew what would happen if they found out." The grip on her seat tightened until she could feel it through the frame. "They found out."

The cloud wall was close now, close enough that she could see it boiling, see the rain curtains hanging beneath it like dirty veils. She banked slightly, hunting for the seam, the gap every storm has if you're desperate enough to look for it.

"Crane—"

"Elias. My name is Elias."

"Elias. Save it. Save your breath. We'll be on the ground in twenty minutes and you can tell it all again to somebody who writes things down."

"There won't be time." Flat certainty. "I know how this goes. I've watched it go this way before." He shifted, and the pain must have been enormous, because he made a sound low in his throat and stopped moving. "Listen to me. The map shows three markers. The creek fork. The old Camp Nine drill site. And the third—" another breath, ragged, "—the third is where they put what wouldn't burn. Drums. Documents. Tom. Everything that could hang them, sunk in the muskeg under two feet of fill dirt, upstream of every trapline and every fish run in that valley. They poisoned the water and then they buried the evidence that they poisoned the water, and they've been paying people to look away ever since."

"Like who?"

"Like anyone who owes them." A pause, and she felt it land between them like a dropped tool. "Like a bush pilot with debts."

Astrid said nothing. The storm filled the silence for her.

"I read about you," Crane went on, gentler now, which was somehow worse. "Your name was on the charter manifest last summer. Ravensworth Air. Rivergate holds your paper. That's why they gave you my supply run—they wanted their own eyes on anything moving upriver." He coughed. "And instead they got me."

"They got a dying man who hijacked a charter flight."

"I paid for the fuel."

"With a map to no gold."

"With a map worth more than gold." His hand released her seat. When he spoke again his voice had gone thin, stretched, a wire about to part. "Promise me something else. Second promise."

"I already promised to fly you out alive."

"Not that." A breath. "If I don't make it—if I die before I can tell it myself—you take it to daylight. Not to the company. Not to the Mounties in Current, half of whom eat from Rivergate's hand. Daylight. Newspapers. Ottawa. A courtroom. Somewhere the light gets in." His fingers found her shoulder, light as ash. "Swear it, Astrid Ravensworth. Swear it on whatever you've got left that's yours."

Ahead, the storm's edge loomed, a mile of falling gray. She checked her heading, checked her altitude, checked the fuel gauge that always lied to her by a gallon and a half. And she realized, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she was going to swear it. That she'd already sworn it, somewhere inside herself, the moment an old man in the snow had wrapped her hands around an oilskin package and trusted her with the only thing he had left.

"I swear," she said. "On the plane. It's the only thing that's mine."

"It's enough," Elias Crane whispered. "It's more than most people ever have."

Then she flew into the storm.

* * *

It lasted eleven minutes and it was the worst eleven minutes of flying she had ever done, and she had once landed a Cessna on a gravel bar in a whiteout with a broken windshield and a passenger having a seizure.

The rain hit the windshield like thrown gravel. The updrafts and downdrafts fought over the plane like dogs over a bone—one moment she was shoved skyward, altimeter spinning, the next the bottom dropped out and the Beaver fell fifty feet with her stomach following after. Lightning walked along the ridge to the west, close enough to turn the world white, and thunder arrived not as sound but as pressure, a fist against the fuselage.

Behind her, impossibly, Crane was singing. Something old, something with wheat in it, a harvest song from a farm he'd hated. His voice rose and fell with the turbulence, thin and off-key and utterly unafraid, and Astrid found herself matching her breathing to it, flying the instrument of her own fear down to something manageable.

"Hold on, old man," she shouted over the roar. "Almost through."

He sang louder.

Then—the other side. It came all at once, the way storms end in mountains: one moment chaos, the next a world washed clean and gleaming, rain-shined river unspooling below her in a long silver S, and there, at the bend, the settlement of Current—forty roofs, a church steeple, the trading post, the dock reaching into the brown water like a finger pointing home.

"Current," she called back. "Current, Elias. We made it."

No answer.

"Crane?"

She twisted around in the harness. His head had fallen forward onto his chest. His chest was still moving—that terrible wet bellows—but his eyes were closed and his lips had gone blue-gray and the dressing on his belly was soaked through, dark as river mud.

"No. No, no, no. Stay with me. We're here. Look—" She hauled the plane into a descending turn over the town, banking hard enough to show him the whole of it, the rooftops, the dock, the handful of figures below who had begun, mercifully, to look up. "Look, there are people, there's help—"

His eyes opened. Just barely. But they found hers in the little mirror, and they were clear, clearer than they'd been since the snow camp, and he smiled.

"Told you," he breathed. "You're the pilot."

* * *

She put the Beaver down on the water in front of the dock with more care than she had ever put into any landing, grease-smooth, barely a kiss of spray, and taxied in while a knot of townspeople gathered—Marta Crane among them, Astrid realized with a lurch; the resemblance was there in the jaw, the iron-gray hair, and the way the woman started running the moment she understood what was in the plane.

By the time Astrid killed the engine and scrambled back to the cabin, Marta was at the door, and two men from the trading post were lifting Elias out, and the dock was chaos of coats and shouting and someone yelling for Doc Halloran, and none of it mattered, because Astrid could see it in the slack of his face, in the stillness of his hands.

But he waited. She would swear afterward that he waited—held on through the carrying, through the laying-down on the rough planks, through Marta gathering his head into her lap and saying his name over and over like a bell being struck. He waited until Astrid knelt beside him, until his hand found hers, cold as lake ice, and squeezed with the last strength he owned.

"Daylight," he said. Only that. One word, spent like a man spending his final coin.

Then Elias Crane died on the dock at Current, in his wife's lap, with the rain just beginning again, soft and steady, washing the blood from the boards into the river.

* * *

Afterward there was the awful administrative quiet of death in a small place. Someone covered him with a tarp. Someone else went for the constable. Marta did not cry, which frightened Astrid more than weeping would have; she simply sat on the dock beside her husband's body with her hand resting on his chest, looking north up the river toward the country he'd come out of, her face closed like a ledger.

Astrid stood at the rail of her plane, dripping, shaking now that the adrenaline had drained out and left the fear behind it. Her jacket pocket pressed against her ribs. The oilskin map. Three markers. A buried grave pit full of drums and documents and a man named Tom Wexler. A promise made at eight thousand feet in the teeth of a storm.

*Daylight,* he'd said.

She should give it to the widow. That was the obvious thing, the decent thing. It wasn't hers. None of it was hers—she was a charter pilot with a suspended license and a debt to the very company the map accused, a woman who'd spent three years learning to fly under the radar of her own conscience. She should hand the oilskin to Marta Crane, pour a drink, sleep twelve hours, and fly home tomorrow to face Corliss and the paperwork and the slow grinding machinery of Rivergate's patience.

Except.

Except she'd sworn. On the plane. On the only thing that was hers.

Across the dock, Marta Crane raised her head and looked at her—looked at her the way you look at a stranger who was present at the death of someone you love, weighing them, cataloguing them—and Astrid understood that the widow knew. Knew about the map, or suspected, or simply recognized the look of a person carrying something too heavy to set down.

Marta stood. Walked toward her, unhurried, boots loud on the planks.

"You," she said, stopping an arm's length away. Rain ran off the brim of her hat between them. "Elias sent word he might come out on a charter. He didn't say whose."

"Astrid Ravensworth. Ravensworth Air."

"Ravensworth." Something moved behind the widow's eyes—recognition, calculation, a door opening onto a room Astrid couldn't see. "Yes. I know the name." She glanced back at the tarp-covered shape on the dock, then at Astrid again, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Before the constable gets here, girl, I need to ask you one question, and I need you to answer it true."

Astrid's hand drifted, without her permission, to her jacket pocket.

Marta Crane watched the hand move, and nodded slowly, as if a long-feared account had finally come due.

"That's what I thought," she said. "We need to talk. Now—before the wrong people hear a plane landed today."
