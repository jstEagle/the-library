## 4. What the Widow Knows

Marta Crane had learned to measure grief in small units. Not the whole of it at once—that would drown you—but in pieces, the way a river takes a bank: a handful of soil this morning, a fence post by afternoon, and one day the whole field is gone and you can't say when it happened.

So she did not cry when Astrid Ravensworth came to her door on the third day after Elias died. She had done her crying at night, alone, into the crook of her elbow so the neighbors wouldn't hear. In daylight there was work to do, and Marta Crane was not a woman who let work wait on sorrow.

She opened the door before Astrid could knock twice. She'd seen the plane come down over the rooftops of Current that morning—heard it first, actually, that particular stuttering note the de Havilland made on approach, like a man clearing his throat before bad news—and she'd known who would be standing on her step within the hour.

"You're the pilot," Marta said.

"Astrid Ravensworth." The woman stood with her cap in her hands, which told Marta something right away. Bush pilots didn't take their caps off for anyone. "I flew your husband out of the interior. I'm—I'm sorry. He didn't make it."

"I know what happened to my husband." Marta stepped back from the door. "You'll come in. You'll have coffee. And then you'll tell me exactly how he died, because the company men who came yesterday said it was exposure, and company men lie the way other people breathe."

Astrid blinked. Whatever speech she'd prepared on the walk up from the dock, Marta had just burned it.

The house was small and clean and smelled of spruce smoke and canned tomatoes. On the wall above the stove hung a photograph of Elias thirty years younger, squinting into sun, a gold pan in his hands like an offering. Marta poured coffee without asking how Astrid took it—black, everyone took it black up here eventually—and sat across the table with her own cup untouched, the way she did when she meant business.

"Start at the camp," she said. "Where you found him."

Astrid told it straight, which Marta appreciated. The weather forcing her down. The abandoned trapper's cabin off Crane's old trapline. Elias half-frozen against the wall, delirious, clutching something wrapped in oilskin. How he'd pressed the map into her hands and made her promise—one promise, fly me out alive—and how she'd raced the storm downriver with him bleeding in the passenger seat, his pulse going thin as thread under her fingers.

"He talked," Astrid said. "Near the end. Mostly nonsense. But he kept saying—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "He said the map doesn't show where he found gold. It shows where Rivergate buried its dead."

Marta set her cup down. Her hand was steady. She'd practiced that too.

"Not dead people," she said. "Dead water."

* * *

For a long moment neither woman spoke. Outside, Current went about its morning—a dog barking at the floatplane dock, someone's generator coughing awake, the river muttering past the pilings the way it always had, gray-green and patient, carrying whatever the mountains gave it.

"Elias came home last spring talking strange," Marta said finally. "Not crazy-strange. Focused-strange. He'd taken a contract with Rivergate—survey work, water sampling, nothing glamorous, but it paid better than prospecting and his knees were shot. Six weeks up the valley at their Camp Nine. When he came back he wouldn't talk about the money. He'd sit at this table"—she touched the wood with two fingers—"and he'd say, Marta, the creek at the north fork runs orange. And I'd say, creeks run orange all through these ranges, it's iron, your father taught you that. And he'd say, not like this. Not with the fish floating belly-up for three miles downstream. Not with the moose refusing to drink."

"Did he have proof?"

Marta almost smiled. It wasn't a kind smile. "He had photographs. Dozens of them. He bought a camera in the spring—spent money we didn't have, which I yelled at him for, God forgive me. He said paper evidence rots and witnesses die, but a photograph is a fact you can hold. He photographed the orange creek. The fish kill. A drum yard behind Camp Nine, rusted-out barrels stenciled with Rivergate's own markings, half-buried where they thought nobody would look. He photographed the drill logs—the real ones, the ones they kept in a locked box and showed nobody—with numbers crossed out and written over. He said any chemist with a week and a conscience could prove what they'd done."

"And what had they done?"

"They hit something at Camp Nine in the fall before last. A seam, or a pocket—Elias never knew exactly, only that the water changed overnight and the company knew it changed. Instead of reporting it they capped the site, moved the operation, and let the creek carry it down through four miles of valley. There are families on that stretch. The Kowalski brothers' trapline. Old Sam Two-Rivers's fishing camp. People who've drunk that water their whole lives." Marta's voice stayed level, but her knuckles had gone white around the cup. "Rivergate's fixer came here in June. Polite young man, expensive coat. Asked if Elias had brought anything home from his contract. Papers, samples, photographs. I told him Elias was a prospector; prospectors bring home rocks. He smiled and left a card and said the company valued its relationship with the community." She looked up. "That's when I understood my husband hadn't taken a job. He'd declared a war. And he'd done it without telling me, because telling me would have put me in the line of fire, and he was old-fashioned that way."

Astrid was quiet. Then: "Why are you telling me this? You don't know me."

"No," Marta agreed. "I don't. I know you're in debt to Rivergate—half this town knows that, the way half this town knows everything. I know you fly charters for them when you can get them. And I know you could have thrown that oilskin package in the river and collected whatever finder's fee Corliss is offering, and instead you walked up my hill to my door with your cap in your hands." She rose, went to the bedroom, and came back with a tin biscuit box, dented at one corner, older than both of them. "Elias mailed these down over the summer. Letters. He wrote one every Sunday whether there was news or not. I want you to read them."

"Marta—"

"Read them." She set the box on the table between them like a weight on a scale. "Then decide what you are."

* * *

Astrid read.

Marta watched her do it—watched the pilot's face change letter by letter, the way weather changes over a ridge. The early letters were ordinary enough. Elias complaining about mosquitoes, praising the beans at Camp Nine, asking after the hens. Then July, and the handwriting tightening:

*Marta — saw something today I wish I hadn't. North fork running the color of rust and the trout floating like leaves. Took pictures. A company geologist named Voss looked at the water, looked at me, and said "natural mineralization" without meeting my eye. Forty years in the bush and I know natural mineralization. This is a wound.*

August:

*They've moved me off the north fork survey. Told me my contract covers the south drainage only. Funny—they assigned me north myself in June. Somebody upstairs got nervous. Found a drum cache behind the ridge, buried shallow, stencils still readable. Photographed it. Buried it again. My hands shook doing it. Not fear. Anger. At my age anger is exhausting.*

September, the letters shorter, harder:

*Voss is gone. Transferred, they say. His bunk was cleared out overnight. I asked Corliss's man where he went and got a smile. I'm mailing the best negatives to you separately, in with the maps. If anything happens to me, don't go to the company. Don't go to the territorial office either—half those men eat from Rivergate's hand. Find someone who owes them nothing and isn't afraid of them. I don't know who that is yet. Pray I find them before they find me.*

And the last letter, dated eleven days before he died:

*Marta, my love. If you're reading this I waited too long to come down. Don't grieve long. Grieve a week, then get angry. Everything I know is either in the tin or in the ground at Camp Nine. The truth is heavy but it floats—it'll come downriver someday no matter what they do. I only wanted to be holding it when it does. Kiss the hens. Yours entirely, E.*

Astrid closed the tin. Her eyes were wet, and she didn't wipe them, and Marta decided in that moment that she trusted her—not fully, trust was a thing built in weather, but enough.

"He wrote that last one like a will," Astrid said.

"It was a will. Of a sort." Marta sat back down. "Now I'll tell you the rest, and then you'll understand why I'm handing you a war you never enlisted in. Yesterday, Corliss himself came. Not the polite young man—Corliss. He offered me five hundred dollars for 'anything Elias left behind.' Five hundred, for a man's life's war. I told him Elias left behind a widow and a debt to the feed store, and he smiled and said the company was prepared to be generous about debts too. Mine, and others'. He looked at me when he said it the way a man looks at a dog he's deciding whether to kick." She folded her hands. "They think I have the photographs. They'll keep thinking that while I'm here, being a loud grieving widow they can watch. Which means the photographs themselves need to be somewhere they aren't looking. With someone who flies."

"I'm not a hero, Mrs. Crane."

"Good. Heroes die stupid. I need a pilot who's angry and careful and has a reason of her own." Marta leaned forward. "You owe Rivergate money. They own a piece of your license, your fuel account, maybe your sleep. Every day you carry their debt, you carry their leash. Elias spent his last season learning what that company really is. I'm offering you what he learned. Take it or leave it—but leave it knowing that the next person I ask might be a worse choice than you."

Outside, the river kept talking. Somewhere down the street a truck door slammed, and both women glanced at the window at the same time, the same flinch, and Marta saw Astrid register it—saw her understand that this was what it cost, this jumping at doors.

"The negatives," Astrid said slowly. "The ones he mailed. Are they here?"

Marta opened the biscuit tin again. Beneath the letters, wrapped in waxed paper inside a flour sack, lay a slim bundle of film negatives, each sleeve numbered in Elias's cramped hand. Orange water. White bellies. Rusted drums. A page of drill logs with the falsifications circled in red.

"Forty-one exposures," Marta said. "Every one of them a fact you can hold." She pushed the tin across the table. "I'm seventy pounds of arthritic widow in a town full of company men. I can't run this upriver or down. But you've got wings, Miss Ravensworth, and from what I hear, a grudge. So here's my question, and think hard before you answer, because there's no un-answering it."

She tapped the tin once, softly, like knocking on a door.

"Are you going to fly this somewhere that matters—or should I start figuring out who else might?"

Astrid looked at the negatives for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, the voice of a woman reading instruments in rough air.

"Who else is there?"

"That," said Marta, "is what I was afraid you'd say."

She was reaching for the tin when the knock came at the front door—heavy, unhurried, twice. Through the window, Marta saw a car parked at her gate that hadn't been there ten minutes ago, and a man in an expensive coat standing beside it, consulting his watch.

"Well," she said quietly. "Speak of the devil, and he checks his appointment book."
