## 5. Company Men

The knock came at seven in the morning, which was the first wrong thing about it. Nobody knocked on Astrid's door at seven in the morning unless they wanted something, and nobody in Current wanted anything from her except hangar rent, which she was three weeks behind on.

She opened the door with the chain still on, coffee mug in hand, and found a man she'd never seen before standing on her step like he'd been built there. Tall, gray at the temples, dressed in a wool overcoat that cost more than her annual fuel budget. Behind him, at the bottom of the stairs, waited a second man—bigger, younger, hands folded in front of him like a deacon at a funeral. The deacon had a jaw like a dropped anvil and the patient stillness of someone paid by the hour to wait.

"Miss Ravensworth." The tall man smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated, the kind that had been practiced in mirrors until it stopped meaning anything. "My name is Corliss. I'm with Rivergate Mining. May I come in?"

"You can say what you came to say from the step."

The smile didn't falter. If anything it warmed a degree, the way a salesman's smile warms when the mark shows a little spine. "Of course. I'll be brief. I understand you were the one who found Elias Crane."

"News travels fast."

"News travels where we carry it." He let that sit for a moment, then went on, pleasant as a man discussing the weather. "Mr. Crane did contract survey work for us, years back. A fine field man. His death is a great loss. The company would like to express its condolences to the family, and to assist with any of his effects. Personal effects can be a burden on a widow, you understand. Paperwork. Legal questions. We'd like to help her by taking that burden off her hands."

Astrid drank her coffee. "You came to my door at seven in the morning to offer to help a widow with paperwork."

"I came to ask whether Mr. Crane left anything with you. A map, perhaps. Notes. Photographs. Men in his line of work carry a lot of paper, and paper has a way of getting lost, and lost paper has a way of causing trouble for widows." He tilted his head, birdlike, courteous. "Did he leave anything with you, Miss Ravensworth?"

She had burned the oilskin wrapper the night before, in her wood stove, after studying the map until her eyes ached. The map itself was folded into a tin of pipe tobacco behind the loose board under her sink, because she hadn't yet decided what she was, and until she decided, she wanted options. She thought of the creek drawn in blue ink that faded to orange halfway down its length, and the small careful X where Crane had written, in a hand gone shaky: *not gold.*

"No," she said.

Corliss sighed, a small, regretful sound, the sigh of a man who has heard a lie so many times it no longer offends him, only tires him. "May I be frank with you?"

"You've been frank since you got here. That's the problem."

That earned a genuine flicker of something—amusement, maybe, or respect, filed away for later use. He glanced past her into the narrow room: the single bed, the chart of the interior pinned above the desk with its red-string debt notations, the framed photograph of her father standing beside the de Havilland in a year when the company that owned it now had still been honest, or had at least been quieter about it.

"You're in a difficult position," Corliss said. "I'll be direct, because I respect directness, and because you strike me as a woman who hates having her time wasted almost as much as she hates being handled. Rivergate holds the paper on your aircraft. We hold a lien on your license bond. We hold your fuel account, your hangar lease, and the note your father signed eleven years ago that you've been carrying ever since. That's a lot of paper, Miss Ravensworth. And as I said—paper has a way of getting lost."

"Or found."

"Or found." The smile again, thinner now, showing the mechanism under it. "Here is my offer, and it is a generous one, and I will only make it once. Anything Elias Crane left with you—maps, journals, film, correspondence—the company will purchase at fair value. In exchange, Rivergate will retire your debt. All of it. Every red string on that chart comes down. You walk out of this conversation owing nothing to anybody, with your aircraft clear, your license clear, and your name clean. You could fly anywhere in the world tomorrow and never look at this valley again."

"And if I say I don't have anything?"

"Then I'm afraid the accounting department will have to do its job." He said it without heat, without threat, in the tone of a man describing rainfall. "Debts get called. It's nothing personal. It's just what paper does."

* * *

They left—or Corliss left, the deacon lingering a moment on the stairs to look at her with flat professional interest before following. Astrid stood in her doorway until the car pulled away, then shut the door and leaned against it and made herself breathe.

She had met company men before. Her father had called them *suits with bush boots*, men who flew into camp twice a season in chartered Beavers, shook every hand, memorized every name, and left behind memos that nobody could decipher and paychecks that shrank. They were a fact of the north, like blackflies and break-up season. But Corliss was a different animal. The suits she'd known flexed. Corliss didn't flex. He simply stood there and let you notice how much of your life he was already holding, the way a man holds a leash and waits for the dog to figure it out.

She looked at the chart above her desk. Red string everywhere. Eleven years of red string. Her father had signed that note when the de Havilland was new and Rivergate was the biggest employer on the river and everybody believed what the brochures said. Then the price of ore dropped and the camps closed and the note came due, and her father had died with the debt still on the books, and Astrid had inherited it the way you inherit a bad knee—something that lived in you and ached when the weather turned.

Eleven years of flying anything, hauling anything, keeping her mouth shut and her questions to herself. *I'll fly anything except questions.* She'd said that to Jonah once, laughing, and he hadn't laughed back.

And now a man in a good coat was offering to make all of it vanish for the price of an oilskin map and whatever else Crane had carried out of the bush.

She got the tin from behind the loose board and sat on the bed with it in her lap and didn't open it for a long time.

*Not gold,* Crane had whispered, bleeding out on her passenger seat, his hand around her wrist with the last of his strength. *Where they hid the dead. Not the men. The river. They killed the river and they buried the proof.* Then Current's dock lights, and Marta Crane's face, and the long gray morning of a man being carried off her plane already gone.

She'd told herself on the flight down that it was grief talking. Delirium. A dying man's confusion. She'd told herself that all the way through the funeral, all the way through Marta's kitchen and the letters Marta had pressed into her hands—Elias's letters, full of photographs he'd taken of creeks the color of rust and game lying dead along the banks, full of a slow-burning certainty that Rivergate had spilled something terrible up the Dead River and buried it under a drill site and a lie.

She'd told herself all of it, and she'd almost believed it, right up until a company man knocked on her door at seven in the morning and offered to buy a dead prospector's paper.

Because that was the thing. If the map was nothing—if Crane had been a mad old man scribbling fictions—Rivergate wouldn't know it existed. Rivergate wouldn't care. You don't send a Corliss to buy smoke.

The map was real. Whatever was buried at the end of those bearings, it was real, and it was worth eleven years of her debt to them, which meant it was worth more than that, which meant it was worth more than her.

She put the tin back behind the board.

* * *

The call came at eight-forty, fifty minutes after Corliss's car had pulled away, and Astrid stood in the Rivergate field office with the receiver to her ear and listened to a man named Denny—she'd known Denny for six years, had flown his brother out of a cut leg once, no charge—read her the notice in a voice that wouldn't meet her eyes.

"The note's been accelerated, Astrid. Full balance due in thirty days. I'm sorry. I got the order an hour ago and there's a flag on your fuel account, too—no credit, cash only, until the note's resolved." A pause. Static on the line like snow. "Astrid, I—"

"Don't." She said it quietly. "You just work there, Denny."

"Yeah." He sounded relieved to be let off the hook, and ashamed of being relieved. "Thirty days."

She hung up and stood in the office while the clerk behind the counter pretended to do inventory. Thirty days. The full balance—airframe, engine, eleven years of compounding interest—was more than she'd make in five years of honest flying, if there was such a thing as honest flying in this country anymore. They hadn't even given her a month to be scared. They'd given her a month to be reasonable. That was the design. The debt wasn't a debt; it was a clock, and somewhere in a glass office Corliss was watching the hands move, patient as winter, waiting for her to come back to him and say *all right.*

Outside, the morning had turned bright and hard, the kind of light that made everything look sharper than it was. The de Havilland sat on the apron where she'd left it after the flight down from the interior, and she walked to it without deciding to, the way you walk to a grave. Her father's plane. Her father's debt. Her father's company, though he'd never lived to see what it turned into.

She put her hand on the cowling. The metal was cold and held the cold the way the north held everything—without apology.

"Anything Elias left," she said out loud, to the plane, to the wind, to the gray ghost of a man who'd frozen alone in an abandoned camp because he'd been carrying the truth in an oilskin wrapper and hadn't trusted a soul to bring it in. "He left me a promise, that's what he left."

*Fly me out alive.* She'd kept it. Barely. And now the company that had killed the river wanted to buy the proof off her and call it condolences, and if she said no they'd take the plane, the license, the sky itself—everything except the one thing they couldn't take, which was that she now knew.

That was the trap, she saw it clearly. It wasn't the debt. The debt was just the door they'd hold open while they waited for her to walk through it. The real trap was knowledge. You couldn't unknow what Crane had whispered at twelve thousand feet with the storm chewing at the tail of the plane. You couldn't unknow the orange creek on the map, or the X, or the letters in Marta's kitchen with their careful lists of dead game and dead water. Corliss had done her one favor this morning, without meaning to: he'd confirmed all of it. The company was buying paper because the paper was true.

And they knew she had it. Which meant they knew she was a witness, and Corliss had been very clear about what happened to paper that caused trouble, and Astrid had been in the north long enough to know that paper and people got lost the same way.

She looked up the valley, north, where the Dead River ran gray between the ridges toward the fog-choked canyons where the first marker waited. Thirty days. She had a map, a dying man's bearings, a plane with a lien on it, a fuel account frozen solid, and a license that a complaint could strip from her the moment Rivergate decided to file the paperwork.

They'd taken away every legal way she had to fly.

She stood there a long moment, hand on the cold cowling, and then she laughed—once, short, not much humor in it—because she'd finally understood the shape of the thing they'd built around her. They thought grounding her would end it. They thought the debt was the leash.

They'd forgotten who taught her to fly. Her father had run supplies into strips no inspector ever approved, in weather no company man would board a plane in, for people no company remembered. She'd grown up on the wrong side of every rule Rivergate wrote, and the rules had only ever been a wall to people who respected walls.

There was a light on in the hangar at the far end of the apron. She could see a shape moving under it—broad shoulders, dark hair, a man who'd been rebuilding her landing gear for free and pretending it was because he was bored. Jonah Reyes looked up as she crossed the gravel toward him, and his face did the thing it always did when he saw her coming, the thing he probably thought he hid.

"Astrid." He set down his wrench. "You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse." She glanced back at the field office, at the town, at the whole quiet machinery of it, then at him. "A company man. Jonah—I need to talk to you. And I need you to not say no until I'm finished."
