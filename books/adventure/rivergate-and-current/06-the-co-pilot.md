## 6. The Co-Pilot

Jonah Reyes heard the Cessna before he saw it, the way he heard everything that came into Rivergate country first with his hands and then with his ears and only last with his eyes. He was under a client's Beaver with a grease gun in one hand and a flashlight clenched in his teeth, and the engine note overhead was wrong—not the flat, healthy drone of a well-kept six-cylinder but a thin, sawing complaint, the sound of a motor being asked to forgive things no motor should be asked to forgive.

He knew that note the way other men knew a lover's voice.

He slid out from under the Beaver, spat the flashlight into his palm, and stood up in the wash of prop-wash and October light to watch Astrid Ravensworth bring her old de Havilland down onto the gravel at Reyes Field—which was not a field so much as a clearing, a fuel tank, a Quonset hut full of parts, and a windsock that had been flying the same faded orange since the Trudeau administration. She flared late the way she always did, kissed the gravel with the mains, and taxied up to the hut with the engine already sounding like a man clearing his throat before delivering bad news.

She killed the mags and sat in the cockpit a moment longer than she needed to. That was new. Astrid Ravensworth didn't sit in cockpits after landing; she was out of them before the prop stopped, already three tasks ahead of herself. Jonah had watched her fly for six years and he had a theory, never spoken aloud, that she loved the airplane most in the seconds after shutdown, when there was nothing left to do but be inside it.

Today she just sat. Then she climbed down, and her face was the kind of tired that doesn't come from flying.

"Oil pressure's low on the left bank," she said, before he could say anything at all. "And don't tell me it's been low for a month, because I know it's been low for a month."

"It's been low for a month."

"I said don't tell me."

He grinned and wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than it had been cloth. "Cylinders are tired. You're burning it past the rings. I can get you another season out of her if I re-ring the left side and you promise—actually promise, Astrid—to stop running her at redline like she insulted your mother."

"She did insult my mother."

"Then we're agreed." He walked a slow circle around the plane, and that was when he saw it: the passenger door's latch hanging a hair proud, the seat rails still set wide for a heavy man, and—stuck to the strut where wind had pressed it—a smear of dried blood, brown now, no bigger than a thumbprint.

He didn't say anything about the blood. He had learned, over six years, that Astrid told him things in her own order or not at all, and that pushing her was like pushing a floatplane off a dock: you got wet and the plane went where it wanted.

But she saw him see it. He watched her decide whether to lie, and he watched her lose.

"Elias Crane," she said. "I brought him out of the interior two days ago. He didn't make it."

Jonah set the rag down slowly. "Crane. The prospector? Old fella, white beard, used to come in for carburetor parts and complain about the price of everything?"

"Complained about the price of everything," she agreed. "He was shot. Or hurt bad enough that shooting would've been a mercy. He made me promise to fly him out and he gave me a map for it." Her jaw worked. "He died on the dock at Current, Jonah. Right there on the dock, with the fish flies and the tourists."

"I heard something about that." He had. Small towns run on two currencies, gossip and debt, and Current was rich in both. "They're calling it exposure. Heart failure. A man his age, out alone."

"Are they."

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't a question in a way that made the hair on his arms stand up. He looked at her—really looked—and saw what he'd been refusing to see since she stepped out of the cockpit: not grief, exactly, though it was there, but a kind of braced stillness, the look of a woman standing in a doorway deciding whether to walk into a burning building.

"Astrid," he said. "What aren't you telling me?"

"Nothing."

"You're a terrible liar. You always have been. It's one of the ten thousand things I—"

He stopped. She raised an eyebrow. He let it go, the way he always let it go, and turned to the engine instead, because engines were honest and women he loved were not, or at least not this one, not to him, not yet, maybe not ever, and he had made his peace with that the way you make peace with weather.

"Leave her with me overnight," he said. "I'll pull the left bank, re-ring it, check the mags. You'll be airworthy by tomorrow noon."

"I can't afford—"

"Astrid." He said her name the way he tightened a bolt: firmly, and only as much as it needed. "You have never once paid me full price and you never will. We'll call it a warranty against the day you finally admit you can't live without me."

"I live without you fine."

"You eat gas-station chili four times a week. That's not living, that's surviving with extra sodium."

For a second—one full second—she laughed. It came out of her like something escaping, and it changed her whole face, and Jonah stood in the sound of it the way a cold man stands in front of a stove. Then it was gone, banked again, and she was all business, pulling her kit bag out of the baggage compartment.

She hesitated over one item. A roll of oilskin, tied with wire. He saw her look at the Quonset hut, at the road, at the tree line, and then she wrapped it in a flight jacket and shoved it deep in the bag like it was something that could bite.

He didn't ask. He was already regretting it.

* * *

He worked through the evening, and she stayed, and that was how he learned the shape of it without her ever quite telling him.

She sat on an overturned oil drum with a cup of his terrible coffee, watching him work, and talked the way she always talked when she was deciding something: sideways, in pieces, testing each fact against him like a coin bitten for gold.

Crane had been half-dead when she found him. He'd had a map. Not a claim map—she'd seen enough of those to know the difference. A hand-drawn thing, creeks and bearings and little X marks, and when he'd talked, delirious, bleeding, he hadn't talked about gold.

"He said it showed where Rivergate hid its dead," she said, to the coffee more than to Jonah. "Not its gold. Its dead."

Jonah's wrench stopped moving. In the silence he could hear the windsock rope slapping its pole, out in the dark.

"Rivergate," he said. "The company that holds your paper."

"The company that holds my paper," she agreed.

"The company that just hired you for charters."

"The very one."

He set the wrench down and turned around, and he made himself say the thing he had been not-saying for six years, the thing every man in this country knew and no man said above a whisper, because Rivergate owned the fuel, the contracts, the council seats, and, some nights, it seemed, the weather.

"You remember Danny Okafor," he said.

Her face went still. "Everyone remembers Danny."

"Danny flew a load of drill core samples out of the upper valley three years ago. Rivergate said the samples were worthless, said he was a drunk, said he'd been flying on a suspended medical. Then Danny's plane came apart over Kettle Lake and the Transportation Safety Board report took eleven months to say 'probable structural failure, cause undetermined,' and his widow got a settlement with a confidentiality clause, and Rivergate got a new contract with the ministry, and nobody ever asked what was in those core samples." He wiped his hands, though they were already clean. "I knew Danny. I overhauled his engine myself, two weeks before he went down. There was nothing wrong with it, Astrid. Nothing. And I have thought about that engine every day for three years."

She was quiet for a long time. The lantern hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, a loon called across the water, that sound that is either beautiful or lonely depending on what year of your life you're standing in.

"So you're telling me to leave it alone," she said.

"I'm telling you what happened to the last pilot who didn't."

"I asked what you're telling me to do."

He looked at her. Lantern light, oil-black fingers, the stubborn line of her jaw that he had memorized the way other men memorized shorelines. Six years of watching her land in weather that grounded better pilots, six years of overhauling her engine and eating gas-station chili across from her and never once saying the actual sentence, because the actual sentence would have made it real and real things could be taken away, and Jonah Reyes had already lost a father to a logging accident and a brother to the coast and he did not lose well.

"You're going after it," he said. "The map. Whatever Crane found."

She didn't confirm it. She didn't have to. She just looked at him with that flat, level stare that had made grown outfitters apologize for things they hadn't done yet.

"The valley's poisoned," she said quietly. "That's what he found. Not gold—poison. Creeks running orange. Game dying. And Rivergate buried it, and they're paying my debts and flying my charters and one phone call from Corliss could put me on the ground forever, and I keep thinking—" She stopped, pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "I keep thinking I flew a dying man two hundred miles and I didn't even ask the right questions until he was cold."

"You asked him to fly. He asked you to promise. That's the trade you had."

"The trade I had." She laughed, without any humor in it. "And now I've got his map and his widow's eyes on me and a company that kills pilots over core samples, and every instinct I have says fly north, take the charter money, keep my head down, keep flying. And every—" she gestured vaguely at her own chest, at the place where the other thing lived— "every other part of me says I already made the promise. Just not out loud. Just the part where he said *fly me out alive* and I said yes, and he meant all of him. The man and what he knew."

Jonah picked the wrench back up. Turned it over. Put it down again.

"Your engine will be ready at noon," he said.

"Jonah."

"I'm not done." He pulled a greasy notebook from his back pocket—his load calculations, his fuel logs, his hand-drawn charts of every strip and lake and gravel bar within three hundred miles—and set it on the workbench between them. "You'll be flying low through canyon country with sketchy bearings and a company that shoots witnesses. You'll need someone who knows every deadfall strip on this river, where the fog pools, which 'lakes' on the old survey maps dried up in '71. You'll need someone who can keep that engine alive when it starts coming apart over muskeg, because it will, because I'm the one who keeps it alive and I won't be there."

"Jonah—"

"Navigation. Fuel math. Radio work. I'm not asking to fly it. I'm asking to sit in the right seat and keep you alive, which is what I've been doing from the ground for six years anyway." He heard his own voice go rough and didn't care. "I know what happened to Danny. I know what they'll do. I'm telling you I'll come anyway. That's the whole pitch. There's no fine print."

She stared at him. "You have a business. You have this field, your clients—"

"My clients are four part-time guides and a church group that fishes. I'll leave a sign on the door." He shrugged, and it cost him more than the shrug showed. "Astrid, I've been waiting six years for you to need me for something that matters, and I hate that this is it, and I'm coming anyway. That's what I'm telling you. That's all of it. You can take it or leave it, but either way the engine's ready at noon."

The lantern hissed. The loon called again, closer now, or maybe that was just the night closing in.

She looked at the notebook. At him. At the notebook again. And Jonah Reyes, who could read an engine by ear and a storm front by the smell of the wind, read her face and saw the exact moment she stopped deciding whether to let him in and started deciding how much.

"You'll follow my calls in the air," she said. "Pilot's word is final."

"Pilot's word is final."

"You fly nothing, you touch nothing, and if I tell you to jump, you ask how high on the way down."

"Copy all."

"And Jonah—" Her voice did something complicated. "This isn't— I'm not—"

"I know what it isn't," he said gently. "It's a right seat. It's a job. Take it or leave it."

She took a breath. Let it out. "Take the oilskin out of my bag before you touch anything else. If Rivergate's people come around asking about Elias Crane, they're not going to find it in my kit."

"They won't find it at all. I've got a hiding place under the floorboards that's survived two tax audits and one very motivated raccoon."

"Then we're agreed." She stuck out her hand, all business, and he took it, and her grip was oil and callus and six years of almost.

She held on maybe half a second longer than a handshake requires. He chose to believe it meant something. He would have chosen to believe it meant something if it lasted a tenth of a second.

"I'll be back at first light," she said, and walked out into the dark toward the bunkhouse, and Jonah stood alone in the lantern light with an engine in pieces and a notebook full of charts and the specific, terrified joy of a man who has finally been given the thing he asked for, knowing exactly what it might cost.

He was still standing there when he heard the floatplane first—or rather heard what he'd heard a thousand times and never once at this hour: the low thrum of a big twin-engine aircraft running dark, no lights, no radio, up the river valley at an hour when no honest aircraft flies.

It passed over Reyes Field without slowing. Running dark. Running quiet. Heading toward Current.

Toward Astrid.

Jonah killed the lantern and stood in the dark, listening to the sound fade, and did the fuel math in his head, the way he did everything: how many hours of night were left, how far to Current, how fast he could have the left bank re-ringed and the old girl ready to fly.

Not fast enough. Not nearly fast enough. But by noon, one way or another, he was going to be in the right seat.

He picked up his wrench and started to work.
