## 7. Grounded

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt like the kind of insult a company like Rivergate would find funny.

Astrid found it wedged in the screen door of the float shack, rain-softened at one corner where the morning's weather had worked at it, and she knew before she opened it. She knew by the weight of it, by the letterhead she could see through the envelope's thin skin, by the fact that it existed at all. People who wanted to talk to you called on the radio or came to the dock. People who wanted to bury you sent paper.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, the envelope in her hand, the lake gray and flat as poured pewter beyond the dock, and let herself have three seconds of not knowing. Three seconds where it was still just a letter, maybe a bill, maybe nothing.

Then she opened it.

*Re: Fuel account No. 4471-C, Ravensworth Air Services.* The language was clean as a scalpel. *Effective immediately, all credit extended to the above-named account by Rivergate Supply & Fuel is suspended pending review.* There was more, paragraphs of it, words like *outstanding balance* and *terms of agreement* and *remedies available to the creditor*, but Astrid read the first line three times and the rest once, and what she took away was simple: as of this morning, nobody in the territory would sell her so much as a drum of avgas. Rivergate Supply wasn't just a company store. It was *the* company store. It held the fuel contracts for every depot from Current to the head of the river, and every one of those depots answered, one way or another, to the same letterhead.

She set the letter on the table, on top of the oilskin map she'd been studying half the night, and put the kettle on because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

The second envelope came by floatplane at noon.

She heard it first — the drone of an engine out of the south, wrong rhythm for Jonah's Cessna, too steady, too corporate — and by the time she stepped out onto the dock, a Rivergate Beaver was already flaring in, throwing a sheet of spray, the pilot not even looking at her as he taxied to the far piling and idled there while a man in a dry, clean jacket stepped out onto the floats and walked the plank like it was a boardroom carpet.

Not Corliss. A lesser animal. A courier with a briefcase and a face like a closed ledger.

"Miss Ravensworth." He said her name the way you'd read an account number. "I have something for you. Signature required."

The second letter was thinner than the first and heavier in the hand. She signed his clipboard without reading the form attached to it, because the form didn't matter, and stood on the dock while the Beaver hauled itself back into the air and droned away south, and only then did she unfold the page.

*Notice of Complaint.* Filed with the regional transport authority by Rivergate Mining, Limited, *alleging unsafe operation of aircraft, falsification of maintenance records, and operation for hire without valid certification.* A hearing date. Thirty days out. And beneath the formal language, a paragraph that made her blood go cold and then hot, because it wasn't legal boilerplate at all — it was a list. Dates. Flights. The charter upriver. The weather she'd flown through in March. The passenger manifest she'd never filed. Whoever had written it knew her logbook better than she did, which meant they'd either stolen it or rebuilt it from the inside, which meant someone had been in her shack, in her files, in her life, with a pencil and a grudge.

*Falsification of maintenance records.* That one she read twice. Her maintenance records were clean. Her maintenance records were the one thing in her life that was clean, because a bush pilot who lies about her own airplane is signing her own death warrant, and Astrid Ravensworth had lied about plenty of things — her fuel reserves, her debt, the state of her heart — but never about the ship.

They didn't need it to be true. That was the part she understood, standing on the dock with the paper trembling in the wind off the lake. They just needed it filed. A complaint meant an investigation, an investigation meant a suspension, and a suspension meant that by the time anyone got around to the truth, she'd be a year grounded and a year forgotten, and whatever Elias Crane had photographed in the valley before they killed him would be so much buried paper in a buried grave in a buried valley.

She looked at the letter a long time. Then she folded it, walked back into the shack, and put it on the table next to the first one, and the two documents lay there together like a pair of hands closing.

* * *

Jonah found her that evening, because Jonah always found her, and she'd stopped pretending she didn't know how he did it. He came up the path from the fuel dock with two paper-wrapped burgers going cold in a sack and the particular walk he used when he'd already heard the news and was deciding how to carry it.

"Fuel account's dead," he said, before he'd even reached the step.

"Word travels."

"Word travels when the depot man in Current calls every other depot man in the territory, which he did by four o'clock." He handed her a burger through the door without coming in, an old courtesy, the shack being hers and small. "And the complaint?"

She told him. All of it, standing in the doorway eating a cold burger in the rain, and he listened the way Jonah listened — still, eyes down, like a man reading water.

"They're not trying to scare you," he said at last. "Scaring you would've been Corliss's second visit. This is the real thing. They're taking the airplane away."

"They can't take the airplane. They can take the license, the fuel, the accounts — they can't take the airplane. It's mine. Paid down to eleven hundred dollars."

"They don't need to take it." He said it gently, which was worse. "A plane without fuel is a sculpture. A plane without a license is evidence. You know what happens the first time you fly it now. They'll have a man at every strip with a telephone."

She knew. That was the hell of it. She'd spent six years flying the line between legal and not, and she knew the exact width of that line, and Rivergate had just erased it. Every hour she flew now was a crime they'd already written down. Every drum of fuel she bought was a favor she'd owe and they'd collect. They hadn't grounded her. They'd made the whole sky hers only at a price she couldn't pay.

"They're waiting," Jonah said. "That's the thing. Corliss could've had the film the day he sat in your shack. He's got men, he's got money, he could've turned this place over. Instead he froze your fuel and filed paper. He's not trying to stop you, Astrid. He's trying to make you *decide*. Make it your choice. So when you quit — and you will quit, everybody quits — it's yours. And if you don't quit, everything that happens after is yours too. He builds it so the law comes down on you, not on him."

She looked at him. Rain ran off the brim of his cap and he didn't shift his weight, didn't look away, and she thought, not for the first time, that Jonah Reyes was the only man she'd ever met who could say a hard thing without making it a weapon.

"And if I do quit," she said, "the valley stays poisoned. The creeks stay orange. Whatever Elias found stays in the ground with him, and Marta spends the rest of her life knowing her husband died carrying the proof and nobody would carry it the last hundred miles."

Jonah was quiet. Down at the dock the lake worked at the pilings, that old slow knocking, the sound the territory made when it had nothing to say.

"I didn't say quit," he said finally. "I said he wants you to think those are the only two doors. Quit or hang. But I've been thinking about that map." He nodded past her, at the oilskin lying on the table under the two letters. "Crane drew those bearings from memory and a survey plate. You told me. Creeks, ridgelines, the old drill sites. You know what that means?"

"That it's guesswork."

"That it's *bush*. Fog country, low country, dead-reckoning country." Something had come into his voice, the mechanic's voice, the voice he used when a problem turned interesting. "You don't need a license for that kind of flying. You don't need a fuel account. You need a plane, gas in it, and weather bad enough that nobody's out looking. Rivergate just took away every way you could do this *legally*." He almost smiled. "So stop doing it legally."

"Jonah—"

"Think about it before you yell at me." He set the second burger on the step, unwrapped, steam curling up into the rain. "I'm going to walk back. You should eat while it's hot."

He went down the path and she watched him go, and she didn't yell at him, and she ate while it was hot, standing in the doorway, and the terrible thing — the thing she'd remember — was that the burger tasted good. Life went on insisting on itself even when the sky was closing.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat at the table with the map and the letters and the lamp turned low, and she did what she always did when the world got too big, which was make it small again, one column of numbers at a time.

Fuel: she had maybe forty gallons in the drums behind the shack, enough for three hours of flying, four if she leaned the mixture and prayed. Not enough to reach the valley and not enough to matter if she did. The depot at Current was closed to her. The depot at Kettle Narrows would be closed by now, the phone calls having done their work. Every legal drum of avgas in the territory had a Rivergate sticker on it.

License: thirty days to a hearing she'd lose. She'd flown the complaint's list in her head twice and every item was either true and survivable or invented and unprovable, and it didn't matter, because the man hearing the case would be a man with a mortgage, and Rivergate held the paper on half the mortgages in the district. She'd seen that arithmetic before. It always came out the same.

Airplane: the old de Havilland sat on its mooring buoy out in the rain, forty years old, honest as a hammer, and for the first time since she'd owned her it looked to Astrid like what it was — a thing that could be taken. Not seized. Just made impossible. They'd found the one lock she couldn't pick, and the lock was paper.

She put her head in her hands.

And underneath the arithmetic, underneath the fear, the other thing kept rising. It had been rising since the dock in Current, since Elias Crane's hand had closed around her wrist with the last of his strength, and it rose now through the numbers like gas through water: *the creeks are orange.* She'd seen the first one herself, or near enough — the tributary off the Kettle, the one where the rocks wore a stain like rust and there were no frogs, and she'd flown over it for six years and told herself it was minerals, it was the country, it was nothing. Elias Crane had spent a season not telling himself that. He'd walked those creeks with a camera and a notebook and he'd died for the negatives, and the company that killed him had just spent one Tuesday morning making sure that the only pilot who knew what he'd found could never fly again.

They'd done it with paper. Two envelopes. No threats, no guns, no men on her dock in the night. That was the part that made her hands go still on the table. Not the malice of it — the *tidiness*. Somewhere in a glass office a man had decided that Astrid Ravensworth was a line item, and a clerk had executed the decision before lunch, and the whole machine had turned over once, quietly, like a bank vault closing, and now the sky was shut.

And the valley would stay shut with it. The fish would keep floating. The water table under Current — Marta's well, the school's well — would keep drinking whatever Rivergate had buried at Camp Nine, one season at a time, until the kids who drank it were old enough to be sick from it, and by then the men who signed the orders would be retired on pensions and the men who buried the drums would be dead, and the paper trail would be a fire in a filing cabinet, and it would all have worked.

It would all have worked, if she let it.

Astrid sat up. The lamp hissed. Out on the black lake the rain had stopped and the wind had come around into the north, cold and clean, and she could hear the old plane knocking at its mooring, restless, like it knew.

Here was the thing she understood then, sitting in the lamplight with two letters and a dead man's map, and she understood it the way you understand a stall warning — not with the mind but lower down, in the body, in the place that keeps you alive: *staying invisible was a choice too.* She had spent six years choosing it. Fly under the radar, owe who you owe, keep your head down, keep your tail number out of the reports. It had kept her alive. It had kept her small. And it had a price like everything, and the price had just come due, and the price was a valley.

She could stay grounded. She could keep her license — or the ghost of it — and keep her name off the reports, and in thirty days stand in a hearing room and lose politely, and spend the rest of her life flying medicine runs and mail runs and looking at the orange creeks from two thousand feet and telling herself it was minerals.

Or.

She got up and went to the window. The north wind smelled like snow coming, and beyond it, two hundred miles of fog and muskeg and dead river, the valley lay under the same sky, holding its dead and its drums and its photographs, waiting for somebody with a working airplane and a reason.

They'd left her one door and told her it was a wall. But Jonah was right, and she'd known it since the dock, and the knowing had been sitting in her chest all evening like fuel looking for a spark: a pilot without a license is only a criminal if she gets caught, and a pilot without fuel accounts is only grounded if she buys her gas from Rivergate. The territory was full of men who owed her — the trapper at Loon Narrows with his cache of drummed gas, the mining camp at Sulphur that paid her in cash, the whole quiet underground economy of the bush that had never trusted a company store and never would. It would be slow. It would be dangerous. It would be exactly the kind of flying that had gotten her into debt in the first place.

She went back to the table and turned the map over, and began, in pencil, on the blank oilskin side, making a list. Not the company's list. Her own.

*Fuel. Route. Weather window. And Jonah.*

The last one she wrote and then sat looking at it for a long time, because writing it down made it a decision, and she wasn't sure she had the right to make it for him. He'd know what it would cost. He'd watched the last pilot who crossed Rivergate get found in the shallows of the Kettle with his plane burned to the waterline. He'd loved her for years without asking anything, and what she was about to ask would cost him everything he'd built with those quiet hands of his.

She heard his boots on the path before she saw the lantern — coming back through the wet dark, the way he always came back, because he'd known, even walking away, that she'd reach the same page he had. She watched the light come up the path toward her door, and she picked up the pencil, and under *Jonah* she wrote one more word.

*Tomorrow.*

The knock came. She opened the door on the rain-smell and the lantern-glow and the man holding them, and before he could speak she stepped aside to let him in, and on the table behind her the map lay face-up under the lamp, and beside it, in her small hard pencil hand, the list that was going to get them both killed or else set a valley free.

"I need a navigator," Astrid said. "And a fuel run nobody's going to sell me."
