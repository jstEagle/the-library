## 8. Night Fuel Run

The moon came up around ten, thin as a coin rubbed nearly blank, and by eleven the airfield at Current had gone quiet enough to hear the river working at its banks a half mile off. Jonah Reyes stood in the shadow of Marta Crane's woodshed with a length of rubber hose coiled over his shoulder like a dead snake and watched the two men in the parked pickup across the field.

They'd been there since sundown. A grey Ford with its lights off, engine ticking now and then when one of them started it to run the heater. Corliss's men didn't even pretend anymore. That was the thing that got under Jonah's skin—not the watching itself but the laziness of it, the confidence. They sat out there in the open like they owned the dark, because as far as they were concerned, they did.

"You're staring," Astrid said beside him.

"I'm counting."

"How many?"

"Two in the truck. One walked the fence line an hour ago and hasn't come back, so either he's circling or he found a warm place to sit. Either way, we've got maybe forty minutes of nobody looking directly at the hangar."

Astrid shifted the lantern she carried, unlit, under her coat. In the dark her face was all angles and exhaustion, three days of it stacked on top of more, and still she had that look—the one she got before a short-field takeoff, jaw set, eyes already somewhere down the runway. He'd been in love with that look for six years and it had never once gotten easier.

"Forty minutes," she said. "The drums are behind the hangar. Fuel's low-sulphur avgas, same as the Otter drinks."

"I know what my own fuel tastes like."

"You've tasted it?"

"Siphoning's an education." He handed her the hose. "You hold the light when I say. Not before. One flicker over that fence line and we're done."

She took it without argument, which told him how scared she was. Astrid argued with weather, with creditors, with God if He happened to be standing nearby. She only went quiet when the stakes stopped being arguable.

They crossed the open ground low and fast, bent double between the snowdrifts the plow had thrown up along the taxiway, and Jonah counted his heartbeats the way his father had taught him to count river currents—by feel, not numbers. The hangar loomed up black against the slightly-less-black sky, its big doors chained since Rivergate froze the field's accounts, and behind it, stacked on pallets under a tarp, the drums Marta had bought for him through a cousin in Fort Nelson, cash, no paper trail. Forty-four gallons each. Enough to get the old de Havilland to the valley and back, if the valley didn't kill them first.

He peeled back the tarp and worked the bung loose with his pocketknife. The smell rose up sweet and chemical, and for a second he just breathed it, because avgas smelled like possibility, like anywhere-but-here, and he'd been breathing it since he was fourteen years old and small enough to crawl inside a wing root.

"Hose," he whispered.

Astrid passed it. He fed it into the drum, tipped the drum onto its rim against his knee, and got the siphon going the old way—mouth on the tube, a lungful of fumes, a swallow of regret—then spat clear and jammed the end into the plane's tank filler while the drum drained.

The Otter stood on the apron where they'd pushed it two nights ago, tail to the wind, looking exactly like what it was: a twenty-year-old bush plane with a patched cowl and a prop that needed dressing. It had belonged to a man named Hollis once. Jonah didn't say that part out loud. Nobody said Hollis's name in Current anymore except Marta, quietly, and Jonah, quietly, and both of them only after dark.

* * *

The work filled the time the way good work does. While the first drum drained he pulled the cowling panels and checked the lines by touch, running his fingers along every fitting he could reach, feeling for the soft spots winter leaves behind. Astrid held the lantern shuttered to a coin of light between her boots, cupping it with both hands like something alive.

"Number three cylinder's been weeping," she murmured. "I can smell it on the plugs."

"Weeping's not leaking."

"On this airplane, weeping is a promise."

He almost laughed. He pressed the sound flat and kept working. "I'll re-torque it. Hand me the wrench."

She did, and their fingers overlapped on the cold steel a beat longer than the job required. Neither of them made anything of it. That was the whole architecture of them, six years of it—everything important passing between them in the space where nothing was said.

The second drum went slower because his arms were burning and the cold had gotten into his knuckles, turning them stupid. Astrid took over the siphon without being asked, cheeks hollowing, and came up coughing, eyes streaming, swearing under her breath in a whisper.

"Told you it was an education."

"Shut up, Jonah."

"Yes ma'am."

She got it flowing and he moved to the tail to check the tie-downs and the elevator cables, and that was when he heard it—a door, far off, the particular clunk of a truck door closing with care instead of habit.

He froze. Across the field, the grey Ford's parking lights came on, dim orange ovals in the frost, and a flashlight beam swung out from the truck and began walking the fence line. Slow. Methodical. A man who'd been told to look bored and had decided to look thorough instead.

"Astrid." No breath behind the word, just shape.

She saw it. She killed the lantern with her palm and they stood pressed against the Otter's flank in the drum-smell and the dark, the last inch of fuel glugging down into the tank with a sound, Jonah thought wildly, like a faucet in an empty house at midnight. Loud. Impossibly loud.

The beam reached the corner of the hangar and stopped.

* * *

There was no plan for this. They hadn't planned past the fuel, because planning past the fuel meant admitting how thin the whole thing was—one old plane, two tired people, and a roll of film sewn into a jacket, against a company that owned the bank, the council, and probably the judge. So when the flashlight started coming along the hangar wall toward them, Jonah did the only thing the geometry allowed.

He grabbed Astrid's sleeve and hauled her around to the far side of the main gear, and the wheel well was right there, open, the access panel they'd removed still lying in the snow—and it was big enough, barely, the way a coffin is big enough.

"In," he breathed.

"It smells like—"

"In."

She went in first, folding herself into the dark behind the wheel assembly, and he came in after her, dragging the panel half-closed behind him, leaving a finger's width of gap. The well was sheet metal and hydraulic stink and old grease, and it was four feet wide, and it held two grown people the way a glove holds two hands.

The flashlight came around the tail.

Jonah felt Astrid's heartbeat through her shoulder where it pressed his chest—or maybe it was his heartbeat, there was no telling whose was whose anymore. Her breath came shallow and fast against his collarbone, and he could feel her fighting it, slowing it, the pilot in her doing arithmetic about oxygen and panic. Outside, boot soles crunched frost. Ten yards. Five.

The beam slid across the gap in the panel and painted a white stripe across his knee.

He didn't move. He thought, absurdly, about the third man, the one who'd walked the fence and never come back, and whether he might be behind them, and decided that if he was, he'd rather not know. The boot steps stopped close enough that Jonah could hear the man breathing through his nose, hear fabric shift, hear him take out a cigarette and light it. The match-flare threw a brief orange glow under the panel edge, and in that glow Jonah looked down and saw Astrid looking up at him, her face inches from his, lit gold for one full second.

Neither of them blinked. There wasn't time to be afraid properly; there was only the animal business of staying still, and inside that stillness something else opened up, quiet and enormous, the way a valley opens when fog lifts. Six years of Tuesdays and borrowed tools and coffee gone cold on a workbench, six years of loving her sideways so it wouldn't cost anything—and here it was costing everything, and here she was, cheek against his chest, trusting him with the weight of her whole body, which was the only kind of trust she'd ever been able to give anyone.

The cigarette burned down outside. The man shifted, muttered something to himself—Jonah caught the word *nothing*, and the word *freezing*—and crunched away, back toward the truck. The parking lights died. The Ford stayed.

They waited another five minutes anyway. Then ten.

When Jonah finally eased the panel wider, the cold poured in like water and he realized he was soaked through with sweat and that his hands were shaking, and that Astrid hadn't let go of his jacket. He didn't point it out. He climbed out, legs screaming, and reached back to help her, and she came up out of the wheel well into the starlight with grease on her cheekbone and her hair wrecked and her eyes bright as struck flint.

"Well," she whispered. "That was cozy."

"Company accommodations. You get what you pay for."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of her, one breath, quickly smothered. She wiped her face with her wrist and left the grease smear worse, and Jonah had to turn away and check the fuel caps because if he kept looking at her he was going to do something stupid, like tell her, out loud, after six years, with a truck full of Rivergate men a hundred yards off and a storm building over the divide.

He finished the caps. She buttoned the cowl panels. They rolled the empty drums back under the tarp and erased their tracks with a pine bough, working in the easy wordless rhythm they'd always had, and it struck him that this—this, right here, freezing and felonious and probably doomed—was the happiest he'd been in years. Because for once there was no pretending about what they were to each other. You don't hide in a wheel well with someone you're not sure about.

At the edge of the hangar's shadow she stopped and put her hand flat on the Otter's cold fuselage, the way you'd steady a horse.

"He dies tomorrow if we don't go," she said. She didn't name him. She never named him anymore—Corliss, the fixer, the man with the clean nails—but Jonah knew who she meant. "And if we don't go, the valley stays dead, and Marta buries Elias twice."

"And if we go and they catch us—"

"They won't catch us." She turned and looked at him, and the moonlight showed him something he'd never seen on her face before, not even on her best flying days. "We're going up the Dead River at first light, Jonah. Before the fog burns off. I need to know you're with me—not helping me. With me."

He looked at the grey Ford, at the field where Hollis used to park, at the woman he'd loved since before he'd known what the wanting was called.

"I siphoned forty gallons of stolen fuel into your airplane tonight," he said. "What do you think?"

She smiled then, quick and crooked, and squeezed his arm once, hard, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

It was only later, walking her back toward Marta's through the blue pre-dawn cold, that he glanced over his shoulder at the airfield one last time—and saw the third man. The one from the fence line. Standing at the edge of the light by the hangar doors, looking not at the plane but at the two of them, and speaking quietly into a radio held close to his mouth.
