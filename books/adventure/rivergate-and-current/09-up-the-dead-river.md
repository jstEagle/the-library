## 9. Up the Dead River

The fog came off the river at dawn like something breathing.

Astrid had flown in weather her whole life—whiteouts over the pass, ice fog that turned the windshield to ground glass, the gray soup that rolled up the valleys of the interior every fall and swallowed small planes whole. She knew its moods. She knew when it was lying and when it meant it. This morning, threading the canyon above the dead river with Crane's map spread across her knees and Jonah's hand steady on the throttle quadrant, she felt like she was flying inside the thing itself, inside the lung of some animal too big to have a name.

"Two hundred feet," Jonah said. "Maybe less."

"I can see the water."

"You can see a rumor of the water."

She grinned despite herself. That was Jonah. Even now, even here—with Rivergate's fixers somewhere behind them, with her license formally under complaint and her fuel accounts frozen into blocks of legal ice—he sat in the right seat like he'd been born there, calling out numbers in a voice so calm it was almost insolent. The old Otter climbed and groaned around them, patched with scavenged aluminum and running on fuel they'd siphoned by lantern light while Corliss's men smoked cigarettes fifty yards away in the dark. The plane smelled of cold metal and kerosene and, faintly, of the wheel well where they'd hidden together pressed chest to chest, holding their breath while boots crunched past. Neither of them mentioned it. Both of them kept touching it anyway—the memory, not each other. Though sometimes, when he shifted gears on the mixture control, his knuckles brushed her wrist, and neither of them pulled away.

"Bearing says the canyon bends left after the second ridge," she said. "Crane wrote 'keep the water on your left or you'll die'—underlined twice."

"He sounds charming."

"He sounds like a man who flew this route scared." She glanced down at the oilskin map, at Crane's cramped pencil annotations, the little crosses marking rapids, the note in the margin that read *fog sits here till noon, do not trust your eyes.* "Which means we fly it scared too. Scared is accurate."

The canyon walls rose on either side, black spruce clinging to rock in the fog like the last hairs on an old man's head. Below them the river ran wide and slow and wrong-colored—not the glacial green-gray of living water but something flatter, duller, like pewter left out in the rain. Astrid had noticed it from altitude yesterday and told herself it was sediment, shadow, anything. Now, down in the throat of the valley with nothing between her and the surface but two hundred feet of cloud, she couldn't lie to herself anymore.

"Left bend coming up," Jonah said. "There's the ridge. There's the—" He stopped.

"What?"

"Look at the bank."

She looked. On the eastern shore, half-hidden by fog, stood a cairn of stones stacked taller than a man, with a pole driven through its center and something pale lashed to the top—a strip of cloth, maybe, or hide, bleached by seasons of weather into a shape like a flag of surrender. Crane's map marked it with a small circle and one word: *first.*

"There," Astrid said, and her voice came out quieter than she intended. "That's his marker. We're on his line."

* * *

They'd taken off from the trapper's lake before first light, in the blue hour when the mist lies flat on still water and the world hasn't decided what it is yet. Old Henrik had stood on his dock with his hands jammed in his pockets, watching them load the film case and the last of the food, and he'd said only one thing: "The river remembers what men pay it to forget. Go see what it remembers."

Astrid had thought about that the whole way north. The river remembers. It sounded like the kind of thing old men said to make themselves feel better about the things they'd done for money. But now, low over the water with Crane's bearings pulling them deeper into country no map company bothered to survey, she began to understand that Henrik hadn't been talking in metaphors. Rivers keep records. Every drum dumped, every tailings pond breached, every chemical that ever kissed that water—it all went downstream, written in the mud, read out in the color of the current, testified to by whatever died along the banks.

"Fuel's fine," Jonah said, reading her glance at the gauges. "We've got three hours of puttering if we need it."

"We won't need it. Crane's notes say the creek comes in on the west side, maybe forty minutes past the cairn. He drew a fish beside it." She paused. "He drew the fish upside down."

Jonah didn't answer that. In the silence she heard the engine's rhythm change as he eased the mixture richer, leaning into the thin cold air, keeping the old girl humming. He was good. Better than good—he flew the way other people prayed, total and unselfconscious, and watching him work the controls in her peripheral vision gave her a feeling she didn't have time to name. Later. When they were out of this valley, if they got out of this valley, she would name it.

The fog thickened as the canyon narrowed. She dropped lower. A hundred and fifty feet. A hundred. The river slid beneath them like a gray vein, and the walls closed in until she could count the individual trees on the ledges, could see water seeping from the rock face in dark stains, could smell—she cracked her window and smelled it—something chemical and faintly sweet, like solvent, like a garage floor after a spill.

"That's not the bush," Jonah said. His voice had lost its calm.

"No," Astrid said. "It isn't."

* * *

They found the creek forty-one minutes past the cairn.

She saw it first as a wound. That was the only word her mind offered: a wound in the hillside, a gash of color bleeding down through the spruce to the river. From a distance it might have been autumn fireweed, might have been iron stain, might have been any of the hundred innocent oranges and rusts the north wears in September. Then they were closer, then they were directly over it, and there was nothing innocent anywhere in sight.

The creek ran orange.

Not rust-orange, not the honest ochre of natural mineral seeps she'd seen a dozen times in real wilderness. This was industrial orange, chemical orange, the orange of things that come out of pipes. It ran bright as paint against the dark river, spreading where it met the current in a fan of stained foam, and the foam lay on the water in long ropes that didn't dissolve, just drifted, trailing downstream like the river was bleeding slowly and couldn't stop.

"Oh my God," Jonah said softly.

Astrid brought the plane around in a slow circle, banking over the confluence, and looked down at the water below the mouth of the creek. At first she thought the river surface was rippling strangely. Then she understood what she was seeing, and her stomach turned over.

Fish. Dozens of them. Grayling, mostly, the beautiful sail-finned fish that filled every clean creek in the territory, floating belly-up in the slack water below the confluence, silver sides catching the gray light, packed against the banks in windrows where the current had sorted the dead. Some were fresh. Some had been there long enough that the gulls had been at them. And below the dead zone, where the poison thinned into the main current, live fish gathered in confused clusters, circling, refusing to pass upstream—as if the river itself had posted a sign.

"Crane drew the fish upside down," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Because they were already floating."

Neither of them spoke for a long moment. The engine droned. The fog breathed against the windows. Below them the creek poured its orange blood into the river without pause, without apology, hour after hour, day after day—for how long? Years? Since when?

"Get the camera," Astrid said finally. "I'll hold it steady over the mouth of the creek."

Jonah unlatched the case between the seats and lifted out Crane's camera, the prospector's old folding Kodak, and beside it the newer camera Marta had given them, loaded with fresh film. He shot the confluence from four angles. He shot the windrows of dead fish. He shot the foam lines trailing downstream, and the stained banks, and the seep stains on the canyon wall where more of it—whatever it was—bled out of the ground itself. Astrid held the plane in slow orbits, wings tipped, fighting the gusts that funneled down the canyon, giving him a stable platform the way she'd once given photographers a stable platform for pictures of bears and waterfalls, back when her flying was innocent.

"It's coming from uphill," Jonah said, lowering the camera. "See the drainage? The creek's just the delivery system. Whatever spilled is up in the high country."

"Camp Nine," Astrid said. "Crane worked there. Crane photographed there. And Crane's map has a second marker." She looked down at the oilskin, at the little cross drawn eight miles farther up the canyon with the words *they will say it is natural — it is not* penciled beneath in letters gone shaky, as if the hand that wrote them had been cold, or frightened, or both.

"The map is true," Jonah said quietly.

"Yeah." Astrid leveled the wings and pointed the nose up the canyon, into the fog, toward Camp Nine. "The map is true."

And that was the terrible thing, she realized. Not the orange creek, not the floating fish, not even the sweet chemical reek seeping through the window gap. The terrible thing was that Elias Crane had known. Had flown this same canyon, seen this same wound in the earth, and instead of flying home to safety he'd spent his last season gathering proof, alone, an old man with a folding camera, because somebody had to remember what the river remembered. Because somebody had to carry it out.

She thought of him dying on the dock at Current, his hand tight around hers, his last breath spent on words instead of air: *not gold. the dead. show them the dead.*

"Hold on," she said. "Upstream."

* * *

The canyon tightened. The fog sat on them like a lid. Astrid flew by instinct and Crane's pencil marks, counting bends, matching ridgelines to his little crosses, feeling her way up the vein of the poisoned river the way a doctor follows an artery toward the tumor. Twice she climbed to clear a spur and lost the river in cloud and had to spiral back down, heart hammering, until the gray water appeared again beneath her like a thread she refused to drop.

Jonah navigated. He'd taped Crane's bearing notes to the panel where she could see them, and he called them out in his level voice, and between the two of them—the pilot who wouldn't quit and the mechanic who loved her—they threaded the needle.

"There." He pointed off the left wing. "Smoke. Or steam."

Ahead, half-veiled in fog, a plume rose from the canyon floor—not woodsmoke, nothing so honest. It was white and oily and it clung to the treetops before dispersing, and beneath it, where the canyon widened into a bench of gravel and crushed rock, Astrid could see the shapes. Buildings. A headframe. Rows of something rectangular and rust-streaked, aligned with military neatness. Tailings ponds like open wounds, one of them breached, its dam slumped sideways, its contents draining downslope toward the creek.

Toward the creek that ran orange. Toward the fish floating belly-up. Toward the river, and everything downstream of the river—every village, every fish camp, every family that drank from it.

Camp Nine.

"Circle once," Jonah said. "Wide."

She circled. Through the drifting fog the camp revealed itself in pieces: the drill pads, the collapsed shed, the drums—hundreds of drums, rusted, some split open, their contents long since soaked into the muskeg. And near the edge of the bench, half-hidden by alder, a scar of raw earth too regular to be natural, too large to be a grave for anything small.

Astrid's hands were steady on the yoke. She was surprised by that. Inside, something was very loud and very cold.

"They buried it," Jonah said. "Right out in the open. They didn't even try to hide it from the sky, because who flies up here? Who looks?"

"Elias looked."

"Elias looked," he agreed. Then, softer: "Astrid. If they did this—if they knew, and they did this—then Corliss isn't hunting us for trespassing."

"No," she said. "He's hunting us for witnessing."

The fog was thickening again, closing over Camp Nine like a curtain falling on the last act of something. Down below, the white plume rose and spread. Somewhere under that raw scar in the earth lay whatever Crane had died trying to show the world, and somewhere behind them, downriver, downlake, down every current that connected this place to the world of people and law and light, Corliss's men were surely already moving.

Astrid found the gravel bar Crane's map marked with a small anchor symbol—*land here, only here, the rest will take your wheels*—and set the Otter down on it soft as a moth landing. The engine ticked into silence. For a moment there was nothing but the river sound, and the fog pressing close, and the faint sweet stink of poison riding the air.

Jonah reached over and touched her hand where it rested on the throttle.

"Together?" he said.

"Together," she said.

They stepped out onto the gravel bar of the dead river, and began to walk toward Camp Nine—and that was when Astrid saw, pressed into the mud at the edge of the bar, a boot print. Fresh. Deep-treaded. Company-issue rubber, the kind Rivergate issued its field crews by the hundred.

Someone had landed here before them. Someone was already walking toward the camp.
