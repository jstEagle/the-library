## 10. Camp Nine

The map said two miles. The bush made it six.

Jonah Reyes counted his steps to keep from counting the things that could go wrong, and by step four hundred he'd given up on both. The ground under the spruce was a sponge that had never learned to hold a shape—muskeg stitched with alder and deadfall, every stride a negotiation. Ahead of him Astrid moved like she'd been born to it, which was a lie, because he'd known her eleven years and he knew exactly what she'd been born to: a hangar floor in Fort Reliance, a father who smelled of avgas, a girl who learned to read weather before she learned cursive.

She stopped at a deadfall the size of a school bus and turned back to look at him. Even now, even out here with Rivergate's fixers somewhere behind them and Crane's map folded into her breast pocket, she checked on him first. That was the thing about Astrid Ravensworth nobody understood who only saw the swagger and the debt and the grounded license. She carried people. She just hated anyone knowing it.

"You good?" she called, low.

"Living the dream," Jonah said.

The fog had come off the river overnight and hadn't lifted, hanging in the trees like gauze over a wound. It muffled everything—their breathing, the drip off the branches, the distant complaint of some bird neither of them could name. It also meant Corliss's men couldn't see them from the air, if they were looking from the air. It also meant Jonah couldn't shake the feeling of being inside something rather than beneath it.

Crane's bearings had gotten them this far. The old man's hand-drawn lines were shaky but honest—a creek bend drawn as a fishhook, a granite knob marked with three strokes like claw marks, and then, in letters pressed so hard they'd torn the paper: *CAMP NINE. LOOK UNDER THE WORLD.*

They crossed the fishhook creek at midmorning. That was when the water told them first.

It ran orange.

Not muddy-orange, not tannin-brown the way northern creeks went in fall. This was iron-orange, rust-orange, the color of a wound left too long unwashed, and where it pooled against stones there was a scum like oil-slick rainbow, and along the banks the moss was gray instead of green, and there were no tracks. None. Not moose, not wolf, not squirrel. A creek in the middle of untouched country should have been a highway of sign. This one was a road closed.

Astrid crouched at the edge and didn't touch it. Jonah watched her face do something complicated—confirmation and grief arriving in the same second, the way they always do.

"Crane wasn't crazy," she said quietly.

"No," Jonah said. "He was just early."

* * *

They found the granite knob an hour later, and Camp Nine below it, and Jonah understood immediately why Elias Crane had written *look under the world.*

The drill site sat in a shallow bowl the glacier had carved ten thousand years ago and men had used for eight months and then abandoned like a body. From above—from any aircraft passing at cruise altitude—it would read as nothing. A clearing. Old cut, regrown. The spruce and alder had been allowed to close over it deliberately, he realized, seeded and encouraged, a living lid on a box.

But down inside the bowl, the disguise fell apart.

There were four buildings still standing, or the skeletons of them: a core shed with its roof half collapsed, a bunkhouse gone soft with rot, a fuel cache platform, and a quonset hut whose curved ribs stuck up through the alder like the bones of a whale. Everything wore the gray-brown of things left to be forgotten. But nothing had been properly decommissioned. Nothing had been cleaned. Companies cleaned up sites they wanted found clean. This one had been tidied just enough to pass a flyover and no more.

"Jonah." Astrid's voice had gone flat, careful, the voice she used on the radio when the engine was making a sound she didn't want to name. "Over here."

The drums started at the tree line and ran in rows into the bowl. Fifty-five-gallon steel drums, dozens of them, maybe hundreds, stacked two and three high where the ground allowed, sunk to their rims where it didn't. Rust had eaten through most; several had split along their seams like overripe fruit, and the soil around them was stained in halos of orange and black and a chemical white that looked wrong against the brown, like frost in July. Some bore stencils he could still read. Others had been painted over—painted over, he noted, not scraped, a coat of gray slopped on in haste—and beneath the gray, in places where the rust had won, the older markings ghosted through.

He knelt by one and worked it out letter by letter. *TAILINGS — R-7 SOLVENT — DO NOT BURY.*

Somebody had buried them anyway.

"They didn't even try," Astrid said behind him. Her voice cracked on the last word and she steadied it. "They didn't even *try* to hide it right. They just—" She gestured at the whole bowl, the whole poisoned acreage. "They figured nobody would ever come."

"Nobody was supposed to," Jonah said. "That was the plan."

* * *

The core shed held the logs.

They were in a steel cabinet that someone had wedged shut with a two-by-four, and when Astrid pulled the board free the door groaned open on hinges gone to orange felt. Inside: cardboard boxes, warped with damp, filled with drill core logs in binders, assay reports, shipping manifests, daily reports in the blocky handwriting of men who'd been paid to write them.

Jonah pulled a binder at random and opened it, and it took him a full minute to understand what he was looking at, because the numbers were fine. Clean columns, tidy figures, assays well within limits, water samples stamped APPROVED. It took him a minute because his eye kept snagging on the wrongness underneath, the way you notice a painting hung slightly crooked before you can say why.

Then he saw it. The ink changed.

Page after page in blue ballpoint, and then—mid-season, same week, same crew chief's initials—a switch to black, and the black pages were the ones with the beautiful numbers. He flipped forward. Blue. Black. Black. Black. Blue again for one day, then black to the end. And on one of the blue pages, squeezed small in the margin as if the writer had been afraid to even write it:

*Core from 9-B does NOT match lab sheet. Asked Vic. Told to log what we're given.*

"Vic," Jonah said aloud. "Vic somebody signed off on all the black ones."

"Astrid." He looked up. She'd gone very still in the doorway, and she was holding another binder, and her thumb was resting on a signature line without reading it, because she already knew whose name would be there. "Whose initials are on the daily reports?"

"E.C.," she said. "Elias Crane. Core technician, Camp Nine, season of—" She looked up at Jonah. "He logged it straight for most of the season. Then he started logging what they gave him. And then near the end—" she turned pages fast, hunting, "—here. Here."

The last entries weren't logs at all. They were notes, dated, initialed, each one a sentence or two, a man building a case the only way he could, in the margins of the lie he was being paid to maintain:

*Aug 14 — Creek below camp running orange since Tuesday. Sampled it myself. Game warden never comes up here anymore. Wonder why.*

*Aug 22 — Vic says barrels go in the pit at night. I asked what pit. Nobody laughs.*

*Sept 3 — Took pictures. Hiding the film in my kit. If anything happens to me it's in the oilskin with the map. Marta will know why.*

Jonah set the binder down with hands that weren't quite steady. Outside, the fog pressed against the broken windows, and somewhere in the alder a raven said one flat syllable and quit.

"He knew," Astrid said. "Right up to the end, he knew exactly what he had and exactly what they'd do about it. And he walked out of here anyway, alone, in October, trying to get it to somebody." She closed the binder gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person. "And we're what he got."

"We're what he got," Jonah agreed. "So let's be enough."

* * *

The pit was north of the quonset, and they smelled it before they saw it.

Not decay—not exactly. Something mineral and wrong, a sour chemical sweetness under the wet-earth smell of the bush, and mixed with it a faint char. When they pushed through the last screen of alder, Jonah stopped so abruptly Astrid walked into his back.

The excavation was maybe forty meters across, a raw scar in the muskeg with its spoil heaped around the rim, and it had been filled in—badly, recently, within the last few seasons, because the fill hadn't settled and the surface was cracked into plates. Contaminated soil, drum casings, drill sludge, whatever wouldn't burn, pushed into a hole in the world and covered with a meter of borrowed dirt. Rain had already begun undoing it. Orange seeps bled from the rim in five places and ran together into a channel that fed, Jonah now understood with a clarity that felt physical, the fishhook creek, which fed the river, which fed every camp and settlement downstream, which fed people.

At the bottom of the far slope, half sunken, half exposed, lay things that were not soil.

He made himself look. Barrels, yes—but also timbers, cable spools, the crushed shell of a pickup truck, and other shapes he catalogued and refused to name, shapes that explained why the fill had been done at night, why Vic had laughed at nothing, why the game warden never came.

"Under the world," Astrid whispered. "He wasn't being poetic."

She unslung the camera then—the old Leica of Crane's, plus her own, both of them wrapped in oilcloth against the wet—and Jonah watched her become someone he'd seen only in fragments over eleven years: Astrid Ravensworth working. No jokes, no bravado, no debt-collector dread riding her shoulders. She moved through the site with a methodical patience that would have shamed a surveyor, photographing the drum rows with their stencils legible, the stained seeps, the cracked fill, the creek confluence, the log cabinet, the individual pages, the margin note, the signature line. She shot the same evidence twice, once with each camera, and shot a third frame of anything critical, and she wrote locations in a waterproof notebook in handwriting so small and square it looked machined.

Jonah did what he could carry. He pried loose a drum stencil plate with his knife and wrapped it. He took one binder—the damning one, blue-and-black—and a handful of loose manifests showing solvent shipments to Camp Nine that appeared on no reclamation filing anywhere. He photographed the tailings pond outflow where it cut the bank, and he measured the channel with a tape because Astrid told him to and because measurements were the kind of truth that photographs couldn't be argued away from.

The light was going. Neither of them suggested stopping.

It was nearly dark when Astrid stood at the rim of the pit for the last frames, and Jonah came up beside her and saw that her jaw was set hard and her eyes were bright and furious, and that she was shaking—not from cold.

"Say it," he said quietly.

"It's not gold." Her voice came out low and ragged. "All of it—all of this, the whole valley, the river, the town drinking it—this is what my debt bought. My fuel. My license fines. Every gallon of gas I've burned in two years, some percentage of it came out of *that hole.*" She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, camera and all, careless. "I flew supplies for them, Jonah. I ferried their men. I kept my mouth shut and my head down and told myself I was just a pilot, pilots don't ask questions, and the whole time I was hauling rope for the people who dug this."

"I know," Jonah said.

"You don't get to absolve me."

"I'm not." He turned her gently by the shoulder until she faced him, fog and failing light all around, the pit breathing its sweet poison breath behind them. "I'm telling you the weight doesn't get lighter by putting it down. It gets lighter by carrying it somewhere. So we carry it. All the way in. That's the only move that means anything."

For a long moment she just breathed. Then she nodded, once, and began wrapping the cameras, and the shaking stopped, and that frightened him more than the shaking had.

* * *

They made cold camp two ridges east of the bowl, off the bearing line in case anyone came looking, in a stand of jackpine with a view down a long corridor of fog. No fire. Cold beans from a tin, passed back and forth. Astrid slept first, negatives sewn into her jacket lining, the jacket folded under her head like something sacred, and Jonah sat up with the rifle across his knees and listened to the country breathe.

That was when he heard it.

Not engines—he'd have known engines, would have felt them in his teeth before his ears. This was smaller. A click of metal on metal, far off, down toward the creek corridor. Then silence. Then, much later, a single flashlight beam sweeping the fog above the treeline, pale and brief as a shooting star, moving the way men move when they're searching a grid.

Corliss's men weren't behind them anymore.

They were ahead.

Jonah put his hand on Astrid's shoulder and shook her awake, and when her eyes opened he put one finger to his lips and pointed down-valley, toward Current, toward home, toward the only direction left that wasn't watched.
