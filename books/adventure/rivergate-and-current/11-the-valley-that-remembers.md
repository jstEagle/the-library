## 11. The Valley That Remembers

Marta Crane had buried a husband on a Tuesday and lied to a company man on Thursday, and she found the second thing harder.

Not because lying came hard to her. She'd been married to Elias for thirty-one years; she could lie in her sleep. It was because Corliss didn't look like a man you lied to. He looked like a man who already knew what you were going to say before you said it, and was simply waiting to see how badly you mangled it.

He sat at her kitchen table with his hat off and his hands folded, like a minister paying a condolence call. That was the trick of him, she decided. He dressed grief in respectability. Grey suit, grey eyes, a voice like a radio announcer reading good news. He'd brought flowers — store-bought, wrapped in cellophane, already wilting in the warmth of the stove. He'd set them on the table between them as if they meant something.

"Mrs. Crane," he said. "I want to start by saying how sorry I am. Elias was known to us. A fine prospector. Rivergate valued his work."

"Elias hadn't worked for Rivergate in six years," Marta said. She kept her hands busy — there was bread to knead, though she'd already kneaded it twice — because hands that looked busy looked honest. "You let his claim lapse. You let his insurance lapse too. I've got the letters."

Corliss smiled the way frost forms, slowly and all over. "Companies are large organisms, Mrs. Crane. Sometimes the left hand doesn't know—"

"I know what the left hand does." She turned the dough out onto the floured board. "The left hand writes the cheques and the right hand burns the paper. You're the right hand, Mr. Corliss. Don't insult me with the sermon."

For a moment — just a moment — the grey eyes went flat and cold as slate, and Marta saw the thing underneath the polish. Then it was gone, smoothed away like a wrinkle under a warm palm, and he was the sympathetic stranger again.

"You're angry," he said gently. "That's natural. Grief makes us suspicious of everyone, even people trying to help."

"What kind of help did you have in mind?"

"Practical help." He reached into his breast pocket and produced an envelope, thick, unsealed, and laid it on the table beside the dying flowers. "Elias spent his last season working alone upriver. Alone is a dangerous way to live. There may be equipment of his out there. Records. Personal effects. Things a widow would want back, and things a company would want to account for — tools, survey gear, that sort of thing. Rivergate is prepared to cover the cost of a recovery expedition. Generously. And to compensate any party who assists in locating... materials."

"Materials," Marta repeated.

"Property," Corliss said. "Company property. Elias carried some items that were never signed back in. We'd like them returned. Nothing more sinister than tidiness, Mrs. Crane."

She punched the dough down, hard, and thought about the oilskin packet she had not yet opened, sitting in the flour tin where no man in a grey suit would ever think to look. Elias had always said the best safe in the north was a woman's kitchen. *Nobody searches a flour tin, Marta. They search mines.*

"And if somebody found these materials," she said, "and didn't bring them to you?"

The kitchen went very quiet. Outside, the river muttered against the pilings the way it always did, but inside, the silence had weight. Marta kept her eyes on the dough.

"Then that person would be withholding property," Corliss said, "and Rivergate would be obliged to pursue the matter through channels. Legal channels first." He paused, letting the word *first* sit there like a stone dropped in still water. "We're reasonable people. But we are thorough people. Thoroughness is how we built what we built."

"You poisoned what you built on," Marta said. "That's how you built it."

The silence changed quality again. When she finally glanced up, Corliss was watching her with an expression of mild, almost paternal disappointment, and she understood that she had just told him something he'd only suspected: that Elias had talked, that the widow knew, or half-knew, or knew enough to be dangerous.

"That's a serious accusation," he said softly.

"It's a dead creek," Marta said. "Go look at Miller's Creek sometime. Take a cup of the water home to your wife. Let your children drink it for a season. Then come back and tell me about accusations."

* * *

He stayed another hour. That was the worst part — the patience of him. He drank the coffee she gave him because refusing would have been a tell, and he asked gentle questions, one after another, each one innocent on its own and damning in sequence, the way a drill works through rock.

Where had Elias outfitted last? Rivergate, same as every year. Who flew him in? She didn't recall. Did he keep a journal? Elias couldn't spell *creek* without asking. Photographs? Elias took pictures of everything — rocks, mostly. Where would those be? In a box somewhere, probably. Where? Where boxes go.

Corliss wrote nothing down. That frightened her more than a notebook would have. A notebook could be stolen. Whatever Corliss kept was kept behind his eyes.

When he finally rose to leave, he put on his hat and stood a moment in her doorway, looking out at the river, at the town of Current strung along its bank like beads on a wire — the cannery, the church, the airfield with its two hangars and its mud runway.

"This valley remembers things," he said, conversationally. "Floods. Fires. Bad winters. It's remarkable, really, how much it forgets too, given time. Rivers are nature's great forgivers, Mrs. Crane. Everything washes downstream eventually. Even grief." He settled the hat. "Even evidence."

"Is that a threat or a philosophy?"

"A hope," Corliss said. "Mine." And then, turning back to her with the sympathy restored like a mask tied on: "You'll call the company office if you find anything of Elias's. Anything at all. The number's on the card. Day or night."

Marta walked him to the gate because her mother had raised her to see guests off properly, and because she wanted to watch which way he went. He crossed the street to the Northern Air office, where a young clerk jumped up so fast he knocked over his chair, and Marta noted that too. Fear moved through this town the way weather moved through it, and Corliss walked in the middle of it dry.

* * *

That evening she opened the flour tin.

The oilskin packet was where she'd hidden it, beneath the flour and the sugar, wrapped the way Elias had wrapped everything he cared about — three layers, tied with waxed cord, sealed against water the way a man seals things when he expects them to get wet. Her hands shook getting the cord loose. Not fear, exactly. Something adjacent to fear. The shaking of a woman picking up a weight she can feel but cannot yet measure.

Inside: a roll of film canisters, twenty-some, each labelled in Elias's blocky carpenter's hand. *Miller Ck. Oct 4.* *Camp Nine, tailings pond.* *Dead game, draw above the fork.* And beneath them, folded into eighths, pages torn from a school exercise book, covered in his writing — the spelling bad, the sentences blunt, the meaning clear as ice water.

*They know I'm taking pictures. Foreman asked me straight out Tuesday. Told him I photograph rocks. He laughed. Wrong kind of laugh.*

*Drums at Camp Nine buried, not hauled out. Counted forty-one. Logs say twelve shipped south. Do the arithmetic yourself.*

*Creek below the site runs orange in the morning. Fish floating by noon. I fished this water with my father.*

*If anything happens to me it wasn't the cold and it wasn't a bear. Put that in whatever paper will print it.*

Marta sat at her kitchen table in the lamplight and read every page twice, and when she finished the second reading she folded them back into their eighths along the same creases, careful as folding a flag, and put them back in the oilskin, and put the oilskin back in the flour tin, and then she sat with her hands flat on the table and listened to the river.

Everything washes downstream eventually, Corliss had said.

Yes, she thought. It does. And what you bury upstream comes down too, sooner or later, whether you want it or not.

* * *

The trouble was that Astrid Ravensworth had flown out four days ago with a map she should never have given her, and hadn't come back, and the weather had turned, and every hour that passed Marta imagined the Beaver down somewhere in the fog-choked canyons, or worse, found — because Corliss had not come to Current by accident. Companies like Rivergate never did anything by accident. He knew Elias had gone out with a pilot. He knew the pilot was Astrid. He probably knew the colour of the airplane's paint.

So when the knock came at her door the next morning — two knocks, polite, and then Corliss's voice saying *Mrs. Crane? Might I impose again?* — she was ready. Being ready, she had learned from Elias, was nine-tenths of courage. The other tenth was breathing.

She let him in. She made coffee. She let him ask his questions, and this time she answered some of them.

"You asked where Elias's photographs might be," she said, stirring sugar into his cup herself, an old widow's courtesy, disarming as a smile. "I've been thinking on it. Memory's a strange thing. It comes back in pieces."

Corliss leaned forward a fraction of an inch. A man less practised would have lunged.

"He had a cache," Marta said. "Up the old winter trail past Kettle Falls. He used it for years — gear, samples, things he didn't want stolen at camp. If he had papers worth keeping, that's where they'd be. He never trusted camps. Said camps had too many ears."

It was a good lie because it was built on truth. There *was* a cache past Kettle Falls; she'd helped him stock it a decade ago, before the trail washed out. Getting there would take Corliss's men two days' hard travel through country that ate strangers, and when they arrived they'd find a rotted tarpaulin, a rusted stove, and nothing else, because the spring floods had taken the rest years since. Two days. Maybe three, if the rain held.

Two days was a lifetime, in this kind of war.

Corliss asked for the location and she gave it — roughly, the way a grieving woman would remember it, a bearing here, a landmark there, enough to be credible and just enough wrong to cost them a day finding the correction. He thanked her. He did not write it down, but at the door he spoke quietly to the man waiting by the truck, a big silent fellow named Brandt who had been watching her windows since yesterday, and Marta read lips well enough to catch *Kettle Falls* and *first light.*

After the truck pulled out she stood in her doorway a long time, watching the river run brown and high with the rains, carrying everything downstream — silt, branches, the autumn's first yellow leaves.

Then she went inside, took the oilskin packet from the flour tin, and sewed it into the lining of Elias's old canvas coat, the one hanging by the door that no searcher would bother with, because widows wore their dead husbands' coats and nobody looked twice.

It was while she was threading the needle that she heard the airplane.

A single engine, high and thin, coming upriver against the wind — and her heart lifted before her mind caught up, because she knew that sound, knew the particular lazy beat of a de Havilland running rich, a sound she'd heard a thousand times over thirty years of bush flying. She was out the door and squinting into the low cloud before she remembered to be afraid.

The plane came out of the murk over the river, low, mud-streaked, flying like something wounded — and behind it, far back down the valley, another engine droned, steadier, purposeful, closing.

Marta stood on her step with the needle still in her hand and understood that the two days she'd bought had just been spent, all at once, and everything now depended on what happened in the next ten minutes, on the mud strip, in front of God and Corliss's watchers and the whole remembering valley.

She put on Elias's coat and started walking toward the airfield.
