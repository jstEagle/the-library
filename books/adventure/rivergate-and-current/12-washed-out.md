## 12. Washed Out

The river told them first.

Astrid heard it before dawn, a sound like a freight train running where no track had ever been laid, and she was out of the tent with her boots half-laced before her mind caught up to what her body already knew. The Kettle had risen. In the grey light it wasn't a river anymore but a brown moving land, whole trees cartwheeling down the current with their roots waving like drowning men, and the gravel bar where they'd cached their gear the night before was gone—simply gone, as if the river had reached up in the dark and taken it back.

"Jonah!" Her voice cracked against the rain. "Jonah, the strip!"

He came out of the tent fast, jacket over his sleep shirt, hair plastered flat, and stood beside her staring at what had been, twelve hours ago, a decent enough gravel landing strip on the high bench above the water. The high bench. She watched the water climb the last three feet of it, curl, and take another mouthful of bank, and she felt something in her chest go cold and quiet, the way it did when an engine coughed on takeoff and you had half a second to decide what you were going to do about it.

The Otter was tied down forty yards up the strip. Forty yards that were, at the rate the river was eating, going to be underwater inside the hour.

They ran. There was no discussion, no plan, just the two of them floundering through rising water already shin-deep and pushing hard, the rain coming sideways out of a sky the color of dishwater. The plane sat nose-down-slightly, tail toward the flood, tied to two spruce stumps and a deadfall Astrid had chosen herself and now cursed herself for choosing. She got the tail tie-down off first. The knot had swelled and she had to work it with her teeth, river water surging around her calves, cold enough to make her bones ache.

"Cut it," Jonah shouted, and pressed his Leatherman into her hand, and she sawed through the wet rope while he fought the anchor at the wing.

The Otter came free with a groan. Moving it was like pushing a house. They got it twenty feet up the strip, thirty, the water chasing them the whole way, and then the bank let go.

It didn't crumble. It *slumped*, a whole slab of the strip's edge sliding into the flood with a sound like a giant swallowing, and the plane lurched, one wheel dropping into a crack that hadn't existed ten seconds earlier. Astrid threw her shoulder against the fuselage and felt the whole aircraft tilt, felt the gear leg take the weight wrong, heard the metal complain in a voice she knew too well.

"Left main's in a hole," Jonah gasped. "Jack it—no, there's no time—"

"Brace it. Anything. Logs, rocks, *anything*."

They built a crib of driftwood and stones under the axle like children building a fort against a tide that did not care. The water reached the wheels. Then the wheel pants. Then, by the time the rain finally thinned toward mid-morning, it stood a hand's depth below the door, lapping at the tires, patient as a creditor.

Astrid sat on the float of the overturned canoe they'd dragged up to the tent site and looked at her airplane standing in a lake that had eaten its runway, and she did the arithmetic every pilot knows and hates: no strip, no takeoff, and a left gear leg that had taken a load it was never built to take. Even if the water dropped—and it would, eventually, rivers always did—she wasn't flying that plane out without a jack, a level patch of gravel, and a long look at the gear attach points. Maybe not even then.

"We're all right," Jonah said. He was wringing out his shirt, shivering, trying to make it sound like a weather report. "Tent's dry. We've got food for—"

"Six days. Seven if we're honest about it." She looked at the water. "Radio's in the plane."

They both looked at the Otter. The radio was mounted low in the panel. The water in the cabin, when Jonah waded in and opened the door, stood over the rudder pedals. He got the battery out and set it on the bench seat to dry, and he got the handheld spare from the survival kit, and when he keyed the mic that evening the only answer was a hiss like a snake in dry grass. The antenna lead had been underwater too long. Everything electronic on that airplane had drowned in its sleep.

* * *

The second day the water dropped six inches. The third day it came back up.

Astrid spent those days the way she spent every grounded day of her life, which was badly. She crawled under the Otter with a flashlight in her teeth and looked at the left gear attach fitting until her neck screamed, and what she saw there she didn't say out loud, because saying it would make it true. A crack. Hairline, maybe nothing, maybe everything. The kind of thing you found during an annual and grounded yourself for a month over, except there was no annual up here, no mechanic, no hangar, nothing but a bent screwdriver and a man who loved her kneeling in the mud beside her saying, "Show me again."

She showed him again. He was quiet a long time.

"It might hold," he said.

"It might."

"For a takeoff."

"For a takeoff." She sat back on her heels. "And a landing somewhere else."

He didn't answer that, because there was nothing to answer with. They both knew the shape of it: you could ask a cracked fitting to hold once, maybe. You could not ask it twice. Whatever runway they left from would have to be the last one, and the river had eaten theirs.

That evening she got out the oilskin packet and the film canisters and counted them again, the way she'd counted them every night since Camp Nine, as if the number might change, as if counting were a kind of prayer. Eleven canisters. Crane's letters, wrapped in waxed paper inside her jacket. The map, soft as cloth now from handling. She spread it on the tent floor by lantern light and looked at the penciled annotations in the dead man's hand, the little crosses where he'd stood with his camera, the dates. *July 14. Creek running orange. July 22. No game sign, six miles of dead water. August 3. They came. Told me to stop. I did not stop.*

"You should sleep," Jonah said.

"So should you."

"One of us should." He lay down on his pad, fully dressed, boots on, the way he'd slept since Camp Nine, and she lay down beside him and stared at the tent roof and listened to the river, and somewhere in the small hours she slept too, her hand on the jacket with the negatives in it, because she'd learned in the last two weeks that she could not sleep without it.

* * *

The shortwave saved their sanity, if not their skins.

It was Jonah's idea to try the trapper's set—old Sorensen's cabin was a half-day's walk down the valley, abandoned since spring, and the man had been a ham operator back in the day, or so the story went. They hiked down through the drowned alder on the fourth morning, fording two creeks that had been trails a week before, and found the cabin standing in a yard of silt and broken brush like a ship run aground. The radio inside was a museum piece, a tube set with a hand-crank charger, and Jonah spent two hours with it, tapping the crystal, swearing at it gently the way he swore at engines, until a voice swam up out of the static like a fish rising.

It was the regional broadcast relay out of Fort Nelson, reading the news on the hour, and they huddled over it in the cold cabin with the rain drumming the tin roof, and the voice read the weather, and the markets, and then it read this:

*"...Rivergate Mining officials confirmed today that prospector Elias Crane, whose body was recovered from the Current dock last month, died as the result of a solo hunting accident. A company spokesperson stated that Crane had been trespassing on leased claim lands and that an investigation is underway into the activities of 'unauthorized pilots' believed to have flown into the restricted valley in recent weeks. The spokesperson declined to comment on reports of water contamination, calling such reports 'unfounded and malicious.' In related news, the company announced a new community fund for the settlement of Current, to be administered at the company's discretion—"*

Jonah reached over and shut it off.

The silence in the cabin was louder than the static had been. Astrid stood very still, looking at the dead radio, at the rain on the window, at her own reflection ghosted in the glass—thin, rain-dark, a stranger wearing her face.

"Trespassing pilots," she said.

"Yeah."

"They're building it already. The whole story. Crane's a trespasser, we're the trespassers, and by the time anyone asks a real question, we're the ones on trial." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used on creditors, and hated it, and couldn't stop it. "They killed him, Jonah. Whatever did it—the water, the exposure, the company men who ran him out—they killed him, and now they're writing him a headstone that says *he asked for it*."

Jonah didn't try to calm her down. That was one of the thousand things she loved about him; he never once had tried to talk her out of a feeling. He just said, "Marta's in Current. With Corliss's people all over the town. And she doesn't know we're alive."

The thought landed in Astrid's chest like a stone. Marta, alone in that town, holding the same silence they were holding, waiting for word of a husband's killer and a pilot who'd promised a dying man a flight. And here they sat, forty miles of drowned country away, with the only proof in the world sewn into a jacket, and a radio that could receive but not speak, and an airplane with a cracked heart standing in a flood lake.

"We could key the mic in bursts," Jonah said. "Morse. Somebody listening might—"

"Who? Who's listening that isn't also listening for Corliss?" She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "Every hour we sit here, the story gets more solid. Every hour, we get more guilty. We're not witnesses anymore, Jonah. We're fugitives. That's what they've made us, and they did it with a press release and a flood."

The fury came up in her then, all at once, the way floodwater comes—silent, then everywhere. Fury at Corliss, at Rivergate, at the smooth-voiced man on the radio reading their obituary as a news item. Fury at Crane for dying and leaving her his war. Fury at herself, most of all, at the woman who'd spent ten years flying under the radar, keeping her head down, keeping her debts quiet, believing that invisibility was safety—because here was the bill for all of it, come due at last, and the currency was a dead man's name.

"I'm done," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "I'm done being careful. When the water drops, I'm flying that plane out of here on that cracked fitting if I have to, and I'm flying it straight to whoever will listen, and if it comes apart over the mountains, then it comes apart, but I will not sit in a drowned cabin while they bury Elias Crane twice."

Jonah looked at her across the dead radio, rain-light on his face, and he didn't say *you'll kill us both*, and he didn't say *there has to be another way*. He said, "Then we'd better get the gear leg right. Because if we're doing this, we're doing it once."

* * *

They worked on the plane for two days.

Jonah jacked the left main with a come-along rigged to a spruce and a chain, inching the wheel up out of the mud, and Astrid lay under the fuselage in the wet gravel with the crack six inches from her eyes and did what could be done, which was not much. She cleaned it. She drilled a stop hole at each end, the old airframe trick, to keep it from running. She wrapped it with safety wire, not because wire would hold anything but because doing something was better than doing nothing, and because her hands needed the work or they would have started shaking.

The water fell. Slowly, grudgingly, the way a debtor pays, but it fell. By the sixth day the strip's high end was dry, a hundred and forty feet of gravel between the tent and the drop-off where the river still gnawed the bank. A hundred and forty feet. An Otter needed three hundred on a good day with no load and no wind.

"It can't be done," Jonah said, standing at the top of the strip, pacing it off. "Not with gear. We strip her to the frame—no survival kit, no camp, nothing but us and fuel enough to reach Current—and even then it's a coin flip."

"Then we take the coin flip."

"Astrid."

She turned and looked at him, and everything she'd been carrying for two weeks was in her face, and she let him see all of it. "They said *trespassing pilots* on the radio, Jonah. That's us. That's my name they're saying. I spent ten years making sure nobody said my name at all, and now the only place it exists is in a lie." She looked down the drowned strip, at the brown water sliding past the bank, patient, certain, taking its time the way rivers and companies do. "I promised a dying man I'd fly him out alive. I was six hours late. I'm not going to be late for the rest of it."

He was quiet a long moment. Then he said, "We'll need a calm morning."

"The forecast said high pressure building."

"The forecast said a lot of things." But he was already walking toward the plane, already pulling the tie-downs, because that was Jonah: he argued with her about the weather and then he started rigging for it.

They spent that evening stripping the Otter to its bones. Out came the camp gear, the spare parts, the canoe, everything but the survival kit's first-aid tin and two days of food. Jonah drained every ounce of fuel they could spare from the ferry tanks to lighten her, keeping just enough to reach Current and circle twice. Astrid sat in the cabin with the lantern and wrote, on the back of one of Crane's letters, a note she addressed to no one and everyone: *If this plane doesn't reach Current, the evidence is in the jacket of Astrid Ravensworth, last seen flying south up the Kettle Valley. Rivergate poisoned the water. Check the creeks. Check Camp Nine. Ask Marta Crane what her husband died of.* She folded it into the oilskin with the map, so that even the wreck would testify.

The last thing she did, before they rolled out their bedrolls in the tent, was take Crane's compass out of her pocket and set it on the instrument panel, where it caught the lantern light like a small brass heart.

"High pressure by morning," Jonah said, zipping the tent.

"Then we fly at first light."

She slept, for the first time in days, without dreaming of water. She dreamed of the strip instead, dry and long in morning light, longer than it was, and an engine pulling clean, and a voice on the radio in Current saying words she couldn't make out yet, but friendly, friendly, coming closer.

She woke before dawn to silence. The rain had stopped. Through the tent's open flap the sky over the eastern ridges was clearing in long grey-gold bands, and the river, for the first time in a week, had fallen back inside its banks, muttering, spent.

And down the valley, faint across the water, came a sound that stopped her heart and started it again: an engine. Not theirs. Aircraft engine, high and steady, working up the Kettle in the clear morning air, coming their way.

Jonah was already up beside her, listening. "That's not a Beaver," he said quietly. "That's a twin."

She knew what company flew twins up dead valleys at dawn. She looked at the compass on the panel, at the hundred and forty feet of gravel, at the sky opening like a door—and started counting seconds until the engine came in sight.
