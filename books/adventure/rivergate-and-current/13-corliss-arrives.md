## 13. Corliss Arrives

The Cessna came up the valley at nine in the morning, riding the fog line the way a knife rides a throat—flat, deliberate, unhurried. Corliss watched it from the gravel bar where the flood had chewed away half of Camp Nine's old landing strip, and he thought: *there*. That was how you flew when someone else was paying for the fuel. That was how you flew when you'd stopped believing the weather could kill you because the paperwork already had.

He stood with his hands in his coat pockets and let the plane land itself. His men flanked him at a respectful distance—Dade on his left, big and patient as furniture; the Kowalczyk brothers behind him, twins who spoke to each other in a shorthand that made other hired men nervous. Four was enough. Five would have looked like an army, and armies invited armies. Corliss had spent nineteen years learning the arithmetic of presence. You never brought more force than the situation could pretend not to notice.

The Cessna taxied up the broken strip, wheels finding the firm gravel between the washouts, and shut down. The door opened. Astrid Ravensworth climbed out with a rifle slung across her back like she'd forgotten it was there, which was either carelessness or theater, and Corliss suspected theater. She looked worse than the reports had said. Thinner. A cut healing along her jaw. Hair tied back with what appeared to be electrical wire.

Good, he thought. Tired people negotiated.

"Mr. Corliss." Her voice carried flat across the gravel. "You're a long way from a lawyer."

"I'm exactly where the lawyers told me to be." He smiled—the one that worked on board members and widows alike, calibrated warmth, nothing wasted. He crossed the distance between them at a walk, hands still visible, still empty. Everything about him said *I am not a threat*, which was true in the way that mattered. Threats were for people who hadn't yet been bought. "Miss Ravensworth. You look like hell."

"Flood did that."

"The flood did a lot of things." He glanced past her shoulder at the treeline, where a second figure had emerged—a broad-shouldered man with a mechanic's forearms and the particular watchfulness of someone standing between a woman and trouble. Jonah Reyes. Corliss knew the file. Everyone at Rivergate who mattered knew the file. "And your friend. Mr. Reyes. How is the Otter? I hear she took water."

"She'll fly."

"They always say that." He let the silence sit for three seconds. Silence was a tool too, and most people rushed to fill it with something they'd regret. Astrid didn't. She just looked at him with those gray pilot's eyes, waiting, and Corliss revised his estimate of her upward by a notch. Pity. It always cost more when they had spine.

"You're wondering why I'm here," he said.

"I'm wondering why you flew four hours over terrain that eats planes to stand on a gravel bar and tell me something you could have said on the radio."

"Because some things shouldn't be on the radio." He turned, gesturing with one open hand toward the valley upstream—the drowned strip, the brown water sliding fast over its banks, the wall of spruce going up into cloud. "Look at this place. Do you know what it was before Rivergate? Nothing. Mosquitoes and muskeg and men like Elias Crane chasing color in the creek beds. We built roads. We built the camp at Current, the school, the clinic. We put two hundred people to work who'd have been trapping pine marten for cigarette money. That's not rot, Miss Ravensworth. That's civilization. Civilization has costs."

"So does murder."

The word landed between them and neither of them flinched, and that was the moment Corliss understood the shape of the problem. She wasn't guessing anymore. She wasn't holding a rumor she wanted confirmed. Somewhere in the last week—in the orange creek, in the drill site, in whatever Elias Crane had left behind in that oilskin—she had found certainty. Certainty didn't negotiate well. But certainty could be priced. Everything could be priced. That was the first thing he'd learned working for men whose fortunes were measured in continents.

"Elias Crane died of a gunshot wound," Corliss said quietly. "Self-inflicted, according to the territorial police report filed Tuesday. Despondent. Failing health. A prospector at the end of his rope. It's all very sad, and it's all very final, and it means that whatever he believed he saw out here, he can't testify to it now." He watched her face. Nothing moved in it. "You can, though. And so can Mr. Reyes. Two witnesses to trespassing on Rivergate survey land, in possession of company property removed from a restricted site. That's not journalism. That's theft. That's potentially industrial espionage, if we decide to frame it that way, and we have lawyers who enjoy framing things."

"You're threatening me."

"I'm describing the weather," Corliss said. "You're a bush pilot. You know the difference between weather and threats. Weather doesn't care whether you believe in it."

Behind her, Reyes shifted his weight, and Corliss let him shift. Let him think about the geometry—four men, one rifle, a flooded strip, forty miles of dead river between them and Current. Not that anything would happen. Nothing ever needed to happen. That was the entire art of it. You built the shape of the thing and let people's own imaginations do the wet work.

"Now," Corliss said, and his voice changed—warmer, lower, the register he used for closing. "Here's what I actually flew out here to say."

He laid it out plainly, because plainness read as honesty, and honesty read as safety, and frightened people ran toward both.

"Rivergate will pay two hundred thousand dollars for the film and any documents Crane left. Cash, wired anywhere in the world, no questions, no paper trail—which should tell you something about how clean our books are, but never mind. In addition, the company will settle your outstanding debt with Meridian Credit in full. Today. Before lunch. You'll owe nothing to anyone." He paused, and gave her the smile again, softer now. "And there's a third item. Unofficial. The license complaint filed against you yesterday—it goes away. Withdrawn. Your medical, your certifications, everything current by Friday. You fly again, Miss Ravensworth. Legally. Anywhere you want, carrying anything you want, for anyone but us."

He watched the words go into her. He was good at watching for it—the flicker at the corner of the mouth, the small involuntary breath, the way the eyes went distant doing arithmetic. Two hundred thousand. A debt that had been sitting on her chest like ballast for three years. A license. He'd read her file until he could recite it: grounded eighteen months ago after the medical examiner's ruling she still contested, flying charters under other people's certificates, dodging Meridian's collection letters into towns without post offices. He knew what she dreamed about at night. He'd made a career of knowing what people dreamed about at night, because dreams were the cheapest levers in the world. Men sold their brothers for less than a license.

"That's a lot of money for photographs," Astrid said.

"It's a fair price for closure."

"For *burial*."

"For the difference between a valley that gets cleaned up and a valley that gets litigated for twenty years while every living thing downstream of Camp Nine keeps drinking," Corliss said, and here he allowed himself the truth, or the shape of it, because the shape of the truth was more persuasive than the truth. "You think I'm here to hide a spill? Miss Ravensworth, I'm here to end one. Do you know what happens if those photographs reach a hearing? Lawyers. Injunctions. Discovery. Expert witnesses testifying for a decade about parts per billion. Meanwhile the cleanup order sits in appeals court, and the creeks stay orange, and the town of Current keeps boiling its water, and the shareholders keep paying themselves dividends out of the escrow. I have seen it happen at three other sites. I am offering you the only version of events in which actual human beings get actual clean water inside five years. Take my money, give me the film, and I will personally see remediation funded before Christmas. That is not a threat. That is the best deal anyone in this valley will ever be offered."

It was a good speech. He'd given versions of it before, and it worked maybe six times in ten, because it contained real facts arranged around a hollow center. The remediation fund existed on paper. Whether money ever moved through it depended on men in Calgary who thought of the interior as a line item.

Astrid listened to all of it with her arms folded, and when he finished she was quiet for a long moment, looking out at the river.

"My partner got a fever," she said finally. "Walking out of your valley. Carrying Crane's evidence through muskeg while your men burned our camp behind us. He's lying in a trapper's cabin right now shaking hard enough to rattle the bunk frame. What does that cost in your arithmetic?"

Corliss felt something pass across his face—he killed it quickly, but not quickly enough, and he saw her catch it. Not guilt. He'd buried guilt somewhere around his second year with the company, next to his first marriage and his belief in boards of directors. It was closer to recognition. She had named the exact cost he'd hoped she wouldn't itemize, and itemized costs were harder to buy than abstract ones.

"Dade," he said, without turning. "Get the kit from the plane."

Dade fetched it—a steel case, and inside it, a satellite phone, a vial case of broad-spectrum antibiotics, a bag of saline, a proper field medical kit worth more than most trappers earned in a year. Corliss set it on a driftwood log between them, deliberately, like a card played face-up.

"Antibiotics, IV fluids, a satphone with a doctor on the other end in Whitehorse. Right now, no strings. Call it a gesture of respect for Mr. Reyes." He held her eyes. "The rest of the deal stands until sundown tomorrow. After that, the offer expires, the complaint proceeds, and Meridian calls your debt in full—which, as of Thursday, includes a default acceleration clause. They'll take the Otter, Miss Ravensworth. The last thing you own in the world."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I fly home, and other people handle it, and they're not like me." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that was the trick of it—the kindness was real, in its narrow way. He genuinely preferred this part to stay clean. "I've done nineteen years of this. I know what 'other people' looks like. It looks like accidents. It looks like a floatplane with a fuel-line fault that nobody investigates because the pilot had debts and enemies and a history of flying drunk, whether or not any of it was true. It looks like witnesses who recant, or move, or stop being reachable. It looks like Marta Crane signing a settlement she doesn't understand because her husband's death benefit is contingent on it." He let that last one hang. He wanted it to hang. Fear traveled faster than greed down bush telegraph lines, and by nightfall the whole territory would know what he'd said here.

"You'd hurt an old woman."

"I would never touch Marta Crane," Corliss said, and meant it. "I'd hurt her *options*. There's a difference, and it's the difference between me and the men I'm trying to keep out of this valley. Which brings me to my last point, and then I'll let you eat whatever it is you people eat out here."

He stepped closer—not into her space, but near the edge of it, close enough that Dade shifted forward and Corliss waved him back with two fingers. Close enough to speak low, under the river noise, so that the words belonged only to her.

"Witnesses are a category of person, Miss Ravensworth, and categories have lifespans. A witness who takes the money stops being a witness and becomes a neighbor. A witness who refuses becomes a problem. And a problem that survives long enough to testify—" he shook his head slowly, almost sorrowfully— "becomes a lesson. I have watched lessons taught in this country for nineteen years. I have signed off on some of them. I am telling you this not because I want to frighten you—I don't, fear makes people stupid—but because I am, genuinely, the best friend you have in this whole rotten arrangement. Take the money. Bury the film. Go be a pilot. In ten years this valley will be green again and no one will remember, and you'll be alive, and free, and flying."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she picked up the medical kit, weighed it in one hand, and set it back on the log.

"Keep the antibiotics," she said. "We'll manage."

"And the money?"

"I've flown cargo for men like you my whole career, Mr. Corliss. Fuel, freight, parts. Things that weigh what they weigh." She shouldered her rifle. "Crane's film weighs what it weighs too. I'm not selling it."

Corliss exhaled through his nose, slow, and nodded as if she'd declined a dinner invitation. Inside, coldly, without drama, he moved her file from the folder marked *Negotiable* to the one marked *Contained*. It was a thin folder. It was always a thin folder. That was the pity of it—people thought containment required imagination, when really it only required patience and a company that owned the weather reports, the fuel contracts, the airfield leases, and two of the three doctors in Current.

"Sundown tomorrow," he said aloud. "The offer stands till then. After that, I never made it."

He turned and walked back toward the Cessna, and his men folded in around him like water closing over a stone. At the cabin door he paused, one boot on the strut, and looked back at her one last time—standing on the broken strip with the flooded river at her back and the burned ruin of her camp smoking faintly in the trees, holding nothing but a rifle and a dead man's certainty.

"One professional courtesy," he called. "Check your fuel filters before you fly. This time of year, there's water in everything."

The engine caught. The Cessna lifted off the ruined strip and climbed into the fog line, and Corliss watched the valley fall away beneath him—the orange thread of the poisoned creek, the brown flood, the two small figures on the gravel bar shrinking to specks—and keyed the radio to the frequency that didn't appear on any flight plan.

"He wouldn't sell," he said. "Both of them. Send Voss and the others up the west bank tonight. And find out where that trapper lives."

He leaned back and closed his eyes, and began composing, in his head, the accident report he hoped he'd never need.
