## 14. Smoke Signal

I smelled the searchers before I saw them. Not the men themselves—men you can smell only up close, sweat and wool and gun oil—but their fire, a day old and two ridges east, woodsmoke threaded with something chemical, kerosene maybe, the way company men build fires when they don't expect to stay long and don't care what they burn.

Astrid smelled it too. She was crouched over the cook fire with a tin cup in her hands, and she went still the way she goes still before turbulence, her whole body listening.

"Jonah," she said.

"I know."

"How many?"

"Can't tell from smoke." I'd been counting all morning without meaning to. The wind had come around in the night, out of the northeast, and it carried things now that it hadn't carried yesterday: the smell of cut pine where nobody had any business cutting pine, the faint clatter of a chain winch, once—just once—a snatch of radio static riding the thermal like a bird. "Three, four men. Maybe a dog."

She set down the cup. "The dog's worse than the men."

"The dog's worse than everything."

We had been at Camp Nine for six days. Six days of photographing rusted drums by gray light, of prying falsified drill logs out of a waterproof strongbox Crane had hidden under the floorboards of his own bunk—Elias Crane, dead on a dock in Current, had been hiding evidence inside his own bed—and of Astrid working the camera with hands so steady it scared me, because I knew what it cost her to be that steady. Six days of shortwave reports crackling through the static: *Crane death ruled accidental. Trespassing pilots suspected of theft at Rivergate sites. Reward offered for information.* Our names hadn't been said yet. They didn't need to be. There were only so many trespassing pilots in this country, and one of them owed Rivergate more money than God.

Now the company had stopped talking and started walking.

* * *

We spent the afternoon getting ready to leave, which meant we spent the afternoon deciding what to burn.

That's the arithmetic nobody warns you about. Everything you carry is weight, and weight is time, and time is the distance between you and men with dogs. The negatives were non-negotiable—thirty-six exposures of orange creeks and floating fish and a grave pit of contaminated soil stamped with Rivergate's own survey markers, plus Elias Crane's letters and the falsified logs, sealed in oilcloth because even dead men know how to keep things dry. Astrid had taken to sleeping with them under her jacket, against her ribs, like a second heartbeat.

Everything else was negotiable. And everything else was evidence of us.

"They'll find the camp whether we burn it or not," I said. We stood at the edge of our little clearing, looking back at it: the lean-to we'd patched with spruce boughs, the fire ring, the cache hung wrong—I'd hung it wrong, three days ago, when my hands were shaking after the flood and the drowned radio, and I'd been kicking myself since. "Question is what they find in it."

"If we burn it clean, they know we knew they were coming." Astrid turned a stick over in her fingers. "If we leave it, they read us like a book. Boots, food, direction of travel."

"So we lie with it."

She looked at me then, and there was something in her face I'd seen only a few times in the years I'd known her—the night she decided to fly into the storm off Kettle Point, the night she signed for the de Havilland with money she didn't have. The look of a woman pricing something she loved and preparing to pay.

"What are you thinking?" I asked, though I already knew. I always knew. That was the trouble with loving Astrid Ravensworth; you spent your whole life one step behind her decisions, watching them arrive.

"I'm thinking," she said, "that Corliss wants the film. He told us that much, standing in the rain with his hat dry and his men wet around him like crows. He wants the film, and he assumes we'll run with it, and he assumes running men travel light and fast and scared." She nodded at the camp. "So let's give him a camp full of scared. Let's give him a fire big enough to see from the river, and boot prints heading north toward the pass, and a cache torn open like we grabbed what we could and bolted."

"And while he's chasing north—"

"We go south. Downstream. Toward Current." She smiled, and it wasn't a happy smile; it was the smile of somebody holding a lit match over everything she owned. "Toward the hearing."

* * *

We worked until dark.

It's strange, the tenderness of destruction. I'd built that lean-to with my own hands four days earlier, in the rain, cursing the spruce boughs and missing my shop in Current with an ache like a tooth. Now I dragged it apart and stacked it for maximum flame, and I caught myself being careful about it, the way you're careful with anything you've made. Astrid scattered the rest of our food along the north trail—pemmican, a sack of flour, even the chocolate she guarded like a dragon, pressed into mud at intervals where a tracker would find it and read *panic*. She took her spare boots and walked a quarter mile north in them, then came back barefoot through the creek to kill her tracks, shivering so hard by the end I had to hold her upright.

"You're going to lose those toes," I told her, chafing her feet between my hands.

"I'll buy new ones," she said through her teeth, "with Corliss's blood."

The negatives we sewed into her jacket that evening, by the last flat light, using sail twine from the canoe repair kit. She held the jacket open while I stitched, and I will tell you honestly what that was like: kneeling in the dirt with a needle in my fingers, sewing thirty-six photographs of corporate murder into the lining of the jacket of the woman I loved, while somewhere east of us men with dogs closed like a hand. My stitches weren't pretty. I'm a mechanic, not a tailor. But I went over every seam twice, and then a third time along the spine, because if a stitch failed in the muskeg or the rapids or the dark, it wouldn't be thread we lost.

"Hold still," I said, though she was holding still. It was my hands that wanted to shake.

"You missed a spot," she said softly, "right over my heart."

"That's not a spot. That's structural."

"Stitch it anyway."

So I did. A little cross of twine over the left breast, under the lining, where no one would ever see it and both of us would always know. She put the jacket on and rolled her shoulders, testing, and the oilcloth packet rode against her ribs without a whisper.

"How does it feel?" I asked.

"Like armor," she said. "Heavy armor."

* * *

We fired the camp an hour after full dark.

Kerosene on the lean-to, kerosene on the cache—we tore it open first, made it look raided, flung Crane's tin plates into the brush—and a handful of embers raked into the fire ring for honesty. Then I touched the match to the lean-to and the whole clearing went up at once, dry spruce burning like a promise kept, and the heat hit my face from twenty feet and the sparks climbed into the black like they had somewhere to be.

We stood at the treeline and watched it. You don't get to watch your own life burn very often. There was my tarp, gone. My tool roll, gone—six years of files and feeler gauges, gone in ninety seconds. Astrid's spare charts, her father's old logbook she'd carried since I'd known her, the paperback novel she'd been reading aloud to me at night in her pilot's voice, doing all the characters, even the women, especially the women. Gone. All of it curling up orange into the dark.

"Say something," she said quietly.

"It's just stuff."

"It's not just stuff, Jonah. Say the true thing."

I thought about it. The fire cracked and spat. Somewhere far off, the dog barked once, a thin sound, and neither of us moved.

"The true thing," I said, "is that I've never owned anything I couldn't walk away from. Until lately." I looked at her—at the jacket, at the shape of the truth riding under the canvas against her heart. "Turns out the thing I can't walk away from isn't mine."

She didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice was rough. "Get used to it. Come on. Creek line, south, before the light finds us."

* * *

We went south through black timber, single file, her ten feet ahead of me because she'd insisted and because she was better at this than me, which I resented and admired in equal measure. The plan was simple, the way plans are simple right up until they aren't: follow the creek down to the river, reach the trapper's cabin if it existed, take the canoe if it existed, run the river at night and pray. Every part of the plan was a rumor someone had told us. The trapper himself was a rumor. The hearing in Current was a rumor with a date attached.

Behind us, the fire lit the underside of the clouds red, a bruise on the sky, and I let myself hope it was doing its work—pulling eyes north, pulling the dog north, giving Corliss exactly the story he expected to read. Scared pilots. Burning their camp. Running for the pass.

Then the flare went up.

It came from the north, from the direction of our decoy trail, a white star climbing and hanging and dying, and for one breath I thought *it worked, they bought it.* Then a second flare rose, and it rose from the south. From downstream. From between us and the river.

Astrid stopped so fast I nearly walked into her. In the green wash of the burning camp behind us I saw her head tilt, listening, and I heard it too: voices. Not close. Not far. Two, maybe three, spread out along the creek we were supposed to follow, calling to each other in low, unhurried tones—the tones of men who have done this before and know the quarry is tired and cold and out of options.

They'd split their line. Or they'd guessed. Or the dog had circled. It didn't matter which. What mattered was that the creek was a corridor with men in it, and the fire behind us was a wall, and between the fire and the creek was maybe half a mile of deadfall and muskeg and darkness, and that half mile was suddenly the entire world.

"Astrid." I kept my voice below the wind. "Options."

She was breathing fast. I could see the calculation moving through her the way weather moves across water. North was the decoy trail, burned now, blown. East was the searchers' own camp, their boats, their radio. West was high country, rock and ice, three days from anywhere, with a dog on our scent and frost coming.

"West," she said.

"West is nothing."

"West is nothing," she agreed. "Nothing is what we've got. They'll watch the river. Nobody watches nothing." She turned, and in the firelight her face was all planes and shadow, and she put her hand flat against her jacket, over the stitching, over the truth, the way you'd check a wound or a compass. "Jonah. Whatever happens in the next few days—whatever it costs—if I go down, you take the jacket and you keep going. Promise me."

"I'm not promising that."

"Jonah."

"I promise you we both make it to Current," I said. "I promise you Marta Crane gets to put those photographs on a table in front of the federal government. I promise you Corliss chokes on every dollar he ever spent buying this valley. Those promises I'll keep. The other one, no. Never. Ask me again in twenty years and the answer's still no."

For a long moment she just looked at me, firelight and fury and something underneath the fury that I have spent years learning to read and will spend the rest of my life being grateful for.

"Twenty years," she said finally. "All right. Move."

And we turned our backs on the burning camp, on the last of everything we owned in the world, and climbed west into the dark, into the nothing, while behind us the sky glowed red and ahead of us the dog began, very softly, to sing.
