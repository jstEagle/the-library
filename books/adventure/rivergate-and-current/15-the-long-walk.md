## 15. The Long Walk

The rain came in off the muskeg like it had a grudge.

Astrid had stopped counting hours somewhere in the second day. She measured time now in smaller units—the interval between Jonah's shivers, the number of times she checked the sewn seam of her jacket where the negatives rode flat and cold against her ribs, the distance between one hummock of sedge and the next. The muskeg didn't care about hours. It cared about weight and patience, and it tested both with every step.

She'd learned the trick of it by the third mile: never trust the green. The bright sphagnum that looked like solid ground was a skin stretched over black water, and it gave way without apology. You walked the ridges of dead moss, the brown and rust-colored hummocks, planting your feet where the ground had already died and couldn't betray you further. Behind them, somewhere back in the grey curtain of rain, Corliss's men were doing the same walk with better boots and worse intentions.

"How's the pace?" Jonah said, from behind her.

It was the third time he'd asked, and the first two times she'd lied.

"Fine," she said, and then, because the rain made liars cheap and she was tired of being one: "Slow. We're slow."

He didn't answer, and she didn't look back, because looking back meant seeing what she already knew. He was favoring his left leg. He'd been favoring it since the night before, when the ground had opened under him on the slope above the burn site and he'd gone down the rocks with his arm out to keep the film case—her jacket, actually, the jacket with the negatives—away from the scree. He'd landed hard on his hip and thigh, laughed it off the way he laughed everything off, and gotten up with blood running into his boot.

Now the blood was a memory, but the heat wasn't. She'd felt his forehead in the lean-to they'd built from spruce boughs during the worst of the night's rain. It had been like touching a stone left too long near a stove.

"Jonah." She stopped on a hummock and turned. "Show me the leg."

"It's fine."

"Show me the leg or I'll cut the boot off and show myself."

That got a ghost of his grin, the one that had survived siphoning fuel by lantern light and hiding in a wheel well and three days of rain. But the grin cost him something now; she could see the price of it in his face. He sat down on the hummock, worked the boot off with his teeth on the lace, and peeled back the sock.

The thigh was swollen tight as a drum, the skin flushed dark and angry from knee to hip, and there was a red line climbing toward the groin like a fuse.

Astrid crouched and didn't touch it, because she knew what it was and touching it wouldn't help. Cellulitis, her father would have called it, back when he was still alive and still flying medevac out of Fort Nelson and still teaching her the things pilots needed to know about the ground they crashed on. Blood infection. Sepsis if it climbed. Dead in a week out here, dead in three days if it climbed fast.

"When did the fever start?" she asked.

"This morning. Maybe last night." He was looking past her at the rain. "It's not that bad."

"Jonah."

"It's not that bad," he said again, and then, quieter, in the voice he used for true things: "Astrid. We can't stop. They're behind us and the inspector's ahead of us and the film doesn't keep. You know that. I know that. So let's not do the scene where you pretend you have a choice."

She sat back on her heels in the rain and looked at him—this man who had loved her for years without ever once making her feel like a debt, who had come along without being asked twice, who was now sitting in a bog telling her to leave him behind with the same tone other men used for asking to borrow a cigarette.

"I'm not doing any scene," she said. "I'm doing arithmetic."

* * *

The arithmetic went like this.

Option one: she carried him. She was strong—bush flying made you strong in the shoulders and the back—but the muskeg was three days wide at his pace and five at a carry's, and a man with sepsis didn't get better in five days. He got dead. Option two: she left him with the rifle and the last of the food and a bearing to the trapper's line cabin, and she ran for Current alone. He'd have a chance. A small one. Better than the bog. And the film would reach the inspector, which was the only thing that mattered, the thing Elias Crane had died on a dock for, the thing Marta was stalling Corliss for back in a town that smelled of woodsmoke and fear.

Option three didn't exist. Option three was the one where she kept both, and she'd already caught herself building it in her head the way you build a landing on a short strip in a crosswind—knowing the numbers don't work and flying it anyway because the alternative is admitting you're already crashed.

She built option three anyway. She was good at flying things that shouldn't fly.

They walked until the light went grey-green and then grey, and then they stopped under a leaning spruce and she got him out of the wet and into the boughs and gave him the last of the trapper's dried meat and half the painkillers from the first-aid tin, keeping the other half against her own need because she was honest enough with herself to know the rain had a claim on her too.

"Tell me about the first time you saw me," he said, when the dark came down.

"You were under a Cub with grease to your elbows, lying to Old Man Fetter about his engine."

"I wasn't lying."

"You were absolutely lying. You told him the cylinders were fine. They were shot. You were buying him a season."

"I was buying him a season," Jonah agreed. "He had nowhere else to go and nothing else to fly. Some things are worth lying for." He shifted, and she heard him hiss through his teeth. "First time you saw me, though. That's not the first time I saw you. First time I saw you, you were seventeen, landing that beat-up Taylorcraft on the gravel bar by the mill, and you came out of the plane mad at the whole world, and I thought—there. That's the realest thing this town's ever produced."

"You were nineteen and you never said a word for six years."

"I was waiting for you to land."

She laughed, and it came out cracked, and then she wasn't laughing, and the rain filled the silence the way it filled everything, patiently, completely.

"Sleep," she said. "I'll take first watch."

"You don't need—"

"Sleep, Jonah."

* * *

He slept, if you could call it that—half-shivering, half-muttering, his skin burning through the wet wool. Astrid sat with her back against the spruce and the rifle across her knees and watched the dark and did the arithmetic again, because arithmetic was the only prayer she had.

The fever would climb. She'd seen it climb. By tomorrow he'd be raving, and the day after he'd be unable to walk at all, and every hour she spent moving at his pace was an hour Corliss's men gained and an hour the infection gained, and the film in her jacket would rot right along with him unless—

Unless.

Unless there was a fourth option. Not the fantasy option three. A real one.

She thought about the trapper, old Sorensen, who had hidden them in his cabin two nights back and given them the canoe and the shame of his own silence, paid off by Rivergate for twenty years to keep his trapline mouth shut. Sorensen had talked about the river. He'd talked about how everything in this country drained the same direction, how you could fight the muskeg or you could let it carry you. He'd talked about a trading post at the mouth of the Kettle, forty miles downstream of where they'd capsized, where an old friend of his ran a boat with an outboard and no questions.

Forty miles downstream. But they'd lost the canoe in the rapids. And Jonah couldn't walk to the river, let alone paddle it.

Or.

She looked at Jonah, asleep and burning, and she looked at the rain, and she thought about her father, who had once flown a man with a severed hand out of a lake camp in weather that grounded the angels, and who had told her afterward: *The trick isn't deciding what you're willing to lose. The trick is deciding what you're not willing to lose, and then never looking at the rest again.*

She wasn't willing to lose Jonah. She wasn't willing to lose the film. Everything else—the plane, the license, her own hide, her clean record of never owing anybody—everything else was negotiable.

Which meant she needed help. Real help. And there was exactly one direction help could come from, and it was behind them, and it wanted to kill them.

Except.

Except Corliss wanted the film more than he wanted them dead. Dead, they might have passed it to someone. Alive and caught, they were a problem that could be solved. Which meant that if she made herself findable—loudly, deliberately findable, on her own terms, in a place of her choosing—she might buy the one thing money couldn't otherwise purchase in this whole sodden kingdom: time. And a trade.

It was a terrible plan. It was the kind of plan that got people killed. She turned it over anyway, the way you turn over a map in bad light, looking for the bearing that isn't there.

And then she heard the shot.

It came from the southeast, a flat crack swallowed almost whole by the rain, and a second one, and then nothing. Signal or hunting or Corliss's men ranging ahead, and there was no way to know which, and that was the whole horror of this country: every sound meant everything and nothing at once.

Jonah's eyes opened. Fever-bright, but open.

"You heard it," he said.

"I heard it."

"They're close."

"They're close," she agreed. "Which means we stop being slow."

* * *

She got him up. It took twenty minutes and most of her strength and all of his dignity, and she didn't waste a word on gentleness because gentleness was a luxury the muskeg didn't stock. She got his arm over her shoulders and took his weight on her right side and pointed them northwest, away from the shots, toward a low ridge she'd marked on the trapper's hand-drawn sketch—a ridge that ran, if Sorensen's bearings were true, down to the Kettle.

"We can't outrun them," Jonah said, through his teeth, as they moved. "Not like this."

"We're not outrunning them. We're changing the race."

"To what?"

"To a negotiation." And she told him, in fragments, between hummocks, while the rain kept its patient score: the trading post, the boat, the fourth option she was building out of wreckage and nerve. And the part about making themselves findable, if it came to that, on her terms, with the film as the only currency Corliss respected.

Jonah was quiet for a long ten steps. Then he laughed—a real laugh, cracked and feverish and fond.

"There it is," he said. "There's the girl on the gravel bar."

"Don't romanticize me. I'm about to do something stupid."

"You're about to do something *you*," he said. "Same thing." He stumbled; she caught him; they kept moving. "Astrid. If it comes to a trade—Corliss, the film, any of it—you make it without me. You hear me? You trade the film, you trade yourself out, you fly home, and you don't look back at the bog."

"No."

"Astrid—"

"I said no." She stopped, and the rain hammered the spruce around them, and she turned her head so he could hear her over it. "You told me once you were waiting for me to land. Well, I landed, Jonah. Years ago. I've just been circling. And I am not leaving you in a bog to be arithmetic. So save your breath for walking."

He looked at her for a long moment, water running off his jaw, fever making his eyes shine like instrument lights at dusk.

"Copy," he said, pilot to pilot, and it was the most romantic word she'd ever heard.

They walked.

* * *

The ridge came at dusk, a spine of gravel and black spruce running northwest like a road somebody had built for exactly this, and they made better time on it—two miles, three—until Jonah's leg simply quit. No warning, no drama. One step he was moving, the next he was down, and when she got him turned over his eyes had gone glassy and his muttering had turned into words that weren't words.

She got him under the biggest spruce on the ridge and built the bough bed and got the survival blanket around him, and then she sat in the rain and did the last of the arithmetic, the honest kind, the kind that doesn't flatter you.

He had hours, not days, before the fever took him somewhere she couldn't follow. Corliss's men were hours behind, maybe less. The trading post was a day's hard walk away, and she couldn't carry him that far and he couldn't walk it, and the film was warm against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and somewhere south of here Marta Crane was holding a line alone against a man who bought towns the way other men bought drinks.

Astrid Ravensworth had spent her whole adult life flying under other people's radar—under the creditors', the license board's, the law's—staying small, staying invisible, staying safe in the only way she'd ever trusted, which was to be nobody's problem.

She was done being nobody's problem.

She dug the signal flares from the bottom of the pack—three of them, salvaged from the plane's emergency kit, meant for a distress call she'd never planned to send because distress was the one frequency she'd sworn off. She loaded one into the pencil flare, stood up on the ridge in the rain where anyone with eyes could see her, and fired it straight up into the grey.

The red star burst and hung and died, and the rain closed over it, and somewhere out there in the wet dark, she knew, men with rifles had just stopped walking.

"Come on, then," she said, to the rain, to Corliss, to God. "Come and negotiate."

Then she went back down into the boughs, took Jonah's burning hand in both of hers, and waited for the lights to come.
