## 16. Trapper's Mercy

The old man saw them before they saw his cabin.

Henrik Voss had spent forty years reading the bush the way other men read newspapers, and the story the treeline told him that gray morning was simple: two people moving wrong. Too slow. Too quiet. Stopping when a grouse flushed instead of walking on. Nobody crossed the muskeg north of the Dead River unless they were lost, running, or both.

He put down the trap he'd been waxing and stood in the doorway of his cabin, rifle leaning against the jamb out of habit rather than intention. The woman came first—lean, mud to the hips, hair plastered flat by nine days of rain. She carried herself like a pilot even on foot, weight forward, eyes working the sky between the branches as if it might do something to her. Behind her came a big man favoring his left leg, fever-bright, half-carried and half-arguing about being carried.

Voss knew who they were before they reached the clearing. Word traveled slow upriver but it traveled true: a woman pilot and her mechanic, gone into the headwaters after old Elias Crane died, and Corliss's hired men sweeping the country like a comb through wet hair. He also knew what was being paid for information. He had taken Rivergate money once himself. That was the wound he'd been licking for eleven years.

"Inside," he said, before either of them could speak. "Before someone sees your smoke."

The woman stopped at the edge of the clearing, hand going inside her jacket—not for a weapon, he noted, but for something flat and precious she carried against her ribs. Her eyes went over him: seventy if a day, hands like tree roots, a face weathered past honesty or dishonesty into something plainer than either.

"We don't want trouble," she said.

"No," Voss agreed. "You've got plenty."

* * *

Astrid Ravensworth did not trust kindness anymore. Nine days of rain and pursuit had burned that out of her along with most of everything else. Kindness was what Corliss offered in the hangar at Current, a fortune folded soft as a bedsheet. Kindness was what Rivergate called its settlement checks, the ones Marta Crane refused to sign.

So when the old trapper put Jonah on his bunk, fed them smoked whitefish and spruce tea without asking a single question, Astrid sat with her back to the wall and waited for the price.

It came an hour later, after Jonah finally slept—the real sleep of fever breaking, breath going long and even—and the old man sat across from her at the table, turning a tin cup in his ruined hands.

"You're carrying Crane's photographs," he said.

Her whole body went still. "I'm not discussing—"

"I don't want to see them." He said it fast, almost angry. "I know what's on them. I helped put some of it there."

The rain ticked against the shake roof. Somewhere outside, a raven said one word and thought better of it.

"My name is Henrik Voss," the old man said. "Eleven years ago I trapped this valley full-time. Then Rivergate came up the river with drills and drums and a payroll, and a man named Corliss—young then, already smooth—sat where you're sitting and offered me a lease buyout. Good money. More than I'd make in ten winters of pelts. All I had to do was move my line west and stop trapping the creek drainages."

"You took it."

"I took it." He didn't flinch from it. "And I told myself it was just business, same as they told themselves the drums were just storage. Two years later the marten were gone off my new line. Then the beaver, belly-up in the shallows like they'd been poured there. Then the moose stopped coming through the pass, and the fish in the Dead River started floating in the spring freshet, every year more of them, every year earlier." He looked at his hands. "I knew. Don't mistake me—I knew what I'd sold, and I told myself I hadn't seen the proof, and that was the same lie every man in this valley told while the water turned the color of rust."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because three days ago two of Corliss's men stopped here. Bought gas I didn't have, asked about a woman and a lame man moving south. Offered me five hundred dollars for anything I'd seen." His mouth twisted. "I told them I'd seen nothing. And when they left I sat in that chair and understood I'd been given a second chance at the same choice, and I am too old and too ashamed to fail it twice."

Astrid found her voice had gone rough. "You lied to them."

"I lied to them," Voss said. "Now I'm telling you the truth. There's a federal inspector coming to Current. Name of Okafor. Lands and water board, out of the capital. He arrives on the steamer Thursday—four days, maybe five if the river's high. Corliss knows he's coming; the company's been scrubbing records for a month and sweetening the council. What Corliss doesn't know is whether anyone will stand up in front of the man and say what happened here." He leaned forward. "Photographs can be called fakes. Letters can be called forgery. But photographs, letters, and a living witness who took Rivergate's money—that's a different animal. That's a man saying: I was bought, and here is what the buying covered."

"You'd testify?"

"I'd testify." He said it the way a man says a thing he has rehearsed alone for years. "If you get those negatives to Current. If you get yourself there alive."

Astrid looked at Jonah sleeping, at the rain, at the map-shaped hole in their plans where the river had eaten Crane's bearings at the rapids. Four days. Corliss's searchers between here and town. A hundred miles of drowned country.

"And how," she said, "do we get there?"

Voss smiled for the first time, and it made his face younger and sadder at once.

"I keep a canoe," he said, "that Rivergate doesn't know about."

* * *

Jonah Reyes woke to the smell of pitch smoke and the sound of Astrid arguing quietly, which was how she argued when she'd already won but wanted the pleasure of the fight.

"—not leaving you here with a fever while I run a river alone."

"It's not a fever anymore, it's a leg." He sat up on the bunk and the cabin tilted politely and settled. "And you're not running it alone because the old man won't let you, and neither will I. Give me the tea."

Voss handed it over, studying him with frank appraisal. "Can you paddle?"

"I can paddle, I can patch, and I can swear in two languages. Which do you need most?"

"The second, probably." Voss crouched by the cold stove, joints cracking like green wood. "Listen, both of you. The river below my line runs forty miles to the Current fork, mostly dead water, one set of rapids you portage on the left. Corliss's men are watching the trails and the airfield, because they think like men who own roads. They are not watching the water, because nobody runs the Dead River in flood season except fools and Indians and me, and they've priced all three as crazy." He tapped the floorboards. "My canoe's under the woodpile. Cedar, canvas, older than both of you together. She leaks a little and she forgives a lot. You put in at dark, run the first day under the fog, camp on gravel bars, no fire, and you'll smell Current's mill smoke the third night."

"And the searchers?" Astrid asked.

"They're behind you now—they swept north yesterday, chasing the story that you doubled back toward Camp Nine. Somebody fed them that story." He looked mildly innocent, which fooled neither of them. "But hear me. If they pick up your trail again, they won't take you prisoner. Not this far from witnesses. Corliss's problem isn't catching you. It's what happens if you arrive."

Jonah drank the tea and felt it lay itself down inside him like a warm plank. "Then we make sure we arrive."

"One more thing." Voss went to a chest in the corner and came back with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, which he opened on the table: a ledger, water-stained, decades thick. "My trap lines. Every pelt count, every season, going back thirty years. Look at '09 through '11." He turned pages with a finger like a chisel. "Marten, forty-one, thirty-eight, thirty-five. Then the year I signed. Forty-two, forty-six. Then nothing. Zero, zero, zero, season after season, on land that had never been empty since my father worked it. That's not a cycle. Cycles come back. This never came back." He pushed the ledger across. "Numbers written by a man with no reason to lie and every reason to stay quiet. Let your inspector read it."

Astrid touched the ledger the way she'd touched Crane's map, Jonah noticed—like something that might still be alive.

"Why now?" she asked softly. "Eleven years, Henrik. Why carry this alone so long?"

The old man was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence.

"Because Elias Crane knocked on my door last fall," he said. "Asked me the same question you just did. I gave him fish and lies and sent him on his way, and he went up that river alone and got himself killed for what he believed." His jaw worked. "I don't get to be the man who failed him twice. None of us do. That's what I've learned, sitting here with my shame and my smokehouse. Sooner or later the valley asks everyone the same question, and the only mercy in it is answering right the second time."

* * *

They put in at moonset.

The river had risen with the week's rain and ran high and brown and silent, sliding past the alders like something poured. Voss walked them down to the bank through the dripping dark, carrying the bow pack himself though Jonah tried to take it, and held the cedar canoe steady while they loaded: the film sewn flat against Astrid's ribs, Crane's letters in a tin, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, three days of fish and bannock, and the old man's own compass, pressed into Astrid's hand without ceremony.

"You'll want that more than me," he said. "I know these woods by their smell."

Astrid stood in the shallows holding the compass and looked at him—a stranger who had become, in one day, the kind of ally she'd stopped believing in. "Why give us your boat? If Corliss finds out you helped us—"

"He won't find the boat. It'll be fifty miles downstream and scuttled." Voss shrugged. "And if he finds out anyway, then he finds an old man with nothing left to take. That's the one wealth I've got left, girl. Nothing left to lose. Spend it well."

Jonah shook his hand, and the old man gripped hard, and said something low in a language Jonah didn't know—his mother's tongue, maybe, or the river's.

"For luck," Voss translated. "It means: go fast, go quiet, and don't look back until you're past the bend."

Astrid took the stern paddle. Jonah took the bow, leg strapped and aching and glad of it, glad of any pain that meant he was still in the world and moving. The canoe slid off the gravel and the current took them the way the current takes everything—without opinion, without mercy, without pause.

At the bend Jonah did look back, once, against orders. Henrik Voss stood on the bank in the last of the moonlight, small and straight, one hand raised. Then the alders closed and the river bent and the old man was gone, and ahead of them the black water ran south toward Current, toward the inspector, toward whatever waited at the end of forty miles of flood.

"Thursday," Astrid said quietly, paddling. "We can make Thursday."

Jonah dug his paddle in and matched her stroke, and the canoe leapt like something alive, and for the first time in nine days the thing he felt wasn't fear.

It was momentum.

Behind them, far upstream, a light flickered along the ridge trail—searchers, doubling back too late, finding an empty cabin and a cold stove and a woodpile that hid nothing but wood.

By the time they found the put-in, the river would tell them nothing at all.
