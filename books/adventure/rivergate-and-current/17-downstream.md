## 17. Downstream

The trapper's canoe was older than either of us and twice as stubborn.

Jonas Hale—just *Hale* to everyone, including himself, apparently—had pushed it off the mudbank at full dark with a lantern held high, saying nothing, which was his way. He'd already said the only thing that mattered, back in his cabin with the stove ticking: *"I took their money once. Twenty years I've been spitting it back."* Then he'd pressed the canoe paddle into my hands like he was handing over a debt of his own.

Now the river had us.

I knelt in the bow, paddle biting water, while Astrid sat behind me with the film case lashed to her chest by its strap and her arms locked around me for balance. The negatives were sewn into her jacket—we'd done that with fishing line two nights ago, by candlelight, her fingers steady and mine not—but the maps, Crane's maps, the oilskin-wrapped original that had started all of this, rode in the case between us. Everything else we owned in the world fit into two dry bags, and one of those was mostly bandages for the fever still smoldering in Astrid's shoulder where she'd taken the fall on the portage trail.

"You feel that?" she said against my ear.

The river had gone glassy for half a mile, black water sliding under a sky the color of wet slate, and I'd been fooling myself into thinking that meant something good. But I felt it now—the deep hum in the hull, the way the current leaned sideways instead of down. Water being pulled somewhere fast.

"Rapids," I said.

"I know. I can hear them."

So could I, now that she'd said it out loud. A long white stutter downstream, like something enormous grinding its teeth. Behind us, farther back than I liked but closer than I wanted, a searchlight swept the treeline on the south bank. Corliss's men. They'd found Hale's cabin an hour after we left—I knew because we'd heard the boat engine, big and expensive, running the river in the dark without lights, the way men run engines when they think nobody's listening.

"They'll wait below the rapids," Astrid said. "Nobody runs this stretch at night."

"Then we run it first."

She laughed, low and cracked. "Jonah Reyes, navigator of the year."

"Somebody has to be. You're busy being the hero."

"I'm busy holding a dead man's photographs against my ribs," she said, "and trying not to throw up from your steering."

But her arms tightened around me, and that was the whole conversation, really. That was everything we'd ever said to each other compressed into five pounds of pressure across my chest. Six years I'd watched that woman walk away from every good thing offered to her because walking away felt safer than wanting. And here she was, running rapids blind at midnight to hand a dead prospector's film to a federal inspector, and her arms around me said she wasn't walking away from this one. From any of it. From me.

The searchlight swung again, caught the far bank, slid off.

"Paddle," she said.

I paddled.

* * *

The river told you things before it showed them. That's what Hale had said, pressing a hand-drawn sketch of the channel into my palm back at the cabin—a few lines, a couple of X marks, the kind of map a man makes who has run a river four hundred times and trusts his ears more than his eyes. *Listen for the drop. If it sounds like rain on tin, go left around the rock. If it sounds like the end of the world, pray.*

It sounded like the end of the world.

The canoe lifted under us—not rocked, *lifted*, the whole hull rising on a swell of water coming downhill—and then the night tore open into white. Spray hit us sideways, cold enough to burn. I couldn't see anything except the pale churn of the drops ahead and the black shapes of rocks appearing out of it like animals surfacing. Astrid shouted something and I felt it more than heard it, her voice buzzing through her arms locked around my waist.

Rain on tin. Go left.

There was no left. There was white water everywhere and a rock the size of a barn dead ahead, shedding the current off both shoulders in long V-shaped scars of foam. I dug the paddle in and hauled right, because right had a gap—a thin black seam between two smaller rocks, maybe wide enough, maybe a lie.

The seam was real. The seam was also wrong.

What I couldn't see past the gap was the drop behind it, the one Hale's map had marked with a second X and the word *SHOOT STRAIGHT OR SWIM*. We came through the seam clean, both of us yelling, and then the bottom fell out of the river and the canoe went weightless for one full, holy second—flying, actually flying, the way Astrid's old de Havilland used to fly before the license people got hold of her—and I remember thinking, absurdly, *she's going to love this part,* and then the world rotated.

Not tipped. Rotated. The sky went under me, the river went over me, and the water closed like a door.

* * *

Cold is a strange teacher. It doesn't lecture. It just takes things away, one at a time, until you understand what you had.

First it took the paddle. Then the canoe, spinning away into the dark with a sound like a struck drum. Then it took my sense of up, and I learned what Hale had really meant about swimming: you don't swim in water like that, you get *handled* by it, tumbled like laundry in a machine the size of God's basement. My boots found rock twice and both times the current peeled me off before I could stand. Somewhere in all of it I heard Astrid scream my name—one clean syllable, *Jo-nah*, torn apart by the roar—and then I didn't hear anything but water.

I came up against the downstream eddy behind a boulder the size of a truck, coughing, half-drowned, alive. The noise of the rapids was already fading behind me. The river had spat us out into a long slow pool under cut banks, mist lying on the water like gauze.

"Astrid."

Nothing.

"*Astrid!*"

A splash, upstream. Then her voice, ragged: "Here. I'm here. Don't—" Another splash. "Don't come out, there's sweepers."

Sweepers. Fallen trees leaning off the bank into the current, the kind that pin swimmers underwater and keep them. I hauled myself onto the boulder, teeth chattering so hard I could barely shape words, and looked for her. Found her ten yards upstream, clinging to a sweeper's trunk, legs streaming in the current, one arm wrapped around the film case—which was still strapped to her chest, still sealed, still—

No.

The case was open. Torn along one seam where the strap had wrenched it against the tree, the lid bent back, and even from the boulder I could see the oilskin packet was gone. Crane's maps. The bearings, the markers, the hand-drawn channels that had taken us up the dead river and back—the whole paper skeleton of Elias Crane's last season, feeding the current somewhere below us, dissolving into the same water Rivergate had poisoned.

"Jonah." Her voice was strange. Flat. "The maps are gone."

"I see it."

"The photos are okay. The photos are sewn in." She patted her jacket with her free hand, the gesture jerky, half-numb. "They're okay. It's just—it's the maps. His maps. Elias drew those. He drew them dying, Jonah, he drew them so somebody would find—"

"Climb out first," I said. "Grieve on land."

That came out harsher than I meant it. But I'd spent enough nights watching her carry other people's wars to know how she drowned in them if you let her do it standing in freezing water. She climbed. Slowly, hand over hand along the trunk, until her feet found the muddy shelf of bank, and then she just stood there dripping, shaking, one hand pressed flat over her jacket like she was holding a wound closed.

I got to her. I don't remember crossing the distance; I remember the impact, both of us soaked and shuddering, her forehead hard against mine, her breath coming in little broken pulls.

"We're okay," I said. "You hear me? We lost paper. Paper floats. People sink. We're okay."

"He trusted me with it."

"And you're going to finish trusting him back. By getting the rest of it to Current." I pulled back far enough to look at her. Her lips were going gray. Fever or cold or both. "We can't stay here. They'll sweep this pool inside twenty minutes. Every man Corliss has will be walking these banks with lights."

She nodded. Didn't move.

"Astrid."

"I'm moving."

* * *

We walked. That was the whole plan—walk downstream along the cut bank, stay inside the treeline, put miles between us and the pool before the boats caught up. The river would take us to Current eventually; Hale had said the water did the navigating, you just had to survive the company it kept. Our boots squelched. Our clothes froze in layers. Astrid stumbled twice and both times I caught her elbow and both times she shook me off and kept going, jaw set, that stubborn pilot's pride burning through the hypothermia like a bad engine burning oil.

Somewhere past the third bend she finally spoke.

"When we capsize—when the case tore—I felt the maps go. Right through my fingers. Like the river just reached in and took them." She stared straight ahead as she walked. "And you know what I thought? Not *oh no, the evidence.* I thought, *good.*"

I stopped. "Good?"

"Because the maps were ours. The route, the bearings, the whole secret way in. If Corliss gets those, he gets the valley. He seals it, burns Camp Nine, salts the ground, and the photos become pictures of nothing." She turned to look at me, and even soaked and gray-lipped and fever-bright, her eyes had that thing in them—the thing that made creditors nervous and made me helpless. "The river kept them. Let it. The photos are what matter. The photos are *his testimony.* Everything else was just directions to a grave."

I thought about that for a long time, walking. About how she'd turned losing into strategy in the space of one breath, the way she turned headwinds into tailwinds, the way she turned every ending into a heading. Six years of loving this woman and I still couldn't tell where the recklessness stopped and the faith began. Maybe they were the same thing. Maybe that was the whole problem with her and the whole reason I'd follow her into a frozen river at midnight without a second thought.

"There," I said.

Downstream, maybe half a mile, a light. Not a searchlight—too low, too steady, orange, flickering. A campfire on the near bank, tucked under an overhang of rock.

Or a trap.

Astrid saw it too. We crouched in the alders, dripping, and watched it for a long time. No voices. No movement except the fire itself, breathing.

"Hale said there was a fish camp past the falls," I whispered. "Old man and his sons, dry nets, dry blankets. Said to knock twice and mention his name."

"And if it's Corliss's men?"

"Then we're wet, exhausted, and about to have a very short conversation."

Her mouth twitched. Almost a smile. "Story of my life."

We moved toward the fire, and behind us, far upstream, a searchlight swept the pool where the maps were sinking into the mud of the riverbed, and ahead of us, forty miles of black water away, Current waited with its federal inspector, its frightened council, and its fixer holding Marta Crane's future in his polished hands.

Everything now hung on wet paper and a woman who refused to drown.

The fire grew closer. I knocked twice.
