## 18. Current, Burning

The river gave them up at dawn like something it was done with.

Astrid felt it before she saw it—the current slackening, the banks widening, the water going from brown fury to a broad gray patience that meant the rapids were behind them and Current lay ahead. She knelt in the canoe with her arms shaking so badly she could barely hold the paddle, and Jonah sat slumped in the stern behind her, fever-bright but conscious, his hands still working the sweep because she'd asked him to and he hadn't let go of it since the capsize two nights ago.

"You see smoke?" he rasped.

She did. Not chimney smoke—Current had maybe forty chimneys on a good day—but a low brown smudge sitting over the town like a hand pressed down flat. And under it, when the river bent and brought the settlement into view between the black spruce, she saw why.

There were three aircraft on the strip where there should have been none. Two of them were Rivergate's chartered Otters, company blue and white, and the third was Corliss's Cessna 206 with its polished cowl and its registration number she'd memorized without meaning to, back when he'd first offered to buy "anything Elias left" and made it sound like kindness.

"They beat us here," Jonah said.

"No." Astrid dug the paddle in. "They've been here. That's different."

The dock came up slow. Marta Crane's cabin stood above the boat shed, and its door hung open, swinging in the wind off the water, and there was no light in the window and no woodsmoke from the stovepipe and no Marta splitting kindling on the step the way she had every morning since Astrid had known her. The axe was still in the block. Half-split birch, frozen mid-swing, days old.

Astrid tied the canoe with hands that wouldn't obey her and looked at the open door for a long moment.

"She stalled him," Jonah said quietly. "Chapter and verse, that woman. She's been stalling him since we went upriver."

"And now she's not stalling anyone." Astrid's voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "Jonah. The negatives."

He touched his own chest out of habit, then hers—the jacket, the inner seam, the oilcloth packet sewn flat against her ribs where it had ridden for nine days through muskeg and rain and one night underwater. Wet paper. Damp but whole. Elias Crane's last season of work: orange creeks, dead game, falsified logs, the shallow pit at Camp Nine where Rivergate had buried a valley's worth of proof along with whatever else it had buried.

"It's there," he said. "It's all there."

"Then get up the hill," she said. "Doc Fenn's. Sleep it off. Don't argue."

"And you?"

She looked at the town. At the community hall with every window lit at six in the morning, though there was no reason for lights like that except a meeting. At the men standing on the hall steps—not councilmen, they moved wrong for councilmen, they stood like hired shoulders—and at the single figure among them in a city coat that had no business north of sixty degrees latitude, hands in his pockets, watching the river the way a man watches a door he's expecting someone to come through.

Corliss.

"He's waiting for me," Astrid said. "So I'm going to stop making him wait."

* * *

Current smelled of wet ash and diesel. That was new. Someone had burned something near the fuel cache—she could see the scorched ring where the company's drums had been stacked, and the drums themselves rolled into the shallows, split and empty. Sabotage dressed as accident, or accident dressed as sabotage; either way, the message was legible. *Nothing here works unless we say it works.*

The streets were not empty, which surprised her. She'd expected a town swept hollow by fear. Instead she found people out on their porches in the cold, standing, not sitting, and old Pieter Voss on his step with his shotgun across his knees, unbroken open, which was its own kind of sentence. He looked at her coming up the mud street, soaked to the waist, hair frozen stiff, jacket bulging wrong over her ribs, and he didn't ask where she'd been or what she carried.

"Hall's full," he said. "Council's been sitting since midnight. Company man won't let them adjourn."

"Is Marta in there?"

Pieter's jaw worked. "He's got her sitting at the front table. Says she's assisting with questions." His eyes came up, and they were red-rimmed and hard as river stones. "He says if anybody leaves town before the council signs his access agreement, Mrs. Crane will be charged as an accessory to trespassing on company leaseholds. He says it polite."

"How many of his men inside?"

"Four in the room. Two more on the steps. And the pilot sleeping in the Otter." Pieter spat, carefully, away from the wind. "You walking in there, Miss Ravensworth?"

"I'm walking in there."

He stood, slow, joints cracking like gunshots in the cold, and closed the breech of the shotgun with a click that carried the length of the street. Then he set it down on the porch rail, in plain sight, empty and unhidden, and said, "Then I'll be standing outside the door. Plenty of us will."

That was the thing about Current she had never understood until this moment, flying home broke and half-drowned after nine days of being hunted through her own country: a small town is not a weak thing. A small town is a compressed thing. Forty chimneys, and every one of them holding somebody who'd drunk that water, eaten those fish, watched the creek run orange and been told it was iron in the shale, natural, nothing to be done. Compressed grief. Compressed shame. And shame, given a door to walk through, becomes something else entirely.

She crossed the last hundred yards with her heart going like a failing engine and her mind gone very quiet and very clear, the way it went on short final in weather, when there was nothing left to decide and only the landing left to make.

* * *

The hall smelled of woodsmoke and coffee gone bitter from hours on the plate. Every chair was taken and men stood along the walls, and at the front, under the mounted head of a moose that had hung there since before Astrid was born, the council sat behind a long table like defendants at their own trial.

Corliss stood beside the table, mid-sentence, and he was good—she'd forgotten how good he was. His voice never rose. It dropped instead, lower and more reasonable with each point, a man explaining arithmetic to children who kept getting the sum wrong.

"—and I want to be clear that Rivergate bears no ill will toward this community. Quite the opposite. The company has invested eleven years and considerable capital in this region. What we're discussing today is simply an access agreement. A formality. Roads, a staging yard, seasonal barge rights. In exchange—" he let the pause do the work, let every eye in the room go to the folder on the table in front of the council chairman, thick as a Bible, "—the company is prepared to forgive certain local obligations. Debts held against residents. There are more of those than some of you might think."

Astrid saw it land. Saw the flinch travel the room like wind through barley, man to man, because everyone in Current owed somebody, and half of them owed Rivergate, and she knew better than anyone in this room exactly what it sounded like when your debt got called within the hour.

At the end of the table, wrists bound with a plastic tie disguised as nothing at all because her hands were folded neatly on the table as if she'd chosen to sit there, sat Marta Crane. Her face was gray with exhaustion. One eye was swollen. When the door opened and the cold came in, she turned, and for one second her whole face changed—not hope, something past hope, something that had stopped spending itself on hope weeks ago—and then she put the face away again, smooth as a drawer closing, because Marta Crane did not give Corliss anything he could use.

Corliss turned too.

For a moment neither of them moved. The hall went silent the way a forest goes silent, everything listening at once. Astrid stood in the doorway with riverwater still dripping off her, and Corliss looked at her the way a man looks at weather he has been promised and hoped would miss him.

"Miss Ravensworth," he said warmly. "You're alive. I confess the river had me worried." He spread his hands, the picture of relief, playing to the room. "Everyone, you know Miss Ravensworth. Our local pilot. Recently in some difficulty with her license, I understand. Struggling. It's been a hard season for independent operators." Sympathy, laid out like bait. "Come in out of the cold. We were just concluding."

"We were just beginning," Astrid said.

She walked down the aisle. Slowly. Letting them look. She heard Pieter Voss take up his post outside the door behind her, heard other boots follow, the scrape of chairs, the town gathering at her back like water finding its level. Corliss's four men shifted along the walls—one of them, a big fair-haired man she remembered from the airfield stakeout, put his hand inside his coat, and Corliss lifted one finger without looking at him, and the hand came back out.

Because that was the whole architecture of the man. He never needed the gun himself. He needed only the possibility of it, held just below the surface of things, so that every conversation happened in its shadow.

"Whatever you think you have," Corliss said, low now, just for her, as she reached the front, "whatever the old man told you or showed you—it's inadmissible. Chain of custody. Provenance. You're a debtor with a suspended license carrying stolen documents off restricted leasehold. You're not a witness, Astrid. You're a defendant. Sit down, and I'll make sure the charges stay civil."

"You're right," Astrid said.

Something flickered across his face—the first honest expression she'd ever seen him wear.

"I'm a debtor. My license is suspended. Everything I've done for nine days has been illegal, probably, and I don't care, because legality is your territory, Mr. Corliss. You own it. You bought it fair and square." She raised her voice then, not shouting, just letting it carry to the back wall, to the standing men, to the porches beyond the open door. "But photographs aren't testimony. They're evidence. And evidence doesn't need my word. It needs daylight."

She unzipped the jacket.

The hall held its breath. She drew out the oilcloth packet—river-stained, torn at one corner, soft as cloth from nine days against her body—and held it up in both hands, high, turning slowly so the whole room could see it, so the men on the steps could see it, so Pieter Voss and his empty shotgun could see it.

"Elias Crane spent his last summer photographing what Rivergate buried at Camp Nine," she said. "Orange creeks. Dead game. Falsified drill logs in his own handwriting, dated and signed. A burial pit of contaminated soil two hundred meters from a tributary that feeds this river. This river." She let that stand. "Every frame is developed. Every negative is here. And copies exist in a place Mr. Corliss can't buy, burn, or drown, because I'm not stupid enough to carry the only set through a flood and a manhunt."

It was a bluff. There was no second set. There was only the packet in her hands and the federal inspector due in Current within days, and both of them hanging by wet paper. But Corliss couldn't know that, and the room believed her, and belief in a room like this was load-bearing.

"So here's where we are," Astrid said. "You can take these from me. Right now. In front of the council, in front of forty witnesses, in front of the widow whose husband died carrying them home." She took one step closer to him, close enough to smell the coffee on his breath, close enough that his men would have to shoot through her to reach the packet, and she looked him dead in the eye and made her voice gentle, which was crueler than shouting. "Or you can let them go to the inspector tomorrow morning, and spend the rest of your life explaining to shareholders why you chose the other thing."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, the river ran on, indifferent, carrying everything toward the sea.

Corliss smiled. It was a fine smile, well-made, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes, and she understood suddenly that she was looking at a man doing arithmetic at terrible speed—four men, two on the steps, one town, one inspector, one packet, one witness who had just made herself unkillable in public. She watched him arrive at the sum. Watched him hate it.

"You always were a poor businesswoman," he said softly.

"I'm an excellent one," Astrid said. "I know what things cost."

And that was when Marta Crane stood up, snapped the plastic tie on the table edge with a motion that said she'd been working at it for hours, and said, clear as a bell across the hall:

"Chairman. The council has heard enough from the company. I move we vote on adjourning until the federal inspector arrives—and I move that every document in this hall be sealed as evidence, starting with the ones Mr. Corliss brought in that folder."

Chairs scraped. Hands went up around the long table, one after another, faster than Corliss could count them, and Astrid watched the vote happen in real time, five hands, unanimous, while the fixer from the city stood very still in a room that had stopped being his.

But she wasn't fooled, and she didn't think Marta was either. Because as the hall erupted—as Pieter Voss filled the doorway and men began pressing in and Corliss gathered his coat with the unhurried movements of a man conceding a battle, never the war—Astrid caught his eye one last time.

He glanced, briefly, almost tenderly, toward the windows facing the airfield.

Toward the hangar. Where her plane sat.

Where the negatives would sleep tonight, sealed in the council's strongbox, because Marta had insisted they be locked somewhere with walls rather than carried loose through a celebrating town.

"Congratulations, Miss Ravensworth," Corliss said, settling his collar. "Enjoy the win."

He walked out into the dawn with his men falling in around him, and Astrid stood in the noise and the triumph with the oilcloth packet still raised in her hands, and felt the first cold thread of understanding go through her:

He hadn't come to Current for the council vote. He'd come for the hangar.

And he'd just told her so.
