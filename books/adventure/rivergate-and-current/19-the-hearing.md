## 19. The Hearing

Marta Crane had dressed for funerals her whole life, and the hearing in the Current town hall had the smell of one—wet wool, coffee gone cold, the particular hush of people waiting to learn what a life had been worth.

She sat in the second row, hands folded in her lap, wearing the grey coat she'd buried Elias's mother in. On the bench beside her lay a manila folder, soft-cornered from handling, holding eleven letters in her dead husband's handwriting. She had read them so many times she could recite them, but she'd brought them anyway, because paper was the only witness the government trusted.

The town hall had never held this many people. Fishermen stood along the back wall with their hats in their hands. The schoolteacher had brought her older students, lined them up against the window like evidence of the future. Old Soren Halvorsen sat in the front row, the trapper, his hands trembling around a cup of tea he never drank—Marta had seen him in the hall an hour early, sitting alone, practicing something under his breath. Confession, maybe. She knew the weight of rehearsed words.

The federal inspector had arrived on the morning mail plane: a compact woman named Okafor with a canvas briefcase and a face that gave away nothing. She'd shaken hands like a man, looked at the leak in the hall roof without comment, and asked where the washroom was. Marta had decided in that first minute that she liked her. You couldn't buy a person who asked about the washroom.

At nine o'clock exactly, Corliss walked in.

Marta had seen him before—from across the street, from behind curtains. Up close he was worse, because up close you could see the care he took. Charcoal coat, silver at the temples, a smile calibrated to the size of the room. He had two lawyers with him and a leather folio, and he took the chair across from Okafor as though the hearing were a meeting he'd called.

"Mr. Corliss," Okafor said, not looking up from her papers. "You're aware this is a sworn proceeding."

"I'm aware this town has been misled by grief," Corliss said warmly. "I'm here to correct the record."

Marta's hands tightened on the folder.

* * *

Okafor ran it like a drill: names, dates, signatures. The fisherman Tomás Reyes spoke first, describing the creek that ran orange below Camp Nine, the nets that came up empty two seasons running, the moose found dead at the waterline with their bellies split and black. Then the schoolteacher, with attendance records and a list of children treated for sickness that the clinic had logged as "stomach flu" every summer for four years.

Corliss's lawyers objected on procedure. Okafor overruled them without looking up.

Then it was Marta's turn.

She walked to the front of the hall feeling Elias beside her the way she sometimes did at night—not a ghost, exactly. A hand at her elbow. *Tell them straight, Marta. They can't argue with straight.*

She held up the first letter. "My husband wrote to Rivergate Mining eleven times in his last season. These are copies. He kept the originals in a lockbox, and the lockbox is gone, and I know who took it, and we'll come to that." Her voice didn't shake. She had practiced too. "In these letters he reports contamination in the waterways downstream of the Camp Nine drill site. He reports dead game. He reports that when he raised it with his foreman, his pay was cut. When he raised it again, he was told his contract wouldn't be renewed. In his last letter, dated three weeks before he died, he writes—" and here she did not read it, because she had promised herself she wouldn't cry in this hall, she would simply *say* it— "he writes: *They're not hiding the spill, Marta. They're hiding the size of it. It's not a creek that's dead. It's the whole valley.*"

She laid the letters on the table in front of Okafor, one by one, like dealing cards.

"Mr. Crane was not a surveyor," Corliss said, rising. "He was a contract laborer with a grudge and a drinking problem, and these are the unsent fantasies of a—"

"They were sent," Marta said. "Receipts are stapled to each one. Your company signed for every letter, Mr. Corliss. Your company read every word and buried every word, and then my husband went into the bush to document it with his own eyes because nobody with a desk would listen." She turned to Okafor. "And then he came out of the bush half-frozen, carrying a map, and a pilot named Astrid Ravensworth flew him toward help, and he died on the dock at Current with that map in his jacket. And I'd like to know what your company's fixer was doing at that dock within the hour, offering to buy 'anything Elias left.'"

The hall went very quiet.

Corliss sat down. It was the first thing he'd done all morning that looked involuntary.

* * *

Astrid Ravensworth arrived at noon, and the hall's reaction told Marta everything about what the town had already decided to believe. People turned. Some stood. The schoolteacher's students pressed to the windows.

She looked terrible—hollowed, rain-burned, one hand bandaged, moving like a woman running on fuel fumes. Jonah Reyes stayed close to her shoulder the whole way down the aisle, close enough that their elbows kept touching, and Marta thought: *there's a man who has decided something.*

Astrid laid a film case on the table. It was torn at one corner and water-stained, and she opened it with the care of someone handling an egg.

"Negatives," she said. "Photographs taken by Elias Crane at Camp Nine and along the dead creek. I took them myself, on site, four days ago. The site is as he described it. Rusted drums. Falsified logs. A burial pit of contaminated soil, capped with gravel, upstream of every camp and fishery on the river." She paused. "I also have a photograph of the grave pit's edge, where the gravel has washed out, showing the drums beneath. Dated. Stamped. I'm a licensed pilot, Mr. Corliss. My word about locations is worth something under oath."

"You're a *grounded* pilot," Corliss said, standing again, and there was something new in his voice now—not warmth, not menace. Urgency. "Your license is suspended pending complaint. Your fuel accounts were frozen for debt. You are, by your own company's records, an unreliable—"

"By *your* company's records," Astrid said. "You froze them yourself. Within the hour of my refusing to sell you a dead man's papers. You want to enter those records as evidence, Mr. Corliss? I'll swear to every date."

Marta watched Okafor's pen move, and watched Corliss understand, all at once, that every weapon he'd brought had been handed to the other side.

* * *

It was Soren Halvorsen who broke him.

The old trapper walked to the front like a man walking to his own trial, and maybe that's what it was. He didn't look at the hall. He looked at the table.

"Twenty-two years I trapped that valley," he said. "Rivergate paid me, every season, to report 'conditions.' I reported what they told me to report. Dead fish—winter kill, I wrote. Sick game—wolves, I wrote. I took their money and I wrote their lies in my own hand, and I've been ashamed of it every day since, and shame is a poor wage." His voice cracked and he let it. "Two years ago they paid me extra to stop running my line along the lower creek. Said it was for my safety. It wasn't for my safety. It was because the creek was orange, and they knew I'd seen it, and they knew I knew."

He put an envelope on the table. "Payment records. Twenty-two years of them. I kept everything. I always figured someday I'd need to prove I was a coward, and here it is."

Marta heard the sound the hall made—not a gasp. A settling. Like a house shifting on its foundation when the frost finally lets go.

Corliss stood for the last time. Marta watched him do it and understood that he knew it was the last time, and watched him reach for the only thing he had left.

"Madam Inspector." His voice was smooth again, or almost. "Rivergate Mining wishes to resolve this matter without burdening this community with years of litigation. To that end, I'm authorized to offer—" he opened the leather folio and drew out a single sheet, laid it on the table with two fingers, the way you'd lay down a winning card— "a settlement. Remediation funding for the valley. Compensation for affected families. A community trust, administered locally. In exchange for a sealed record and a mutual release." He looked around the hall, at the fishermen, at the schoolteacher, at the children in the window. "This money rebuilds Current. The alternative is a decade of lawyers, and the valley dies while you argue."

Silence.

Then Tomás Reyes stood up in the back, hat in his hands, and said, "My daughter's sick every summer. You want to pay me to agree my daughter's sick *mysteriously*?"

"Sealed record means it never happened," the schoolteacher said. "It happened."

Old Soren turned around in his chair, slow, and looked at Corliss the way a man looks at a river he's finally decided to cross. "You offered me money for twenty-two years, Mr. Corliss. I took it. I know the taste. You're offering these people the same taste, and they can smell it on you."

One by one, the town stood up. Not a speech. Not a vote. Just the hall rising, fishermen and teachers and the widow in her grey coat, until the only people seated were Corliss, his lawyers, and the inspector.

Okafor picked up the settlement sheet, looked at it, and set it facedown.

"The record will note that Rivergate Mining's offer was presented and declined," she said. "This hearing is adjourned pending formal referral. Mr. Corliss, don't leave Current."

Marta watched Corliss gather his folio with hands that were, for the first time, not quite steady. She watched him look at Astrid—looked at her the way a man looks at a locked door when all his keys are gone—and then at the window, at the airfield beyond it, where a battered de Havilland sat on the apron.

He left quickly. His lawyers had to hurry to keep up.

The hall emptied slowly, people touching Astrid's shoulder, gripping Soren's hand, and Marta stood by the table collecting her letters, and that was when she felt it—that prickle at the back of the neck her husband had taught her to trust. She looked out the window.

Down at the airfield, a light was on in the hangar. It was late for the fuel man. Later for anyone.

And Corliss's car was not on the road out of town.
