## 20. Last Flight

Corliss had always believed that a man's reputation was a ledger, and that every entry could be bought, burned, or buried. For thirty years he'd kept Rivergate Mining's books clean in the only way that mattered—not accurate, but *quiet*. He had paid off inspectors with envelopes and widows with settlements and whole towns with the promise of jobs. He had never once raised his voice. Men who shouted were men who had lost control of the arithmetic.

Tonight the arithmetic was failing him.

He stood at the edge of the Current airfield in the blue hour after the hearing adjourned, watching the crowd disperse through the rain-slicked street, and he understood with cold clarity that he was finished unless he did something unforgivable in the next six hours. The inspector had the photographs. Marta Crane had the letters. The trapper—Grieves, some ancient name like that—had given testimony that would have been laughable from any other mouth but landed like scripture coming from a man who admitted he'd taken Rivergate's money and hated himself for it ever since.

But there was one more set of negatives. The originals. Astrid Ravensworth had them sewn into her jacket lining, or so the town gossip said, but she'd surrendered copies to the inspector, and somewhere in this muddy little town sat a floatplane that had carried those copies out of the bush—a plane that belonged, by every lien and loan paper Corliss's lawyers had ever drafted, to Rivergate Mining.

The plane was in the hangar.

Corliss lit a cigarette and did not smoke it. He let it burn down between his fingers while he thought, which was an old habit, a way of measuring time in ash. Three of his hired men were already in town—Voss, Delacroix, and a kid named Penny who was mostly muscle and mostly afraid. They'd flown up on the company charter before the hearing, back when Corliss still believed a bribe could buy a council. Now they were the only tool left that didn't require a signature.

"You understand what I'm asking," Corliss said to Voss, not looking at him, looking instead at the hangar's tin roof catching the last grey light. "I'm asking you to burn a building. Not a person. If anyone is inside when it goes, that's their own stupidity, and we were never here."

Voss was a big silent man with a jaw like a dock piling. He'd done arson before—Corliss knew because Corliss had arranged it, a warehouse in Terrace, a drill shed on the Stikine—and he'd never asked questions, which was why he was still employed. But tonight even Voss hesitated.

"The pilot," Voss said. "She's been sleeping in the hangar. On a cot, next to the plane. Half the town's seen her go in there at night."

"Then someone should warn her," Corliss said mildly. "Knock on the door. Give her five minutes. If she chooses to stand in a burning building, that is a decision, not a crime." He flicked the dead cigarette into a puddle. "I'm not a monster, Voss. I'm a bookkeeper."

It was a lie and both of them knew it, and the lie sat between them like a third man walking.

* * *

Astrid Ravensworth had spent the evening being congratulated by people who had avoided her eyes for months, and she had hated every minute of it, because praise felt like a eulogy for something that wasn't dead yet. She knew Corliss. She had sat across a desk from him while he offered her a fortune for film she didn't have, and she had watched his face do nothing at all while he described what happened to witnesses. A man like that did not lose a hearing and go home. A man like that went to work.

So she'd moved the negatives. All of them—the true originals, the ones Crane had shot, the ones Jonah had helped her develop in the trapper's cabin with a red lantern and shaking hands—were now in three places: one set sewn into her jacket, one set taped inside the inspection file the federal man was carrying, and one set hidden where no one would think to look, because no one in their right mind hid evidence inside the thing everyone wanted to destroy.

She'd told no one about the third set. It was insurance, and insurance only worked if it was secret.

She was on her cot in the hangar, boots off, jacket folded under her head as a pillow, listening to the rain work the tin roof, when she heard footsteps on the gravel outside. Two sets. Heavy, unhurried. Then a knock—not urgent, almost polite—and a voice she recognized through the wood.

"Miss Ravensworth. You have five minutes to leave the building."

She was on her feet before the sentence ended, boots on without lacing, and she stood in the dark hangar doing the fastest arithmetic of her life. They were going to torch the hangar. Which meant they believed the evidence was here. Which meant they didn't know about the jacket, or the file, or—

Or they knew exactly, and this was theater, and the fire was meant to be the story: *pilot dies in suspicious blaze, evidence destroyed, tragedy closes the chapter.* A fire explained everything. A fire needed no witnesses, no bodies recovered, no awkward questions about how a woman came to be shot in a town full of people who liked her.

Either way, the plane was the variable. Her de Havilland Beaver, half-repaired since the flood, fuel tanks topped off that afternoon because she'd planned to fly the inspector's copies downriver at first light. Full tanks. Parked forty feet from a door that was about to stop being a door.

She looked at the old girl in the dark—the dents, the patched cowling, the fabric on the left aileron she'd sewn herself by lantern light—and she made the decision the way she made all of them: too fast, and then forever.

If the hangar burned with the plane in it, the fire would be written off as an accident, and the last physical thing tying her to Crane's valley would be ash, and Corliss would walk into the wreckage of his own reputation having erased the one piece of proof that couldn't be re-photographed—the drums, the grave pit, the orange creek, all of it documented on film that existed nowhere else in the original.

No. The plane was leaving. Tonight. With her in it, or not at all.

She hauled open the hangar doors just as Voss and the kid reached for the handles from outside, and the four of them stared at each other in the wet dark like a photograph nobody meant to take. Voss had a gas can in one hand. The kid had a shovel. Neither moved.

"Evening," Astrid said. "You're late. I was just heading out."

Voss looked at her, then at the plane, then at the gas can, and something almost like respect crossed his flat face. "Five minutes," he said again, softer. "That was the deal."

"I heard. I'm taking eleven." She walked past them to the fuel drum, dragged it to the plane, and began hand-pumping the tanks fuller, slow and deliberate, daring them to watch. "Tell your boss the hearing starts again Monday. Tell him I'll be there. Tell him—" she paused, pumping, breath fogging, "—tell him he should have bought me when I was cheap."

They didn't stop her. Later, Corliss would understand why, and it would keep him awake: Voss had looked at Astrid Ravensworth standing alone in front of a plane she had no legal right to fly, in a town that had turned against his employer, and he had seen the ending of the story before the rest of them did. Some men can smell a losing side. Voss put the gas can down in the mud and walked away, and took the kid with him, and that was the moment Corliss's night stopped being a plan and became a panic.

Because Corliss didn't know any of that yet. Corliss was standing in his rented room above the Northern Hotel with the window cracked, watching the airfield, waiting for the glow.

* * *

The glow came at 11:40.

Not the hangar—Voss had quit—but the fuel cache at the far end of the field, two drums of avgas that Corliss's remaining man, Delacroix, had opened and ignited as a message. The flames stood up orange against the rain, twenty feet tall, and the heat of them reached the hotel window as a faint warmth on the glass, and Corliss watched the fire climb and felt, for the first time in thirty years, the specific vertigo of a man whose instruments are all reading wrong.

Delacroix was supposed to torch the hangar. Instead he'd torched the fuel, which meant either cowardice or initiative, and both were fatal in different ways. The burning drums were fifty yards upwind of the hangar. In this wind, in this rain-soaked night that somehow wasn't soaking enough, the fire would walk.

Corliss picked up the telephone in his room, an actual wire running down through the hotel wall to the exchange, and asked for the airfield, and got no answer, because the airfield's one phone was in the hangar, and the hangar was emptying.

He put the receiver down and stood very still.

There was a version of this night where Corliss drove to the field himself, waded into the firelight, and pulled the pilot out of a burning building with his own hands, and the story wrote itself the way stories used to write themselves for him: *company man saves local hero, charges quietly dropped, everyone reasonable.* He had run that play before. It worked on towns smaller than this one.

But Astrid Ravensworth was not in the hangar. Word came up the hotel stairs within minutes, carried by a boy who'd run from the fire: the plane was gone. Already off the water, already climbing, already turning south over the river with its running lights blinking steady and insolent against the smoke.

Corliss went to the window and looked south and saw it—a single set of lights, low over the black water, dragging a shadow behind it like a signature.

And hanging beneath the lights, in his mind's eye, he saw what the plane was carrying. Not the copies—the copies were with the inspector, safe, useless to him now. What the plane carried was the *originals*, the ones Crane had buried in oilskin under a cairn at Camp Nine, the ones Astrid had dug up with her own hands, the ones with the edge markings and the dates and the handwriting on the backing paper that tied every image to Elias Crane's own camera, his own hands, his own dying testimony. Those negatives could hang Rivergate. Copies could be disputed. Originals could not.

And they were flying away from him over a river, in weather, in a plane with a patched aileron and a fuel system he'd once paid a mechanic in Terrace to sabotage—no. That had fallen through. The plane was sound. The plane was sound, and that was worse, because it meant the only thing between Corliss and the end of his career was weather, and weather had never once taken his side.

He called Delacroix's room. No answer. He called the charter office in Terrace, woke the dispatcher, and ordered the company Beechcraft fueled and ready by dawn with a ferry permit and a story about a medevac. Then he sat down on the edge of the bed in his suit, shoes still on, and did the thing he had trained himself never to do.

He waited.

* * *

At 12:15 the fire reached the hangar.

It went up fast—tin walls conducting heat, forty years of grease and dope and fabric scraps and a drum of linseed oil catching all at once—and from the river the glow must have looked like dawn arriving four hours early, sideways, wrong. The whole town turned out on the bank. Marta Crane stood among them in Elias's old coat, and when the roof beams collapsed inward with a sound like a struck bell, she said quietly, to no one, "Elias would have liked a loud ending."

Jonah Reyes was not watching the fire. Jonah was on the dock, staring south along the river with binoculars borrowed from the cannery, tracking a pair of lights that blinked twice, paused, blinked twice—Astrid's signal, the one they'd agreed on weeks ago in a wheel well that smelled like hydraulic fluid and fear. Two-and-two meant *airborne, evidence secure, don't follow.*

Except Jonah was going to follow. Jonah had spent the afternoon doing something Astrid didn't know about: he'd taken the cannery's old boat, the one with the bilge pump and the spotlight, and loaded it with blankets and a first aid kit and a fire extinguisher, and he'd told the harbormaster exactly one lie—that he was going out to check trap lines—and pointed the bow south into the smoke.

Because Astrid was flying a plane with a fuel line she'd spliced herself, over a river, at night, in wind that gusted off the canyon walls, and the man who loved her had learned long ago that love in this country wasn't a feeling. It was equipment. You packed it before the trip. You carried it whether or not you needed it.

* * *

Over the river, Astrid felt the fire at her back as a warmth on the tail, a faint orange shimmer in the rearview mirror of her mind, and she flew north-to-south low over the black water with the negatives in her jacket and her hands light on the yoke and grief and fury burning in the same chamber as the engine's fire, and she thought about Elias Crane dying on the dock with snow in his beard, and she thought about the orange creek, and she thought about a phrase Corliss had used across a desk once, in a voice like a filing cabinet closing: *"Everything burns eventually, Miss Ravensworth. The trick is choosing what you're standing near when it does."*

She was standing near the truth. Let it burn.

The engine coughed.

Once. A small dry hiccup, the kind you log and forget. She trimmed for it, checked the mixture, watched the gauges. Fuel pressure steady. Temperature fine. The cough came again, longer, and the propeller stumbled, caught, stumbled, and the Beaver sagged a hundred feet toward the black water before she forced the nose down and the airspeed up and coaxed the old girl back to life, and for nine seconds—one thousand one, one thousand two, all the way to nine—Astrid Ravensworth fell out of the sky over the Rivergate with a jacket full of justice and a heart full of ice.

Then the engine caught clean and held, and she climbed, shaking, back to eight hundred feet, and she understood what the cough meant: water in the fuel. From the hand-pump. From the drum that had sat open in the rain while she filled her tanks in front of Voss, daring him, showing off, being brave when she should have been careful.

Brave and careless. The same flaw, wearing different clothes.

She was twenty miles downriver when the coughing started for good.

* * *

In the hotel, Corliss saw the plane's lights stutter.

He was watching through binoculars now—he'd gone down to the bank, mingled with the fire crowd, anonymous in a borrowed coat, because a man in his position learns to witness his own disasters personally—and through the glass he saw the running lights dip below the tree line and rise, dip and rise, the unmistakable rhythm of a struggling aircraft, and he felt something rise in his chest that he refused to name, because naming it would make it hope, and hope was for men who hadn't yet read the final page.

The lights steadied. Climbed. Kept going south.

Corliss lowered the binoculars and stood in the crowd of his enemies—firemen, fishermen, the widow Crane, the council members whose bribe he'd withdrawn an hour too late—and none of them knew his face mattered anymore, and that was the strangest feeling of his life: irrelevance, arriving at last, wearing a borrowed coat.

Behind him the hangar burned to its skeleton. Ahead of him the plane limped south carrying the end of everything he'd built. And somewhere between those two fires—the one behind and the one ahead—Corliss finally understood the shape of his failure, and it was not the hearing, and it was not the photographs, and it was not the widow or the trapper or the inspector.

It was simpler than all of that. He had spent thirty years believing people were entries in a ledger, and he had never once audited himself, and the audit, when it finally came, was conducted by a bush pilot with a spliced fuel line and a dead prospector's compass on her dash, and the finding was this:

Some things do not burn. Some things fly.

He turned back toward the hotel to pack, and to call Terrace, and to begin the long retreat of a man who has just realized the fire followed him home. Behind him, over the black river, the Beaver's lights blinked twice, paused, blinked twice—still airborne, still holding, still coming—and Corliss walked into the dark knowing that the last flight of Rivergate Mining had already taken off, and he was not on it, and there was no runway anywhere in the world long enough for him to catch it now.

But the night wasn't finished with either of them. Twenty miles south, an engine was drinking water and dying by inches, and a pilot was scanning the black water for a place to put down, and a boat with a spotlight and a bilge pump was running the rapids below her with its bow aimed straight at the falling sky.
