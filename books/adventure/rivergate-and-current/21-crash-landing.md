## 21. Crash Landing

The engine coughed the way a dying man coughs—wet, irregular, apologetic—and Astrid felt the de Havilland Beaver sag beneath her like something giving up an argument it had been losing for twenty miles.

"No, no, no. Not yet." She fed it throttle she didn't have, coaxing, begging. "Not yet, you stubborn old girl."

Smoke curled up through the floorboards in gray ropes, thin but persistent, the way smoke had been curling since Corliss's man touched the match to the hangar wall. She'd gotten airborne with the paint still bubbling on the struts. She'd gotten airborne with the negatives sewn into her jacket and Marta's handprint still wet on her shoulder and half of Current screaming on the dock behind her. She'd gotten airborne because the alternative was letting Rivergate win by arson what it couldn't win by bribery.

But getting airborne and staying airborne were two different prayers, and the engine was answering only one of them.

"Astrid." Jonah's voice came from the right seat, steady the way his hands were steady, the way they'd been steady on the rapids, on the fuel run, on every bad night she'd ever dragged him into. "River bends left ahead. Maybe eight hundred yards of open water before the narrows."

"Eight hundred yards." She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist—smoke or tears or both—and squinted through the windshield at the black world below. The river was a ribbon of dull silver under a torn sky, moonlight breaking through in patches like coins dropped on dark cloth. "That's not enough."

"It's what we've got."

The engine coughed again, longer this time, and the propeller's rhythm went ragged. Steam or vapor trailed back past the windows. Somewhere inside the cowling, metal was grinding against metal with a sound like teeth.

* * *

She had put planes down on water exactly twice in her life, both times on purpose, both times calm, both times with floats that were rated for it. This was different. The Beaver wore wheels—she'd flown out of Current on wheels because there hadn't been time to do anything else—and wheels meant no gentle settling onto the surface, no long sweet kiss of hull to lake. Wheels meant the plane would hit the river like a thrown stone and then it would be a fight, all of it, physics and luck and whatever mercy the water had in it.

"There's the gravel bar," Jonah said. "Two miles down. Wide, shallow—"

"I see it." She didn't, quite. But she'd seen it in daylight once, three years ago, flying supply runs for a company she was already beginning to hate. A long pale scar of stones mid-river, maybe four hundred feet of it, sloping gently out of the current. "If we make two miles."

"If we make two miles," he agreed, and didn't say the other thing, the thing they were both thinking: *if the engine makes two miles.*

The smoke thickened. It came up through the floor seams now in earnest, stinging her eyes, catching in her throat. The fire wasn't in the cabin—not yet—but it was under them, in the belly of the plane, eating wiring and fabric and who knew what else, and every second it climbed toward the fuel lines was a second borrowed from a lender who always collected.

"The film," Jonah said.

"Is fine." Her hand went to her chest without her permission, pressing flat against the jacket where the negatives rode in their oilskin sleeve, sewn in by lamplight with sail twine, tight against her ribs. Elias Crane's last season. Dead creeks. Orange water. Fish floating white as fingernails. The whole poisoned testimony of a valley, riding over her heart like a second heartbeat. "It's fine. It stays fine."

"You're the bravest person I know," Jonah said, "and also the worst liar."

"Fly your headings, Reyes."

"I'm not flying anything. You are. I'm just talking." A pause, and then, softer: "Left rudder coming up. Bend starts now."

She banked left, gentle, so gentle, and the engine screamed—a high thin note that had nothing to do with power and everything to do with parts that no longer agreed on what they were for. The river swung beneath them, wide and black and beautiful, and somewhere downstream, faint through the racket, she thought she heard the low growl of an outboard motor. Corliss's boat. Running the rapids with its spotlight sweeping the banks.

Let them come. Let them watch. Let them see what it cost.

* * *

One mile.

The engine lost power in steps now, like a man descending stairs he couldn't stop on. Each step she corrected with nose-down and throttle, trading altitude for airspeed, airspeed for distance, running the arithmetic pilots run when the numbers are all going the wrong way. Twelve hundred feet. Nine hundred. Six hundred.

"Gravel bar dead ahead," Jonah said. "Starboard side. There—the pale patch."

And there it was, materializing out of the dark like a promise kept: a long low smear of stones, silvered by moonlight, the river splitting around it in two channels, the near one fast and deep, the far one—

The far one was where the shallows lay. The far one was where she wanted to go.

"Wind's out of the northwest," she said, mostly to herself, hands moving through the checks her body knew better than her mind did. Flaps. Mixture. Fuel—what was left of it. "I come in downwind off the bend, drop it on the bar from the upstream end, wheels first."

"Wheels first into water." Jonah's voice stayed level. "You're going to flip us."

"I'm going to *try* not to flip us."

"That's what I said."

She laughed—one short bark, surprised out of her—and in the middle of everything, in the smoke and the falling sky and the fire chewing through the belly of the plane, it felt like the truest sound she'd made in months. He looked over at her, and even in the red glow of the burning annunciator panel she could see him smiling, and God help her, she smiled back.

"Elias Crane died on a dock waiting for someone to keep a promise," she said. "We're not dying tonight. Neither of us. Say it."

"We're not dying tonight."

"Louder."

"We're not dying *tonight*, Ravensworth."

The engine chose that moment to give its final answer. It didn't quit—it *detonated*, one last convulsion, a bang like a rifle shot, and the propeller windmilled loose and free and the whole aircraft went silent except for the wind in the wires and the crackle of the fire below and the sound of Astrid Ravensworth breathing.

Four hundred feet. The bar rushed up at them.

"Brace," she said, and pushed the nose over.

* * *

The world became noise.

Wheels hit stone and water together—a shattering impact that slammed her forward into the harness, that snapped her head back, that filled the cabin with a shriek of tearing metal. The right main gear folded instantly, crumpling like paper, and the wingtip caught and the plane slewed sideways, digging its left float-strut—she'd never taken the fittings off, thank God, thank God for lazy mechanics and unfinished conversions—into the shallows, and suddenly they weren't flying or crashing but *plowing*, plowing up the gravel bar in a rooster tail of spray and stone, decelerating so hard her vision went gray at the edges.

Something heavy came loose behind them and hit the bulkhead. The windshield starred white. Smoke poured up around her seat now, real smoke, hot smoke, and through the shattered window on her side the river came at her face—

And stopped.

Silence. Total, ringing silence, broken only by the tick of cooling metal and the lap of water against the fuselage and, distantly, the growl of that outboard motor, closer now, maybe a quarter mile downstream.

"Astrid." Jonah's hand found her arm, gripped hard. "Astrid. Talk to me."

"I'm here." Her voice sounded like someone else's. "I'm here. Are you—"

"I'm good. I'm good. Get out. Get out *now*—the fire's in the cabin."

He was already moving, kicking the jammed door twice, three times, and it gave with a groan and cold river air flooded in, sweet as anything she'd ever breathed. She fumbled her harness release with fingers that wouldn't obey, got it on the third try, and hauled herself across the ruined cockpit and out onto the wing strut and down into water that grabbed her to the waist, shockingly cold, gloriously solid.

Jonah came out beside her, and they stood there for one full second, hip-deep in the shallows of the Rivergate, watching the airplane burn.

It burned beautifully. There was no other word for it. Flames crawled up from the wheel wells and along the belly and found the fuel remaining in the wings, and the fire bloomed orange against the black water and the black sky, and the heat pushed against their faces from thirty feet away, and the Beaver—her Beaver, the plane she'd flown for six years, the plane that had carried her out of debt and into ruin and finally into this—settled slowly onto its bent gear and gave itself to the flames like a sacrifice on an altar.

She should have felt grief. She knew that. Some part of her registered that she would feel it later, at three in the morning in some borrowed bed, waking up reaching for a yoke that wasn't there.

But standing in the freezing shallows with the negatives safe against her ribs and Jonah Reyes alive beside her, what Astrid Ravensworth felt was *light*.

Six years of debt. Six years of flying charters for men like Corliss, keeping her mouth shut, keeping her head down, paying interest on a mistake she'd made at twenty-four because she'd been hungry and proud and stupid about the difference between them. Six years of being owned. And now the thing they'd held over her was burning on a gravel bar in the middle of nowhere, and the evidence that could burn them back was stitched against her heart, and the federal inspector was sitting in a hearing room in Current waiting for exactly what she carried.

"Hey." Jonah's voice, close, quiet. "You're laughing."

She was. She couldn't stop. It came out of her in gasps, half-hysterical, half-holy, steam rising off her shoulders in the cold. "We made it," she said. "Jonah. We actually—we *made* it."

"Not yet." But he was grinning too, soaked and singed and bleeding from a cut above his eyebrow he clearly hadn't noticed, and he pulled her against him, and she let him, and for a moment the fire and the river and the distant motor all fell away and there was only the two of them standing in the shallows holding each other up. His heart hammered against her cheek. Hers answered. "But yeah," he said into her hair. "Yeah. We made it."

The outboard growled louder.

They turned together. Downstream, around the bend, a spotlight swept the water—a hard white blade cutting the dark—and the low black shape of a boat came on against the current, riding heavy with men.

"Corliss?" Jonah asked.

"Corliss doesn't run boats at night." Astrid's laughter died. Her hand went flat against her jacket again, checking, confirming. "Corliss is in Current, being arrested or bribing his way out of it. That's somebody else."

The spotlight found the burning plane, held on it, and began to swing toward the shallows—toward them, two figures hip-deep in the water with nowhere left to run.

Jonah stepped slightly in front of her. Old habit. Useless and beautiful.

The boat slowed. A figure stood at the bow, silhouetted against its own light, and raised one hand—not a weapon, a lantern—and called across the water in a voice cracked with age and cold:

"Astrid Ravensworth? Marta sent me. Inspector's men—they've been running the river since sundown looking for you." A pause, and then, almost gently: "Get in the boat, girl. You're late for your own hearing."

* * *

The fire behind them collapsed inward with a sigh, sending up a column of sparks that rose and rose and finally scattered among the stars, and Astrid waded toward the light.
