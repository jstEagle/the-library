## 22. Rivergate and Current

The compass sat on the dash of the Cessna where the old radio had been, its brass face catching the morning sun as Astrid ran up her preflight. Elias Crane's initials were scratched into the back of the case—E.C., 1961—and beneath them, in a shakier hand added later: *North is still north.* She'd read those words a hundred times now. They never got smaller.

"Oil's good," Jonah said, ducking out from under the wing with a rag in his teeth. "Landing gear's good. Pilot's—" He looked her over. "Pilot could use breakfast."

"Pilot had toast."

"Pilot had half a piece of toast and gave the other half to a dog."

"The dog was persuasive."

Jonah grinned and tossed the rag into the baggage compartment. Six months since the river. Six months since she'd crawled out of the shallows on her hands and knees with a waterproof case clutched to her chest like a newborn, while the wreck of the de Havilland burned itself down to black ribs behind them. Six months since the hearing, since Corliss's handcuffs, since the federal indictments came down like weather.

Her license hung in a frame over the workbench at the airfield now—not because she needed it framed, but because Marta Crane had insisted, and nobody in Current told Marta Crane no.

"You're stalling," Jonah said.

"I'm doing a preflight."

"You've done it twice." He climbed into the right seat, the one that used to be empty, and buckled in. "Medicine run. Forty minutes each way. Kids' vaccines and insulin for the Hendersons. The most dangerous thing on this flight is Mrs. Henderson's opinion of your landings."

"My landings are *renowned*."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

She laughed, and it came easy, and that was the thing she was still getting used to—the ease. For years everything had been tight: money, fuel, sleep, the space between herself and the people who might ask questions. Now the sky just opened up in front of her like a road with no tolls.

Astrid keyed the mic. "Current traffic, Cessna One-Seven-Two Bravo, taxiing for departure, runway two-seven."

No one answered. Nobody ever did. But she said it anyway, every time, because there had been months when saying anything into a radio felt like a luxury bought with other people's blood, and she wasn't going to forget that.

She pushed the throttle forward and Current fell away beneath her—the town, the river, the sawtooth hills turning gold at the edges with the first bite of autumn.

* * *

Rivergate Mining had been dead for four months, and the obituary had run in papers across the country.

*INDICTMENTS UNSEALED IN VALLEY POISONING CASE.* *EXECUTIVES CHARGED; FIXER CORLISS PLEADS GUILTY, NAMES NAMES.* *$400 MILLION SETTLEMENT FOR KETTLE RIVER VALLEY.*

Corliss had gone quietly, which surprised everyone who'd met him. Astrid had watched the news footage from the courthouse steps—a man in a good coat, flanked by lawyers, saying nothing while cameras shouted his name. He'd glanced once at the lens, and she'd have sworn he found her in whatever crowd he imagined beyond it. Not a threat. Not a promise. Just acknowledgment, the way one pilot nods to another across a ramp. *You flew it. I didn't.*

He was serving eleven years. The executives above him were serving more. The company's assets were frozen, sold off, carved up by courts, and the cleanup fund was the largest in territorial history.

And the valley—the valley was coming back.

Not fast. Not all at once. But the orange creek they'd found on Crane's map, the one running like rust past the fish floating belly-up, had been capped and rechanneled in July. Crews in white suits had hauled out eleven hundred drums from Camp Nine. The grave pit of contaminated soil had been excavated down to clean gravel and reborn as a monitoring station with a solar panel and a sign: *KETTLE RIVER WATERSHED RESTORATION SITE 1.*

Astrid had flown over it last week, low and slow, and seen a herd of caribou crossing the new creek bed like nothing had ever happened. Like the valley had simply been waiting for someone to stop hurting it.

Marta said it differently. Marta said, "Elias always claimed the land keeps score. He just wanted the score made public before it collected."

* * *

They dropped the medicine at the Hendersons' strip—a mowed hayfield with windsocks at both ends because the wind couldn't make up its mind—and Mrs. Henderson inspected the landing with pursed lips and pronounced it "adequate," which Jonah later declared the highest praise either of them had ever received.

On the way back, they flew the long way. Neither of them suggested it; they just did it, the way you visit a grave.

The canyon opened below them, fog-choked even in September, and Astrid brought the Cessna down into it the way she had that first terrible day with Crane bleeding in the seat behind her—low, slow, following bearings from a dead man's map. The map itself was gone, lost to the rapids, dissolved into the river it had mapped. But she didn't need it anymore. She knew this country by heart now. It lived behind her eyes.

"There," Jonah said softly.

Site One. The monitoring station. And beside it, small from the air but unmistakable, a cairn of stones that hadn't been there in July.

"Marta's been busy," Astrid said.

"Elias Crane, prospector," Jonah read, though at this distance he couldn't possibly be reading anything. "I talked to her about moving him up here, you know. After the hearing. She said he'd spent his whole life looking for something worth finding, and the creek finally showed him. Said he'd want to stay."

Astrid held the plane steady, wings level, and let the moment sit. Below, the water ran clear over clean gravel. You could see the bottom. That alone still caught in her throat sometimes—water you could see through, in a valley where for twenty years every creek had run the color of old wounds.

"He got his claim after all," she said.

"Yeah." Jonah reached over and put his hand on hers where it rested on the throttle. "Biggest one in the territory."

* * *

The debt was gone. That was the part she still woke up some mornings not believing.

When the indictments unsealed, the financing arrangement Rivergate had used to hold her charter contract—and her fuel accounts, and eventually her license—had come apart like wet paper. The federal receiver handling Rivergate's assets had reviewed her file, found the complaint against her license had been filed by a company under criminal investigation specifically to silence a witness, and thrown it out so hard it left a mark on the wall. Her license had been restored by mail. A letter, an apology of sorts, a stamp. Eleven years of flying reduced to an envelope.

She'd stood in Marta's kitchen holding it and cried, which embarrassed her until Marta said, "Good. Let it out. Then we'll have coffee," and that was that.

The creditors had settled for pennies rather than fight a bankruptcy tangled in a criminal case. The de Havilland was a write-off—insured, barely, and the payout plus the settlement of the fraudulent debt had left her, for the first time in her adult life, solvent. She'd bought the Cessna with cash. Used, ugly, honest. It smelled like avgas and Jonah's engine degreaser and, faintly, forever, like smoke.

Some nights she dreamed about the burning hangar. Corliss's man with the gas can. The flames climbing the wing of the plane with the negatives locked inside, and her decision arriving whole, without debate, the way real decisions do: *fly it out.* Smoke in the cockpit, the engine coughing, Jonah shouting headings over the roar, and then the river rising up to meet her and the hard slam, the hiss, the cold.

In the dreams she always landed it. In life, she had too. She tried to keep the ratio that way.

"You're quiet," Jonah said. They were back over Current now, circling for the approach.

"Just thinking about how much I hate this town's runway."

"It's a hayfield with delusions."

"I'm going to grease it on so smooth Mrs. Henderson feels it in her soul."

"She'll call it adequate anyway. It's her love language."

Astrid laughed and rolled out of the turn, and Current rose up to meet her—the town, the river, the council hall with its new roof (paid for, the plaque said, by the Kettle Valley Restoration Fund), the airfield where the hangar had burned and been rebuilt twice as big with Jonah's name on the lease.

She greased it on. Perfectly. Mrs. Henderson wasn't there to see it, which Astrid considered a personal mercy.

* * *

That evening, the airfield office smelled of coffee and solder. Jonah was rewiring the radio bench—there was always something to fix; that was Jonah, a man who expressed affection through torque wrenches—and Astrid sat at the desk going over the winter schedule. Medicine runs Tuesdays and Fridays. Mail when the weather allowed. Charter work only for people whose questions she liked.

There was a knock, and Marta came in out of the cold with a tin wrapped in a tea towel and snow in her gray hair.

"Bannock," she said, setting it on the desk. "And I need a favor."

"Name it."

"The school wants me to talk to the kids about Elias. About what happened. And I will, but—" She paused, and Astrid saw for the first time how tired she looked, and how unbowed. "I thought maybe you'd come. Tell them about the flight. Kids should know the truth doesn't deliver itself. Somebody has to carry it."

Astrid looked at the compass on the dash of the Cessna visible through the open hangar door, brass face glowing in the last light.

"I'll come," she said. "But Marta—it wasn't me. I just flew. Elias carried it for twenty years. You carried it. The trapper carried it. I just happened to be the one holding the controls when it was time to land."

Marta smiled, thin and fierce. "Elias used to say the same thing about himself. 'I just swung the hammer.' The hammer doesn't decide where it falls, Astrid. The hands do." She tapped the desk once, gently, and turned for the door. "Tuesday. Four o'clock. Bring the compass if you want. Kids like objects."

* * *

After she'd gone, Jonah set down his soldering iron and came around the desk. He didn't say anything for a while. He was good at silence, Jonah. He'd learned it watching her, all those years when she kept everyone at wingspan's length.

"Tuesday," he said finally. "School talk. Then Wednesday we fly the Henderson insulin again. Thursday the survey crew wants a lift upriver. Friday medicine." He counted on his fingers, theatrical. "Astrid Ravensworth, bush pilot with a full schedule and a clean license. However will you survive the tedium."

"I'll manage. I hear the tedium has excellent bannock."

He laughed, then went quiet again, and she knew what was coming before he said it, because she'd known this man for eleven years and loved him for most of them and he only went quiet like this about one subject.

"I ordered the ring," he said.

She kept her eyes on the schedule. Her heart was doing something undignified.

"Oh?"

"Size seven and a quarter. Don't act surprised, you left the catalog open on the kitchen table for three weeks like a man leaving bait for a trout."

"I did no such thing."

"You circled one."

"In *pen*. Casually."

"Astrid." He came around and knelt there by the desk chair, in the smell of solder and coffee, with the hangar door open behind him and the first stars coming out over the rebuilt hangar and the river running dark and clean toward the sea. "Ask me or don't. But I'm telling you straight, the way I tell you everything: I'm not going anywhere. I loved you when you were broke and hunted and too proud to eat. I'll love you solvent. I'll love you grounded. I'll love you at forty thousand feet. The conditions were never the point. You were the point."

She looked at him for a long time. Outside, the wind sock turned lazily, pointing nowhere in particular, and somewhere down the valley a wolf started up, and the compass on the dash of the Cessna held its needle steady on north, because north is still north, and some things don't move no matter what the storm does.

"Yes," she said.

"I haven't actually asked yet—"

"Yes, Jonah. Obviously yes. Ask properly tomorrow. Tonight I'm flying us home before the fog comes down the canyon, and I want my hands free."

He stood up laughing, and she pulled him in and kissed him, and the radio bench hummed, and the bannock cooled on the desk, and none of it mattered except all of it did.

* * *

Later, walking out to the plane under the stars, Astrid stopped at the door and looked back at the airfield—at the hangar rebuilt from the ashes of the one Corliss burned, at the runway that was really a hayfield with delusions, at the town strung along the river like beads on a wire.

Rivergate and Current. She'd named the story in her head long ago, back when it was just a debt and a dying man and a map she shouldn't have taken. She'd thought it meant the company and the town. Two forces, opposed.

Now she understood it differently. Every rivergate opens onto a current. Every wall is also a door. Elias Crane had died on a dock holding that truth in an oilskin wrap, and she had carried it the rest of the way, and the current had done the rest—currents always do, given time and enough stubborn hands.

She climbed in, checked the compass out of habit, out of ritual, out of remembrance. North. Still north.

"Current traffic," she said into the mic, keying it, smiling, "Cessna One-Seven-Two Bravo, departing runway two-seven, heading home."

This time, faintly, through the static, someone answered.

"*Roger, Bravo. Welcome home.*"

She laughed out loud, alone in the cockpit with the whole lit sky ahead of her, and pushed the throttle forward, and flew.
