## 1. The River Keeps Its Own Books

The Rivergate ran high and brown that spring, the color of strong tea left too long, and Orsen Vasquez loved it best like this — swollen, impatient, rewriting its own margins.

She stood on the cut bank two miles downstream of Harrow's Ferry with a leveling rod in one hand and a thermos of black coffee going cold in the other, watching the water do what water always did after a flood: confess. Every river kept books. Most people never learned to read them, but five years of contract surveying had taught Orsen that a flood was just an audit — the river going through its accounts, moving silt from one ledger to another, and occasionally, if you knew where to look, leaving an entry exposed that somebody upstream had tried very hard to bury.

The flood had come through eleven days ago. The ministry's hydrology office had posted their damage assessments within forty-eight hours, all of them wrong in the same direction, because ministry men surveyed from helicopters and helicopters couldn't see what a flood did to a bank at eye level. Orsen could. She'd been hired by the ferry cooperative to assess scour and bank stability along six miles of the lower reach, a small contract, three weeks' work, the kind of job she took between larger jobs to keep her instruments honest and her name off anything that mattered.

That was the arrangement she'd built her life on: competent enough to be hired, mobile enough to be forgotten. Five years of it. She could pack her gear into two cases and a rod tube in under twenty minutes, and she had done it in under twenty minutes more times than she liked to count.

She drank the cold coffee and started walking the bank.

* * *

The morning was doing that thing early spring mornings did along the Rivergate — fog burning off in slow rags, herons standing in the shallows like punctuation marks, the smell of wet clay and rotting willow. Orsen worked the toe of the cut bank methodically, pacing out intervals, shooting elevations, logging everything in the waterproof field book she'd bought used and filled with a hand so cramped her old professors would have wept. Bank crest at 14.2 meters above gauge datum. Scour depth at station 3+40: 1.9 meters, exposing a lens of older alluvium, gray-blue, compacted. Standard stuff. The kind of data that went into a report nobody read until something collapsed.

At station 4+10 she stopped pacing.

The flood had undercut the bank here and dropped a slab of turf the size of a dining table onto the point bar below, tipping it forward like a drawer pulled open. In the raw face where the slab had torn away, the river had sliced a clean cross-section through sixty years of sediment — dark organic layers, a band of orange flood sand, more organics — and at the base of the cut, right at the waterline, something that didn't belong.

Wood. Lacquered wood, black as a beetle's back, maybe thirty centimeters of corner protruding from the blue-gray clay like the prow of something very large and very patient. The lacquer hadn't craze-cracked the way old furniture lacquer did. It had the deep, glassy finish of work made to be sealed against water, made by people who expected their things to get wet and planned for it.

Orsen crouched. She told herself she was assessing whether the object represented a hazard to the bank profile, which was technically true and completely beside the point. She touched the lacquer with one fingertip. Cold. Harder than it should be. Under the mud film, the surface was unblemished.

She looked up and down the reach. A fisherman's skiff sat anchored mid-channel near the ferry landing, too far away to make out faces. Otherwise the river belonged to her and the herons.

She should have photographed it in place, flagged it, called it in. That was procedure. Unidentified cultural material in a survey corridor went to the ministry antiquities desk, which logged it, claimed it, and buried it in a warehouse in the capital for a decade while three lawyers argued about jurisdiction. She knew how that machine worked. Everyone who worked river contracts knew.

Instead she took out her trowel.

Not to dig — not yet. She cleared the mud from around the protruding corner, working gently, the way she'd been taught by a woman whose name she didn't let herself think about, and the shape emerged slowly. A chest, or part of one. The corner joinery was dovetailed, precise, the kind of craftsmanship that came from a workshop tradition, not a farmstead. And set into the wood where the lid met the body, half-swallowed by clay, was metal. Stamped metal — a fitting, or a seal plate, about the size of her palm.

She worked around it for twenty minutes, heart doing something steady and strange, and freed enough of the plate to read it.

A device was stamped deep into the surface: a bridge over water, flanked by two uprights that might have been towers or reeds or hands. Around the device ran a border of script in an alphabet she didn't recognize — angular, confident, nothing like the trade script used along the Rivergate, nothing like anything she'd seen in five years of pulling bottles and fence posts and one memorable Model T out of riverbanks.

Orsen sat back on her heels in the mud.

She was good at identification. It was half her trade — knowing what a thing was by its corrosion, its context, its manufacturing tells. This thing refused to be identified. It was old enough to have settled beneath a meter of undisturbed alluvium, which meant decades at minimum, and preserved well enough to suggest deliberate sealing, which meant intention, which meant someone had wanted this chest to survive being lost.

And it was stamped. Things got stamped when they belonged to institutions. Governments. Guilds. Nations.

"Nation," she said aloud, and the word sat oddly in the fog.

She photographed the exposure — she wasn't a fool, she documented everything, documentation was armor — then made a decision she would spend a long time not examining. She didn't flag the site. She didn't call it in. She noted the station number in her book and, beside it, wrote *bank slumping, no material observed*, which was the first lie she'd put in a field book in five years, and it went down smooth as oil.

Then she pried loose the one thing she could carry without a crowbar.

It had worked free of the chest during the flood — she found it a meter downstream in the scour line, half-buried in sand, and she only saw it because the light hit it at the exact right angle. Coin-sized. Heavier than a coin. Bronze or brass, worn nearly smooth, but on one face the same device survived: the bridge, the uprights, and around the rim, a fragment of that unknown script. On the reverse, a numeral. Seven, maybe. Or a letter that looked like seven.

She turned it over in her palm. It was warm already, warmed by her hand, as if it had been waiting to be picked up.

Taking it was theft. She knew exactly what it was — cultural material, unreported, removed from context, the whole litany. She'd written reports condemning less. But standing there with the river talking its endless brown monologue and the fog lifting off the water in sheets, Orsen found that the theft felt clean. Cleaner than anything she'd done in years. Every contract she'd signed lately had felt like a small surrender — survey the channel, don't ask why the ministry wanted the reach mapped, take the money, move along before anyone learned her surname and did the arithmetic on it. Her whole life had become a series of things not reported. One more, at least, would be hers.

She pocketed the token, finished the day's stations like a professional, and rowed back to the ferry landing as the light went long and gold across the water.

* * *

Harrow's Ferry was four buildings and a church, and Orsen lodged in the third floor of the ferry cooperative's office, a room with a cot, a desk, and a window over the river that she paid extra for and pretended she hadn't. She ate dinner at the cooperative's common table with the ferrymen, who talked about water levels the way other men talked about weather, and afterward she climbed to her room and put the token on the desk under her lamp.

Under lamplight it gave up a little more. The bronze had a greenish bloom in the recesses of the stamping, and the rim showed file marks — finished by hand, individually, not struck from a die in quantity. Someone had made these one at a time, or in small numbers, for people who mattered. A credential. A token of membership. A seal to press into wax, maybe, though the metal was too thick for that; more likely it was meant to be carried, shown, believed.

She thought about the alphabet again. Angular, upright, dense. She'd catalogued every script she'd ever encountered on river work — trade marks, ministry stamps, the occasional gravestone Latin — and this matched none of them. Which meant either some local guild she'd somehow never heard of, or something from outside the valley entirely, something that had come down the river and gone under.

There was a map on her wall. Not a working chart — a proper historical atlas page she'd bought in a capital bookshop years ago, the kind of purchase that looked decorative and was actually a habit she couldn't break: buying maps of places she'd never survey, tracing routes she'd never run. She took the token to the map and held it against the glass, next to the Rivergate's course, and let her eyes wander the valley the way the atlas drew it two hundred years back.

The atlas named everything. Every tributary, every rapids, every ferry crossing, every minor duchy and dead town. That was the genre's whole conceit — completeness. And yet her eye snagged, as it always snagged, on the stretch of the upper escarpment country where the cartographer had done something strange: he'd drawn the terrain in full detail, hills hatched, rivers inked, villages marked — and then labeled none of it. Just blank space where names should be, as if the engraver had been handed the geography and forbidden the history.

She'd noticed it before and filed it under printing error. Tonight, with the token warm in her fist, it looked less like an error and more like a scar.

"Who were you," she said to the token.

It didn't answer. Tokens never did. But it sat there on the desk with its little stamped bridge, and Orsen caught herself doing something she hadn't done in a long time: caring. Actually caring, past the professional interest, down in the place where she kept everything she'd decided not to want. She wanted to know. She wanted it the way she'd once wanted things, before she'd learned that wanting things led to staying places, and staying places led to people knowing your name, and her name was a door she kept locked for reasons that had a woman's face attached to them.

She put the token in the tin box where she kept her passport and her mother's last letter — unopened, five years old, forwarded through three addresses before she'd let it catch up with her — and shut the box, and lay on the cot listening to the river.

Sleep came slow. When it came, she dreamed of a corridor of black lacquered wood stretching away underwater, and script running along the walls like a current, and somewhere far off a voice reading names aloud, one after another, patient as water.

* * *

Three days later she'd surveyed the rest of the reach and was back at the cut bank with a permit amendment application she had no intention of filing, photographing the chest corner again now that the river had dropped half a meter and given up more of it. The flood had done her excavation for her. Another hand's width of the lid was exposed, and along the visible edge ran a strip of brass inlay, and in the inlay, repeated three times, the same word in that same unknown script.

She was sketching it into her book — sketching, not logging, the distinction mattering enormously to her and to no one else — when she heard boots on the point bar behind her.

"You're the surveyor." A man's voice, pleasant, unhurried. "Vasquez, isn't it? The ferry co-op said you were working this stretch."

He was fifty-ish, dressed like a fisherman and carrying himself like a clerk, and he stood looking at the cut bank with his hands in his pockets, at the exposed corner of black lacquer, for exactly as long as a man with no interest in it would look.

"Flood moved a lot of material," Orsen said. "Standard post-event assessment."

"Mm," he said. "They say the river keeps its own books."

"That's what I tell my clients."

"And does it ever show you something you weren't expecting?" He smiled. It was a good smile, easy, the kind that made itself at home. "Old bottles, usually. My uncle found a cannon once, up near the escarpment. Ministry took it away and nobody ever heard another word about it." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Anyway. Enjoying the weather."

He walked on downstream, unhurried, and Orsen watched him go and did not move for a full minute after he'd rounded the willows.

That evening, back in her room, she took the tin box down and sat with it on her knees for a long time. Then she took out the token, and then — because the day had loosened something, or because the man's smile had frightened her in a way she refused to name — she took out the letter, five years old, soft at the folds, and read the return address properly for the first time since it had chased her down.

*Dr. I. Vasquez, Department of Antiquities Concessions.*

Her mother's handwriting on the envelope was as precise as joinery. Orsen didn't open it. She wasn't ready for that particular audit. But she looked at the department name, and she looked at the token lying on the desk with its stamped bridge and its foreign script, and she felt the two facts lean toward each other across the room like magnets finding their line.

The river kept its own books. Fine. But somewhere upstream, apparently, somebody else kept them too — somebody who'd erased a name off every map in the valley and then come looking, smiling, when the river tried to give it back.

Orsen locked the token in the box, and in the morning there was a letter waiting for her at the ferry office, official cream stock, a ministry seal in wax, and her own name — her full name, the one she'd spent five years keeping off paperwork — typed without a single error.

She stood holding it in the doorway while the ferrymen stepped around her, and the river ran on below, brown and patient, turning its pages.
