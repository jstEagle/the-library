## 2. A Dead Nation's Seal

The token rode in Orsen's breast pocket for six days before she let herself look at it properly.

She told herself it was practicality — that a surveyor didn't pull strange metal out of a flood-scoured bank and then forget about it, that leaving it in a drawer would be worse than carrying it, that any competent professional would want to know what they'd found. She told herself these things while making coffee at four in the morning, while calibrating the depth sounder on the *Marrow*, while running transects upriver past the town of Grayling with the river's brown water sliding under the hull like something poured rather than flowing.

The truth was simpler and less flattering: she couldn't stop touching it.

It was smaller than a coin, heavier than its size suggested, and warm in a way metal shouldn't be after six days against her skin. The face bore a stamped design — not struck, she'd decided, but pressed into soft metal with a die, the way wax took a seal. A river, rendered as three parallel lines bending around a central peak. Beneath the peak, characters in an alphabet she didn't recognize: angular, deliberate, each glyph built from strokes that met at right angles, as if whoever had designed them had distrusted curves.

The reverse was smoother, worn nearly blank by handling. Someone had carried this thing for years. Someone's thumb had polished it the way water polishes stone.

On the seventh day, moored at Grayling waiting out a rain squall that turned the Rivergate the color of strong tea, she stopped lying to herself and went to see Vess.

* * *

Antiquities brokers clustered where rivers did — at confluences, at crossings, in towns old enough to have buried things worth finding. Grayling had two. One worked out of a converted chapel with bars on the windows and dealt mostly in forged Roman glass to tourists who wanted to believe. The other was Vess.

Vess kept shop above a chandlery, down a hallway that smelled of lamp oil and old paper, behind a door with no sign at all. He was seventy if he was a day, thin as a wading bird, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and hands that trembled until they touched an object — at which point they became, miraculously, steady.

"You're Marchetti's girl," he said when she came in. "No — Vasquez. The daughter."

"I'm a freelance hydrographic surveyor," Orsen said. "I need something identified. Discreetly."

"Everyone who comes through that door needs something discreetly." He held out his hand. "That's what discretion costs. Sit."

She sat. She did not give him the token. Instead she laid it on a square of chamois she'd prepared, folded over so only the stamped face showed, and watched him lean across the desk with the unhurried hunger of a man who had spent fifty years learning to read objects the way priests read scripture.

His breath changed first. Then his hands, hovering above the chamois without touching, began very slightly to shake again.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you get this?"

"Flood scour. Below the escarpment reach, near the old ferry cut." She'd rehearsed the answer and hated how quickly it came. "A bank slumped after the spring crest. This was in the exposed strata."

"In situ." Vess said the word the way other men said *holy*. He finally touched it — one fingertip, lifting the token free of the cloth as though it might object. He turned it toward the window light. "River. Mountain. Karvathi script. Do you know what you're holding, Miss Vasquez?"

"That's why I'm here."

He set it down with exaggerated care, removed his glasses, and looked at her with eyes gone suddenly sharp behind the wet-film blur of age. "Then you'll forgive me for asking whether you're here because you don't know, or because you know enough to be frightened."

"Both," Orsen said. "In that order."

Something like approval moved through the old man's face. He rose, crossed to a cabinet that clearly held more than cabinets usually hold — she heard locks disengage, plural — and returned with a flat folio bound in cracked green leather. He opened it on the desk between them like a man laying out cards he'd been saving for a long game.

"Sixty years ago," he said, "there were eleven nations with territory along the middle Rivergate. You could name them off a school map. Corvane. Tessaly Reach. The Free Cantons. And Karvath." He turned the folio toward her. It held a map — an original, hand-tinted, the kind of document that made Orsen's professional heart ache — and there, between the escarpment and the eastern bank, sat a country drawn in confident ink. Rivers marked. Towns named. A border surveyed and agreed.

"And now?" Orsen asked, though she already knew. Everyone knew the shape of the absence even if no one knew its contents. There was a stretch of the middle Rivergate that every modern chart labeled simply *Territory — Administration Pending*, in the passive voice of bureaucrats describing something they preferred not to look at. Sixty years of pending.

"Now there is a border war nobody won, a treaty signed in a room full of tired men, and a decision — made at ministerial level, never debated publicly — that Karvath had ceased to exist." Vess tapped the map. "Not defeated. Not annexed, formally. *Erased.* Its name struck from the registers. Its towns renamed or left unnamed. Its records consolidated into ministry archives that require permissions no one grants. I have seen catalogues, Miss Vasquez, listing Karvathi holdings — and I have never once seen a single item released from them. Not a coin. Not a potsherd. Not a page."

"The descendants," Orsen said slowly. "There were people. Where did the people go?"

"Downriver, mostly. Into the river villages that were allowed to keep their names because renaming forty fishing settlements was more paperwork than anyone wanted." Vess's mouth twisted. "They exist. They are legally ghosts. Their grandparents' land is administration-pending. Their language isn't taught in schools because officially there was never such a language. And anything material that proves they were ever *here*—" he lifted the token between two fingers, "—is contraband of a very quiet kind. Not illegal to own, technically. But findable. Catalogued. Reported. And once reported, it disappears into the same archive that swallowed everything else."

Orsen looked at the token in his fingers and felt the pocket-shaped absence against her chest where it had lived all week.

"What does it say?" she asked. "The script."

Vess bent close, lips moving. His trembling hands had gone steady again, holding the little disc up to the light.

"It's a household mark," he murmured. "Karvathi river families registered their claims with tokens like this — pressed lead, river-and-peak, the family line beneath. This one reads..." He squinted. "*Ondrel. Of the third bend.*" He lowered it. "A land claim, Miss Vasquez. Somebody's proof that their family owned a piece of the world. Carried, most likely, on the day everything was taken from them."

The rain had thickened against the windows. Somewhere below, the chandlery man called out to someone about rope. Orsen sat very still and thought about a bank of gray mud giving up a corner of lacquered wood and a single small weight, and about how the river kept its own books and paid out its debts sixty years late.

"How many of these survive?" she asked.

"On the open market?" Vess smiled without humor. "None. Officially none exist. Unofficially, I've handled two in fifty years, both smuggled, both bought within the hour by buyers whose names I wasn't given and didn't ask for." He set the token back on the chamois and slid it toward her, and his eyes when they met hers were not the eyes of a merchant anymore. They were the eyes of a man watching a younger version of himself walk into weather he recognized. "Take it back. Don't show it to anyone else. And Miss Vasquez — sell me nothing, tell me nothing more, and go home by the back stairs."

"Why?"

"Because you asked your questions in the order of a woman who intends to do something about the answers," Vess said, "and in my experience, that is precisely the kind of person the ministry's letters find first."

* * *

The letter found her thirty-one hours later.

She knew it was coming the way you know weather — not from evidence but from pressure. She'd spent the day after Vess's running sonar lines downriver, doing honest work badly, her mind circling the token hidden inside a false bottom in her instrument case. She'd weighed selling it. She'd weighed throwing it in the river. She'd weighed, at two in the morning, moored in the dark below the escarpment with its black mass shouldering up against the stars, doing exactly nothing, which was the family specialty.

The letter was waiting at the Grayling post office, addressed in a clerk's immaculate hand:

**ORSEN M. VASQUEZ — LICENSED SURVEYOR, REG. 4471**

Ministry of Interior Records and Territorial Administration. The letterhead alone was a door closing.

*Dear Licensed Surveyor,* it began, in prose that had been sanded smooth of all human texture. *It has come to the Ministry's attention that artifacts of unregistered provenance may have been recovered during recent contract work within the Rivergate corridor (Contract GR-8841, Grayling Hydrographic Services). Under Statute 12 of the Territorial Records Act, all materials of potential historical significance recovered within administered territories must be declared within fourteen days of recovery. Failure to declare constitutes...*

Orsen read the rest standing in the post office doorway while the rain came down and the river ran brown and fast beyond the pilings. The statute numbers marched on. The penalties assembled themselves in orderly rows: fines, license revocation, referral to territorial prosecutors. And then, near the end, one sentence that was not like the others — one sentence that had been written by a different hand, or at least by a different mood:

*The Ministry notes that Licensed Surveyor Vasquez's professional history includes certain associations which may bear upon the Ministry's assessment of her good faith in this matter.*

Her professional history. Her *associations*.

She stood there with the paper shaking slightly in her grip and understood two things at once, the way you understand lightning and thunder as a single event. First: someone had been watching her since the bank at the ferry cut — maybe since before, maybe always, maybe everyone who shared her surname lived under that particular lens. Second: the sentence hadn't been included to inform her. It had been included to frighten her, and it was working, and whoever wrote it knew exactly which name to press.

Because there was only one association that mattered. Only one name that followed her like a shadow with better credentials.

*Vasquez.*

* * *

The summons arrived the next morning, and it arrived wrong.

Not by post. A boy of perhaps twelve brought it to the dock at dawn, rowing out to the *Marrow* in a skiff too big for him, handing over a heavy cream-colored envelope sealed with actual wax before Orsen had finished tying off. No return address. Her name written in fountain pen, in handwriting she had not seen in five years and recognized before she finished drawing the first breath of that morning — the sharp downstrokes, the way the capital V stabbed downward like something driven into earth.

She made coffee she didn't drink. She sat in the cabin with the envelope on the chart table between the depth logs and the tide tables, and outside the rain had stopped and the escarpment stood clear against a washed sky, and she thought about all the miles she had put between herself and that handwriting. Five years. Four hundred contracts. Two continents' worth of rivers, none of them this one, because this one flowed out of the same country her mother's reputation flowed from, and she had planned it that way with a cartographer's precision.

The past, it turned out, had found a forwarding address.

She broke the seal.

Inside, a single card, cream to match the envelope, and beneath the printed formalities — *Dr. Ilse Vasquez requests the pleasure* — a handwritten line, brief as a scalpel cut:

*You have something of mine. Ninety days. Come to Kilbride House before Thursday, or the Ministry's next letter will be longer than its last.*

Orsen read it three times.

*Something of mine.* Not *something you found.* Mine. As if Ilse Vasquez had already claimed the token from sixty miles away, from whatever network of dealers and informants fed her intelligence the way tributaries fed the Rivergate. As if the flood had washed up more than lead and lacquer — as if it had washed up Orsen herself, dredged her out of her careful anonymous life and set her down, dripping, in the current her mother swam in.

And underneath the anger, unwelcome as groundwater seeping through a foundation, another feeling entirely: the old, humiliating quickening of the pulse. *Come home. Be seen. Be measured against me again.* Five years of outrunning it, and one sentence in her mother's handwriting had her heart hammering like a first-year surveyor's.

Orsen got up, walked to the rail, and looked at the escarpment — at the long stone shoulder hiding whatever the flood had uncovered, at the brown river carrying somebody's stolen land claim down toward villages of people who weren't allowed to remember being a nation.

Ninety days. The permits, the concession, the clock — she didn't know the details yet, but she knew the shape of them, the way she knew the shape of a drowned channel before the sonar confirmed it. Her mother was digging for something below that escarpment, and her mother's time was running out, and her mother had just reached sixty miles upriver and closed a hand around her daughter's ankle.

Orsen went back inside, took the token from its false bottom, and set it on the chart table beside the card. River, mountain, angular script. *Ondrel, of the third bend.*

"You're mine," she told it quietly. "Whatever you are. Whoever you belonged to. That part's not negotiable."

Then she pulled her charts toward her, found the rail line to Kilbride, and started planning the trip she'd sworn she'd never make — telling herself, with the last of her honesty, that she was going for the money, for the license, for self-preservation.

Telling herself, and knowing the river didn't keep books that bad.
