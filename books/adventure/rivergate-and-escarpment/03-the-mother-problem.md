## 3. The Mother Problem

The summons came on a Tuesday, which felt like a joke someone had forgotten to finish.

Orsen had spent the morning recalibrating her flow gauges at the Marlow Street office — a rented room above a chandlery that smelled of tar and old rope, where she kept her instruments in foam-lined cases and her life in two duffel bags. The knock came at eleven. Not the mail carrier's double rap; something softer, more deliberate, the way you'd knock on a door you already knew would open.

The courier was young, gray-suited, and carried an envelope of heavy cream stock with no stamp and no return address. Just her name, typed: **O. VASQUEZ**. No "Ms." No "Orsen." The initial and the surname, the way a file would read it.

She signed for it because refusing to sign would have been its own kind of answer, and she wasn't ready to give any answers yet.

Inside was a single card, letterpress-printed, the kind of stationery that cost more per sheet than she made in a day:

*Dr. Ilse Vasquez requests the pleasure of your company. Thursday, seven o'clock. The Halvard Room, Grand Meridian Hotel.*

Below it, in different ink — fountain pen, blue-black, a hand she knew better than her own handwriting — one line:

*We should talk about the token before someone else does.*

Orsen sat down on the case holding her theodolite and read the line four times. The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way a boat does when a wake comes through from far away.

Her mother knew about the coin.

Which meant her mother had eyes in Rivergate City, or at least ears, and had known about the token since before the broker — since possibly before Orsen herself had pulled it out of the flood-scoured bank and slid it into her coat pocket with hands that shook only afterward. Which meant this wasn't a summons. It was a net, drawn closed with beautiful stationery.

She thought about not going for exactly thirty-six hours. She thought about it while eating, while running her evening survey lines, while lying awake in the narrow bed listening to the river work at the pilings below her window. She thought about all the places she could be by Thursday — upriver, downriver, across the strait, gone the way she'd gotten good at going.

But the card said *before someone else does*, and that was the hook set deep. Because someone else *was*. The ministry letter had come within a day of the broker's identification, polite as a knife laid on a pillow, requesting that any artifacts recovered from the recent flood event be registered under Statute 44-C. And now Ilse Vasquez wanted to talk about it first.

In her mother's world, being talked to first was either an honor or a sentence. Usually both.

* * *

The last time Orsen had stood in a hotel room with her mother, she'd been nineteen and the room had been in Karvath-adjacent territory neither of them named — a conference hotel outside Vessik, all glass and imported marble, paid for by a university that would later pretend it had never funded the dig.

That had been the summer everything broke.

She remembered it in fragments now, the way you remember a flood: not the whole water, just what it left stranded. Her mother at a podium, silver-haired at fifty even then, saying the words *preliminary findings suggest* with the calm of a surgeon announcing complications. Reporters leaning in. A ministry liaison standing at the back of the room with his arms crossed, his face doing nothing at all, which was somehow worse than anger.

And later — much later, past midnight — her mother in the hotel bar, still in her lecture clothes, telling the man from the ministry, quietly, over untouched drinks: *If we publish the full stratigraphy, your government will have to acknowledge continuous settlement predating the annexation. You understand what that does to every land grant issued since.* And the man saying nothing, and her mother understanding the silence perfectly, because silence was a language she'd been fluent in for decades.

Orsen hadn't been meant to hear any of it. She'd been meant to be asleep, or studying, or being the daughter who existed at the edges of her mother's brilliance like a moon too small to raise tides. But she'd come down for her forgotten room key and stopped behind a pillar, and she'd heard her mother trade truth for access — heard her say, in effect, *I will bury it if you let me keep digging somewhere else.*

Two weeks later the scandal broke anyway — leaked from a direction Ilse never identified, though Orsen always suspected the ministry had burned her deliberately, a demonstration of what happened to archaeologists who tried to negotiate. Careers ended. Colleagues scattered. Her father, who had never been more than a footnote in Ilse's story, took Orsen and moved three hundred miles downriver and never once said his wife's name aloud again.

Orsen had said hers constantly, for years — angrily, in arguments, in letters never sent. Then she'd stopped saying it at all, and discovered that a name unused goes quiet in the mouth, like a path nobody walks.

Five years of freelance survey contracts. Five years of measuring rivers that didn't care whose daughter she was. Five years of miles.

Now here was the cream-colored card, and the miles had run out.

* * *

The Halvard Room at the Grand Meridian was on the fourteenth floor, and it was less a hotel room than a statement — sitting area, wet bar, windows overlooking the Rivergate's industrial bend where the cranes stood against the dusk like patient herons. Ilse Vasquez stood at those windows with her back to the door, and Orsen hated how instantly recognizable she was from behind: the straight spine, the silver hair pinned with mathematical precision, the stillness of a woman who had learned long ago that moving first was losing.

"You look well," Ilse said, without turning around. "River work agrees with you."

"You know what I look like," Orsen said. "You've had me watched."

"I've had the *token* watched." Her mother turned then, and Orsen braced herself and looked at her properly for the first time in five years. Older. The silver had won more territory. But the eyes were the same — pale, assessing, the eyes of a woman who read landscapes and daughters with the same instrument. "Sit down, Orsen. Please."

There was a decanter on the sideboard and two glasses, already poured. Orsen didn't touch hers. She sat on the edge of the sofa, knees together, duffel-bag posture in a silk-upholstered room.

"Ninety days," Ilse said. "That's what remains on my concession permits. After that, the escarpment site reverts to ministry control, and everything my team has spent two years finding goes into a warehouse drawer." She lifted her own glass but didn't drink. "I need a surveyor. Specifically, I need the best hydrographic and subsurface surveyor working the Rivergate basin, which — according to three independent references I purchased last month — is you."

"Purchased."

"Commissioned. You were expensive to find. You've been careful." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like acknowledgment of a move well played. "You change contract names twice a year. You invoice through a holding company in Corren Port. You haven't published so much as a technical note in four years, which is a waste, frankly, because your methodology paper on flood-scour stratigraphy was genuinely elegant."

"I wrote that when I was twenty-three."

"Yes. Before you decided the world shouldn't get to read you." Ilse set the glass down. "You hid very thoroughly, Orsen. It took me eighteen months to find you after the flood report flagged the bank anomaly. Do you know why it took so long? Because you're good. I want that on the record between us: I'm proud of how well you disappeared. It's the only thing I ever taught you that you mastered completely."

The words landed the way her mother's words always landed — praise shaped like a blade. Orsen kept her voice flat. "What do you want from me?"

"A ninety-day contract. Subsurface mapping below the eastern talus slope. Your instruments are better than anything my team has, and your instincts are better than your instruments." Ilse finally sat, across from her, hands folded — the lecture posture, the podium posture. "You'll have full authority over your survey lines. Full credit in any internal documentation. Pay at double your standard rate, which I also know."

"And in exchange I hand myself back into range of whatever destroyed you at Vessik."

"In exchange," Ilse said, "you stop pretending the river is a place to hide and admit it's a place to work. And you do it under my protection rather than theirs."

"The ministry sent me a letter."

"I know. I've seen it. Statute 44-C registration demand — boilerplate, but the timing wasn't. They flagged the broker's inquiry within hours. Someone at the antiquities house reports directly to Dallet's office."

"Dallet?"

Ilse waved a hand, dismissive, though Orsen caught the flicker of genuine distaste beneath it. "Ministry observer assigned to my concession. He'll be at camp within the week. A courteous man. That's what makes him dangerous." She leaned forward slightly. "Here is what happens if you refuse me, Orsen, and I'm going to say it plainly because you've earned plain speech. The moment you refuse, you become a loose end. A freelance surveyor with no institutional affiliation, no publication record, no legal counsel on retainer, found to be in unregistered possession of a Karvathi artifact recovered from a protected flood zone. Statute 44-C carries a minimum fine that would take you six years to pay off at your current rates. The ministry doesn't need to prosecute you. They just need to *open a file*. Files have gravity. Files follow people. Ask me how I know."

The room was very quiet. Down on the river, a barge horn sounded, long and low, and Orsen felt it in her sternum like a second heartbeat.

"You'd let them do that to me," she said. "Your own daughter."

Something crossed Ilse's face then — fast, quickly governed, but real. Grief, maybe, or its fossil. "I am telling you what will happen so that it doesn't happen to you," she said. "There is a difference, and the difference is that I'm sitting in this chair instead of making a phone call. Don't confuse the warning with the threat. I don't make threats. I make forecasts. There's a distinction, and it has cost me everything, and I still believe in it."

Orsen stood. She hadn't planned to; her body simply decided the conversation needed altitude. She walked to the window and looked down at the river, at the dark water carrying its cargo of lights, at the city that had never once asked her who her mother was until this week.

"Why me?" she asked the glass. "Really. You know a dozen surveyors. You trained half of them."

"Because they'll map what I tell them to map," Ilse said behind her. "You won't. You'll map what's *there*. It's your single most infuriating quality and it's the reason the dig needs you and the reason I've dreaded hiring you since the day I realized I had to."

Orsen turned. "That's not a compliment."

"It's the only honest one I have left."

They looked at each other across the lamplit room — across five years, across a bar in Vessik, across a childhood of podiums and packing crates and a father's silence. Orsen felt the old resentment rise in her like groundwater, steady and cold, and she did what she'd learned to do with groundwater: she measured it, and worked with it, and refused to pretend it wasn't there.

"Terms," she said.

Ilse's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Go on."

"One. My survey data is mine. Raw readings, field notes, backups — I keep originals, you get copies. If this arrangement ends badly, I walk out with my work intact."

"Accepted."

"Two. I don't sign anything that gives you or anyone else editorial control over what I report. If my instruments say there's a structure under that talus slope, that fact exists whether you log it or not."

A pause. Long enough to measure. "Accepted," Ilse said, "with the note that interpretation and data are different animals, and interpretation is mine."

"We'll see. Three." Orsen took a breath. "The token. When this contract ends — however it ends — it comes back to me. Unregistered, unrecovered, mine. You don't use it, you don't trade it, you don't hand it to Dallet as a goodwill gesture. It's the only thing I've ever taken that wasn't given to me, and I'm not giving it up as part of some package deal."

For the first time, Ilse Vasquez looked genuinely surprised — a small fracture in the masonry, there and gone. "Why?" she asked. "It's a coin. It's worth almost nothing."

"It's worth that I found it," Orsen said. "Nobody gave it to me. Nobody arranged it. The river handed it up and I took it, and it's the only honest thing I've done in years, and I know how that sounds coming from me, in this room, negotiating with you. But it stays mine."

Her mother studied her for a long moment, and Orsen had the strange, vertiginous sense of being read — fully, instrument by instrument, the way she read a riverbed. Whatever Ilse found down there, she didn't share it.

"Accepted," she said quietly. "The token returns to you at contract's end."

"Then I'll need the contract papers tonight. I start Monday."

"There's a staging camp below the escarpment. Forty miles past Harrow Bend. My foreman's name is Tavik — he'll meet you at the junction." Ilse rose, smooth as a tide change, and extended her hand.

Orsen looked at it. Five years of not touching that hand. Then she took it, briefly, professionally, and felt her mother register the brevity and file it away.

"One more thing," Ilse said as Orsen gathered her coat. "At camp, you'll be introduced as a contracted specialist. Not as my daughter. Dallet will figure it out eventually — he's thorough — but I'd rather he learn it late than early."

"Fine by me," Orsen said. "It's been five years since it was true anyway."

She was at the door when her mother spoke again, and this time the lecture voice was gone entirely, replaced by something lower and older that Orsen hadn't heard since she was a child falling asleep in a survey tent to the sound of her parents arguing softly about stratigraphy.

"Orsen. The inscriptions at the site — if my readings are right, they name villages. Living ones. People downriver who've spent sixty years being told their homeland never existed." A pause. "Whatever you think of me, when you see what's under that escarpment, you'll understand why I can't let the wrong people reach it first."

Orsen stood with her hand on the handle, heart beating oddly, the river roaring faintly fourteen floors below.

"Monday," she said, and left before her face could give anything away.

In the elevator, descending, she watched the numbers count down and understood with total clarity that she had just negotiated terms with the one person on earth who had taught her every trick she'd used to win them — and that her mother had conceded each point a half-second too easily, like a current giving way around a stone.

The doors opened onto the marble lobby, onto the ordinary evening, onto the last free days of her carefully hidden life.

Ninety days, she thought. She could survive anything for ninety days.

She was already wrong, and wouldn't know it for weeks.
