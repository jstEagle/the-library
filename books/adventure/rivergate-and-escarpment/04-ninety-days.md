## 4. Ninety Days

The contract was four pages long and I read it three times before I signed it, which was two more times than I'd given the last eleven contracts of my life. That should have told me something. When you read a river survey agreement the way I read this one — hunting for the trap in every clause, testing every sentence for hidden weight the way you'd test ice — you already know you're not signing for the money. Money you sign fast, with a beer sweating on the desk beside you.

I signed anyway. The pen scratched, and the sound of it was very small for a decision that large.

*For the money,* I told myself, capping the pen. The retainer was genuinely obscene — triple my best year, paid against ninety days of work that would have taken me sixty if they let me do it properly. *For the money, and because refusing would look worse than accepting.* Both true. Neither sufficient. But I'd gotten good at living in the gap between what was true and what was enough, and I carried the contract down to the harbor office like a woman carrying a receipt for something she couldn't remember buying.

The launch left at dawn two days later. Ilse didn't see me off. She sent a note instead, in her narrow, upright hand — the handwriting of a woman who had never once written anything she wasn't prepared to defend under oath:

*Camp is below the escarpment at the old ferry landing. Introduce yourself to Brandt first; he runs the ground truth. We'll speak when there's something to say.*

No *welcome aboard*. No *I'm glad you came*, which she wouldn't have been, and wouldn't have lied about even for form's sake. Just logistics, delivered like ordnance. I read it twice on the deck while the Rivergate unspooled behind us, brown and muscular with spring melt, carrying whole trees past us like it was clearing its throat.

Sixty years ago, my mother's maps said, this stretch of river had a different name on the far bank. Not a different name — no name. A blank where a country used to be. Cartographers call it a lacuna when they're being polite. Everyone else calls it what it is: an erasure.

I stood at the rail and watched the escarpment grow out of the haze ahead of us, a limestone wall running north-south as far as the eye could follow, pale as bone in the morning light. The river ran hard along its base, and above the talus slopes — those great aprons of fallen rock, some blocks the size of chapels — the cliff face went up and up into scrub pine and hawk country. Somewhere inside all that stone, if Ilse's permits meant anything, was a city that had been deleted from the world's memory so thoroughly that even the word *Karvath* sounded made-up when I said it to myself.

I said it anyway. Twice. Testing the ice.

* * *

The staging camp sat on a gravel bench between the river and the talus, a settlement of canvas and corrugated steel that looked temporary the way a scar looks temporary. Thirty-odd people moved through it with the purposeful rhythm of a dig in its second month: the clang of the mess tent kitchen, the whine of a generator, someone swearing musically at a jammed winch. It smelled of diesel, cut limestone dust, and river mud — and underneath all of it, faintly, of coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in.

Brandt found me before I'd found my own tent.

He was a head shorter than me and built like something the river had failed to move for forty years, gray-bearded, forearms mapped with old rope burns. He took my duffel off my shoulder without asking, which I normally hate, and set it down outside the survey tent, which I immediately forgave.

"Orsen Vasquez." He said it like he was checking a measurement against a known standard. "Your mother says you're the best hydrographic surveyor on this river."

"My mother says a lot of things."

"She does." He almost smiled. It fit his face badly, like furniture from another house. "I've worked for her nineteen years. She's never once been wrong about a person's competence and never once right about their character. So we'll find out which one you are." He stuck out a hand like a boat cleat. "Rud Brandt. Foreman. Around here, that means I'm the one who tells you when your instruments are lying and you tell me when my crews are. Fair?"

"Fair." His grip was careful, which surprised me. Strong men who are careful with their strength are usually careful with other things too. "Which am I so far?"

"Unknown." Now the smile came easier. "That's the honest answer, and I figure honesty's the currency here, since nobody's spending much of it." He nodded upriver, toward a cluster of white stakes marking out a grid on the gravel. "Come on. I'll show you the ground before anyone else gets to tell you what it means."

We walked the grid together for an hour, and he talked the way good foremen talk — in specifics, in loads hauled and meters drilled, in the names of his crew and their bad knees and their card debts. He pointed out where the flood had scoured the bank in March, the same flood that had given up the chest with the seal I still carried sewn into the lining of my jacket, though I hadn't told a soul that. He showed me the bore cores laid out in wooden racks, each labeled in Ilse's hand: depth, stratum, anomaly codes.

"She logs everything herself," Brandt said. "Won't delegate it. Nineteen years, she's logged every core."

"That sounds like her."

"It sounds like control," he said mildly, "but yes. Also her." He glanced at me sideways. "You'll hear things, working here. People will want to know whose side you're on before they decide what to tell you. I'm telling you now so you don't waste time: I'm on the side of the dig. Whatever else happens."

"And whatever else happens," I said, "is what, exactly?"

He looked at the escarpment for a moment. The morning sun had climbed enough to throw the talus slopes into sharp relief, and the shadows between the fallen blocks were deep and blue and full of nothing visible.

"Ninety days," he said. "Ask me again on day eighty-nine."

* * *

Sef Ilnar I met at dusk, bent over a drafting table in the map tent, and I knew within thirty seconds that he was the kind of man who apologized to furniture.

He was slight, dark-haired, maybe thirty, with ink on three fingers and the haunted look of someone who checks his work nine times and still dreams about the tenth. The map tent was his whole world: lamp-lit, paper-smelling, walls hung with base maps of the concession at three scales, each one annotated in a small, precise hand.

"You're the daughter," he said, then went gray. "I mean — Dr. Vasquez's — I'm sorry, that was—"

"Sef, right? Brandt mentioned you. Cartographer."

"Yes. Sef Ilnar." He wiped his hand on his shirt, remembered the ink was wet, and made it worse. "Welcome. Sorry. The light's bad in here, I know, but the lamp flickers if I run the second one, and the generator—" He stopped himself visibly, like a man reining a horse. "Can I show you something?"

What he showed me was beautiful. His field sheets were immaculate — triangulation stations, shot lines, the talus survey plotted to a tolerance I'd have bragged about. And overlaid on them, in red, were the coordinates as they appeared in the official log.

They didn't match.

Not grossly. Not obviously. A station here shifted twelve meters east. A bore line there rotated two degrees. Individually, each discrepancy was the kind of thing a tired crew or a sloppy instrument might produce. Together, over six weeks of records, they formed a pattern with a shape I recognized the way you recognize a face across a street — someone systematically walking the official record away from the strongest readings.

"Probably instrument drift," Sef said quickly. "Probably. I've checked the instruments. Twice. They're fine, which means it's probably me, except I've also checked me, and—" He pressed his palms flat on the table, as if steadying the maps by force. "Dr. Vasquez reviews every sheet before it goes in the log. Every one. And every sheet comes back with these little corrections. Her hand. Always her hand."

"What do you do with the originals?"

His eyes came up, and for a second the nervousness burned off and something older and sadder looked out. "I keep them," he said quietly. "In a tube under my bunk. I don't know why. Yes I do. Because if I ever have to prove I didn't make these mistakes, I'll need to prove somebody else did." He swallowed. "Please don't tell her I told you. It's day one. You don't know me. There's no reason you'd—"

"Sef." I put my hand briefly on the table, near his, not touching. "Nobody knows anybody here yet. That's the point of ninety days."

He laughed — a single startled note, gone as fast as it came — and I left him to his lamp and his flickering generator and his tube of truths under the bunk, and walked out into the cold blue evening thinking: *she's managing it. Whatever's down there, she's already decided what it's allowed to be.*

I should have walked straight to her tent. Instead I stood in the dark between the map tent and the mess, listening to the river, and did the arithmetic of a coward: confront her tonight, alone, with nothing but a cartographer's fear for evidence — or wait, watch, build a case. I told myself the second option was strategy. Some nights I can still believe that.

* * *

Dallet found me on the third morning, and he found me smiling, which I think was the point.

I was at the river edge calibrating the echo sounder when a voice behind me said, "You must be the famous daughter," in a tone of such warm, unhurried friendliness that I turned around already disliking him on principle.

He was perhaps fifty, silvering at the temples, dressed in expedition khaki that fit too well to have ever been worn in actual mud. Ministry observer, Brandt had said, with a flatness that had told me everything. The ministry's eyes and ears, lodged in camp like a well-fed tick, required by the permit terms and tolerated by everyone at a distance of exactly one polite sentence.

"Casimir Dallet." He offered his hand. His grip was dry and brief and precisely calibrated to seem modest. "I know — observers aren't popular. I've made peace with it. I try to be furniture that smiles."

"You're doing very well."

He laughed, delighted, as if I'd confirmed something he'd suspected all along. "Brandt says you're the best surveyor he's seen on this water. High praise. Rud doesn't spend praise; he invests it." He settled onto a boulder nearby without asking, arranging himself with the ease of a man who had never once been asked to leave anywhere. "Tell me about the flood scour. March flood, wasn't it? Unusual timing. The bank gave up quite a lot, I understand."

My hands kept working. Calibrate, check, record. "Floods give up silt and dead cattle, mostly."

"And occasionally chests with seals on them." He said it lightly, watching the far bank, giving me room to laugh it off. "The recovered chest is in the ministry's custody now, of course. Fascinating object. The seal especially. Karvathi workmanship is distinctive — the double-keyed border, the anchor-and-star. You'd know it if you'd seen it."

"I've read about Karvath," I said. "Everyone has. That's how you know a thing's been erased — it gets a whole genre of books about how it doesn't exist."

"Mm." He smiled at the river. "There's a token, too, I believe. Coin-sized. Lacquered wood, stamped metal. Recovered from the same scour line." A pause, exactly long enough. "The inventory lists it as unrecovered."

The echo sounder pinged. The river answered. I let both sounds sit there between us like stones dropped in a well.

"Inventory errors happen," I said finally.

"They do." He stood, brushing off his immaculate trousers. "I'm not the police, Ms. Vasquez. Truly. I'm an observer. I observe. And what I observe, mostly, is that this expedition contains a great many people keeping a great many private versions of the truth — your mother most elegantly of all — and that in eighty-seven days, when these permits lapse, every one of those private versions becomes someone else's problem." He smiled again, warm as lamplight, and it reached all the way to his eyes, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't. "I do hope yours lands softly. Do come to dinner sometime. Thursdays. I cook."

He walked back toward camp whistling something I half recognized — an old river song, the kind dock workers sing — and I stayed at the waterline a long time, recalibrating an instrument that didn't need it.

So. He knew about the token. Which meant either the broker had talked, or the flood site had been watched before I ever knelt in that mud, or both, and both were worse. He knew about the falsified logs — or suspected, which for a man like Dallet amounted to the same ammunition. And he'd told me he knew without saying so outright, the way you show a card player your chips instead of your hand.

Belonging, I thought, looking up at the camp — the tents, the generator smoke, Brandt's crew laughing at something by the winch, Sef's lamp burning in the map tent, my mother's silhouette moving behind the office canvas. Belonging was being offered to me everywhere I turned. Brandt offered it conditionally, on the side of the dig. Sef offered it desperately, in a cardboard tube under a bunk. My mother offered it as a leash. Dallet offered it as a trap with the door propped open, so I could watch myself walk in.

Four days in, and I was already a piece on two boards, possibly three, and the only person in camp whose game I couldn't see was the one whose blood I shared.

That night I took out the token for the first time since the harbor. Small as a coin, dense as a secret, the stamped metal worn nearly smooth — but under my thumb, in the lamplight, I could still feel the ghost of the design. An anchor. A star. A nation's whole argument with oblivion, pressed into something you could lose in a pocket.

Eighty-seven days. Outside, the river ran dark and endless against the escarpment, keeping its own books, patient as stone.

Somewhere beneath the talus, something waited that had already outlasted one erasure.

It was about to find out whether I was worth outlasting a second.
