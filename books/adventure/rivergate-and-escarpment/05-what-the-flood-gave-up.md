## 5. What the Flood Gave Up

The escarpment did not look like a place that kept secrets. It looked like a wall of sun-bleached limestone that had been standing there since before anyone thought to name it, its face streaked with the mineral tears of ten thousand rainy seasons, its base buried under a fan of talus — broken stone slid down over centuries, gray and indifferent as a closed eyelid.

Orsen Vasquez did not trust it anyway.

She had learned early in her surveying career that landscapes lied the way people lied: not with obvious falsehoods but with emphasis. A riverbank showed you what it wanted you to see. The escarpment wanted to be seen as inert, as geology, as nothing. Which was exactly why she spent her third morning at the staging camp walking its base with a ground-penetrating radar unit strapped to her back like a pilgrim's burden, dragging the antenna sled through the talus while the morning heat climbed out of the river valley and pressed down on her shoulders like a hand.

"Anything?" called Tomas Reyes from twenty meters behind her. He was the foreman Ilse had hired eleven years ago and never let go — a broad, quiet man with forearms like dock rope and a face that gave away nothing except when he was pleased, and then only slightly, like a door opening a crack onto a lit room.

"Give me a minute," Orsen said, though she'd already seen it.

She crouched over the display screen, wiping grit from it with her thumb. The readout rendered the subsurface in bands of amber and black, and there, forty meters from where Ilse's published theory said anything should be, the black was interrupted. Not by a rock lens. Not by a water table. By geometry.

Straight lines do not occur in nature. Every surveyor carried that sentence somewhere near the spine. And beneath the talus, running parallel to the escarpment face for at least thirty meters before the signal degraded, lay a lattice of straight lines — walls, or something shaped like walls, buried under six to nine meters of collapsed stone.

Her heart did something unprofessional. It leapt.

She marked the point with a red pin flag, then another, then another, working the grid methodically while sweat ran down between her shoulder blades. By noon she had flagged eleven anomalies in a pattern too regular to dismiss. By two o'clock, when she fed the coordinates into the camp's plotting software and overlaid them on the concession map, she sat very still in the survey tent and looked at what the flood had given up.

Because that was the thing nobody at camp had said aloud yet, though everyone knew it: none of this would be visible at all if the Rivergate hadn't flooded in the spring. The flood had scoured the lower valley, torn out vegetation, rearranged the talus at the escarpment's southern end, and — according to the satellite comparison Orsen had run her first night — slumped an entire section of the slope outward, like a drawer pulled open by a careless hand. Whatever was underneath had been covered for centuries, maybe millennia. The river had decided otherwise.

Ilse's published theory — the one that had gotten her the concession, the one she'd defended in three journals and one humiliating public debate — placed a modest Karvathi waystation against the escarpment's northern face: a supply depot, maybe a shrine, forty square meters at most. A footnote of a structure. Enough to keep her permit alive, not enough to change any textbooks.

What Orsen's instruments were showing her was closer to four thousand square meters.

She printed the overlay. She checked it twice. Then she rolled it up, walked across the camp — past the mess tent where Sef Adeyemi the cartographer was hunched over his drafting table with the anxious posture of a man who expected his work to be criticized, past the ministry's white Land Cruiser gleaming like a tooth — and ducked into her mother's command tent without knocking, because they were not yet at the stage of their acquaintance where knocking seemed necessary.

"Ilse."

Her mother looked up from a correspondence ledger. At fifty-eight, Dr. Ilse Vasquez remained a handsome woman in the way that certain cliffs are handsome — all structure, no softness, weathered into authority. Reading glasses perched on her nose. Her gray hair was pinned back with the same kind of ruthlessness she applied to everything.

"You've been in the field since dawn," she said. "Eat something first."

"Eat after." Orsen unrolled the printout on the desk, weighting the corners with a rock hammer, a compass case, and her mother's own brass inkwell. "Southern talus field. Eleven confirmed anomalies. Consistent masonry-scale returns at six to nine meters depth, aligned parallel to the escarpment face." She tapped the overlay. "It's big, Ilse. It's bigger than your paper allows. It's bigger than your paper *can* allow."

For a moment — just a moment, less than a breath — something moved behind her mother's eyes. Orsen would spend weeks trying to classify it afterward. Fear? Wonder? She couldn't say. It was gone too fast, replaced by the expression Ilse wore like armor: measured, professional, faintly disappointed in the world for being so predictable.

"GPR returns at that depth are ambiguous," Ilse said. "Collapsed karst can produce rectilinear shadows. So can old quarry workings. There were limestone cuts in this valley two hundred years ago."

"Not with load-bearing cross-walls at regular intervals. I ran the transects twice, perpendicular grids. Karst doesn't repeat itself like that. Quarries don't have floor levels." Orsen kept her voice level, professional, the voice of a contractor delivering findings. Underneath it, something younger was shouting *look, look what I found, look*. She hated that voice. She'd been drowning it for years. "This is a structure. Probably multiple structures. Possibly a compound."

"A compound," Ilse repeated, flatly.

"Beneath the talus. Intact, or mostly intact. The flood pulled the cover off the southern edge." Orsen straightened. "Your theory says waystation. This says city section. This says everything you wrote about Karvath being a minor river polity might be wrong, and if it's wrong, the concession isn't a footnote anymore, it's—"

"It's nothing," Ilse said quietly, "until we dig."

The temperature in the tent changed. Orsen felt it the way you feel a door open onto cold air in winter.

"Meaning?"

"Meaning science is a discipline of patience." Ilse rose, came around the desk, and studied the printout with genuine attention — Orsen could grant her that much; her mother never pretended not to see good data. Her finger traced the anomaly line, paused at the densest cluster, and moved on. "We have ninety days and a ministry observer who files reports weekly. If we announce a major urban find in week one, Dallet's superiors will descend on this site within the month, and every trowel in this valley will belong to the capital. We dig carefully. We confirm incrementally. We start—" her finger stopped, decisively, at the northern transect, where Orsen's weakest return sat, a smudge of possible wall barely distinguishable from noise, "—here. Shallow. Low risk. If it confirms, we expand south in our own time."

"That's the weakest reading on the sheet."

"It's the shallowest. It's the fastest to verify. Time is the resource we don't have, Orsen. You of all people should understand scarce resources."

There it was. The needle, slipped in so smoothly you almost didn't feel the puncture. *You of all people* — the daughter who left, who took contracts in three countries rather than sit through one more family dinner, who had spent five years making sure her surname opened no doors she couldn't walk through alone.

"Fine," Orsen said. "Northern trench first. But I want it on record that the strongest returns are southern, and I want my full survey logged in the site record. All of it. If we're doing incremental science, the data goes in the book."

Something flickered again across her mother's face — faster this time, and this time Orsen caught it clearly enough to name it later, though she wouldn't believe herself for days.

It was calculation.

"Of course," Ilse said. "Log everything. That's what the record is for."

* * *

That evening the camp smelled of diesel, dust, and Reyes's rice-and-dried-fish stew, which he made in a pot older than half the crew and defended with the ferocity of a border garrison. Orsen ate second helpings at the mess bench while the talk ran along its usual channels: water rationing, the mule handler's gambling debts, whether the ministry observer ever slept.

"He sleeps," said Sef gloomily. He was a thin man in his thirties with ink-stained fingers and the permanent wince of someone whose inner world was more orderly than his outer one. "I saw him at five this morning, walking the perimeter. Smiling. Who smiles at five in the morning?"

"Men who are paid to," Reyes said.

Dallet himself appeared at the edge of the firelight as if summoned by the conversation — a neat, silvering man in a khaki shirt pressed sharp enough to shave with, carrying two cups of coffee. He offered one to Orsen with a small bow.

"Dr. Vasquez. Your mother speaks highly of your instruments."

"My instruments speak highly of themselves. They don't need help."

He laughed, warm and easy, and sat down uninvited on the bench across from her. Up close his smile had too many teeth in it, like a map with too many roads — impressive until you wondered where they all led. "A productive day, I hear. Flagging on the talus."

Orsen kept her spoon moving. "Standard survey work."

"Mm. The ministry appreciates thoroughness." He sipped his coffee. "Though I'll confess the reports make dull reading. Coordinates, depths, soil classifications. What makes a young woman choose such a dry trade?"

*Young woman.* She was thirty-four. He knew it; her file was probably in the Land Cruiser. The diminutive was a probe, testing how she reacted to being made small.

"Rivers," Orsen said. "I like rivers. They're honest about what they take."

"And what do they take, Dr. Vasquez?"

"Everything, eventually. Banks, bridges, towns. Names." She met his eyes. "They gave us a good scour this spring, though. Moved a lot of stone."

Something adjusted behind Dallet's pleasantness — a fraction of a degree, a watch being wound. "So I'm told," he said, and stood, and wished the table a good night, and walked back toward the white vehicle with his cup still steaming.

Sef exhaled when he was out of earshot. "Does he always do that?"

"Do what?"

"Ask questions that aren't questions."

Reyes ladled more stew into Orsen's bowl without being asked. "Every day," he said. "Get used to it. And eat. Tomorrow you dig."

* * *

The northern trench opened two days later under a sky the color of hammered tin. It was honest work — Orsen would give it that. She ran the total station while six local hires moved talus under Reyes's direction, and by the third afternoon they had a clean profile down 2.4 meters, and by the fourth they had a wall.

A real one. Limestone block, mortared, running eight meters before it vanished into the trench baulk. Ilse stood over it for a long time with her hands clasped behind her back, and even Orsen felt the pull of it — the ancient human signature rising out of the earth like a voice after long silence. Whoever had laid those blocks had squared them with care. Somebody had lived here. Worked here. The wall proved nothing and everything.

"Waystation," Ilse said finally, with satisfaction. "Perimeter wall of a service compound. Consistent with the published model."

Orsen crouched at the wall's base, brushing soil from the footing, and said nothing, because the footing told a different story than her mother wanted to hear. The blocks were dressed in a style she recognized from the comparative plates in Ilse's own monograph — Karvathi civic masonry, the kind used in administrative buildings, not storage sheds. And the wall's orientation matched, precisely, the axis of the strong southern anomalies. This wasn't an isolated compound. It was an edge. The visible corner of something whose body lay buried under the talus to the south, exactly where her instruments said, exactly where her mother had decided not to look.

She logged it. Wall, dimensions, masonry classification, orientation bearing. All of it, in the site record, in her own careful hand, because she had demanded that much and been granted it.

Then, that night, going through the day's paperwork to reconcile her field notes with the master log — a habit drilled into her by five years of contracts where sloppy records meant lawsuits — she found the discrepancy.

Her southern survey was in the record. Technically. Reduced to a single line: *GPR transects, southern talus, results inconclusive; possible geological origin; no action recommended.*

No coordinates. No anomaly count. No depth figures. No mention of four thousand square meters of rectilinear returns. Just *inconclusive*, in her mother's handwriting, filed under her mother's authority, in a record that would eventually go to the ministry, to the universities, to history.

Orsen sat in the survey tent with the lamp turned low and read the line four times, looking for a benign explanation. Data compression happened; summaries were written; a hundred legitimate reasons presented themselves and were dismissed one by one, because each of them required incompetence, and whatever else could be said about Ilse Vasquez, incompetence had never been among her sins.

This wasn't summarizing. This was editing.

She thought about the flicker in the command tent — the thing she hadn't been able to name. *Calculation.* She thought about the northern trench, chosen not because it was fast to verify but because it was small. Because a waystation confirmed a footnote, and a footnote kept a permit alive without inviting the world. Because her mother had built a twenty-year career on knowing exactly how much truth a discovery could bear before it became dangerous.

And underneath the anger, unwelcome as groundwater, another thought rose: *she's done this before.* The certainty arrived whole, the way Orsen sometimes knew a river had shifted course before any instrument confirmed it. Somewhere in the architecture of her mother's caution was a prior structure, an earlier decision to manage the truth rather than serve it. You didn't get this fluent at suppression without practice.

She rolled up the original printout — her copy, the one with real numbers — and slid it into the waterproof tube she used for contract documents, the one that never left her kit. She told herself it was standard practice. Surveyors kept their raw data. Any contractor would.

She slept badly, and dreamed of straight lines under black water.

At dawn she was at the southern flags again, running transects she had already run, confirming what she had already confirmed, telling herself it was diligence. The escarpment stood over her, patient as ever, keeping its counsel. Below the talus, the dead waited under their roof of fallen stone — and above them, alive and breathing and writing careful falsehoods in a tidy hand, her mother was deciding how much of them the world would be allowed to know.

Orsen planted one last flag at the densest point of the strongest return and stood looking at it, red cloth snapping in the valley wind, a small bright accusation no one had asked her to raise.

Behind her, boots crunched on talus. She turned, expecting Reyes.

It was Sef, pale, holding his notebook against his chest like a shield, glancing back over his shoulder toward the command tent before he spoke.

"Dr. Vasquez," he said. "Can I show you something? About the maps?"
