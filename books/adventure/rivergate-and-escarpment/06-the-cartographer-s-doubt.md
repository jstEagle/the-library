## 6. The Cartographer's Doubt

Sef Aldous had been drawing maps since he was nine years old, and in all that time he had learned one rule that mattered more than any projection or scale: a map is a confession. Every line you draw, you are swearing you saw it. Every coordinate you record, you are telling whoever comes after you — *I stood here. I measured this. It is true.*

Which was why he could not sleep.

He sat in the survey tent with a hurricane lamp turned low, the escarpment a black shoulder against the stars, and compared two documents that should have been identical and were not. On his left, the field notes — his own field notes, taken at theodolite and chain, in his own cramped hand, cross-checked twice against Orsen Vasquez's instrument readings. On his right, the fair-copy master map that Dr. Ilse Vasquez had signed off on three days ago and sent downriver with the supply barge for the ministry's quarterly filing.

The two documents disagreed.

Not wildly. That was the thing that made his hands sweat. A wild error he could forgive, could chase down, could correct. These were small discrepancies — a bearing shifted two degrees here, a datum point moved eleven meters there, the strongest magnetic anomaly from Grid Four quietly relocated to Grid Seven, where nothing lived but gravel and scree. Each one, alone, was the kind of mistake a tired man makes. Together, they were a pattern. Together, they were a map of a different country — one where Dr. Vasquez's published theory of a modest waystation beneath the talus slope was exactly, perfectly, conveniently true.

Sef set down his pen and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until he saw constellations that had nothing to do with the sky.

*You saw it,* he told himself. *Now what.*

The problem with seeing a thing was that seeing obligated you. His father had been a harbor pilot in Corvane, a man who had once refused to bring a ship in during a fog because the charts and the water didn't agree, and had lost his commission for it, and had never once, in all the years Sef knew him, called it a mistake. *The chart serves the truth or it serves nothing,* his father used to say, usually while mending nets he no longer needed to mend, in the long unemployed years after.

Sef had taken this job because the expedition needed a cartographer and because the pay would clear his sister's medical debt in Vellin and because, if he was honest, because working under Dr. Ilse Vasquez was the kind of name on a résumé that opened doors for a man from the harbor districts. He had not taken it to become a forger by association.

He looked at the master map again. His own signature was on it. He had signed it in good faith, believing the fair copy matched his field work, because Dr. Vasquez had handed him the finished sheets at dinner and said, *Check the lettering, Sef, you're the only one here with a hand worth reading,* and he had checked the lettering, and the lettering was beautiful, and he had signed.

That was how it was done, then. That was how it was done.

* * *

He found Orsen Vasquez at dawn, at the edge of the survey grid, running a magnetometer along a line she had already run twice, her boots planted in the wet scree like she was growing out of it. She worked the way her mother's reputation said she didn't — patiently, without flourish, with the particular humility of someone who trusted instruments more than people. Sef had liked her from the first week for exactly that. He had also been slightly afraid of her, in the way you are afraid of anyone whose last name is the same as the name on the tent.

"Vasquez." He kept his voice level. "Got a minute?"

"Two." She didn't look up. "The line's drifting. Either the instrument's sick or there's something under Grid Four that wants to be found, and I don't believe in sick instruments."

"About Grid Four." His mouth had gone dry. "That's — actually why."

That got her attention. She straightened, set the magnetometer down on its case, and looked at him with those flat river-gray eyes that seemed to take measurements of people the way she took them of banks. "Okay. Talk."

"Not here."

She read him in a heartbeat — he saw her read him, saw the flicker of calculation, the decision — and then she said, "The cutbank, then. Past the latrine trench. Nobody walks there before breakfast."

* * *

The cutbank was raw earth and river smell, the flood's leavings still visible in the strata like the rings of a terrible year. Orsen leaned against a fallen willow and waited. Sef realized he was holding the two documents against his chest like a schoolboy with stolen answers, and made himself lower them.

"Field notes," he said. "And the fair copy. The one I signed. The one that went downriver on Tuesday."

Orsen took them without a word. She read fast — faster than anyone he'd ever seen read a map, her finger moving along the coordinate columns like a needle along a groove. The dawn came up behind her, gilding the escarpment, and her face went slowly out of the gold and into something colder.

"When did you notice?"

"Two nights ago. I told myself I'd mis-transcribed." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I checked my originals against my originals. Then I checked her published survey parameters from the concession filing. Vasquez — Orsen — the discrepancies all run the same direction. Every anomaly that supports a structure larger than her theory gets moved, diminished, or reassigned to dead ground. Grid Four's central reading — the one you've been chasing all week — it's logged at Grid Seven in the master. Eleven meters off. A two-degree bearing shift. That's not error. Error scatters. This *points*."

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. The river worked at the bank below them, patient as a clerk.

"Who else has seen the field notes?" she asked.

"You. Her. Me. Tam has the duplicate logbook but Tam can't read a coordinate column without moving his lips."

"And Dallet?"

Sef's stomach dropped, because that was the question he'd been avoiding for two days. "The ministry observer gets the fair copy. That's the arrangement. That's why the fair copy exists."

"So the ministry has a map that says everything is where it isn't." Orsen folded the documents once, crisply, and handed his field notes back but kept the fair copy. "And we have a map that says where things actually are. And your signature is on the lie."

"My signature is on the *lettering*," Sef said, and heard how it sounded even as he said it, the smallness of it, the weasel-turn of it, and he made himself say the rest. "I signed the sheet. I should have checked the numbers. I'm the cartographer. The chart serves the truth or it serves nothing — my father used to—" He stopped. "It's my fault. That's the part I keep circling. Whatever she's doing, I made it official."

"Or she made you the notary so that when it surfaced, it surfaced as *your* fault." Orsen said it flatly, without cruelty, the way you'd note a current in a chart. "Think about it. She hands the finished sheets to the one man on this dig with a debt back home and a family name that isn't hers. If the ministry ever compares documents, who's holding the discrepancy?"

Sef felt the ground tilt, the way it did when you realized the bank you were standing on was undercut. He had spent two nights constructing his own guilt, and she had dismantled it in a sentence, and the strange thing was that it didn't make him feel better. It made him feel *used*, which was worse.

"So what do we do?" he asked. "Go to her? Confront her?"

"With what? A discrepancy?" Orsen shook her head. "She'll have an explanation. Datum recalibration. Instrument error. She's forgotten more surveying than I'll ever know, and she'll bury us both in the difference. No. If she's logging false coordinates, there'll be more than one document. There'll be a working set. Draft sheets. Her private log. Something that shows the change happening — the moment the truth got edited." She looked up at the escarpment, at the tent cluster at its base where the expedition's command tent sat with its lamp already lit. Ilse Vasquez kept surveyors' hours; she'd been awake for an hour at least. "We need to see her working papers."

"That's her tent."

"That's her tent," Orsen agreed.

"Orsen. If she catches us—"

"Then we're two surveyors who walked into the wrong tent looking for the duplicate datum register, which is a real document that really lives in there, and which we really need." She pushed off the willow. "Sef. Look at me."

He looked at her.

"I've known my mother for twenty-eight years, and I've spent twenty-three of them trying not to," she said. "I'm telling you she's managing this dig the way she managed my whole childhood — you find out what the story is supposed to be, and then you find out what happened to the parts that didn't fit. I need you with me when we look. Because if it's just me, she'll make it a daughter thing. A grudge. But if it's the two of us, with your field notes and my instrument logs, it's *evidence*." A pause. "And if I'm wrong — if there's an innocent explanation — then I'll be the one who apologizes to her, and you'll have lost nothing but an hour's sleep."

"And if you're right?"

Orsen looked out at the river, and something crossed her face that Sef couldn't read — something old, something that had been waiting a long time for this exact question.

"Then we decide what kind of mapmakers we are," she said. "Before somebody else decides for us."

* * *

They went at seven, in the flat gray hour after breakfast when the camp's attention was on the hoist line and the morning's first descent into the trial trench. Orsen carried the duplicate datum register request like a talisman; Sef walked half a step behind her, heart going like a chain winch.

The command tent was as he'd imagined it from outside and nothing like it from within — not a general's tent but a scholar's, every surface papered, the cot a rumor under a drift of reports, the air smelling of lamp oil and the particular dry smell of old vellum. Ilse Vasquez herself was not there. Her field desk was.

It was locked. Of course it was locked.

But the drafting table beside it was not, and on the drafting table, under a weight of brass, lay a working sheet — a draft, pencil, uncommitted, the cartographic equivalent of a thought still being had. Sef leaned over it before he could stop himself, and his blood went cold and then hot.

Grid Four. The central anomaly. Drawn at its true position — *his* position, the position in his field notes — with a small, precise pencil notation beside it that read: *relocate to G7. Consistent with published parameters. I.V.*

She had drawn the truth first. She had drawn the truth, looked at it, and then written herself instructions to move it.

"Orsen," he whispered. "Orsen, look—"

"I see it." Her voice was very quiet. She was photographing nothing, touching nothing, just standing over the sheet with an expression Sef had last seen on the faces of people reading their own names on a casualty list. "She drafts it true and then she files it false. There's a whole — there's a *system* here." Her hand hovered over the desk's edge, over a stack of draft sheets weighted under the brass, and Sef watched her decide, in real time, whether to lift it.

She lifted it.

Beneath: six more working sheets, and a leather logbook, and the logbook was open — Ilse Vasquez kept it open, always, Sef realized, because she was the only one who entered it — and the columns ran down the page in her small fierce hand, and every third or fourth entry had a mark beside it. A small tick. And beside the ticks, in the margin, a notation Sef had to read three times before he believed it:

*Adjusted for filing.*

*Adjusted for filing.*

*Adjusted — see G7 protocol.*

"There," Sef breathed. "There it is. That's not a mistake, that's a *procedure*. She has a protocol."

Orsen's face had gone the color of the cutbank clay. "She's not hiding a site," she said slowly. "She's hiding the *size* of it. Everything that makes it bigger than her theory — bigger than what the concession permits her to dig — gets moved to dead ground on paper. Which means the ministry thinks she's found a waystation, and she's standing on top of a—" She stopped herself. Looked at Sef. "How big does Grid Four say the structure is?"

Sef did the arithmetic he'd been doing in his head for two nights, the arithmetic he'd hoped was wrong. "If the anomaly shell is what it looks like — if the readings I took are real — the buried footprint isn't a waystation. It's not even a building. It's a *district*. Walls, streets, the works. Under the talus. Under her dig." He swallowed. "Orsen, there's a city down there. And your mother has been redrawing it smaller every week."

The tent flap moved.

Neither of them had heard footsteps — the hoist engine covered everything — but the flap moved, and Ilse Vasquez stood in the opening with a tin cup of coffee in one hand and the morning's instrument returns in the other, and for one long second the three of them were perfectly still, a tableau: the archaeologist, the daughter, the cartographer, and the truth spread open on the table between them.

Ilse's eyes went to the lifted brass weight. To the exposed draft sheets. To her daughter's hand, still resting on the logbook.

She did not raise her voice. That was the terrible thing, Sef thought afterward; she never raised her voice. She set down the cup, set down the returns, and looked at Orsen with an expression of such weary, intimate disappointment that Sef felt it in his own chest, secondhand, like a bruise.

"Close the logbook, Orsen," Dr. Vasquez said quietly. "And then the two of you are going to tell me exactly how much you understand — so I can explain exactly how much you don't."
