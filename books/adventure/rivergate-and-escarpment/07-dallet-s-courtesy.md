## 7. Dallet's Courtesy

The invitation came on cream paper, hand-delivered by a runner who wouldn't meet Orsen's eyes.

*Dr. Dallet requests the pleasure of your company for dinner. The ministry tent, eight o'clock. Formality optional. Good wine guaranteed.*

Orsen read it twice, standing in the survey tent with the evening's instrument notes still damp in her hand. Around her, the camp was settling into its rhythm — the clatter of the mess line, Sef's voice raised in argument with the foreman over a contour discrepancy, the escarpment going purple and then black as the sun dropped behind it. Somewhere in that ordinary noise, a ministry observer had decided tonight was the night.

She'd been at the staging camp nine days. Long enough to know that Dallet ate alone, that his tent was the only one with a proper table, and that his courtesy never once slipped into warmth. He remembered everyone's name. He asked about their families. He laughed at the right moments, a half-beat after everyone else, as if humor were a language he'd learned from a book. And every evening, without fail, he sat outside his tent with a small notebook and wrote in it for exactly twenty minutes, the lantern turned so its light fell on the page and not his face.

She had watched him do it from the instrument station. She was fairly sure he'd watched her watching.

"Formality optional," she said to the empty tent. Her voice came out flatter than she intended.

The truth was she'd known this dinner was coming. You didn't spend five years freelancing on contested rivers without learning the shape of the thing: the official who wants nothing, offers nothing, and then asks for everything. She'd sat across from customs men and concession brokers and one memorably drunk port authority in Vessik who'd wanted a cut of a survey contract she didn't even hold. The script was always the same. The only variable was the wine.

She dressed in her cleanest shirt anyway. Not out of respect. Out of the same instinct that made her check her instruments twice — if you're going to walk into a current, you walk in knowing the depth.

* * *

The ministry tent was larger than it needed to be, and Dallet had made it feel like a room in someone's house. A proper table, linen that had no business existing below the escarpment, two chairs, a lamp with a green glass shade. The wine was already open, breathing, which meant he'd decided the timing of this dinner before he'd decided to issue the invitation.

"Surveyor Vasquez." He rose and shook her hand — dry, brief, precise. "Thank you for coming. I know the field days are long. Please, sit."

The tent smelled faintly of ink and lamp oil and, underneath it, something like cedar — the smell of stored paper, of archives. Orsen sat. Dallet poured the wine without asking, filling her glass a precise two-thirds, and she noted that he'd already placed her glass on the side where a right-handed person would reach for it. He'd been paying attention.

"You've been busy," he said, taking his own seat. "Nine days, and your gravity readings have already forced a revision of the dig plan. Your mother must be proud."

"My mother is careful with pride."

"Yes." He smiled — the easy smile, the one that arrived a half-beat late. "She would be. Careful is the family word, I think."

Orsen let the silence sit. She'd learned it from the river: don't fill the quiet, and the quiet will fill itself. Dallet seemed content to let it run. He ate with genuine appetite — the food was simple, river fish and root vegetables, but cooked well — and made small, pleasant talk about the escarpment's geology, about the flood two seasons ago that had reshaped the lower bank, about the quality of the light at this latitude in autumn. He was good at it. That was the unsettling part. Every minute of pleasantness had the texture of something measured out.

It was over the second course that he set down his fork, folded his hands, and said, mildly, "Tell me about the token."

The word landed in Orsen's chest like a dropped stone in still water. She kept her face still — years of practice, keeping her face still — and reached for her wine.

"Token," she said.

"Coin-sized. Lacquered wood, or the wood was lacquered once. Metal fitting on one edge. A seal stamped into the fitting." He turned his glass by its stem, not looking at her, looking at the wine. "Found on the flood-scoured bank of the Rivergate, roughly four kilometers downstream of the old ferry crossing. You did not report it. You kept it."

"You have a very specific imagination, Dr. Dallet."

"I have a very specific file." He looked up then, and his eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse. "The broker you consulted in Harrowgate — Marenko, on Candle Street — is known to us. He reports unusual acquisitions. Standard practice for anyone dealing in pre-annexation materials. You paid him in cash, which was sensible of you, and you used your mother's surname, which was not."

The tent was very quiet. Orsen could hear the camp outside — the last mess sounds, a lantern being trimmed, the wind moving along the talus slope with its dry whisper of stone on stone. Her heart was doing something steady and stupid against her ribs.

"I found a piece of wood in a riverbank," she said. "I didn't know what it was. I still don't."

"May I?" Dallet extended his hand, palm up.

She should have said no. She knew, even as her fingers went to her shirt pocket, that she should have said no — that the token was the one thing in her possession that was hers, found by her hands, kept by her choice, the small theft that had felt like the only honest thing she'd done in years. But refusing would confirm it was worth something. And it *was* worth something, and Dallet knew that she knew it, and the whole geometry of the evening had been built toward this exact moment with the patience of a man setting a table.

She put the token in his palm.

He held it to the lamp. Turned it. The stamped fitting caught the green light — the seal of Karvath, the interlocked river-and-mountain device that no living map carried anymore, that appeared in no registry, that had been struck from every chart and census and schoolbook sixty years ago when the border war ended and the nation ended with it.

"Beautiful," he said softly. "You know what this is."

"I know what Marenko said it was."

"And you didn't report it." He set it down between them on the linen, precisely centered, like a piece on a board. "Which is, technically, an offense under the Antiquities Act. Removal of a protected artifact from a concession zone. The concession, I should say, held by your mother — which makes you not a finder but, in the ministry's eyes, a thief from your own family's permit. There's a poetry in that, don't you think?"

Orsen's mouth had gone dry. She drank anyway.

"What do you want, Dallet?"

"There it is." He sounded almost pleased. "You know, most people take another hour to ask that. Your mother would have taken two. She'd have told me three stories first, all of them true, none of them relevant." He leaned back. "What I want is simple. I want you to keep working exactly as you're working. I want your survey reports — the real ones, the field originals, not the summaries your mother files. And I want to know when she deviates from them."

The words took a moment to find their weight. When they did, they were heavier than she'd expected.

"You're asking me to report on my mother."

"I'm asking you to report on her *records*." He held up a finger. "Let me tell you what I know, and you can decide how it sounds. In the last nine days, your mother has logged four dig locations. Your instruments — your excellent, honest instruments — have flagged readings at seven. The three she's declined to log all cluster in the same sector, northeast of the main trench, beneath the deepest talus. Your cartographer, Sef, has noticed. He's worried. He brought it to you, I believe, or was working up to it." He paused. "Your mother is redirecting the dig away from her strongest readings, Surveyor. Do you know why?"

Orsen said nothing. The fish had gone cold in her stomach.

"Neither do I," Dallet said. "That's the honest answer. I have theories — I always have theories, it's my trade — but I don't have facts. And facts are what the ministry pays me for." He refilled her glass. "Here is what I can offer in exchange. The Antiquities Act offense — the token — it goes away. Not forgotten; *resolved*. A finding of inadvertent discovery, properly documented, no penalty. Your name clean. And more than that: protection. Because I'll tell you something for free, Surveyor Vasquez, something you may not have fully understood when you signed on." He lowered his voice, and the lamplight made his face gentler and more terrible at once. "This expedition is going to end badly for someone. Concessions like this one, with stakes like these, they always do. When it ends, the ministry will need someone to stand in front of. Your mother is building the case against herself — falsified logs are prosecutable, and she knows it, and she's doing it anyway, which should tell you how much she fears whatever's under that talus. When the reckoning comes, I can make sure it isn't you standing there. Or I can do nothing, and let the current take whoever it takes."

He picked up the token again, weighed it, and — to her surprise — slid it back across the linen toward her.

"Keep it," he said. "Reported, documented, resolved. It's yours now, legally. Consider it a gesture of good faith." The easy smile again, arriving right on time this time, which was how she knew it was the false one. "I'm not a monster, Surveyor. I'm a clerk. A very well-funded clerk. And clerks only want one thing in this world: accurate records."

Orsen looked at the token lying on the white cloth. Sixty years erased, and it sat there small as a button, catching the lamplight.

"And if I say no?"

Dallet spread his hands, and for a moment — just a moment — the courtesy dropped away and she saw the thing underneath, which was not cruelty exactly, but a kind of vast, patient indifference, the indifference of an institution that had already erased a nation from every map and could certainly erase one surveyor from one expedition.

"Then nothing happens tonight," he said. "Nothing happens tomorrow. That's the beauty of my position — I never have to do anything at all. The permits expire in eighty-one days. The ministry reviews every concession at expiry. And at review, every record gets read. Including the ones your mother didn't file." He stood, signaling the dinner was over, and shook her hand again — dry, brief, precise. "You're a good surveyor, Vasquez. The best on this river, I'd wager. Good surveyors know that the truth is in the readings, not the reports. Think about whose reports you want to be standing behind when the water rises."

* * *

She walked back through the camp in the dark, past the banked cookfire, past Sef's tent where a lantern still burned and a pencil scratched against paper — he was working late on the maps, on the maps that didn't match the field notes, on the maps he was worried about. She kept walking.

At her own tent she sat on the cot and took out the token. It was warm from Dallet's hand, which she hated. She held it under her headlamp and looked at the seal for a long time: the river and the mountain, interlocked, the device of a country that had existed for six hundred years and now existed nowhere except in riverbanks and in the files of a well-funded clerk.

She had walked into the dinner knowing the shape of the thing. She'd thought she knew the depth, too. But she hadn't accounted for this: that Dallet didn't want her to lie, or steal, or sabotage. He wanted her to do the thing she was already inclined to do — to trust the readings, to distrust the reports, to pull the thread her mother was so carefully winding back in. He'd offered her protection for her own honesty. That was the elegance of it. That was the trap of it.

Because she had seen the false logs. She had watched her mother redirect the dig away from the strongest readings with a calm that had frightened her more than any shouting would have. And some part of her — the part that had pocketed the token instead of reporting it, the part that had fled her mother's name for five years — that part wanted to hand the field originals to Dallet first thing in the morning, just to watch Ilse Vasquez have to account for something, anything, to someone.

And another part, deeper, older, the part she didn't like to name, remembered a woman teaching her to read a contour map by lamplight, a woman who had once said *the river doesn't lie, Orsen, so neither should we* — and that part understood that whatever her mother was burying under the talus, she was burying it at a cost that was eating her alive.

A piece on two boards. Dallet had said it without saying it. Move for me, and I'll shield you from the game. Refuse, and the game plays itself, and you're still on the board.

Outside, the wind moved along the escarpment, and somewhere downriver, in villages that appeared on no map she'd ever been hired to check, people who still called themselves by an erased name were going about their evenings, not knowing that a sealed chest had come up out of the flood, or that their proof was buried under a hundred tons of stone, or that a surveyor sat in a tent holding it in her hand.

Orsen closed her fist around the token. Eighty-one days.

She reached for her field notebook — the real one, the one Dallet wanted — and opened it to a blank page, and for a long moment her pen hovered above it, not writing, not yet.

Then, in the corner, small, where no clerk would think to look, she drew a single line: the contour of the northeast sector, the readings her mother had refused to log. And beside it, one word, in shorthand only she used.

*Tomorrow*, she told herself. *Decide tomorrow.*

But she knew, folding the notebook shut in the dark, that the decision had already been made — she just didn't yet know which way.
