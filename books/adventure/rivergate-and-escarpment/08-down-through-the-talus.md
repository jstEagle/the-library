## 8. Down Through the Talus

The argument with my mother lasted eleven minutes and ended the way all our arguments ended — with her winning on paper and me losing in a way she'd regret.

"You're not running a night survey," Ilse said, standing at the edge of the trench with her arms crossed against the cold coming off the escarpment. Behind her, the floodlights of the main dig threw her shadow long across the talus slope, a thin dark spear pointing downriver toward everything we hadn't found yet.

"The readings say otherwise."

"The readings are noise. Thermal bleed from the rock shelf. I've seen it a hundred times."

"You've seen it when it was convenient to have seen it." I kept my voice level. We had an audience — half the crew pretending to coil ropes and check lamps within earshot, Dallet nowhere in sight, which somehow made it worse, because I could feel his absence listening. "Mother, there's a void under that slope with a regular geometry. Voids don't have corners."

"Orsen." She used the name like a scalpel, the way she had since I was nine years old and first contradicted her in front of students. "You signed on as my surveyor. That means you survey where I tell you to survey. If you can't do that, the ministry will be delighted to find someone who can."

There it was. The old arithmetic. Obey or be replaced, and if replaced, be ruined — because Dallet's file on me was still fat with the token, the broker, the letter I should never have opened twice.

"Fine," I said. "I'll survey where you tell me to."

She almost smiled. That was her mistake. She turned back toward the trench, satisfied, and didn't see what Sef saw from twenty feet away — the way I said *fine* like a man signing a contract he intended to break.

* * *

I waited three nights. Long enough for the pattern to set, for Ilse to believe the matter closed, for Dallet to settle into his evening ritual of wine and cheerful questions by the mess fire. On the fourth night the moon came up late and thin, and the temperature dropped hard enough to make the talus sing — that faint ticking of settling stone that every river rat learns to hear through and every flatlander mistakes for ghosts.

Sef met me at the instrument cache at eleven. He had his field jacket buttoned wrong and his hands shaking, though whether from cold or conscience I couldn't tell.

"She'll know it was us," he whispered.

"She already knows it's true. Knowing who confirmed it is the smaller crime." I slung the gravimeter's harness over my shoulder. "Bring the lamps. Both of them."

We went up the talus in the dark, boots finding the same route I'd memorized by day — the long scree fan where the escarpment had been shedding itself into the Rivergate valley since before anyone thought to write anything down. My mother's published theory put any Karvathi structure two hundred meters east, tucked behind a limestone nose where the geology cooperated with her maps. My instruments disagreed. They had disagreed for six days, politely, in the language of milligals and microseisms, and tonight I meant to make them shout.

The anomaly sat under forty meters of collapsed rubble, roughly circular, thirty paces across, with a density gradient at its northern rim that looked — no other word for it — *built*. Stone doesn't arrange itself in straight lines, even after sixty centuries of trying to fall down.

I planted the gravimeter stations in a grid while Sef held the lamp low and red, and by one in the morning the picture was undeniable. Not a chamber. A shaft. Vertical, lined, descending from somewhere near the old slope face — buried when some ancient flood or earthquake brought the cliff down over it, sealed the way a hand seals a letter.

"There," I said, crouching at the grid point where the lines converged. I pulled off a glove and pressed my palm flat against the rubble. Cold came up through the stone, but underneath the cold was something else — a draft, faint as breath, moving *upward*. Air goes where air can. Somewhere below us, that air had a way out, which meant somewhere below us, there was a way in.

Sef knelt beside me. In the red light his face looked stripped of everything young. "It's real," he said. Not a question. A man reading his own death warrant and finding his name spelled correctly.

"It's real."

"And she knew."

I didn't answer that. Some answers you only get to give once, and I wanted to be standing in front of her when I gave mine.

* * *

Breaking through took four nights of work disguised as drainage maintenance. Ilse had ordered a culvert cut above the camp to channel snowmelt away from the trenches — legitimate, necessary, and blessedly far from her sightline. I moved the crew assigned to it forty meters north. Bren, the foreman, asked exactly one question — "How deep?" — and when I told him, he spat, rolled his sleeves, and said nothing else for three days. That's the kind of loyalty you can't buy; you can only earn it by being right in front of people who check.

On the fourth night, Bren's bar hit something that rang instead of crunched.

We all heard it. Four men and me, frozen in the lamplight, dust hanging around us like weather. That sound — metal-edged stone ringing hollow — is older than language. It's the sound of a door.

"Hand tools now," I said. "Slow. Nobody swings."

We cleared the last meter with brushes and bare hands, kneeling in a half-circle like mourners, and what emerged from the talus was not rubble at all. It was masonry. Fitted blocks of grey riverstone, each cut square and dressed smooth, laid in courses so tight that sixty years of flood silt hadn't found a seam. Karvathi work. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting — from the fragments in textbooks, from the one shattered arch in a museum basement I'd visited at nineteen and stood in front of for an hour while my mother waited impatiently by the door.

Ilse had written her dissertation on those fragments. She had built a career on the idea that the Karvathi were river traders, minor players, a culture too small to leave more than shards. Every map in every atlas agreed with her, because every map had been redrawn after the border war, and the nation they erased had been erased so thoroughly that even its stones were supposed to be shy.

The stones were not shy.

Bren worked the crowbar into the gap between two blocks — not prying, just testing — and the block shifted with a grinding sigh, exhaling sixty years of held breath past our faces. The air that came out smelled of dry stone and something mineral and sweet, like water that had run over iron a very long time ago.

"Lamp," I said.

Sef passed it forward. His hands had stopped shaking. I remember noticing that — how fear burns off a man once the thing he feared becomes simply the thing that is happening.

We widened the gap block by block until it would take a body sideways, and then, because I was the surveyor and because nobody else was fool enough, I went first.

* * *

The corridor ran straight into the heart of the escarpment, walls of the same fitted grey stone, ceiling just high enough that Bren had to duck and I did not. The lamp pushed the dark back maybe ten meters and gave up. Underfoot, the floor was paved in slabs worn into a shallow trough down the center — the wear of feet, thousands upon thousands of them, moving the same way for generations. You don't get that wear in a tomb. Tombs don't have traffic.

"Corridor," I called back, my voice doing strange things in the acoustics, arriving at my own ears a half-beat late like a stranger's. "Structural. Dry. It runs deep."

Nobody answered right away. Then Bren's voice, rough: "Surveyor's call, Marchetti. How far do we go?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Everything downstream of this moment hung on it. Go far enough to confirm and pull out — log a structure, file a report, let my mother manage whatever managing she intended — or go all the way, and find out why a dead nation had built a door into a mountain and then let a river bury it.

I walked.

The corridor bent once, gently, following some logic of the bedrock, and opened into a wider gallery where the lamp finally found the walls properly — and I stopped understanding anything except that I was looking at writing.

Not glyphs. Not decoration. Writing — dense, confident, carved in clean vertical registers that ran the length of the gallery on both sides, interrupted every few meters by a carved emblem: a stylized river fork inside a circle. The seal from my token, scaled up until it could crown a doorway.

"Sef," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable. "Get in here. Bring everything. Camera, paper, the tracing kit. And—" I made myself say it. "And wake my mother."

He went. I heard him running, heard the crew murmuring outside, heard the ordinary world scrambling toward the extraordinary one, and I stayed alone in the gallery with the lamp and the words.

I couldn't read them. Sixty years of erasure takes the language with it — that was the whole cruelty of what had been done to Karvath, that they hadn't just taken the land, they'd taken the alphabet, the schoolbooks, the names. But writing has a grammar even to the illiterate eye, and these registers repeated, grouped, separated by those little emblems. Lists. I was looking at lists.

Then the lamp caught a section of wall lower than the rest, protected by a ledge, and I saw that whoever carved it had also painted it, and the pigment had survived in the dry dark — faded ochre and black, and beneath one register, small figures. Houses. Boats. Rows of tiny painted dwellings along a painted river, each cluster marked with a symbol above it, and the symbols repeated down the wall, village after village after village, a census in stone.

I counted fourteen clusters before my eyes stopped working right.

Fourteen villages. On a river. *Our* river — the Rivergate's course was fixed in the bedrock long before any nation drew or erased a line on top of it. Whatever river these carvers had lived on, it bent and braided the way the Rivergate bends and braids. These weren't abstract settlements in some lost homeland. These were addresses. Places with names, carved here in permanent witness, and if even a few of those places had kept their ground through sixty years of official nonexistence—

Downriver, people were sleeping in houses whose names were written on this wall.

I put my hand against the stone, below the paint, where the surface was bare, and the stone was cool and patient under my palm. Somewhere behind me, footsteps and lamplight were growing louder — my mother coming to see what I'd found, Dallet's absence suddenly the loudest thing in the camp, Sef's camera, Bren's silence, the whole fragile machinery of ninety days and expiring permits and a ministry that smiled too easily.

Awe is a strange feeling to carry alone in the dark. It wants witnesses. But dread had arrived with it, holding its coat, and dread was whispering the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the night I pocketed that token: *a discovery like this isn't yours. It never was. It belongs to everyone whose name is on that wall, and everyone who spent sixty years pretending those names were never written.*

The lamp flickered. The painted villages stared back at me, fourteen small clusters of homes on a painted river, waiting the way stone waits — without hurry, without mercy, certain that eventually someone would come down through the talus and read them aloud.

Behind me, at the mouth of the corridor, my mother's voice reached me for the first time in five years sounding genuinely afraid.

"Orsen. What have you done?"

What I had done was turn on a light. What happened next was already walking down the valley toward us — I just didn't know yet that it would arrive wearing a schoolteacher's coat, leading a delegation of the living to stand in front of the wall that remembered them.

But the river knew. The river keeps its own books.
