## 9. The Descendants

They came up the river on a Tuesday, in three flat-bottomed boats that rode low with the weight of too many people and not enough cargo. Orsen saw them first from the survey tent, where she'd been hunched over the resonance readings for two hours, pretending the numbers hadn't already told her everything she was afraid of.

The boats came around the bend below the escarpment in single file, moving against the current with the patient, unhurried rhythm of people who had made this journey before — or whose grandparents had. Not refugees. Pilgrims, maybe. Or something older than either word.

She stepped out of the tent into the white heat of late morning. Down at the landing, Hesk the foreman was already standing with his arms crossed, watching the boats come in, and beside him Dallet had appeared from nowhere, as he always did, his ministry badge catching the sun like a small bright lie.

"Visitors," Orsen said to no one.

"Villagers," Dallet said pleasantly. "From downriver. They do this sometimes. Come up to look at the dig." He smiled his easy smile. "Nothing to worry about."

Orsen didn't answer. She was counting heads. Eleven in the lead boat, nine in the second, and in the third — fewer, maybe six, mostly children, which meant this wasn't a delegation so much as a community that had decided its children should see whatever happened here. That decision told her more than any manifesto could have.

The boats grounded on the shingle below the camp. The passengers climbed out and stood for a moment, all of them looking up — past the tents, past the scaffolding of the trench, up the gray face of the escarpment itself, as if checking that it was still there. As if they'd been told stories about it their whole lives and needed to confirm the stories were true.

Then a woman stepped forward from the lead group, and Orsen understood immediately who she was, though no one introduced her. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with river-gray hair pulled back and the kind of face that had spent decades being patient with institutions that did not deserve it. She wore a schoolteacher's cardigan despite the heat, buttoned to the throat, and she carried a canvas satchel the way other people carried weapons.

She walked up the shingle alone. The rest waited.

"I'm Maren Osta," she said, when she reached them. Her accent was river-flat, the vowels rounded the way speech gets in towns that have been talking to themselves for generations. "I teach at Lower Vessel school. Fourth generation." She looked at each of them in turn — Hesk, Dallet, Orsen — cataloguing them without hurry. "We've come to ask a question."

"Lower Vessel," Dallet said warmly. "Lovely town. I did an inspection there two years ago, very welcoming people." He extended a hand. "Corvin Dallet, Ministry of Cultural Affairs. If you've come about permits or access—"

"We haven't." She didn't take his hand. She turned instead to Orsen, because Orsen was the one holding a surveyor's staff, and staffs mean work, and work means answers. "Are you the one who found the corridor?"

Orsen felt the question land somewhere under her ribs. Word traveled fast on a river; faster than permits, faster than ministries. "I'm the surveyor," she said carefully. "Dr. Vasquez is directing the excavation."

"Dr. Vasquez." Maren said the name the way you'd read a line item off an invoice. Then her eyes came back to Orsen and stayed there, and Orsen saw what was in them and had to look away first: not anger, not pleading. *Recognition.* The look of someone reading a familiar text in an unfamiliar hand. "You have her jaw," Maren said quietly. "Ilse Vasquez's daughter. We know your mother's name downriver. We've known it for twenty years."

* * *

Ilse came out of her command tent four minutes later, which Orsen knew because she counted — Ilse Vasquez never let visitors wait longer than five, but she never came immediately either. The delay was choreography. It said: *I am busy, and you are fortunate.*

Her mother crossed the camp ground in her field khakis, silver hair tied back, spine straight as a plumb line, and Orsen watched her perform the oldest trick in her repertoire: warmth deployed at exactly the distance required to control a conversation.

"Ms. Osta. Welcome to the concession." Ilse's voice carried easily across the shingle. "I understand you've come a long way. Please, sit — we can offer tea, shade—"

"We don't need tea." Maren stood her ground on the hot stones. Behind her, the villagers had begun to move up from the boats, slowly, the children herded to the front where they could see. One of them, a boy of eight or nine, was staring at the trench mouth with his mouth open. "We need one question answered, Dr. Vasquez. One. And then we'll go home."

"Ask it," Ilse said, "and I'll answer honestly within the limits of what I'm permitted to disclose."

Maren almost smiled. "Permitted. Yes." She opened her satchel then, and drew out a folded cloth, and unwrapped it with both hands, careful as a surgeon. Inside lay a token — coin-sized, lacquered bronze gone green at the edges, stamped with a seal Orsen knew better than her own signature, because an identical one sat wrapped in a sock at the bottom of her duffel bag, sixty meters away in her own tent.

The camp went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold off.

"My grandmother carried this out of the escarpment when she was six years old," Maren said. "Before the removals. Before the maps were redrawn. Her father worked these tunnels — he was a keeper of records, family story says, though we lost the word for what that meant. When Karvath was erased, this was all she kept. She gave it to me the way you give someone a key to a house that's been demolished." She held it up between finger and thumb, turning it so the seal caught the light. "For sixty years, our villages have been listed in ministry registers as *unaffiliated river settlements*. No history. No claim. No name. My students learn their own town existed only since the war. Their grandparents' graves are filed under nothing."

She closed her fist around the token.

"So here is the question, Dr. Vasquez, and I'll ask it once. Under this escarpment — in the structure your machines have found, in the corridor your crew broke into eleven days ago — is there proof? Proof that we were here. Names. Records. Anything with our ancestors' hands on it." Her voice didn't rise at all. That was the terrible thing about it. "Did you find proof we were here?"

* * *

Later, Orsen would replay the silence that followed many times, trying to measure it. It lasted perhaps six seconds. In those six seconds she watched her mother do something she had seen her do only twice before in her life: choose, deliberately and visibly, to lie.

"No," Ilse said.

The word dropped onto the shingle like a stone into still water.

"The structure shows promise," Ilse went on, smooth as poured concrete, "but it's early. Collapsed sections, water damage, minimal recoverable material so far. Nothing — *nothing* — that would constitute evidence of continuous habitation by any specific population. I understand the hope. I understand why the erasure of Karvath left wounds. But archaeology is not wish fulfillment, Ms. Osta. If proof emerges, it will be published through proper channels, in proper time. Until then, I'd ask you not to raise expectations in your community. Hope is heavy cargo to carry home."

Orsen heard the craftsmanship in it. Every sentence load-bearing, every phrase deniable. *Minimal recoverable material.* She had personally logged forty-one inscribed masonry blocks. She had traced the corridor wall where names were cut into the stone in Karvathi script — village names, she now realized, staring at Maren's face. *Village names.* Names that matched settlements still standing downriver, still inhabited, still waiting.

"You're certain," Maren said. Not a question. A door held open one last moment.

"I am certain of my data," Ilse said.

Maren nodded slowly. She rewrapped the token in its cloth with the same surgical care, and put it back in the satchel, and Orsen thought: *she brought it to show us she'd believe us. She brought her grandmother's whole life in her bag, ready to lay it on the table.* And Ilse had looked at it and said *no*.

"Thank you for your honesty," Maren said, and her voice finally cracked, just barely, on the last word, and Orsen felt the crack travel through her own chest like a fault line finding its depth. "My grandmother waited her whole life for someone to ask her what she remembered. Nobody official ever did. She died in a town that officially had never been anywhere else." She turned to go, then stopped. "One more thing, Doctor. Free of charge. The river keeps its own books. It always has. Whatever you're keeping down there—" she touched the satchel, "—it won't stay buried just because you want it to. Water finds its level. So does truth. Eventually."

They went back down to the boats. The children went last, reluctantly, the small boy craning his neck at the trench until his mother tugged him along. The boats pushed off into the current and slid away around the bend, and the camp exhaled, and Dallet said something pleasant about tea after all, and Ilse turned back toward her command tent as if a delegation of thirty people asking about their erased homeland had been a minor scheduling inconvenience.

Orsen didn't move. She stood on the shingle with the sun hammering the stones and her mother's lie still ringing in her ears, and she thought about her own life in a way she hadn't let herself think in years. Five years of contracts. Five years of forwarding addresses that expired. Five years of measuring herself in miles because miles asked nothing of her — no roots, no claims, no name on anyone's register. She had told herself it was freedom. Standing there, watching the wake of Maren's boat flatten back into the river, she recognized it for what it was: the same wound, self-inflicted. A homeland erased from paper. A name filed under nothing. She had done to herself, voluntarily, what the ministry had done to Karvath — and called it independence.

*Maren's people had lost their country and kept their question. Orsen had kept her country and abandoned her questions.*

It was the most honest thing she'd felt in years, and it arrived, as honest things tended to lately, at the worst possible time.

* * *

She found her mother that evening at the map table, running a grease pencil along contour lines, planning tomorrow's grid. Orsen stood in the tent flap until Ilse acknowledged her with a glance.

"That woman," Orsen said. "Osta. You looked her in the eye and lied to her."

"I managed a disclosure risk," Ilse said, not looking up. "There's a difference, and the difference is whether this expedition survives the next eighty days."

"She brought her grandmother's token. She brought her *children*, Ilse. They put their kids in a boat and rowed them upriver to see if their own history was real."

"And if I'd confirmed it?" Now Ilse looked up, and her eyes were tired in a way Orsen had never seen before — tired the way stone is tired, worn by pressure rather than age. "Thirty witnesses. By nightfall it's in every river town, by week's end it's in the ministry's morning brief, and Dallet files to revoke our permits before the month is out. The site gets sealed pending review. Sealed, Orsen. Sealed means locked, and locked means the archive — *if* there is an archive — sits behind a ministry padlock for a decade while lawyers argue about jurisdiction. Is that what those children came for? A padlock?"

"You don't know that's what would happen."

"I know exactly what happens," Ilse said, and something moved behind her face, some memory surfacing and being pushed back down, quick as a fish. "I've watched it happen before."

"To whom?" Orsen asked. "What happened before, Mother? Twenty years ago — what did you bury?"

The grease pencil stopped moving. For one long moment the tent was silent except for the generator drone and the river beyond, and Orsen watched her mother stand at the edge of an answer the way you stand at the edge of dark water, judging the depth.

"Back to work, Orsen," Ilse said quietly. "First light tomorrow. Grid seven needs resurveying."

It was a dismissal. But it wasn't a denial, and they both knew it, and that difference hummed in the air between them like a struck wire.

Orsen walked back to her own tent in the blue dusk. At the trench mouth she paused, as she did every night now, and looked down into the corridor where the lamplight fell across cut stone and carved names. Village names. Living names. Names that belonged to people who had rowed upstream today carrying a dead grandmother's bronze token and a question, and been sent home with nothing.

She took out her field notebook, flipped past the false grids Sef had flagged, past the coordinates she was supposed to log, and on a clean page she wrote, in her tightest surveyor's hand:

*Lower Vessel. Fourth generation. Keeper of records — family word lost. Token matches mine.*

Then, underneath, after a long pause:

*Water finds its level.*

She closed the book. Somewhere downriver, a schoolteacher was telling thirty children the truth their own government wouldn't speak. And somewhere in this camp, Orsen realized, watching the lamplight flicker over the carved wall — she was going to have to decide, soon, which side of that sentence she stood on.

Behind her, gravel crunched. She turned.

Dallet stood ten feet away in the dark, hands in his pockets, smiling his easy smile.

"Beautiful evening for reflection," he said. "Got a minute? I think it's time you and I had that dinner."
