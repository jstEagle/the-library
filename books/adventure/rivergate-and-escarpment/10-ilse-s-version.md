## 10. Ilse's Version

The generator coughed twice before settling into its rhythm, and in the half-second of silence Ilse heard the escarpment breathing — that low, mineral exhalation of cooling stone that had been the last sound of her nights for twenty years, on three continents, in four dig houses that all smelled of diesel and dried river clay. She sat at the survey table with the lamp turned low and the false coordinate ledger open in front of her, and she did not look up when the tent flap moved, because she had known for six days that this conversation was coming, and she had spent those six days choosing her words the way an engineer chooses load-bearing stone.

Orsen came in the way she did everything now — like someone crossing ice she no longer trusted. She had her mother's shoulders. Ilse had noticed that at the staging camp and hated noticing it, the way you hate seeing your own handwriting in a stranger's letter. Twenty-nine years old, five years of river work in her hands, and she stood in the doorway of her mother's tent with the posture of a woman who had already decided to leave.

"You sent for me," Orsen said.

"I asked if you'd come by. There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, and you came anyway."

"Maybe I wanted to watch you lie to my face in a comfortable chair."

Ilse gestured to the camp stool across the table. Orsen ignored it. Fine. Standing was better for what this had to be. Standing meant short visits, and short visits meant fewer chances for the truth to leak out sideways, the way it always did between them, through the cracks where love had dried and cracked and let the old arguments seep back through.

"You found the false logs," Ilse said. "Or Sef did, and Sef told you. Either way, you've spent three days deciding whether to hate me for it, and I'd rather you hate me with the full picture than with the half one you're carrying around."

"That's generous of you."

"It's practical of me." Ilse closed the ledger. Under it, where she had left it that afternoon, lay the thing she had decided to show no one — not Dallet, not the foreman, not the ministry, and certainly not the daughter who had stolen a coin from a flood-scoured bank and thought her mother wouldn't hear about it within the week. A photograph, taken eleven years ago, in a country that no longer existed on paper, in a trench that had been backfilled by order of the same ministry now smiling at her across the mess tent.

She slid it across the table.

Orsen looked down. Ilse watched her daughter's face do what faces did when they met the dead: the flicker of recognition, the refusal, the recognition again. The photograph showed a corridor. Karvathi masonry, the same tight-fitting pale limestone they had broken into nine days ago, the same incised script running in registers along the wall. And standing in the corridor, younger by two decades, mud to the elbow and triumph all over her face, Dr. Ilse Vasquez, holding a lamp up to names.

"That's not the escarpment," Orsen said slowly. "That's — where is that?"

"Sixty kilometers downriver. Under a bend the river abandoned four hundred years ago. I found it in my second season. I was thirty-one, and I thought I had found the answer to everything." Ilse kept her voice level, the voice she used for lectures, for funding boards, for the ministry men who came to her digs with clipboards and left with her conclusions. "I published a preliminary note. A single note, four pages. And eleven days after it appeared, the ministry arrived, sealed the trench, confiscated my field notes, my photographs, and my samples, and opened a review into my 'methodological irregularities.' There were no irregularities. There was only the finding itself. Karvath could not be permitted to have been real. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Not disputed. Not debated. *Not permitted.*"

Orsen hadn't sat down after all. She lowered herself onto the stool now, slowly, the photograph still in her hands. "I never knew about this."

"No. You knew about the scandal. Everyone knew about the scandal — the plagiarism accusation, the retraction, the disgraced Dr. Vasquez crawling out of the profession on her hands and knees while her daughter learned to stop saying her name at school." Ilse let that sit. It was a wound she had let her daughter carry for fifteen years because the truth had been more dangerous than the shame, and she was not going to apologize for it now, in this tent, with ninety days on the clock and Dallet's ears everywhere. "The plagiarism accusation was manufactured. I could have fought it. Fighting it would have meant producing the field notes they'd already confiscated. So I let it stand. I let my daughter grow up ashamed of me because shame was survivable and the alternative was watching them bury a nation twice."

The generator coughed again. Somewhere outside, the night crew was winding down; she could hear Tomas's voice, low and even, counting crates. Good man. He'd keep them away from this tent for an hour. She had asked, and he hadn't asked why, and that was the difference between loyalty and curiosity, and she had built her whole late career on people who had the first and not the second.

Orsen set the photograph down with exaggerated care, the way she handled artifacts, the way she handled everything except herself. "So this is the speech. This is where you tell me the falsified logs are actually heroism."

"This is where I tell you what happens if we publish."

"Then tell me."

Ilse folded her hands. She had rehearsed this in the shower, on the escarpment rim at dawn, into the dark of the tent ceiling at three in the morning, and every rehearsal had ended in the same place: her daughter's face going through exactly what it was going through now, the anger looking for a door and finding only arithmetic.

"Day one," Ilse said. "The archive chamber is logged. Within seventy-two hours, Dallet files for an emergency seizure — he doesn't need to fabricate grounds, the concession terms give him grounds, any unreported find of 'national historical material' reverts to state custody. The state that erased Karvath from its maps takes custody of Karvath's census rolls. You understand what that means? The land grants in that chamber are the legal foundation of the river villages' claims. In ministry hands, those documents will be 'under review' for thirty years. Maren's grandchildren will die waiting for the review to conclude."

"Or they get published and the claim is filed now."

"Published *how*?" Ilse leaned forward. "Through what channel? The concession requires all findings to be reported through the ministry's own review office. There is no independent press within four hundred kilometers that isn't licensed by the same government. We publish, and what gets published is a ministry communiqué: 'unverified materials of disputed provenance recovered, investigation ongoing.' And then — day four, day five — the prosecutions begin. Falsified survey logs. Unreported finds. Sef goes to prison, Orsen, not fined, *prisoned*, and they will make an example of him because he's the one who held the pen. Tomas loses his license. The camp hands lose their livelihoods. And you—" she stopped, and made herself finish it, because the whole speech was built to arrive here, "—you are the surveyor of record. You ran the instruments. Your name is on the true coordinates. You would be the one they buried, and they would bury you *gladly*, because your mother is already a convicted fraud and a daughter following her into disgrace is a tidy little story that writes itself."

Orsen's jaw was tight. "And the site?"

"The site gets sealed pending investigation. Sealed means concrete, Orsen. I've seen it. I stood on the trench I found at thirty-one and watched them pour it, and the ministry engineer was polite about it, he even offered me a cigarette, and he said — I've never forgotten the phrasing — he said, 'Doctor, some things are more stable unrecovered.'" She heard her own voice thin and pressed it flat again. "The archive is paper. Sixty years of damp and one careless seal and it's pulp. The descendants don't lose a claim. They lose the *possibility* of a claim, permanently. There is no second archive chamber. There is no second flood to hand us another chest."

"So your answer is to sit on it."

"My answer is to wait." Ilse pulled the concession schedule across the table — the laminated sheet she kept like a talisman, ninety days marked off in her own handwriting, sixty-one remaining. "Permits lapse at midnight on the final day. When they lapse, the concession terms expire — *all* of them. Including the reporting requirements. Including the seizure provisions. A find made under a lapsed concession on land that reverts to customary use is a different legal animal entirely. The villages have customary claims to that bank that predate the ministry's maps. If the archive surfaces *after* the permits die — surfaced by whom, no one can say, found in the flood debris, who could prove otherwise — then it enters the world through the villages, not through us. Their claim. Their documents. Their name read back into the record by the people it was stolen from, not by a ministry that stole it."

For the first time, Orsen's expression wavered. Ilse saw it happen and hated how much she wanted it — that small loosening around her daughter's mouth, the recalculation behind her eyes. She pressed on, gently now, because the hammer part of the speech was done and what remained had to be laid down like a hand.

"You think I falsified those logs to protect my career. My career is over, Orsen. It has been over for twenty years; everything since has been an afterlife. I falsified them because the truth, released at the wrong moment, is not the truth — it's ammunition, and I know exactly which side holds the guns." She tapped the photograph. "I have been on the other end of this. I was younger than you are now, and I believed that finding a thing was the same as saving it. It isn't. Finding is the beginning of the danger. What you do in the weeks after the finding — that's the actual work. That's the discipline. Anyone can dig up a nation. Keeping it alive while the world decides whether to let it exist — that takes patience, and it takes silence, and it takes a daughter who doesn't hand her mother's ledger to a smiling ministry man because she's angry about a childhood she doesn't fully understand."

"That's not fair."

"No," Ilse said. "It isn't. Very little of this is fair. Fairness is what people want when they can't have control, and control is the only thing keeping eleven people out of prison and a nation's paper corpse out of a shredder."

The silence that followed was long. Ilse let it be long. She had learned that from the escarpment: things settled on their own schedule, and rushing them broke them.

Orsen looked at the photograph again. "The villages," she said finally. "Maren's people. They came here asking one question, and you stonewalled them. You let them stand at the edge of the camp like beggars at a fence."

"I let them stand at the edge of the camp *uninvolved*," Ilse said. "The moment Maren's delegation sets foot in this dig as claimants, Dallet has his seizure order by morning. Their patience is not weakness, Orsen. It's the whole strategy. They wait, the permits die, the documents surface, and they are the ones holding them. Clean hands. Their hands. That's the only version of this that ends with their name on the land instead of a ministry seal on a concrete slab."

"And if Dallet moves first? If he revokes early?"

Ilse had not let herself think about that more than twice a day, and each time the thought had arrived with the same cold weight. "Then I adapt," she said. "That's what I do. I have been adapting for twenty years."

Orsen stood. She stood the way she'd entered, like leaving was a decision already made and this was just the execution of it, but she paused at the table, and she picked up the photograph, and she looked at it for a long moment — at her mother, young and mud-streaked and lit by her own lamp in a corridor of names.

"Eleven days," Orsen said. "You had eleven days between publishing and the seizure. How long do you think we have?"

Ilse looked at the laminated schedule. Sixty-one days. Dallet's smile at dinner. The way he'd started asking Sef about map versions.

"Less than that," she said. "Which is why I need to know, right now, tonight, whose side you're on. Not mine — I'm not vain enough to ask for that. The side of the eleven people in this camp who will stand in a courtroom if you're wrong about how the world works. The side of a schoolteacher's students who get their grandparents' names back in ten years instead of never. Tell me you understand what the silence is *for*, even if you hate it. That's all I'm asking. Not agreement. Understanding."

Orsen put the photograph down. For a moment Ilse thought — allowed herself, dangerously, to think — that her daughter's face was softening into something like belief, the arithmetic landing, the anger finding a door after all.

"I understand what you say it's for," Orsen said quietly. "I'll give you that much tonight."

She left. The tent flap fell. Ilse sat alone with the lamp and the ledger and the photograph of her younger self holding light up to a nation's name, and she told herself the conversation had gone as well as it could have, and almost believed it.

Then she opened the ledger to check the false coordinates against the day's field notes, and found, tucked into the binding where it had not been that morning, a folded page in her daughter's handwriting — the true coordinates of the archive chamber, copied out fair and complete, with a single line beneath them:

*In case you're right. And in case you're not.*

Ilse sat very still for a long time, listening to the escarpment breathe, holding the paper that was either a peace offering or a fuse.
