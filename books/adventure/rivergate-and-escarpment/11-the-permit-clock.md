## 11. The Permit Clock

Thirty days.

I wrote it on the top margin of my survey ledger that morning, the way I'd once written tide tables and flow rates — a number to plan around, a fact as neutral as water depth. But the ink sat there on the page like a splinter. Thirty days until the concession permits lapsed and everything Ilse Vasquez had staked her last career on reverted to the ministry, to the escarpment, to the dark.

Thirty days until the earth closed its mouth again.

I was in the instrument tent before dawn, recalibrating the gradiometer because it had drifted a quarter-degree overnight and because calibration was the kind of problem I could actually solve. The tent glowed amber with the lamp. Outside, the Rivergate ran high and brown with snowmelt from the escarpment, and the talus field below the cliff face steamed where the sun touched it, as if the whole slope were exhaling.

The strongest readings still lay under the eastern talus — the place Ilse had redirected us away from six weeks ago, with her quiet, reasonable voice and her reasonable, poisoned logic. We'd been digging west ever since, following a thread of masonry that was real but minor, a service corridor, the wrist of the giant while the body slept under our feet to the east.

I had the false coordinates memorized now. Grid 14-E, logged at forty meters south of where we'd actually stood. Grid 17-C, elevation adjusted by eleven meters to flatten the anomaly. Sef had drawn the maps to match, his hand so steady you'd never know it shook at night. And somewhere in the camp office, in a locked case, sat the true logs — mine, Sef's, the ones that would hang us all if they surfaced and damn the discovery if they didn't.

"Orsen."

Dallet's voice. Of course it was. The man had a gift for arriving at the exact moment a thought turned dangerous, as though he kept a wire tapped directly into my skull.

He ducked into the tent without waiting for an invitation. Ministry observers didn't wait. He was dressed as always in his gray field suit, immaculate, the creases sharp enough to draw blood, and he carried two enamel mugs of camp coffee, which was the most sinister thing about him. Nobody that courteous wanted nothing.

"Gradiometer trouble?" he asked, setting a mug on my worktable like an offering.

"Thermal drift. It happens when the nights turn cold."

"Everything drifts, given time." He smiled. He had a smile like a door left ajar — you could never tell if it was welcome or trap. "I wondered if I might borrow a few minutes of your morning."

"You can have the minutes. The coffee I don't trust."

He laughed, genuinely, which was worse. He sat on the supply crate across from me, unhurried, a man settling in for a pleasant conversation he'd already won in his own mind.

"Thirty days," he said.

"You've been counting."

"It's my job to count." He turned his mug in his hands. "The permits expire at midnight on the last day of the concession. At that moment, the ministry assumes custody of the site, all equipment, all records, and all findings. Everything your mother's team has logged becomes ministry property. Everything she hasn't logged becomes—" he paused, savoring it, "—an interesting legal question."

"Is there a point to this visit, Dallet?"

"The point," he said, "is that I like you, Orsen. I've liked you since the day you arrived. You're the only person on this expedition who looks at the ground like it's telling the truth." He leaned forward. "Your mother is lying to her crew, to the ministry, and to herself. Sef is lying because he's frightened. The foreman lies by not asking questions. But you — you haven't decided yet. I find that refreshing."

"I haven't decided to talk to you, either."

"You already have." He sipped his coffee. "Here's what I know, and I want you to understand that I know it precisely. I know that grid 14-E exists in two versions. I know that the eastern anomaly was suppressed in the field reports. I know that a sealed chest bearing the Karvathi seal came out of the Rivergate bank five months ago, and that a river surveyor named Orsen Vasquez removed a token from the find site and did not report it." He let that sit. "I know your mother's career has a Karvathi-shaped hole in it from twenty years ago. And I know that when the permits lapse, if the ministry's record of this dig is the falsified one, then the falsification is the story. Not the city. Not the archive, if there is one. The fraud. Your mother prosecuted. Her team as accomplices. You, Orsen — you'd be the surveyor who faked the data. Your name would be in the file forever. You've seen how long ministry files live."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the foreman, Brannoc, was shouting the morning's work assignments, his voice cheerful and enormous, a man who still believed this was just a job.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"The true logs. A signed statement. And your testimony that the falsification was directed by Dr. Ilse Vasquez alone." He said it the way he said everything — reasonably, gently, like a man reading a menu. "In exchange, you walk away clean. Protected. I'll see to it personally. And when the ministry opens the site properly — and it will, Orsen, the eastern anomaly is too large to stay buried — you'll be the one who told the truth. That counts for something with the oversight board. That counts for a great deal."

"And if I don't?"

He spread his hands, regretful. "Then when this ends — and it ends in thirty days no matter what — you're inside the file with the rest of them. I'd hate that. I genuinely would."

I looked at him for a long moment. He let me. That was his technique; he never flinched from being seen, because he believed — correctly, most days — that there was nothing ugly in him to see. He was just weather. Weather didn't hate you. Weather didn't save you. It just decided where it rained.

"I'll consider it," I said.

"Of course you will." He rose, straightened a jacket that didn't need it, and paused at the tent flap. "One more thing, since we're being honest with each other. The villagers downriver. Maren's people. They've been asking questions in the market town. Filing inquiries. Quietly, politely — very Karvathi of them, I imagine, if anyone remembered what Karvathi was like." His smile returned, thinner. "The ministry has noticed. Politeness has a shelf life, Orsen. So do vigils. Do encourage your friends to be patient. Patient people stay safe."

He left. The coffee he'd brought sat untouched on my table, steam curling off it, and I found I couldn't look away from it — this small warm hostage, offered and refused.

I poured it out on the ground anyway. It seemed like the only honest use for it.

* * *

They came on the fourth evening after Dallet's visit, at the hour when the light goes long and gold and the escarpment throws its shadow clear across the valley floor.

I was coming up from the western cut, hands gray with talus dust, when Brannoc touched my arm and pointed. "You'll want to see this," he said, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before — not alarm. Recognition.

They came down the river road in a line, on foot and in flat-bottomed boats they'd hauled over the shallows: forty people, maybe fifty, from the villages downstream. I knew some of the faces from Maren's first delegation — the ferrymen, the old women with the river-stone jewelry, the children who'd been told to stay home and had not stayed home. They carried what they could carry: blankets, cookpots, lanterns, a rolled banner of undyed linen that I didn't understand until they unfurled it at the edge of camp, on the strip of gravel between our survey stakes and the water.

The banner was blank. That was the point. A nation erased from every map had learned not to write its name where it could be struck through again.

They didn't chant. They didn't hold signs. They set their lanterns in a line along the gravel — one lantern for each village that appeared in the corridor inscriptions, Maren had told me once, though I hadn't understood then what I was hearing — and then they sat down. Old people on folding stools. Children cross-legged in the dust. Maren in the center, straight-backed, her schoolteacher's spectacles catching the last light, a ledger open on her knees as if she'd come to teach a class and the class was history and the classroom was everything.

"What are they doing?" one of the crew asked. Pell, the young excavator. He'd stopped digging, trowel in hand, staring.

"Waiting," Brannoc said.

They waited. That was all. That was the whole strategy, and it was more unnerving than any protest could have been, because you couldn't argue with it. Every evening they came. Every morning they folded their blankets and went home to work their fields and fish their nets, and every evening they returned, and the line of lanterns grew — word traveling downriver, village by village, until there were more lanterns than villages and Maren had to start lighting them for families, for households, for the dead.

Ilse watched from the office tent the first night, arms crossed, her face lit blue by the lamplight on her desk. I stood beside her in the flap's shadow.

"You could talk to them," I said.

"I have talked to them."

"You stonewalled them. That's not the same verb."

"Orsen." She didn't look at me. Down on the gravel, Maren had begun to read aloud — I could just catch the cadence of it on the wind, the rise and fall of a woman reading names, or prayers, or possibly both; with Maren I'd learned the categories overlapped. "If I go down there and tell them what we've found, and the ministry hears I told them, Dallet has his pretext to revoke the permits tomorrow. Unauthorized disclosure. Endangering state interests. He'd write the citation tonight." She turned then, and her eyes in the lamplight were the same gray as mine, which had always felt like a theft. "Is that what you want? Thirty days of patience traded for one warm evening?"

"And if the thirty days run out?" I said. "If the permits lapse and the ministry seals the site and the archive — if there is an archive — goes into a warehouse with a state seal on the door? What do you say to them then? *We waited too*?"

She didn't answer. That was her answer. I walked down to the lantern line instead, because I couldn't stand in that tent one more minute, and because there was a person down there I needed to see.

Maren looked up when I stopped at the edge of the gravel. She didn't rise. The vigil had rules, and rising to greet the expedition's surveyor was apparently not among them, and I respected her for it.

"Thirty days," she said. She knew the count to the day. Of course she did; she was a schoolteacher, she kept calendars the way rivers keep rain. "The permits expire at midnight. We know."

"Dallet came to camp," I said, keeping my voice low. "He knows you're here. He used the word *safe* in a sentence about you, which from Dallet is a threat with the grammar inverted."

"We've been threatened by better and worse," Maren said. "Sixty years ago they took our name off the maps. Do you know how that's done? Not with soldiers. With a pen. A clerk, a decree, an atlas reprinted. My grandmother watched them strike the village out of the school atlas with a ruler and ink." She touched the blank banner beside her. "We can sit on gravel for thirty days. We've sat for sixty years."

I crouched down to her level, and the lantern light moved across her face, and I said the thing I'd been carrying for three days like a stone in my boot.

"There may be more down there than inscriptions. I can't say more. But if it's what I think it is — if it's what the corridor geometry says it should be —" I stopped. Even this was too much, out here, under open sky, with Dallet's ears presumably everywhere. "Don't stop the vigil. Whatever happens. Be patient a little longer. And be ready to move fast when I tell you."

Maren studied me for a long moment. She had the gaze of someone who had spent a career watching children decide whether to lie about homework, and I felt every year of it.

"You're going to do something," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I don't know what yet."

"That's all right." She picked up her ledger. "Neither did the river, the day it decided to flood."

* * *

I found Sef at the survey tent an hour after full dark, and I knew before I got within ten meters that something had broken in him. He sat at his drafting table with a map half-inked and abandoned in front of him, and he was holding his pen the way you hold a thing you're afraid to put down.

"Sef."

"Orsen." He didn't look up. "You should sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Suit yourself." He exhaled. In the lamplight I could see how much weight he'd lost since the dig began — the cartographer who'd arrived that first week round-faced and fussy, defending his inks like relics, was gone, and a thinner, grayer man sat in his chair. "A letter came. Today. From the ministry." He turned a paper toward me. It was brief. Bureaucratic. I read it twice and felt the floor tilt under the second reading.

*Re: S. Ferrowan. Records review. Re: dependents, M. Ferrowan, T. Ferrowan, residency status, District 9. Please present for interview regarding accuracy of professional submissions, Rivergate concession, at your earliest convenience.*

"They're going after his family," I said. "His kids' *residency status*."

"They've been going after them since the day I signed the false maps. This is just the first time they put it in writing." Sef laughed, a terrible dry sound. "You know what the worst part is? I did it for the expedition. Ilse said — she looked at me with those eyes, and she said, *Sef, if the true coordinates get out, Dallet revokes the permits and the site is lost, and you'll have ended it, do you understand, you personally* — and I believed her. I still believe her. That's the trap. She's not lying. She's just right in a way that makes everything else wrong." He finally looked up at me. "Dallet interviewed me last week. Did you know that?"

"I guessed."

"He has copies, Orsen. He has copies of my false maps, and he showed them to me, and he said — very kindly, that's the awful thing, he was *kind* — he said, *Mr. Ferrowan, your family's situation is fluid, and fluid situations respond to cooperation.*" Sef's hands were flat on the table now, pressing down as if the table might float away. "And I sat there and I thought: I can lose the dig, or I can lose them. The dig is a city that's been dead for sixty years. Them is a nine-year-old who thinks her father draws treasure maps for a living."

"Sef—"

"I didn't give him anything," he said quickly, too quickly. "Not yet. I told him I'd need time. He gave me time. He's very generous with time — he knows we're all counting it." He pushed the abandoned map away from him. "But I'm telling you because you're the only one in this camp who might understand, and because if I break — if I go to him and hand over the originals and sign whatever he puts in front of me — I wanted one person in this world to know that I hated it. That I know what it costs. That it's not cowardice, or — no. It is cowardice. I've decided it's allowed to be. I have children, Orsen. Courage is a luxury item and I checked the price."

The lamp hissed. Outside, faint on the wind, I could hear Maren's voice reading to the vigil, the cadence of it, names or prayers or both.

"Keep the copies," I said at last. "Give him whatever you have to give him. Your family comes first — I mean it, and I'll swear it to anyone who asks. But Sef. Before you hand him anything, come to me. One hour. That's all I ask. One hour before you sign."

"Why?" His voice cracked. "What difference can one hour make?"

"Because in one hour I can tell you if there's something worth more than both of our skins down there. Because I think there is. And because if you feed Dallet the false maps and he moves on them, and it turns out there was an archive under the eastern talus the whole time — census rolls, land grants, *names*, Sef, the names of everyone Maren's ever prayed for — then you won't just have lost the dig. You'll have buried a nation twice, and your daughter will grow up in a country that did it, and you'll know."

He stared at me. The pen trembled in his hand, and then stopped, and then trembled again, and I understood that I had just done to him exactly what Ilse had done, and exactly what Dallet had done — hung the weight of history on a frightened man and asked him to hold it. I hated myself for it. I did it anyway.

"One hour," he whispered.

"One hour."

He nodded, once, and turned back to his drafting table, and I left him there with his lamp and his shaking hands and his half-inked map of a country that officially did not exist.

* * *

I walked the camp's edge after that, past the lantern line, past the sleeping vigil, up onto the low rise where the theodolite stood and the whole valley lay open under the stars. The Rivergate ran black and silver below. Somewhere under the eastern talus, thirty meters down, maybe less, the thing I'd seen in the gradiometry waited — a void in the earth with the signature of walls, of rooms, of a structure far too regular and far too large to be anything but what Ilse had spent six weeks pretending it wasn't.

Thirty days. Dallet had his clock, Ilse had hers, the ministry had its lawyers, and Maren's people had sixty years of patience banked like grain. And Sef had one hour — an hour I'd promised him out of nothing but my own conviction that the ground under my feet was telling the truth, and that the truth was about to need every friend it had.

I stood on the rise until my hands stopped shaking, and then I went to the equipment locker, and I took out the night-survey kit, and I checked the charge on every lamp.

Because tomorrow, while the camp slept and the vigil kept its lanterns and Dallet counted his days, I was going to run the eastern talus one more time — full grid, full depth, no false logs, no redirected lines. Whatever was down there, I was going to put its true coordinates in my own hand.

And then I was going to decide what a truth was worth, and who got to pay for it.
