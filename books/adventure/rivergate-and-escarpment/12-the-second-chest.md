## 12. The Second Chest

The talus gave up its secret on the ninth day of digging past the corridor, and it did so with a sound Orsen would carry for the rest of her life: a low, hollow exhalation, like a held breath finally released, when Bren's pick broke through a plaster seal older than any nation still standing on a map.

"Air pocket," Bren said, pulling back. Dust curled out of the gap in a thin gray ribbon and hung in the lamplight, unmoving, as if the air itself had been waiting.

Orsen pressed her palm flat against the broken seal. Behind it, she could feel it — not with her hands, exactly, but with some older sense that five years of reading rivers had taught her — the particular emptiness of a man-made void. Not a cave. Not a collapse. A room.

"Everybody back," she said. "Slowly."

The corridor behind them was Karvathi masonry, fitted stone without mortar, and they had been following it for nine days at Ilse's direction — north by northwest, away from the strongest readings on Orsen's own gradiometer maps, into a section of the escarpment that Ilse's published theory said should be solid rock. It wasn't. It never had been. And every meter they advanced, Orsen felt the crack in her trust widen another degree, because her mother was not lost down here. Her mother knew exactly where she was going. She simply didn't want anyone to know where that was.

Now the corridor ended at a wall that had been sealed deliberately, from the inside, with lime plaster pressed over a wooden lintel — and someone, sixty years ago or six hundred, had wanted this room forgotten so thoroughly that they had entombed it behind their own backs.

Orsen thought of Maren standing at the camp's edge with her schoolteacher's patience and her people's question: *did you find proof we were here?*

She thought: *we're about to answer you.*

* * *

It took two days to open the chamber properly. Ilse insisted on protocol — scaffolding, ventilation, a documented entry sequence — and for those two days she was every inch the celebrated archaeologist, precise and commanding, and Orsen almost let herself believe the last three weeks had been a misunderstanding. Almost.

On the second evening, Ilse stood at the breach with Orsen beside her, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The lamp beams crossed the threshold and fell into darkness that swallowed them whole.

"You first," Ilse said quietly. "You found the way in. That's yours."

It was the closest thing to tenderness her mother had offered in five years, and Orsen hated how much it landed.

She went through.

The chamber was not large — perhaps eight meters by twelve — but it had been built to hold something more valuable than treasure, and the first thing Orsen understood, standing in the center of it with dust sifting down through the lamplight, was that she was standing inside a country.

Shelves lined three walls, cut directly into the living stone, and on them sat chest after chest after chest — lacquered wood, banded in that same stamped metal as the token in her pocket, each one sealed with wax and cord. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Along the fourth wall, rolled documents lay in ceramic tubes stacked like cordwood, and above the shelves, painted directly onto the rock in pigment that had not faded because no light had ever touched it, ran lines of Karvathi script — census tallies, if Sef's partial lexicon was right, district by district, household by household.

"Bren," Orsen called, and her voice came out strange. "Get Dr. Vasquez. Tell her—" She stopped. Told her what? *Tell her we found everything.* "Tell her to come see."

Ilse was already coming through the breach.

Orsen watched her mother's face in the lamplight, and what she saw there was not wonder. Or rather — it was wonder, raw and enormous, crossing her features like weather — and then it was gone, shuttered, replaced by something colder and more calculating before the expression finished forming. Orsen had spent her childhood learning to read that face. She knew the difference between her mother's joy and her mother's arithmetic.

Ilse walked the chamber slowly. She touched nothing. At the third shelf she stopped, lifted a lantern, and read the wax seals in silence.

"These are the land grant registries," she said at last. Her voice was level. Too level. "District rolls. If the lexicon holds, these chests contain the complete cadastral record of Karvath at the time of the erasure."

"Land grants," Orsen repeated. "You mean—"

"I mean proof of ownership." Ilse turned to face her. "Parcels. Boundaries. Names of holders, names of heirs. Sixty years ago the ministry burned what it could reach and rewrote what it couldn't. This is what they couldn't reach." She looked around the chamber the way a gambler looks at a table. "This is an entire nation's deed to itself."

The words went through Orsen like current through water. Somewhere upriver, in villages that appeared on no official map under their true name, a schoolteacher was teaching children a language the ministry called extinct. Down here, in the dark, the answer to every question those children would ever ask had been waiting in lacquered boxes.

"We log it," Orsen said. "All of it. Tonight. Every chest, every tube, photographed in place before anything moves."

"Yes," Ilse said. "Of course. Protocol."

And because Orsen wanted so badly to believe her, she almost missed it — the slight delay before *of course*, the half-second in which her mother's eyes moved not to the chests but to the breach, measuring it, the way she'd measured the gradiometer readings three weeks ago before redirecting the dig.

* * *

They worked through the night. Orsen ran the camera; Sef logged positions; Bren and Tomas carried nothing, touched nothing, only held lights steady while Orsen documented chest after chest, seal after seal. The work was holy and they all felt it. Even Sef, who had been nervous since the day he arrived, had gone quiet in the way of a man standing in a cathedral.

Around four in the morning, Orsen opened the first chest — Ilse authorized it herself, gloves and spatula and full documentation — and the smell of cedar and old ink rose out of it like a voice.

Rolls. Hundreds of rolls. Orsen unrolled the first with hands that were not quite steady.

*Registry of Holdings, District of Selvane. Holder: Adran Kolvek, ferryman. Parcel: riverfront, 40 rods. Heirs: wife Lissa; daughters Imre and Danna.*

A ferryman. Forty rods of riverfront. Two daughters with names.

Orsen sat back on her heels. Sixty years of erasure, and here was a man who poled a ferry across the Rivergate, written down in his own country's hand, with his wife's name and his daughters' names, preserved in cedar and darkness by people who had known what was coming and had buried their nation's memory to keep it alive.

"Maren needs to see this," she said. "Her village is in one of these districts. I'd bet my license on it."

Silence.

Orsen looked up. Ilse was standing at the breach, and the look on her face was the one Orsen had learned to dread across twenty years of small betrayals: the look of a decision already made.

"No," Ilse said.

The word dropped into the chamber like a stone into a well.

"The chamber is sealed effective immediately," Ilse went on, in her lecture-hall voice, the one that permitted no questions. "Nothing leaves this room. No photographs leave this room. The documentation stays in the site safe under my key. As far as the official log is concerned, the corridor terminated in a collapse at station forty-one. Unstable strata. We reroute the dig east."

"You can't be serious." Orsen was on her feet. "Mother. This is *it*. This is everything Maren asked for. This is the claim — the whole claim, parcel by parcel—"

"And the moment it exists on paper, it belongs to the ministry!" Ilse's voice cracked against the stone. "Dallet has been filing reports since the day he arrived. The moment a logged photograph of a land registry reaches the capital, this concession is revoked within the week, the site is seized, the archive disappears into a ministry vault, and every person connected to this dig faces charges for unauthorized excavation. Including you. Including *Sef*, who has a family." She drew a breath, and when she spoke again her voice was lower, and worse, because it was reasonable. "I am not burying the truth, Orsen. I am keeping it safe until it can survive daylight."

"It survived sixty years in the dark," Orsen said. "It doesn't need your protection. It needs to be *read*."

"By whom? To whom?" Ilse gestured at the shelves, and the lamplight made her shadow climb the wall like something with too many arms. "To a ministry that erased a nation once and will erase it again, cleaner this time? You think they'll lose the court case? They won't even need a court case. Paper goes into a vault, the permit lapses, the escarpment gets a highway bypass, and Karvath dies a second death — this time with our signatures on the certificate." She stepped closer. "I have watched this ministry destroy people, Orsen. I built my career in the wreckage of the last time. I will not hand them the knife again."

"There it is," Orsen said quietly. "The last time."

Something flickered across her mother's face — the scandal, the thing they did not say, the suppressed dig twenty years ago that Ilse had never explained and Orsen had never forgiven — and then Ilse shut it away, and turned, and gave the order.

"Tomas. Bring the scaffold planks. Bren, mix plaster. We seal the breach tonight, before first light. Nobody enters without my authorization."

* * *

They worked in silence, and the silence was the worst part.

Orsen stood at the top of the shaft and watched men she respected carry planks and buckets of plaster down into a room full of names, and she understood with terrible clarity what she was witnessing: not the discovery of an archive, but its funeral. Again. The same ceremony, performed sixty years apart, and this time the officiant was her own mother, reciting safety like a liturgy.

Sef came to stand beside her at the rim. His logging book was closed in his hands, held tight enough to whiten his knuckles.

"The log entry," he said softly. "Dr. Vasquez wants 'collapse at station forty-one.' My field notes say otherwise. My *maps* say otherwise."

"I know."

"If I write what she's asking me to write, that's falsification. If I write what's true, she'll know I'm the one who—" He stopped. Swallowed. "My brother works the ministry docks, Orsen. Dallet knows his name."

Orsen looked at him — this anxious, decent man, caught exactly where she herself was caught, between a woman who protected people by lying to them and a ministry that destroyed people by telling the truth weaponized — and she put her hand over the closed log book.

"Write nothing tonight," she said. "Buy yourself a day. One day. I need one day to think."

Below them, the last plank went up against the breach. Bren began mixing plaster. In a few hours the chamber of names would be stone-faced again, unlogged, unnumbered, unknown — existing now in exactly two places: beneath the talus, and in the memory of the five people who had stood inside it.

Sixty years ago, someone had sealed this room to save it.

Orsen watched the plaster go up and understood, with a certainty that settled over her like cold water, that she could not let it stay sealed. Not for ninety days. Not for nine. Whatever her mother believed she was protecting, the cost was being paid by people upriver who had never been consulted — by a schoolteacher and her students, by a dead ferryman named Adran Kolvek and his daughters Imre and Danna, whose entire existence now lived in one memory and one memory alone.

Hers.

She climbed out of the shaft into the gray hour before dawn. Below the escarpment, far off toward the river villages, a light was burning in Maren's camp — the vigil, night after night, patient as water.

At the mess tent, Dallet was waiting with two cups of coffee and his easy smile, and Orsen realized, as she took the cup her traitor hands needed, that the observer had heard the generators running all night, and that he was watching her now with the pleasant, bottomless attention of a man doing arithmetic of his own.

"Busy night," Dallet said. "Find anything interesting down there?"

Orsen looked at him over the rim of the cup, and made her choice about what kind of liar she was going to be.

"Not yet," she said. "But we're close."
