## 13. Sef Breaks

Sef had learned to read a man's intentions by where he put his hands. Dallet kept his in his pockets the whole walk down from the ministry tent, which meant he wanted nothing — or wanted everything and was patient enough to wait for it. The evening light came long and amber off the escarpment, and the two of them walked the survey line together like colleagues, like friends, like men with nowhere else to be.

"You draw well," Dallet said. "I mean that. I've seen a lot of field cartography. Most of it's chicken scratches and prayer. Yours is art."

"Thank you." Sef kept his eyes on the talus slope, on the dark seam where the corridor mouth had been shored up with timber. He did not look at his own tent, twenty meters off, where the false maps lay rolled in their tube beneath his cot, and the true ones — the ones Orsen didn't know existed — lay flat between the boards of his drafting table.

"I'd like to commission something," Dallet said. "Off the books. A clean copy of your working surveys. Everything through yesterday."

The river said what it always said, which was nothing. Sef made himself breathe.

"The working surveys are expedition property," he said. "Dr. Vasquez logs them."

Dallet smiled. It was the smile Sef hated most, the one that arrived without any warmth behind it, like lamplight through a window of an empty house. "Of course they are. And Dr. Vasquez will be delighted to hear you refused to cooperate with a routine ministry audit. She'll say so at the hearing. In writing, probably. She's very loyal that way."

"There's no hearing."

"Not yet." Dallet stopped walking. His hands came out of his pockets at last, and one of them held a letter — heavy paper, official crease, the kind of envelope that changed the temperature of a room. "There will be, though. Falsification of government survey records is not a technicality, Sef. It's a crime with a name and a sentence attached. And it isn't yours alone. You drew them. Your hand is on every sheet. When they ask who prepared the documents, there's exactly one signature."

"My family—"

"Is in Corren Hollow," Dallet said gently. "Your mother teaches piano. Your brother drives a gravel truck for the district works department, and the district works department answers to the ministry, and the ministry has a very long memory and a very short list of people it forgives." He held out the letter. "I'm not threatening anyone. I'm offering you a way to be helpful. Helpful people have very boring lives. Boring is underrated."

Sef took the letter because his hand decided before he did. Inside was a summons, pre-dated, pre-addressed, waiting only for a date to be filled in. His own name sat in the subject line in someone's tidy bureaucratic script: *Vasquez Expedition — Cartographic Irregularities — Witness Statement Requested.*

He thought about his mother's hands on the piano keys. He thought about gravel dust and his brother's laugh. He thought about Orsen Vasquez standing in the archive chamber three nights ago with her lamp raised, her face lit gold by sixty years of sleeping paper, saying *this changes everything* in a voice that believed it.

"I need time," he said.

"You have until tomorrow's supper." Dallet tucked his hands back into his pockets and resumed walking, unhurried, a man strolling after dinner. "You know what I've always admired about maps? They can't lie forever. The ground keeps checking them."

* * *

That night Sef sat at his drafting table with the lamp turned low and both sets of maps unrolled, and he understood, finally, the shape of the trap he'd been living inside since the day he'd first noticed the coordinates drifting.

It had started small, the way rot starts small. A benchmark moved forty meters. A sounding logged from a station that hadn't been occupied. He'd told himself it was error, then fatigue, then — when the pattern became undeniable — that it wasn't his place. Ilse Vasquez was a legend. Ilse Vasquez had reasons. And he was a nervous man from a river town whose entire career fit inside one canvas tent, and legends do not fall alone; they take everyone standing under them.

So he'd drawn what she asked him to draw, and he'd kept a private set of true maps the way a man keeps a knife under a floorboard — not to use, but to prove to himself, in the small hours, that he still knew the difference between the thing and its picture.

Now the ministry wanted the pictures, and the ground was starting to check them.

He worked until the candle guttered. He copied the false surveys faithfully — every shifted benchmark, every invented sounding, every elegant lie rendered in his best hand, because if he was going to hang, he would hang with clean linework. And then he sat for a long time with the true map of the lower gallery spread before him, the one showing the second chamber, the archive, the room full of names.

Dallet had asked for everything through yesterday. But Dallet didn't know the archive chamber existed. Ilse had ordered it sealed and unlogged; only five people alive knew where it was, and Sef was one of them.

He could give Dallet the lies and let the lies lead the ministry away from the truth. It would be betrayal wearing the mask of betrayal — handing over falsified records as though they were real, letting Dallet build his case on Ilse's own fabrications. It might buy the dig time. It might buy Maren's villagers time. It might buy nothing at all and cost him everything, because Dallet was not a stupid man, and stupid men did not get posted to expeditions like this one.

Or he could give Dallet everything, protect his family, and watch a dead nation get erased a second time — this time with his own pen.

Sef pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw constellations. Then he rolled the copies, sealed the tube, and left the true map of the archive chamber folded flat beneath the floorboard where knives belonged.

* * *

Orsen found out the way she found out most things: by being awake when she shouldn't have been.

She came to his tent near midnight with a question about tomorrow's grid extension and found the drafting table littered with tracing paper, the ink still tacky on sheets she recognized instantly — because she had spent five years reading rivers, and a woman who reads water for a living learns to read lines too. She knew those contours. She'd shot half of them herself, from stations that were not where these maps claimed they were.

"Sef." Her voice was quiet in the way that frightened him more than shouting. "What is this?"

He could have lied. He was, by now, practiced at it. Instead he heard himself say, "Copies," and watched her face change.

"Copies of what?"

"Of the surveys. The ones Dr. Vasquez has been logging." He stood, because sitting felt like hiding. "Dallet wants them. He has a summons with my name on it and my mother's address already written down, and he smiled the whole time, and I—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "I gave them to him. An hour ago. They're in his tent right now."

The silence that followed had weight, like deep water. Orsen set down the lamp. Very carefully, the way she did everything when she was angriest, she picked up one of the tracing sheets and held it to the light.

"These are the false ones," she said.

"Yes."

"You gave him the false ones. Not the archive map."

"He doesn't know about the archive." Sef's throat was dry. "Nobody outside this camp knows about the archive. If I'd given him the true set, I'd have handed him the one thing the descendants can't afford to lose. So I gave him the lie. I let him have the lie."

Orsen lowered the sheet. In the lamplight her face was unreadable, and Sef realized that unreadable was worse than furious. Furious he could answer. This was a woman doing arithmetic.

"You've known for weeks," she said. "You came to me with the mismatched coordinates. You brought me into it. And the whole time you were keeping a second set of books yourself."

"Yes."

"And tonight, cornered, you chose alone. Again. Without telling me, without telling Maren, without—" She stopped. Breathed. "Do you understand what happens when Dallet compares those maps against the ministry's own regional surveys? He'll find the discrepancies. That's the point of them. He'll file to revoke the concession for falsified records, and he'll be *right*, Sef. He'll be holding proof we cooked the books, and every legitimate claim we make afterward will smell like cover-up. You just armed him."

"I armed him with her weapon," Sef said, and heard how thin it sounded even as he said it. "Not mine."

"It doesn't matter whose weapon it is once it fires."

He sat down heavily on his stool. The tent felt smaller than it had an hour ago, the walls closer, the lamp smaller. "My brother drives a gravel truck," he said, to no one, to the floor. "My mother plays piano for weddings. That's the whole empire of us. Dallet read out their street like a menu."

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the night shift called softly to each other along the shoring line, ordinary voices, ordinary work, a world that didn't yet know what had changed in this tent.

"If I expose you," she said at last, "to Ilse, to the crew — you're finished. Maybe prosecuted alongside her. The team fractures, and a fractured team doesn't finish a ninety-day dig in thirty days." She picked up the lamp. "If I don't, I'm complicit in whatever comes next, and so are you, and so is everyone who trusts us."

"What are you going to do?"

"That depends on whether you're done choosing alone." She looked at him then, and there was no softness in it, but there was something steadier than softness. "Dallet thinks he bought your loyalty tonight. Good. Let him keep thinking it. From this minute, everything you give him, you show me first. Every conversation, every request, every smile. You're not his informant anymore. You're ours. Do you understand the difference?"

"No," Sef admitted.

"Neither do I, entirely. But it's the difference between drowning and swimming, and we're past the point where I can tell them apart either." She moved toward the tent flap, then paused. "The archive stays buried in paper, Sef. Whatever it costs. Whatever he offers. There are ten thousand names in that room, and they've already been erased once. Not twice. Not while I'm breathing."

She left. Sef sat alone with his lamp and his guilt and the faint, stubborn relief of a man who has confessed to something and lived — and underneath the relief, cold and rising like water in a shaft, the knowledge of what he'd set in motion. Somewhere across the camp, in a tidy ministry tent, Dallet was unrolling his copies by lantern light, running his finger along contours that did not match the ground, smiling his empty-house smile, and beginning — patiently, courteously — to write.

* * *

Sef did not sleep. Near dawn he heard footsteps stop outside his tent, pause, and move on — unhurried, courteous, a man taking his morning walk past a witness he already owned. Through the canvas seam he watched Dallet continue down toward the riverbank, where the mist was lifting off the water, and where, at the camp's eastern edge, the first shapes of Maren's vigil were settling into their places for another day: old men on folding stools, women with children wrapped against the chill, a schoolteacher standing at the front with her arms crossed, watching the escarpment as if it owed her an answer.

Dallet stopped at the edge of the vigil. He looked at them for a long moment, hands in his pockets. Then he took out a small notebook, wrote something in it, and turned back toward the ministry tent — moving, Sef noticed, a little faster than courtesy required.

By full light, a rider was saddling at the ministry lines, dispatch case strapped behind the saddle, headed downriver at a hard trot.

Toward the oversight board. Toward the permits. Toward everything.

Sef stood in the cold outside his tent with ink on his fingers that wouldn't wash off, and understood that whatever he had tried to buy last night, the bill was already in the mail.
