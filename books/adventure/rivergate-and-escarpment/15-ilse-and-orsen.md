## 15. Ilse and Orsen

*Orsen*

Her mother sent for her at dusk, the way she'd once summoned survey crews — a folded note carried by the foreman, Bren, who wouldn't meet Orsen's eyes when he handed it over. *Come to my tent. Alone. After the vigil lamps are lit.* No signature. No please. As if twenty years of silence could be crossed in nine words.

Orsen almost didn't go. She had spent the whole day avoiding both of them — Dallet's dinner invitation still sitting unanswered on her cot like a coiled thing, Maren's people singing low at the camp's edge, their lanterns strung along the talus slope like a constellation someone had dropped. She had a decision to make about Dallet, and she knew, in the marrow-deep way she knew riverbeds, that whatever happened in her mother's tent tonight would decide it for her.

She went anyway.

Ilse Vasquez's tent was the largest in camp and the most austere: a folding table buried under maps, a cot made with military corners, a single lamp turned low. Her mother sat behind the table with her reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair, and for one unguarded second she looked not like Dr. Ilse Vasquez of the Royal Institute, whose name was printed in three textbooks Orsen had been assigned in school, but like a tired woman of sixty-one who hadn't slept properly since the flood.

"You got my note," Ilse said.

"You knew I would."

"I know you better than you think." She took off the glasses. Folded them. Set them down with the deliberate care of someone buying time. "Sit down, Orsen."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, not quite grief. "You've been standing since you were nineteen."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the vigil singers moved into a slower verse, and the sound came through the canvas like water through limestone.

Orsen didn't sit. But she didn't leave either, and they both understood the negotiation in that.

"The archive chamber," Ilse said. "You were there when we opened it."

"I was there when you ordered it sealed."

"Yes." Her mother looked at her hands — hands Orsen knew from a hundred childhood memories, trowel-calloused, ink-stained, always moving, always pointing at something worth looking at. They were very still now. "I want to tell you why. Not the version I gave the crew. Not the version I've been telling myself for twenty years. The actual one. And then—" her voice caught, recovered, "—then you can do whatever you came here to do. Report me. Ruin me. Walk out. I only ask that you hear it standing, if standing is what you need."

Orsen said nothing. The silence was permission enough.

* * *

*Ilse*

Twenty years ago there had been another dig. Ilse had never spoken its name aloud in two decades, and now that she began, the name came out of her like a splinter working loose from old wood.

"Kellin Hollow," she said. "That's what the valley was called then. Before Karvath was struck from the registries, before the border war, before your grandfather's generation decided a nation could be erased by renaming its rivers. There was a temple complex under the east bluff. Smaller than this one. But it had the census annex — the rolls. Names. Land grants going back four hundred years."

Orsen had gone very still. Ilse recognized the stillness; it was her own, mirrored back at her, the stillness of a person bracing against a current.

"I was thirty-nine. Junior faculty, one monograph, a marriage already failing, a daughter who thought I hung the moon." She let that land. Let her daughter flinch. "The ministry funded the dig. And when we opened the annex — when I read those names, Orsen, thousands of them, families, villages that still existed downriver, people who had been legally told their grandparents never lived — I did what I was trained to do. I documented everything. Catalogued it. Wrote the report."

"And?"

"And my director read it, went white, and burned the draft in front of me. Not metaphorically. In an ashtray, in his office, while I watched." Ilse's laugh was dry as old paper. "He explained the arithmetic. The ministry that funded us was the same ministry that had signed the erasure. Publication meant prosecution — not of the ministry, of *us*. Site seizure. Concession revocation. Criminal charges for 'unauthorized recovery of state records.' Two of my crew had families. One had a sick daughter. He asked me — he actually asked me — whether names on paper were worth more than living children fed and housed."

"So you buried it."

"So I buried it." She said it plainly. No absolution sought. "I logged false coordinates. I published a monograph on the pottery instead — it won a prize, did you know that? The Kellin Hollow ceramics study. It's what built my career. Every grant, every chair, every invitation — all of it grew out of the year I helped erase a nation a second time."

Outside, the singing stopped. In the new quiet, Ilse could hear the Rivergate itself, far off, moving through its channel with the patience of something that intended to outlast everyone present.

"What does this have to do with me?" Orsen asked, and her voice was flat in a way Ilse knew too well — flat the way water goes flat just before it goes over the drop.

* * *

*Orsen*

Because, her mother said, the scandal wasn't the ministry's. It was theirs.

"You left home at nineteen," Ilse said. "You told everyone — yourself included — that it was because I was cold, because I chose work over you, because I missed your graduation for a conference. Some of that was even true. But that's not why you ran, is it?"

"Don't."

"A month before you left, you found the Kellin Hollow materials. In my office. You were looking for—"

"I was looking for wrapping paper," Orsen said, and heard how young she sounded, how nineteen. "It was your birthday. I wanted to wrap your present without you seeing."

"—and you found a box of photographs instead. Temple walls. Census pages. And a letter from my director, on ministry letterhead, instructing me to destroy or return all recovered documents." Ilse met her eyes across the lamp. "You confronted me. I lied to you. I told you it was nothing, that you'd misunderstood, that some things were above your head. And then I locked the office, and three weeks later you were gone, and you have spent five years taking river contracts that keep you moving so fast no one can ask you where you're from."

The tent swayed slightly in the wind off the escarpment. Orsen realized her fists were clenched and forced them open, finger by finger.

"You could have told me the truth then."

"Yes."

"One sentence. *I found something terrible and powerful people made me bury it.* One sentence, and I'd have—"

"What? Stayed?" Ilse rose from behind the table, slowly, joints stiff from decades of crouching in trenches. "Orsen, I was trying to protect you. That's what I told myself, anyway, and I've had twenty years to learn how much of protection was really cowardice wearing its coat. If you'd known, you'd have been a witness. Witnesses get subpoenaed. Children of disgraced archaeologists get followed by ministry men the way you've been followed since the day that token surfaced. I lied to you because lying was the only tool I had left, and I kept using it until it was the only language we spoke to each other."

"That's not an excuse."

"No," Ilse agreed quietly. "It isn't. It's an explanation. Those are different things, and I'm too old to confuse them anymore." She came around the table and stood close enough that Orsen could see the fine lines around her eyes, the tremor in her jaw she was fighting the way she fought everything — silently, structurally, like a wall holding back a river. "Silence became my religion, do you understand? Every year it cost more and confessed less. I told myself I was keeping the site safe for a better political season. Then I told myself I was protecting my crew. Then I was protecting you. And underneath all of it, the truth was simpler and uglier: I was afraid. I built a life on top of a buried thing, and after enough years, digging it up would have destroyed everything I am. So I kept digging *down*, instead. New sites. New layers. Always deeper, never back."

Orsen thought of the archive chamber — the smell of cedar and stone dust, the census rolls in their oilcloth, tens of thousands of names waiting in the dark like seeds. She thought of Maren's face when she'd asked her one question: *did you find proof we were here?*

"And now?" Orsen asked. "Now that it's open again. Now that they're singing outside your tent. What are you protecting?"

Her mother was silent for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had dropped into something raw.

"Myself. Still. Even now. Because if I publish — if I stand up and say *I found them, I found them twice, and I hid them twice* — then everything I am dies. The career, the name, the textbooks. And I don't know who I'd be afterward. A woman who erased a nation twice and is sorry about it. Is that enough? Is that a person?" She laughed, and it broke in the middle. "You want to know the worst part, Orsen? The truly unforgivable part? When I saw you pocket that token on the riverbank — yes, I knew, I've known since the first week — I felt *proud*. My daughter, the thief of small honest things. Because it meant you were willing to take what the world owed you even if the world said it wasn't yours. I never learned how to do that. I only learned how to give things back."

* * *

*Orsen*

They stood there in the lamplight, two women shaped by the same buried room, and Orsen felt the strange vertigo of understanding someone completely and forgiving them not at all.

"I used to measure everything in miles," Orsen said. "Contracts. Rivers. Distance from you. Five years, I told myself. Five years and I'd be somewhere you'd never been, doing something you couldn't claim credit for." She shook her head. "And then the flood opens a chest with a dead nation's seal on it, and where do I end up? In your trench. Under your escarpment. Holding your lie in my hands."

"Fate has a sense of humor."

"Fate has a *grudge*." But something in her chest had come loose — not forgiveness, nothing so clean, but the recognition that the wall between them had been load-bearing, and that her mother had built it too, brick by brick, out of fear that had started as love and calcified into something else. "Mama." The word escaped before she could stop it; she hadn't used it in fifteen years. She watched it hit Ilse like a physical blow. "If you seal that archive — if you log false coordinates one more time — I will not lie for you. I need you to hear that. I will not become you."

Ilse closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet and furious and, beneath it all, terribly clear.

"I know," she said. "I've known since the night you stood between Dallet's men and that schoolteacher. You photograph well, by the way. The camp hand's picture is already making rounds in the valley." She straightened, and the doctor, the director, the institution reassembled itself around her like armor closing. "Go to bed, Orsen. We both have decisions to make, and ten days to make them in."

"Nine," Orsen said at the tent flap. "Dallet filed the revocation petition this afternoon. Sef's maps. You should know your own observer beat you to it."

For the first time in Orsen's memory, her mother looked genuinely afraid.

"*What did you say?*"

"Dallet has the falsified surveys. He filed to kill the concession early — before the permits lapse on their own terms. If he wins, the site gets sealed under ministry lock, and the archive goes back in the dark for another sixty years." Orsen held her mother's gaze. "So whatever you've decided to protect, Mama — decide fast. The river doesn't wait, and neither does he."

She stepped out into the night. Behind her, through the canvas, she heard the crash of a folding table, and the vigil lamps along the talus burning on and on, patient as names.
