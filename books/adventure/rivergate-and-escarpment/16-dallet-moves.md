## 16. Dallet Moves

The summons came at breakfast, folded into a ministry courier's gloved hand like it was something perishable.

Orsen knew before she broke the seal. She'd learned to read weather in paper — the weight of official stock, the angle of the fold, the way Dallet's office used blue thread instead of string because blue thread meant *proceedings had commenced*. She'd seen it once before, years ago, delivered to a dredging contractor whose survey had inconveniently proved a landowner's boundary wrong. The contractor had lost his license by autumn.

She read it twice anyway, standing in the mess tent with her coffee going cold in her other hand.

*Notice of Intention to Revoke Concession Permit 77-K, pursuant to Article 14 of the Antiquities Act, on grounds of falsified survey documentation submitted to the Ministry of Cultural Heritage.*

Ten days early. The permits didn't lapse for ten more days, and Dallet wasn't willing to wait for them to die of old age. He was going to kill them himself, and he was going to do it with Sef's maps.

* * *

She found him at the survey tent's entrance, holding the flap open for her like a gentleman, which was somehow worse than if he'd posted guards.

"Ms. Vasquez." Dallet's smile was the same as ever — warm, unhurried, the smile of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice. "Thank you for coming so promptly."

"You could have just told me at breakfast."

"I could have." He gestured her inside. The tent had been rearranged since yesterday; someone had set up a folding table with a lamp, and on the table lay an array of documents squared to its edges with a precision that made her think of autopsy photographs. "But I wanted you to see this properly. You're a professional. You'll want to review the evidence."

The evidence was Sef's maps. Or rather — copies of Sef's maps, the ones Sef had surrendered three nights ago under whatever pressure Dallet had applied, the ones that matched Ilse's false field logs coordinate for coordinate. Orsen had known they were gone. Knowing it and seeing them laid out under a government lamp were different animals entirely.

"Cartographer Sefren Oduya," Dallet said, tapping the corner stamp, "certified these surveys himself. His seal, his initials, dates matching your mother's submission schedule. And here—" he turned one sheet over, revealing the margin where Sef had logged instrument readings, "—the raw transit data, which does not match the coordinates reported. Which means either your cartographer is incompetent, which I don't believe, or he knowingly filed false documentation, which is a criminal matter, or he was instructed to." He looked up. "You can see how the ministry would find the third option most interesting."

"Interesting," Orsen repeated. "That's what we're calling it."

"That's what the law calls it. Article 14 is quite specific. Falsified records void the concession immediately upon determination — no hearing required until after revocation, and by then the site will have been sealed pending investigation." He said it gently, the way a doctor delivers a prognosis. "Sealed sites are not reopened quickly, Ms. Vasquez. Sometimes not for decades. Sometimes never. There's a chamber down there full of paper that turns to dust if the drainage fails, and sealed sites do not have drainage budgets."

Her mouth had gone dry. "You've read the site reports."

"I read everything." Dallet straightened the maps, aligning their edges, a small compulsive tenderness in it. "I told you weeks ago that I knew about the token you kept from the riverbank. I told you I knew about the false logs. I offered you a way out then. I'm offering a better one now, because my position has improved and yours has collapsed, and I'd rather be generous from strength than desperate from weakness. It's unbecoming."

"What do you want?"

"The same thing I've always wanted. Your testimony. A signed statement that Dr. Ilse Vasquez directed the falsification of survey records, dated before the revocation order issues tomorrow morning. With that, the ministry doesn't need Article 14 at all — we proceed against her personally, the concession transfers to a ministry-led excavation, and you walk away clean. Employed, even. The ministry always needs good surveyors."

"And the archive?"

Dallet paused, and for a moment — half a second, maybe less — something moved behind his pleasant face that wasn't pleasant at all. It was quick and cold and it looked almost like hunger.

"The archive," he said, "is a matter for the ministry to assess. Karvathi materials have significant political sensitivity. Sixty years of border treaties rest on the legal fiction that there was never anything down there worth naming. You understand that. You've understood it since the river gave up that chest." He gathered the maps into a folder, unhurried. "Some nations come back from the dead, Ms. Vasquez, and when they do, they bring their lawyers, their grievances, and their borders with them. The ministry prefers its fictions intact. I am simply a man who prefers to be employed."

"So this isn't about falsified records at all."

"This is about falsified records. That's the beauty of it." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which was the worst thing she'd seen all season. "The law doesn't care why the records were false. It only cares that they were. Your mother built her whole career on controlling what gets found and what stays buried. I'm merely using her own foundation. You should appreciate the craftsmanship."

* * *

Ilse took the news standing up, which Orsen should have expected. Her mother received catastrophe the way other people received mail — with a slight tilt of the head and an immediate assessment of what required answering.

"Article 14." Ilse set the notice down on her camp desk, next to the oil lamp and the small brass compass she'd carried for thirty years. "He's fast. I'll give him that. Ten days left on the permits and he moves to revoke with nine."

"He has Sef's maps. He has everything he needs."

"He has what Sef gave him." Ilse's eyes came up, sharp. "Which may not be everything Sef has. Did you look at the copies? Actually look?"

Orsen hesitated. "They matched the false logs."

"They matched the false logs *he wanted us to see*. Sef is frightened, not broken. There's a difference." Ilse pulled a chair out and sat, and only then did Orsen see the fatigue underneath — the gray in her mother's face that hadn't been there a month ago, the way her hands stayed flat on the desk as if the desk might move otherwise. "Sit down, Orsen."

"I'd rather stand."

"Suit yourself." Ilse slid the notice back across the desk. "Here's our position. If the revocation issues tomorrow, an inspection team arrives within the week. They seal the shafts, they inventory what's on the surface, and the archive below stays exactly where it is — undocumented, unprotected, and legally invisible. No claim can be filed on evidence the state refuses to acknowledge exists. Maren's people lose everything. Again. Sixty years becomes seventy."

"And your plan?"

"My plan hasn't changed. We publish nothing, we log nothing further, we contest the revocation on procedural grounds — Dallet's notice has a defect, the certification of service is incomplete, I've already flagged it — and we run out the clock. In ten days the permits expire naturally. An expired concession reverts to open status. Open status means any licensed party can petition to survey, including the descendants' advocates, including the university, including anyone with standing. Expired is survivable. Revoked is a tomb."

Orsen stared at her. "You've been planning for this."

"I've been planning for every version of this since the day I saw Dallet's appointment letter. He's not the first ministry man I've outlasted." Ilse's voice was level, but her thumb had begun moving against the edge of the desk, a small metronome of strain. "What I haven't planned for is you deciding, somewhere in these last ten days, that your conscience requires you to hand him the rest of Sef's material. Or your own testimony. Or the photographs I know someone has been taking down in that corridor, because I count the film canisters, Orsen, I have always counted."

The tent felt smaller. Outside, the vigil fires at the camp's edge burned in their patient line, as they had for two weeks now, the villagers taking shifts through the cold nights without complaint, without demand — just presence, just witness, the oldest technology there was.

"If you know about the photographs," Orsen said slowly, "then you know what's down there. The census rolls. The land grants. Every name."

"I know what's down there better than you do. I've known for twenty years." Ilse said it flatly, and then, as if hearing herself, closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them again she was the director again, the strategist, the woman who had turned silence into a discipline. "Ten days. Hold together for ten days. Don't testify, don't publish, don't give Dallet a single sheet of paper he doesn't already have. Can you do that?"

Orsen thought about the notice. About the word *revoked*, and what it meant — a tomb, her mother had said, and her mother was right, and that was the trap of it. Dallet had built his weapon out of true things. The records *were* falsified. The law *did* require revocation. Every fact in his filing was accurate, and the sum of all those accurate facts was the permanent burial of a nation's proof that it had ever existed.

Justice required perjury now, or silence, or both. The law itself had become the shovel.

"For ten days," Orsen said. "Then the clock runs out, and everything changes."

"Everything changed twenty years ago," Ilse said quietly. "We're just living in the long shadow of it. Go on. Check your instruments. And Orsen—" Orsen paused at the flap. "Sef. Watch him. Not for betrayal. For what he does when a frightened man decides he can't carry it anymore. That's the dangerous hour. Not the decision to break. The hour after."

* * *

She went looking for Sef at dusk and found him at the map table in the survey tent, alone, doing nothing. That was the tell. Sef was never doing nothing; Sef drew in his sleep, probably. He sat with his hands flat on the table the way Ilse sat with hers flat on her desk, two people bracing against the same invisible current.

"You gave him copies," Orsen said. No accusation in it. Just setting the fact on the table between them, like a stone.

Sef flinched. Then, visibly, decided not to lie. "Yes."

"Did you give him everything?"

"No." He said it fast, too fast, and then made himself slow down. "No. I kept the archive chamber off every copy. The corridor extensions, the shaft survey — those were already compromised, he'd have gotten them from the filed logs eventually. But the second chamber exists on none of the maps I handed over. As far as the ministry knows, the dig ends at the inscription corridor." He rubbed his eyes. "It's the only thing I had left to hold back. I have a sister in Vellmark. Dallet showed me her employment file. Her landlord's name. Her daughter's school. He never threatened anything. He just... showed me he knew where everyone I love sleeps at night."

"I know," Orsen said. "I know exactly how that works."

"I keep telling myself the maps I gave him were lies anyway. False coordinates. So what did I actually betray? False things?" He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Except they weren't false to him. He knows they're false. He's using our lies as evidence of our lying, which means the lies became true the moment he picked them up. Do you understand what I'm saying? I can't even work out what I betrayed anymore. The truth? The cover-up? Both?"

Orsen sat down across from him. The lamp between them hissed softly. Through the canvas she could hear the evening sounds of camp — the generator, the cook tent, the low murmur of the vigil line where Maren's people sang, very quietly, songs in a language the maps said had never existed.

"Sef. Tomorrow Dallet files the revocation. If it goes through, the site seals and the archive rots. Everything — the names, the grants, all of it — gone for another generation, maybe forever. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the archive stops being only down there." She held his gaze. "In ten days the permits expire anyway. Dallet is trying to kill them early because he's afraid of what happens in an open-status window. He's right to be afraid. In that window, the truth can surface and find standing. But we have to be ready before the window opens. All of it copied. All of it photographed. All of it out."

Sef was quiet for a long moment. "You're asking me to help you do the thing I was too scared to do alone."

"I'm asking you to do the thing you already started. You held back the archive. Now finish holding it — and then put it in daylight where no one can take it from you." She leaned forward. "Your sister's file, your niece's school — Dallet showed you those because secrecy is his whole architecture. Sunlight is the only thing that collapses it. One copy in one ministry drawer disappears. Five hundred copies in five hundred hands don't."

Sef looked down at his hands. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — steadier, the cartographer's voice, the voice of a man reading terrain.

"There's a problem. Dallet's inspection team arrives with the revocation order. Probably within forty-eight hours of filing. They'll seal the shafts first and inventory second. Once the shafts are capped, we can't get down at all."

"Then we go down before they arrive." Orsen stood. "Tomorrow night. While the filing is being processed and everyone's watching the paperwork instead of the dig. Four people, one night, everything copied."

"Four people," Sef repeated. "Who else?"

"I'm working on that." She was, in fact — she'd already spoken to Bren, the foreman, two days ago, and watched him weigh it in his big careful way and then say *tell me when and where* without another word. That left one more, and the one more was the hardest, because the fourth person she needed was the one person who owned the equipment, the permits, and the legal standing — and who would call it theft.

The fourth person was her mother.

Orsen pushed out of the tent into the dark. At the camp's edge the vigil fires burned on, and beyond them the escarpment stood black against the stars, holding its dead city, holding its ten thousand names, holding its breath.

Nine days. Maybe fewer. Somewhere in the valley below, a ministry clerk was feeding Dallet's filing into the machine, and the machine was beginning, with its terrible patience, to grind.

She had one night to convince her mother to rob herself.
