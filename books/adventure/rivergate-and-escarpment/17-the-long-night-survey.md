## 17. The Long Night Survey

The message came folded inside a ration tin, the way contraband had moved through the camp for a week now — tucked into the bread crate, passed hand to hand with the casualness of men who had learned to lie with their elbows.

*Midnight. Shaft two. Bring nothing you can't eat.*

Orsen read it twice by the light of her headlamp behind a zipped tent flap, then fed it to the camp stove and watched it curl. Outside, the escarpment stood black against a sky rinsed clean of cloud, the stars hard as nailheads. Somewhere downriver, Dallet's inspection team was packing vehicles. Ten days left on the permits — or fewer, if the revocation filing went through, and everyone in camp knew it would.

She checked her watch. Eleven-forty. She'd slept in her clothes for three nights running, which was either preparation or surrender; she hadn't decided which.

The lantern at the mess tent still burned. Under it sat Sef, alone, a mug of tea going cold between his hands. He looked up when she approached, and there was something new in his face — not the nervousness she'd known since the staging camp, not the flinching courtesy of a man waiting to be caught. Something steadier. Something that had already survived its worst night.

"You got the tin," he said.

"I ate the evidence."

"Good." He turned the mug a quarter-turn, a cartographer's habit, squaring it to an invisible grid. "I want to say something before we go down, because once we're in that corridor I'm going to be too busy shaking to say it properly."

Orsen leaned against the trestle table and waited.

"What I did — giving Dallet the maps — I've been telling myself it was coercion, that he had my sister's address and my mother's pension and a smile like a knife being sharpened. All true. None of it matters." He looked at her directly, which Sef almost never did. "I can't unhand him the maps. But I can make sure what he bought with them is worthless. That's tonight. If you'll have me."

She thought about the moment she'd found out — the cold drop in her stomach, the arithmetic of betrayal, how quickly she'd started calculating whether Sef was a liability to be managed or a man to be saved. She'd hated herself for the calculation more than him for the maps.

"You'll carry the heavy case," she said. "And you'll do exactly what I say when I say it."

"That's the most anyone's offered me all month."

They went down through the talus together, past the sleeping generators, past the windbreak of stacked crates where two figures detached themselves from the dark: Bren Oduya, the foreman, built like the drill rig he maintained, and beside him Tam Weller, the youngest hand on the crew, who had photographed the vigil at the camp's edge and hadn't yet been made to regret it. Both of them carried lamps wrapped in cloth to kill the spill of light.

"No one followed us," Bren said. It wasn't reassurance; it was a report. He'd spent twenty years reading terrain and had transferred the skill seamlessly to reading people. "Dallet's man Voss took the evening shift at the motor pool. He's asleep in the cab of the second truck. I checked twice."

"And Ilse?" Orsen asked, and heard how carefully she kept the title out of her voice and failed anyway.

Bren's silence lasted three seconds. In those seconds lived everything the expedition had become: the foreman who loved Dr. Vasquez enough to follow her into a collapsing trench, and who had decided, sometime in the last week, that love didn't extend to burying a nation twice.

"She took a sedative at nine," he said. "Doctor's orders. She hasn't slept properly since the inspection notice came." A pause. "I put the tablets by her cot myself."

Orsen let that sit. Her mother, chemically stilled for six hours while her own crew emptied the archive she'd ordered sealed — it should have felt like treachery. It felt instead like the first honest logistics of the entire dig.

"All right," Orsen said. "Shaft two. Single file. Nobody speaks below the third landing unless the lamp's covered."

* * *

The corridor received them the way it had received them the first time — with a breath of cool air that smelled of stone and old resin, and a silence so complete it pressed against the eardrums. Karvathi masonry rose on either side, blocks fitted so precisely that the joints were hairlines, and above them the painted band ran the length of the passage: processions of figures, rivers rendered in blue pigment that sixty years of darkness had only deepened.

Tam went first with the lamp, counting landings under his breath. At the archive chamber the air changed — drier, warmer, the way a room breathes differently when it has been sealed and opened and sealed again. The door stood as they'd left it, wedged with a timber shim, the bronze fittings green with age.

Inside, the shelves climbed to the ceiling.

Census rolls in lacquered tubes. Land grants bound in oiled leather gone stiff as bark. Tax ledgers, marriage registers, water-rights adjudications, school rosters — the whole bureaucratic heartbeat of a country that officially had never existed, laid out in careful Karvathi script by clerks who could not have imagined that one day their ink would be the last proof of anything.

"We photograph everything," Orsen said quietly. "Then we copy the critical documents by hand. Census rolls first. Then land grants. Then anything with village names on it." She set her watch on the shelf where everyone could see it. "We have until four-thirty. At five, Voss does his rounds. If we're not out and the shim's not reset, this was all for nothing."

"How many tubes is *everything*?" Tam asked, already unpacking the camera.

"Two hundred and eleven," Sef said. He'd counted days ago, during the survey runs, and Orsen saw now that his obsessive precision — the same trait Dallet had exploited — had been quietly re-aimed. "At four minutes a tube, that's fourteen hours. We have five."

"Then we don't do four minutes a tube." Orsen pulled the first roll from its cradle. "Two lamps, both sides lit, shutter on cable release, no bracketing. If an exposure's marginal, we log it and move on. Perfect is what gets us caught."

Bren rigged the second lamp from a drill battery and a reflector he'd bent himself, and suddenly the chamber had working light, flat and even, and the archive stopped being a tomb and became a workplace. Tam knelt at the camera. Sef opened the first census roll across the copying board with hands that trembled only until they touched the paper — and then, touching it, went still.

"First name," he said, reading aloud without meaning to. "*Adessan Kell, ferryman, born at Lowbank Reach.*" He looked up. "Lowbank Reach is Maren's village. The school sits on the old ferry landing. She told me that the first day, and I thought it was just a story people tell about their towns."

"It's not a story," Orsen said. "It's a deed. Write it down."

And they worked.

* * *

There is a particular exhaustion that comes from doing forbidden work well — the body running hot on purpose and adrenaline while the mind stays cold and procedural, every sound outside the chamber catalogued and dismissed: wind in the talus, a rock settling, the generator's distant pulse. By two o'clock Orsen had stopped feeling her knees. By three she had stopped noticing.

The rhythm found itself. Tam shot the rolls in sequence, calling numbers in a whisper — *forty-one, forty-two* — while Sef transcribed names onto camp ledger paper in a hand far neater than his field notes, and Bren ferried finished tubes back to their cradles in exact reverse order, so that even if someone inspected the chamber tomorrow, the dust would tell no story. Orsen moved between them, checking exposures, resolving disputes of translation, keeping the clock.

It was during the ninety-first tube that Sef began to laugh.

It was a small sound, strangled almost before it escaped, but in that silence it rang like dropped glass. Four heads turned.

"Sorry. Sorry." He pressed the back of his hand to his mouth. "It's just — I spent six years learning to draw maps that would pass inspection. Every course, every certification, it was always *accuracy to standard, legibility to standard.* And here I am at three in the morning committing about nine crimes, and it's the first time in my career the work has ever meant anything." He shook his head. "I used to think my problem was nerves. It isn't. My problem was that I was precise about things that didn't matter."

"Tube ninety-two," Orsen said, but she put her hand briefly on his shoulder as she said it, and he understood.

The census rolls gave them the dead nation in installments. *Born at Lowbank Reach. Born at Stony Ford. Born at the Two Rivers, district of Karvath proper.* Village after village, name after name — thousands of them, generations deep — and Orsen found herself reading them the way you read a river's gauge, watching the level rise. Each name was a claim. Each claim was a family downriver who might get back what a border war and a ministry's pen had erased. Sixty years of official nothing, and here was the antidote, tube by tube, in a lamp-lit hole in the earth, copied out by four tired people eating cold flatbread.

Around half past three, Tam spoke without lifting his eye from the viewfinder.

"My mum's from downriver," he said. "Not a descendant village — farther down, past the concession line. But when the flood came through last spring, she lost the garden her grandmother planted. And the whole town turned out to replant it. Took a day. Everyone brought cuttings." The shutter clicked. "I keep thinking about that. How fast people move when it's about putting something back."

Nobody answered. There wasn't anything to add. The shutter clicked again.

* * *

At four-fifteen they hit the wall every conspiracy hits: the last hour, when fatigue makes hands stupid and judgment worse. Sef mislabeled a transcription and caught it himself, but the near-miss changed the temperature in the chamber. Bren called it.

"Wrap the copies. Reset the chamber. We walk out slow."

They worked the final minutes in tight silence — copies bundled into oilcloth and distributed across four packs so that no single capture ended the night, the camera disassembled into innocent-looking parts, the timber shim tapped home until the door sat flush and the dust along its sill lay undisturbed. Orsen swept the copying board with a brush taken from her own kit, erased their knee-marks from the floor grit with the side of her palm, and stood a moment in the dark after the lamps were killed, listening to the archive breathe behind its sealed door.

*Hold on,* she thought. *Four more days. Maybe less.*

The climb out was slower than the climb down. Above ground the sky had gone the color of wet slate, the stars fading, the escarpment emerging from darkness like a decision being reconsidered. They separated at the windbreak without goodbyes — Bren toward the motor pool to confirm Voss still slept, Tam to the mess tent to be seen yawning over breakfast preparations, Sef and Orsen drifting toward the survey tents by different paths.

At the fork, Sef paused.

"The copies are safe?"

"As safe as anything is here."

"And your mother—" He stopped, corrected himself. "Dr. Vasquez. When she finds out. Because she will find out."

Orsen watched the light come up along the escarpment's edge, gilding the talus slope, the shaft head, the whole buried argument beneath it.

"When she finds out," Orsen said, "she'll understand that I learned everything I know about protecting something from her. She just won't like what I chose to protect."

Sex hours later — no. Six hours later, she was woken not by her alarm but by the crunch of tires on gravel, and pulled open her tent flap to see three ministry vehicles rolling into camp through the morning haze, Dallet riding in the lead with his elbow out the window, smiling his easy smile at the assembled crew as though he'd come for a social call.

Behind him, on the second vehicle, sat Voss — awake, alert, and looking at the ground around the shaft head with the particular attention of a man who has been told to look for something and intends to find it.

Orsen's hand went to the oilcloth bundle beneath her cot, and she began, calmly, to count the days she had left.
