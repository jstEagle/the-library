## 18. The Inspection

The message came up the river on a ministry launch at dawn, riding a flat gray wake that slapped against our pilings like a hand demanding entry. Orsen watched it come from the survey tent, a cup of tea going cold in her hand, and she knew before the boat touched the dock what it carried. You learned to read water after five years on it. You also learned, eventually, to read the way men sat in boats.

Dallet stood at the bow. He was not smiling.

"He's early," Sef said beside her. His voice had the flatness of a man who had already given away everything he had and was waiting to learn the price. Three days since he'd handed over the map copies. Three days since Orsen had looked at him across the mess table and said nothing at all, and watched him understand that she knew.

"Early means he's sure," she said. "Sure men don't wait."

The launch grounded. Dallet came up the bank in his pressed ministry grays, two clerks behind him carrying document cases, and behind them — this was new, this was the part that made Orsen's stomach drop — four men in field uniforms with the bored, patient posture of people who had sealed sites before. They carried crowbars wrapped in canvas. Not weapons. Tools. That was somehow worse.

"Dr. Vasquez." Dallet's voice carried across the camp, pleasant and carrying, a man performing procedure for an audience. "Where is she?"

"In her tent," Orsen said. "You could have written."

"I did. You didn't answer." He smiled then, briefly, the old easy smile, and it landed on her like a debt being called in. "Miss Vasquez. You look tired."

"Ninety-day digs do that."

"Eighty-one days, actually. But who's counting." He turned toward the center of camp. "I'm here under Article Twelve. Falsified survey records, submitted to a licensed concession. The revocation hearing is filed. Until it's heard, the site is to be sealed pending inspection. Where is Dr. Vasquez?"

And then Ilse Vasquez came out of her tent.

Orsen had seen her mother in a hundred modes over a lifetime of watching from a careful distance: the lecture-hall mode, the trench-side mode, the mode she used on ministry officials, the mode she used — rarely, and badly — on daughters. She had thought she knew them all.

She had never seen this one.

Ilse walked toward Dallet slowly, unhurried, a woman of sixty-one with gray in her braid and river mud on her boots, and she was smiling. Not warmly. The smile of a hostess who has caught a guest arriving early to a dinner not yet ready, and intends to make him feel it.

"Mr. Dallet," she said. "You've brought crowbars."

"Standard sealing equipment, Doctor."

"For a site you haven't inspected." Ilse folded her hands. "Article Twelve requires an inspection before sealing. You've filed the paperwork — I can see the cases — but the paperwork isn't the inspection. The inspection requires a licensed site assessor, a ministry archivist, and a neutral surveyor of record." She paused. "You have clerks. And men with crowbars."

Dallet's smile thinned by a degree. "The assessor is on the next launch."

"Then we have a few hours." Ilse turned, raised her voice with the effortless projection of forty years of lecture halls, and the camp came alive around her. "Tomas — the tarps off the corridor entrance. Sef — I want the grid boards squared, every stake re-taped. If the ministry is going to look at our site, they're going to look at it *properly*." She looked back at Dallet. "You'll want tea. We have terrible tea. You'll drink it anyway, because refusing hospitality at a concession site is noted in the inspection record, and you are a man who cares very much what's noted in the record."

Orsen stood very still and watched her mother work.

It was, she had to admit, magnificent. In the space of four minutes Ilse had taken Dallet's ambush and converted it into a scheduled event — an inspection, on her ground, on her timeline, with her crew bustling around him like stagehands — and every step of it was procedurally unimpeachable. He couldn't seal a site that hadn't been inspected. He couldn't inspect it without an assessor. And until the assessor arrived, he was a guest.

The terrible tea was brought. Dallet drank it.

* * *

Orsen found her mother an hour later at the corridor entrance, supervising the re-taping of grid stakes with the focus of a surgeon.

"You knew he was coming," Orsen said quietly.

"I suspected. The revocation filing was posted to the regional board yesterday. Public record, if you know where to look." Ilse didn't look up. "You should know where to look by now."

"You've had all night to prepare a defense of the dig."

"I've had twenty years to prepare a defense of the dig." Now she looked up, and her eyes were steady and clear and utterly without apology. "This is what I do, Orsen. This is the only thing I have ever done. I keep the work alive. Whatever it costs."

"The archive is under forty centimeters of backfill right now," Orsen said. "You ordered it reburied. If Dallet's assessor runs a sonar sweep of the corridor, he'll find the second chamber, and your unlogged find will become evidence."

"Then the assessor won't run a sonar sweep."

"Mother—"

"Ilse. Not here." Her mother's voice dropped, and for a moment — just a moment — something flickered under the performance. Not warmth. Something more like a warning. "Watch. Learn. This is the part they never teach in survey school: how a site survives. Anyone can find a city. Keeping one alive is the actual work."

She walked back toward Dallet, who was standing at the edge of the corridor entrance with his hands clasped behind him, looking down into the dark with the expression of a man reading a menu.

* * *

The assessor arrived at noon: a small, precise woman named Vey with ministry-issue instruments and the manner of someone who had sealed more sites than she'd opened. Ilse received her like an honored colleague. That was the masterstroke, Orsen realized — Dallet had come to catch a fraud, and Ilse spent the first hour making him watch her be generous to his own inspector, walking Vey through the corridor, explaining the Karvathi masonry techniques with genuine, infectious scholarship, answering every question with more than was asked.

Orsen trailed them at a distance, her instruments slung on her back, and told herself she was there to monitor the inspection. She was really there to watch her mother, the way you watch a fire you can't decide whether to warm your hands at or run from.

Vey was good. Better than Dallet had expected, maybe better than Ilse had expected. She ran a magnetometer line along the corridor floor without asking permission, and Orsen saw Ilse's shoulders set — a millimeter of tension, invisible to anyone who hadn't spent a lifetime reading her — as the instrument passed over the sealed breach to the second chamber.

The reading would show the void. It had to. Forty centimeters of backfill over an open chamber was not subtle to a magnetometer.

Vey stopped. Looked at the instrument. Looked at the wall.

"Dr. Vasquez," she said. "There's a cavity behind this section."

"There is," Ilse said.

Dallet had drifted over, and now he leaned in with the satisfaction of a man watching his trap close. "A cavity that does not appear in the site log."

"No," Ilse agreed. "It doesn't."

Orsen's heart was slamming. *This is it. This is where it comes apart.* Dallet had the falsified maps, Sef's copies, the whole architecture of the cover-up — and here was Ilse, standing at the breach, admitting the omission in front of the ministry's own assessor, and Orsen could not understand it, could not—

"Because," Ilse continued, in the calm voice of a professor correcting a student's sloppy note-taking, "the cavity was discovered *after* the last log submission, and Article Nine requires that new finds be logged within seventy-two hours *of stabilization*. The section was unstable. We backfilled it to prevent collapse — your own site-safety bulletin, Mr. Dallet, circular 44-B, recommends exactly that procedure — and we have not yet had seventy-two stable hours. The river rose Tuesday night. Check your own gauges." She smiled at Vey. "The log amendment is drafted. It will be filed the moment the section is certified stable. I'd be happy to show you the draft."

And she produced it. From her tent. Already written, already dated, already stamped with the expedition seal — a document that could not possibly have been drafted overnight, unless a woman had sat up all night by lamplight writing it in advance of an inspection she'd known was coming, which was of course exactly what had happened, and which was exactly the kind of thing that could never be proved.

Vey read the amendment. Read it again. Made a small note.

"The backfill procedure was correct," she said finally. "The amendment is timely. I'll note the chamber in the inspection record as *discovered, stabilized, pending documentation*." She looked at Dallet. "That's not a falsification, sir. That's a scheduling question."

Dallet's face went through a series of small, expensive adjustments.

* * *

They sealed nothing that day.

By late afternoon the crowbar men were back on the launch, the clerks' document cases were closed, and Vey had signed an inspection report that Orsen read over her shoulder in the last light: *Site found in good order. Documentation current within regulatory tolerance. Sealing order deferred pending revocation hearing.* Dallet signed beneath her, his signature smaller and angrier than the assessor's, and before he stepped onto the launch he turned to Orsen.

"You're quiet today," he said. "That's not like you. Usually you have questions. Usually you want things." He said *want* the way other men said *owe*. "The hearing is in nine days. Think about which side of it you'd rather be standing on. I'm told family testimony carries a great deal of weight in falsification cases. Either direction."

"Goodbye, Mr. Dallet."

He smiled his easy smile, and this time it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, and the launch pulled away into the dusk.

* * *

The camp celebrated. Tomas broke out the good bottle. Sef laughed at something for the first time in a week. Even the vigil at the camp's edge — Maren's villagers, thirty of them now, sitting in a silent line by the water every evening with lanterns — seemed to lift, and someone brought the lanterns closer, and for an hour there was something almost like a festival on the riverbank.

Orsen found her mother alone at the corridor entrance, checking the tarps against the night wind.

"That amendment was written before he arrived," Orsen said. "You knew the exact regulation he'd cite. You knew Vey by name. You had the whole thing staged like a play."

"I told you. Twenty years."

"You protected the dig. You protected the archive — it's still buried, still unlogged, still *yours* to bury again the moment we're alone." Orsen heard her own voice shaking and didn't stop it. "You could have ended it today. Vey was standing right there. One sentence — *the chamber contains the national archive of Karvath, and it should be documented now* — and it would have been in the ministry record where no one could ever make it disappear. You had her pen in your hand, Mother. You handed her a scheduling note instead."

Ilse set down the tarp stake. When she turned, her face in the lantern light was tired and calm and absolutely immovable, and Orsen understood, with a clarity that felt like a rib cracking, that she was not looking at a woman who had almost chosen truth.

She was looking at a woman who had never once considered it.

"Do you know what the ministry does with a national archive of an erased nation?" Ilse said softly. "It doesn't publish it. It *files* it. Into a records annex in the capital, under a classification stamp, where it sits for another sixty years while the lawyers argue and the permits rot and Maren's grandchildren grow up stateless. I have watched this ministry swallow a find whole, Orsen. I watched it happen when I was younger than you are now. I will not hand them the meal. The archive stays in the ground, in *my* ground, until it can come out into daylight that won't swallow it."

"And when does that daylight come? The permits lapse in nine days. Dallet's hearing is in nine days. There is no version of the calendar where your careful, protected, buried truth survives past midnight nine days from now. You've built a perfect machine for keeping it safe forever, and forever just ran out."

For a moment — one long, terrible moment — Orsen saw something move behind her mother's eyes. Not agreement. Recognition. The look of a woman doing arithmetic she had done before and always gotten the same answer, and hating the answer, and getting it anyway.

"You don't understand," Ilse said. "What I am protecting—"

"I understand exactly what you're protecting. You're protecting it from *everyone*. Including the people it belongs to." Orsen stepped back. "I used to think you were a coward. You're not. That's the worst part. You're the bravest person I know, and you are going to be brave in exactly the wrong direction, all the way to the end, and you will never — not once, not even at the very last hour — choose to open the door yourself."

Her mother said nothing. The lantern hissed. Down at the water, Maren's lanterns burned in their patient line.

Orsen walked away across the camp, past the celebrating crew, past Sef's tentative wave, into her own tent, and sat down in the dark with her instruments around her and the archive photographs hidden beneath the floor matting, and she did the only arithmetic that was left.

Nine days. One night. One hour at midnight when the permits would lapse and the law's grip would open, just for a moment, before Dallet's hearing could close it again.

Her mother would never open the door.

So Orsen would have to.

She took out the survey sheets and began, by lamplight, to plan the longest night of her life.
