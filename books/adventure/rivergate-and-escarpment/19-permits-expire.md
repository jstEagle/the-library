## 19. Permits Expire

The last day of the concession began the way all the worst days of my life had begun: with me awake before dawn, watching light come up over the escarpment and thinking about my mother.

I hadn't slept. Nobody had, really. The camp below the escarpment had the held-breath quality of a hospital waiting room — people moving quietly, speaking in half-sentences, everyone aware that something was ending and nobody willing to say what came after. Dallet's revocation filing sat in a ministry office two hundred miles downriver like a loaded gun on a table. His inspection team was due at noon. The permits died at midnight. And somewhere between those two hours, I had to decide — finally, irrevocably — who I was going to be.

I made coffee at the survey tent and drank it standing up, looking at the escarpment. Sixty years ago, Karvath had been erased from every map in the country with a stroke of ministerial ink. Sixty years of silt and talus and official silence had buried it deeper than any flood could. And for five months I'd been digging it back out one instrument reading at a time, telling myself I was doing it for the money, then for Sef, then for Maren's villagers, then for the truth itself — each reason a rung on a ladder I'd been climbing without admitting where it led.

Today there were no more rungs. Today was the top.

* * *

At seven, Sef found me at the transit table. He looked like a man who'd been wrung out and hung to dry — which, in fairness, he was. Since handing his false maps to Dallet, he'd barely spoken except to apologize, over and over, in the particular way guilty people do: not asking forgiveness, just stating the fact of their guilt like weather.

"The copies are done," he said. "All of them."

"Show me."

He spread them across the table: photographic plates of the archive chamber, every shelf, every chest, every census roll and land grant, shot by lantern light during the long night survey. Beside them, the true survey — my coordinates, honest ones, showing the shaft, the corridor, the archive chamber Ilse had ordered unlogged. And beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth, the physical duplicates: Sef's corrected maps drawn twice, my field notebooks copied page by page in Maren's careful schoolteacher hand, because her script was better than any of ours and she'd insisted.

"It's enough," Sef said. "It has to be enough."

I touched the edge of a photograph. In the lantern light of that image, the Karvathi archive gleamed — lacquered wood, stamped metal, rows upon rows of names. A nation's paperwork. Everything a bureaucracy needs to prove a people existed, filed neatly by the people themselves before anyone thought to erase them.

"It's enough," I said. "The question is whether we're brave enough."

* * *

Dallet's inspection team arrived at 11:40, twenty minutes early, because men like Dallet are always early — punctuality is just another form of pressure. Four of them, gray-suited, carrying document cases and a portable seal press. Dallet himself wore the same easy smile he'd worn at dinner six weeks ago, when he'd told me he knew about the token in my pocket and offered protection in exchange for betrayal. He shook hands with the crew. He complimented the mess tent. He asked after everyone's health like a man visiting relatives.

Ilse met him at the site gate, and I watched her work.

She'd dressed in her field best — the faded expedition jacket with the university crest, the one from the old photographs. She walked him through the trench lines slowly, pointing out stratigraphy, explaining sediment layers, delivering a flawless forty-minute lecture on fluvial deposition that was so technically perfect even I learned something. She showed him the corridor of Karvathi masonry and let him stand in it, let him touch the inscriptions naming villages that still existed downriver. She answered every question with precision and patience.

And she never once mentioned the second chamber. Never once opened the sealed door at the corridor's end behind which the entire national archive of Karvath sat in the dark, catalogued, photographed, and copied.

I stood at the trench edge and watched my mother lie with her whole body — her posture, her enthusiasm, her genuine love of the science shining through like a lamp — and I understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she would never choose truth. Not today. Not ever, if left to herself. She would stall Dallet, see the permits lapse, seal the site "for its own protection," and spend the rest of her career writing papers about a discovery no one would ever be allowed to verify. She'd protect the dig and the team and the descendants' future claim by burying the evidence that made the claim real. Sacrifice as religion. Silence as love.

It was almost noble. It was absolutely wrong.

Dallet knew it too, I think. As his team packed up their instruments — they'd take formal possession at midnight, when the concession lapsed and the site reverted to ministry control — he paused beside me at the trench edge.

"A remarkable day," he said pleasantly. "Dr. Vasquez is quite the steward."

"She's quite a lot of things."

"Mm." He looked out over the excavation, and for a moment the smile thinned and I saw the ministry underneath, the machinery, the sixty years of erasure wearing a gray suit. "You know, Ms. Vasquez, whatever happens tonight — the site will be secured. Properly. Sealed pending review. Indefinitely, if necessary." He said *indefinitely* the way other people say *forever*. "Some things are better kept quiet. You of all people understand discretion."

"I'm working on it," I said.

His eyes flicked to mine, and for the first time all day something uncertain moved behind them. Then the smile reassembled itself and he walked away to supervise the sealing of the corridor entrance.

* * *

Afternoon. The sun crawled. I checked my watch so often the band left a pale stripe on my wrist.

The plan was simple, and its simplicity was its whole point. One release, split four ways, all at once. Copies to Maren and the village delegation waiting at the camp's edge — the descendants, the people whose names filled those census rolls. Copies to the press: I'd spent three days getting word to a river correspondent named Adela Kray who worked the Rivergate towns and had, according to Sef, "no fear of anything, including editors." Copies to the ministry's own oversight board — the internal watchdog Dallet's department answerable to, the bureaucratic immune system that only activates when an infection becomes public. And copies to the regional records office, filed officially, timestamped, so the documents entered the legal record at the exact moment the concession expired and became, legally, unownable by anyone.

Four directions. Same hour. No single door to close, no single throat to cut. My mother had taught me archaeology; Dallet had taught me strategy; between them I'd learned the only lesson that mattered: you don't hide the truth in one place and defend it. You scatter it like seed and let it come up everywhere at once.

Maren came to the survey tent at dusk. She moved carefully these days, favoring a bruised hip from the near-charge two weeks ago, when Dallet's men had tried to clear the vigil and I'd stood in front of her while a camp hand photographed the whole thing. That photograph was already in the packet. It was worth a thousand pages of testimony.

"You're sure about the hour?" she asked.

"Midnight. Not a minute before. If we release early, Dallet gets twelve hours to spin it, seize the originals, file injunctions. At midnight the concession lapses and the site stops being ours or his. Everything lands in the gap."

"And your mother?"

The question hung there. Maren wasn't cruel; she was precise, the way teachers are.

"My mother," I said, "made her choice this afternoon. She chose the seal."

Maren nodded slowly. Then she did something I didn't expect. She reached into her coat and took out a small object — a coin-sized token, lacquered wood, stamped metal. The twin of the one I'd pocketed off the flood-scoured bank a lifetime ago, the theft that had started everything.

"Our people carried these," she said. "River tokens. You gave one to someone to say: wherever the water takes you, this is where you belong." She set it on the table between us, next to the oilcloth packets. "I've been waiting months to give you yours. I wasn't sure you'd stay long enough to take it."

I picked it up. The lacquer was warm from her pocket. Somewhere under my ribs, something that had been clenched for five years — since the scandal, since the flight, since I'd started measuring my life in miles to avoid measuring it in years — loosened by a single degree.

"Thank you," I said, and found I meant it more than I'd meant anything in months.

* * *

Ten o'clock. Eleven. The camp emptied by degrees. The loyal ones — Bren the foreman, Toma and Kesha from the night survey — drifted toward the survey tent without being asked, the way conspirators do, pretending casualness and fooling no one. Sef sat with the packets arranged in order of release, checking and rechecking seals that didn't need checking.

At eleven-fifteen, Ilse appeared at the tent flap.

She looked at the table — the photographs, the true survey, the duplicated archive, the river token — and I watched her assemble the picture the way she assembled any excavation: quickly, completely, without flinching.

"So," she said. "This is tonight."

"This is tonight."

She came into the tent. Bren shifted; Toma looked at the floor. Ilse ignored them all. She looked only at me.

"I could have the ministry seize these right now," she said quietly. "Dallet's men are fifty meters away. One word."

"One word," I agreed. "And then the oversight board still gets its copies at midnight, and the press still gets theirs, and Maren's people still get theirs, and everything you're doing right now becomes the thing they write about in the second paragraph instead of the first."

Something moved across her face — not anger. Recognition. Her daughter had learned to count doors.

"You think I don't know what this costs," she said. "You think I haven't spent twenty years paying exactly this bill. Orsen—"

"I know what you think it costs." My voice came out steadier than I expected. "You think silence protects people. Maybe it does. But it protects them the way a tomb protects them, Mum. And there are eight hundred names in that archive who've been protected to death already."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lantern hissed. Outside, far off, the river ran on, keeping its own books.

"If you do this," she said finally, very low, "they will come for you. Not Dallet — worse. The ministry doesn't forgive, and neither does the profession. Your name will be mud before spring. Everything I built, everything I endured, to keep you clear of this—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "I did it all to keep you out of it."

"I know," I said. And I did know, finally — knew it the way you know a thing after it's too late for the knowing to matter. "That's the problem, Mum. You kept me out of my own life."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she straightened, and the field jacket with the university crest settled on her shoulders like armor going on, and she said, in the voice she used for opening lectures:

"Midnight, then."

And she turned and walked out of the tent, and I couldn't tell you — even now — whether she'd surrendered or declared war. Both, maybe. With my mother it was always both.

* * *

Eleven-forty. We moved.

Bren took the village packet down to the vigil fires at the camp's edge, where Maren waited with her delegation and a mule cart. Toma rode for the telegraph office in Harrow Bend with the press copies and a letter of transmittal in my handwriting, dated and signed. Kesha took the oversight board's set to the night clerk at the regional records office, who had been paid — legitimately, in survey fees — to remain at his desk until midnight and file whatever arrived, timestamped, no questions.

Sef and I stayed with the last set: the master copies, the originals of everything, plus the one item no one else had. The sworn statement. Ilse's statement — signed, witnessed by Bren and Toma two nights ago in this same tent, corroborating the find, authenticating the survey, surrendering her credibility to the record. She'd signed it in the small hours, in terrible lamplight, without ceremony, and then had gone back to stalling Dallet by daylight as if nothing had happened. I still didn't fully understand her. I suspected I never would. But the paper existed, and at midnight it would exist everywhere.

We stood at the survey table with our watches synchronized, and the last minutes of the concession drained away like water out of a cut bank.

"Orsen." Sef's voice cracked on my name. "Whatever happens—"

"Don't," I said gently. "You already paid. Tonight's how."

The minute hand climbed. Fifty-eight. Fifty-nine.

Outside, the escarpment stood black against the stars, holding its dead city. Downriver, eight hundred names lay in oilcloth, about to be spoken aloud for the first time in sixty years. Somewhere in a gray office, Dallet was presumably raising a glass to a clean seizure in the morning. Somewhere in her quarters, my mother sat alone with a signed confession and a career's worth of silence.

I thought about a corner of lacquered wood in a flood-scoured bank, and a coin-sized token I'd stolen because it was the only honest thing I'd done in years.

The watch ticked past midnight.

"Now," I said.

Sef sealed the last packet. I stepped out of the tent into the cold starlight, and I released everything — all of it, everywhere, at once — into the gap where a nation had been erased and where, starting now, it would have to be written back in.

There was no thunderclap. No alarm. Just the river sound, and the vigil fires down at the camp's edge suddenly flaring as word spread, and my own heart going like a surveyor's hammer on bedrock.

Then, from the direction of the quarters, footsteps — fast, furious, unmistakable.

My mother was coming, and she was not alone.
