## 21. Names Read Aloud

The notices went up on the ferry landing at dawn, nailed to the same posts where the ministry had once posted the banishment orders — Maren had found the old nail holes herself, running her fingers over them the way you'd touch a scar to be sure it was real. The new paper read simply: *Tonight. The rolls will be read. All are welcome. Bring your children.*

She had written it in her own hand because no printer in Lowmarsh would take the job without a ministry stamp, and because she wanted the words to look like what they were: a schoolteacher's work, patient and unglamorous, letters formed the way she taught them to eleven-year-olds every morning.

By noon the whole river seemed to know. Boats came up from Sallow Bend and down from the terraced farms above Kettle Reach, and by late afternoon the landing at Vess — Maren's village, the largest of the seven that still remembered what they were — held more people than the harvest festival ever drew. Old women who hadn't left their kitchens in years sat on crates brought from the fish sheds. Men who fished the Rivergate under permits that listed them as *unaffiliated persons of the riparian districts* stood with their hats off, holding them like they were waiting for a photograph that would never come.

Maren stood at the top of the landing steps with the copies spread across two trestle tables, weighted down with stones against the river wind. Orsen Vasquez had copied them three times over — one set for the oversight board, one for the press, one for this. The originals stayed sealed in the archive chamber beneath the escarpment, where the law now said they would stay forever, protected as heritage rather than seized as evidence. Maren had read the order twice before she believed it. *Heritage.* Sixty years of being an error in the record, and now they were a monument.

The copies were not beautiful. Orsen's team had photographed the census rolls through a single night by lamplight, and the pages showed it — shadows across the margins, a thumbprint in the corner of the third roll, the ghost of water damage along the bottom edge where the flood that started everything had once reached. But the names themselves were crisp. Thousands of them. Karvathi script in the left column, the old tongue; the colonial transcription in the right; household, village, trade, age. A nation reduced to a ledger, and a ledger raised back into a nation.

"You'll want water," said her grandmother, appearing at her elbow the way she always did, silent as driftwood. Tam was ninety-one years old and had been seven when the maps changed. She wore her good shawl, the grey one with the border pattern that the ministry's textile inspectors had spent a decade trying to tax out of existence. "Your voice will go."

"I've read aloud to thirty children every day for nine years, Gran."

"Not like this."

No. Not like this. Maren looked out over the crowd and felt the weight of it settle onto her chest — not dread exactly, but something adjacent, the pressure of a door that could only be opened once. If she faltered, if the reading became a performance or a grievance, the ministry's lawyers would find the seam and pull. It had to be plain. It had to be true. It had to be *names*, nothing but names, until the names did their own work.

A launch came around the bend below the landing — the survey boat, the one with the shallow draft and the patched hull. Orsen stood at the bow beside Sef, who clutched his satchel like a man carrying something breakable, which he was. Behind them, uninvited and unexpected, came a figure in a ministry-grey coat: Dallet. He stepped onto the landing planks, looked at the crowd, and made no move toward the tables. He simply took a place at the rail's end, hands folded, watching. Maren decided she would not give him the satisfaction of acknowledgment. If he had come to witness, let him witness. There was worse penance than standing through four hours of the dead returning.

Orsen climbed the steps. Up close she looked wrung out — Maren had heard about the final night, the copying, the midnight release, the mother signing her sworn statement with a hand that witnesses said didn't shake until afterward — but her eyes were steady.

"You should do the first page," Maren said. "You carried them here."

"They're not mine to carry." Orsen touched the top sheet, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "They're yours. I just want to hear them." She glanced at the crowd, then back. "All of them. Don't skip the damaged parts. Even the ones you can't fully read. Read what's legible and say 'the rest is lost' — my grandmother used to say that at gravesides. The lost deserve to be counted too."

Maren nodded. Her throat had gone tight. She turned to the tables, lifted the first page, and the crowd quieted so completely she could hear the river working at its pilings below.

She began.

*Ademar Vesselik. Household of six. Ferryman, Vess Landing. Age 44.*

An old man near the fish sheds made a sound like a struck bell. Maren kept reading. *His wife, Ilsette. His daughters, Brava and Toma. His sons—*

"That's my grandfather's grandfather," the old man said, not loudly, not to anyone. "That's us. We're in the book."

*We're in the book.* Maren felt those four words land somewhere behind her ribs and stay there. She read on.

* * *

It took four hours. She had planned for three, but the crowd kept asking her to slow down, and she understood why: people wanted to hear their own households, and households came in no particular hurry, and between the entries there were silences that belonged to the listeners and not to her. When she reached a name someone recognized, a murmur ran through the crowd like wind through wheat, and sometimes a person would stand — just stand, briefly, hat in hand — and sit again. By the second hour the standing had become a custom nobody had announced. By the third hour, when Maren read the entry for the village of Sorrel Ford — erased entirely, all forty-one households, drowned when the dam went in two years after the banishment — the entire crowd rose together and stayed standing until she finished the last name.

Tam stood through all of it. Ninety-one years old, and she never sat down.

There were gaps. Water damage, fire damage, one entire section of the fourth roll eaten through. At each gap Maren stopped and said, as Orsen had told her, "The rest is lost," and each time the crowd answered — softly, in unison, a response nobody had rehearsed — "*Counted.*" By the fifth time it happened Maren had to pause and press her knuckles to her mouth. This was liturgy. Nobody had designed it, and it had arrived anyway, complete, the way rivers arrive at the sea.

Dallet stood at the rail through all four hours. He did not check his watch. Once, near the end, Maren saw him mouth a name along with her — a name from the middle of the sixth roll — and she understood, with a small cold shock, that the observer had family in these pages too. That was the thing about erasure, she thought. It never asked anyone's permission. It took whoever was standing in the wrong district when the borders moved.

When the last legible name was spoken — *Yeva Marchetti-Korvas, infant, household of Sorrel Ford* — Maren closed the roll and the crowd did not cheer. They exhaled. All of them at once, a sound like the river changing course, and then the weeping started, quietly, in pockets, grandmothers and fishermen and the ferry pilot who had steered the launch, and Maren stood among it holding a piece of paper that weighed nothing and everything.

Orsen came up beside her. "You did it," she said.

"No." Maren shook her head. "They did. I just had the loudest voice." She looked out at the crowd, at the boats already lighting their lamps for the trip home, at the children asleep against their parents' shoulders who would grow up knowing their own names meant something. "What happens now?"

"Now the lawyers earn their keep." Orsen's mouth curved, tired. "Land claims filed Monday. The escarpment site stays sealed and protected — heritage status, permanent. And the school registers get amended." She met Maren's eyes. "Your students can write *Karvathi* on the nationality line again. Legally. Starting tomorrow."

Maren laughed, and the laugh broke halfway through into something else, and Orsen put a hand on her shoulder and let her.

* * *

The claims were filed on Monday, as promised, and the ministry's response came faster than anyone expected — which was how Maren learned that fear moves quickly when it has been caught in daylight. By Wednesday there were surveyors on the riverbanks, official ones, measuring the old boundaries against the land grants in the archive. By Friday a magistrate sat in the Vess schoolhouse — Maren's own classroom, desks pushed back, chalk still dusting the tray — hearing the first petitions.

She testified twice. The second time, the magistrate asked her a question no one had prepared her for.

"You teach these children the old script," he said. "Why? Practically speaking. The transcription is legally sufficient."

Maren considered the rows of faces in the photographs she'd brought — her students, holding up their slates, writing characters their grandparents had been punished for writing. "Because a name you can only receive is a gift," she said. "A name you can read and write yourself is a possession. We were dispossessed once by people who controlled our records. I intend that never to happen again, in any form, in any language."

The magistrate wrote for a long time. Then he approved the claim.

The escarpment protection order came through in the spring, signed, stamped, published in the gazette that had once run the banishment decrees. Maren pinned the notice to the schoolhouse wall next to the map — the new map, the one with the seven villages named, the one her students had helped proofread, catching two spelling errors that the cartographers, sheepish, corrected in the second printing. Sef sent the corrections himself, with a note: *Thank you for letting me fix what I helped break.*

Below the escarpment, the dig site was closed to everyone except a conservation team and, once a season, the descendants. Maren went in the autumn, with Tam, who walked the corridor of old masonry leaning on her granddaughter's arm and reading the wall inscriptions aloud in a voice that grew stronger with every line. The walls named the villages. Their villages. Tam stopped at the list, pressed her palm flat against the stone, and said, "Well. Here we are. Still on somebody's wall after all."

On the walk back down to the boats, Tam told Maren something she had never told anyone — about the night the soldiers came to burn the parish records, and how her own mother had stood in the doorway of their house and recited the family line from memory while the smoke rose, so that at least one living copy would survive. "She said, *as long as a mouth remembers, the paper doesn't matter.*" Tam smiled, thin and fierce. "She was almost right. It took sixty years and a stubborn surveyor to prove the paper mattered too. But the mouth mattered first."

Maren thought about that for a long time afterward. She thought about it especially on the morning, late in the season, when a letter arrived at the school bearing the return address of a women's correctional facility upriver — the facility where Dr. Ilse Vasquez was serving her sentence for falsification of records, awaiting appeal, correspondence permitted.

Maren had never met the woman. She knew the shape of her story the way everyone on the river did now: the suppressed dig twenty years ago, the silence built career by career, the daughter who chose truth at midnight and the mother who chose her daughter at dawn. The newspapers had made a morality play of it. Maren distrusted morality plays; she taught history, and history was messier.

She opened the letter at her desk, after the children had gone home.

It was short. The handwriting was precise, a scholar's hand, unhurried.

*Mrs. Osten,* it began — not quite getting her name right, or perhaps testing whether Maren would correct it —

*I am writing to ask a favor that I have no right to ask. In the archive chamber there is a shelf of school registers. Karvathi school registers, from the last generation before the erasure. Lesson plans, reading lists, a teacher's marginal notes in the hand of someone who clearly loved the work. I have thought about those shelves every day since the release. I have spent my life deciding what should be preserved and what should wait, and I was wrong about nearly everything, but I am not wrong about this: those registers belong in your classroom, not in a sealed vault. You are the living continuation of whatever teacher wrote those notes. If you petition the conservation board — and they will listen to you, though they will not listen to me — I believe the registers should be lent to your school. Not given. Lent. So that someone must always be responsible for returning them, and responsibility, I have learned too late, is the only honest way to hold the past.*

*I understand if you decline. I would understand better than most.*

*— I. Vasquez*

Maren read it three times. Outside her window, the river went on doing what it had always done, keeping its own books, carrying everything eventually to the sea and bringing things back. On the wall, the new map caught the afternoon light. On her desk, forty exercise books waited to be marked, full of her students' careful, resurrected letters.

She took out a clean sheet of paper and began to write her reply — and her petition — and found, to her surprise, that the first word came easily, as if it had been waiting sixty years for someone to say it.

*Yes.*
