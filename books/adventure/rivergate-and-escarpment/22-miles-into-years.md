## 22. Miles Into Years

The Rivergate ran low and clear in October, the way it always did after the autumn rains failed to arrive on schedule, and Orsen Vasquez stood in the shallows below Harrow Bend with a leveling rod in one hand and mud to her knees, and did not want to be anywhere else.

That was the strange part. That was the part she kept testing, the way you press a bruise to confirm it's healing.

Five years of contracts had taught her to read a river like a ledger — every meander an entry, every cutbank a debt being paid. She'd worked the Vell, the Sorrel, the high braids above Kestrel Pass, and she had been good at all of them and at home in none. Good was a portable skill. Home wasn't supposed to be. But here she was, third week of a six-month contract for the town of Lowmark, renting a room above the chandlery with a view of the water tower, and every morning when she woke she knew exactly which river was outside, and it was this one, and something in her chest had stopped bracing.

"You're smiling at the rod," said Pell, her apprentice this season, a fourteen-year-old with ink-stained fingers and her grandmother's stubbornness. "That's either a good reading or you've finally gone soft."

"It's a good reading," Orsen said. "Bank's stable through the bend. Write it down."

Pell wrote it down. She wrote everything down, in a hand so small and precise it looked typeset, and Orsen had already caught herself feeling the specific pride that belongs to teachers — the pride of watching someone do a thing correctly because you showed them how, which is a different thing entirely from doing it yourself, and better.

The contract was ordinary. Flood mitigation, bank surveys, a new gauge station upstream of the weir. The town council had argued about the budget for two sessions and then hired her anyway, because after everything — after the escarpment, after the archive, after the name Karvath came back into the world like a man pulled from cold water — nobody in the valley wanted to be the town that hadn't taken its river seriously.

Her reputation had arrived ahead of her, as reputations do. *The surveyor who gave a nation back its name.* She'd heard the phrase twice now, once from a councilman and once from a boy selling eels off a cart, and both times she'd wanted to correct the record. She hadn't given anything back. She'd just stopped holding it.

* * *

Maren's school stood at the end of Ferry Lane, a long low building with a slate roof and a bell that had been recast the previous spring — recast, Maren told anyone who'd listen, from the bronze of the old boundary marker the ministry had hauled away sixty years ago and never gotten around to destroying. The bell rang flat and sweet, and when it rang, children came out of the door like water finding its level.

Orsen came on Thursdays. It had started as a favor and become a fixture: two hours with the older students, instruments spread across trestle tables, the whole class learning what a datum was and why a lie on a map was worse than most lies, because maps outlive the people who tell them.

"Show me again," said a girl named Tess, holding the clinometer wrong for the fourth time, "why we can't just guess."

"Because guessing is how rivers eat towns," Orsen said. "And because somebody guessed about your grandparents' village once, on paper, and sixty years later there were people alive who'd never seen their own name written down." She adjusted the girl's grip. "Instruments don't care whose side you're on. That's the whole point of them. You point them at the truth and they hold still."

Tess squinted through the sight glass, got her angle, and wrote it down in handwriting almost as small as Pell's. Outside the window, the Rivergate glittered, indifferent and generous, carrying silt from the escarpment country down toward the delta where the descendant villages stood — villages that now appeared on the new survey maps under their own names, spelled the way their own elders spelled them.

Maren brought tea at the half hour, as she always did, and sat on the edge of a desk with her ankles crossed, listening without interrupting, which was her gift and her discipline. Afterward, walking out together past the bell, she said, "They asked me last week if you'd stay. Not for the term. For good."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them you were a river person." Maren smiled. "I told them river people come back."

Orsen looked at the water. The light was going gold and long, the way it did in October, and somewhere downstream a barge horn sounded, low and patient.

"I used to count my life in miles," she said. "Contracts finished. Rivers put behind me. I thought that was freedom — leaving nothing behind anywhere, so nothing could take anything from me." She turned the token over in her pocket — she still carried it, the little lacquered-backed coin with the dead nation's seal, though it wasn't contraband anymore; it hung in the heritage museum at Lowmark now, on loan, indefinitely, from a lender who preferred not to be named. "Turns out that's just another way of having nothing."

"And now?"

"Now I'm trying seasons." She held up fingers. "This is my second autumn here. If I'm still here for the third, I'll have earned the word."

Maren didn't say *stay*. She was too wise for that. She just squeezed Orsen's shoulder once, hard, the way you set a stake in firm ground, and went back inside to her students.

* * *

The prison at Corvel sat on a hill above a tributary of the Vell, three hours by coach, and Orsen made the trip on the first Saturday of every month, which meant she had now done it four times and could predict the exact spot on the road where the escarpment came into view — blue and vast and scarred, its talus slopes stitched over with the scaffolding of the heritage works.

The site itself was closed to digging. That had been the settlement, or part of it: no more excavation until the descendant councils finished their own archaeological protocols, with their own overseers, on their own timeline. The ministry's oversight board had wanted a faster schedule; the courts had sided with Maren's people; Dallet had been reassigned to a customs post so far inland his letters took eleven days to arrive, and Orsen had received exactly one, courteous and chilling, congratulating her on the outcome and noting, in passing, that history was long and careers longer. She'd burned it in the chandlery stove and felt nothing but warmth.

Ilse Vasquez had been at Corvel for five months now, pending appeal. Obstruction, evidence tampering, falsification of survey records — the charges had landed on her like weather, and she had faced them the way she faced everything, with a spine like a surveyor's rod and a silence that made the prosecutors work for every inch. She had signed the sworn statement in the end — the statement that corroborated the find, that surrendered twenty years of carefully buried work, that saved her daughter from prosecution at the cost of whatever remained of her name. She had signed it in front of witnesses, in a steady hand, and then she had looked at Orsen across the table in the hearing room and said only: "You measured true."

It was the closest thing to forgiveness either of them had managed yet. Maybe it was the only kind that existed between them.

The visiting room smelled of floor wax and boiled cabbage. Ilse sat on the far side of the partition with her hands folded, gray coming in at her temples now, thinner, but her eyes unchanged — those pale, instrument-precise eyes that had catalogued half a buried civilization and nearly buried it twice.

"You look settled," her mother said. It was not quite an accusation. With Ilse, nothing was ever quite an accusation; the accusation lived underneath, load-bearing.

"I am settling. There's a difference."

"A difference I spent your entire childhood failing to teach you." Ilse's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "How are my students?"

"Your students. I found them."

"You found them where I pointed. That's not nothing." She leaned back. "Tell me about the gauge station."

So Orsen told her — the weir readings, the sediment load after the first storm, Pell's flawless field notes, Tess and the clinometer. Ilse listened the way she had listened to data her whole life: completely, greedily, storing it. This was the strangest mercy of the visits, Orsen had decided. In here, stripped of permits and prestige and the machinery of control, her mother had rediscovered the original thing — the love of measurement itself, clean and unweaponized. Whether it would survive release was a question the appeal would answer eventually. Orsen had stopped needing the answer urgently. That, too, was new.

"The appeal hearing is in March," Ilse said, near the end of the hour. "If it goes well—"

"If it goes well, you'll have conditions. Supervised work. No sole custody of any site, ever." Orsen said it evenly. "I helped write the proposal."

"I know. I read it." A pause, and then, quietly, in a voice Orsen had heard maybe twice in her life — the voice from the corridor outside the archive chamber, the night the lamps were burning low and the truth was stacked in crates around them: "You proposed that I work under Maren's council. Under the descendants. You proposed the one arrangement in which I could never again decide alone what stays buried."

"Yes."

"It's the best offer I've ever received." Ilse unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table, palms up, an old surveyor's gesture — *here is the ground, take it as it is.* "Third Saturday in March. Bring the field notes from your autumn survey. I want to see real numbers. I've missed real numbers."

The bell rang. Visiting hours ended the way rivers end, without drama, just a gradual arriving. Orsen walked down the hill to the coach road with the escarpment at her back and the prison windows catching the afternoon sun, and she realized she was already composing the letter she'd write on Sunday, and that the letter would be full of ordinary things — weather, budgets, a student's breakthrough — and that ordinary things, sent regularly to one fixed address, were what a life looked like when it had roots.

* * *

Winter came early that year, a hard blue December that locked the shallows in lace and drove the survey indoors. Orsen spent the short days at the trestle tables in Maren's school, teaching reductions and error-checking while snow ticked against the windows, and the long evenings in her room above the chandlery copying the season's notes into fair form.

On the solstice, the whole town walked down to the weir with lanterns — an old custom, Maren said, older than anyone could prove, which was the best kind of old. The descendant families came up from the delta in two longboats, and someone read names aloud from the census rolls, a handful each year, so that the reading would never finish, so that it would go on the way a river goes on. This year they read the names of the dig crew. Sef's name was in there — Sef, who had broken and then unbroken himself, who had spent the spring testifying and the summer redrawing every false map from scratch, unpaid, meticulous, redeemed in the only currency that mattered, which is corrected work. He was in Kestrel Pass now, teaching cartography. He wrote to Orsen in that same tiny precise hand. *The mountains hold still,* his last letter began. *I find I trust them more than I used to.*

Pell found her at the rail of the weir, lantern light jumping on the black water.

"They're saying the council wants to renew your contract," Pell said. "Through next year. Maybe make the post permanent. Town surveyor." The girl tried to say it casually and failed completely. "Is that true?"

"It's on the agenda for January."

"And?" Pell's whole face was a held breath.

"And I'm staying through the winter regardless. After that—" Orsen looked downstream, toward the delta, toward the escarpment standing dark against the stars, toward the prison hill three hours east where a lamp burned in a window every third Saturday. She thought about miles, all those thousands of them, and how each one had been a door closing softly behind her. She thought about seasons, and how a season doesn't leave; it turns into the next one and takes you with it.

"After that," she said, "I'm measuring in years."

Pell grinned and ran off to tell everyone before it was official, which was the correct way to handle news in a river town, and Orsen stayed at the rail a while longer with the cold coming up off the water and the names still being read somewhere behind her, one after another, unhurried, a nation speaking itself back into the world at the speed of a lantern procession.

Then, downstream, around the dark shoulder of the bend, she saw lights she didn't recognize — a boat running without lanterns of its own, riding low, moving fast against custom and current both, heading for the weir.

She straightened. Some habits were permanent. She reached for her notebook, and stepped off the rail, and went to meet the river.
