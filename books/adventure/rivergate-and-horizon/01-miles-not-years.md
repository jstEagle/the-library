## 1. Miles, Not Years

The trouble with living under a borrowed name, Tamsin Dunmore had learned, was that eventually you answered to it faster than you would have answered to your own.

"Corven! Corven, you deaf old goat, the kettle's on!"

He looked up from his ledger with the mild surprise of a man who had genuinely forgotten he was called Corven. The name belonged, as far as he knew, to a clerk who had died of a fever in 1889 and left no relatives to object. Tamsin had found him in a parish register seven years ago and had been quietly wearing him ever since, the way a man wears a coat that isn't quite his size but keeps off the rain.

"I'm coming," he called back, in the flat northern vowels he'd practiced until they stopped feeling like a costume. "If the kettle's boiled dry again, Marta, I'm not responsible."

"You're always responsible. That's your whole personality." Marta Kessler, landlady of the Halfway House at Mile 114 of the closed line, stood in the doorway with her arms crossed and her gray braid swinging like a pendulum that kept honest time for the whole valley. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a bridge pier, and she had decided within a week of his arrival that the quiet surveyor who paid cash and asked no questions needed feeding whether he liked it or not.

He closed the ledger. Seven years of measurements lived in those pages: every rotted tie, every cracked pier, every inch the old trestles had sagged toward the river that had once made them rich. The Rivergate & Horizon Railroad had been dead since the disaster—officially abandoned, unofficially forgotten by everyone except the men who still walked it, counting what remained.

Tamsin counted better than anyone. It was, he sometimes thought bitterly, the one thing he'd never been able to stop doing.

* * *

The morning came up silver off the river, fog burning off in slow ribbons, and Tamsin set out along the ballast path with his level, his chain, and his notebook wrapped in oilcloth. This was the part of the work he loved without reservation—the part that had survived everything else. There was an honesty in decay. A bridge that had failed told you exactly why it had failed, in rivet rust and shear cracks and the particular lean of a pier whose footing the river had spent forty years undermining. Bridges didn't lie. Men did. He had learned the difference at a cost he preferred not to itemize before breakfast.

Mile 117 held the Kestrel Road trestle, a hundred and forty feet of timber and iron thrown across a gorge where the river ran green and cold even in August. He'd surveyed it twice already this year. In spring it had been dying gracefully, the way old things do when nobody's watching.

Today somebody was watching.

He saw the stakes from two hundred yards and stopped so abruptly that a grouse exploded out of the gorse beside him, offended.

Survey stakes. Fresh ones. Cedar, pale-cut, the bark still on the bottoms, driven plumb into the shoulder of the embankment at fifty-foot intervals. And painted—painted with a neat band of blue at the top, the kind of marking convention used by crews that expected their work to be read by other professionals.

Tamsin stood very still and let the unease arrive in its own time, the way weather does.

There were only three reasons anyone staked a dead line. One: salvage, pulling iron and timber out for scrap. But scrap crews didn't stake; they tore. Two: a new crossing, some road or pipeline wanting a cheap right-of-way over an old alignment. Possible. Three—

Three was the reason he didn't want, which was that someone was surveying the line the way *he* surveyed the line. Carefully. Thoroughly. As though something on it mattered enough to measure twice.

He walked the stakes slowly, reading them the way other men read letters. The spacing was fifty feet, standard. The elevations marked in chalk on each stake were correct—he checked three of them against his own notebook, and they were correct to the inch, which meant whoever drove them had run levels properly, with a proper instrument, by someone who understood why you checked back-sights.

And there, on the fourth stake, half worn by rain but unmistakable: a date. Three days old.

Beside the date, a crew mark. A letter and a number.

*D-7.*

Tamsin's hand went cold around his notebook. He told himself it was the river air. He was lying, and the river knew it, because the river had watched him lie to himself for seven years and had developed opinions on the subject.

Dunmore Survey. Crew designation seven. His own mark, from another life—the little code he'd chalked on every stake his crews ever drove, from the first cut above Grayling to the last grade below the falls. He'd invented it himself, a private vanity, a way of signing work he couldn't sign. Nobody else used it. Nobody else *knew* it.

Unless.

He crouched at the stake and looked a long time. The chalk could have faded wrong. The D could have been a B gone soft. He wanted very much for it to be a B.

It was not a B.

* * *

He worked the rest of the day anyway, because that was the discipline—if your hands stopped, your head started, and his head was a bad neighborhood after dark. So he shot his levels, chained his distances, recorded the trestle's continued decline with the tenderness of a man writing to a dying friend. The middle span had dropped another quarter inch. Pier three's cap beam had opened a crack you could have lost a coin in. He wrote it all down in his small, square engineer's hand, and all the while the blue-banded stakes stood along the embankment behind him like a row of patient strangers.

At noon he ate bread and hard cheese on the abutment and let himself think, because noon was the appointed hour for thinking and he kept appointments.

Seven years ago he had run from Rivergate with nothing but a rucksack, a forged reference, and the certain knowledge that if he stayed, he would hang for a crime he hadn't committed—or worse, be pardoned by men who wanted him grateful and silent. Eleven workers dead when the Grayling viaduct went down during a flood it should have ridden out standing. Eleven names he still said to himself on the first of every month, like rent. The inquiry had found negligence. The negligence had been his, officially. The truth had drowned somewhere in the paperwork, and the paperwork had been loaded onto a salvage steamer called the Meridian Star, and the Meridian Star had gone down in the lower reaches with everything aboard, and the river had closed its account.

So he had become Corven. Corven the careful, Corven the dull, Corven who mapped dead railroads for a land registry that paid late and asked less. It wasn't much of a life. But it was invisible, and invisibility, he had come to believe, was a form of safety the way a cellar is a form of architecture: dark, cramped, and entirely dependent on nobody digging.

Now somebody was driving stakes.

He tried the reasonable explanations again, lining them up like ties on a grade. Scrap assessment. Right-of-way study. A government inventory of abandoned assets—there had been talk of such things in the newspapers Marta brought up from town. All reasonable. All possible.

None of them explained the crew mark.

Because here was the thing about D-7: it wasn't just a mark. It was a signature inside a signature. He'd never written it anywhere public. It existed in his field books, in his crews' habits, in the memory of perhaps thirty men—and eleven of those were dead, and most of the rest would have spat at his name if they heard it, because as far as the world knew, Tamsin Dunmore had killed their brothers.

Which left a very short list. And every name on the short list frightened him in a different direction.

* * *

Marta had stew on when he got back, and she read him the way she always did, which was instantly and without mercy.

"Someone's walked over your grave," she said, ladling. "You've got the face."

"Do I have a face?"

"You've got six faces and I know them all. That's the one where the world's done arithmetic at you." She set the bowl down hard enough to make a point. "Stakes on your trestle, is it? Saw a boat go past yesterday evening, low and quiet, running without lights. Thought it was smugglers. We don't get many professionals up this stretch."

Tamsin's spoon paused for exactly half a second. Marta caught the half second. Marta caught everything; it was why the Halfway House had survived when the railroad died.

"Professionals," he said.

"Engineers," she said, watching him. "Or surveyors. Or men who want to be taken for such." She sat down across from him, folded her forearms on the table, and gave him the look she reserved for sick animals and stubborn men. "Corven. I've had you under my roof seven years. You pay fair, you fix my roof, you taught the Henderson boy his figures, and you flinch at the word *Rivergate* like it's a nail gun. I've never asked. I'm not asking now. But if there's weather coming up the valley, I'd take it kindly if you told me whether to board up windows."

The stew was good. It was always good. He ate three spoonfuls to buy time, which she allowed him, because she was generous in her tyrannical way.

"There are stakes on the Kestrel Road trestle," he said finally. "Fresh ones. Marked with a code only my old crews would use."

"My old crews," she repeated. "Not *the* crews."

"Marta."

"I'm only listening. You hear how I'm only listening." She refilled his bowl unasked. "Who's left? Of these crews."

"Good men, mostly. Some scattered. Some—" He stopped. *Some who testified against me.* Halloway's face rose unbidden, broad and tired and steady as a pier, the foreman who had stood in the inquiry room and said, in a voice like gravel poured slow, *I checked the drainage specs myself, and Dunmore signed off on them anyway.* Tamsin had believed, for one mad year afterward, that Halloway had been coerced. Then he'd stopped believing anything, because believing was how you got hurt.

"And the boat," Marta pressed. "Running dark. Going upstream. Toward the falls, I'd say, the way she was trimmed."

Upstream. Above Kestrel Falls lay the upper valley—the deep closed country, the flooded quarries, the old works. And somewhere in the drowned reaches below the falls, if the rumors that reached even the Halfway House were worth their salt, lay the wreck of the Meridian Star.

Tamsin put down his spoon.

"Marta," he said carefully, "what do you know about salvage ships?"

She raised one gray eyebrow. "What do *you* know about them, is the question, and by your face, too much." She stood, gathered his empty bowl, and paused at his shoulder long enough to say, quieter, "Whatever name you're wearing, lad, it fits you worse than it did. That's all I'll say. Stew's on the hob if you're up in the night."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat at his window with the lamp turned low and the notebook open to a page he'd never shown anyone—a list, eleven lines long, names and ages and the sections of viaduct each man had been working when the world came down. Ferro, the youngest, nineteen. Okafor, who sang while he spiked. Halloway's own nephew, which was a wound Tamsin still couldn't look at straight.

Outside, the river talked to itself in the dark, the way it always did, patient and enormous, keeping all its records.

He thought: *if they've staked the line, they'll work upstream next. The trestle at 117 is a base. From there, the falls. From the falls—*

From the falls, the wreck.

And if someone was measuring his old line with his old mark, then either a ghost was doing survey work—and he'd have welcomed the company—or one of thirty men was looking for something in the valley, and had chosen a signature guaranteed to reach one particular pair of eyes.

His.

The lamplight steadied. His hands, he noticed, had gone perfectly calm, which was how he knew it was serious. Fear made him fidget; dread made him precise.

He took out a fresh sheet and began, in the small square hand, to calculate. Distances to the falls. Tide windows on the lower river—he'd need to ask someone local, someone who knew the water, because seven years away had not made him a sailor. Money. Boots. The good instrument, not the registry's battered one.

Halfway down the page he stopped and laughed once, short and without humor, at the figure he'd drawn without thinking: a stake, banded blue, marked D-7.

The past had begun measuring him back. The only question—the question that walked the dark line outside his window all night, tie by tie, mile by mile—was whether it had come to bury him, or to tell him it had finally found the body of the truth, and needed help bringing it up.

By dawn he had his answer, or the shape of one, because there was a boat coming up the river through the morning fog, running slow, and it was stopping at the Halfway House.

And the woman at its tiller, when the mist peeled back far enough to show her—a lean, weathered pilot with a river-rat's squint and a harbor scarf knotted like a challenge—looked directly at the window where he stood, as if she had known exactly which pane of glass to find him behind.

"Corven!" Marta called up the stairs, cheerful and terrible. "There's a lady here says she needs a surveyor. Says it's urgent. Says—" a pause, and Marta's voice came again, slower, puzzled, carrying the weight of a word she didn't yet understand, "—says she's looking for the man who trained the Dunmore crew."
