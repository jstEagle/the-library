## 2. The Silence Above the Falls

The rain came in off the estuary that morning the way it always did in October—sideways, patient, and with an opinion about your choices. Tamsin Dunmore stood in the doorway of the survey hut at Mile Forty-One and watched it flatten the river to pewter, and he thought, not for the first time, that water was the most honest thing in the valley. It went where it was told by nothing but gravity and time.

He'd been Corren Vale for seven years now. The name fit like a coat bought secondhand: serviceable, unremarkable, and never quite warm enough at the shoulders. On paper he was a contract surveyor for the Northern Decay Assessment Board, a body so obscure that even its own clerks mispronounced it. In practice he walked dead rail lines and measured how fast the world forgot things.

He was good at forgetting too. That was the arrangement.

The kettle on the iron stove had just begun its low complaint when he heard the boat.

That was wrong, first of all. Nothing came upriver to Mile Forty-One. The closed lines ended here; the last scheduled steamer had stopped turning around at this bend four years ago, and even the fishermen gave the stretch above the old trestle a wide berth, mostly out of habit and partly because Tamsin had once, gently, spread a rumor about unexploded ordnance. He wasn't proud of the ordnance lie. He was proud of how well it worked.

He set down his cup and listened harder. Not an engine—a sweep and knock of oars, muffled by rain, coming up the current against all sense. Whoever it was rowed like they were angry at the water personally.

He stepped out onto the sagging platform of the hut and saw the boat resolve out of the gray: a flat-bottomed pilot skiff, tarred black, riding low, and at the oars a woman in a yellow oilskin slicker with the hood thrown back despite the rain, dark hair plastered flat, rowing with a long economy of motion that told him she'd done it ten thousand times and resented perhaps nine hundred of them.

She shipped oars ten feet out, looked up at him, and said, "You're Vale."

It wasn't a question, which was interesting, because he had gone to some lengths to make sure nobody asked questions.

"Depends who's asking," he said.

"Odile Brack." She said it the way other people said their blood type—useful information, no sentiment attached. "Harbor pilot, Rivergate. I need the man who trained the Dunmore Survey crew, and everyone from here to Kestrel Falls says that's you, Vale, whatever else you are."

The rain filled the silence between them. Tamsin felt the old cold move through his chest, the one that lived there permanently now, the way a draft lives in a house. *Dunmore.* Seven years since anyone had said that name to his face. He'd heard it secondhand plenty—whispered in harbor taverns when he passed through under the other name, spat in newspaper accounts he read once and burned. Eleven men dead when the Kestrel viaduct staging collapsed. The engineer who cut corners. The engineer who ran.

"Never heard of him," Tamsin said.

Odile Brack looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off her nose. Then she reached into the boat, pulled out an oilskin packet, and held it up.

"Then you won't want this," she said. "Radio log transcript, certified copy, from the relay station at Gull Narrows. Last transmission from the Dunmore crew, three days ago, before they went quiet." She turned it over in her hands, considering. "I could leave it with the assessment board office in Rivergate. They file things very thoroughly there. Things stay filed for years."

The kettle, inside, had come to a boil and was whistling like something small being murdered. Neither of them moved.

"You came three days' row to blackmail a stranger," Tamsin said.

"I came because I row past your hut twice a season and I've watched you check that trestle like a doctor checking a pulse, and doctors don't grieve over strangers." She tossed the packet onto the platform boards at his feet. It landed with a wet slap. "And because I don't have time for your pride. Read it or don't. But if you're not Dunmore, then I've wasted my tide, and if you are—" she paused, and something flickered behind her eyes that wasn't anger, though it lived next door to anger, sharing a wall— "then eleven families have been waiting seven years for you to stop hiding, and so has someone else."

* * *

He made tea because his hands needed a task that wasn't shaking. She sat across the little table from him, dripping politely onto his floor, and watched him with the frank appraisal of someone used to reading weather and men and finding both unreliable.

Tamsin opened the packet with fingers that were steadier than he'd expected them to be. Inside: a single typed sheet, relay-station letterhead, timestamped.

**14:22 — DUNMORE SURVEY (K.S. CAMP 2) TO GULL NARROWS RELAY:** Position confirmed above falls. Salvage vessel HORIZON anchored mid-channel, flying private house flag. Advance party ashore at wreck site. Requesting instructions re: documents recovered. Repeat, documents recovered. Will hold position until—

**14:24 — GULL NARROWS TO DUNMORE SURVEY:** Dunmore Survey, your signal is breaking up. Say again your last.

**14:26 — DUNMORE SURVEY:** —Horizon's party moving toward our camp. Halloway says pull back to the culvert line, we're pulling ba—

**14:27 — [TRANSMISSION ENDS]**

Three days ago. Three days of static where six voices used to be.

He knew those voices. He could put faces to every call sign. Halloway, his foreman, sixty years old and built like a bridge pier, who'd testified at the inquiry with his hat in his hands. Ferro, the youngest, nineteen when Tamsin left, who used to whistle off-key on the rope lines and couldn't be taught otherwise. The Voss brothers. Marta Ellery, who kept the levels and had once thrown a transit case at a contractor who lied to her about load ratings. Six people. His people. The only people left in the world who might still believe—if any of them still believed—that Tamsin Dunmore hadn't killed eleven men.

"They were working the wreck," Odile said quietly. "The Meridian Star. Off the record, off any permit. Looking for something in her."

"The inquiry records," Tamsin said. His voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "The Meridian Star carried the original inquiry files when she went down. Everyone said the records were lost. Everyone said it very quickly and very conveniently." He looked up. "Why is a salvage ship called Horizon sitting on her with a private house flag?"

"That," said Odile Brack, "is the question I rowed three days to ask you."

* * *

They talked while the rain kept on. She told him what Rivergate knew, which was little, and what Rivergate suspected, which was more. The Horizon had appeared above the falls eight days ago without filing a salvage claim, which was either arrogance or protection, and both flavors meant money. Her harbor master had signed her clearance papers without blinking, which told Odile everything about whose pocket the harbor master's hand was in. And the Dunmore Survey crew had gone upriver three weeks back on a legitimate decay-assessment contract—the same board Tamsin nominally worked for, which was either coincidence or a thread worth pulling—and had simply drifted, day by day, toward the wreck site, as if the survey were a pretext and had been from the start.

"They were looking for you," Tamsin said slowly. "Or for what you could find. Halloway—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Halloway testified against me. Why would he—"

"Maybe he was sorry," Odile said. "Sorry men do strange work. Or maybe he knew something seven years ago and waited until it was safe to go digging, and it's only safe now because whoever buried it thinks everyone's forgotten." She turned her cup in her hands. "People forget. Rivers don't. Everything the Meridian Star carried is still down there in the mud, waiting for somebody with a reason."

"And your reason?" Tamsin asked. "You're a pilot. This isn't your river to dig up."

For the first time, Odile Brack looked away from him. Out the rain-blurred window, toward the south, downriver, toward Rivergate, though from here you couldn't see anything but gray.

"My brother," she said, "was a rigger on the Kestrel staging. Third shift." She said it evenly, the way you'd report a depth sounding. "He was twenty-four. They gave our mother a form to fill out for compensation and the form had a section asking whether the deceased had contributed to structural negligence, and our mother, who raised two children and could gut a fish in nine seconds, wrote across it in ink: *ask the engineer.*"

Tamsin's tea went cold in his hands.

"So yes," Odile said, meeting his eyes again, and now the thing next door to anger had come fully into the room and was standing there with its arms crossed. "I know exactly who you are, Tamsin Dunmore. I've known for two years—I watch the way a man walks, and engineers walk like they're checking the ground's arithmetic. I could have sold your name a dozen times. There are men in Rivergate who'd pay well for it." She let that sit. "I didn't. Because a part of me wanted you to be guilty, and another part of me—the bigger part, some days—wanted you to be innocent, because if you're innocent, then my brother wasn't killed by one cowardly man. He was killed by a machine. And machines can be broken."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the river went on doing its honest work.

"If I'm innocent," Tamsin said carefully, "why did I run?"

"That," said Odile Brack, "is the question I've been carrying for two years, and I suggest you answer it for yourself before you answer it for me." She stood, slicker rustling, and gathered her hood. "Here's what matters tonight. Your crew is silent for three days. The Horizon sits on the wreck with armed advance parties and a financier's flag. Whatever they found—whatever Halloway found for you—they're holding it, or holding your people, or both. And in—" she glanced at the window, calculating tides the way other people checked watches— "six tides, the main salvage fleet comes upriver with cranes big enough to take the Meridian Star apart rivet by rivet. After that, whatever's in her hull belongs to whoever paid for the cranes."

"Six tides," Tamsin repeated.

"Six." She looked at him, and beneath the flint there was something almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "So. Engineer. You can stay here and keep measuring how fast the world forgets things. That's a life. Some men would call it a good one." She paused at the door, rain crowding in behind her. "Or you can remember that six people who trusted you are sitting above Kestrel Falls not answering their radio, and decide what kind of man checks on that."

* * *

After she left—she said she'd anchor below the point until dawn, and he believed her, because she struck him as a woman who did precisely what she said in the precise order she said it—Tamsin stood alone in the hut with the transcript and the rain and the long habit of seven years.

His instinct said what it had always said: *stay hidden.* Stay hidden and live. It was a good instinct. It had kept him breathing through seven winters. Every survival he owned had been purchased with that instinct, and the interest on it was this hut, this false name, these empty miles where the only creature who knew his face was a heron with poor manners.

But his conscience had never once obeyed his instinct. That was the trouble with consciences. They kept their own books.

He thought about Halloway saying *we're pulling ba*—cut off mid-word, mid-thought, mid-retreat. He thought about Ferro, nineteen and whistling, somewhere in the fog above the falls with a radio full of static. He thought about Marta Ellery's transit case and the Voss brothers finishing each other's sentences and six sets of boots going quiet all at once, three days ago, in country he knew better than anyone alive.

And underneath all of it, quieter and heavier, he thought about the word *documents.* Documents recovered. After seven years of telling himself the truth was drowned beyond reach, after building an entire invisible life on the foundation that the river had swallowed the proof along with the men—it was sitting up there in the fog, forty miles north, and men with a private flag were standing on it.

If he stayed, the Horizon would take it apart rivet by rivet, and the eleven would stay murderers' victims, and he would stay their murderer, forever, comfortably, invisibly, alive.

Tamsin Dunmore looked at the map on his wall—the closed lines, the dead locks, the condemned bridges he'd inspected himself and privately memorized—and felt something in his chest unlock that had been rusted shut for seven years. It hurt. It hurt like a joint remembering motion.

He took down the chart of the upper river, weighted its corners with tin cups, and began, in pencil, to calculate.

Outside, the rain eased. Below the point, a single riding lamp swung at anchor, patient as a promise. Six tides, the pilot had said.

Tamsin counted the first one going out, and picked up his pencil.
