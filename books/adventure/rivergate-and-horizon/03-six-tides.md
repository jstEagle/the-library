## 3. Six Tides

The chart Odile Brack unrolled across my kitchen table was older than either of us, and it had been annotated by someone who thought in pencil and erased in anger. Tide tables were scrawled up one margin in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. Somebody had cared about this river the way you care about a difficult relative—exhaustively, and with occasional profanity.

"That would be my father," Odile said, catching me looking. "He piloted this stretch for forty years. He used to say the Kestrel didn't have tides so much as moods."

"And which mood is it in now?"

"The kind that kills the careless." She tapped the chart with two fingers. "Sit down, Dunmore. Or whatever you're calling yourself these days. This will take a while, and we don't have much of it."

I sat. It's remarkable how quickly a man can learn to sit when the alternative is standing in front of something he doesn't want to look at.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the tide above Kestrel Falls before that morning: nothing. Here is what I knew about it after an hour with Odile Brack and her father's chart: enough to be frightened, which she assured me was the correct amount.

"Everyone thinks the falls are the problem," she said. "They're not. The falls are honest. You can see them coming from a mile off, roaring their heads off like a drunk uncle at a wedding. The problem is the estuary below them." She traced a long finger down the river's lower reach, where the channel widened into a braided sprawl of sandbars and drowned forest. "Salt water comes up this far twice a day and meets the current coming down. When they fight, the whole middle stretch turns into moving soup. When the tide drops, the bars surface, the channels braid apart, and half of them close entirely."

"So there are windows."

"There are windows." She drew six small crosses along the chart's edge. "Six usable low-tide windows between here and the wreck site. Each one lasts about ninety minutes, if the weather behaves, which it won't. Miss a window and you don't just lose time—you lose the channel. The bars shift. A channel that was twelve feet deep at Tuesday's low water might be four feet by Thursday's. You'd be navigating by memory, and your memory is seven years old."

"I've been gone five years," I said, before I could stop myself correcting the record. Old habits. A man who lives under a borrowed name gets very precise about small true things; they're the only currency he has.

Odile looked at me for a moment too long. "Five years, then. Either way, the river doesn't keep records. It keeps scores."

I got up and made tea, mostly because my hands wanted employment. The kettle on my woodstove was dented copper, salvaged from a line shack, and it whistled flat, like everything else in my life that I hadn't gotten around to fixing. While it heated, I did what I always do with a problem: I took it apart.

* * *

Let me explain how an engineer thinks about a river, because it isn't the way a pilot thinks about one, and the difference mattered that morning more than either of us knew.

A pilot reads a river as a living thing—a creature with habits and tempers, to be coaxed and flattered. An engineer reads it as a system. Inputs and outputs. The tide is a pump on a schedule. The current is a gradient doing arithmetic. And the locks—the eleven locks of the old Rivergate flight, built by men with slide rules and unreasonable confidence—are machines sitting in the middle of that system, machines that obey laws whether or not anyone remembers them.

The locks had been closed for six years. Officially, the reason was structural. Unofficially—and I say this as the man who wrote the unofficial inspection reports under a name that wasn't his—the reason was money, indifference, and the fact that nobody important ever needed to move anything heavy upriver anymore. The gates still stood. The machinery still existed. Machinery doesn't stop being machinery just because the government forgets about it.

"You're doing the thing with your eyes," Odile said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at a wall like it owes you an explanation." She accepted the tea I handed her, blew across it. "You've thought of something."

"Maybe." I sat back down. "How fast does the salvage fleet travel?"

"Freight pace. They're dragging dredges and a crane barge. Call it four knots against the current, slower through the narrows."

"And they left when?"

"Two days ago. Word came down from the harbor master." Something crossed her face when she said *harbor master*, quick as a fish under ice, but I was too deep in my own arithmetic to read it properly. That failure cost me later. Note it down.

"Four knots against the spring current," I said, pulling the chart toward me. "That puts them at the estuary mouth on the fourth tide. Through the braids on the fifth. At the wreck—" I measured with my thumb, then with a pencil, because thumbs lie, "—at the wreck by the sixth low water. Maybe earlier if they run lights at night."

"So we have until the sixth tide."

"We have less. They'll want to work the wreck at low water same as us, which means they'll push to arrive ahead of a window, not during one. Call it five and a half tides." I looked up. "And we're leaving when?"

"Within the hour, if you've got any sense."

"Then we get three windows' worth of head start, roughly, if we can hold pace. Which we can't, because your boat—"

"*My* boat," Odile said, with the mild menace of a woman discussing her child, "is the *Kestrel Lark*, thirty-two feet, shallow draft, and faster than anything Crane's fleet owns in skinny water. What she lacks in dignity she makes up in stubbornness."

"I wasn't going to insult her."

"You were going to suggest she couldn't make the windows."

"I was going to ask whether her engine has been serviced recently."

"She made the trip down to fetch you, didn't she?"

"Downstream," I said. "Downstream is gravity's opinion. Upstream is engineering."

She laughed—one short bark, surprised out of her—and for a moment the kitchen felt almost warm, almost ordinary, almost like a life where a man could sit at his own table with a cup of tea and argue about boats without the word *wreck* sitting in the corner like an unpaid debt.

Then she said, "So tell me about the locks," and the warmth went out of the room like a door opening onto February.

* * *

Here is the shape of the problem, laid out plainly, the way I wish someone had once laid things out plainly for me:

Eleven locks climbing the Rivergate valley, spaced along twenty miles of river, each one a stone chamber with wooden gates at either end and gearing to raise and lower them. Closed means closed officially—chains on the windlasses, notices nailed up, the keeper's cottages empty. But chains rust. Notices blow away. And the gates themselves, if the balance weights haven't seized, can be worked by hand. Slowly. By people who know which gears to grease and which ones to leave alone, and who aren't afraid of the dark inside a lock chamber at two in the morning.

Three of the eleven worried me. Lock Four, because its upper gate had been listing when I last saw it, and a listing gate is a gate deciding whether to fall. Lock Seven, because it sat directly beneath the old quarry tramway, and quarries shed rock the way dogs shed fur. And Lock Nine—

Lock Nine I'll come back to. Every story has a lock nine.

"If we take the river proper," I said, marking the channels, "we race the fleet through open water where their size stops mattering and ours starts. If we take the locks, we climb while they crawl, and we come out above them." I looked at the numbers again, hoping they'd changed. They hadn't. "It's the locks or nothing."

"Nobody's been through the flight since it closed."

"Somebody's been through parts of it. There's fresh grease on the survey stakes up at the trestle, and grease doesn't walk there on its own." I turned the pencil over in my fingers. "Which brings me to the question you've been avoiding asking me since you walked in."

"Ask it yourself."

"Why me? You found me. You knew where I was, which means somebody told you, which means somebody knows I'm alive and where I live, and you want me to lead you upriver through country I spent five years learning to disappear into. That's not a favor. That's a purchase. So what am I buying, and what's it costing?"

Odile drank her tea. Outside, the river made its endless argument with the bank, patient as erosion. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone quiet in a way that made me think of load-bearing walls.

"Your crew is up there. The men you trained. Three days silent." She set the cup down with a click. "I don't know yet why that matters to you. I'll find out—I make it my business to find out things. But I watched your face when I said Ferro's name, and whatever you're hiding, it isn't from them."

I said nothing. There was a version of me, seven years younger, who would have said something clever. He'd died somewhere on a night train out of Rivergate with a false name in his pocket and eleven names in his head that wouldn't stop counting themselves.

"The sixth tide," I said instead. "Then."

"The sixth tide," she agreed. "After that, the fleet arrives, and whatever's left of your crew and whatever's aboard that wreck belongs to Aldous Crane." She stood, gathered her chart, and rolled it with the crisp efficiency of someone who had done ten thousand small hard tasks without applause. "Get your kit. Bring grease, spare pins for the windlass gears, and a lamp that doesn't smoke. And Dunmore—"

"Tamsin," I said. "If we're doing this, use the name. I'm tired of hearing myself called something I invented."

A flicker of something—approval, maybe, or pity, or the professional respect of one careful person for another—moved behind her eyes. "Tamsin, then. One more thing about the tides. My father used to say the river gives you exactly enough time and not one minute more, and that the men who drown are the ones who argue with him about the extra minute."

"Your father sounds like he'd have made a good engineer."

"He'd have made a terrible one. Engineers believe things can be fixed." She shouldered her bag and paused at the door, silhouetted against the gray light coming off the water. "Pilots know better. We just try to be somewhere else when it breaks."

* * *

I packed in twenty minutes, which is the advantage of living like a man ready to run. Spare shirt, oilskin, tools wrapped in canvas, the little tin box of papers that constituted my entire legal existence—such as it was—and one item that had no practical value whatsoever: a photograph, creased soft from handling, of eleven men squinting on a scaffold platform the summer before the collapse. I looked at it for exactly as long as I could bear, which was three seconds, and put it in my breast pocket, over the heart, where useless things belong.

At the door I stopped and did the last calculation, the one I'd been circling all morning the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

Going upriver meant being seen. Meant leaving tracks, spending fuel, existing in the world's ledger again after five years of staying off it. Somewhere in Rivergate or above it, men who had built their fortunes on my ruin would learn that Tamsin Dunmore was moving toward them, and they would do what such men always do. The invisible life I'd built—small, gray, safe as a culvert—ended at the moment I stepped onto Odile's deck. I understood that completely. Understanding a thing and accepting it are different trades, and I stood in my doorway for a long minute trying to strike the exchange rate.

Then I thought of Ferro, who'd been nineteen when I taught him to read a grade stake, who used to whistle off-key on the morning runs, who was up there somewhere in the silence above the falls. Eleven men had died because I signed off on a trestle I'd been lied to about, and I had carried that arithmetic alone for seven years. I would not carry a twelfth.

I shut the door. I did not lock it. Some part of me, cold and clear as the water under the trestle, understood that I was never coming back to this house as the man who lived in it.

The *Kestrel Lark* sat low on the gray water, engine already ticking over, Odile at the wheel with her collar up against the spray. The first low-tide window opened in forty minutes. Five and a half tides to the wreck. Sixty-some hours of river, fog, rusted machinery, and whatever waited at the top of the valley wearing my past like a coat.

"About time," Odile said, and threw off the bowline.

We slid out into the current, and the river—my old enemy, my old employer, my oldest witness—took us the way it takes everything: without comment, and keeping count.
