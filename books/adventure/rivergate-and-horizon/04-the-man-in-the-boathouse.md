## 4. The Man in the Boathouse

Odile Brack had been running the *Kestrel's Daughter* out of Rivergate harbor for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about the man who called himself Merrick Vane.

First: he paid his mooring fees on time, in cash, and never haggled.

Second: he could name every rivet on the old trestle bridge but claimed not to know what year it was built.

Third—and this was the one that had kept her awake some nights—he walked like a man who used to be somebody. Not swaggering. The opposite of swaggering. A man who had once stood tall in front of crowds and now made a profession of taking up as little of the world as possible.

She'd known his real name since the second winter.

It had happened by accident, the way most important things do. She'd come to the boathouse at dawn to collect a debt—three months of slip rent, because even ghosts have landlords—and found him asleep at his drafting table, one arm curled around a set of blueprints like a man embracing a sleeping child. On the corner of the topmost sheet, half-hidden under a coffee tin full of pencils, was an old inspection stamp. *T. DUNMORE, PRINCIPAL ENGINEER.* The ink had faded to the color of weak tea, but she'd have recognized it blindfolded. Her brother Colm had talked about Tamsin Dunmore the way sailors talk about weather—constantly, reverently, and with a great deal of complaint.

She had stood there for a long moment with her hand on the door, watching him sleep, doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish. Eleven men dead in the Kestrel Line collapse. One engineer fled. And here was the engineer, seven years later, renting a boat slip from the sister of victim number six, paying cash, keeping his head down, fixing her hull fittings without being asked.

She had closed the door very quietly and gone home and not told anyone, and she had hated herself a little for both halves of that sentence.

* * *

Now he sat across from her in the boathouse loft, lamplight catching the gray coming into his temples, and listened while she laid it out for him—the silence above the falls, the Horizon, the last garbled transmission from his own crew. She watched his face do something complicated when she said the word *Horizon*, like a lock mechanism engaging somewhere behind his eyes.

"You understand what I'm telling you," she said. "Your men stopped answering three days ago. The salvage fleet is already moving upriver. And you're sitting here drinking my coffee."

"I'm listening," he said.

"Listening isn't deciding." She leaned forward. "I've watched you decide things, Mr. Vane. Which rivets need replacing. Whether a storm warrants double lines. You decide fast and you don't look back. So decide."

He turned his cup slowly on its saucer, a quarter turn, then another. "You said their last transmission mentioned the Horizon by name."

"By name and by bearing. Above Kestrel Falls, holding station near the old channel marker." She recited it from memory; she'd played the relay recording eleven times. "Then static. Then nothing."

"And the salvage fleet—whose flag?"

"Private house flag. Blue field, white crane." She watched him flinch, just barely, at the word *crane*. Interesting. "You know them."

"I know the bird," he said carefully.

"Mm." She let that sit. There were layers to this man like there were layers to the river silt, and she'd learned you couldn't dredge all of them at once or you'd foul your own propeller. "Here's my problem, Mr. Vane. Or should I say—"

"Don't." It came out flat and quick, and for the first time since she'd known him, Merrick Vane looked genuinely afraid. Not of her, exactly. Of the word itself. Of hearing it spoken aloud in a room where anyone might be listening through the walls.

"Tamsin," she said anyway, softly, because seven years was long enough for a word to go unsaid in her presence. "Dunmore."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere outside, a gull complained about the economics of existence. He didn't deny it. She'd wondered, sometimes, what he'd do—whether he'd laugh, bluff, reach for some elaborate fiction about clerical error. He did none of those things. He just set down the cup and looked at her with the weary steadiness of a man who has been waiting seven years for a bill to come due.

"How long?" he asked.

"Second winter. You fell asleep over your drawings. Your inspection stamp was showing." She shrugged. "Colm used to talk about you. Dunmore this, Dunmore that. Best engineer on the line. Taught us all to read a stress diagram before we could read a tide table. I'd have known that stamp if it were written in Sanskrit."

Something moved through his face—grief, maybe, or its distant cousin. "Colm Brack," he said. "Rigger. Second crew. He liked to sing while he worked. Badly."

"He sang beautifully," Odile said, "and everyone pretended otherwise because he was enormous and sweet and would have been crushed if we told him the truth. Which is more or less how he died, too. Crushed. Under forty tons of span that wasn't supposed to fall."

The words came out level. She'd practiced them level for seven years, in front of mirrors, in front of magistrates, in front of the inquiry board that had nodded sympathetically and blamed the engineer who wasn't in the room to defend himself.

Tamsin Dunmore went very still. "You knew," he said slowly. "All this time. You knew who I was, and what I was accused of, and you rented me a slip anyway."

"I needed the money," she said, which was true and also completely beside the point, and they both knew it.

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to see what you'd do." She got up and paced to the loft window, where the harbor lay gray and patient under the early light. "The inquiry said you cut corners. Said you falsified the load calculations to rush the span. Eleven men went into the river because Tamsin Dunmore wanted his bonus. That was the finding. And then Tamsin Dunmore vanished, which the inquiry took as a confession, and the families took as an insult, and I took—" She stopped. Started again. "I took personally.

"But then I watched you. Seven years. And I kept thinking, either this is the finest long con ever performed by a guilty man, or—" She turned back to face him. "Or the inquiry was wrong, and the man who actually killed my brother is still walking around free, wearing a good coat, and my brother's engineer has been living in a boathouse eating tinned fish because the truth had nowhere to live."

Tamsin's voice came out rough. "That's quite a speech."

"I rehearse."

"What did you conclude?"

"That I don't know." She said it plainly, because he deserved plainness even if he didn't deserve comfort. "That's the honest answer, Dunmore. I've had seven years to make up my mind and I haven't. Some nights I think you're innocent and it breaks my heart. Some nights I think you're the best liar on the coast and it breaks everything else."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it the way he seemed to absorb everything—like load readings, like structural reports, weighing and filing. "And yet you brought me news of my crew. You came all the way up from the harbor at dawn to tell me men I trained are missing above the falls."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because whatever you are," Odile said, "your men believed in you enough to go looking into that wreck, and now they're silent, and someone with a crane on their flag is moving upriver toward them. And because—" Here she paused, because the next part had taken her all night to assemble and it still didn't fit together properly. "Because if there's even a chance the truth about the collapse is aboard that salvage ship, then I want it. Not for you. For Colm. For the other ten families. They've spent seven years being told their husbands and brothers died because of a bad engineer, and if that's a lie, I will drag the proof out of that river with my own hands."

The silence between them stretched, filled with gull noise and the creak of hulls against pilings.

"So we're not allies," Tamsin said at last.

"We're not anything," Odile agreed. "We're two people pointed at the same wreck for different reasons, sharing a boat because the river doesn't care why you're on it. You want your name back. I want my brother's death to mean something besides a magistrate's signature. Those aren't the same thing, and if it comes down to choosing, I'll choose Colm every time, and you can hate me for it."

"I wouldn't hate you for it," he said quietly. "I'd probably agree with you."

"Don't agree with me. It's unsettling."

Despite everything—a dead brother, a fugitive, a missing crew, a salvage fleet with a private flag—she felt the corner of her mouth twitch. Seven years of watching this man, and she'd forgotten he could almost be funny. It was disorienting, like seeing a lighthouse blink in a color it shouldn't.

"There's a practical matter," she said, businesslike again, because feelings were fine but boats ran on logistics. "You'll need a pilot. The closed locks above Grayling Bend haven't run properly since the line shut down, and the falls channels change with every season's flood. You could no more find your way to Kestrel Falls than a cat could file its own taxes."

"Could you?"

"I grew up poling barges through that valley before I could swim, which my mother considered poor planning. Yes, I can get us there." She held up a finger before he could speak. "But understand the terms. I'm not ferrying you. I'm going after that evidence, and you happen to be the only person alive who knows what it looks like and where it might be stowed. You're cargo with opinions."

"A fair description," he said. "Most people have been less generous."

"The fee," she continued, "is passage, my fuel, and first claim on whatever documents concern the collapse. You get your name back or your vindication or whatever it is you're after—I honestly don't care—but the families get the proof. Agreed?"

For a moment he looked at her with an expression she couldn't parse, something old and tired and grateful all tangled together, and she thought: there it is. Whatever he is, guilty or framed or something stranger in between, that look isn't a liar's look. Liars don't look relieved to be caught.

"Agreed," he said.

"Then shake on it, and don't call me Captain unless we're sinking."

They shook. His grip was dry and strong, an engineer's grip, calibrated. She noticed he hesitated half a heartbeat before taking her hand—as if he understood exactly what it meant for Colm Brack's sister to touch the hands the inquiry had blamed—and she noticed, too, that he did it anyway. That counted for something. She wasn't sure how much. She'd know better by Kestrel Falls.

"One more thing," she said, shrugging into her oilskin. "The man you're pretending to be—Vane. He stays ashore. If we're doing this, I'm not shouting 'Merrick' across a deck while armed salvagers are shooting at us. I'll trip over my own tongue and get us both drowned."

"Understood." He pulled his coat off its hook, and she saw the way his shoulders settled under it—not the apologetic stoop of the invisible man, but something straighter, older, like a structure remembering its designed load. "Though I'll warn you, I haven't answered to Tamsin Dunmore in seven years. It may take me a while to come when called."

"Take all the time you like above Grayling Bend," Odile said, shouldering open the loft door onto the gray morning and the smell of the river. "After that, you answer fast. Because I've seen the tide tables, Dunmore, and I've done the sums, and we have five low-water windows before the salvage fleet reaches the falls—which means we leave within the hour, and you're going to show me a way through the closed locks that doesn't exist on any chart I own."

He paused in the doorway beside her, looking out at the water as if it were an old colleague he'd left on bad terms.

"The locks," he said quietly, "were built to my specifications. Every gear, every gate. If anyone alive can take us through them, it's the man they were built for."

"That," said Odile Brack, stepping out into the rain, "is either very reassuring or deeply ominous, and I haven't decided which."

Behind them, the boathouse lamp guttered and went out, and the river waited.
