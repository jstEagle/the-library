## 5. Closed Lines

The locks above Marrow Bend had been closed for nine years, and the river had spent every one of those years trying to take them back.

Tamsin stood on the upper gate of Lock One with his lantern shuttered to a coin of light, listening to the water work at the masonry like a patient animal licking a bone. Moss had climbed the walls in green velvet ropes. Alders grew straight out of the weep-holes, thin and stubborn as whiskers on an old man's chin. Somewhere below, in the dark throat of the chamber, the water made a sound he remembered from his first year out here alone—a slow, swallowed breathing, as if the lock were asleep and dreaming of ships.

"You're smiling," Odile said from the boat. She said it the way other people accused.

"I'm not."

"Your voice does something when you smile. Goes up at the corners." She was coiling rope with the economy of a woman who had never once wasted a motion in her life, or if she had, she'd buried the evidence. "Nine years sealed and you look at this rusted coffin of a lock like it's an old dog that waited up for you."

"It did wait up." He crouched, laid his palm flat against the gate timber. Wet, but sound. The iron straps beneath were another matter—he could smell the rot in them, that sweetish blood-rust tang. "Gate's good for one more opening. Maybe two. After that she's firewood."

"And after that?"

"After that we walk."

Odile snorted and went back to her coils. That was the thing about her he was still learning: she didn't argue with bad news. She just filed it away somewhere behind her eyes, in whatever drawer she kept the memory of her brother, and moved on to the next practical thing. He found it restful, in the way that standing next to a loaded winch is restful—so long as you respect what it is.

Above them the valley held its breath. No moon worth mentioning; a lid of cloud the color of wet slate, and under it the river running high with snowmelt, loud enough to cover the sound of their engine if they kept it low. Three days since Odile had come down the closed lines to find him with news of the Dunmore Survey crew gone silent above Kestrel Falls. Two days since he'd stood in his own boathouse among his borrowed tools and his borrowed name and understood, with the calm of a man reading his own blueprint, that hiding had stopped being an option the moment those men stopped answering.

Now there was a countdown instead of a life. Six tides until the salvage fleet reached the wreck. They intended to be there for the fourth.

* * *

Lock One opened the way old things open—reluctantly, then all at once, with a groan that went through Tamsin's boots and up into his teeth. He worked the handgear by feel mostly, because the lantern stayed shuttered, and his hands remembered these mechanisms better than his eyes ever had. Seven years of walking dead rail lines will teach you a man's hands things his head is afraid to know.

The boat slid into the chamber and the gates closed behind them, and for a few minutes they existed in a stone box full of river, dropping through the dark while Tamsin cracked the sluices a hand's width at a time and listened to the pitch of the outflow the way a doctor listens to a chest.

"Too fast," Odile said once.

"I know." He eased the paddle back. "Left sluice's undercut. Feel it?"

She paused. "I feel the boat wanting to lie to me about where it is."

"That's the undercut." He adjusted again, and the shudder smoothed out of the hull. "Whoever built these locks knew what they were doing. Whoever abandoned them didn't care. There's a difference, and you can hear it."

"You always talk this much when you're working?"

"Only when I'm happy."

"Hm," said Odile, which from her was practically applause.

They dropped through Lock One, poled the half-mile of stillwater between chambers with the engine off and the night enormous around them, and came to Lock Two. This one fought him. The culverts were silted half-shut and the gate gear had swollen with nine years of rain, and twice he had to stop and simply breathe against the mechanism, coaxing it, apologizing to it under his breath in the language engineers use for machines they love and have failed.

"You talk to it," Odile observed from the bow, poling them true against the inflow current.

"It talks first."

"What's it saying?"

"That it's tired. That nobody visited." He threw his weight on the lever and the gate gave with a crack like a knuckle. "There. See? Just lonely."

* * *

It was between Lock Two and Lock Three, in the long cut where the canal ran between walls of cut granite with the stars showing in a narrow ribbon overhead, that Tamsin began to notice the wrongness.

He noticed it the way he'd learned to notice everything out here—not all at once, but as an accumulation of small debts the landscape owed his expectations. A mooring ring polished bright where it should have been furred with moss. A drag mark in the towpath mud, fresh, the edges still sharp-edged and dark with unweathered earth. And then, floating in the black water of the cut, turning slowly: a sliver of wood, white-cut, recent.

He brought the pole up and fished it out. Pine. Saw-fresh. Someone had cut timber here within days.

"Odile."

She was beside him before he finished the word. That was another thing about her; she heard a change in a man's voice the way she heard a change in an engine.

He showed her the sliver. Then he shuttered the lantern entirely and let his eyes go to the dark, and after a minute the dark gave up its shapes: the cut walls, the towpath, the looming silhouette of Lock Three's upper gates a hundred yards on—and, on the gates themselves, where no light should be, a faint wrongness in the outline. Something rigged. Something waiting.

They tied the boat in silence. They went forward in silence, Tamsin first, moving along the towpath with the particular soft-footed care of a man who has spent seven years being nobody. At the head of Lock Three he put his hand on the gate beam and felt it trembling faintly, and understood why.

The paddles were wired open. Both of them, wired wide, so that any boat entering the chamber would be caught in a churning cross-current with no way to control its descent—the kind of trap that doesn't need to kill you outright, just needs to put you against the masonry at speed and let the river finish the arithmetic.

And the gears—the great iron rack-and-pinion gears that drove the gates, gears Tamsin had oiled with his own hands the last season before everything went to ruin—were stripped. Not worn. Stripped. Teeth sheared off deliberately, the cuts clean and recent, done by someone with tools and time and a reason.

He crouched there a long moment with his fingers resting in the ruined gearing, and the cold that went through him had nothing to do with the river air.

"This isn't decay," he said quietly. "Decay takes the weakest part first. This took the parts that matter." He looked up at Odile, a pale shape against the darker sky. "Someone did this on purpose. Recently. Days."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning someone wanted these locks unusable—or usable only by them. Meaning—" and here the sentence arrived at the place he'd been circling, the place his stomach had already been for ten minutes, "—meaning someone knows the closed lines are open tonight. Someone knows someone is coming upriver."

Odile was quiet. In the dark he heard her breathing change, slow and deliberate, the way she breathed when she was choosing not to say the first thing she thought.

"The fleet," she said finally. "Scouts ahead of the salvage fleet. It wouldn't be hard. Buy a harbor clerk, buy a pilot, find out who left Marrow Bend in a fast boat with a shut engine."

"No." He shook his head, though she couldn't see it well. "This isn't aimed at the fleet. Look at the wiring on the paddles—that's a trap for a single boat coming through at night, not a barricade against a squadron. And the gears—" His hand found the sheared teeth again. "You'd strip gears to stop a fleet? No. You'd blow the gates. This is finer than that. This is a door locked against one visitor."

"Then who—"

"I don't know." And that was the truth, and the truth sat in his chest like swallowed river-stone. Seven years he had built his invisibility the way he built everything: load by load, calculated, redundant. Nobody knew the routes he walked. Nobody knew the boathouse. Nobody except—

Except a river pilot who had appeared at his door three days ago carrying exactly the news required to put him in a boat.

He hated himself for the thought even as it formed, and said it anyway, because seven years ago men had died because people hadn't said the suspicious things aloud.

"Odile. Who else knows I'm on the river?"

The silence lasted four seconds. He counted them against his heartbeat, the way he counted anything that mattered.

"My harbor master," she said at last. "He saw me take on stores. He asked where I was bound and I told him a survey run, which was even true." A pause. "Tamsin. I've known your name since before you gave it to me. If I meant to sell you, I'd have gotten a better price by now."

"I believe you." And the strange thing was that he did—or believed her enough, which out here was the same currency. "But somebody talked. Somebody always talks. That's how eleven men ended up under a collapsed trestle and my name on the blame—not one big betrayal, just a dozen small ones, each one reasonable, each one deniable."

Below them, in the trapped chamber of Lock Three, the cross-current thundered through the wired-open paddles, hungry and patient.

"So what do we do?" Odile asked. "Go back?"

He looked at the ruined gears. He looked at the dark water. He thought about Halloway, his old foreman, and the survey crew gone silent above the falls, and young Ferro who couldn't have been more than twenty-two, and the six-tide countdown ticking away in the dark like something mechanical and merciless.

"We do what the river does," he said. "We don't use the door. We make our own."

He was already measuring the lock wall with his eye—height, soundness, the old crane pad on the towpath side where maintenance crews had once swung gate timbers—as he spoke, and the engineer in him was laying out the problem the way it laid out all problems: as forces, loads, and chances. But underneath the engineering, quieter and colder, a new thing had settled into him, and he recognized it because he'd felt it last seven years ago, the night the trestle came down and his life with it.

Fear. Not of the river. The river he understood.

Someone ahead of him in the dark had studied these locks the way he studied locks. Someone had known precisely which teeth to shear to stop a man like him.

And that meant that somewhere upriver, past the falls, past the wreck, past the counting tides—someone was waiting, and had been for longer than three days.

"Get the block and tackle," Tamsin said. "We're going over the top."
