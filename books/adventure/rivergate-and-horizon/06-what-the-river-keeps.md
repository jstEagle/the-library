## 6. What the River Keeps

The cache was supposed to be under a leaning slab of granite shaped like a sleeping dog, forty paces above the waterline on the north bank of the gorge. Odile knew it because every pilot who worked the upper river knew it. The Dunmore Survey crews had used the same caches for years—waterproofed crates tucked into the stone, stocked against the day the radio died or the weather turned mean. River people left things for each other that way. It was one of the few laws Odile still believed in.

Except this cache had been opened like a can of sardines.

She crouched beside the displaced slab and ran her fingers along the edges of the crate beneath it. The lid had been pried off with something flat and impatient—a boat hook, maybe, or a crowbar. The wax seal that should have kept the river out lay in brittle crumbs. Inside, the stores were wrecked in the particular way that told her everything: not looted, *searched*. Tins flung to the back of the cavity. Blankets slashed open, their stuffing bleeding out like the stone had been gutted. A first-aid kit emptied of its morphine and its bandages both, which struck her as the work of men who wanted no witnesses and no survivors.

"They were looking for something," she said.

Behind her, Dunmore—or Voss, or whatever name he was wearing today; she'd stopped keeping track somewhere around the second lock—stood very still, the way he did when his mind went somewhere cold. He didn't touch anything. He read the site instead, top to bottom, the way other men read a page.

"Three days," he said finally. "Radio's been silent three days. This happened before that."

"How can you tell?"

"The moss." He nodded at the crate's underside, where green fuzz had already begun creeping across the raw wood where the lid used to sit. "Torn moss takes about two days to look unhappy. This looks miserable."

Odile decided not to ask how he knew what miserable moss looked like. There was a whole category of knowledge the man carried around like ballast, and most of it made her want to lie down in a quiet room.

"So they came through here," she said, "took what they needed, moved upriver toward the falls. And then someone else came through after them and turned the place inside out."

"Someone who knew the cache existed." His jaw tightened. "That list is short. It's survey crews and river pilots and—" He stopped.

"And harbor masters," Odile finished, because if she didn't say it, it would just sit there between them growing teeth. She thought of old Merrick Vane at the Kestrel harbor office, who had signed her brother's river permit with a flourish and later signed off on the barge manifest that put him under the Meridian Star's crane. She pushed the thought down where she kept all her thoughts about her brother: in a sealed hold, below the waterline, where they couldn't shift in rough weather.

They searched the rest of the shelf of rock in silence. It gave them little: a shattered lantern, half a coil of good rope cut clean—which meant knives, not panic—and, wedged behind the sleeping-dog stone, a single boot. Survey issue, size small, laces still tied. Nobody leaves a tied boot unless they left it in a hurry, or unless somebody else removed it for them.

Odile held the boot and felt the gorge get colder around her.

* * *

They found Ferro an hour later, and Odile would spend the rest of her life being grateful they hadn't found him sooner.

It was Dunmore's ears, ironically, that caught him—the man who'd spent seven years learning to hear nothing heard something. They had come down off the shelf onto the old towpath, a moss-slick ledge of cut stone running along the gorge above the rapids, when he raised a hand and stopped walking so abruptly that Odile nearly went past him into the water.

"Listen," he said.

She listened. Wind in the pines. Water doing its eternal arithmetic over the rocks. And underneath it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall: breathing. Ragged, wet, human breathing, coming from the culvert.

The culvert was a drainage mouth punched through the towpath's retaining wall, big enough for a badger or a desperate man, and it had been both. Odile knelt at its entrance and looked into a darkness that smelled of wet iron and old blood, and two feet inside, a pair of eyes looked back at her.

He was young—younger than she'd expected, twenty-two at the outside, with the kind of face that hadn't finished deciding what it was going to be. He was filthy from crown to sole, crusted with culvert mud, and so thin that his cheekbones seemed to be trying to escape through his skin. One arm was wrapped in a filthy bandage gone brown and stiff. In his right hand, held in a grip that must have taken everything he had left, was a surveyor's stake hammer, raised in a trembling threat against the world.

"Don't," he croaked. "I'll—I've got a hammer."

"I can see that," Odile said gently. "It's a lovely hammer. I'm going to put my hands where you can see them, and then I'm going to take some food out of my pack, very slowly, and you're going to watch me do it. All right?"

His eyes flicked past her shoulder, found Dunmore standing in the light, and something happened in the young man's face that Odile would think about for years afterward. It wasn't fear. It was recognition, and then disbelief, and then a kind of drowning hope, all in the space of a breath.

"You're—" His voice cracked apart. "You're *him*. You're Dunmore. Ferro said—no, I'm Ferro. Josiah Ferro. Mr. Dunmore, we thought you were *dead*, sir, we thought—"

"Easy." Dunmore came forward slowly and knelt beside Odile, and she watched him take in the boy the way he'd taken in the ransacked cache: arm wound, dehydration, three days minimum without proper food, longer without sleep. "I'm here. We're getting you out of that hole. Can you crawl?"

Ferro tried. That was the thing about him, Odile thought later—he always tried, even when trying was clearly beyond what his body had left. He got halfway out of the culvert and his good arm buckled, and Dunmore simply reached in and drew him out the way you'd pull a drowned kitten from a drainpipe, and the boy clutched at his coat and shook, and did not cry, though it cost him visibly.

They got him onto a dry stretch of the towpath with his back against the sun-warmed stone. Odile dug out hardtack and the last of the smoked fish and a tin of condensed milk, and stood guard over him while he ate—not because she feared he'd bolt, but because she'd seen starving men eat themselves sick, and this boy had information in him she needed intact.

"Eat slow," she said. "Small bites. The river will still be there when you're done."

Between bites, in fragments, the story came out.

* * *

There had been nine of them in the crew. Good men, mostly—Dunmore's men, trained by him back when he still had a name worth training under, and hired again this season because the salvage consortium wanted the upper river charted before the Horizon's operation began. Ferro was the youngest, the sounder-of-depths, the boy who swam the cold pools to set the gauge lines nobody else would swim.

"We found her eleven days ago," Ferro said. His voice was steadier now, fed and warmed, though his eyes kept going to the water as if expecting it to produce more of his crewmates. "The Meridian Star. Low tide pulled back far enough that her ribs showed above the mud below Kestrel Falls. She's been there seven years and nobody ever marked her on a chart. Like the river was keeping her private."

"She was keeping her cargo private," Dunmore said quietly.

Ferro looked at him. "You know what she was carrying?"

"I'm beginning to."

"The documents." The boy leaned forward, urgent, wincing when his bandaged arm reminded him of itself. "Mr. Halloway figured it out first. The inquiry records—from the collapse. Seven years ago they shipped the whole file downriver on the Star for the tribunal at the capital, and she never arrived. Everyone said she sank in the gorge with the loss of all hands, and the inquiry proceeded on whatever copies Crane's people allowed it to have. But she didn't sink natural, sir. Halloway saw the hull. Said the breach was from the *inside*. Said somebody scuttled her with the inquiry aboard."

Odile felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped down a well. Her brother had been deckhand on a salvage tug working that stretch of river the season the Star went missing. He'd been one of the divers sent down to look for her. They'd never found the wreck. They'd never found him either, and the river had declined to explain itself, the way rivers do.

"Go on," Dunmore said, and his voice had gone very level, the levelness of a man holding something heavy at arm's length.

"Halloway opened the strongroom. Tide was right, air was still trapped in the compartments—it took him two dives. The case was there. Sealed inquiry records, manifests, depositions, the clerk's own logbook. Everything the tribunal never saw. Enough to hang Crane's whole operation, sir. Enough to clear—" Ferro stopped himself, ears going red. "Enough to matter. To a lot of people."

"To me," Dunmore said. "You can say it, lad. I've had seven years to get used to the shape of it."

But Ferro's face had collapsed into something worse than embarrassment. Guilt, Odile realized. Pure, corrosive guilt, the kind that eats a man from the inside out faster than any hunger.

"It's my fault they scattered," he said. "The advance party—the Horizon's scouts, the ones flying the private flag—they came upriver two days behind us, and I was on watch at the falls, and I saw their boat and I *waved*. I waved them in, because I thought they were the resupply run out of Kestrel. I told them where our camp was." His voice broke entirely. "They hit us at night, sir. Took the case off Halloway, burned the camp, scattered us into the gorge. Mr. Adeyemi went into the water. Mr. Quill took a bullet in the shoulder. Halloway and Osei, they got marched away downstream on the scouts' boat, and I don't—" He swallowed. "I don't know if they're alive. I hid. I hid in a *culvert* and ate snails for three days while they took everything."

Silence, except for the river.

Then Dunmore reached out and gripped the back of the boy's neck, firm, the way you'd steady a piece of equipment you intended to keep using.

"Listen to me, Josiah Ferro. You waved at a boat because on this river, boats are friends until proven otherwise. That's not a fault. That's the reason any of us survive out here." His voice was rough. "And you kept yourself alive for three days in a hole in the rock, which is more than the men who hunted you managed to prevent. So you're going to stop apologizing and start telling me exactly what the scouts did with the case, and where their boat went, and how many of them there were. Can you do that?"

Ferro nodded, scrubbing his face with his good hand.

"Good man." Dunmore sat back, and Odile watched him assemble the pieces behind his eyes, quick and cold as a man laying track. "A case that size doesn't burn. If they took it off Halloway, they took it aboard their boat. Which means it's either headed back downriver to Crane, or it's waiting aboard the Horizon itself."

"That's the thing, sir." Ferro's voice dropped, and dread sharpened his features into something older than his years. "The scouts didn't leave. They're camped at the head of the falls narrows. Waiting. Because the Horizon isn't coming with the sixth tide like everyone says." He looked up, and his eyes were terrible. "We heard them talking on the radio relay. She's coming early. Two tides early. And the order they were given—" He had to stop and start again. "The order was to make sure nothing came out of that wreck but water."

Odile looked at Dunmore and saw the arithmetic finish behind his eyes. Two tides. One wounded boy. A gorge full of hunters between them and the only proof in the world that eleven dead men—and one drowned deckhand named Brack—had been murdered twice: once by a collapse, once by a cover-up.

"Well," she said, standing and shouldering her pack, and was surprised to find her voice almost cheerful, because that was the Brack way—laugh at the flood, curse politely at the lightning. "It's a good thing we left early, isn't it?"

Dunmore was already pulling the tide tables from his coat. "Low water at the wreck comes in four hours," he said. "After that, the next window won't open for two days, and we won't have two days."

"Then we'd better go get your documents," Odile said, "before the river remembers why it kept them."

Ferro struggled upright on his good arm, hammer still clenched in his fist like a talisman.

"Take me with you," he said. "I know the falls narrows. I know where the scouts sleep. Please, sir. Let me carry something. Let me *do* something."

Dunmore looked at him for a long moment—at the bandaged arm, the hollow cheeks, the guilt sitting on him like a soaked coat—and Odile saw him decide.

"Can you swim?" he asked.

"In this river?" Ferro almost smiled, and for a second he looked twelve years old and immortal. "Sir, I grew up in it."

"Then pick up your feet," Dunmore said, turning toward the falls. "We're going to rob a grave."
