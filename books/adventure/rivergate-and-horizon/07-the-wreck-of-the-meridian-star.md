## 7. The Wreck of the Meridian Star

Ferro ate the way drowning men breathe—desperately, and without any thought for elegance. Tamsin had given him half a tin of beans and a heel of bread gone hard as lock-grease, and the boy had demolished both in the time it took Odile to get the boat moving again. Now he sat wrapped in Odile's spare coat in the stern, color coming back into his face by degrees, like a lamp being turned up on an old rheostat.

"Slowly," Odile said, steering with one hand. "You've been living in a culvert for three days. Your stomach will thank you for dignity."

"My stomach," Ferro said thickly, "will thank me for anything it gets."

He was younger than Tamsin had expected—younger than he'd been when he'd trained him, anyway, which was a strange sentence to finish even inside his own head. Ferro had been nineteen then, all elbows and enthusiasm, the kind of apprentice who asked *why* about everything until the older men threatened to throw him in the river. Seven years was long enough that Tamsin had stopped thinking of his crew as people and started thinking of them as a chapter. Sitting three feet away from one of them, hollow-cheeked and shivering, made the chapter feel suddenly, terribly recent.

"You said they scattered," Tamsin said. "The Horizon's advance party. Start from there."

Ferro swallowed, wiped his mouth on his sleeve, and looked out at the water for a moment as if arranging his thoughts along the bank.

"We'd been up above Kestrel Falls two weeks. Mr. Halloway had us running lines through the gorge—said we were surveying for a new haul road." He paused. "That's what he told us. I don't think any of us believed it by the end, but you don't ask questions when the pay is good and the questions are dangerous."

"What changed?"

"The Meridian Star." Ferro said the name carefully, the way you'd handle something with a frayed wire inside it. "It came upriver in the spring tides, before we arrived. Nobody official knew about it. No manifest at the harbor, no pilot assigned—she ran the closed locks with her own crew. The fishermen talked about her for weeks. A salvage ship working the deep pool below the falls, they said. Divers going down every low tide."

Tamsin felt something shift under his ribs, the way a bridge shifts when a load it wasn't designed for rolls across it.

"The deep pool," he said. "Below Kestrel Falls."

"That's where she went down, too. In the end."

Odile cut the engine back to a murmur so the boy's voice would carry. They were running the long slack reach between the third and fourth rapids, water flat as poured tin, fog sitting on the banks like a patient animal.

"Tell it in order," she said. "Start with why a salvage ship with no papers comes seven miles up a dead river to dive a pool nobody's ever lost cargo in."

Ferro hunched deeper into the coat. "Because of what's in the pool. That's what we figured out too late. Mr. Halloway got a tip—I never learned from who—that the Meridian Star hadn't sunk by accident. She was scuttled. Deliberately, seven years ago, with her whole hold still full."

"With what in her hold?" Tamsin asked, though some cold part of him already knew, and the knowing arrived ahead of the words like frost arriving ahead of winter.

"Inquiry records." Ferro turned and looked at him straight on, and there was no fear in the look anymore—or rather, the fear had been joined by something steadier. "The Dunmore inquiry. The collapse. Everything—the depositions, the load calculations, the steel certificates, the correspondence. It was all being shipped downriver for the tribunal when the storm hit, and the ship went down in the narrows, and eleven men died in the collapse, and you—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. "And the engineer was blamed, and the case closed, because the evidence went to the bottom of the river. Except it didn't go anywhere. It sat there. For seven years. And somebody finally decided it was time to make sure it stayed there."

The river kept flowing. That was the obscene part, Tamsin thought. The water didn't care what it carried over its bed; it moved the same whether the cargo was timber or truth.

*Seven years.* He did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way his mind did all arithmetic—compulsively, uselessly, precisely. Seven years since he'd stood in the tribunal room with wet boots and a borrowed suit and watched men read from documents he'd never seen, describing stresses he'd never calculated, failures he'd never sanctioned. Seven years since the verdict had come down like a dropped spanner and he'd run—run upriver, into the closed lines, into a name that fit him like someone else's coat. Seven years believing, in whatever cellar of himself he kept such beliefs, that the truth was simply *gone*. Sunk. Dissolved. Filed forever in the one archive no subpoena could reach.

And the whole time it had been sitting forty feet under Kestrel Falls, sealed in a steel hold, waiting.

"Tamsin." Odile's voice, quiet. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're doing the thing where your face goes very still and your hands do the feeling for you. Breathe anyway."

He looked down and found his hands had indeed been busy—working the seam of his trousers, worrying it the way he worried a stubborn bolt. He made them stop.

"Ferro," he said. "The divers. Whose were they?"

"That's the part that doesn't add up." The boy leaned forward, and now the words came faster, tumbling. "The Meridian Star's own crew salvaged her first—months ago, quietly. We found their camp when we got above the falls. Old fires, a broken dive ladder, crates. They brought things *up*, Mr.—" he caught himself again, winced, "—sir. We found a crate they'd missed, half-buried in the silt near their camp. Documents inside. Water-stained but legible. Deposition transcripts. And a ledger."

"A ledger of what?"

"Payments." Ferro's voice dropped. "Dated the month before the collapse. To a shell company. From Crane Holdings."

The name landed in the boat like ballast. Odile's hands tightened on the wheel, and for a moment the only sound was the water talking against the hull.

Aldous Crane. Tamsin had met him exactly once, seven years and one lifetime ago—a big soft-spoken man with a philanthropist's smile, who had shaken Tamsin's hand at the inquiry recess and said, with genuine warmth, *Whatever happens, son, remember the valley will need men like you again someday.* At the time Tamsin had taken it for kindness. Later, running, he'd taken it for nothing at all. Now it reassembled itself into something else entirely: a man making sure the condemned felt comforted. Men like you again. Men who could be blamed again.

"He profited from the rebuild," Tamsin said slowly. "Everyone knew that. The reconstruction contracts—"

"He *engineered* the failure." Ferro said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "The ledger shows payments to the mill that supplied the lower truss steel. Substandard heat numbers, certified as prime. The certificate of conformity for that shipment is in the crate, sir. I held it. I read it twice. It has a signature on it."

Tamsin closed his eyes.

Here was the vertigo, then—not the falling kind but the standing-still kind, the ground proving false while you kept obediently upright. For seven years he had built his exile on a foundation of *if only*: if only he'd inspected closer, if only he'd refused the schedule pressure, if only he'd been better, the eleven would be alive and he would be a man instead of a ghost. Guilt had been the load-bearing wall of his invisible life. He had furnished it. Kept it swept. Learned to live in it.

And the wall had never been his. The steel had been rotten before it left the mill. The inspection reports had been forged. The schedule pressure had been manufactured to rush the pour. He hadn't failed the bridge. Someone had failed it *on purpose*, with him attached to the front of it like a figurehead, so that when it broke, the breaking would have a face.

Eleven men. Halloway's brother-in-law among them—he remembered that much, the foreman drinking alone at the wake, refusing everyone's hand. Eleven men whose deaths had been not a tragedy but a *transaction*, entered neatly in a ledger, and the ledger had spent seven years within swimming distance of the town that mourned them.

"Sir?" Ferro's voice, careful. "You've gone somewhere."

"I've gone back seven years," Tamsin said. "Give me a moment and I'll come back the rest of the way."

* * *

They tied up at midday in a side slough, willows screening them from the main channel, and ate cold rice while Ferro told the rest of it.

His crew—Tamsin's crew, the thought still snagged like a nail head—had found the abandoned salvage camp on their fourth day above the falls. Halloway had gone quiet in the way he went quiet before decisions, and sent two men down to the pool at the next low tide with the camp's spare dive gear. They'd come back up with a document case, silt-caked, its seal intact.

"And then the Horizon's boats came around the bend," Ferro said. "Four of them, fast, armed, flying no colors. Like they'd known exactly where to look and exactly when."

"Because they did," Odile said. "The advance party wasn't chasing rumors. They were following their own ship's history. Somebody at Crane Holdings always knew where the Meridian Star went down. They just needed the political weather right before going to fetch it."

"Fetch it or destroy it," Tamsin said.

"Both. Best to know exactly what you're burning." Odile passed the rice tin down the boat. "Though burning is sloppy. Paper survives fire more often than people think. Better to raise the whole wreck and sink it somewhere deeper. Ocean-deep."

Ferro nodded miserably. "We scattered when the boats landed. Mr. Halloway ordered it—told us to take to the woods and stay dark, and he and Osei kept the case and drew them off downstream. That was three days ago. I hid in the culvert. I heard them searching. I heard them find the cache we'd made and tear it apart." His jaw worked. "I led them to it. I marked our supply drop on a map I was carrying, and I lost the map in the culvert mud, and when I went back for it, it was gone. So whatever they know about where we cached things—they know because of me."

"You were starving and hunted," Odile said. "Losing a map is not a confession."

"It feels like one."

"Most true things do, at first." She said it lightly, but Tamsin caught the edge underneath it, the private gravel in her voice, and remembered—she'd told him on the first night, in the boathouse, in the flat tone she used for facts that hurt—her brother had been on the night shift. Her brother was one of the eleven.

So they were all carrying the same weight up this river, he thought. Just packed differently. Ferro carried his as guilt. Odile carried hers as fury. Halloway—who knew what Halloway had carried, testifying the way he had, and Tamsin filed that question away where the painful questions lived, in the drawer that stuck.

"And the case," Tamsin said. "Halloway and Osei drew them off downstream. With the documents?"

"That was the plan." Ferro hesitated. "But sir—if they were caught—and the Horizon's still up there, anchored over the pool, and they haven't left—"

"Then either the case is hidden, or it's aboard the Horizon, or it's back in the pool with the rest of it." Tamsin laid it out like a route card, because laying things out like route cards was the only way he knew to keep his hands steady. "Three possibilities. Two of them are recoverable."

"And the third?"

"The third means Halloway talked." He let that sit. "Which I don't believe yet. Halloway once argued with a flood about where the high-water mark should be. The flood lost."

Ferro made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed his sleeve to his eyes. "That's him. That's exactly him."

* * *

Afternoon came down golden through the fog-burn, and the river widened, and Tamsin climbed to the bow with the chart board balanced on his knee and did what he always did when the world became unbearable: he measured it.

The pool below Kestrel Falls. He'd sounded it himself, years ago, in another life—sixty feet at the throat of it, cold year-round, fed by the falls' undertow. A steel-hulled salvage vessel of the Meridian Star's class, scuttled with valves opened and holds flooded: she'd settle on her keel, list maybe ten degrees into the silt, and stay. Seven years of current would bury her to the gunwales. But hulls that size didn't rot through in seven years, not in cold fresh water. Not quickly enough, anyway, to ruin paper sealed in a document case inside a cargo hold—

If the case was still in the hold. If the first salvage crew hadn't emptied it. If the Horizon's divers hadn't already—

Stop, he told himself. You can't chart water you haven't sounded.

But oh, the vertigo of it, even so. His whole exile reframing itself around him like a landscape seen from a train that has just changed direction. Every year of the closed lines, every borrowed name, every night he'd lain in a trackside shack telling himself the truth was buried and unrecoverable and *his fault*—all of it built on a premise that had been false from the first week. The proof hadn't drowned. It had been *parked*. And the men who parked it had waited seven years, patient as rust, until the statutes ran thin and the witnesses scattered and the valley forgot, and then they'd sent a ship to tidy up.

Except his crew had stumbled onto it. His ragged, curious, question-asking crew, surveying a road nobody needed, had tripped over the biggest buried secret in the valley's history. And now two of them were missing and the rest were scattered in the woods, and the sixth tide was coming whether anyone was ready or not.

"Tamsin." Odile had come forward without him noticing; she did that, moved as quietly as fog. She leaned on the rail beside him and looked at the chart upside down, which she could apparently read perfectly well. "Your face is doing the still thing again."

"I'm calculating."

"You're grieving. It looks similar on you. I've had seven days to study the difference." She tapped the chart, on the pool below the falls. "Say it plainly. What's down there?"

"Seven years of my life," Tamsin said. "Sitting in a steel box, forty feet down, waiting for me to come collect it." He laughed, and heard how unsteady it came out. "I spent seven years learning to live without my name. Turns out my name's been underwater the whole time, holding its breath."

"Names usually are." Odile watched the water slide past, and her voice gentled. "My brother's name is on a plaque in the Rivergate station hall. Bronze. Everyone walks past it. Do you know what I've wanted, all these years? Not the plaque. I've wanted the *ledger*. The page with the payments. Because a plaque says a man died. A ledger says a man was *killed*, and by whom, and for how much." She looked at him. "You're not going up this river to clear yourself. I've known that since the boathouse. You're going up to open a grave that other people filled in and hoped everyone would forget."

Tamsin didn't answer right away. Ahead, faint through the last of the haze, he could hear it—the long white mutter of Kestrel Falls, six miles off and audible anyway, the way big truths are audible long before you reach them.

"Ferro said the Horizon hasn't moved," he said finally. "Anchored over the pool."

"She hasn't. We glassed her masts at dawn."

"Then here's what I keep catching on." He turned to Odile, and the engineer in him laid the worry out flat where they could both see it. "A ship like that doesn't sit idle for three days. Either they've got what they came for and are waiting on orders—or they're waiting on something else. More men. Cranes. Heavy lift gear to raise the wreck whole." He paused. "Or a fleet."

Odile was quiet a moment.

"There's been barge traffic," she admitted. "Downbound traffic, past my mooring, three nights running. Big barges, running dark, no lights but their steering lamps. I told myself it was smugglers. Smugglers I understand." Her mouth thinned. "Smugglers don't usually move in convoy."

Tamsin looked upriver, toward the sound of the falls, and felt the countdown he'd traded his invisibility for ticking softly in his chest, tide by tide.

"How far," he said, "to the next place we can see around the bend?"

Odile smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all.

"Half an hour," she said, "if we hurry."
