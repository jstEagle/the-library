## 8. Fleet on the Horizon

The trouble with a river, Odile Brack had always maintained, was that it only ever told you one direction at a time. Downstream it said *hurry*. Upstream it said *not yet*. It never once said *you're doing fine*.

She said as much to Tamsin Dunmore—though she still thought of him as "the engineer," because saying his real name out loud felt like stepping on ice that hadn't been tested—and he grunted in the way of men who had spent seven years talking mostly to rust and rot. They were camped on a gravel shelf above the second lock, the boat hauled up and tied to a dead alder, Ferro wrapped in every blanket they owned and shivering himself back toward the living. The boy had eaten two cans of cold beans and half a tin of condensed milk and then fallen asleep mid-sentence, which Odile considered the most honest review of her cooking she'd ever received.

It was Tamsin who spotted the smoke first.

"Odile." He didn't point. He never pointed; pointing was for people who wanted to be seen. He just tilted his chin at the bend below Kestrel Narrows, where a smudge of gray sat against the green like a thumbprint on a window.

She took the field glasses from their oilskin case—the good ones, brass-cased, her father's, the only inheritance she'd ever wanted—and glassed the bend.

For a long moment she saw nothing but water and the usual morning theater: herons staging their silent dramas, mist peeling off the current like old wallpaper. Then the smudge resolved into shapes, and the shapes resolved into hulls, and Odile's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, the way it did when a lock gate failed or when someone said *we need to talk about money*.

"Three," she said. "No—four. Four hulls, running light, no barges. That's not a working fleet, that's a *race*."

Tamsin held out his hand for the glasses without asking. She gave them over. There was a time—she could admit this now, privately, in the locked room of her own head—when handing a man her father's field glasses would have required a signed affidavit and a blood oath. Seven days upriver with Tamsin Dunmore had worn that down to nothing. Or perhaps it wasn't the days. Perhaps it was watching him work the locks at night, patient as tide itself, hands knowing things his mouth had forgotten how to say.

He found them through the lenses. She watched his shoulders change.

"Two tides early," he said quietly. "At least."

"Salvage fleets don't run early. Salvage fleets run *late*, with invoices." Odile took the glasses back and settled them against her eye sockets, the brass cold enough to ache. "Working crews stop for fuel, for weather, for arguments about pay. Whoever's driving those boats hasn't stopped for anything."

The lead vessel came around the narrows and the sun caught her superstructure, and Odile made a sound she wasn't proud of—a small involuntary *huh*, the noise of a woman revising an opinion downward at speed.

"She's flying a private flag," Tamsin said.

"I see it."

"Do you recognize it?"

"I'm getting there." She was. The flag was dark—black or very deep blue—with a device at the center that the distance kept teasing her about. A bird? A wheel? Her father would have known instantly. Her father had known every flag on the river the way other men knew card faces, and he'd have also known exactly which company could afford four fast hulls running empty above the falls, and he'd have said something dry and devastating about it, and Odile missed him with a suddenness that arrived sideways, the way grief always did when she was busy.

Then the wind shifted the flag full out, and the device caught clean: a crane, rendered in white thread, elegant as a signature.

"Aldous Crane," she said.

Beside her, Tamsin went very still. Not frozen—she'd seen him frozen, at the culvert, at the ransacked cache, and stillness under fear looked different from this. This was the stillness of a man setting down something heavy so he could pick up something heavier.

"You know him," she said. It wasn't a question. Everything about him these last days had been questions she'd decided not to ask yet, hoarding them like a pilot hoards water over a shallow bar. But this one came out on its own.

"I know *of* him." His voice was flat and careful, a plank laid across a gap. "Everyone from Rivergate knows of him. Crane Holdings bought the valley's freight contracts six months after the collapse. Bought them cheap, because nobody else would touch a line with eleven bodies in its ledger."

"And you think he's here for the wreck."

"I think men like Aldous Crane don't come up rivers. They send people up rivers. If he's aboard—" Tamsin stopped. Rubbed the bridge of his nose, a gesture she'd catalogued early on as his version of swearing. "If he's aboard, then whatever's in that wreck is worth more than I thought, and worth less time than we have."

Odile lowered the glasses and looked at him properly. Morning light did him no favors—it showed every year of the exile he wouldn't name, the gray coming in at his temples like frost finding a window frame. But his eyes were working, moving, calculating, and she realized she was watching him do what he did: turn a disaster into a schedule.

"Say the numbers," she said. "You've been doing it since dawn. Say them out loud before they eat you."

A ghost of a smile crossed him and vanished, embarrassed to be seen. "Ferro said the crew scattered when the Horizon's advance party hit them. That was—" he counted silently, lips moving, "—five days ago. An advance party moves ahead of the main fleet by however much the financier wants to be sure. If the advance party is already past us, and the main body is two tides early..."

"Then somebody told them where to look."

"Somebody told them everything." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "The crew's route. The cache. Maybe even that I'm on the river."

The word *traitor* hung between them, unspoken, too large to say aloud and too obvious to bother. Odile turned back to the glasses because looking at the fleet was easier than looking at that particular silence.

Through the lenses she studied the lead ship with a professional's resentment. Fast hull, twin screws by the wake pattern, deck cranes folded flat for speed. Salvage vessels were supposed to wallow. They were supposed to be floating workshops, ugly and unhurried, all elbows and derricks. This one moved like a yacht that had gone to trade school.

And on her afterdeck, small with distance but unmistakable in the way that money is unmistakable—money has a posture all its own—stood a figure in a pale coat, hands clasped behind his back, watching the river go by as though he were inspecting it for purchase.

"He's actually here," she murmured. "The man himself. Standing on deck like it's a Sunday cruise."

"Crane likes to watch things happen." Tamsin's voice had dropped into a register she hadn't heard before, low and even, the voice of a man reading a structural report about his own gallows. "That's what the inquiry transcripts said. He never ordered anything anyone could prove. He just liked to be nearby while it happened."

Something in the way he said *transcripts* made her glance over. "You've read them."

"I read everything about that collapse for three years. From four hundred miles away. In rooms with bad lamps." He almost laughed; it came out as breath. "You can learn a lot about a disaster if you're willing to be the only person still taking notes."

Odile let that sit. There was a whole country inside this man—fenced off, unlit, posted with signs she couldn't read—and she had made her peace with not being invited in. Mostly. Some mornings, watching him check a lock gear by feel alone, she caught herself wanting to pick the lock on him, and then she remembered her brother, and put the tools away.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said yet, the thing she carried in the same pocket as the field glasses: her brother Joss had died in that collapse, and the man standing beside her was the name on the blame. She knew who he was. She had known since the boathouse, since before the boathouse, since the day she'd first heard that a surveyor named Dunmore was asking careful questions on closed lines and had recognized the carefulness itself, the specific caution of a man measuring his own grave. She had agreed to take him upriver anyway, and she still wasn't entirely sure why, except that Joss had believed in the railroad the way saints believe in heaven, and someone owed the truth to men like Joss, and Tamsin Dunmore was either the worst liar on the river or the only honest man in the whole rotten story.

Some days she genuinely could not tell which. Those were the days she checked the boat's lines twice.

"Odile." Tamsin's hand hovered near her elbow, not touching—he never touched, another entry in the catalogue—but near. "You've been on those glasses for two minutes without blinking. What are you seeing?"

"What I'm seeing," she said, "is that the lead boat is taking the narrows channel on the wrong side of the marker, which means either their pilot doesn't know this stretch, or they don't care, because they plan to drag anything that goes wrong off the bottom later. And what I'm *concluding* is that we are no longer in a race." She passed him the glasses. "We're in a chase, and we're the fox, and I hate being the fox. Foxes don't get to choose the ground."

"We'll choose the ground." He raised the glasses. "There's always ground left to choose. That's half of engineering—finding the ground everyone else wrote off."

"And the other half?"

"Convincing the ground to hold."

Below them, the fleet's engines carried up the gorge, a sound like slow thunder learning patience. Ferro stirred in the blankets and mumbled something about the manifest, about numbers that didn't add, and Odile's chest tightened. The boy had led the Horizon's scouts to the crew—not knowingly, not maliciously, but the guilt had gotten into him like river water into a boot, and he'd confessed it twice more in the night, each time smaller, until the third confession had been barely a whisper: *I showed them the chart because they said Halloway sent them.* Halloway. Tamsin's foreman. Another thread in a knot she was tired of pretending she could see the end of.

"How far to the falls?" Tamsin asked, lowering the glasses.

"Half a day's hard poling, if the fog holds. Less if it lifts, and then they'll see our smoke."

"No smoke, then. Cold camp from here."

"Cold food too, unless you've developed a talent for raw beans."

"I ate worse for seven years."

"That," Odile said, "is somehow the saddest thing you've ever told me, and you once told me about your boots."

The ghost-smile again, briefer. Then he crouched beside Ferro and touched the boy's shoulder, gently, and Ferro surfaced from sleep with the flinch of a creature that had learned waking was dangerous. When he saw whose hand it was, some of the panic drained out of his face, replaced by something that looked painfully like devotion. The boy had decided Tamsin Dunmore was the fixed point the world turned around. Odile wondered what it cost a man to be someone's fixed point when he didn't believe the world should turn around him at all.

"They're early," Tamsin told him simply. "Ahead of schedule. Which means the schedule matters to them, which means the documents matter, which means—" he paused, choosing honesty over comfort, and Odile noted it, filed it, added it to the growing pile of evidence for the defense, "—which means we're not safe, and won't be. Can you walk?"

"I can walk." Ferro's voice was gravel and apology. "I can pole. I can—"

"You can eat first." Odile pressed the last tin of beans into his hands. "Nobody outruns a salvage fleet on an empty stomach. That's not courage, that's arithmetic."

Ferro managed something like a smile, and for a moment the three of them were just people on a gravel bar sharing bad breakfast, and the river ran on beneath them, indifferent, carrying its secrets the way it always had—one direction at a time, and never the one you asked for.

Then the wind shifted again, and brought them the sound clearly: not just engines now, but voices, faint and carrying off the water, and a single sharp blast of a whistle from the lead ship—two short, one long, the private signal of a fleet answering to one master.

Tamsin stood. He didn't say anything. He just looked upriver, toward Kestrel Falls, toward the Meridian Star sleeping in the mud with eleven years of truth in her hold, and Odile watched him do the thing she was beginning to understand was the truest thing about him: he measured. Distance, time, tide, weight of a man's conscience—all of it, measured, weighed, entered into some ledger behind his eyes that had never once balanced in his favor.

"Fourth tide," he said finally. "They'll make the falls by the fourth tide if the water cooperates."

"The water," Odile said, "has never cooperated with anyone in the history of this valley."

"Then we have until it changes its mind." He turned to her, and for the first time since the boathouse she saw something in his face that wasn't calculation or grief or the careful blankness of a hiding man. It was closer to gratitude, awkward and unpracticed, like a handshake from someone who'd forgotten how. "Odile. If Crane is aboard that lead ship, then he thinks this ends today. He's come to close an account."

"Accounts can be disputed."

"Yes." And now the smile came all the way, thin and hard and entirely new. "I know. I used to draw them for a living."

She was still deciding whether that was funny or terrible when Ferro, beans halfway to his mouth, went rigid and pointed downstream with the tin.

"Smoke," the boy whispered. "Behind us. On the bank trail."

Odile snatched up the glasses, spun, and found it: a thin column rising from the tree line a mile back the way they'd come, exactly where no honest traveler would build a fire. Someone had been following their wake. Someone had been following it carefully, patiently, keeping just far enough back to stay unseen.

Someone who knew the river well enough to know exactly where a cold camp would smell like nothing at all.

"Tamsin," she said quietly. "We're not being chased."

He turned. Followed her gaze. She watched the understanding land on him like a dropped girder.

"We're being *herded*."
