## 9. Portage

The third lock was a corpse.

Tamsin stood on the lock wall in the gray first light and looked down into the chamber where the water should have been waiting to lift them the last forty feet toward the upper river. Instead the gates hung open like a dropped jaw, and the machinery that was supposed to close them lay scattered across the stonework in pieces that had no business being apart. The great spur gears—each one taller than Odile, each one cut from solid bronze by men who'd expected their work to outlive them—had been stripped of their teeth. Not broken. Stripped. Someone had come with tools and patience and taken the teeth off one by one, so that when the winch tried to turn them, they would spin uselessly against nothing.

"You said you could get us through," Odile said behind him. It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse.

"I said I could get us through if the locks were merely abandoned." Tamsin crouched at the edge of the wall and studied the gear housings. "This isn't abandonment. This is surgery."

Ferro came up the ladder from the boat, moving better than he had two days ago but still walking like a man whose bones were borrowed from someone older. He looked into the lock chamber and went pale in a way that had nothing to do with his hunger.

"That's days of work," he said. "Stripping gears that size. You'd need a crew."

"You'd need a crew," Tamsin agreed. "And a barge to haul the teeth away, and time, and the certainty that nobody official would come looking." He straightened up and wiped his hands on his trousers, though he hadn't touched anything. "The Rivergate Commission hasn't sent an inspector up this line since before I left. Whoever did this knew that."

"Crane's people," Odile said flatly.

"Or the fleet's advance scouts. Or both. They're often the same people." Tamsin turned and looked upriver, where the gorge walls rose steep and green on either side of the dead waterway. Somewhere beyond the next bend, past the falls, past the wreck, the Horizon was coming with its private flag and its cranes. Two tides ahead of schedule now. Maybe less. "We can't go through. So we go over."

Odile followed his gaze up the gorge wall and her expression made it very clear what she thought of the idea. "Over."

"There's an old tramway. Maintenance road for the lock construction crews, back when they built this line. It runs along the ridge above the gorge for about three miles, drops down to the navigation channel above the falls." He was already doing the arithmetic in his head, the way he did it without meaning to, the way he'd done it all his life: weight, distance, gradient, friction. "It's rough, but it's graded. We can haul the boat."

"Haul the—" Odile put both hands on the lock wall and leaned there. "That boat is my livelihood. My *father's* livelihood before me. You want to drag her up a mountain."

"I want to drag her up a hill," Tamsin said. "Three miles of hill. With rollers and a sledge and four strong backs." He paused. "Three strong backs and whatever Ferro's got left."

Ferro flinched. Tamsin hadn't meant it as cruelty—it had simply come out as engineering, which was sometimes indistinguishable—but he watched the young man absorb it anyway, watch him square his shoulders as though the words were a pack he could carry too.

"I've got more than you think," Ferro said quietly.

* * *

They spent the morning building the sledge.

There was a maintenance shed half-collapsed into the brambles below the lock, and inside it, under seven years of leaves and one very indignant family of martens, the old construction-era tools had survived: iron rollers gone to rust but still round, chain in coils heavy enough to anchor a church, and—Tamsin's hands actually shook a little when he uncovered it—a timber cradle built to hold a lock gate during transport, its oak still sound beneath the rot on its skin.

"They built things to last, then," Odile said, watching him knock the worst of the decay off the cradle with the flat of an axe.

"They built things assuming someone would maintain them," Tamsin corrected. "That's different. That's a kind of faith." He tested a bolt, found it seized, and soaked it in lamp oil while he talked. "My father used to say a machine is just a promise written in steel. Break the promise and the steel doesn't care—it fails exactly when you need it most."

"Your father sounds like he'd have hated this place now."

"He'd have hated what was done to it. He'd have loved the fixing." Tamsin worked the bolt free with a grunt. "Which is why I have to be careful. I'm more my father than I'd like some days."

The boat came out of the water at noon. It took all three of them and every length of chain they owned, and it took Odile standing chest-deep in the shallows guiding the bow while Tamsin and Ferro hauled on the windlass they'd rigged from a fallen tree trunk, and when the keel finally settled onto the timber cradle with a groan like a settling house, Odile stood in the river and put her hand flat on the hull and stayed there a long moment, saying nothing, saying everything.

"We'll bring her through," Tamsin said.

"You don't know that."

"No. But we'll bring her through."

She climbed out of the water, wrung out her sleeves, and picked up a chain end. "Then stop talking and start hauling."

* * *

The gorge road was worse than Tamsin remembered, and he had remembered it honestly, which was the bitter part. Seven years of frost had chewed the tramway bed into a staircase of loose stone. The first mile took them four hours. They learned the work the way you learn anything worth knowing: badly, then less badly.

Rollers went under the cradle's runners, six of them leapfrogging front to back in a rhythm Tamsin called out like a chant. *Lift. Roll. Set. Haul.* On the steep pitches they rigged the chain to trees and used the windlass in relays, and on the switchbacks they pried the whole load around with levers, inch by screaming inch, the boat's hull complaining above them like a ship in weather.

By the second evening Ferro had stopped apologizing.

He had started the portage full of apologies—the way he'd started everything since the culvert, since they'd pulled him out half-starved with the story spilling out of him between shivers. *I led them to the cache. I ran and they followed me and I didn't even know I was being followed, I was so scared, I just ran.* Every rest stop, every meal, every silence, he filled it with the same confession wearing different clothes. Tamsin had heard men do this before, after the disaster, in the inquiry tent—grief trying to find a shape small enough to hold.

But somewhere on the second pitch, hauling chain hand over hand with his palms wrapped in rags, Ferro stopped talking. And somewhere on the third pitch, when the windlass jammed and the load began to slip backward toward the drop, Ferro threw himself against the handle beside Tamsin and held it, arms shaking, feet skidding on stone, and held it, and held it, until Tamsin got the pawl seated and the world stopped trying to kill them.

They sat afterward with their backs against the cradle, breathing hard, the gorge dropping away below them into blue shadow.

"You held that," Tamsin said.

"Barely."

"'Barely' counts. In engineering, barely counts. Everything that stands is barely standing. That's what a safety factor is—an admission that barely is all any of us ever manage." He passed Ferro the water flask. "You've been carrying that cache around like it's yours alone. It isn't. A crew gets scattered, a man runs—that's not betrayal, that's being twenty years old and terrified. What matters is you're here. Carrying."

Ferro drank, and looked at the flask instead of at Tamsin, and his voice came out smaller than his body. "Halloway always said you talked like a textbook."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool.

Tamsin took the flask back. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "You knew him well?"

"Everyone knew Halloway. He taught half the valley's boys their knots." Ferro hesitated. "He talks about you. Past tense, mostly. Like you're dead. But once—when we signed on for the survey—he told us who trained him. Wouldn't say your name. Just said, 'Best engineer I ever worked under, and the river owes him an apology.' Then he made us all check our rigging twice."

Tamsin looked out at the gorge. The evening light was turning the far wall copper, and somewhere below, the river kept its own counsel.

"The river owes a lot of apologies," he said. "Come on. Another hour of light. Let's spend it."

* * *

They camped that night on the ridge with the boat lashed down and the fire small, and Tamsin took first watch because sleep had been arriving late and leaving early for him these past days, and he'd stopped fighting it.

He heard Ferro move before he saw him—the boy had learned to walk quiet on the trail but not yet around camp—and then Ferro settled onto the rock beside him without asking, which was itself a kind of progress. For a while neither spoke. Below them the river murmured through the dark, patient as ever, keeping what it kept.

"I keep thinking about the man in the culvert," Ferro said at last. "Before you came. Three days, maybe four. I'd hear the scouts on the trail and I'd make myself smaller. And I kept thinking—if they find me, it's over, but if they *don't* find me, then nobody's looking for the others either, and that's worse somehow. Being safe felt like another thing I'd done wrong." He rubbed his face. "I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because it's true, and true things want out." Tamsin fed a stick to the fire. "I lived in a culvert of my own for seven years. Different dimensions. Same architecture."

"The closed lines. Odile told me. Working under a false name." Ferro glanced sideways. "Is it strange? Being Tamsin Dunmore again?"

Tamsin considered the question with more care than he'd expected it to deserve. "It's like putting on a coat you outgrew. It fits worse than you remember and better than you feared. Mostly I keep waiting for it to mean something it doesn't. I thought getting my name back would be the finish line. Now I think it might be the starting gate."

"And if it costs everything else? The quiet life. The invisible one."

"Then I'll build another one. I'm good with my hands." He smiled despite himself. "Ask the martens. I evicted an entire family today and rebuilt their roof better than they had it."

Ferro laughed—a real laugh, short and startled out of him, the first Tamsin had heard—and something in the camp loosened by a degree. Odile stirred in her blanket and muttered something about engineers and their noise, and did not actually wake, and the night went on.

Near dawn, Tamsin woke Ferro for his watch and found himself saying, before he could weigh it: "When this is done—whatever's done—there's work on the upper lines. Real survey work. Honest stakes in honest ground." He shrugged, embarrassed. "If you want it."

Ferro stared at him. "You're offering me a job. After I brought the wolves to your door."

"I'm offering you a job because you carried a windlass past your limit to save a boat that wasn't yours, for people who owed you nothing. That's the whole reference I need." Tamsin rolled into his blanket. "Sleep when you can, Ferro. The river doesn't wait, but it does forgive. Eventually. If you keep showing up."

* * *

They reached the top of the descent on the fourth morning, and the gorge opened like a book.

Below them the upper channel ran wide and slow between willowed banks, and there—there, at last—was water deep enough to float her. The last half-mile of tramway dropped gently through birches, and they rode the cradle down it with brakes improvised from chain and green logs, shouting like schoolboys, Odile laughing outright for the first time since Tamsin had known her, and when the keel touched the current she waded out and held the bow again and this time she was smiling when she said, "Off my boat, both of you. You're dripping."

They loaded in the gold of mid-morning. Ferro took the tiller without being asked and held her straight through the first bend, and Odile watched him do it and said nothing, which from Odile was a medal.

Tamsin stood at the bow with the current taking them, the gorge falling away behind, the falls ahead—Kestrel Falls, and beyond them the wreck, and beyond the wreck whatever truth the river had been holding for seven years. His shoulders ached. His palms were raw. He felt lighter than he had in years.

Then Ferro, at the tiller, went stiff.

"Tamsin." His voice had gone thin. "Smoke. Ahead. Past the falls. Lots of it."

Tamsin raised the field glasses Odile kept lashed under the thwart. Through the morning haze, above the white line of Kestrel Falls, a thread of dark smoke rose from the tree line near the old relay station—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable on the wind, came the crackle of a radio transmitter working hard.

Someone was talking. Someone was transmitting on the survey band.

And Tamsin knew that rhythm of transmission the way he knew his own heartbeat—short, clipped, disciplined, the sending style of a man who'd drilled it into every crew he'd ever run.

"Halloway," he said aloud, before he could stop himself. "That's Halloway's fist."

The boat ran on toward the falls, and toward the voice.
