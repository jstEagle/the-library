## 10. Radio Ghosts

The relay station sat on a shelf of rock above the river like an old man who'd been left out in the weather and had decided not to take it personally. Its paint had gone the color of driftwood. Its antenna leaned at the angle of someone listening hard. A hand-painted sign on the door read KESTREL RELAY — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, though the "authorized" had been crossed out by some long-ago joker and replaced with "sober," which Odile Brack felt was asking rather a lot of anyone working this far upriver.

She brought the boat in under the shelf with the engine cut, letting the current do the work, and Tamsin—no, *Corren*, she reminded herself, though her mouth refused to learn it—stepped onto the rocks without being told, because he'd already seen what she'd seen: the fresh tire tracks in the mud below the station, and the smell of coal smoke that hadn't finished leaving.

"Someone's been here recently," he said.

"Someone's always been here recently. That's what makes it a relay." She tied off the line. "Question is whether they were passing messages or reading them."

Ferro climbed out last, moving better than he had two days ago but still walking like a man apologizing to the ground for standing on it. The boy had eaten half a tin of beans and most of a loaf Odile had baked in the boat's little stove—burned on one side, which Ferro had declared the best thing he'd ever tasted, and Odile had decided not to hold his starvation against his judgment—and there was color in him now, and something worse than hunger behind his eyes: the particular look of a young man doing arithmetic about his own guilt and getting a bad number every time.

"The crew used this station," Ferro said quietly. "Halloway called in from here twice. I carried the batteries up."

"Then you know where the set is," Odile said. "Good. You can carry them back down."

* * *

Inside, the station smelled of dust and mouse and old varnish, and of something else underneath—the cold mineral smell of a place the river owned even indoors. The main room held a desk, a chair with three legs and one ambition, a wall of switchboards with brass fittings gone green at the edges, and the wireless set itself, squat and serious, its dials like closed eyes.

Odile checked the logbook first. That was habit; harbor people lived and died by logs. The last entry was eleven days old, routine traffic, signed by a relay operator named Petch whose handwriting looked like a spider had drowned in the inkwell. But beneath the routine entries, someone had written a single line and then scratched it out so hard the paper had thinned.

Tamsin stood over her shoulder. "Can you read it?"

"A bit. Under the scratching." She tilted the page toward the gray light through the window. "'Fleet moved up. Not on the schedule.'" She paused. "And then, 'They're not salvage.'"

The room got very quiet. Outside, the river went on talking to itself, the way rivers do, saying nothing useful.

"They're not salvage," Tamsin repeated. He said it the way a man repeats a diagnosis.

"Ferro." Odile turned. "When your crew stopped answering radio—think carefully now. Was it silence? Or was it *cut*?"

Ferro's brow worked. He was maybe twenty-two, with the kind of face that would look young until it suddenly didn't. "I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "There was static. Bad static, for a day before. Halloway said the atmosphere was turning. And then nothing."

"Atmosphere doesn't turn for three days running above Kestrel Falls," Odile said. "I've piloted that stretch since I was fourteen. The atmosphere up there has exactly two moods, and neither of them is static." She pulled the chair over—it clattered, complained, held—and sat down at the set. "Somebody was jamming them. Cutting their voice off before they could say too much. Which means somebody knew what they might say."

"And whoever it was," Tamsin said slowly, "has been sitting on this frequency ever since. Listening."

"Or talking." Odile's hands were steady on the dials, which surprised her, because inside she was doing the same arithmetic Ferro was, only with different numbers. Her brother's name kept trying to insert itself into every calculation. She kept striking it out. There'd be time for that arithmetic later, when the river was quiet and the gin bottle was closer. "We're going to find out which. Corren—I need you outside."

He looked at her. In seven days she had learned his face well enough to read the large print of it: the wariness, the stubbornness, the guilt worn smooth as river stone from handling. Now there was something else, something rawer.

"You think I'll break cover," he said.

"I think you'll hear a voice you've spent seven years not hearing, and I don't know what you'll do, and neither do you." She said it plainly, because kindness between pilots and passengers is mostly just clarity delivered gently. "That's not an insult. That's seamanship. You don't put a man on the wheel in fog if you don't know how he steers."

For a moment she thought he'd argue. His jaw did a thing. Then he nodded once, short, and took Ferro by the shoulder—not roughly, but with the unmistakable weight of a man accustomed to being obeyed on work sites—and walked him out to the rocks, where they sat down together in the wind like two crows on a fence.

Odile put on the headset.

* * *

The band was mostly dead air and river-hiss. She swept the frequencies slow, the way you'd drag a net: nothing, nothing, a fisherman complaining about eels to nobody, nothing, nothing—

Then a carrier wave, faint, upstream, and a voice.

"—confirm receipt. Say again, confirm the hold is clear."

Odile's breath went shallow. She turned the gain up with two fingers, delicate as defusing something.

A second voice answered, flat and official, the kind of voice that reads regulations aloud for pleasure. "Hold inspection complete. No documents recovered aboard the wreck site. Advance party reports the survey cache destroyed. Repeat: destroyed."

"*Destroyed,*" the first voice repeated, and there was something in the repetition that wasn't satisfaction. It was the tone of a man checking a lock twice because he doesn't trust the door. "And the men? The survey crew?"

A pause. Long enough to have furniture in it.

"Two accounted for. Two unaccounted."

"That is not the same as two alive."

Another pause. When the flat voice came back, it had dropped its officialdom, just slightly, the way a mask slips when the wearer gets tired. "They're alive. Crane wants them breathing until he sees the ash himself. They're leverage. If Dunmore surfaces—if anyone comes asking—the men answer for it."

Odile closed her eyes.

*Halloway.* She'd never met the man, but she knew the name the way everyone in Rivergate knew it: the foreman who testified, the foreman whose testimony had put the disaster on Tamsin Dunmore's shoulders like a yoke. Eleven dead. One man blamed. And now here was his voice coming out of the wire, alive, captive, calling from aboard the Horizon like a ghost haunting the wrong house.

She should have called Tamsin in then. That was the plan, and plans are the religion of people who go upriver against fleets.

Instead she listened another moment, because the first voice spoke again, and something in it snagged her.

"You're certain the documents were destroyed. Not merely... relocated."

"Crane's man burned the cache personally. Ash in the gorge."

"Ash in the gorge," the first voice echoed. And then, softer, almost to itself, in a voice that had given up pretending: "Eleven men, and it comes down to ash in a gorge."

Odile sat very still.

She knew that voice. Not the name attached to it—she'd never heard the name—but the *voice*, the accent, the particular way it rounded the word *gorge* like a stone in the cheek. Rivergate speech. Valley-born. Somebody from home was on that frequency, high up enough to question Crane's orders and low enough to be afraid while doing it.

She reached for the logbook, wrote down the frequency, the time, the phrasing, her hand moving fast. Harbor training: write everything, trust nothing to memory, because memory is a dock rat that steals whatever isn't nailed down.

On the rocks outside, she saw Tamsin stand up.

* * *

Later, she would tell herself she'd meant to stop him. Later, with gin, she would construct a whole version of events in which she rose swiftly and caught his sleeve and the tragedy was averted by her quick thinking. What actually happened was that the door banged open, wind came through carrying grit and one indignant moth, and Tamsin Dunmore crossed the room in four strides with his hand already reaching for the transmitter key, and Odile Brack—who had once talked a drunk barge captain off a bridge at flood tide using nothing but her voice and a borrowed lantern—did the only thing that worked.

She took off the headset and held it out to him.

"Listen first," she said. "Break cover after. If you still want to."

He froze, hand hovering. Through the open door, Ferro appeared, alarmed, holding a rock for some reason, as though the situation might require geology.

Tamsin took the headset. Put it on. And Odile watched seven years move across his face like weather.

The transmission had shifted. The flat-voiced officer was speaking again, reciting: "...prisoners remain in good condition. Halloway requests—"

Tamsin made a sound. Small. Odile had heard drowned men make it.

"—requests confirmation that no rescue attempt will be made, as any attempt will result in—"

Tamsin's hand came up toward the key. Odile didn't grab it. She simply laid her own hand flat on the desk beside it, palm down, the way you'd calm a horse, and waited.

"—in result in disciplinary measures. Request denied pending Crane's inspection. End of segment. Next scheduled contact, fourth tide."

The carrier wave faded into hiss. The hiss faded into the sound of the river and the wind and the moth bumping the lamp glass, and Tamsin Dunmore stood in a derelict relay station with his hand six inches from the thing that could save him or kill everyone he loved, shaking.

"He asked about me," Tamsin said. His voice came out sanded flat. "Halloway. First words out of him in seven years, and he's asking after me."

"He asked if anyone would come," Odile corrected gently. "Different question."

"No." Tamsin pulled the headset off, folded it with terrible care, set it on the desk as though it were made of eggshell. "It's the same question. It's been the same question for seven years. Did anyone come. Does anyone believe me. Is my name—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I could have answered him. Right now. One call sign, thirty seconds, and he'd know I'm alive and coming."

"And Crane would know too," Odile said. "Every set on this river would know. You'd trade two lives for one sentence."

"I know the arithmetic." He dropped his hands. His eyes were red-rimmed and furious, mostly at himself. "I built bridges for eleven years, Mrs. Brack. I understand load-bearing. I'm telling you the load is unbearable, not that the math is wrong."

Something in Odile's chest went loose and warm and painful all at once. She recognized the feeling. It was the feeling of deciding to trust someone, arriving uninvited, the way weather does.

"Well," she said, "for what it's worth, you nearly steered us onto the rocks, and then you didn't. That's more discipline than half the pilots I've worked with, and I include myself in that assessment, particularly regarding the winter of the brandy incident."

Tamsin blinked at her. From the doorway, Ferro lowered his rock.

"Fourth tide," Tamsin said finally. "You heard it. Next contact, fourth tide. That means the Horizon expects to be inspected by then—that's when Crane confirms the destruction. That's our window. Whatever happens has to happen before fourth tide."

"It also means they don't know we're here," Odile said. "Which is worth more than both our boats. They're watching the water for a fleet, not the rocks for a pilot, a ghost, and a boy with a rock."

"I wasn't going to *use* it," Ferro said, with dignity.

"You were absolutely going to use it," Odile said, "and I respect that."

But the humor sat thin on the room, like frost on a warm window, gone as soon as breathed on. Because Odile had one more thing, and she'd been deciding for ten minutes whether to say it, and the decision had just been made for her by the look on Tamsin's face—the look of a man who had decided, again, at great cost, to keep hiding, and who deserved to know what his hiding was buying.

"There was a third voice," she said. "Valley accent. Someone aboard the Horizon from Rivergate, and not happy about the burning. He questioned whether the documents were truly destroyed. He counted the dead out loud—'eleven men'—like a man counting debts." She slid the logbook across the desk, her notes facing up. "Whoever he is, he's cracked somewhere along the line. Men like that either break entirely or turn. And I've known this valley my whole life, Corren. We turn slow. But we turn."

Tamsin read her notes. Read them again. When he looked up, the fury had settled into something harder and more useful—the cold resolve she'd first seen on the dock, but warmer now, aimed outward instead of in.

"Fourth tide," he said. "Then we move tonight. Before they expect anything."

"Tonight," Odile agreed. Then, because the river had gone quiet outside and the light through the window had turned the color of pewter, and because some things shouldn't wait for gin: "One more thing. The officer said two unaccounted for. Your crew was five."

Tamsin went still. "Ferro makes three accounted for."

"Which leaves two unaccounted," Odile said, "and two prisoners aboard the Horizon. Unless the numbers are wrong." She let that sit. "Or unless somebody else from your crew is still out here in the valley. Alive. Hiding. And knows these waters better than Crane's whole fleet."

Through the window, far downstream, where the river bent toward the falls, a smudge of smoke rose against the evening sky. Too small for a steamer. Too steady for a campfire.

Too deliberate for an accident.

Odile reached for her field glasses, and beside her, very quietly, Tamsin Dunmore said a name she hadn't heard before—a woman's name—and his voice cracked clean down the middle.
