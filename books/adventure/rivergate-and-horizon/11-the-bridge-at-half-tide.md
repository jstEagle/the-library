## 11. The Bridge at Half Tide

The gorge narrowed the way a fist does—slowly, then all at once. By midmorning the river had squeezed itself between walls of black shale no wider than a wagon road, and the water ran green and furious below us, chewing on boulders with a sound like grinding teeth.

"There," Ferro said, and pointed.

The bridge came out of the fog in pieces: first the far abutment, a shoulder of mortared stone jutting from the cliff; then one pier, standing alone in the current like a man wading; then, high above, the span itself—a lattice of rusted iron sagging between its anchors, deck planks long since rotted to lacework, a rail line that hadn't carried anything but rainwater in a decade.

I knew it before I'd finished looking at it. Of course I did.

"That's the Kestrel Viaduct," I said. "Span forty-one."

"You've been here?" Odile asked, easing the boat toward a gravel shelf below the abutment.

"I inspected it." I said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. "Six years ago. Condemned it myself. Wrote the report in my own hand, back when my own hand was still allowed to sign things."

Ferro made a small noise, half laugh, half prayer. "So we're crossing a bridge that you personally killed."

"Condemned," I corrected. "Killing comes later. Usually on schedule."

* * *

We beached the boat in the shadow of the abutment and stood looking up. The viaduct crossed the gorge two hundred feet above the water, and the only alternative was three days around the head of the gorge—which meant arriving at Kestrel Falls a full tide behind the fleet, which meant never arriving at all.

Odile put her hands on her hips and studied the span the way she studied weather: without affection, but with respect for what it might do to her. "You're going to tell me it's safe, aren't you. And then you're going to tell me all the ways it isn't."

"It's not safe," I said. "It's crossable. There's a difference, and the difference is about eleven minutes."

"Ferro," Odile said, "he's doing the thing where he sounds calm and means we're all going to die."

"I'm not dying on a bridge I condemned," Ferro said. "That's too much like being right twice."

I let them have their jokes, because the truth was I was already somewhere else—up on the span, walking it plank by plank in my head, six years younger and carrying a clipboard and a hammer and the particular loneliness of a man whose job is to decide which structures get to keep lying about their health.

"Here's what I know," I said. "When I inspected her, the failure was already underway. It always is, with wrought iron in river country. The wind bracing on the north truss had corroded through at the lower chord joints—the iron was never galvanized, and this gorge holds fog the way a sponge holds water. The south side got more sun. South side's tired but honest. North side is a liar."

"So we cross on the south rail," Odile said.

"We cross on the south *deck beams*, under the rail, hands on the south truss, and we do not touch the north side for any reason whatsoever. Not if the boat drifts. Not if Ferro drops something. Not if the bridge itself asks politely."

Ferro looked up at the span, then down at the river, then at me. "And the eleven minutes?"

"The pier in midstream." I pointed. The lone pier stood in the deepest, fastest channel, and even from here I could see the scour crater around its base—the river had been digging at its footing for years, patient as a debt collector. "When I condemned her, the pier had settled four inches out of plumb. Ice jams every winter lever it a little further. At some point—and I couldn't tell you when, that's the honest answer—the footing goes past the point where the masonry can hold its own weight plus the dead load of the span. When that happens, the whole center section folds. Not falls—*folds*, into the channel, in maybe ninety seconds."

"And you know when that point arrives how?"

I looked at the sky, at the fog burning off the ridge, at the angle of the light on the upstream face of the pier. Then I took off my boot, turned it over, and shook a pencil stub, a folded chart, and a small brass protractor out of the toe, because a man who lives under a borrowed name should at least carry his instruments honestly.

"Because bridges fail on a schedule," I said, "and the schedule is written in geometry. Come here and hold this end of the string."

* * *

What followed took an hour and looked, I suspect, like three lunatics measuring a corpse.

I sent Ferro scrambling up the abutment with the end of a fishing line weighted with a bolt, to hang it plumb against the upstream face of the near truss. From the gravel bar, using the protractor and the horizon, I measured the lean of the first span. Then I did the same by eye against the midstream pier, which required Odile to hold the boat steady in the current while I lay in the bow with the protractor balanced on my knee, close enough to the pier to read the masonry courses, close enough to the scour hole to feel the pull of the eddy trying to take us into it.

"Four inches when I inspected her," I said, reading the offset of the plumb line against the course lines. "She's at nine now. Nine and a half."

"Is that bad?" Ferro called down. "Nine sounds like a small number."

"Ferro, everything sounds like a small number until it's falling on you."

But nine inches told me what I needed. Masonry piers don't lean linearly—they lean slowly while the footing grinds, then quickly once the downstream edge starts crushing. The rate of change was the message. I sat on the gravel with the chart on my knee and worked it: settlement per year, multiplied by the ice season just past, corrected for the spring flood marks I could see stained on the pier's stones. The arithmetic was simple enough to do with a pencil stub, and simple arithmetic has a way of arriving like bad news.

"Half tide," I said. "Today, roughly—" I checked the sun against the ridge line— "two o'clock, give or take. The center span folds within the hour after that. Maybe sooner if the wind picks up out of the gorge."

Odile crouched beside me and looked at the numbers as if they were a foreign language she suspected was insulting her. "You're telling me the bridge knows what time it is."

"Bridges are the most punctual things men ever built. They keep their appointments to the inch and the minute. People are the ones who show up late." I rolled the chart. "We cross now, fast, single file, and we're on the far side before noon with hours to spare."

"And if your arithmetic is wrong?"

"Then I'll have been wrong about the same bridge twice, and there's a symmetry in that almost worth dying for."

She looked at me for a long moment. Seven days on the river had taught me her silences the way I'd once taught myself truss loads: there was the silence that meant *no*, the silence that meant *not yet*, and the silence that meant *I'm going to follow you into this stupid thing and I want you to know I know it's stupid*. This was the third kind.

"Single file," she said. "Lightest first. That's Ferro."

"Hey," said Ferro.

"She's right," I said. "Every pound matters on a structure this far gone. You go first because you weigh least and because if the deck fails, Odile and I are heavy enough to fall slower."

"That's not how falling works," Ferro said.

"It's exactly how falling works. Gravity is impartial but mass has momentum. Now move."

* * *

The climb to the deck level went up the old maintenance stair inside the abutment—iron treads gone to lace, but anchored into living stone, so every step held if you trusted the stone more than the iron. We came out onto the span through a hatch that shrieked like a scalded cat, and then there we were: two hundred feet above the green water, standing on a skeleton.

From a distance the viaduct had looked like a bridge. Up close it was a forest of rusted members, cross-braced and riveted, with the deck beams running transverse beneath our feet like ribs. Between them, gaps. Through the gaps, the river, moving fast enough that looking straight down produced a sensation I can only describe as the earth tilting.

"Don't look down," Odile said.

"Everyone says that," Ferro said, already looking down, gripping the south truss with both hands, face the color of the fog. "Nobody explains what to do instead."

"Look at me," I said. "Keep your eyes on my boots. Step where I step. Left hand on the lower chord, right hand free. If a member moves under your hand—a real movement, not vibration—you stop and you say so, and nobody panics, because a moving member means we have minutes, not seconds, and minutes are enough if we don't waste them screaming."

"And if it's seconds?" Odile asked.

"Then scream. It won't help, but I'd hate for your last act to be quiet on my account."

That got a laugh out of Ferro, thin and high, but a laugh. Good. Fear shared sideways is ballast; fear swallowed is cargo that shifts.

I went last. Somebody had to watch the others' footing, and somebody had to be the heaviest thing on the span, and I preferred that weight at the rear where I could feel the structure answer it. And answer it did—with every step, a low groan rolled through the iron ahead of us, traveling away toward the far abutment like a rumor.

"She talks," Odile said over her shoulder.

"They all talk. The trick is knowing which complaints matter." I pressed my palm flat against the lower chord. Warm from the sun, humming faintly. "This is normal. Expansion, wind, the river shaking her bones. What I'm listening for is a change in pitch. A structure under distress doesn't get louder—it gets higher. Squeals instead of groans. Metal shortening its sentences."

"You make it sound alive."

"Everything built bears weight the way people bear grief. Quietly, for a while, and then all at once, and usually in front of witnesses."

We made the first span in good time. The deck beams were sound enough underfoot—south side, sun side, honest iron—and where planks remained they were mostly trustworthy, tested first with a stomp and a listen. Twice I stopped us to shift route around a gap where the decking had gone entirely, and once Ferro's foot found a plank that wasn't there, and for one long second his whole body hung from his hands on the chord, boots bicycling over two hundred feet of nothing, before he swung a knee up and rolled onto the beam, breathing like a man surfacing.

"My fault," he gasped. "I looked down."

"Everyone looks down eventually. The trick is to do it while holding something." I hauled him upright. "Again. Eyes on my boots."

The midstream pier rose past us as we crossed the second span, and this close I could read it like a ledger. The mortar joints on the downstream face had opened into dark seams; a wedge of masonry the size of a coffin had already dropped from the cutwater into the scour hole below, and the water swirled around the wound without pause or pity. High on the stone, the stain-lines of this spring's flood. Lower, older stains, a history of the river rising year after year, patient, thorough, unimpressed by anything men had built.

I put my hand on the pier cap as we passed over it. Cold. And beneath the cold, a tremor—not the span's hum but something deeper, slower, the grinding of nine inches of lean working its way toward ten.

"Not long now," I murmured, and moved on.

* * *

The third span was the liar's side of the river's bargain—the north truss began here, and I kept us hard against the south rail, close enough that Ferro's shoulder brushed the rusted stanchions. Halfway across, the wind came up the gorge the way it does in the afternoon, funneling between the cliffs, and the whole structure leaned into it and sighed, and somewhere above us a cross-brace on the north side let go.

Not dramatically. That's the thing about failures that kill you—they rarely announce themselves properly. A single riveted connection parted with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and a length of angle iron swung down on its last bolt and hung there, turning slowly, ticking against the truss like a clock that had given up counting.

Ferro froze. Odile froze. I did the math.

"That's the north side," I said. "We're on the south. It changes nothing. Keep moving."

"It's *hanging there*," Ferro whispered.

"It's been hanging there for years in some form. Everything on this bridge is hanging somewhere. Move."

He moved. So did Odile, and so did I, and the wind gusted again and the hanging iron ticked and ticked, and I will admit—I, who had signed that bridge's death warrant with a steady hand six years ago—that my own pulse picked up. There's a difference between condemning a structure from a clipboard and hearing it die behind you. The report had been honest. The bridge was being honest back.

We came off the span onto the far abutment through a hatch that shrieked even worse than the first one, and then we were standing on solid mortared stone with the gorge at our backs, and Ferro sat down where he was and put his face in his hands and laughed until it nearly became the other thing.

"What time is it?" Odile asked.

I checked the sun. "Half past eleven."

"So we have—"

"Two and a half hours. Maybe less, with this wind loading the north truss." I looked back at the span. From this side, in full sun, it looked almost dignified—an old soldier at attention, unaware of the letter already drafted. "We portage the boat around the slide below and make Kestrel Falls by nightfall. The fleet can't beat us there now. Whatever Crane's engineers think they know, they don't know this river's shortcuts, and they certainly don't know her timetables."

We worked the rest of the morning hauling the boat along the cliff path, and I'll say this for fear: it makes strong backs. By the time we lowered her into the slack water above the slide, the bridge was nearly forgotten.

Nearly. Because as we pushed off, the wind dropped, the gorge went still, and across the water, clear as a bell, we heard the Kestrel Viaduct begin to speak.

Groans first. Then squeals—high, shortened sentences, exactly as promised. Then a sound I felt through the hull and up my arms before I heard it, a deep structural exhalation, the whole center span settling another fraction of an inch and finding, at the bottom of that fraction, nothing left to stand on.

"Off the water," I said quietly. "Now. Around the bend."

We rowed hard, rounded the bluff, and pulled the boat up among the alders. And there, through the trees, we watched the middle of the bridge fold—slowly, almost courteously, the way an old man sits down—until gravity remembered its appointment and took the rest. Two hundred feet of iron went into the channel in under two minutes, exactly as calculated, raising a wall of spray that drifted through the alders and beaded on our faces like the river applauding.

Ferro stared. "You knew. To the minute."

"To the hour," I said. "Bridges are punctual. I'm only approximate."

Odile was quiet a moment, watching the ripples spread. "That's a strange thing to be good at," she said finally. "Knowing exactly when things fall."

"It's the family business," I said. "Somebody in every generation has to count the seconds."

We pushed back onto the river, running now with the current toward Kestrel Falls and whatever waited there—and we'd gone perhaps a mile when Ferro, standing in the bow, went rigid and pointed downstream.

Smoke. Thin, black, deliberate—a signal fire on the point below the falls, and drawn up on the shingle beside it, a long white parley boat flying a private flag, with a man standing in the stern holding something that caught the light.

Not a weapon. A lantern, lit in broad daylight, burning oil that cost more than most men earned in a year.

"He's waiting for us," Ferro breathed.

"No," I said, watching the flag snap in the wind—a crane, wings spread, worked in gold thread. "He's waiting for *me*."
