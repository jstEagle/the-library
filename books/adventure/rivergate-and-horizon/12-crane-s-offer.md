## 12. Crane's Offer

The parley boat came out of the mist the way bad news always does—politely.

We were two miles below the gorge narrows, running the channel Odile had memorized from a chart she kept folded in her boot, of all places, when Ferro spotted it first. He didn't shout. He just went very still in the bow, the way a dog goes still before thunder, and pointed with his chin.

"Smoke," he said. "White smoke. Steam launch."

Odile took the field glasses off their hook and studied the shape resolving out of the river haze. "Gentleman's boat," she said at last, with the particular disgust she reserved for anything polished. "Teak rails. Brass fittings. Somebody's valet has been polishing that bell since dawn." She handed me the glasses. "And somebody aboard wants to talk, or they'd have run us down by now. That hull could do twenty knots and we're doing six on a good lie."

Through the lenses I saw what she meant. The launch was beautiful in the way expensive things are beautiful—unapologetically, almost insultingly—and she was coming alongside us at a courteous distance, matching our speed like a dance partner waiting to be asked. A man stood at her rail in a gray coat too clean for the river, holding a white handkerchief aloft on a boat hook. Not waving it. Displaying it. The way you'd display credentials.

"That's a parley flag," Ferro said quietly. "River law. Old law, but real. You stop, you listen, nobody touches anybody until the talking's done." He swallowed. "My father used to say the parley flag was the only honest thing left on this water. Then he'd say most men who fly it are liars anyway."

"Your father sounds like my kind of pessimist," Odile said.

"He drowned," Ferro said, "in a lock Crane's company let rot. So. Yes."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The launch's bell rang once, bright and absurd against the gulls and the water.

"Tamsin," Odile said, low, not looking at me. "You don't have to hear this."

"Yes, I do," I said. "I've spent seven years not hearing things. It hasn't worked yet."

* * *

We lashed alongside in the lee of a gravel bar, our little boat knocking gently against teak worth more than everything I owned in the world combined, which after seven years of borrowed names was: one duffel bag, one theodolite, and a tin cup with a dent shaped like the letter D, which I had always found faintly funny and now found unbearable.

The man in the gray coat introduced himself as Mr. Vess. He said it the way a man says his name when he expects you to have heard of it, and when it became clear we hadn't, he adjusted without missing a beat, which I'll admit took talent.

"You'd be the pilot Brack," he said to Odile, consulting a small notebook. "Ferro, late of the Dunmore Survey. And—" here he looked up, and his face did something careful and pleasant, "—you would be the engineer."

"I'd be a surveyor," I said. "Dunmore's dead. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows lots of things," Vess agreed pleasantly. "Mr. Crane is a great believer in knowing true things. It's why he sent me instead of, say, the harbor fleet's enforcement skiff, which is currently forty minutes behind us and carrying eight men who are paid by the result rather than the hour." He smiled as if sharing a joke among professionals. "You've been treated courteously so far. I'd like to keep it that way. May I sit?"

"There's no seat," Odile said.

Vess looked at the thwart, then at her, and sat on the gunwale of his own launch instead, balancing there with the ease of a man who had never once fallen off anything. "Then I'll stand. This won't take long, because Mr. Crane respects your time even if circumstances haven't respected yours."

He produced a leather folder and opened it on his knee like a man laying out a hand of cards he already knew he'd win.

"First," he said, "the money. Two thousand crowns, bank draft, payable tomorrow in any city you care to name. Second, the pardon—not the legal kind, those take courts, and courts are slow—but the practical kind. Every charge, every warrant, every file with your name attached, gone. Shredded, misfiled, lost in a flood, whatever verb you prefer; Mr. Crane owns several archives and all of their accidents. Third—" and here he paused, and for just a moment the polish thinned and something almost human looked out, "—the name. Tamsin Dunmore, restored. Walking around in daylight. Buying a drink under your own signature. Seven years is a long time to be nobody, engineer. We both know what that costs."

The river made its small noises underneath us. Ferro had gone gray. Odile's hand rested on the tiller, loose and ready, the way she held everything.

"And in exchange," I said.

"In exchange," Vess said, "you surrender the boy to Mr. Crane's custody—he'll be treated well, I give you my word, though I understand my word is currently valued somewhere near the mudflats—and you walk away. Upstream, downstream, overland, I genuinely don't care. You go back to being nobody, comfortably, and the Horizon collects what's hers, and everyone lives."

"And the documents?" Odile asked. "The inquiry records? The eleven families who've spent seven years being told their men died because an engineer miscalculated?"

Vess closed the folder halfway, then thought better of it and opened it again. Honest man, maybe. Or a man who knew that pretending would cost him more than candor.

"The documents," he said, "are going to burn, Ms. Brack. That isn't a threat, it's arithmetic. Mr. Crane has judges, clerks, editors, and two ministers. Even if those papers reached a courtroom—which they won't, because the wreck will be stripped and the hold pumped and every page turned to pulp by Friday—even if they did, they'd be ruled inadmissible, forged, or irrelevant inside a month. Justice isn't on the menu. What's on the menu is whether you eat it as a free man with money or a fugitive in irons." He spread his hands. "Mr. Crane finds sentiment expensive. He's offering to pay yours so you don't have to."

* * *

Here is the thing I will not pretend about, because pretending is the whole disease I'm trying to recover from: for about four seconds, I wanted it.

Not the money. Money stopped meaning anything to me around year three, when I realized I could map an entire abandoned valley and the only signature required was my own fake one. Not even the pardon, exactly, though God knows I'd rehearsed that fantasy on cold nights—the front door of Rivergate station opening, someone saying *welcome back, engineer*, the word *guilty* quietly deleted from the world.

It was the name. He'd found the soft place on the first try, the way a good agent always does. *Tamsin Dunmore, restored.* Seven years I'd been a ghost haunting my own life, signing other men's names to my own work, flinching when anyone said *Dunmore* like it was a curse word. And this man in the gray coat was offering to hand it back across the rail like a returned umbrella.

Four seconds. Then I looked at Ferro.

He was nineteen years old, half-starved, guilt riding him like a second skeleton because he'd led the fleet's scouts to his own crewmates through nothing worse than trusting the wrong voice at the wrong fire. He'd hidden in a culvert eating raw crayfish for three days rather than give up names. And Crane's offer, translated out of its polite language, was: *give us the boy, and you can be yourself again.*

There it was. The whole architecture of my disgrace, rebuilt in miniature, offered to me with a bank draft. Seven years ago, men like Vess had needed a scapegoat for a collapse that killed eleven workers, and they'd chosen me because I was convenient and because I was, in the end, expendable. Now the same machine was offering to un-choose me—one small trade, one boy, one signature. The machinery hadn't changed. Only the price tag had gotten friendlier.

"No," I said.

Vess nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something in his notes. "May I ask why? Professionally. I have to write a report, and 'he said no' reads poorly."

"Because the last time I traded my name for someone else's safety, eleven men died," I said. "I'm not doing it twice. Once was a mistake. Twice would be a policy."

Something flickered behind Vess's eyes—respect, maybe, or the memory of having once been a person who understood such sentences. He clicked the folder shut.

"For the record," he said, standing, brushing off his coat, "Mr. Crane predicted this. He said, and I quote, 'the engineer will refuse, because guilty men take offers and innocent men want trials, and he's spent seven years wanting his trial.'" He stepped back onto his own deck. "He also said to tell you the offer doesn't expire. It ripens. There's a difference, and you'll feel it before the falls."

The bell rang again, cheerful as a dinner gong, and the launch peeled away into the mist, leaving a wake that rocked us side to side like a scolding.

* * *

We ran on in silence for a while. The gorge narrowed ahead, walls closing in, the water going green and quick between them.

"Well," Odile said finally. "That was the politest attempt to buy a human being I've ever witnessed. I've seen cruder ones at fish markets."

"He offered me my name," I said. My voice came out strange, flat, like someone else reporting on me. "Seven years. Do you know what I'd have done with it, year one? Year two? I'd have crawled to Rivergate on my knees for that pardon. I'd have given him anything he asked."

"And now?" Odile asked. She wasn't mocking. She was navigating, the way she navigated channels—reading the water under the words.

"Now I don't know what I'd do," I admitted. "That's the poison of it. He wasn't wrong about me. I do want my trial. I've wanted it every single day. And he's right that I'll never get it—his judges, his clerks, his newspapers. So what's left? If justice can't come through the courts, and vindication can't come through the law, then what am I actually rowing toward? Because if it's just my own name back, then Crane wins either way. He can afford to lose the papers and win the man. He said as much. *The offer ripens.*"

Ferro spoke up from the bow, surprising us both. His voice cracked in the middle, the way nineteen-year-old voices do when they're carrying too much.

"My father used to say that when a rich man offers you a fair deal, count what he keeps, not what he gives." He turned around, and his jaw was set in a way that made him look briefly like a much older man. "He keeps the truth. Whatever you decide, Mr.—whatever your name is—that's what he keeps. Don't let him keep it."

The gorge swallowed the sound of the launch's engine somewhere behind us, or ahead of us, or both. Odile trimmed the sail and glanced at the sky, reading weather the way other people read letters.

"Fog's coming down off the high country," she said. "By morning it'll be thick enough to chew. Which means Kestrel Falls portage happens blind, with the fleet's engines close enough to hear." She looked at me, and there was no comfort in her face, only partnership, which is worth more. "So whatever question Crane planted in you, engineer, you'd best answer it before the fog does. Rivers don't wait for men to finish their philosophy."

I took the tiller from her and steered us into the narrowing green dark of the gorge, and the question rode along with us, patient as ballast: *what if the man who gets his name back and the man who deserves it aren't the same man anymore—and which one am I bringing upriver?*

Ahead, faint through the gathering mist, came the first far-off mutter of Kestrel Falls.
