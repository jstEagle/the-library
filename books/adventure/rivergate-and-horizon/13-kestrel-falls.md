## 13. Kestrel Falls

The fog came up the gorge like something being poured.

Odile Brack had piloted the Kestrel reach in every weather the river could invent—ice fog, coal smoke, the gray wool of a spring thaw—but she had never seen it this thick, this deliberate. It didn't drift. It arrived. One moment she could see the far bank's black pines; the next, the world was the width of her own outstretched arms and nothing else, not even Tamsin at the bow, though she could hear him coiling rope by sound alone, each loop landing soft as a held breath.

"Engines," Ferro whispered from the stern. "Still behind us."

"I know." She did know. She'd been hearing them for an hour now—a low, patient thrum through the water, the way you feel a train before you see it. The fleet wasn't hurrying anymore. That was worse than hurrying. A man who hurries is worried. A man who slows down is *sure*.

"Portage point should be on the left," Tamsin said. His voice had that flat calm she was learning to distrust and rely on in equal measure, like a bridge rated for exactly as much weight as you were about to put on it. "There's an old haul road above the falls. My crew cut it."

*Your crew*, Odile thought, and said nothing. Seven years of hiding and he still said *my crew* the way other men said *my hands*. Some things a man couldn't borrow a name big enough to cover.

She brought the boat in against the current, feathering the tiller, feeling for the eddy line with her palms more than her eyes. The fog gave them one mercy: the falls themselves were invisible, which meant they were also, briefly, deniable. You could almost pretend the roar on your left was wind.

"There." Tamsin's arm materialized out of the white. A stone landing, moss-slick, and beyond it the ghost of a road climbing into the trees.

They got the boat over on rollers and their backs, which took the better part of two hours and cost them all whatever dignity they had left. Odile hauled the bow line; Tamsin took the stern and most of the actual weight, because of course he did, the stubborn mule; Ferro carried the gear in three trips because he refused to take less and refused to be left behind, both at once, which told Odile more about the boy than anything he'd said since the culvert.

Halfway up the haul road, Ferro sat down on a stump without meaning to. His legs simply ended the conversation.

"Five minutes," Tamsin said, and set his load down.

"We don't have five minutes."

"We have exactly five minutes," Tamsin said. "A man who won't rest when the rest is cheap pays for it later when it's expensive. I learned that from a foreman who never once took his own advice."

Odile lowered herself onto a boulder, worked a pebble out of her boot, and let the fog press close around the three of them. Somewhere below, the falls went on roaring to an audience of nobody. Somewhere behind, the engines kept their patient time. It occurred to her, sitting there in the wet gray middle of everything, that she had spent four days in a boat with two men and had not once asked the question that had been sitting in her chest since the boathouse like a stone in a shoe.

She asked it now, because the fog made it easy, because you can say hard things to people you can't quite see.

"Ferro. The night your crew stopped answering radio. What actually happened up there?"

The boy looked at his hands. Young hands, river-ruined already, nails cracked from hauling. "We found her," he said. "The Meridian Star."

* * *

He talked slowly at first, the way men do when they've rehearsed a lie so long the truth has to be dug out around it.

"The Horizon's advance party had been over the wreck twice before we got there. We knew that from the tide tables—they dive sloppy, leave gear. So Mr. Halloway—" the name caught in his mouth, and Odile saw Tamsin go very still across the trail, "—Mr. Halloway planned our dives for the dead-water window between tides, when their cranes couldn't work. We went down into the cargo hold through the gash on the starboard side."

"The inquiry records," Tamsin said quietly. "That's what you were after."

"That's what we thought we were after. Papers. Depositions. The clerk's case." Ferro wiped his face with a sleeve that was more river than sleeve. "It was there. Sealed tin case, clerk's initials stenciled on it, wedged under a collapsed bulkhead frame like somebody had hidden it in a hurry. But that's not what stopped us. What stopped us was the logbook."

Odile felt the word land. "The Meridian Star's own log?"

"The ship's log. Official. Signed by the master." Ferro's voice had gone strange, thin, like wire pulled too tight. "We brought it up thinking it might show where the records were stored. Instead—" He stopped. Started again. "Instead it shows the last week of her life. She was chartered out of Crane's own shipping office. Her final entry orders her to hold position over the wreck site of the Dunmore collapse—not to salvage, not to survey. To *hold*. And then there's an entry dated the night she sank, in the master's hand, saying he'd received instructions to open the sea valves and abandon with all hands' pay doubled, and that he'd refused, and that he was writing it down because if anything happened he wanted it written somewhere that wasn't Crane's paper."

The fog seemed to lean in.

"He scuttled her himself?" Odile said. "Crane ordered the Meridian Star sunk—with the inquiry aboard—and the master wrote it down rather than do it, and then—"

"And then the ship sank anyway," Ferro said. "With him aboard. With eleven of the inquiry's witnesses and clerks and divers aboard. The log says the master locked the case in the strong room and stayed at the wheel. The last entry is just his name and the words *I will not be party to murder.*"

Nobody spoke. Below them the falls filled the silence, doing what rivers do with everything eventually.

Tamsin stood up very slowly, the way a man stands when the ground has moved and he wants to check whether it's him or the world. "Seven years," he said. "Seven years I've been running from a crime, and the proof of who committed it has been lying in forty feet of cold water the whole time. Not lost. Not destroyed. *Waiting.*" He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "I mapped every mile of dead rail in this valley. I surveyed decay for a living. And the answer to my whole life was sitting under a buoy I've rowed past."

"You couldn't have known," Odile said.

"No. But Crane could have. He's known for seven years. That's why the Horizon's here. That's why my crew went silent." He turned to Ferro, and even through the fog Odile could see the change in his face—the engineer's calm cracking to show the thing underneath, which was not rage exactly but something older and more tired. "The advance party. When they found out your crew had the logbook—"

"They didn't find out," Ferro said. "That's the thing. That's the whole terrible thing." He was crying now, quietly, the way boys cry when they've decided crying doesn't excuse anything. "Halloway split us. He sent me and Dree and the tin case downstream toward Rivergate, and he kept the logbook with the others, planning to follow at second light. He thought splitting the evidence meant splitting the risk. But the fleet's scouts were already in the valley—I led them straight to our cache, I *showed* them where we slept, I thought they were fishermen, I *told* them about the second group—" His breath hitched. "They got the others because of me. They got Halloway because of me. And I've been hiding in a culvert eating raw crayfish ever since because I couldn't make myself go back and I couldn't make myself run any further either."

The fog held them all in its gray palm.

"Ferro." Tamsin crouched in front of the boy, and Odile watched him do it—watched the man who had spent seven years perfecting the art of distance choose, deliberately, to close it. "Look at me. Did you sink the Meridian Star?"

"No."

"Did you kill those men seven years ago?"

"No."

"Did you betray your crew, or did you walk into a trap built before you were born, by a man rich enough to build it slowly?" Tamsin's voice was steady, but Odile heard the effort in it, the way you hear effort in a bridge under test load. "Because I've spent seven years asking myself versions of that question, and I'll tell you what I finally learned: guilt is honest, but it's a terrible navigator. It'll steer you into a culvert every time."

Ferro made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "You're quoting yourself."

"I'm quoting a foreman who never took his own advice. I told you." Tamsin put a hand on the boy's shoulder, brief and hard, then stood. "The logbook's gone. The fleet has it, or Crane does. What they don't have is the tin case, because you and Dree got it downstream, and Dree—"

"Dree's on the Horizon," Ferro said, and the bottom dropped out of the morning again. "Him and Halloway both. The scouts took them alive. That's why the fleet slowed down. They're not chasing us anymore. They're *escorting* us."

* * *

They reached the head of the portage an hour later, where the haul road crested a shoulder of granite above the falls and the fog, for one merciful moment, thinned enough to show them the world.

Below, the river gathered itself and threw itself down Kestrel Falls in three long silver steps, and below that, far below, the channel ran wide and brown toward the low country. And behind them—Odile raised the field glasses and her stomach performed a slow, professional roll—the fog was peeling back off the lower gorge to reveal masts. Four of them. Then six. The Horizon leading, black-hulled, with a crane arm folded along her deck like a sleeping mantis, and behind her the salvage barges in echelon, moving at a speed no honest salvage fleet had ever moved at, flying a private flag she recognized from harbor gossip and bad dreams: a white horizon-line on green.

"They're through the Narrows," she said. "One tide behind us. Maybe less, if that fog breaks for them the way it just broke for us."

"Then we go down the steps," Tamsin said.

Odile lowered the glasses. "The *steps*."

"My crew blasted a fish ladder through the east chute in the first year of the survey. For moving equipment. It's narrow, it's ugly, and it drops thirty feet in three stages, but it's water all the way down, and it comes out below the falls where their cranes can't follow until the channel widens." He was already measuring with his eyes, reading the white water the way other men read print. "Your boat can run it."

"*Can* is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"Tamsin," Ferro said, and then blinked, because apparently he'd only just noticed he'd used the name. The real one. It hung in the fog between them, naked and enormous.

Tamsin didn't flinch. Odile watched him not flinch and understood that something had finished settling in the man during that conversation on the stump—some long argument concluded. "It's all right," he said, to the boy, or maybe to the name itself. "Somebody was going to say it eventually."

"It's a good name," Ferro offered, wobbly.

"It's a heavy one. That's rather the point." He turned back to the falls. "Odile. The east chute. Can you run it?"

She looked at the water. Thirty feet of drop in three stages, in a boat loaded past its rating, with a fleet of hired wolves one tide behind and two prisoners aboard the lead ship whose lives were now, apparently, arithmetic. She thought about her brother, who had died in the Dunmore collapse with his pay ledger in his pocket showing he was owed nine days' wages, and how she had promised herself, standing at his funeral in the rain, that she would never again let another man's schedule decide what she did with a river.

Then she thought about the fish ladder, and grinned despite herself, because it was such a perfectly Tamsin solution: not brave, not clever, just *engineered*—a way through that existed because seven years ago a careful man had imagined someone desperate standing exactly where she was standing now.

"Aye," she said. "But you're going to wish you hadn't asked."

* * *

The east chute was everything she'd feared and one thing she hadn't: it worked.

She ran it the way her father had taught her to run water you couldn't see—by ear, by the pull on the hull, by the smell of spray changing pitch. First stage: a slot barely a boat's width, walls of wet granite pressing close enough that Ferro could have touched both sides at once, dropping them into a boiling pool with a slam that rearranged the cargo. Second stage: a slide, actually fun, in the way that things are fun afterward and terrifying during. Third stage: the big one, where the ladder's builders had cut a ramp through the lip of the fall itself, and the boat went down it like a toboggan with opinions, and Odile shouted something joyful and profane that she would deny under oath, and then they were through, into the wide brown quiet below the falls, whole and upright and laughing in the shocked way of people who have recently expected to die.

Even Tamsin laughed—one short bark, quickly suppressed, but she heard it, and she banked it away like evidence.

They drifted while she bailed and Ferro re-lashed the gear, and the fog closed in again behind them, sealing the falls away, and ahead the channel opened toward the low country and the sixth tide and whatever waited at the wreck.

"So the truth is assembled," Ferro said at last. He said it carefully, like setting down something breakable. "The logbook proves Crane sank the Meridian Star with the inquiry aboard. The tin case proves what the inquiry found—that the collapse was engineered, that the piers were undercut on purpose, that eleven men were murdered for an insurance payout and a land option. Between the two of them, it's the whole story, start to finish. Except half of it is in Crane's safe by now, and the other half is in a strong room under forty feet of water, and the men who can testify are chained to his crane ship."

"Not chained," Odile said. "Leverage. There's a difference, and the difference is that leverage gets released the moment it stops being useful." She heard her own voice go flat and cold and let it. "Which means our job isn't just to get the documents. It's to make sure Crane never reaches the point where they stop being useful."

Tamsin was silent at the bow for a long moment. When he spoke, the fog nearly ate the words.

"Fourth tide is tomorrow's dawn window," he said. "Low water, dead calm, the hold accessible for maybe ninety minutes. If we're at the wreck when it opens, we can get the case. If the fleet is there first—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to.

Ahead, faint through the fog, the river ran on toward the sea, and somewhere on it, black and patient, the Horizon was already waiting.

Odile put the tiller over and pointed the bow into the dark.
