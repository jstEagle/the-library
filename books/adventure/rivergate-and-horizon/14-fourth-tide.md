## 14. Fourth Tide

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and the river began to give back what it had borrowed.

Tamsin felt it before he saw it—a change in the pull against the hull, the way a man feels a handshake loosen. He stood at the rail of Odile's boat with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea that had gone cold an hour ago, watching the water fall away from the rocks below Kestrel Falls like a curtain being drawn by patient hands.

"There," Odile said quietly beside him. She pointed with her chin, because her hands were busy coiling rope the way she always did when she was nervous—fast, precise, as if the rope might get away from her otherwise.

Below them, emerging from the brown water in stages, lay the ribs of the Meridian Star.

He had imagined it a hundred ways over seven years—burned, broken, scattered across the riverbed like dropped toys. What he had not imagined was how *orderly* it looked. The old salvage ship sat upright on the mud, canted maybe fifteen degrees to port, her superstructure collapsed but her hull whole, as if she'd simply knelt down into the silt one day and decided to stay. River weed hung from her rails in long green banners. A heron stood on her bridge wing, motionless, regarding them with the flat indifference of a creature that has outlasted something.

"Seven years," Ferro said behind them. His voice still hadn't recovered fully from the culvert—the words came out scraped and small—but there was wonder in it now, pushing through the fear. "She's been down here seven years and nobody came."

"Somebody came," Odile said. "Somebody scuttled her."

The words settled over the deck like fog. Nobody answered them. There wasn't anything to answer *with*.

* * *

They anchored above the wreck in the slack water between tides, and Odile laid out the plan on the chart table with the economy of a woman who had run this river through two floods and one fire and did not waste breath.

"The hold is under the forward cargo deck. At low water you'll have maybe four feet of air space near the bow, less toward the stern. The tide comes back fast here—faster than anywhere else in the valley, because the gorge squeezes it. When I ring the bell twice, you come up. Not when you're finished. When I ring." She looked at Tamsin over the lamp. "I mean that. I've pulled drowned men out of this river. They all thought they had more time."

"How much time do we have?" Ferro asked.

"Two hours if the river's feeling generous. Ninety minutes if it isn't." She paused, and something flickered across her face—not quite humor, but its cousin. "And it hasn't been feeling generous since the day I met you two."

Tamsin checked the gear for the third time. Two sets of diving apparatus, salvaged from a lock-keeper's shed three miles back—old-fashioned hard-hat rigs, brass and canvas, the kind that breathed on surface air pumped down a line. Odile had found them wrapped in oiled cloth like a gift nobody had ever claimed. They were older than the wreck itself, probably, and Tamsin had spent half the night going over every gasket and valve because he trusted his own inspection more than he trusted luck.

"You could wait aboard," he said to Ferro, not for the first time. "You've done your part. More than."

Ferro shook his head. His face in the lamplight was still hollowed out, cheekbones standing sharp, but his eyes were steady. "Mr. Dunmore—I led their scouts to my own crew. I sat in a culvert eating snails while Halloway and the others—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "I'm coming. If there's anything left of them down there, or anything they left, I want to be the one carrying it up. That's not courage. It's just arithmetic."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Seven years ago, Ferro would have been too young to hire. Now he was the only member of the Dunmore Survey Tamsin had seen since the world closed over his head, and he was volunteering to go down into a flooded grave.

"All right," Tamsin said. "Arithmetic."

* * *

The water took him the way cold rivers do—all at once, a fist closing around his chest, then a strange calm as the helmet filled with sound and the world narrowed to lamplight and breathing.

Down through the green murk, along the Meridian Star's flank. The hull rose beside him like a canyon wall, furred with weed, and his lamp played across rivet lines and portholes gone blind with silt. Fish scattered at the edge of the light, quick silver question marks. Somewhere above, muffled through forty feet of water and brass, he could hear Odile working the air pump—rhythmic, steady, the heartbeat of the operation.

The cargo hatch stood open. Of course it did; the sea doesn't close doors behind people who are never coming back.

He went in.

The hold was a cathedral of ruin. His lamp swept across fallen derricks, crates burst open and fanned with sediment, a cargo net hanging from the overhead like a hammock someone had stepped out of mid-dream. The four feet of air Odile had promised existed as a low ceiling of black water-mirror, trembling with every ripple, reflecting his lamp back at him upside down. He moved through the flooded lower half, boots stirring up clouds that glowed and drifted and slowly settled, and it occurred to him—with the detached clarity that deep water imposes—that he was walking through the exact moment his life had broken, preserved like a fly in amber.

The inquiry records had been loaded aboard this ship seven years ago, bound for the capital and the tribunal that would have cleared his name. He had known that even then, running north with nothing but a bedroll and a false name. He had known the proof of his innocence was somewhere behind him, sinking or burning or locked in some Crane warehouse, and he had chosen not to look back. You can't spend seven years not looking back unless you believe there's nothing back there worth finding.

There had been. It had been here the whole time, waiting in the dark.

His boot struck something solid. He looked down.

A desk. An actual wooden desk, bolted to the deck, the kind that lived in a purser's office—and beside it, wedged beneath a fallen shelf, a strongbox case, steel-strapped, government-stamped. Even crusted in seven years of silt, the seal on the clasp was intact. INQUIRY OF THE RIVERGATE COMMISSION, the stencil read, and beneath it a number Tamsin knew better than his own birthday, because it was the number of the hearing he had never attended.

His heart slammed against the inside of the helmet so hard he heard it echo.

He got his hands under the shelf and lifted. Rust flaked, wood groaned, silt billowed—and the case slid free into his arms, heavier than paper had any right to be, heavy with everything it held.

That was when his lamp caught the hand.

* * *

It reached out from under the desk, or what remained of it—bones in a rotted sleeve, fingers curled not in a claw but almost gently, the way a sleeping man rests his hand on a table. The rest of him was folded into the gap between the desk and the bulkhead, small now, diminished, a clerk's coat still buttoned to the collarbone. On the coat, above the breast pocket, a badge of office had survived: tarnished brass, but legible. CLERK OF THE COMMISSION.

Tamsin went very still. In the helmet, his own breathing sounded enormous, tidal.

He knew the name without looking for it. Everyone connected to the inquiry had known it. Elias Wrenn, the commission's records clerk—the quiet man with ink-stained cuffs who had interviewed Tamsin for six hours before the collapse, taking down everything in a hand so neat it looked printed. The last person to examine the evidence honestly. The man who had insisted the records travel by sea rather than rail, because he didn't trust who controlled the railroads.

Crane controlled the sea route instead. And the Meridian Star had gone down in calm weather with eleven crew and one clerk aboard, and the inquiry had been adjourned for lack of documentation, and a railroad engineer named Tamsin Dunmore had been convicted in absentia by a court that no longer needed to pretend.

Wrenn had died down here with his arms around the records. Not near them. *Around* them—the position of the bones said it plainly, the body curled over the case like a man shielding a child. He had known the ship was going down, and he had spent his last minutes doing the only thing left to him: holding on.

Seven years. Seven years the dead man had kept faith in a flooded room while the living forgot there was anything to keep.

Tamsin knelt in the silt beside him, the case balanced on his knee, and did the thing he had not done at any point in seven years of exile. He wept. It was awkward and graceless inside the helmet, tears cutting channels through the grime on his face, breath hitching loud in his own ears, and it went on longer than he would have guessed he had grief left to spend. He had thought he'd cried himself dry years ago, on a night train north. He had been wrong. There had been more, stored away, waiting—like everything else—for the tide to bring it back up.

When it passed, he did what a surveyor does. He worked.

From his kit he took a small tin he'd carried since Rivergate, since before Rivergate, since a life that seemed to belong to another man. Inside were two things: a folding knife and a stub of pencil. He broke the pencil in half and set one piece in the dead man's palm, closing the bone fingers around it. It meant nothing. It meant everything. It was what a surveyor leaves at a datum point: *you were here, and I have marked it, and you will not be lost.*

"I'll take it from here," he said aloud, and heard his voice boom strangely in the helmet, absurd and holy in the flooded cathedral. "You did your watch, Mr. Wrenn. Go off duty."

Then he gathered the case against his chest, hooked his lift line to it, and gave the signal rope two sharp pulls.

* * *

Ferro surfaced ten minutes after him, gray-faced and shaking, clutching a second find: a canvas dispatch bag, waterlogged but sealed, with the Dunmore Survey's own stencil on the side. Halloway's crew had gotten this far, then. Had touched this same hold, maybe seen these same bones—or maybe the Horizon's advance party had driven them off before they ever reached the stern office. Ferro wouldn't say what he'd seen down there. He came up the ladder in silence and sat on the deck with the bag in his lap and his eyes fixed on the middle distance, and Tamsin didn't ask. Some rooms you don't follow a man into.

Odile rang the bell twice with thirty minutes of tide to spare, and they hauled the finds aboard like midwives delivering something enormous.

The case came open on the chart table under the lamp. Odile cut the government seal with her belt knife, carefully, the way you'd cut a bandage off an old wound. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen, packed tight as a ship's biscuit: the inquiry transcripts. Witness statements. Engineering reports—Tamsin's own surveys among them, his handwriting jumping out at him like a stranger's face in a crowd. Load manifests for the Meridian Star showing cargo weight falsified in three places. And at the bottom, in a separate envelope marked in Elias Wrenn's immaculate hand, a single sheet:

*If this reaches anyone: the sinking was ordered. I have recorded by whom, and how. Do not trust the tribunal. Trust the families. They will not be bought, because everything that could be bought was already taken from them.*

Silence held the cabin. The lamp hissed. Out on the black water, the river ran soft and endless toward the falls.

"He knew," Ferro whispered. "Seven years ago, he knew, and he wrote it down, and he—" His voice cracked. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "We were right above him. All this time. Everybody in Rivergate was right above him."

"Not everybody," Odile said. Her voice was rough. She was looking at the manifest, at the name signed in the margin of the falsified load order—a name Tamsin recognized, and knew she recognized too, though neither of them said it. Some names you say out loud when you're ready to act on them. This was not yet that night. "Somebody kept the case dry. Somebody built that seal to last. Somebody down there made sure that when the right man finally came, there'd be something left to find."

"The right man," Tamsin repeated. He laughed, or something adjacent to laughter, tired and strange. "I spent seven years making sure I'd never be found by anybody."

"Well," Odile said, folding the oilcloth with the brisk tenderness of a woman wrapping bread. "You were found anyway. Turns out the dead are more patient than the living, and considerably better organized."

For the first time since the culvert, Ferro smiled. It was a small, cracked thing, but it was real.

Tamsin stood at the table with the transcripts under his hands and felt the strangest sensation of his long strange exile: not triumph, not relief, but *weight*—the good kind, the kind a foundation has. For seven years he had been a ghost haunting his own life, and ghosts have no duties. Now he had a case full of paper and a dead clerk's instructions and eleven families upriver who had waited just as long as Elias Wrenn had.

The bell on Odile's mast ticked against its bracket in the current. Below them the tide was already turning, the river beginning its slow inhale, filling the channel, covering the wreck, covering the little tin with the pencil stub, covering everything except what they carried.

"We make the families by the sixth tide," Tamsin said. "Whatever it costs."

"Ambitious," Odile said. "Considering—"

She stopped. She had turned toward the window, and the look on her face made both men turn too.

Far downstream, where the gorge bent toward the lower locks, lights were moving on the water. Not lanterns—searchlights. White beams sweeping the cliffs in long deliberate strokes, and beneath them, faint across the miles of black river, the deep industrial throb of engines too many and too large to belong to anything honest.

Odile raised her field glasses. Lowered them. Her voice, when it came, was flat as slate.

"That's the Horizon. And she's not a tide ahead of us." She handed him the glasses. "She's two."
