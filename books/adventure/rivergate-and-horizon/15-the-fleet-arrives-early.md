## 15. The Fleet Arrives Early

Odile Brack had a theory about rivers and liars: both moved fastest when they were carrying something they didn't want you to see.

She was thinking about this at dawn on the fourth day, crouched in the reeds above the narrows below Kestrel Falls, because the river was moving fast. Too fast. The current had come up overnight with the rain in the high country, which she'd expected — but the water wasn't what had woken her. What had woken her was a sound.

Engines. Not one engine. Many engines, bunched close together, running hard against the current, and coming from downstream.

She put the field glasses to her eyes and waited for the bend to give them up.

The Horizon came around the headland first, black-hulled, riding low and mean, her crane arm folded flat like a mantis at prayer. Behind her came two barges and a tender, all flying the same private flag — a white gull on a field of grey, the Crane mark, stitched so large it could be read from the far shore. Odile counted heads along the railings and stopped counting at thirty. Thirty men, at least, where yesterday's intelligence — Ferro's intelligence, wrung out of him in shivering fragments between mouthfuls of Tamsin's salt pork — had promised no more than twelve aboard an advance party still two tides behind them.

Two tides behind. She lowered the glasses and did the arithmetic again, hoping it would come out differently. It never does, she told herself. Arithmetic is honest even when people aren't.

"They're early," said Ferro beside her, who had a gift for stating the obvious that would have gotten him thrown out of any pilot's guild in the valley. "That's — they can't be. We'd have heard them through the night."

"You slept through the night," Odile said. "You snored through the night. I listened to the night."

Ferro had the grace to look ashamed of the snoring, at least. He was still half-starved, still wrapped in every spare layer they owned, but three days of food and one good portage had put some color back in him, and color meant opinions, and opinions meant he wouldn't stop talking now unless she made him. She didn't make him. It was easier to let him talk than to let herself think about what the engines meant.

"Someone told them," Ferro said quietly, and there it was — the thought she'd been circling since before dawn, laid out flat between them like a fish on a cutting board.

"Someone told them," Odile agreed.

* * *

They went back down to the boat, where Tamsin was already awake, because Tamsin Dunmore struck her as a man who considered sleep a professional courtesy extended only to people whose consciences were lighter than his survey instruments. He was crouched over the chart case with a pencil stub, marking the falls approach, and he looked up when their footsteps came down the bank, and he read her face the way he read waterlines — quickly, completely, without flattery.

"How early?" he asked.

"A full tide," Odile said. "Maybe more. They're running the narrows under power, which means either their pilot is very good or very drunk, and nobody drunk flies a flag that clean."

Tamsin stood. He didn't say anything for a moment, and Odile watched him do the thing he did — the engineer's thing — where the panic went somewhere internal and got converted into numbers. She'd known men who panicked outward, loud and useful in their way; she'd known men who froze; but watching Tamsin convert fear into arithmetic at speed was like watching a lock take a boat up a rise. You couldn't see the mechanism. You just saw the level change.

"Then we have hours, not days," he said. "Can we run the falls?"

"Not in fog, not loaded, not with him." She jerked her chin at Ferro, who had the decency to look relieved rather than insulted. "We portage. It costs us four hours and buys us our lives. That's the trade."

"Then we start now."

"We start now," she agreed, "but Tamsin." She waited until he looked at her. "There's something else. Someone told them where we'd be. Someone who knew the route, the timing, the tide windows. That's not salvage gossip. That's pilot's knowledge."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. "You have a list," he said.

"I have a list," Odile said. "And I hate everyone on it."

* * *

The portage was brutal work and Odile was grateful for it, because brutal work leaves no room in the skull for anything except the next hundred yards. They hauled the boat on rollers Tamsin improvised from fence posts and barrel staves, up the old towpath that hadn't seen a tow since before the war, through alder scrub wet enough to soak a man to the ribs in twenty minutes. Ferro carried the lightest load and complained about it anyway, which Odile found oddly restorative. There was something honest about complaint. It was the opposite of a man who scuttles a ship full of inquiry records and calls it weather.

It was somewhere past the second mile, while they paused to breathe and eat cold oatcake, that Ferro said the thing that turned her stomach over.

"The harbor master's launch passed us the night before the Horizon's scouts found us," he said. He was looking at his oatcake as if it might confess something. "I didn't think anything of it. Vessels pass. But he slowed down. Asked our business. And Master Pell — my crew chief — gave him the truth, because Pell trusted harbor men the way you trust lighthouses."

Odile sat very still.

"Name," she said.

"Gorrick," Ferro said. "Harbor master Gorrick."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. Not because it was a stranger's name. Because it was *Emmett Gorrick's* name — the man who'd signed her first pilot's license, who'd taught her to read the Kestrel eddies, who kept a tin of peppermints on his desk for the children of the harbor families and a ledger behind his desk for everybody else. The man whose voice she could hear right now, clear as a bell across thirty years: *A pilot who lies about the river will lie about everything else, girl, so don't start.*

"He tipped the fleet," Ferro said. "Or someone did using his launch. I'm sorry. I should have said sooner. I keep finding things I should have said sooner."

"Don't apologize for other men's rot," Odile said, more sharply than she meant to, and then, because fairness was a habit she couldn't break: "But yes. Sooner would have been better."

She looked downriver, back the way they'd come, toward the harbor town she'd called home her whole life, and felt something shift in her that she suspected would not shift back. It wasn't heartbreak exactly. Heartbreak is sudden. This was slower and worse — it was the sound of a foundation settling, one crack at a time, each one small enough to ignore until you realized the whole house was listening to itself come apart.

Gorrick. Peppermints. Gorrick. She tried to build the betrayal into a shape that made sense and couldn't, and then she could, and wished she couldn't. Because it did make sense. It made perfect sense. Gorrick had been harbor master through the lean years after the collapse, when the river traffic died and the salvage rights got sold off cheap to men with grey flags, and every year his ledgers balanced a little too neatly for a man whose harbor had nothing left to balance. She'd noticed. She'd noticed and filed it away as luck, or thrift, or the mysterious competence of old men. People see what they can bear to see. She'd learned that on the river long ago; apparently she'd needed to learn it again about people.

"Tamsin," she called down the path, where he was chocking the rollers. "Your list of men who profited from the collapse. Does it include a harbor master?"

He came back up the slope without hurrying, which was its own answer. "It includes every man whose books improved the year eleven workers died," he said. "Gorrick took the salvage concession on the upper channel. Why?"

"Because he sold us," Odile said. "Or sold the crew, at least. Ferro says his launch ran the night before the scouts hit them."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then he said, "Did you know?"

"No," she said. And then, because lying to this man felt like littering in a cathedral: "I suspected nothing. That's worse than knowing and staying quiet. Knowing takes a decision. I just... didn't look."

"Seven years ago I didn't look either," Tamsin said. "Whole town didn't look. That's how men like Crane afford grey flags." He picked up his end of the boat. "Come on. We're losing the tide we don't have."

* * *

They made the head of the falls by midafternoon, and that was where the fog found them — rolling up the gorge in fat white ropes, swallowing the far bank, turning the world down to forty feet of wet grey certainty. Odile stood at the lip of the portage trail and listened. Under the fog, under the falls' permanent roar, the engines were louder now. Close. An hour out, maybe less, if they were pushing the narrows blind, which they were, because men with cranes and schedules don't wait for weather.

"They'll ground themselves on the shallows if they run blind," Ferro offered hopefully.

"They won't," Odile said. "They bought a pilot. A real one, or a bribed one." She thought of the pilots' guild rolls, the names she'd checked twice last night by lantern light, and the ugly little asterisk she'd finally set beside one of them. Dree Halloran. Young, hungry, deep in debt to the harbor office — to Gorrick's office — for his license fees. She'd lent the boy her own chart of the narrows two winters ago. He'd thanked her with a pie.

Everyone on this river fed you something, she reflected bitterly, right up until they sold you.

Tamsin appeared at her shoulder, fog beading in his hair. "How long to move the boat through the falls portage and relaunch below?"

"With three of us? Six hours if the rain holds off. Four if it doesn't and we're idiots."

"Let's be idiots," he said, and there — there it was, the ghost of the man the valley used to know, the one who'd once talked a whole bridge crew into working through a flood with nothing but confidence and a thermos. Odile surprised herself by laughing, short and sharp, the laugh of a woman who has just watched her hometown's foundations crack and decided the alternative to laughing was screaming.

"Four hours," she said. "Move."

* * *

They were halfway down the portage trail, boat groaning on its rollers, when the first engine broke through the fog above them — close, shockingly close, the sound bouncing off the gorge walls so it seemed to come from everywhere. Ferro froze. Tamsin didn't; he put his shoulder harder into the hull and said, "They can't see us. Keep moving. Sound lies in fog; distance doesn't."

But Odile climbed the little rise instead, one last time, and pulled out the glasses, and looked down through a tear in the fog at the river below the falls.

The Horizon slid through the white like a knife through milk. On her foredeck, searchlights swept uselessly through the vapor, and figures moved along her rails, and at her stern the grey flag hung dead in the windless air. And standing at her bow rail, leaning on it with the easy posture of a man admiring property he already considered his, was a figure in a pale coat whom she didn't need glasses to identify, though she used them anyway, because some things deserved to be seen clearly.

Aldous Crane. In person. A full tide early, on waters he had no legal right to run, guided by a boy whose pie she could still taste.

Below her, Tamsin had stopped hauling. He was looking up at the rise, at her face, and whatever he saw there made him set down his end of the boat and climb up beside her, slow, like a man approaching a wound.

"That's him," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"That's him," Tamsin said.

"Then here's what I want you to understand," Odile said, and her voice came out low and steady, which surprised her, because inside she was burning like a signal fire. "I spent my life believing the river was honest and the town was honest and the men who ran both were mostly decent, mostly trying, mostly worth trusting. I believed it the way you believe in water. I've lost the town today, Tamsin. Gorrick saw to that. Which means the river is the only honest thing I've got left — and your wreck is sitting in my river, holding the proof of what these men did. So we are going to reach it. Do you hear me? Whatever it costs, whatever tide it takes. These are my waters, and they do not get to dredge their sins out of them and burn them."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. Fog dripped off the alder branches. Below them, faint through the roar of the falls, came the clang of the Horizon's crane arm unlocking for work.

"You know," he said at last, almost gently, "for seven years I thought I was the only one the river was keeping secrets for."

"It keeps secrets for anyone who'll carry them," Odile said. "Now pick up your end of the boat. Low water's in five hours, and we're going to be early too."

She turned down the trail — and stopped, because through the fog, thin and distorted and unmistakably human, a voice was calling across the water from the direction of the wreck site. Calling a name.

Calling *Dunmore*.
