## 16. Flooded Hold

The tide turned the way bad news always does—slowly at first, then all at once.

Tamsin felt it before he saw it: a change in the water's voice against the Meridian Star's hull, a low groan traveling up through the ribs of the wreck like an old man shifting in his sleep. He was chest-deep in the cargo hold, one hand on the document case strapped across his back, the other braced against a bulkhead furred with seven years of river weed.

"Ferro," he said quietly. "She's turning."

Above them, through the shattered skylight of the hold, the sky had gone the color of wet slate. And cutting across it, black against the gray, came the shapes he'd been dreading—boats, six of them, sliding around the bend of Kestrel Falls with their engines muffled and their rails crowded with men.

Ferro surfaced beside him, spitting water. "That's not possible. Odile said we had until fifth tide."

"Odile said the fleet was two tides out yesterday," Tamsin said. "Somebody's been lying to somebody." He didn't say *or to everybody*, because Ferro had spent the last three days looking like a man carrying his own guilt in a wheelbarrow, and there was no sense loading more onto it. "How much air is left in your tank?"

"Maybe ten minutes."

"Then listen carefully, because we're about to do something that will either save our lives or make us the two stupidest corpses on this river."

Ferro managed something that was almost a smile. "You always talk like this when you're scared?"

"I talk like this when I'm *right*," Tamsin said. "Being scared is just what it sounds like from the inside."

* * *

Here is what Tamsin Dunmore knew about the Meridian Star, and here is why knowing it mattered:

He had read her survey file twice during the long nights above the falls, by candlelight, while Ferro slept. She'd been a coastal salvage tug converted for river work, ninety meters of iron ambition, and like most ships built in a hurry she carried her flaws in plain sight if you knew where to look. Her ballast system was her pride and her curse: twelve tanks running along the keel, fed by sea cocks that could flood her down forty centimeters in under four minutes—a feature designed so she could settle herself into soft mud to lift wrecks heavier than herself.

The inquiry records had been stored in the aft hold. The aft hold sat directly above tanks nine, ten, and eleven.

And tank eleven's sea cock—the one furthest aft, the one nobody ever touched because it controlled nothing the crew needed day to day—was operated by a handwheel on the orlop deck below them, behind a hatch that seven years of river silt had welded shut for anyone who didn't know that the hinge pins were brass and could be driven out with a hammer and any blunt object you happened to have.

Tamsin had a hammer. He'd been carrying it since the portage, mostly because a man feels better with a hammer.

"The ship is already settling," he told Ferro as they swam for the aft ladder. "The tide's coming in over her stern, and her stern gate's been open since she sank. In twenty minutes the hold floods to the overhead. Anyone who comes down here after us is going to be swimming in the dark in a steel box."

"And we won't?"

"We will," Tamsin said, "because we're going to be the ones holding the key." He hauled himself up the rusted ladder rungs, the document case dragging at his shoulders like a second spine. "Her ballast system still works, Ferro. Seven years underwater and it still works, because the builders over-engineered everything except their own honesty. We're going to flood her faster than the tide can. We're going to sink her the rest of the way down, and we're going to do it with Crane's boarding parties inside."

* * *

They heard the boarders before they saw them—the slap of hulls against the wreck's flank, boots on deck plating, a whistle cutting the fog. Then lantern light spilled down through the forward companionway in wobbling yellow blades.

"There," someone shouted above. "Light in the aft hold! They're in the aft hold!"

"Of course they're in the aft hold," Tamsin muttered, hauling himself through the orlop hatch. "Where else would anyone sensible keep documents? On the *deck*?"

The orlop was a cathedral of dark. Cable runs hung from the overhead like kelp. Tamsin moved by memory and by count—four frames aft of the ladder, then port side, then down—and found the hatch he wanted exactly where the drawings said it would be, crusted white with mineral scale, its brass hinge pins the only clean metal in sight because brass doesn't care about rivers.

"Hammer."

Ferro handed it over. Tamsin set the point against the first pin and drove. The sound rang through the whole hull like a bell, and he winced, because there was no hiding a bell.

"That's going to bring them," Ferro said.

"It already has," Tamsin said. "Which is the idea. You can't spring a trap on people who aren't coming." He drove the pin free, caught it before it dropped, and started on the second. "Count the boats for me."

"Six. Maybe thirty men."

"Thirty men on a sinking ship is thirty men thinking about ladders instead of us." Third pin. The hatch sagged, sighed, and swung open on a smell of cold iron and old water. Beyond it, the handwheel of tank eleven stood in the beam of Ferro's lamp, green with growth, waiting the way patient things wait.

Tamsin put both hands on the wheel. It didn't move. Of course it didn't move; seven years of river had opinions about moving parts. He braced his boots against the frame, wrapped the chain of the document case around one fist for grip, and threw his whole weight into it—not pulling, but breaking, the short sharp torque that cracks corrosion loose the way you crack an egg.

The wheel shrieked. Gave. Turned.

Somewhere far below them, deep in the drowned belly of the Meridian Star, a valve that hadn't spoken since the year Tamsin's life ended opened its throat, and the river began pouring in where no river was invited.

* * *

"Now," Tamsin said, "we go up. Slowly. Like gentlemen leaving a party early."

They climbed back through the orlop and into the aft hold as the first of the boarders came down the forward companionway—a big man with a lantern and a boat hook, followed by two more, all of them moving with the confidence of people who believed the trap only had one animal in it.

Tamsin watched them from the shadow of a collapsed cargo net and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since the wheel turned. Tank eleven held sixty tons. The tide would add its own weight on top. The Meridian Star's stern was already resting in mud; the added ballast would drive her down another half meter, maybe more, and the water in the hold would rise faster than any man could climb a fouled ladder in the dark.

The boarders' lanterns swept toward the document shelves—the empty shelves, because the case was on Tamsin's back—and the big man shouted up the companionway, voice echoing off the curved overhead:

"They're not here! Shelves are stripped! Check the orlop!"

They checked the orlop. Tamsin and Ferro were already in the water, swimming the length of the hold along the starboard side where a cargo crane's fallen boom made a bridge of black iron just under the surface—a smuggler's walkway, invisible from below, leading to the maintenance scuttle near the hold's crown.

The scuttle was the size of a barrel head. It opened into the chain locker, which opened to the main deck through a hatch Tamsin had unshipped and re-shipped loosely an hour earlier, when the plan had still been merely reckless instead of genuinely deranged.

Behind them, the water began to rise.

* * *

It rose the way Tamsin had promised himself it would, and it was still the worst thing he had ever listened to.

From the chain locker, sealed in the dark with Ferro's shoulder warm against his own, he could hear everything. The boarders' voices, first puzzled, then sharp, then not sharp at all but *high*—the particular pitch men reach when the floor moves under them and the rules change. The water coming into the hold did not sound like water. It sounded like a crowd, like breathing, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"She's going down," someone yelled, and the words bounced around the steel until they seemed to come from everywhere. "She's going down, get out, GET OUT—"

Ladders creaked under desperate hands. A lantern went into the water and died with a hiss. Somewhere a man began to pray, quickly and without much originality, and Tamsin pressed his forehead against the cool iron of the locker wall and hated every second of it, because he had built this, he had turned the wheel, and the men drowning in the dark were not Aldous Crane—they were hired backs and borrowed courage, boys from harbor towns who'd signed on for wages and found themselves in a steel box with a rising tide.

"They'll make it," Ferro whispered. His hand found Tamsin's arm in the dark. "The forward ladders clear the waterline. You planned it that way."

"I planned it that way," Tamsin said. "Planning it and hearing it are different animals." He listened hard. The splashing was moving forward, toward the high ground of the bow, dozens of hands and boots climbing toward air. One voice, younger than the others, sobbed once and then got angry at itself and stopped. That one, Tamsin thought, would be all right. Anger floats.

The Meridian Star settled beneath them with a long grinding sigh, mud taking her stern the way the river took everything eventually, gently and forever. The water in the hold topped out a hand's width below the chain locker's sill and stayed there, lapping, patient.

Above, through the deck hatch, came the sounds of men regaining a deck that was now considerably closer to the river than it had been, and the sound of an officer's voice trying to assemble panic into formation.

"Nobody goes below again. NOBODY. Seal the hatches. If those two are still aboard, they're sealed in with the documents, and the documents are what matters. Crane wants—"

The officer's voice dropped too low to carry. But Tamsin had heard enough. *Sealed in.* He looked at Ferro, whose teeth were chattering in the cold, and Ferro looked back, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because the situation had achieved a kind of perfect absurdity that deserved a moment of respectful silence.

"He's locked us in the ship," Ferro said finally.

"With the documents," Tamsin agreed.

"Which we have."

"On my back, yes."

"So they've sealed us in a safe," Ferro said, "and forgotten to take the valuables out first."

A laugh escaped Tamsin before he could stop it—a real one, rusty, startling him with its own existence. It echoed strangely in the locker, and Ferro laughed too, high and giddy, the laughter of two men standing waist-deep in a flooding wreck who had just realized that being alive and being doomed are not always the same thing.

"All right," Tamsin said, when they'd both gotten hold of themselves. "New problem. This hatch is dogged from outside now, or will be within the hour. The scuttle's our way back down to the orlop, but the orlop leads nowhere but the hold, and the hold leads nowhere but deeper. So we need a third door."

"Is there one?"

"There's always a third door. Ships are full of doors engineers put in for exactly this reason and then forgot to tell anyone about." He was already moving, feeling along the locker wall in the dark, counting rivets the way other men counted sheep. "Chain lockers on river conversions share a bulkhead with the steering gear flat. There's an access panel—waist height, port side, behind the cable rack. It's how you're supposed to service the quadrant without crawling through the whole ship. Nobody services steering gear. Everybody forgets the panel exists."

"And if it doesn't exist?"

"Then we'll have a great deal of time to discuss whose fault that is."

His fingers found the cable rack, climbed over it, and found—rivets, rivets, rivets, and then the seam. A rectangle. A hairline of lighter dark in the greater dark. He nearly wept at it.

"Hammer," he said, and Ferro put it in his hand like a man passing a sacrament.

* * *

The panel came away in pieces, loudly, but by then the boarding party had withdrawn to their boats—all but a skeleton watch topside, their voices thin through the deck, arguing about whether to report the loss of the hold before or after they'd searched it properly. Tamsin and Ferro wormed through into the steering flat, a low iron room smelling of grease and dead years, and there Tamsin allowed them both five minutes of sitting still, backs against the quadrant housing, lamps hooded, breathing.

The document case sat between his feet. Inside it: the inquiry records, the clerk's final letters, and the logbook pages Ferro had risked his neck to describe. Eleven men dead in a collapse engineered for profit. Seven years of Tamsin's life spent as a ghost because the truth had been sunk in a river instead of spoken in a courtroom.

"You know what's strange?" Ferro said quietly. "I hid in a culvert for three days thinking I was the coward of this story. And now I'm sitting in a sunken ship I helped flood, and I feel—" He groped for the word.

"Alive," Tamsin supplied.

"I was going to say *terrible*. But alive also."

"Those often travel together." Tamsin tipped his head back against the iron. Above them, faintly, boots crossed the main deck. The skeleton watch, walking their rounds over a sealed hold full of rising water and two men they believed they'd trapped. He let himself enjoy that for exactly four seconds, and then stopped enjoying it, because a new sound had joined the boots—one he knew, one that reached down through seven years and closed around his heart like a hand.

A voice, at the forward hatch, giving orders in a flat Rivergate drawl he would have known blindfolded, underwater, at the bottom of his grave.

Halloway.

His foreman. The man whose testimony had buried him. Standing on the deck of the wreck, working for the fleet, close enough to touch.

"Tamsin," Ferro whispered. "Your face."

"I know," Tamsin said. His hands had closed into fists without asking his permission. "I hear him."

Through the deck, muffled by iron and river, Halloway's voice went on—tired, clipped, giving orders about search patterns and tide tables—and every word of it landed on Tamsin like a blow he'd been rehearsing for seven years and was somehow still not ready for.

He stood up in the dark of the steering flat, and the document case swung heavy against his hip, and somewhere above them the tide kept rising.

"Halloway's up there," Ferro breathed. "What do we do?"

Tamsin looked at the ladder that led to the main deck, and at the water creeping up the steering flat's drain, and understood that the ship had one more door in it after all—and that this one opened inward, onto the past.

"We go up," he said. "I've waited seven years to ask that man a question."
