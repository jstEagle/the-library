## 17. The Foreman's Voice

The ballast valve hissed shut behind them and the hold went dark, and Tamsin stood in the blackness with his hand still on the wheel and his heart going like a trip-hammer, and he thought: *I have just flooded a ship to save two men, and one of the men I flooded it for is the reason I lost everything.*

Ferro was coughing somewhere to his left, the wet racking cough of a boy who'd spent three days in a culvert and one afternoon being chased across a sinking wreck. "Did we—did that work?"

"It worked."

"It worked *how*? The water's coming in, Tamsin. I can hear it coming in."

"That's the point of it." Tamsin struck a match and cupped it, found the lantern hanging from its hook by the cargo ladder, and lit it. The flame steadied. The hold of the *Meridian Star* opened around them, low and ribbed like the inside of a whale, and along the starboard side the water was rising through the ballast channels he'd opened, a black glass floor climbing an inch at a time. Above it, on the catwalks and in the doorways, the boarding party from the *Horizon* was discovering, one by one, that the deck under their boots had opinions.

There was shouting. There was the particular slap-and-scramble of men finding that a ladder they'd come down by now ended in moving water. Somebody's lamp went into the flood with a hiss. Tamsin felt no triumph at all. He felt the specific, exhausted clarity of a man who has been awake for two days and has just realized the cleverest thing he's done all week is trap himself.

"Where do they go?" Ferro whispered. "The boarders. When the water takes the lower decks."

"Up. Or out. The tide's turning; the wreck will be awash in an hour, and their launch can't sit in this channel at half tide without going on the rocks. They'll pull back to the *Horizon*." He paused. "Most of them."

"Most of them."

"Ferro." Tamsin turned and looked at the boy properly in the lantern light — the hollow cheeks, the hands still shaking, the document case strapped across his chest like a breastplate, because Tamsin had made him carry it and Tamsin was not sorry. "There were six men on that boarding party. I counted five going back up the ladder."

Ferro's face did something complicated. "The foreman," he said. "The big one. He didn't go up. He went *down* — toward the aft hold. I tried to shout, but—"

"I know. I heard the hatch."

They both listened. Under the slosh and groan of the old ship, under the muffled thunder of the *Horizon's* cranes already biting into the hull somewhere above them — he could feel it through the deckplates, a shudder like a tooth being pulled — there was another sound. Footsteps. Heavy ones. Unhurried ones, which was worse, because hurried men were dangerous and unhurried men were *confident*, and this man knew exactly where he was going, because this man had walked this ship when she was afloat, and had helped load her, and had testified — Tamsin's jaw tightened — had stood up in a courtroom with his cap in his hands and told eleven families exactly whose fault it was.

The footsteps stopped on the other side of the aft bulkhead. Then a voice, muffled, then not, as the man found the voice-tube that ran between the holds — Tamsin had forgotten that tube, and hated himself for forgetting it, and made a note to hate himself more thoroughly later.

"Dunmore." The voice was gravel in a sack. Older than Tamsin remembered. Or maybe Tamsin remembered it younger because the last time he'd heard it, it had been saying *I saw him cut the tie-rods myself* in a voice that didn't shake until the third sentence. "I know you're in there. I saw you come aboard. I've known your walk for twenty years, lad. You don't get a new walk."

Tamsin closed his eyes. *Halloway.* Of course. Of course it was Halloway. Crane wouldn't send hired muscle to do a job that needed doing *properly*; he'd send the one man whose face would stop Tamsin cold, the one man who could stand in a flooded hold and say the thing that had been sitting in Tamsin's chest for seven years like a swallowed anchor.

"He's alone," Ferro breathed. "Why would he come alone?"

"Because Crane doesn't know he's here," Tamsin said slowly, working it through, the old engineering habit of mind turning the problem over. "Or because Halloway asked to come. Or—" and the thought arrived cold and unwelcome, "—or because there's nothing left in Halloway for Crane to threaten, and he knows it, and so does Crane, and this is Crane's way of disposing of a loose end and a problem at the same time."

The voice-tube crackled. "I'm coming through the aft hatch. Alone. If you've got a wrench, you can brain me with it, and I'll die knowing I earned it. But I'd rather talk first. I've been rehearsing a speech for seven years, Dunmore, and it's a bad speech, and you're going to hear it anyway."

Ferro looked at him. Tamsin looked at the lantern, at the rising water, at the document case on the boy's chest. Somewhere above, a crane cable groaned and something structural in the old ship answered with a long metallic sigh, like an old man settling into a chair he intended to die in.

"Let him come," Tamsin heard himself say.

* * *

Halloway came through the aft hatch the way he did everything: too big for the space, shoulders first, an apology made of meat and grizzle. Seven years had turned him from a barrel into something the barrel had been left out in the rain with. His beard was grey to the chin. His lamp hung from a hook on his belt and lit him from below, so his face was all shadow and his hands were all light, and his hands were shaking.

He stopped three steps down the ladder and looked at Tamsin for a long time.

"You got old," Halloway said finally.

"You got honest," Tamsin said. "It doesn't suit you."

"No," Halloway agreed. "It doesn't." He came down another step, and the water lapped at the bottom rung, and he looked at it without surprise. "You've opened her ballast. That's your work, that is. I'd know the neatness of it anywhere. You always were too clever for the work and too decent for the cleverness."

"Stop," Tamsin said. His voice came out flat and strange. "Whatever the speech is. Whatever you rehearsed. Don't. I have spent seven years in empty railway cuttings and dead-end tunnels with nothing to do but imagine this conversation, and in every version of it, you had a reason. You were threatened. You were paid. You were lied to. And in every version, I let you say it, and it fixed nothing, because there is no sentence in this world that puts eleven men back on the morning shift."

Halloway stood there. The water rose another inch. Above them, faintly, through three decks, came the sound of the *Horizon's* boats pulling back, engines whining, and the cranes going quiet, and then — this was worse — a great industrial clanking as they repositioned, patient as vultures on a fence.

"I testified," Halloway said, "because they had my daughter's name."

Tamsin said nothing. The river said nothing. The ship said nothing, which was its way of saying everything.

"Not a threat, you understand. Not a letter, not a visit. Nothing so crude. Crane's man — the solicitor, Pell — he sat in my kitchen and drank my tea and talked about the inquest, and about how sad it was, a man of my years facing charges, and how the railway board could be generous to widows of men who *cooperated*, and how accidents of record could happen to anyone's family. My Ruthie was fourteen." Halloway's hands had stopped shaking, which somehow made it worse; they were very still, the way hands go still when a man is holding them down. "She'd done nothing. She *was* nothing, she was a girl who liked horses. That's what I understood that day, Dunmore. That to men like Crane, my daughter was a line in a ledger, and the line could be moved, and moving it cost them nothing, and it cost me everything, and I chose her." His voice cracked down the middle. "I chose her and I have not slept properly since. There. That's the speech. I told you it was bad."

The lantern flame bent in a draft from the voice-tube. Tamsin found that he was gripping the ballast wheel so hard his knuckles had gone white, and that he could not make himself let go, because if he let go of the wheel he would have to hold something else, and there was nothing else to hold.

"Eleven men," Tamsin said. "Brannigan. The Osei brothers. Young Pike, who was nineteen and lied about his age to get on the crew. You knew them. You ate lunch with them. You signed their time sheets. And you stood in that box and you watched them hand their deaths to me, and you said *I saw him cut the tie-rods myself.*"

"I did."

"Did you?"

Halloway looked at him then, straight on, and there was something in his face Tamsin had never seen in twenty years of working under the man — not guilt, exactly. Something older and simpler. Shame, the kind that has stopped trying to hide and has instead gotten comfortable, moved in, put its feet up.

"No," Halloway said. "I didn't see you do anything. I was three hundred yards away in the pump house. I saw the dust come up and I ran, and I was the first one to the face, and I dug with my hands until they were ruined, and I found Brannigan, and he was—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "No. I didn't see you cut anything. I said it because Pell wrote it out for me, word for word, on the back of an envelope, in my kitchen, while my daughter's boots were drying by the stove. And because I was so frightened and so tired that his words were the only ones I could remember how to say."

The water had reached the second rung of the ladder. Ferro had gone very pale and very quiet, and had put his hand flat on the document case at his chest, the way a man touches a wound to be sure it's his.

Tamsin heard his own voice from very far away. "Why are you here, Hal?"

"Because Crane sent me to find you and the papers." Halloway said it simply, without drama, the way he'd once reported a cracked rail. "And because I'm seventy pounds past caring what Crane wants. And because—" and here the big man's composure finally went, all at once, like a dam in spring, and he sat down heavily on the ladder with the water around his boots and put his ruined hands over his face, "—because Brannigan's widow wrote me a letter last winter. First letter in seven years. She said the family had heard the wreck of the *Meridian Star* had been found, and that if the papers in her were what folks said, she wanted to know if her Tam's name would come right. Tam. Her Tam. He was forty-one years old and she still called him Tam." He lowered his hands. His eyes were wet and furious and exhausted. "And I couldn't write back. Seven years, and I couldn't write back, because what would I say? *Your Tam is dead because I was a coward*? So when Crane said *Halloway, go get Dunmore*, I thought: there. The river's giving me one more chance to say it to his face, and whatever he does to me after, I'll have earned that too."

The hold was filling. It was filling faster now — Tamsin could feel the trim of the ship changing under his feet, the old girl settling by the stern as the tide came up around her, and above them the cranes had started again, and this time the bites were harder, and dust sifted down through the lantern light like grey snow.

"You should have said it seven years ago," Tamsin said.

"I know."

"You should have said it at the inquest, in front of everyone, and let them take your daughter's name if they could, and fought them for it."

"I know."

"You should have—" Tamsin's voice broke, and he hated it, and let it. "Hal, I built that cut. I walked that cut every morning for a year. I knew every tie and every rod in it, and when it came down, I was in the survey tent *half a mile away*, and I heard it, and I ran, and I dug, and I found them too. All of them. And then the whole world turned around and said I did it, and the only man alive who knew I didn't — the man who taught me to read a level, who carried me on his shoulders at the works picnic, who I named—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You were my foreman. You were the closest thing I had to a father that wasn't a disappointment. And you sold me for a kitchen and a cup of tea."

"Yes," Halloway said. There was nothing left in his face at all now except the plain terrible truth of it. "I did. And I'd do it again, because it was Ruthie. And that's the thing I can't make you forgive, lad, and I've stopped trying to make it forgivable. It isn't. It's just true. Frightened men do arithmetic, Dunmore, and the sums are always wrong, and we do them anyway, every day, and some of us spend the rest of our lives trying to carry the remainder."

For a long moment, the only sounds were water, and cranes, and Ferro's careful breathing.

Then Tamsin let go of the wheel.

He crossed the flooded hold — three steps, the water at his knees, cold as a verdict — and stood in front of the man on the ladder, this ruined giant who had once been the strongest thing in his world, and he did not hit him, though his fists wanted it, though every buried hour of seven years wanted it. Instead he did the harder thing. He put out his hand, and hauled Halloway up off the ladder, and stood him on his feet in the rising water like a man raising a collapsed beam.

"Then help me carry it," Tamsin said.

Halloway blinked. Water dripped off his beard. "What?"

"The remainder. The papers. The truth. I'm not giving it to the courts, Hal — Crane owns the courts, we both know it, I've had his offer and it was shiny and it was poison. It goes to the families. Brannigan's widow first. And you—" Tamsin's voice steadied, and something in it was new, something seven years in the empty country had been quietly building, "—you are going to stand up in front of this whole valley and say what you said in this hold, and it will be the worst day of your life, and it will be the first day of the rest of it, and I will stand next to you while you do it. That's the only forgiveness on offer. It isn't absolution. It's work."

Halloway looked at him. And then the old foreman did something Tamsin had never once seen him do in twenty years of iron and timber and flood: he laughed. It was a broken, wet, astonished sound, half sob, and it echoed around the hold of the dead ship like a bell in a church with no congregation.

"Work," Halloway said. "Aye. I can do work. Work's the one thing I never failed at."

"Then we'd better start," Ferro said from the catwalk, and his voice had gone tight and high. "Tamsin. The water. Look at the water."

Tamsin looked. The black floor of the hold was climbing fast now — too fast, faster than the tide alone could explain — and as he watched, the ship shuddered from keel to rail, a long rolling groan, and somewhere above them a crane cable sang like a snapped fiddlestring, and the lantern swung wildly, shadows wheeling.

The *Horizon* hadn't pulled back after all. She'd stopped salvaging and started *hurrying*, and through the voice-tube, faint and tinny, came the sound of Odile Brack's voice on the relay, ragged with fury, saying words Tamsin couldn't make out — except the last four, which came through clear as a bell across the water:

"—the whole fleet's turning—"
