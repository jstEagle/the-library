## 18. Fifth Tide, Rising

The Meridian Star was dying by inches, and Odile Brack could hear it.

She stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Daughter*, close enough to the wreck to smell the rot coming off her—seven years of river soaked into oak and iron, now being torn open by the Horizon's cranes like a man ripping the lid off a coffin he had no business touching. Every groan that came off the hull was a sentence in a language Odile had spoken since she was nine years old, standing at her father's elbow on a pilot boat half this size. The hull was saying: *I am letting go. I am letting go. I am—*

"Odile." Ferro's voice from below, thin and urgent. "Tide's turning."

"I can hear the tide turning," she said. "I can also hear a hundred-ton crane taking the stern off a dead ship, which is frankly louder."

She raised the field glasses. Through the fog-thinned morning she counted what she'd been counting all night: two cutters holding position downstream of the wreck, the Horizon herself anchored broadside with her cranes swung out like a mantis at prayer, and four boarding parties already swarming the Meridian Star's exposed deck, their lanterns bobbing in the gray like fireflies with bad intentions.

And somewhere inside that drowning hull, sealed into the flooded hold behind his own clever ballast trick, Tamsin Dunmore was running out of ship.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a river pilot: your whole life is spent learning one body of water so intimately that you stop thinking of it as scenery and start thinking of it as a colleague. A temperamental colleague. A colleague who has drowned better men than you and will again, but who will also, if you ask correctly, do exactly what you need.

Odile had been asking the Kestrel for favors since before she could swim properly.

The channel above Kestrel Falls ran narrow here—a throat of deep water between the mudflats on the north shore and the shingle spit on the south, maybe sixty meters across at high water, less now as the fifth tide drained out. It was the only way in or out for anything bigger than a skiff. The Horizon sat square across it like a fat man in a doorway, and her boarding parties came and went on her launches along the same line, back and forth, back and forth, ferrying men and cutting gear to the wreck.

Back and forth. On a schedule. Salvage men were many things—greedy, careless, arrogant—but they were nothing if not punctual about tides. Their whole trade lived and died by the water table, same as hers. Which meant they would keep to their rhythm even when the rhythm stopped making sense.

Which meant, if a person were inclined toward audacity—and Odile Brack had been accused of worse—that rhythm could be broken.

"You've got that face," Ferro said. He'd come up beside her, wrapped in two blankets and still looking like something the river had rejected for being too soggy. "I've seen that face before. On Tamsin. Right before he made us cross the condemned bridge."

"That was his face. This is my face. They're different faces."

"They're the same face."

"Ferro." She lowered the glasses. "Do you see that barge?"

He followed her arm. Half-sunk in the shallows off the south shingle lay a derelict gravel barge, beached there so long she'd grown a small forest on her deck—alder saplings, gorse, one ambitious birch doing its best. Her name had been the *Marta June*, once; now she was just a green hill with ribs. Odile had marked her on the way upriver and thought, idly, *shame, good lines on her.* Pilots thought that way. It wasn't sentimental. It was professional appreciation.

"A barge," Ferro said.

"A barge sitting directly athwart the channel, forty meters upstream of the Horizon's anchor line, in water shallow enough to work in but deep enough to matter." She felt the grin trying to get out and let a little of it show, because Ferro looked like he needed to see somebody smile today. "That barge is going to sink. Properly. Across the channel. And every launch the Horizon owns is going to be on the wrong side of her."

Ferro stared at the derelict. Then at the fleet. Then at her.

"With respect," he said, "she's already sunk."

"She's *beached*, which is different. Beached means she's resting. Sunk means she's committed." Odile turned back to the wheel. "There's a difference between lying down and lying down where nobody can move you. I'm going to teach that barge the difference."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took shape over the next hour while the fog did its slow morning business of deciding whether to lift or thicken. It required three things: the *Kestrel's Daughter*'s spare anchor chain, the demolition charge Ferro admitted—he said it very quietly, with the specific shame of a young man confessing to a crime he hadn't meant to enjoy—the survey crew had carried for clearing snags, and roughly forty minutes of the falling tide, because the *Marta June* would only settle across the channel if the river helped pull her down onto the flats at the right angle.

It also required getting alongside a derelict barge within sight of an armed salvage fleet, which Odile elected not to think about too precisely, on the principle that fear was a fine advisor and a terrible captain.

"Tamsin would hate this plan," Ferro said as they cast off.

"Tamsin isn't here. Tamsin is currently inside a shipwreck playing hide-and-seek with Crane's boarders, which I note is *also* a terrible plan, so I don't want to hear it."

"He'd say the current's wrong."

"The current is perfect. The current is my oldest friend." She put the wheel over. "Hold on to something."

They came down on the *Marta June* out of the fog on the flood side, engine at a whisper, and Odile laid the *Kestrel's Daughter* against the old barge's flank as gently as a kiss. The impact was barely a bump. Forty years of handling boats in the Kestrel narrows, and her father's voice in her head the whole time—*the river forgives nothing twice, girl, so don't need forgiving*—and here she was, needing no forgiveness at all.

Ferro went over the rail first with the charge bag, moving better than he had any right to after a week in a culvert eating whatever culverts provide. Odile followed with the chain. The *Marta June*'s deck crunched underfoot—gravel silt, alder roots, a family of mussels who had opinions about visitors.

"Now," she said, "we open her up."

The *Marta June* had been built honest, which was her tragedy. Double-bottomed, compartmented, designed by some long-dead shipwright who believed cargo barges deserved to survive. To sink her properly—not just swamp her, but *commit* her—they needed to persuade several of those compartments to accept the river permanently, and they needed to do it in the right order, bow first, so she settled nose-down across the channel like a bar across a door.

Ferro set the charges low on the starboard side, in the forward compartments, working fast with hands that shook only a little. Odile paid out the anchor chain from the *Daughter* and made it fast to the barge's bow cleat—a cleat so furred with weed it looked like it was wearing a hat—and then walked the length of the hull reading her the way she read any vessel: where she'd break, where she'd hold, how she'd lie once the water had its way.

"She'll swing as she goes down," she said. "Current takes the stern, we hold the bow. She pivots on our chain and lands across the throat. Like swinging a gate shut."

"And if the chain parts?"

"Then she sinks where she sits and blocks nothing, and we have wasted the only demolition charge in the valley and approximately the entire morning." She checked the chain wrap a third time anyway. "So it won't part."

"You don't know that."

"I know this chain. I spliced it." She patted the cold links. "It knows me. We have an understanding."

Ferro looked at her the way people had been looking at her since she was a girl—the look that said *you're joking, except possibly you're not, and either way I'm frightened.* She'd decided long ago that this was simply her face among strangers, and she'd made peace with it.

"Light it," she said.

* * *

The charges went with a sound like the river clearing its throat—three flat concussions in quick succession, muffled by water and hull, felt more in the boots than the ears. A column of spray jumped off the *Marta June*'s starboard side, and then the old barge sighed, a long structural exhalation seven years held in, and began to drink.

Odile was back aboard the *Daughter* before the second charge fired, because she was audacious, not stupid. She brought the boat around to the chain and held station, engine breathing against the current, watching the derelict settle.

It happened slowly, then all at once, the way everything important on a river happens. The bow dipped. The chain came taut with a groan that traveled up through the water and into the *Daughter*'s keel and up Odile's spine like a struck tuning fork. The current took the stern and swung it downstream, exactly as promised, the whole dead weight of the barge pivoting on her link to the living boat—and for one horrible ten-count the chain sang and stretched and Odile stood at the rail with both fists closed, whispering to it, *you know me, you know me, you know me.*

The chain held.

The *Marta June* came about through the last meter of her sinking, slow as a door closing in a dream, and settled across the channel with a final grinding crunch that Odile felt in her teeth. Her green-decked stern grounded on the south shingle. Her bow buried itself in the north mudflat. Between them, her broken midsection filled and filled and filled, and then the falling tide did the rest, laying her down firm, listing hard to starboard, a wall of rotten timber and growing birch trees planted squarely across the only road in or out.

Above the falls, the river kept flowing. Below the falls, the salvage fleet was suddenly, comprehensively, stuck.

* * *

The shouting started almost immediately.

Odile took the *Daughter* back into the fog and watched from eighty meters out, field glasses up, feeling a warmth spread through her chest that had nothing to do with the weather. The Horizon's nearest cutter came down to investigate and fetched up hard fifty meters short of the new obstruction, her pilot clearly having assumed the channel was where he'd left it. A launch tried the gap between the barge's stern and the shingle and turned back trailing dignity. On the Horizon's deck, tiny figures gestured at each other with the universal body language of men whose morning has gone wrong in a novel way.

"There," Ferro said softly. "Look at the cranes."

The great cranes had stopped tearing at the Meridian Star. They hung motionless, loads forgotten, while the Horizon's master confronted the geometry of his situation: his boarding parties—two dozen men, at least, with their cutting gear and their lanterns—were stranded on a dying wreck with the tide rising, and the only boats that could fetch them off were on the wrong side of a forested hill that used to be a barge.

"He'll have to lighten," Odile murmured. "Send men overland around the north flats, or wait for the next high water and try to warp a launch over the top of her. Either way—" she checked her watch against the tide tables she kept in her head, where all the important things lived, "—he's lost two hours. Minimum. Maybe three, if his pilot is as bad as he looks, and he looks *very* bad."

"Two hours." Ferro was staring at the wreck, and the warmth went out of his face. "Is two hours enough? For Tamsin?"

The question landed where questions like that land—in the quiet place under the ribs. Odile put the glasses down.

Inside the Meridian Star, sealed behind his own valves and gauges, a man who had spent seven years being nobody was running out of time to become somebody, and she had just bought him the only currency that mattered. Two hours of river. Two hours in which the Horizon couldn't reinforce, couldn't retreat, couldn't do anything but shout at a barge full of trees.

"It has to be," she said. "Because it's what we've got."

She swung the wheel and took the *Kestrel's Daughter* up toward the wreck through the thinning fog, close along the north shore where the Horizon's eyes couldn't follow, and as they came she saw it—a flicker of movement at the wreck's scuppers, a hand, a signal lamp blinking the old survey code, three short and one long.

*Tamsin,* the lamp said. Or rather: *coming out. Now. Tide's mine.*

Odile blinked back the acknowledgment—one short, *understood*—and found, to her considerable irritation, that her eyes had gone hot. She blamed the fog. There was still plenty of fog to blame.

"Hold us steady," she told Ferro, and put the helm over toward the dying ship, toward the mudflats, toward the man in the water, while behind them the Horizon's horns began to sound—angry, frustrated, and for the first time in this whole gray war, *late*.
