## 19. The Choice at Low Water

The tide had turned against us, as tides do, without apology or negotiation.

Tamsin felt it first in the soles of his boots—the Meridian Star shuddering beneath him like an old dog trying to stand on ice. The hull that had held its secrets for seven years was giving them up to the river now, along with everything else: air, ballast, dignity. Water poured through the gash Odile's barge had torn in the channel side of things, and somewhere above, the Horizon's cranes were screaming against the wreck's ribs, tearing at her like gulls at a dropped fish.

He ran.

There was no dignity in running either, and he'd made a private peace with that somewhere around the third lock. The document case rode against his chest inside his coat, strapped there with two lengths of survey tape and a buckle he'd stolen from Ferro's pack while the boy slept—Ferro would forgive him; Ferro forgave everyone lately, which was either growth or exhaustion. The case was heavier than paper had any right to be. Seven years of dead men's testimony, sealed in oilcloth and lead-lined tin by a clerk named Merrick Voss who had died sitting upright beside it, one hand still resting on the latch, as if even drowned he meant to check the lock.

*The dead have been waiting for you,* Tamsin had thought in the hold, and it had not felt like poetry. It had felt like a schedule.

The cargo hold's aft ladder was gone—torn away when the Horizon's boarding party blew the hatch—but Tamsin had spent seven years learning how ruined things were put together, which was the same education as learning how they came apart. He went up through the coal chute instead, shoulders scraping rust, lungs burning, and emerged onto the main deck in a world of gray water and grayer sky, the fourth tide sucking at the wreck's flanks with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Ferro was waiting at the rail with the skiff, white-faced, gripping the painter like it might bolt.

"You're bleeding," Ferro said.

"It's mostly rust." It was not entirely rust. "Where's Odile?"

"Channel side. She says—" Ferro squinted against the spray. "She says the mudflats are your only road now, and she says it in a way that means she's sorry about it."

Odile Brack apologizing for geography. That was new. Tamsin swung over the rail, took the rope, and understood why. The wreck sat canted on the shelf of Kestrel Flats, and between the hull and the far bank lay four hundred yards of exposed riverbed—mud the color of old bruises, pocked with tide pools, treacherous as a liar's promise. At high water it would drown a man to the chest. Right now, at low ebb, it offered the one thing the Horizon's boats could not follow them through: ground too shallow for anything that floated.

It also offered no cover whatsoever.

"Go," Tamsin said. "I'm right behind you."

They went. The mud took each step and argued about giving it back. Fifty yards out, Tamsin looked back once—a habit from the closed lines, where you always checked what was following—and saw what he already knew he'd see.

A launch, coming around the Horizon's bow. Small, fast, flying no flag at all, which told Tamsin more than any flag would have. Men who flew flags wanted to be identified. Men who didn't wanted to be mistaken for weather.

"Ferro. Keep going. Don't stop for anything short of a miracle."

"What if it's a bad miracle?"

"Those don't exist. Run."

* * *

The launch grounded thirty yards behind him with a sound like a slapped cheek, and Aldous Crane stepped out before it finished settling, which told Tamsin the man had done this before—stepped out of boats onto hostile shores, in good boots, expecting the world to arrange itself around his arrival.

It mostly did. That was the trouble with Crane.

He was smaller than Tamsin had imagined, and older, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned. He wore a coat worth more than the skiff. Two men flanked him at a respectful distance, carrying themselves with the loose readiness of professionals, and behind them the Horizon loomed against the fog line, her cranes frozen mid-tear, her decks crowded with men watching the mudflats like theatergoers who suspected the play might end badly for their patron.

"Tamsin Dunmore." Crane said the name carefully, tasting it, the way a man tastes a wine he intends to buy the vineyard of. "Seven years, and you look terrible."

"You should see the other fellow," Tamsin said. He kept walking. Slowly. Mud is a poor place for sudden movements; it punishes drama.

"I did, actually. Halloway." Crane picked his way along the firmer ridge near the tide pools, unhurried, conversational, a man taking a turn about his garden. "He's a mess, if it matters to you. I imagine it does. Sentimental types usually find that sort of thing affecting."

"It matters."

"Good. Then we understand each other." Crane stopped, and the two professionals stopped with him, and for a moment there was nothing but the suck of the tide and the distant groan of the dying ship. "Here's my last offer, engineer, and I'll make it plainly because I respect plainness in working men. Walk off these flats and keep walking. You get your name back—cleared, restored, printed in the Rivergate *Courier* with my compliments. You get the pension you were cheated of, with interest, which is a figure with a satisfying number of zeros. You get Halloway and the boy released by nightfall. And you get—" he smiled, thin and genuine, which was somehow worse than a false smile, "—the truth, published. Your disaster, explained. Your innocence, established. Everything you rowed up this miserable river to take back, handed to you gift-wrapped."

"And in exchange?"

"The documents burn. All of them. On my boat, tonight, with you watching if you like—I find people want to watch. And the families wait another seven years. Or forever. Forever has a certain tidiness to it."

Tamsin walked. Ten more steps. Twenty. He let the silence stretch until Crane filled it, because powerful men hate silence the way fish hate dry land.

"You're considering it," Crane said, and there was something almost wounded in it. "Good. A man who doesn't consider is a man who can't be reasoned with, and I've found those men end up in rivers."

"I lived in rivers for seven years," Tamsin said. "They're not so bad."

"No," Crane agreed pleasantly. "But they're permanent."

And there it was. Not a threat, exactly. An invoice. Tamsin stopped at last, forty yards from the tree line where Odile waited—he could see her now, half-hidden in the alders, and he knew she could hear every word, because Odile Brack heard everything within a mile of moving water—and he made himself look at Aldous Crane properly. Not the monster from seven years of nightmares. Just a man. Well-fed, well-tailored, tired around the eyes in the way of someone who had been managing fear for a very long time and was very good at it.

"You testified men died because of engineering error," Tamsin said. "Eleven of them. My error, the inquiry said. My trestle, my calculations, my signature."

"History is written by whoever pays the printer," Crane said. "I never pretended otherwise. That's the difference between us, Dunmore. You believed the courts would save you. I bought the courts. We were both wrong about the same institution."

And that—that was the moment the whole architecture of the last seven years quietly collapsed, and Tamsin felt it go, the way he'd felt the condemned bridge fold at Half Tide: not with a crash but with a long, structural sigh, every load finding a new path to the ground.

Because Crane was right, and that was the poison of it. Every court in the valley had a Crane in its pocket or a Crane-shaped shadow across its bench. Hand the documents to a magistrate and they'd spend eleven years in appeals while witnesses aged and died and paper mysteriously burned. Hand them to a newspaper and the story would run twice, then die under an editor's sudden promotion. Justice, Tamsin realized, standing knee-deep in the river's oldest mud, was not a building you delivered evidence to. It was a fire, and fires needed people to carry the flame, hand to hand, family to family, kitchen to kitchen, until it was everywhere and could not be stamped out by any single bought man.

The courts could give him vindication. They could never give the Voss family—or the Brack family, or the eleven families whose names he'd carried like stones in his pockets for seven years—one honest hour of reckoning.

Only the families could give each other that. If someone brought it to them.

Someone invisible.

"Your offer is generous," Tamsin said aloud, and heard Odile shift in the alders, sharp with alarm, and loved her a little for it. "Name restored. Money. Halloway freed. The truth, published."

"All of it. Tonight."

"In exchange for the documents and my disappearance."

"In exchange for your *silence*, which is rarer and worth more." Crane extended his hand across the mud, an absurd little bridge of goodwill. "Well, engineer? Do we have a bargain?"

Tamsin looked at the hand. Then he reached slowly inside his coat—and watched two professional men tense like drawn wire—and drew out not the case but a battered tin flask, took a long swallow of Odile's terrible coffee, and offered the flask toward Crane with perfect courtesy.

"Thirsty work, buying a river," he said. "Have some. It's awful."

For one unguarded instant, Aldous Crane's face did something complicated. Then the professionals moved, and Tamsin turned and ran—not toward the trees, but along the flats, parallel, drawing them, letting the mud do its slow treacherous work on city boots while his own seven years of river-legs read the ground like print. Behind him Crane's voice cracked out, no longer pleasant: "The case! Get the case!"

They never touched him. He'd counted on that; men sent to grab a thing chase the thing, and the thing was visibly strapped to his chest, and so they chased Tamsin Dunmore across four hundred yards of sucking flats while the real cargo traveled the other way entirely—in the hands of a small, quick, guilty, forgiven young man named Ferro, who had slipped into the alders during the first ten seconds of conversation and was at that moment pressing the document case into Odile Brack's arms with instructions Tamsin had drilled into him since the falls: *not the magistrate, not the paper, not the pilot's guild. The widows. Marta Ellery first, on Cordwainer Street. She keeps the list of names. She's kept it seven years.*

By the time the professionals bogged down to their thighs and Crane stood shouting orders that the tide was cheerfully ignoring, Tamsin had reached the shallows at the flat's edge, turned, and raised both empty hands.

"The case is gone, Mr. Crane," he called. "Gone before you stepped out of your boat. It's in Rivergate by tomorrow night. In eleven kitchens, then twenty-two, then forty-four. You can't buy a fire that's already spread."

Crane stared at him across the mud, and Tamsin watched the arithmetic move behind the man's eyes—the offers recalculating, the threats re-pricing, the whole elegant machine discovering it had been negotiating with a decoy. It was, Tamsin thought, the finest engineering he'd ever done, and it required no mathematics at all. Only the willingness to let himself be worth nothing.

That was the trick nobody taught you. To win, he had to stop being the prize.

"You're throwing your life away," Crane said at last, almost wonderingly. "Your name. Your pardon. Everything you came for."

"I came for eleven families," Tamsin said. "I just delivered."

He waded out into the channel, where Odile's skiff slid from the reeds to meet him, low in the water and heavy with purpose, and behind them the Meridian Star gave a final groan and settled another foot into the riverbed that had kept her secrets faithfully and was glad, perhaps, to be done with them.

On the flats, small and silver-templed and suddenly mortal, Aldous Crane stood alone in the rising tide, believing he had lost everything except the one thing he hadn't yet noticed was gone: control of the story.

By dawn, Marta Ellery's kitchen on Cordwainer Street would smell of woodsmoke and strong tea, and eleven chairs would be pulled up to one table, and a woman river pilot nobody had ever elected to anything would lay a lead-lined case among the cups and say, "These are yours. They always were."

And upriver, in the dark, a man with no name worth stealing rowed north alone—smiling, bleeding a little, and lighter than he had been in seven years.
