## 20. Sixth Tide

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and Odile Brack knew it the way she knew most things—by the smell of it. The river exhaled its mud-and-iron breath through the harbor, and somewhere out in the dark channel, the Meridian Star settled another foot into the silt that had held her for seven years.

Odile stood on the pilot's walk above the Rivergate harbor with a canvas satchel heavy against her hip and her brother's name folded up inside it like a letter she'd been carrying too long to mail.

Below her, the Horizon blazed with light.

That was the thing about men like Aldous Crane—they never learned to be quiet when they won. The salvage fleet's flagship glowed from rail to waterline, lanterns strung along her decks like a carnival come to drown, and the sound drifting up off the water was laughter. Actual laughter. Corks popping somewhere below decks. A fiddle, of all things, sawing away at a jig while the boarding parties Crane had stranded on the mudflats all night were finally hauled back aboard, muddy and furious and drinking anyway, because a man will celebrate almost anything if you hand him enough liquor first.

"They think they've won," said Ferro beside her. He was wrapped in two blankets and one of Odile's spare oilskins, and he still looked like something the river had tried to keep and failed. "They let us go. They just—" He shook his head. "Why would they just let us go?"

"Because Tamsin Dunmore handed Aldous Crane exactly what Crane wanted," Odile said. "An empty case, an apology, and a man who looked beaten." She had watched it happen from the tree line on the mudflats, close enough to see the whole theater of it: Crane in his immaculate coat, standing on the flats like the river belonged to him personally; Tamsin limping toward him with the document case; the exchange—papers surrendered, terms accepted, Crane's hand extended and Tamsin taking it because refusing would have cost them everything. Then Tamsin walking away downriver, alone, not looking back.

What Crane didn't know—what no one aboard the Horizon knew—was that the case Tamsin carried onto those mudflats had been empty since the fourth tide. And what only three people in the world knew was that the real documents, the inquiry records, the Meridian Star's logbook with Crane's own orders scrawled in his agent's hand—*scuttle her, cargo intact, no survivors' accounts*—were pressed flat against Odile Brack's ribs under her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the same kind of cord her mother had used to bundle fish.

"You're shaking," Ferro said.

"I'm cold."

"You're furious."

"I'm both," Odile admitted. "It's a very efficient condition. I recommend it."

Ferro managed something that was almost a laugh, then lost it. "My crew's still aboard the Horizon. Halloway and the others. Crane said he'd release them at the sea buoy."

"He'll release them," Odile said. "Dead men make questions, and living ones make witnesses against him now. He thinks the evidence is ash or ballast by tomorrow. He has no reason to keep them." She paused. "And by noon, he'll have every reason in the world to wish he'd kept his mouth shut instead."

She looked down at the satchel. Inside it, besides the logbook and the inquiry records, were eleven letters. She had written them herself over the past two days, in the cramped cabin of her boat, while Tamsin worked by lamplight copying nothing and explaining everything. Eleven families. Eleven names. Her brother Rennick's name third from the top, because she'd insisted on alphabetical order and Rennick had always been annoyed by anything that put him ahead of other people.

Each letter said the same thing, more or less: *Come to the harbor at dawn. Come to the pilot's walk. Bring everyone who loved him. That's all. Just come.*

* * *

They came.

Odile had expected dozens. She got the whole town.

Rivergate had spent seven years learning to live around a wound—the collapse of the Kestrel viaduct, eleven men dead, one engineer blamed and vanished, a company settlement that paid for coffins and silence in equal measure. The town had folded the grief away like winter clothes, brought it out once a year on the anniversary, put it back. But grief doesn't fold flat. It bulges. And when Odile walked down from the pilot's walk at first light with the satchel and found the harbor already filling—widows and grown children and old workmates and the ferryman and the chandlery women and half the crews of the fishing fleet, all of them drawn by some current they couldn't name—she understood that the whole town had been waiting seven years for someone to give them permission to stop folding.

"I don't understand," said Marta Voss, whose husband had been a riveter, whose son had been born four months after the collapse. She stood at the front of the crowd with her arms crossed like a woman braced against weather. "Why are we here? What is this?"

Odile climbed onto the bollard by the customs shed so she could be seen, which meant standing on the spot where she'd been seasick at age six in front of the entire pilot corps, and decided not to think about that.

"Seven years ago," she said, and her voice carried the way voices do over water, clean and flat and far, "eleven men went to work on the Kestrel viaduct and did not come home. You were told it was an engineering failure. You were told the engineer responsible fled rather than face what he'd done. Some of you believed it. Some of you never did. All of you were lied to."

The crowd made a sound—not words, just a low collective hum, like a hull taking strain.

"Yesterday morning, at low tide, a man dove into the flooded hold of a wreck called the Meridian Star and brought up the truth. The ship was scuttled deliberately, seven years ago, carrying the official inquiry into the viaduct collapse. The orders to sink her came from Aldous Crane. The inquiry records say the viaduct failed because Crane's company used condemned steel and falsified inspections, and that the engineer who flagged it—Tamsin Dunmore—reported it in writing, twice, and was ignored. Then he was blamed. Then he ran, because running was the only way to stay alive long enough to prove any of this."

She let that sit. Somewhere in the crowd a woman began to cry, quietly, the practiced way, the way you cry when you thought you were finished crying years ago.

"The man who brought these records up doesn't want credit. He asked me to tell you that, and I'm telling you because I promised, but I'm also telling you it's the stupidest request I've ever heard, and I've known fishermen." A ripple went through the crowd—almost a laugh, wet-eyed and grateful for it. "He says the credit belongs to the dead and to you. So here they are."

She opened the satchel.

She read the names first. All eleven, slowly, letting each one land. When she reached Rennick Brack, her voice caught for exactly one syllable, and she let it, because hiding it would have been a lie, and she had spent six tides learning that lies were how men like Crane stayed afloat.

Then she handed the logbook to Marta Voss, because Marta had organized the memorial every year and argued with the company every year and never once been thanked, and Odile figured justice could start with a thank-you even if it ended in handcuffs.

"The courts are next," Odile said. "Crane owns most of the judges between here and the capital—I know, I checked, it took me one afternoon and a bottle of wine with the harbormaster's clerk, who talks. So don't wait for the courts. Read these. Copy them. Send them to newspapers, to the capital, to Crane's investors, to his wife if she'll have them. Paper travels faster than money when enough hands carry it." She looked out at their faces. "You are enough hands."

Marta Voss opened the logbook right there on the cobbles, and the crowd closed around her, and Odile stepped down off the bollard and let history happen without her, which she was beginning to suspect was the entire job of a good pilot: get the ship where it needs to go, then get out of the way.

* * *

The arrests began at nine.

Odile watched them from the pilot's walk again, because it had the best view and because she'd earned the right to loiter there by nearly dying six times in as many days.

It wasn't dramatic, which somehow made it better. No shouting, no chase. Crane's local agent—a soft-bellied man named Quill whom Odile had seen a hundred times buying expensive cigars—came down the customs house steps at five past nine, was met by the river magistrate and four constables and Marta Voss holding the logbook open to the relevant page, and simply stopped walking. Stood there. Put his cigar out with great care, as though tidiness might save him. It did not. They walked him back up the steps he'd just descended, and the crowd parted for him in silence, which was worse than jeering, and Odile thought: *that's what it looks like when the river takes someone. No drama. Just depth.*

By ten, word had gone out by telegraph. By eleven, two of Crane's investors had publicly demanded answers. By noon, the Horizon's own purser—facing the arithmetic of which side of the law his wages came from—had rowed ashore with a copy of the ship's charter party and a very cooperative expression.

Aboard the Horizon itself, the mood curdled visibly. The fiddle stopped. The lanterns came down. Crane's crew, who had celebrated all night on the strength of a victory that had evaporated before breakfast, now moved about the deck with the particular silence of sailors calculating whether their pay was about to become worthless paper.

And Aldous Crane?

Aldous Crane came down his own gangway at half past twelve, coat immaculate, face composed, and walked to the harbor master's office to lodge what Odile later learned was a formal complaint about "harassment of lawful salvage operations." He walked past the crowd. He walked past Marta Voss, who held up the logbook as he passed and said, clearly, "Your handwriting, Mr. Crane. Your initials. Page forty-one."

Crane did not look at it. That was the detail Odile would remember longest—not fear, not rage, just the small deliberate turn of a head, the refusal of a man to look at page forty-one, as if the page were beneath him. As if the river were beneath him.

The river, Odile reflected, had drowned prouder men than Aldous Crane. It would manage.

* * *

Halloway and the surviving crew came ashore at two o'clock, released at the sea buoy in the ship's longboat exactly as promised, and Odile was waiting at the stairs.

Tamsin's old foreman climbed the steps like a man climbing out of his own grave, gray-faced, salt-stiff, seven years of guilt riding him harder than the river ever had. He saw Odile and stopped.

"Dunmore," he said. "Is he—"

"Gone," Odile said. "Upriver, probably, by now. He wouldn't stay. He wouldn't take credit. He made me promise." She studied Halloway's face—the face she'd hated for seven years on behalf of a dead brother, the face that had testified against an innocent man because he was frightened, and she found, to her considerable irritation, that hatred didn't fit anymore. It had been tailored for a villain, and Halloway was only a man. "He said to tell you he read your testimony. All of it. He said he knew, afterward, why you did it. He said frightened men build the worst things and live in them longest."

Halloway sat down on the stairs, right there, in front of everyone, and put his head in his hands, and wept like the workingman he was—loudly, ungracefully, honestly. Two of his crew put their hands on his shoulders. Nobody told him to pull himself together.

Odile gave him a minute. Then she said, "There's a magistrate inside who wants your statement, and eleven families outside who deserve to hear it from your mouth before they hear it anywhere else. Which order you do that in is between you and whatever's left of your conscience."

Halloway looked up at her, eyes red. "You're Brack's sister."

"Rennick Brack's sister."

"He talked about you. On the viaduct crew. Said you were the best pilot on the river and insufferable about it."

"He was right on both counts," Odile said, and her voice held, mostly, and she counted that as a triumph.

* * *

She found the note at dusk.

It was tucked under the mooring line of her boat, weighted with a stone, written in the careful blocky hand of a man who drafted bridges for a living:

*I owe you a debt I can't repay and a conversation I can't have yet. Both are upriver. — T.D.*

Underneath, smaller: *Tell Ferro the culvert wasn't his fault. Tell Halloway I meant what I said. Tell yourself the harbor master's clerk talks too much and you should watch that.*

Odile laughed out loud, alone on the dock, startling a gull into indignant flight.

Ferro appeared at the end of the pier, looking less like a castaway and more like a person by the hour, wearing borrowed clothes and carrying two bowls of the chandlery's fish stew. "Good news?"

"Tamsin's gone upriver."

Ferro set the bowls down on a piling with the care of a man handling explosives. "Gone? Just—gone? After all of it?"

"After all of it," Odile confirmed. She took her bowl. The stew was too hot and too salty and exactly right. "You know what I figured out, hauling that man through six tides of hell? He didn't spend seven years hiding from Rivergate. He spent seven years becoming someone who could come back and do the right thing instead of the satisfying one. Satisfying would've been dragging Crane before a judge with his name on every newspaper. Right was giving the proof to the people it belonged to and walking away before anyone could turn him into a story." She blew on her stew. "Courts want heroes. Families wanted the truth. He gave the truth to the families."

"And what does he get?" Ferro asked.

Odile looked upriver, where the water ran dark and gold in the last light, toward the falls, the locks, the closed lines, the long miles of track where a man could walk unseen until he chose otherwise.

"A survey manifest," she said. "Eventually. Signed with his own name. I'd bet the boat on it." She ate a spoonful. "But first he gets the river. Best cure there is. Second best, anyway." She glanced sideways at Ferro. "First best is stew. Sit down before it goes cold."

Ferro sat. The harbor lights came on one by one along the quay, and out in the channel the Horizon raised anchor with unseemly haste, running downriver ahead of the magistrates' warrants, and nobody on the shore waved goodbye.

Above the falls, invisible in the dark, a man walked north along a closed line with the wind at his back and, for the first time in seven years, nothing to hide.

Odile raised her spoon to him across the miles.

"Sixth tide," she said to the river. "Right on time."
