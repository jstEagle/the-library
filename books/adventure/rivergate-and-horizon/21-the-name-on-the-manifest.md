## 21. The Name on the Manifest

The pen was the hardest part.

Not the signing itself—Tamsin had signed things before, seven years' worth of things, in a handwriting that belonged to a man named Alder Voss, a careful, unremarkable hand that slanted just enough to the right to be forgettable. He'd signed manifests, waybills, requisition slips, a lease on a drafty room above the old freight depot, and once, memorably, a condolence card for a stationmaster's dog. Alder Voss had a signature like a handshake with gloves on. It had kept him alive.

But this was the first time in seven years he was going to write *Tamsin Dunmore*, and his hand knew it. It hovered over the manifest like a bird deciding whether to land.

"You're going to blot it," said Ferro, who had appointed himself supervisor of the operation on the grounds that he'd been present for the worst of Tamsin's history and therefore had seniority.

"I'm thinking."

"You've been thinking for four minutes. The ink's going to dry in the well."

"The ink can wait. The ink has waited seven years. It's patient."

Ferro grinned and leaned against the doorframe of the survey office, which was really a converted signal box at the head of the reopened lower line, really a room with a desk, a stove, a window that stuck, and a view of the Rivergate valley that made Tamsin's chest do something complicated every morning. The valley looked different in autumn. The scars of the collapse had softened into green terraces, planted by the families, of all people—the widows and the sons and the daughters, who had taken the ruin and put gardens on it, because grief needs somewhere to put its hands.

The case against Aldous Crane had been moving through the courts for six weeks now, and Tamsin had attended exactly none of it. That had been the arrangement. Odile carried the logbook and the sealed documents to the families—carried them the way she did everything, like a woman throwing a rope in weather—and the families did with them what families do, which was everything, patiently, relentlessly, without needing a hero. Halloway had come forward on his own, which had surprised everyone including, Tamsin suspected, Halloway. His testimony was a shabby, trembling, human thing, full of apologies and half-truths that unraveled under his own conscience, and it was worth more than anything clean would have been. Nobody trusted a clean story. Everybody believed a man who couldn't look up from his shoes.

Tamsin had read about it in the papers, which still called him, when they called him at all, *the engineer who vanished*. He liked that phrasing. It made him sound like a rumour rather than a man, and rumours, unlike men, were under no obligation to appear in court.

"You know," Ferro said, "when I was hiding in that culvert, eating candle wax and my own bootlaces, I used to imagine what I'd do if I got out. Big things. Testifying. Pointing at Crane across a courtroom. Very dramatic."

"And instead?"

"Instead I'm watching you fail to sign a piece of paper." He shrugged. "Honestly? Better. The big things were going to be about me. This is just a piece of paper about a bridge."

It was, in fairness, a very good bridge. Or rather, it was a very good *plan* for a bridge—a replacement span over Kestrel Cut, the first new construction on the closed lines since the disaster, commissioned by the families' trust with the settlement money that had finally, after six weeks and a great deal of shouting, begun to move. They'd asked for an engineer. Odile had given them a name. Not his. Just a description: *tall, stubborn, insufferable about load tolerances, works cheap, answers to nothing but will show up at dawn.* The families had hired the description on the spot.

The manifest was the survey crew's daily record—elevations, soil samples, the small honest arithmetic of building something that would hold. And at the bottom, where it said *Surveyor in charge*, there was a line, and at the end of the line there was him.

"You could still be Voss," Ferro said, quieter now. "Nobody would blame you. Crane's people are still out there. Two of his agents skipped bail last week. Odile told me."

"I know."

"You could sign Voss, build the bridge, and be nobody forever. It's safer."

Tamsin looked out the window. The morning fog was lifting off the river in long slow ribbons, the way it did, the way it always had, the way it would long after everyone currently arguing in this signal box was gone. Somewhere downriver, a train whistle sounded—short, two notes, the freight line running again on the repaired trestle. He'd inspected that trestle himself. Twice. The second time he'd found a hairline fracture in the third pier and made them close the line for nine days while it was re-poured, and the foreman had sworn at him in three languages, and the line had reopened and held, and nobody had thanked him, and that was the entire job. That was what engineering *was*. You did the invisible work so that strangers could cross without thinking about it.

He had spent seven years being the invisible work. It occurred to him now, standing there with the pen, that he'd mistaken the method for the man.

"It's not about safe," Tamsin said. "It was never about safe. I told myself it was—stay hidden, stay useful, stay small—but that was just fear wearing a surveyor's coat." He picked up the pen. "Voss was a coat too. A good one. It kept the rain off. But you can't build anything wearing a coat, Ferro. You have to take your arm out and *reach*."

"That's the worst metaphor I've ever heard."

"It's been seven years since I talked to anyone. My metaphors are rusty."

"Clearly." But Ferro was smiling, and he stepped back from the doorframe, giving him the room.

Tamsin set the pen to the paper.

His hand did not shake, which surprised him. The letters came out slower than Voss's ever had—each one deliberate, a little formal, the handwriting of a man who had learned to write in a different decade and had never fully updated—but they were his. *T.* The crossbar sat where his father had taught him to put it, low, so the letter looked like it was leaning into the wind. *a-m-s-i-n.* Then *Dunmore*, and the *D* was too large, the way it had always been too large, because his mother had been a Dunmore and had held opinions about how the name should be presented to the world, and apparently seven years of exile had not overruled her.

He set the pen down. The ink gleamed, wet and black and entirely unremarkable, and the world did not end, and no one came to arrest him, and the fog kept lifting off the river, and Ferro said, "Huh," in a tone of profound anticlimax.

"That's it?" Tamsin asked.

"What did you expect? Trumpets?"

"I don't know. Something. Seven years is a long time to—"

"It's a name," Ferro said, not unkindly. "You had it the whole time. You just kept it in a drawer." He tapped the manifest. "Now it's out of the drawer. It'll take a while to get the creases out."

* * *

The creases took a while.

The first week, Tamsin signed the manifests and then, out of habit, almost burned them. He caught himself standing at the stove on Thursday evening with the day's paperwork in hand, and had to stand there a full minute, arguing with himself, before he filed them instead. Old habits were load-bearing structures. You couldn't just knock them out; you had to shore them up while you replaced them, one beam at a time.

The second week, the first letter came.

It was addressed to *T. Dunmore, Survey Office, Kestrel Cut*, in handwriting he didn't know, and he held it for a long time before opening it, because a letter with his real name on it was a thing that had only ever brought him ruin. It was from Marta Ellery. Her husband had been a riveter on the third span. She wrote four lines: that she had seen the survey stakes going in above the Cut, that she had heard whose name was on the manifests, and that she wanted him to know the bridge should have a plaque, and the plaque should say *built by Tamsin Dunmore*, and if he refused she would come up the hill and say so in person, and she was seventy-one and walked faster than she looked.

He wrote back to say the bridge was a crew effort and plaques were for the dead. She wrote back in one line: *Good. Then it can say both.*

He kept that letter in the drawer where Voss used to live.

The third week, Halloway came to see him.

Tamsin heard the boots on the signal-box stairs and knew them before he saw the man—knew them the way you know a voice on a bad radio, half-static, all memory. Halloway stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands, and he had aged the way men age when they've been carrying something: all at once, and then all at once again.

"I'm not here to—" Halloway started.

"I know why you're here."

"I testified against you."

"You did."

"I'd do it different now." His jaw worked. "I'd do it different if you gave me the chance, which you haven't, and I don't blame you, and I'm not asking for—" He stopped. Started again. Tamsin waited, because waiting was half the job. "I'm not asking for anything. I just wanted to say it to your face, out loud, where the river could hear it. I testified because Crane had photographs of my daughter's school and my wife's sister's address and a man came to my house and smiled at me, and I was a coward, and eleven men are dead, and I have been dead too ever since, only slower."

The stove ticked. Down in the valley, the freight whistle blew its two notes.

"You came forward," Tamsin said at last. "That's not nothing, Halloway. That's the heaviest thing there is."

"I'd have come forward seven years ago if I'd had your spine."

"No, you'd have come forward seven years ago if you'd had my luck, which was bad, and my options, which were worse." Tamsin stood. "Sit down. There's tea. It's terrible. Ferro made it."

They drank terrible tea and did not fix anything, because nothing gets fixed in an afternoon, but something was *shored up*, which is different, and when Halloway left he stood on the stairs a moment and said, "The manifests. I've seen them. You sign it proper now." And it wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite a blessing, but it was in the neighbourhood of both, and Tamsin took it.

* * *

By the sixth week, the strange part wasn't signing the name. The strange part was that people started using it back.

"Morning, Dunmore," said the supply clerk at the lower yard, a woman named Petra who had one glass eye that never quite agreed with the other and a memory for requisition numbers that bordered on the supernatural. "Morning, Dunmore," said the families' trust lawyer, a brisk young man who called everyone by their surnames including, reportedly, his own mother. "Dunmore," said Odile, always just that, one word, flat as a sounding line, but she'd started saying it in the tone people use for things that have stopped being dangerous and started being *theirs*.

He'd expected—what? He honestly didn't know. Applause, maybe. Or stones. What he got instead was the most ordinary thing in the world, which was being known: being greeted, argued with, blamed for the delay on the Cut footings (fair), credited with the redesign that saved the trust four thousand crowns (mostly Ferro's math, though Ferro kept giving it back), and invited, twice, to dinners he did not attend, because a man can only reclaim so much in one autumn and he was pacing himself.

The papers had finally printed his name. It was a small item, buried on the inside page under an advertisement for a cure for rheumatism: *Dunmore, exiled engineer in the Meridian Star affair, believed to be assisting the Kestrel Cut reconstruction.* No photograph. No reward. Just the name, sitting in print, in public, in daylight, like a man on a park bench.

He cut it out. He told himself it was for the records. He put it in the drawer with Marta Ellery's letter, and if the drawer was becoming a small archive of a man's return, then so be it; some men kept trophies of hunts, and he kept clippings of being real.

Ferro caught him at it once and had the grace not to say anything, which was the kindest thing anyone had done all year.

* * *

On a grey Thursday in late autumn, the trust lawyer came up the hill with news and a folder, and the news was that Crane's trial had a date—spring, the assizes at Rivergate, and the families would be there in force, and Halloway's testimony was holding, and the two agents who'd skipped bail had been found in a fishing hut with a great deal of Crane's money and none of Crane's luck.

"And there's this," the lawyer said. "The prosecution asks—formally, and you're under no obligation—whether you'd be willing to appear. As a witness. To the original survey. Your name is on the old documents, Dunmore. The defence is already arguing the survey was falsified, and the man who did it ran away, which is their whole case, really: *if he was honest, why did he flee?*"

Tamsin stood at the window a long time. The fog was coming in off the river again, evening fog, soft and grey and familiar, and somewhere below it the water moved the way it always moved, keeping its own time, asking nothing of anyone.

"Why did I flee?" he said finally. "That's the question?"

"That's their question. Your answer is your own business. But if you give it in a courtroom, in the spring, under oath—" the lawyer shrugged, "—it stops being their question and starts being yours."

After the lawyer left, Tamsin sat down at the desk and pulled the current manifest toward him and looked at the line at the bottom, where his name now lived, week after week, in ink, in daylight, in the record. *Tamsin Dunmore, Surveyor in charge.* It had stopped being an event. That was the thing nobody told you about reclaiming yourself: it was not a door you walked through once, with trumpets. It was a thing you did daily, in small letters, until the small letters added up to a man.

But a courtroom was different. A courtroom was the old bridge, the condemned one, the one he'd have to cross knowing exactly where it would fail.

He got out a fresh sheet of paper, and at the top of it he wrote his name—not a manifest this time, just the name, practicing, the way you'd test new rope—and under it he wrote: *I will appear.*

Then he sat back, and looked at the two words, and heard, very faintly, down through the fog and the valley dark, a train whistle blow its two notes on the repaired trestle—short, sure, going somewhere.

Spring was a long way off. But the river was already moving toward it, and so, it seemed, was he.
