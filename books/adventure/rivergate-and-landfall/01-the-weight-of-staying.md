## 1. The Weight of Staying

The dredge coughed twice, caught, and settled into its grinding rhythm, and Sable Sterling allowed himself the small luxury of believing the night might go smoothly after all.

It did not, of course. Nights like this never did. But for eleven minutes — he counted them, because counting was what he did when his hands were busy and his mind wanted to wander somewhere it had no business going — the black-water channel off Saltmere Quay behaved itself, and the dredge hauled up silt and shell and the occasional sad fragment of someone's drowned commerce, and Sable crouched on the deck of a barge that existed on no registry in the harbor master's office and let the work carry him.

"Line's fouling," said Brack, the salvage foreman, a man built like a bollard with a voice to match. He said it the way he said everything, as though the world had personally disappointed him and he'd long since stopped expecting otherwise.

"It's not fouling," Sable said. "It's dragging. Feel the difference." He held out one hand, palm down, and Brack, grudging as a mule, laid his own palm beside it. Both of them felt it through the deck plating — that faint stutter in the dredge's pulse, the rhythm gone slightly wrong, like a heartbeat with a murmur. "Something's hung on the head. Chain, probably. Anchor chain off one of the wrecks they dumped out here in the eighties. Everyone dumped everything out here in the eighties."

"So we pull it up and lose the head."

"So we shift forty feet south, drop the head where the silt's softer, and cut around the obstruction. There's a channel in the mud. Old scour line. Water's been running through there for years — it'll have kept a path open." He said it without thinking, the way he'd say his own name, and only afterward registered Brack's expression: the flat, narrowed look of a man recalculating what he'd hired.

"You've dived this mud before."

"I've dived every mud," Sable said, and turned back to the dredge controls before the conversation could become a question.

That was the trick of it, the trick he'd refined across ten years and more coastlines than he could honestly count without a chart: be useful enough that nobody looks too closely, be gone before they do. Competence was the best disguise ever invented. A man who could read a scour line in a mudflat, who could hear a fouled line through forty feet of steel deck, who could look at a harbor and tell you what was buried under it and what it would cost to raise — that man got paid well and questioned never. Nobody asked a good tool where it had been.

The shift south worked. Of course it worked. By two in the morning the dredge was bringing up what Brack's unnamed employers had paid for: copper ingots, stamped with a shipping mark that had been officially written off as lost with the *Coriander's Bell* thirty-one years ago, which meant the insurers had paid on them once and someone was going to be paid on them again tonight, and the space between those two payments was where men like Brack lived, and men like Sable passed through.

He worked the rest of the shift with his sleeves rolled and his hands in the machinery when the machinery needed him, and he did not think about the postcard.

He was very good at not thinking about the postcard. He'd had three days of practice.

* * *

Saltmere had been a fishing town once, then a smuggling town, and was now the kind of boomtown that grew on drowned money like mold on a damp wall — half-finished hotels leaning over the harbor, cranes on the skyline like herons frozen mid-strike, and everywhere the smell of diesel and hot asphalt and the sea underneath it all, patient, waiting for the boom to end so it could take everything back. Sable liked towns like this. They had short memories. A man could be anyone for the length of a job, and when the job ended, the town forgot him faster than the tide forgot the beach.

He took his pay in a rented office above a chandlery that smelled of tar and old rope, from a man named Quill who wore a linen suit too fine for the climate and counted out banded notes with the reverence of a man handling relics.

"Full amount," Quill said. "Brack says you saved him a week of downtime."

"Brack says a lot of things."

"He also says you knew that scour line was there before your instruments did." Quill looked up, and for a moment the affable paymaster fell away and something more careful looked out. "He wants to know where you learned that."

"Where does anyone learn it," Sable said, taking the notes. "In the water."

"He says you dive like a man who was trained, not a man who learned."

"Brack should stick to salvage." Sable folded the money into an inner pocket, felt its weight settle against his ribs like a second heartbeat. "He's good at it."

"He also says you'll be gone by morning."

Sable paused at the door. Below, through the floorboards, the boomtown rattled and clattered its insomniac music — generators, cranes, a drunk singing somewhere near the fish market. "Brack talks too much," he said, and went down the stairs.

It was true, though. He'd be gone by morning. He was always gone by morning. There was a ritual to it, worn smooth as a river stone by ten years of repetition: pack the bag while the town still slept, leave the pay's bulk in an account under a name that wasn't quite his, settle the small debts in cash — the room, the fuel, the woman at the chandlery who'd sold him coffee at hours coffee wasn't sold — and be on the first road out with the sun not yet up and the streetlights still burning their yellow holes in the fog. He'd done it in Port Anselm and Greywater and the Cays and a dozen towns whose names he'd let blur together like rain on a window. He had a system for it. He had, if he was honest with himself — which he tried not to be, honesty being a door best kept shut — a liturgy.

Pack light. Pay cash. Don't say goodbye. Don't look back at the water.

The last one was the hard one. He always looked back at the water.

* * *

His room was on the third floor of a boarding house that leaned slightly seaward, as if listening. He climbed the outside stairs at half past three, moving quietly out of habit rather than necessity, and stopped at his door because the door was ajar.

He hadn't left it ajar. He never left it ajar. A man who lived out of one bag learned to close things.

He stood on the landing and listened — the sea, a dog two streets over, the boarding house's pipes ticking as the night cooled — and then he pushed the door open with two fingers.

The room was as he'd left it: narrow bed, narrow desk, a window with a view of harbor cranes and, beyond them, the black suggestion of open water. Nothing moved. Nothing had been moved. Except that on the desk, in the exact center, weighted down by nothing, lay a postcard.

He knew before he touched it. Some knowledge bypasses the mind entirely and arrives in the body — a coldness in the wrists, a tightening behind the sternum. He stood in the doorway for a long moment, letting the harbor sounds fill him, and then he crossed the room and picked it up.

The front was a photograph of a lighthouse. Not a local one — the card showed the old light at Cape Merren, white tower, red cap, standing on black rock with the sea exploding around it in a permanent white fury. He knew the lighthouse. He'd climbed it once, as a boy, his hand in a larger hand, his father pointing out the shipping lanes and naming the wrecks beneath them the way other fathers named constellations.

He turned it over.

The handwriting was his father's.

Not *like* his father's. Not a forgery's approximation, the way forgeries always leaned too hard on the flourishes. It was the real thing — the quick, slanted, impatient script of a man who wrote the way he worked, fast and precise and with no patience for his own elegance. Sable knew it the way he knew the sound of the sea. He'd spent ten years trying to forget it and had failed completely, and the handwriting sat on the back of the postcard like a voice from a locked room:

*The channel at Rivergate opens twice a year whether we like it or not. Some things stay buried only until the water decides otherwise. Come see me before it does. — E.S.*

Sable sat down on the bed because his legs informed him they were no longer interested in holding him up.

His father had been dead for ten years. Dead in the Rivergate channel, dead with the *Landfall* expedition, dead in the collapse that had killed three men, ruined four reputations, and written Sable Sterling's life for him ever after — the son of the man who'd cut the corners, who'd pushed the schedule past the tide tables, who'd taken a crew into a working channel at spring flood because the backers were impatient and Elias Sterling had, the official record said, agreed with them. The inquiry had been very thorough about Elias Sterling. The inquiry had been less thorough about the backers, whose names had scrolled past in the testimony like weather.

Sable had spent ten years running from that name. He'd changed towns, changed employers, changed the name on his dive certifications twice. He'd built a life out of leaving, and he'd told himself it was freedom, and some nights — the nights when the work was done and the money was banked and the road out was empty and black — some nights he'd stood on whatever dock he was leaving from and admitted to the dark water that it wasn't freedom. It was just running with better footwork.

And now a postcard. In a dead man's hand. Postmarked — he turned it over again, checked, because checking was what he did — postmarked four days ago, from a town two hundred miles up the coast. A town he'd been meaning to avoid.

He read the message a third time. *Some things stay buried only until the water decides otherwise.*

His father had said things like that. Had a whole liturgy of his own, water-logic and tide-wisdom, the kind of sayings that sounded like fortune-cookie nonsense until you'd been underwater long enough to learn they were engineering. *The sea keeps honest books. The mud keeps dishonest ones.* Sable had hated those sayings as a teenager, had rolled his eyes at every one, and now here they were, ten years on, living in his own head rent-free, and the handwriting on the card was the handwriting on every one of his father's dive logs, the ones the inquiry had seized, the ones Sable had never gotten back.

Someone had written this. Someone who knew the hand well enough to fake it — or someone who'd had access to old letters, old logs, old samples. His father had written thousands of pages. Half the coast's maritime archives probably held specimens.

But the postmark was four days old. And nobody sent a fake postcard from a dead man to a stranger in a boomtown boarding house. Whoever sent it had known where he was. And nobody knew where he was. That was the entire architecture of his life: nobody knew where he was.

He sat on the narrow bed with the card in his hands and watched the sky over the harbor begin the long gray process of becoming morning, and he thought about the ritual. Pack light. Pay cash. Don't say goodbye. Don't look back at the water. Ten years, unbroken. He could be on the north road in an hour, and by noon the card would be one more strange thing in one more town, and Saltmere would forget him the way it forgot everything.

The card sat on his knee like a small, patient weight.

*Come see me before it does.*

There was no return address. Of course there wasn't. But the postmark town sat up the coast like a lit window, and Sable knew — with the same wordless certainty with which he'd known the scour line under the mud, with which he read water the way other men read faces — that the card was a hook, and that he was already on the line, and that somewhere out there someone was counting on exactly this: that a man who'd spent ten years running would find one message he couldn't run from.

He should burn it. That was the smart play. Burn the card, pack the bag, north road, gone.

He got up instead and stood at the window. The fog was lifting off the harbor in long gray rags. The first ferry of the day was easing out past the breakwater, its lights doubled in the flat water, and beyond it, past the buoys, the open sea lay black and enormous and full of everything anyone had ever lost.

He looked back at the water. He always looked back at the water.

Then he took the bag from under the bed, and began — slowly, without the usual efficiency, as though his hands were learning the ritual for the first time — to pack.
