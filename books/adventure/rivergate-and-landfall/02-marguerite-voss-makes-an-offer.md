## 2. Marguerite Voss Makes an Offer

The bar was called the Drowned Rat, which Sable Sterling considered the only honest name in Port Calloway. Everything else in the town was dressed up to look like something it wasn't — the fish cannery rebranded as a "heritage seafood experience," the rusting trawlers repainted in cheerful colors that would flake off by October, the boardwalk planks replaced just wide enough along the tourist stretch so visitors wouldn't notice how the rest of the town sagged into its own foundations like a tired man lowering himself into a chair.

Sable sat at the far end of the bar with his back to the wall and a glass of something local and regrettable in front of him, and he watched the door the way he always watched doors — not out of paranoia, exactly, but out of habit so old it had worn a groove in him the way water wears stone. Ten years of leaving towns before dawn taught a man certain postures. Back to the wall. Sightline to the exit. Cash on the wood, never a tab, because a tab was a name in a ledger and a name in a ledger was a thread someone could pull.

He'd been in Port Calloway eleven days. The dig had gone clean — a sunken lighter barge off the old cannery pier, forty tons of pre-war machinery the black-market brokers wanted quietly recovered before the harbor authority got ambitious. Sable had run it tight: three nights of diving in water cold enough to ache in the teeth, Okafor on the hookah line, Dessa keeping the compressor coughing through its fourth resurrection, Pip standing watch on the pier with a thermos and a pair of binoculars and the solemn importance of a boy who'd finally been trusted with something real. They'd been paid in cash, in full, no questions asked by either side, which was how Sable liked every transaction in his life to work.

Tomorrow they'd be gone. He'd already told them. Dessa hadn't argued, which meant she was saving the argument for later; Okafor had just nodded and started checking the boat's fuel lines, because Okafor understood that preparation was its own kind of agreement; and Pip had looked at him with that expression the boy didn't know he was wearing yet — the one that said *again?* without daring to say it aloud.

Eleven days. It was getting shorter. In the early years he could stretch a town to three months, sometimes four, if the work was good and nobody asked about his name. Then two months. Then weeks. Lately it felt like he was packing his bag before he'd even unpacked it, as though some part of him had stopped pretending there was anywhere he was going to stay and had simply begun living out of the motion itself.

The bar filled around him with the evening shift from the cannery, men and women smelling of brine and machine oil, laughing too loudly at jokes that weren't funny because they were tired and the beer was cheap and tomorrow would be the same as today, which for them was apparently a comfort rather than a sentence. Sable envied them in a way he'd have denied under oath. They belonged to the repetition. He belonged to the leaving.

The door opened at nine-forty, and the groove worn into his attention sang.

Not because of anything dramatic. Because of the quality of the silence she carried in with her. Port Calloway's regulars were loud people; this woman entered like a held breath. Tall, silver-haired, cut close at the sides, wearing a coat that cost more than the entire bar's stock and somehow not looking wrong in it. She scanned the room once — not searching, *confirming* — and then walked toward him with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never in her life been told to wait.

Sable put money over his glass and did not move. Running now would confirm something he refused to confirm.

"Marguerite Voss," she said, arriving at his end of the bar as though it had been reserved. She didn't sit. She set a slim leather folder on the wood between them, square and deliberate, like a card player laying down a hand face-up.

"I know who you are."

"Of course you do." Her voice was low and dry, an accent that had been sanded down by decades of international water until only the grain remained. "You were fourteen the last time we spoke. You threw a wrench at my head."

"It missed."

"It did not miss. It hit the rail six inches from my ear, which at fourteen is what missing means." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one, kept in storage. "Your father laughed for a week. Said you had my arm."

"My father said a lot of things."

The words came out flatter than he intended, and Voss heard the flatness for what it was. She let it sit. That was the thing about Marguerite Voss that the diving world had learned to fear: she never rushed past a silence. She stood inside it and waited for it to give her what she wanted.

Sable drank. The whiskey was bad enough to be honest about itself. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

"I'm not selling." She turned the folder ninety degrees, so it faced him. "I'm hiring."

He looked at the folder the way you look at a dog that has bitten you before. "I don't take contracts."

"You take contracts. You've taken eleven in three years that I know of, and I suspect there are others that don't leave paper trails. What you don't take is *names*. Big ones. Wrecks anyone's ever heard of." She pulled out the stool beside him and sat, finally, folding her hands on the bar. Up close he could see the years on her — the deep lines around eyes that had spent too many hours squinting into glare off open water, the faint surgical scar along her jaw that the society pages had never explained. "So I'll skip the pretense. You know why I'm here."

"I genuinely don't."

"The Landfall."

The word landed in his chest like a dropped anchor. He kept his face still because he'd had ten years of practice keeping his face still, but inside him something old and heavy shifted its weight, the way a wreck shifts on a seabed when a storm passes overhead — nothing visible, everything changed.

"There is no Landfall," he said. "There's a rumor of a Landfall. There's a chart notation and a salvage claim that's been tied up in courts since before I could drive. There's a story divers tell each other over beers. That's all."

"That's all *publicly*." Voss opened the folder herself, since he wasn't going to touch it. Inside: charts, photographs, a contract thick enough to stop a door. "Three months ago a hydrographic survey out of Meridian picked up a hull signature at depth in the Rivergate Channel. Iron, pre-war construction, length consistent with a coastal freighter of the Landfall's class. Sitting upright. Remarkably intact." She tapped a photograph — a gray sonar shadow, featureless to anyone else, and Sable hated himself for reading it instantly, hatefully well: the flare of the bow, the collapsed section aft, the scatter field trailing off the starboard side where cargo had spilled as she went down. "Her backers want her raised before the spring tides close the channel. I want her raised first. And I want you running the dive."

"No."

"You haven't heard the number."

"I don't care about the number."

"Everyone cares about the number."

"Then everyone's easier to buy than I am." He turned back to his glass. "Find another diver."

"I found twelve other divers. They're all competent and none of them are right." She said it without flattery, which was somehow worse. "You read seabed the way your father did. Corrupted charts, shifted coordinates, silted-over landmarks — most divers need the wreck to come to them. You go to the wreck. I've watched your work for ten years, Sterling, from a distance, and every job you've taken has had your father's hands all over it. You dive like a man arguing with a ghost."

Sable set the glass down carefully, because the alternative was setting it down hard. "You don't know anything about me or my father."

"I knew him better than you did, probably. Toward the end." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under a boat — there and gone before he could name it. "We were rivals for twenty years, Aldous and I. We raced each other across half the oceans of the world and insulted each other in three languages and I would have trusted him with my life and he with mine, and neither of us ever said so out loud, because that's not what rivals do." She straightened the folder's edge with one finger, aligning it precisely with the bar's grain. "When the Rivergate expedition went down, when the report came out, when they buried him under it — I read every page. Twice. Looking for the lie."

"And?"

"And I found it." Her voice didn't change at all, which was how he knew. "But that's not tonight's conversation."

The bar noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Sable became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and hard, and of the fact that his hands were perfectly steady, which took effort, which meant it showed nowhere except in the effort itself.

"Here's what I think," Voss continued, mercifully quiet now, pitched for him alone beneath the cannery workers' laughter. "I think you've spent ten years taking small jobs in small towns because small jobs can't hold your father's name. Nobody looks at a sunken lighter barge and sees Aldous Sterling. But the Landfall—" she touched the photograph again, gently, almost tenderly, "—the Landfall is where he died. And I think you've known, somewhere under that admirable detachment, that someday someone was going to find her. And I think you'd rather it be anyone but you, and I think you'd rather die than let it be anyone but you. Both things. At once. That's a very exhausting way to live, from what I understand."

"You should write books."

"I should. I'd make more than I do salvaging." She slid the folder the last few inches toward him. "Half a million to run the dive. Your crew, your methods, your terms. My fleet, my permits, my lawyers. We raise her before the syndicate's salvage fleet gets on station, we split the recovery per the attached schedule, and you never have to see me again."

Something in the phrase *my fleet* snagged on him, a burr in the wool. Voss was wealthy, legendary, self-made twice over after some catastrophe in her forties that the trade papers still whispered about — but fleets cost syndicate money. Fleets cost backers. And backers wanted returns.

"Who's behind your money, Voss?"

For the first time since she'd walked in, she hesitated. It lasted less than a second, but he caught it, and he could tell she knew he'd caught it, and something passed between them in that half-second that was almost honest.

"Investors," she said. "Diversified. Maritime interests, mostly."

"Name one."

"No." She met his eyes steadily. "Because you'd recognize it, and then you'd walk out, and I've come too far and spent too much to lose you to a name. Judge the contract, not the letterhead."

"That's not how contracts work. That's exactly how contracts fail."

"Sterling." She leaned in, and now there was something raw under the polish, something that had been carried a long way and set down carefully only once, here, tonight. "I have wanted to reach the Landfall for ten years. Not for the cargo — whatever's in her holds, I promise you, is worth a fraction of what this operation will cost. I want her because I was there when the story got written wrong, and I have lived with the wrong version every day since, and I am sixty-one years old and I would like, before I die, to stand on her deck and correct the record." Her jaw tightened around the scar. "You may not owe your father anything. Fathers are complicated; I had one; I understand. But the truth doesn't care whether you owe it. It sits down there in the dark either way, waiting for whoever's brave enough or stupid enough to come get it. I'm offering to make you rich while you're being brave. Most people get one or the other."

Sable looked at the folder. The sonar shadow looked back at him, patient as the dead.

And underneath the resentment — the old, familiar, well-worn resentment, the one he kept oiled and close like a tool — something colder stirred. Not anger. Anger would have been simple. This was the other thing, the thing he'd been outrunning for a decade, gaining on him now the way weather gains on a boat: the understanding that the Landfall wasn't a rumor and wasn't a story and wasn't a grave he could keep visiting from a safe distance. It was a room. A sealed room at the bottom of the Rivergate Channel, and inside it were the last minutes of his father's life, preserved in iron and silt and cold black water, and Marguerite Voss was holding out a key and asking him, politely, to walk in.

He didn't know what was in that room. That was the whole terror of it. Ten years ago he'd built his life on not knowing — on the official report, on the verdict, on the comfortable, corrosive story that Aldous Sterling had been a brilliant, reckless man whose luck ran out in bad water, and that the falsified logs and the erased diver and the doctored tide tables were bureaucratic noise, the sea's paperwork, nothing a son needed to look at too closely. If he dove that wreck and found nothing, he lost the story. If he dove it and found something —

If he found something, he'd have to spend the rest of his life knowing it.

"You're quiet," Voss observed.

"I'm counting the ways this goes wrong. It's a long list."

"Good. A diver who can't count the ways a dive goes wrong is a dead diver. Your father used to say that."

"Don't," Sable said, and his voice came out with an edge he hadn't authorized, sharp enough that the bartender glanced over. "Don't quote him to me. Don't stand in a bar in a town I'll be gone from by Friday and tell me what my father used to say. You had twenty years of him. I got nineteen. Whatever's left of him is at the bottom of a channel behind a court injunction, and you don't get to handle it."

Voss received the anger without flinching, without softening, without any of the responses that would have made it easier. She simply absorbed it, the way deep water absorbs a dropped stone, and nodded slowly.

"There he is," she said softly. "Nineteen years of him. More than enough."

She closed the folder but left it on the bar. She stood, buttoned her coat, and looked down at him with an expression he couldn't fully read — pity, maybe, or patience, or the particular respect one professional extends to another who is making a mistake she herself made thirty years ago.

"The offer stands until the spring tides. Six windows, starting nine days from now. After that, the channel silts shut until autumn, and the syndicate's fleet will have her to themselves all season — and their idea of archaeology involves grapples and dynamite." She put a card on top of the folder. Heavy stock. Just a satellite number, no name. "You won't take the contract tonight. You're not a tonight decision-maker; you're a three-a.m.-staring-at-the-ceiling decision-maker. So take the folder. Read it. Hate it. Throw it in the harbor if it makes you feel better — I printed four copies." 

At the door she paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the neon sign in the window — DROWNED RAT, the R flickering — and said, not loudly, not needing to be:

"For what it's worth, Sterling — I hope you say no. I hope you keep running. It would be easier for everyone, especially you." A beat. "And I hope to God you say yes, because I'm not sure anyone else alive can finish what your father started."

Then she was gone, and the door swung shut behind her, and the bar noise rushed back in like water filling a void.

Sable sat for a long time. The cannery workers drained and laughed and filtered out into the fog. The bartender wiped down the far end and pointedly didn't look at him. Outside, the harbor lights smeared themselves across black water, and somewhere beyond the breakwater, past the buoys, past the shipping lanes, down through fathoms of cold green dark, a ship that had killed his father lay upright in the silt, holding her secret, patient as arithmetic.

The folder sat on the bar. He didn't touch it.

He thought about leaving tonight instead of tomorrow. Pack the crew onto the boat, run south ahead of the weather and the memory both, find some other town with some other bottom-of-the-barrel job and some other dawn to disappear before. It would work. It always worked. That was the whole architecture of his life: it always worked, and it had never once made anything better.

Instead he heard himself saying, in his father's cadence, in the voice that lived in him whether he wanted it or not: *A diver who can't count the ways a dive goes wrong is a dead diver.* And beneath it, unspoken, the corollary the old man had always added with that crooked grin: *so count them all, and then go anyway.*

Sable finished the whiskey. He picked up the card — just the card, not the folder — and turned it over twice in his fingers, feeling the expensive weight of it, the absurdity of it, the way it felt less like an invitation than a summons.

Nine days. Six tides. A syndicate fleet already fitting out somewhere beyond the horizon.

He put the card in his pocket, left the folder on the bar, and walked out into the fog toward the harbor — toward the boat, the crew, the argument Dessa had been saving — moving faster than a man walks who has decided nothing at all.

* * *

He found them where he knew he'd find them: aboard the *Magpie*, their patched-together survey boat, lit yellow against the dark. Dessa cross-legged amidships with a laptop balanced on a crate, surrounded by the blue glow of leaked documents she'd been pulling apart for a week. Okafor oiling the winch. Pip asleep in the wheelhouse chair with a paperback tented on his chest, rising and falling.

They all looked up when he came aboard. Three faces. His whole impossible family, chosen by accident and kept by stubbornness.

Dessa's eyes went from his face to his pocket, where the corner of the card showed, and narrowed. "You've been drinking with somebody expensive," she said. "I can tell. You have a guilty walk."

Sable stood on his own deck, in his own lamplight, with his own people watching him, and found that the speech he'd prepared — *we're leaving tomorrow, earlier than planned, don't ask* — had come apart somewhere between the bar and the gangway.

"Get up, Pip," he said instead, and his voice sounded strange to him, like someone else's. "All of you. There's something you need to hear."

And below the keel, below the harbor mud, below the whole sleeping town, the channel waited — nine days from closing, and something at the bottom of it beginning, very quietly, to call his name.
