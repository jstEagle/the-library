## 3. The Crew That Keeps Choosing Him

The *Magpie* smelled of diesel and old coffee and the particular salt-rot of a boat that had been patched more times than she'd been loved, and Sable Sterling stood in her galley at six in the morning with Voss's contract folded into his jacket like a knife he hadn't decided whether to use.

He had not slept. He'd walked the seawall until the bars closed, then walked it again after, and somewhere around three he'd found himself standing at the end of the north pier looking at the black water and thinking — not for the first time, but with an unwelcome new clarity — that Marguerite Voss had known exactly which door to knock on because she'd watched him use it for ten years.

Now the crew was assembling, one by one, drawn by the smell of the coffee Dessa had started before anyone asked her to. That was how it worked on the *Magpie*. Nobody called meetings. Dessa made coffee when something needed deciding, Okafor appeared when the coffee appeared, and Pip materialized out of the rigging or the engine room or wherever he'd been sleeping, which Sable suspected was nowhere good.

Dessa came down first, wiping her hands on a rag that had once been white. She was a compact woman built like something designed to fit into small spaces — which she was, functionally speaking — with burn-scars up both forearms and eyes that read gauges the way other people read faces. She poured herself a cup, looked at Sable sitting at the chart table with his hands flat on the wood, and said nothing, which from Dessa was a full paragraph.

Okafor came next. He filled the galley doorway the way he filled every doorway, a big quiet man with diver's shoulders and a face that gave away less than the sea did. He took his coffee black, sat across from Sable, and waited.

Pip arrived last, seventeen years old and all elbows, hair cut by somebody who'd clearly done it with a deck knife and no mirror. He'd been with them eight months, long enough to stop being cargo and start being crew, though nobody had ever formally voted on it. Pip just kept showing up, and eventually showing up became a job.

"So," Dessa said. "You met her."

"You followed me."

"I followed the woman in the thousand-dollar coat into the Salt Cellar, yes." Dessa sat. "Hard to miss."

Sable turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. "It's nothing. I turned her down."

"Obviously," Okafor said. His voice was low and even, river-deep. "Otherwise we'd be talking about money."

"We're not talking about money."

"We're absolutely talking about money," Pip said, and then shrank slightly when three heads turned toward him. "I mean. Rent is due on the slip. And the port authority flagged our survey cert last week, and the fine alone is—"

"Pip."

"—which I know you know, because you paid half of it out of your own pocket and told me it came from a salvage job that I'm pretty sure never happened, because I checked." Pip said it fast, the way he said everything, and then had the grace to look embarrassed about it. Only slightly. Pip's embarrassment had a short half-life.

Sable exhaled through his nose. Of course the boy had checked. Pip checked everything; it was his one redeeming quality besides being able to climb anything, and even that was really just checking with his hands.

"The Landfall," Dessa said, and let the name sit there in the galley like ballast dropped on the deck plates.

"That's not the job."

"It's the only part of the job that matters." Dessa leaned forward. "Voss isn't hiring us to dredge a channel or pull a snagged barge. She wants the wreck. Your father's wreck. And you turned down enough money to fix this boat twice over without asking us first."

"There was nothing to ask. The answer was no."

"See, that's the thing," Okafor said mildly. He blew on his coffee. "That's the thing I keep coming back to. You didn't ask. Ten years I've worked this boat with you, and I could count on one hand the times you've asked any of us what we wanted, and most of those were weather calls." He set the cup down. "So we're asking now. Formally. As crew."

"Okafor—"

"Crew vote," Dessa said. "It's in the articles you wrote yourself. Any contract over five hundred shares goes to a vote. You wrote it so you couldn't be a tyrant, back when you still cared about being fair. Funny how that works."

Sable looked at the three of them around his galley table — the engineer with her scarred arms folded, the diver with his patient immovable calm, the kid trying very hard to look like he belonged at this table and mostly succeeding — and felt the trap close with a click so gentle he almost admired it. They'd learned it from him. Every trick he used to keep people at arm's length, they'd studied and turned around and were now using to pull him back in.

* * *

He tried the practical argument first, because it was the one he could say out loud without his voice doing anything embarrassing.

"The site's a graveyard. Not metaphorically. Rivergate lost four divers in that channel, and the channel hasn't gotten friendlier in ten years. The silt runs are unpredictable past the second shelf, the wreck's broken-backed, and whatever's left of her hull is holding up a column of water that would like very much to discuss the matter with anyone who goes inside." He ticked the points off against his cup. "And that's before we talk about Voss's backers, who can afford a salvage fleet while we can barely afford bottom paint. You want to race armed professionals to a cursed wreck in a dying channel. That's the pitch."

"Armed?" Pip said, perking up in a way that made Sable regret mentioning it immediately.

"Security contractors. Private maritime enforcement. Whatever the lawyers call men with guns on boats."

"And the curse part," Dessa said dryly, "is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Four divers died on Rivergate because the expedition was run by cowards and liars, not because the channel's haunted. You know the incident report better than I do. Bad tides, bad leadership, a captain who pushed past his own safety window to save schedule." She paused. "Your father's report. The one that got him blamed."

"My father's report got him blamed because my father was reckless," Sable said. It came out flat and automatic, the way a prayer comes out of a man who stopped believing decades ago but never learned another way to speak. "He pushed the window. Everyone knows it."

"Everyone knows what the record says." Dessa's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the unnerving thing about arguing with Dessa; she just lowered, like a submarine going deep where the pressure would crack you open. "I've been reading the record. All week. Since I heard Voss was in port."

Sable went very still. "Since what?"

"She booked her berth eleven days ago. Under a company name, but the company name traces back to a shell that traces back to a shell, and I'm an engineer, Sable, tracing things back through systems is literally my entire personality." She pulled a folded printout from her coveralls and laid it on the chart table between them, smoothing it flat with two fingers. "So while you were brooding on the seawall, I was working. Somebody on this boat has to."

The printout was a fragment of the official Rivergate inquiry — publicly available, buried, boring to anyone without a reason to care. Tide tables. Dive logs. A timeline of the final forty-eight hours. Sable had seen copies of it maybe a hundred times over ten years, in lawyers' offices and archive rooms and once, memorably, projected onto the side of a courthouse during a protest he'd walked past quickly with his collar up.

"What am I looking at?"

"The tide logs. Look at the timestamps on the third day. Then look at the barometric readings logged the same hours from the harbor station, which is a separate instrument run by a separate office." Dessa tapped the page. "They don't match. The tide log says the surge started at 0410. The barometer says the pressure drop that would have driven that surge began at 0230 — nearly two hours earlier. Any competent expedition master reading real-time data would have known the window was collapsing. Any competent expedition master would have recalled the dive teams."

The galley was very quiet. Somewhere above them, the harbor gulls were conducting their eternal argument with the morning.

"Or," Sable said slowly, "the tide log was corrected afterward. Cleaned up. To make the timeline look—"

"To make the timeline look like your father ignored data he never received." Dessa nodded. "Yeah. That's what a doctored log looks like. And here's the part I can't explain." She rotated the page and slid it toward him. "Look at the margin notes. On the archived copy. Someone annotated the original submission — pencil, before it was typed up. Whoever made those notes knew tides the way a surgeon knows anatomy."

Sable looked. And the floor of his chest quietly gave way.

He knew the handwriting. Of course he knew it. He'd grown up watching it march across charts and napkins and the fogged glass of portholes: the cramped, slanted notation, the little checkmark-tildes his father used instead of proper symbols because he'd learned hydrography from a Portuguese fisherman and never fully converted to standard form. *Check the ebb against the baro*, one note said. *Station data doesn't lie; men do.*

His father's hand. In the margins of the document that had destroyed him.

"He flagged it himself," Sable heard himself say. His voice sounded like it was coming from underwater. "He saw the discrepancy. He wrote it down. And then someone erased him from—" He stopped. There was a name field in the dive roster section, third day, second team. It had been struck through so thoroughly the paper had thinned, and the replacement name typed beside it belonged to a man Sable had never heard of, a man who had never existed as far as any record showed.

"They erased a diver," Okafor said quietly. He'd been reading upside down, which he could do, being insufferable. "Not just your father. A whole person, gone from the record."

"Why does that matter to us?" Sable said, and hated how thin it sounded even as he said it. "It's a ten-year-old paperwork fraud. It changes nothing. My father is still dead, the Landfall is still on the bottom, and Voss's backers are still—"

"Still the same syndicate whose fingerprints are all over this," Dessa finished. "Because I traced them too. The underwriters on the original Landfall policy? Same corporate lineage as the outfit backing Voss's fleet. Same names recurring through three shells and a decade." She leaned back. "I don't believe in curses, Sable. I believe in patterns. And this pattern has your family's name written all over it in someone else's handwriting."

* * *

He stood up. He needed to stand up; the galley had become too small for what was happening inside it. He carried his cup to the sink, ran water into it, watched it swirl, performed the entire theater of a man with somewhere else to be.

"No," he said to the porthole. "Vote it down. We're not taking the contract."

"You don't get to vote 'no' on our behalf before the vote," Pip said. "That's not how votes work. I'm fourteen—"

"Seventeen."

"—seventeen, and even I know that's not how votes work."

"Pip." Sable turned around. "Do you understand what you're voting for? These people falsified records that ruined my father. They erased a human being from existence. If we take their money — if we work for their client — we're working for them. Everything we touch becomes theirs. Including us."

"Then we take the money and we don't trust them," Pip said. "Isn't that what you always say? Trust the work, not the employer?"

"That's what I say about dredging contracts."

"It's what you say about everything." Pip's voice cracked a little, adolescent and furious, and he pushed through it the way he pushed through everything, headfirst. "You say trust the work, not the words. You say every port is temporary. You say don't get attached to boats or towns or people because everything floats away eventually. I've been keeping a list. Eight months. I could recite it."

"Pip," Okafor said gently.

"No, let me finish, I've been practicing this." Pip stood up too, which put him nearly eye-level with Sable, which he clearly enjoyed and was clearly terrified by. "Here's what I actually think. I think you're not afraid of the syndicate. I think you're not even afraid of the wreck. I think you're afraid that if you go out there and look at the place where your father died, you'll find out it wasn't his fault. Because then you'd have spent ten years running from a lie, and everything — every port, every midnight departure, every fake name on a permit — all of it would've been for nothing." He ran out of air. "And I think that's why you won't go. And I think it's why we have to."

The silence afterward had weight and texture. Sable could hear the bilge pump cycling, the gulls, his own pulse.

"Dessa," he said finally. "You want to tell me it's about the money?"

"It is about the money," Dessa said. "I'm not going to pretend otherwise. I have a daughter in Corvane who thinks I work on container ships, and I'd like to someday afford to tell her the truth. The contract pays enough to buy the *Magpie* outright and retire the rest of us for a year." She held his gaze. "But it's also about this." She tapped the printout again. "Somebody doctored those logs. Somebody erased that diver. And the only person on this coast with both the skills and the reasons to prove it is currently hiding behind a coffee cup."

Sable looked at Okafor last, because Okafor had saved him, deliberately, for the end — the way you save the heavy lift for the strongest diver.

"You've heard the kid. You've heard the engineer." Okafor spread his enormous hands on the table. "Here's mine. Ten years ago I dove commercial out of Rivergate myself, before the scandal killed the whole industry there. I knew men on that expedition. Good ones. When the verdict came down, everyone scattered, and the ones who knew it stank stayed quiet because staying quiet was survivable." His dark eyes held steady. "I've been quiet for ten years, Sable. I'm tired of it. You're the only captain I've ever worked for who reads water the way your father did — and I mean that as fact, not flattery, because I've seen you do it. Whatever's true about Rivergate, you're the one who can find it. So the question isn't whether we take the job." He leaned back. "The question is whether you're going to make us spend another decade watching you carry a ghost you won't even look at."

* * *

Later — much later, after the vote had gone three to one against him and he'd signed the contract amendment with a pen that felt heavier than any tool he'd ever held — Sable climbed to the wheelhouse alone and stood in the dark looking out at the harbor lights.

Three to one. His own articles, his own rules, his own crew, and they'd chosen the wreck over his objection like it was easy. Like it was obvious. And the worst part, the part he'd never say aloud, was that some sealed compartment deep in his chest had unsealed half an inch when they did it, and what leaked out wasn't anger.

It was relief.

Ten years of leaving before dawn, and these three had simply refused to be left. They'd taken his own rules and his own boat and his own stubborn loyalty and built a cage out of them, and then they'd locked themselves in it with him, voluntarily, and called it a crew.

He thought about his father's handwriting in the margin. *Station data doesn't lie; men do.* He thought about the struck-through name, some erased diver with a family somewhere who'd spent ten years believing whatever lie the record told them. He thought about Voss's face in the Salt Cellar, and the way she'd said *your father* like she was handling something fragile.

Below him, the anchor light burned its single stubborn point into the dark, and the *Magpie* rocked on water that ran, far out past the buoys, all the way to a channel that was silting shut around a dead ship and a dead man's secrets.

In the morning they'd sail. Tonight, Sable stood at the wheel and allowed himself, for the first time in ten years, to plot a course toward something instead of away from it — and it was only when he finished marking the chart that he noticed the envelope someone had slipped under the wheelhouse door while the vote was happening. No stamp. No return address. Just his name in handwriting he knew better than his own, and a postcard of the Rivergate lighthouse, and four words on the back:

*Come see what sank.*
