## 4. What the Rivergate Files Say

Dessa Kwan did not sleep well in port. She never had. A boat at anchor breathed differently than a boat underway — the hull ticked and sighed against the pilings, the tide pulled at her lines like a restless animal testing its tether — and Dessa's mind, which was happiest when it had a problem to chew, found too much quiet to work with. So on the third night after the crew voted to take Marguerite Voss's money, she gave up pretending, climbed out of her bunk without waking Pip in the bunk below, and went up to the wheelhouse with a thermos of black coffee and a data slate she'd been keeping like a loaded weapon.

The files had come to her eleven days ago, through three cutouts and a dead-drop server she'd built herself back when she still worked salvage audits for the Port Authority, before she'd burned that career down over a falsified tonnage report and decided that if the world insisted on lying in ledgers, she'd rather lie to it than for it. The package was labeled RIVERGATE_EXPEDITION_FULL_DISCLOSURE, which was a joke, because nothing about it was full and almost none of it was disclosure. It was 2,300 pages of scanned documents: manifests, insurance filings, crew rosters, weather faxes, and — the reason she'd paid a favor in Marseilles she hadn't finished paying off yet — the raw instrument logs from the survey vessel *Corvina*, the ship that had carried Sable Sterling's father out to the Landfall and come back without him.

She'd been sitting on it for eleven days because she kept telling herself she wasn't ready to read it, and because she knew exactly what she was afraid of finding.

The wheelhouse smelled of old canvas, diesel residue, and the lemon oil Okafor insisted on rubbing into the woodwork every Sunday whether it needed it or not. Rain came down soft and steady on the glass, blurring the harbor lights into smears of amber and green. Dessa spread the slate flat on the chart table, propped her elbows on either side of it, and began.

* * *

The official Rivergate report was a beautiful document. That was the first thing that struck her, and the thing that made her skin crawl. She'd spent six years reading incident reports, and honest ones were always ugly — coffee rings on the drafts, contradictory statements, an investigator's marginal note saying *check this again*. This one read like it had been typeset by a funeral director. Every section closed neatly. Every timeline dovetailed. The language had that particular cadence of prose written backward from a conclusion already reached: *the expedition proceeded according to plan; conditions deteriorated unexpectedly; the loss of the dive team was attributable to unforeseeable environmental factors.*

Dessa had learned long ago that *unforeseeable* was the most expensive word in maritime English. Nobody wrote it unless they'd been paid to.

She started where she always started: the numbers, because numbers were harder to lie about than words, though not impossible, and liars always got lazy somewhere. Tide tables first. The report cited the Corvina's own tidal observations for the day of the loss — a spring tide cycle, peak current at 14:40 local, slack water window of eighteen minutes. She pulled up the harmonic constituent data for that stretch of coast, ran the model herself twice, once conservative and once aggressive, and got the same answer both times.

The real slack window that afternoon had been thirty-one minutes.

Not eighteen. Thirty-one. Nearly double. Enough time, if you knew what you were doing, to run a two-man penetration dive into a wreck's lower deck and come back out with margin to spare. The report's timeline said the dive team had gone down at 15:05, twelve minutes after peak ebb began, into a channel that by the report's own numbers should have been running hard enough to peel a diver off a handrail.

Either the dive team had been incompetent, or the numbers were wrong. And Elias Sterling had not been incompetent. Even ten years of rumor and ruin hadn't managed to make that stick; even the men who'd cursed his name admitted he could read water like other people read print.

So Dessa went looking for the raw logs. The Corvina's tide gauge output was appended as Exhibit C — forty-eight hours of continuous readings, timestamped, digitized from the original paper trace. She overlaid them against the harmonic model and there it was, so clean it was almost elegant: from 13:00 to 16:00 on the day of the loss, the gauge readings didn't deviate from the report's claims. They deviated from *physics*. The trace showed the ebb building a full forty minutes later than the moon said it must. Every reading outside that three-hour block matched the model within centimeters. Inside it, the whole curve had been redrawn by someone who understood tides well enough to fake them and not quite well enough to remember that the moon keeps its own books.

Someone had altered the tide log. Someone who wanted the record to show that Elias Sterling had taken his divers into a channel at the worst possible moment — that the disaster was his error, his arrogance, his fault.

Dessa sat back and looked at the rain on the glass for a while. Her coffee went cold and she didn't notice.

* * *

It got worse, the way these things always do, in small increments rather than leaps. The crew roster listed nine names. But the Corvina's mess requisitions — the food orders, which no one ever bothers to forge because no one ever thinks food matters — covered ten men for the full duration of the voyage. Ten sets of gear signed out of the equipment locker. Ten life vests logged at departure, nine logged at return. And in the corner of one grainy photograph, the expedition posed on the Corvina's foredeck in their last hour of optimism, Dessa counted the figures twice, then a third time with the image blown up until it pixelated.

Ten men. Nine names in the report. One diver erased so completely that even the photograph had been cropped — she could see the ghost of it, a strip of sky and rail where a figure had stood, the compression artifacts around the edit bleeding like a bruise.

She spent two hours on the erasure alone, because the shape of a thing removed leaves a shadow of itself, and shadows can be measured. Whoever had done the editing had been thorough but not perfect. In the high-resolution scan of the original negative — included in the leak, which meant whoever leaked this had access to the syndicate's own archive, which was a thought she filed away under *later, and carefully* — there was a smear of motion blur at the crop line, and inside the blur, half a shoulder, the curve of a jaw, and a wristwatch with a distinctive perforated band.

She didn't know whose face it was. But she knew what it meant: the tenth man had existed, had dived that day, and had been written out of history by the same hands that rewrote the tide.

And then she turned to the working charts, and stopped breathing for a moment.

* * *

The report reproduced the Corvina's survey chart of the Landfall site as Exhibit F — the master plot showing the scatter field, the wreck's orientation, the marked hazards. It was a competent piece of drafting, unremarkable except for one thing: the annotations. Real hydrographic annotation has a signature, the way handwriting does. Symbols crowded toward the pencil's natural arc, depth figures slanted at a habitual angle, hazard marks drawn with a particular impatient double-stroke. Dessa had catalogued the annotation styles of dozens of surveyors during her audit years; it was a party trick that had once won her a bottle of rum and had twice caught a forged chart.

Exhibit F's annotations were Elias Sterling's. She was certain of it. The depth figures with their forward lean, the current arrows drawn as open chevrons, the little hooked crosses marking obstructions — she'd seen the style a hundred times in the published papers, in the textbook examples, in the legend of every chart Sterling had ever signed. The man had taught half the coastal surveyors on this seaboard how to mark a chart, and his notation had become a dialect all its own.

But here was the thing that made her get up and pace the wheelhouse with her fists in her pockets: the annotations on Exhibit F didn't match the depths. The symbols said one thing; the soundings beneath them said another. At four separate points, a hooked cross — *obstruction, verify before approach* — sat over water the printed soundings called clear and deep. At two others, a chevron cluster warned of crosscurrents where the plotted depths implied open channel. The annotations described a different seafloor than the numbers did. As if the chart had been drawn from one set of data and then re-plotted from another, with the annotations left behind like fingerprints on a doorframe.

Or as if Elias Sterling had seen something in the real data that the clean version was built to hide, and had marked it the only way left to him — in a shorthand the forgers wouldn't recognize as a message, because they didn't speak his dialect.

Dessa stood at the wheelhouse windows with the rain running down the glass and thought: *he knew.* Not everything, maybe. But he knew the numbers were being cooked, and instead of confronting it — instead of making noise, filing objections, giving them a reason to act early — he'd annotated the truth into the margins of the lie and gone out on the water anyway. To prove it. To bring something back.

He'd brought himself back dead, and the margins had sat in an archive for ten years, speaking a language almost nobody read anymore.

She thought about Sable, asleep in the aft cabin, ten years of leaving folded into the way he packed his kit. She thought about the vote in the galley, three hands raised against his one, and the look on his face that wasn't anger — she'd recognized it now, finally, after two years on his boat. It was the look of a man standing at the edge of a hole he'd spent a decade walking around, hearing something move at the bottom.

She poured out the cold coffee, made fresh, and worked until the rain stopped and the sky went the color of dishwater, assembling it all: the overlay plots, the mess requisitions beside the roster, the cropped photograph with its bruised edge, the chart with its orphaned annotations. Then she saved it to two drives, put one in the false bottom of her toolbox where the customs inspectors had never thought to look, and went to wake Sable Sterling before the sun did.

* * *

He was already awake. Of course he was. She found him in the aft cabin sitting on the edge of his bunk with his boots on, staring at the postcard pinned above the little desk — the one that had arrived in Grayharbor with handwriting ten years dead, that he'd shown no one and asked no questions about, that had hung in the air of the boat ever since like smoke after a shot. He heard her at the door and turned, and whatever he saw in her face made him stand up slowly.

"You've been up all night," he said.

"I need you to look at something." She held out the slate. "And I need you to sit down first."

"I'm fine standing."

"Sable." She used his name the way you'd use a hand on someone's chest. "Sit down."

He sat. She put the slate in his hands and knelt opposite him so she could watch his face, because the documents were only half the experiment; the other half was what ten years of scar tissue would do when you pressed on it.

She walked him through it the way she'd walk a jury, if juries still mattered: the tide model first, the impossible curve, the thirty-one minutes stolen down to eighteen. His expression didn't change. The roster and the requisitions — ten men, nine names. Nothing. The photograph, the cropped edge, the blurred shoulder. His jaw tightened, a small movement, quickly mastered. She saved the chart for last, zoomed to the annotations, and watched him go very still.

"That's his notation," he said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"It's your father's notation. On the official exhibit. Over soundings that contradict it." She tapped the screen, enlarging the hooked crosses. "Four obstruction markers over clear water. Two current warnings over open channel. Somebody re-plotted this chart from different data, and they left his marks on it because they didn't understand what they meant. Or—" she hesitated, because the next part was hers, her reading, her risk, "—or because he put them there knowing they'd survive the forgery. Knowing anyone who spoke his dialect would read them."

Sable stared at the screen for a long time. The harbor light came gray through the porthole and lay across his face in bars. When he finally spoke, his voice was flat in the way she'd learned meant the opposite of calm.

"He marked it." A breath. "He *knew*, and he marked it, and he went down there anyway." His thumb moved across the screen, tracing one of the chevron clusters, following the arrow the way a man follows a sentence in a letter. "These aren't standard placements. He's pointing. Look — the crosses form a line. Running northeast off the stern section, toward the deep scatter." He looked up at her, and his eyes were bright and terrible. "He was telling whoever came after him where to look. He just never thought it would take ten years for anyone to—"

He stopped. Dessa watched him arrive at the rest of the sentence and decide not to say it: *for anyone to care.*

"The tenth man," she said gently. "Somebody dove that day who isn't in any record. Somebody the syndicate needed forgotten. If we could identify the watch in that photograph—"

"Voss." The word came out of him like a stone dropping into a well. He was on his feet before Dessa realized he'd moved, pacing the narrow cabin, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a man trying to wear a groove in a floor that wouldn't hold one. "She was on Rivergate. She told us she was a young diver, she told us—" He stopped mid-stride. "Her file. The contract annexes. I skimmed them, I didn't — Dessa, pull her employment history. Pull her certification dates. Cross-reference the Corvina's voyage dates."

Dessa was already moving. She took the stairs to the wheelhouse two at a time, hauled the slate out of its cradle, and ran the search with fingers that wanted to shake and didn't. Certification records, guild registries, vessel articles — the boring scaffolding of a working life, which is where everyone hides. She felt Sable come up behind her, close enough that she could hear him not breathing.

Marguerite Voss, certified saturation diver, age twenty-four. Vessel assignments, seven years of them, each one documented, each one ordinary.

Except one. A gap of eleven days in the spring of the Rivergate year — a gap no other record filled, no other vessel claimed her, no pay ledger explained. Eleven days that began the week the Corvina sailed and ended the week the inquiry opened.

And on the Corvina's mess requisitions, in the column for rations drawn on the final morning, the count was ten.

Dessa turned around. Sable was looking at the screen, but she could tell he wasn't seeing it anymore; he was seeing something further off, some interior horizon. When he spoke, his voice had gone quiet and cold and absolutely steady, which from Sable Sterling was more frightening than shouting.

"She was the tenth diver. She was in the water with him." He straightened, and something in his posture had changed — the restless weight that had lived in his shoulders for ten years settling into something harder, something aimed. "She's known for a decade what happened down there. She's been Voss's secret and the syndicate's leverage and God knows what else, and now she wants us to dive her father's grave on her contract, on her timetable, with her backers' fleet on our horizon."

"Sable—"

"Get Okafor and Pip up," he said. "Full crew, wheelhouse, now. And Dessa—" he paused at the companionway, half in the gray morning light, and for a second she saw the boy who'd lost his father layered over the man who'd run from it, "—print everything. Twice. Because if Voss was the tenth diver, then she didn't come to us to salvage a wreck."

He went up the stairs into the rain-washed dawn, and Dessa, reaching for the printer, understood the rest of it without needing it said.

She'd come to find out what happened to the truth.
