## 5. Six Tides

The harbor pilot's name was Corvane, and he smelled of kelp and pipe smoke, and he had the kind of face that looked like it had been assembled from things the sea had rejected. Sable found him at dawn on the third morning after the crew's vote, sitting on an upturned dory outside the pilot house, mending a net that didn't need mending — an old man's way of keeping his hands busy while his mind worked something over.

"You're the Sterling boy," Corvane said, without looking up.

"I'm nobody's boy."

"Sure." He tied off a knot with the unhurried precision of a man who had never once needed to hurry in his life. "You're nobody's boy, and you're asking about the channel to the Landfall shelf. Sit down before you fall down. You've got the look of a man who hasn't slept in a boat's worth of days."

Sable sat. The harbor was waking around them in stages — first the gulls, then the fish carts, then the low diesel cough of the trawlers heading out with the morning tide. Somewhere across the water, Voss's flagship *Coriolis* rode at anchor, her salvage cranes folded like the legs of a sleeping insect. He had spent two days avoiding the sight of her and had failed every time.

"I need to know about the channel," Sable said. "The deep-water cut past the Bittern Shoals. The charts say it's passable."

"The charts say a lot of things." Corvane set down the net at last and looked at him, and his eyes were the pale gray of weather coming. "You want the truth of it, or you want the chart?"

"The truth."

The old man nodded slowly, as if Sable had passed some small examination. "Then here it is. That channel is a throat, and the sea is always trying to close it. Every tide moves silt through those shoals like a man sweeping a floor. Most of the year, the sweep is gentle — you can take a boat of moderate draft through at high water if you know the line. But the spring tides—" he held up a knotted, rope-scarred hand and began to count. "The spring tides are the sea taking a deep breath. Big water comes in, big water goes out, and it drags half the seabed with it. When the big water stops — after the sixth spring tide — the silt settles. It settles in the channel like flour in a bowl. Two weeks, maybe three, and the cut is shut. Not shallow. *Shut.* Nothing bigger than a dinghy gets through, and not even that without a prayer. Then the channel sleeps through the neaps, and the whole coast forgets the Landfall is out there at all."

"How long," Sable said, "until the sixth tide?"

Corvane smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a man who had been waiting for someone to ask him exactly this question, the way a trap waits for a foot. "That's the arithmetic, isn't it. Spring tides started three days ago. You've got six tides left, boy. Three days of big water, and then the door closes on that wreck for a season. Maybe longer, if the weather turns and the storm swell packs the silt in early. It's happened before. Ask the *Meridian's* people. Oh — you can't. They're at the bottom of the cut."

* * *

Sable walked the length of the harbor after that, not going anywhere, letting the old man's arithmetic settle into his bones the way cold water settles into a boot. Six tides. He had spent ten years building a life out of departures — out of being gone before the bill came due, before the name Sterling could catch up with him, before anyone could ask him to stay for something that mattered. And now the sea itself had drawn a line in the silt and told him: *here. Here is the last moment. After this, no one goes out and no one comes back, and whatever is on that shelf belongs to whoever holds it when the water shuts.*

He stopped at the chandler's window and looked at his own reflection in the glass, superimposed over coils of rope and brass fittings and a rack of diving knives. He looked tired. He looked, he thought, like a man being hunted by something that wasn't chasing him at all — something that was just standing still, waiting, patient as the tide.

The syndicate's fleet was already underway. He'd confirmed it the night before, through a harbormaster's clerk whose discretion had cost Dessa most of her savings: three vessels, steel-hulled, out of Meridian Sound, registered to a shell company whose name Sable had recognized from the leaked files Dessa had spread across the galley table like a hand of very bad cards. *Halcyon Marine Recovery.* The same letterhead that had signed off on the falsified Rivergate report ten years ago. The same ink that had written his father's name into disgrace and left it there to drown.

They were two days out, the clerk said. Maybe less, if they'd pushed through the night.

Which meant they'd arrive with the third tide. Which meant the race wasn't a race at all — it was a head start, and the head start belonged to the other side.

* * *

He found the crew aboard the *Magpie*, their patched-together survey boat, tied up in the working basin among boats that had all seen better decades. Pip was in the rigging, because Pip was always in the rigging, sixteen years old and part spider, re-serving a shroud with a length of tarred marline and narrating his own work to nobody in a voice that carried across the whole basin. Okafor was below, running the dive compressor through its checks for the fourth time that day — Okafor, who checked things the way other men prayed. Dessa was in the engine room, and from the sound of it, losing an argument with a fuel line.

Sable came down the ladder and stood in the engine room doorway, watching her work. She was small and quick and had hands that could coax sense out of anything with a fuel system, and she had voted for this job over his objection, and she had looked at him while she did it with an expression he was still trying not to think about — the expression of a woman telling a man something true that he did not want to hear.

"We have six tides," he said.

Dessa didn't stop working. "I heard. Half the harbor heard. Corvane's been selling the arithmetic at every bar since dawn, two drinks for the full forecast."

"The syndicate's fleet is two days out. They'll make the third tide, maybe the fourth. They've got a salvage crane that could lift the Landfall's engine block, and we've got—" he gestured at the engine room, at the boat entire, at the whole patched and welded and prayed-over reality of the *Magpie*.

"We've got you," Dessa said. She set down the wrench and finally looked at him, wiping her hands on a rag that was more oil than rag. "That's the inventory, Sable. That's what's on the manifest. One washed-up wreck diver who knows that seabed better than any living man because his father taught him, one engineer who can keep this boat breathing, one diver who's worked black water in three oceans, and one kid who can climb anything and won't stay in the boat no matter what we tell him. That's the fleet. That's what we're sailing against three steel hulls and a syndicate's checkbook."

"You say that like it's a plan."

"It's not a plan. It's an inventory. Plans come later, after we admit what we've got." She came up out of the engine room, past him, and stood in the companionway with the daylight behind her. "You've been walking the harbor all morning looking like a man deciding whether to jump ship from a boat that hasn't left the dock. So I'll ask you straight, because I voted for this job and I'd like to know what I voted for. Are you in? Not your body — your body's been in since the bar in Saltcross. Are you *in*? The way you were in on the Kestrel run, the way you were in when we pulled those fishermen off the shoal in the storm? Because there's a difference, and everybody on this boat can feel the difference, and the difference is the only thing that's going to get us through six tides."

Sable didn't answer right away. Above them, through the deck plating, he could hear Pip's voice, bright and tireless, singing some dockside song with half the words wrong. He could hear the compressor cycling. He could hear the water slapping the hull, in and out, in and out, the sea sweeping its floor.

Ten years, he thought. Ten years of leaving before dawn. Ten years of jobs wrapped up and pay taken and no forwarding address, and he had told himself it was freedom, and some nights he had almost believed it. But standing there in the engine room of a boat that couldn't outrun a fishing trawler, he understood — with the flat, cold clarity of a depth gauge hitting bottom — that the running had never been freedom. It had just been a slower way of drowning.

"I'm in," he said. And heard, in his own voice, something he hadn't heard in it for a decade. Not resolve, exactly. Resolve was a word for men who still had choices. This was something older and simpler — the sound of a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying, only to pick up a heavier one, on purpose, because it was his.

Dessa studied him for a long moment, then nodded once, the way she nodded at a gauge reading that finally made sense. "Then let's go tell the others before the kid climbs down and asks why you're making that face."

* * *

They gathered in the galley because the *Magpie* had no other room that held them all, and Sable laid it out the way his father used to lay out charts — flat, honest, every danger marked.

"Six tides," he said. "The channel closes after the sixth spring tide. The syndicate makes the third or fourth. They have the crane, the tonnage, the lawyers, and the permits — probably forged, but forged in their favor. We have this boat, four days of big water, and whatever we can pull up before they decide to take it from us."

"Cheerful," said Okafor from the doorway, ducking his head under the lintel. He was the largest man Sable had ever dived with and the gentlest, and he carried the compressor spares like other men carried groceries. "What's the play?"

"The play is the seabed." Sable pulled the chart across the table — the corrupted one, the one with the wrong coordinates, the one Voss had given them with a straight face and a fortune in the balance. "Their charts are wrong. Whoever doctored the Rivergate files doctored the survey charts too, and the syndicate is navigating off the same poisoned data. They'll burn their first tide looking for a wreck that isn't where their paper says it is. Maybe their second. That's our margin. I know where the scatter field actually is — my father taught me to read a seabed before he taught me to swim, and the Landfall broke up on the shelf in a way only somebody who's dived that ground would understand. We go to the true site on the first tide. We work while they search."

"And when they stop searching?" Pip asked. He had come down the rigging and in through the fore hatch without anyone noticing, because that was Pip, and he was sitting on the icebox now with his legs swinging, eyes too bright, the way they got when he was frightened and refusing to admit it.

Sable looked at him — at the kid who had voted for this job, who had said, in front of everyone, that he needed Sable to stop running, and who had no idea what he was asking for — and felt the weight of the answer settle over him like a dive hood.

"Then it gets complicated," Sable said. "Then we're four people in an aluminum boat between a syndicate and a closing channel, and we find out what we're made of."

"Steel," Pip said. "We're made of steel. I checked the hull plates myself."

"Rust," Dessa said. "We're made of rust and stubbornness. Steel's what we're pretending to be."

Okafor smiled his slow smile. "Then we'd best get pretending. Tide turns in three hours."

* * *

Sable went up on deck alone while the others made ready, and stood at the rail, and looked out through the harbor mouth toward the open gray water where the Landfall lay on her shelf with her secrets still sealed in her hull.

The tide was beginning to turn. He could feel it in the mooring lines, in the slow swing of the boats at their anchors, in the change in the water's voice against the pilings — the whole harbor breathing in, the sea taking its great spring breath. Three days of big water. Three days and six tides, and then the door would shut on all of it, and whatever was true about his father — fraud or martyr, coward or the only honest man on the Rivergate expedition — would be locked under the silt for another season, or sold to the syndicate, or lost to him forever.

For ten years he had run from that question. He understood that now, standing at the rail with the wind coming off the water. He hadn't been running from his father's shame. He'd been running from the possibility that the shame was a lie — because a lie meant the world had broken in a way that couldn't be fixed, and a fixed world would have obligations in it, and obligations were the one cargo Sable Sterling had spent his whole adult life refusing to carry.

No more. The tide was turning, and so was he.

He heard footsteps behind him and didn't turn. "Cast us off, Pip," he said. "We're going out with this tide."

And from the foredeck, where the boy was already coiling the lines like he'd been waiting all morning for exactly those words, Pip laughed — a bright, unguarded sound that carried out over the water, ahead of them, like a promise.

The *Magpie* came off the dock and turned her bow toward the channel, and Sable Sterling stood at her rail and did not look back.
