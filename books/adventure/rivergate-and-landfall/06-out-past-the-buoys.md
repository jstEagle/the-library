## 6. Out Past the Buoys

Okafor had been diving wrecks for nineteen years, and in that time he had learned that a boat tells you everything about the people who sail her. Not the paint job, not the brass, not the cleverness of her lines — those could be bought. He meant the seams. The places where two things had been joined by hands that either cared or didn't.

The *Magpie* was all seams.

She'd been a coastal freighter once, then a fishing boat, then nothing at all — a hulk rotting at a mooring buoy until Dessa found her and saw, the way Dessa always saw, not what a thing was but what it could be made to hold. Now she was forty feet of welded patchwork with a rebuilt diesel that coughed like a consumptive and a hull Okafor trusted about as far as he could throw the man who'd welded it. Which was to say: he trusted it, because he'd done half the welding himself, and he did not trust it, because the other half was Pip's, and Pip was seventeen and welded like he was signing his name.

She wallowed out past the harbor mouth into the first honest swell of open water, and Okafor stood at the rail with his coffee going cold in his hand and read his crew the way he read water before a dive — patiently, looking for the rips underneath.

Pip was at the bow, of course. Seventeen-year-olds went to the bow; it was a law older than navigation. The boy stood with one foot up on the anchor winch like a figurehead, hair whipping, watching the gray-green water peel away from the stem, and every few minutes he glanced back over his shoulder toward the wheelhouse. Not checking their course. Checking on Sable.

That was the fracture Okafor worried about most, though it looked the least dangerous from outside. Hero-worship looked harmless right up until the day the hero did something human, and then the worship curdled, and boys like Pip did stupid things to prove they hadn't been fools to believe. Okafor had seen it on crews before. He'd been it, once, thirty years ago, standing at a bow and staring at a dive master who turned out to breathe the same air as everyone else. It had nearly cost him his left arm. So he watched the boy watch Sable, and he said nothing yet, but he kept it filed where he kept the things that mattered.

Dessa was below, in the engine room she'd claimed within an hour of taking ownership, lying under the main with a work light clamped to a stanchion and grease to her elbows. She came up every twenty minutes to check the exhaust temperature and scowl at the horizon, and every time she surfaced she looked first toward the stern, where Voss's tender trailed them on a line like something they'd caught and weren't sure they wanted to keep.

That was the second fracture. Dessa didn't trust Marguerite Voss, and she didn't pretend to, which Okafor respected even as he thought it was the wrong instinct pointed at the right target. Dessa distrusted Voss the way you distrust a rival firm — professionally, on principle, because Voss had money and connections and a salvage fleet somewhere behind them. But Okafor had sat across a table from Marguerite Voss twice now, and what he'd read there wasn't greed. Greed was easy to read; it sat in the face like weather. What he'd read was older and more careful than that. A woman who wanted something she couldn't buy, and hated that she couldn't buy it, and had come to them anyway because they were the only ones who might find it. That wasn't a rival. That was someone carrying weight. He'd know. He carried his own.

And Sable —

Sable was in the wheelhouse, both hands on the wheel although the autopilot had been holding course for an hour, eyes moving in the old pattern: horizon, compass, horizon, water, horizon. Trusting no one, including the compass. Including himself, probably, though he'd never say so. Ten years of leaving had built him into a man who treated every deck as temporary and every crew as a tide that would go out. Okafor had watched him refuse this job, get voted down by three people who needed the money and one person who needed something else entirely, and then watch him pack anyway — not because he'd agreed, but because he would not let them go without him. That was its own kind of loyalty, the kind that never admitted its name. Okafor could work with that. You couldn't build trust out of words anyway. You built it out of dives, out of shared rope and shared risk, out of the thousand small moments when someone's hands did the right thing without being told. They had six tides' worth of those ahead of them. It would have to be enough.

The coast fell away astern, green hills going blue, then going haze. By midmorning they were truly out — no bottom visible, the water gone from bottle-green to a deep cold slate, the swell long and regular beneath them. The *Magpie* rolled her patched shoulders and pushed on, and Okafor felt the old lift in his chest that open water always gave him, the feeling of having left the world's arguments ashore.

He should have known better. The world's arguments were exactly what they were sailing toward.

* * *

They raised the syndicate fleet just after noon.

Okafor saw it first — years of standing watches will teach your eye to catch what doesn't belong. Three miles off the port bow, right where the chart said there was nothing but empty shelf water: a smudge. Then the smudge resolved into masts. Then into ships. Three of them, big, white-hulled, working a slow station-keeping pattern that no fishing fleet ever used. Salvage vessels. Modern ones, with crane arms folded along their decks like sleeping insects and helipads aft.

"Contact," he said, not loudly. On a small boat you never shouted about trouble; shouting made it real faster than it already was. "Port bow. Three hulls, standing off."

Sable came out of the wheelhouse with binoculars before Okafor finished the sentence, which told Okafor he'd been watching them too, or watching for them, which amounted to the same thing. Sable braced against the rail and glassed the fleet for a long minute. His face did nothing at all, which was how Okafor knew how much it was doing.

"Corvane Marine," Sable said finally. "Voss's backers' flag. Those are their hulls." He lowered the glasses. "They're not closing."

"No," Okafor agreed. "They're shadowing."

There was a difference, and everyone on deck understood it. Closing meant boarding, hailing, confrontation — things that happened in daylight, in front of witnesses, things with paperwork attached. Shadowing meant patience. Shadowing meant *we see you, we know where you are, we will be here when you stop*. A shark doesn't rush a seal. It just swims, and lets the seal do the arithmetic.

"They're faster than us," Dessa said, coming up the ladder wiping her hands on a rag, leaving black streaks. She'd heard through the hull; she always did. "Every one of them is faster than us. If they wanted the site first, why haven't they taken it?"

"Because they can't find it," Sable said. "Same reason we're going to have to work for it. The charts are corrupted. Whoever sank the Landfall buried her location under bad numbers, and Corvane's fleet has the same garbage we do." He paused, and Okafor watched him decide whether to say the rest. "Or they're waiting to see if we lead them to it."

Nobody said anything after that. The wind filled the silence, and the diesels thudded, and three miles off the port bow three white ships swam their patient circles.

It was Pip who broke it, because Pip was seventeen and hadn't yet learned that some silences should be left alone. "So we just — let them follow us? All the way in?"

"We let them follow us," Sable said, "and we make sure they follow us to the wrong place first." And for the first time since the bar in Rivergate, since the contract and the refusal and the vote that overruled him, something moved behind Sable Sterling's eyes that Okafor recognized from wrecks and from men: interest. The cold kind, the kind that keeps a diver alive. "Dessa. How's our spare transponder?"

Her grin was slow and mean. "Been waiting all morning for you to ask."

* * *

They spent the afternoon becoming, officially, a different boat.

Dessa pulled the transponder apart on the aft deck while Okafor held parts and Pip fetched tools and asked questions too fast to answer. The plan was simple enough to explain in one breath and delicate enough to ruin in one mistake: feed the watchers a false course. Let the *Magpie* appear, on every screen in that white fleet, to be making for a stretch of broken ground twelve miles north of the true site — a reef system littered with old wrecks, any one of which could pass for the Landfall to a sonar operator who wanted to believe. Meanwhile the *Magpie* herself would run dark and quiet on the final approach, letting the real coordinates live only in Sable's head and, grudgingly, in a sealed envelope Dessa had taken to keeping inside her jacket, because trust aboard the *Magpie* was still a currency in short supply.

Okafor worked the parts and listened to the crew talk around each other, and understood that what they were really building out here wasn't a deception. It was a crew.

Because look at them, he thought, passing Dessa a soldering iron. Look at the boy. Pip had stopped asking questions and started anticipating them — handing Dessa the flux before she reached for it, steadying the housing before she asked. And Dessa, who trusted no one's hands near her machinery, had stopped flinching when Pip touched her tools. Small thing. Everything is small things.

Look at Sable. He'd spent the whole afternoon within arm's reach of the transponder work without touching it, hovering the way he hovered over everything, and then Pip dropped a screw over the side — a ten-cent screw — and froze with that stricken look boys get when they've failed in front of the man they've decided matters, and Sable said, without heat, without cruelty, "We've got four hundred more. Breathe." And Pip breathed. And Okafor thought: there it is. The hero being human, and the worship surviving it. That was the test passed, and neither of them knew they'd been tested.

Look at Voss, standing apart at the stern rail with her coat collar up, watching the white ships with an expression Okafor couldn't file anywhere. Not fear. Not calculation. Something almost like grief, aimed at steel. He'd ask her about it eventually. Or he wouldn't. Some cargoes people carry alone until they choose to set them down.

By dusk the transponder was reborn as a liar, and the *Magpie* altered course north with great theatrical honesty, broadcasting her new heading to anyone with a receiver. Behind them, after a decent interval, the white fleet shifted its pattern to follow.

* * *

Night came down soft and starless, low cloud pressing the sea flat. Okafor took first watch because he always did; sleep came hard to him on boats he didn't fully trust, and the *Magpie* was still earning it.

At midnight, Sable climbed into the wheelhouse with two mugs and handed him one without asking. They stood together in the red glow of the instruments, watching nothing, which was most of what a night watch was.

"You clocked them before I did," Sable said.

"I clocked them before you wanted to," Okafor said. "Different thing."

Sable almost smiled. Almost. "You've been reading this crew all day. Don't think I don't see it. You read everybody."

"Somebody has to," Okafor said. "You read water. Dessa reads machines. The boy reads you. I read people. A crew's just four ways of seeing the same ocean."

Sable drank his coffee and was quiet for a long time. Then, in a voice lower than the engine noise, he said: "If this goes wrong out here — if Corvane takes the site, or worse — you should take the boat and the boy and go. There's no version of this where I'm worth dying for."

And there it was, Okafor thought. The fracture under the fracture. Not that Sable trusted no one — that Sable believed himself untrustworthy. Believed he was the load-bearing flaw in every structure he joined. Ten years of leaving hadn't been cowardice. It had been quarantine.

Okafor considered several answers and discarded them, and settled on the truth, because at midnight on open water the truth was the only cargo worth carrying.

"When my daughter was born," he said, "I swore off deep work. Swore it on her head. Two years later a friend's crew lost a diver on a wreck I'd surveyed, a wreck I knew had a soft overhead, and I went back down to prove the survey wrong and got pinned for six hours." He sipped his coffee. "My wife left me before I surfaced. My daughter grew up thinking the ocean loved me more than she did. And I'm telling you this because you need to hear that I know exactly what it costs to believe you're the danger, and I'm telling you also that you're wrong. You're not the danger, Sable. You're the reason the rest of us haven't run. The boy would follow you into a burning sea. Dessa re-welded a hull she didn't believe in because you'd be sailing on it. Whatever you think you owe us — the debt runs the other way."

Sable stared out at the black water for a long moment. "That's a lot of weight to hand a man at midnight."

"I'm a diver," Okafor said. "I know how to hand off weight."

Sable didn't answer. But he stayed in the wheelhouse past his watch, and when Okafor finally went below, Sable was still there, standing his own unnecessary watch over a boat full of sleeping people, and Okafor thought: *there*. Seam by seam. That's how a crew gets built.

* * *

The contact came at 0400, and it came close.

Okafor was back on watch when the shape detached itself from the darkness off the starboard quarter — a low black hull running without lights, fast, cutting inside the *Magpie*'s track at maybe four hundred yards. No transponder ping. No hail. Just a knife of a boat sliding past close enough that Okafor could hear her engines, could see, briefly, in the wash of the *Magpie*'s own running lights, two figures at her rail. Watching. One of them raised something — not a weapon, he thought, though in the dark he couldn't swear to it. A camera, maybe. A phone. Taking their picture, or counting heads.

Then the black boat was gone southward, toward the white fleet, carrying whatever it had come to carry.

Okafor woke Sable with a hand on the shoulder, the way you wake divers — gently, because waking wrong starts the day wrong. Sable came up out of sleep already listening, which was a skill Okafor both admired and pitied.

"Black hull," Okafor reported. "Starboard to port, four hundred yards, no lights, no hail. Two souls visible. Headed back to the fleet."

Sable was silent, working it. "Counting us," he said at last. "Or photographing the transponder fit. They'll want to know if we're spoofing." He looked at the southern horizon, where the white ships' masthead lights hung like a constellation that had learned to hunt. "Which means by tomorrow they'll either believe our false course or they won't. And if they don't—"

He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. They both knew what happened next: the patient pattern would break, the sharks would stop swimming, and the race they'd been running quietly would become a chase run loud, with six tides falling away beneath them like sand through a fist.

Below them, the *Magpie* thudded on through the dark, patched and honest and outmatched, carrying four people who had chosen each other without ever quite saying so, toward water that held a dead man's ship and a dead man's name and whatever truth the syndicate had sunk her to bury.

Okafor went back to the rail and stood his watch, and did not wake anyone else, and did not need to. Whatever came at dawn, they would meet it awake. That was what crews were for.

At first light, the white fleet turned north — following the lie — and for one whole hour the sea belonged to them.

Then the fleet stopped. All three hulls, together, in open water, and began, slowly and deliberately, to turn south.

Toward the *Magpie*.

Toward the truth.
