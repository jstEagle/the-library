## 7. Voss's Version of the Truth

Marguerite Voss had spent thirty years learning to read water, and in all that time she had never once learned to read Sable Sterling.

She stood at the rail of the *Corvina*, watching his patched-together boat swing at anchor two hundred meters off her stern, and told herself the discomfort in her chest was only the coffee. It was not the coffee. It was the fact that she had asked him to come alone, and he had actually come — which meant either that he trusted her, or that he wanted something badly enough to walk into a room with a woman who had spent a decade being his father's ghost.

She had dressed for the meeting like armor. Charcoal wool, no jewelry except the dive watch — her father's, its crystal scratched from forty years of salt — and her hair pulled back so severely it made her face look like a verdict. She knew what she looked like: the woman from the trade papers, the salvage queen with the cold ledger eyes. She had built that woman plank by plank over ten years, and tonight she intended to take her apart in front of the one man whose opinion could still cut her.

"Drink?" she asked when he came up the ladder. He moved well for a man who hated boats, she noted. Or perhaps he didn't hate them. Perhaps he hated what they carried.

"No."

"You used to drink. Your father kept rum on the *Landfall* like it was ballast."

"Don't." The word came out flat, not angry. Flat was worse. Anger meant he still thought of her as a person; flatness meant she had been filed away with the syndicate and the forged charts and every other instrument of the thing that had killed Elias Sterling.

She poured one for herself anyway, because her hands needed occupation, and led him aft to the little deckhouse where she did her real thinking — not the mahogany salon the brokers photographed, but this: a chart table scarred with compass points, a kettle, a cot she never slept in, and a wall of tide tables going back eleven years.

Sable stopped when he saw the tide tables.

He was quick. She'd known he would be. His eyes went down the column of years — 2014, 2015, 2016 — and stopped on the oldest set, the ones with the Rivergate dates flagged in red grease pencil. She watched him understand that this room had been prepared for him, or for someone exactly like him, for a very long time.

"You said you had information," he said. "About your backers' fleet. Positions, timetables. That's why I'm here. That's all I'm here for."

"I know. Sit down anyway."

"I'd rather stand."

Of course he would. She sat herself, because one of them had to be human tonight, and folded her hands on the chart table where the lamplight caught the old scar across her knuckles — a winch wire, the *Landfall*'s winch wire, though he would never know that unless she told him. And God help her, she was going to tell him.

"The fleet you're worried about," she began, "is called Meridian Recovery Group on paper. Four vessels, a crane barge, and a decompression chamber that cost more than my first three boats combined. They'll be on station in five days. Their charter specifies exclusive salvage rights to the *Landfall*, which their lawyers will enforce with injunctions before your crew finishes rigging their first lift bag. I can give you their positions because I signed the charter. My company is the local partner of record. On paper, I'm leading that expedition."

His jaw tightened. "Then why are you telling me how to beat yourself?"

"Because I'm not trying to beat anyone." She heard her own voice go quiet, and hated how much it cost her. "I'm trying to lose. For once in my life, I'm trying to do it right."

* * *

The wind had come around with the evening, backing into the northeast, and it pushed small confused chop against the hull in a rhythm that sounded almost like breathing. Voss let it breathe for a while. There were sentences she had rehearsed for ten years and now that the moment had arrived they all felt like borrowed clothing.

"Elias Sterling and I were not friends," she said finally. "You know that. Everyone in the trade knows that. We competed for the same contracts for six years and I won more than I lost and I told myself that was all it was — rivalry, professional, clean. But that's not true, Sable. I applied to join the *Landfall* expedition twice. He turned me down both times. Said I dove for money and money divers get people killed." Her mouth twisted. "I decided to prove him wrong by beating him to everything he ever wanted. And then Rivergate happened, and I got what I'd always wanted, and I have spent ten years discovering there is no prize at the bottom of that."

"He wasn't wrong about money divers," Sable said. "Look how that ended."

"It ended because of me." She said it plainly, because she had earned plainness the hard way, one sleepless year at a time. "I wasn't on the crew. I want that understood first. I was twenty-six, running my own outfit out of Port Calloway, and the Rivergate consortium hired me as an independent survey diver for the approach channel work. Three weeks, good pay, and the chance to watch Elias Sterling's operation from the inside. I took it for spite. I've had time to learn the exact weight of that word. Spite."

The lamp flickered as the boat rolled. Neither of them moved to steady anything. Sailors learn early that most things right themselves.

"The official story is that the *Landfall* went down in the outer channel during a spring ebb, that the tide logs show the window closed early, that Elias pushed past safe margins to beat a contract deadline, and that the wreck and four lives were the price of his impatience. You've heard it your whole life. So have I. I helped write it."

Sable had gone very still. It was the stillness of deep water, and she recognized it because she lived there too.

"The tide logs," he said.

"Were altered. Not after the fact — before. I know because I was the one who found the discrepancy and I was the one who was paid to stop looking." She opened the drawer beneath the chart table and took out nothing at all; her hand just rested there on the wood, remembering the weight of the envelope. "There was a second set of readings. The harbor authority's own gauges, the ones on the channel marker buoy. They showed the ebb holding another ninety minutes beyond what the expedition's logs claimed. Ninety minutes. If the truth had been in those logs, the *Landfall* would have had time. Elias would have had time. I brought the discrepancy to the consortium's survey director, a man named Corben Hale, and I watched him look at the numbers and then look at me, and I watched him decide — right there, in front of me, in less time than it takes to light a cigarette — that I was for sale."

"And you were."

"Yes." No flinch. She owed him the unflinched version. "There was an envelope. There is always an envelope. Mine had enough in it to clear my debts and buy my first real vessel, and a promise underneath it that if I ever spoke, every certification I held would be reviewed, every client I had would hear I falsified surveys, and I would end my career in the same disgrace they were building for Elias. Except he'd be dead and couldn't defend himself, and I'd be alive and wouldn't deserve to."

The silence that followed was long. Out in the dark, somewhere between the two boats, a night heron complained once and gave up.

"You're lying," Sable said at last, but his voice had no conviction in it, and they both heard that. "Or you're setting me up. This is exactly the story a rival would tell to get close to me. To get me to trust you, lead you to whatever's down there—"

"To what? What do you think is down there, Sable?" She leaned forward. "Bullion? Manifests say machine parts and grain. Insurance says otherwise, insurance always says otherwise, but even if there were gold in that hull it would be worth a fraction of what your hatred of me is worth to whoever's been feeding it. Think. Who benefits from Sable Sterling hating Marguerite Voss? Who benefits from the two people closest to the truth of Rivergate spending ten years aimed at each other instead of at them?"

He didn't answer. But he sat down. She counted that as a kind of surrender, and it gave her the courage for the last of it — the part she had never said aloud to any living soul.

"There was a fifth diver on the survey team. A man named Teodoras. Old Greek, twenty years on the channel works, the best bottom-reader I ever met until I met your father. The night before the *Landfall* sailed, Teddy came to my bunk on the survey tender. He was frightened, and Teddy had never once been frightened in his life. He said he'd been diving the marker line that afternoon and found fresh scouring under the outer channel sill — new sand channels, cut within days, the signature of a hull that had already grounded there once and been pulled off. Someone had taken the *Landfall* out on a trial run through a channel they knew was marginal, run her aground deliberately at slack water, and refloated her before anyone noticed. Testing whether she'd stick." Voss's voice dropped. "Teddy thought it was insurance preparation. He was right. He said he was going to report it, and I told him not to. I told him he'd ruin the contract for everyone. I told him no one would thank him and the consortium would bury him. And he looked at me — I will see that look until I die — and he said, *the girl is afraid too*, and he went back to his bunk."

"What happened to him?"

"He was on the marker buoy crew the morning the *Landfall* went down. His skiff was found swamped eight miles offshore, engine dry, fuel cap sealed. A man with his sea hours did not swamp a skiff in conditions like that morning's. His name was removed from the survey roster in the final report. You can check. The report lists four survey divers. There were five."

Sable's breath had gone shallow. She watched him do the arithmetic she had done a thousand times — the erased name, the doctored tides, the pre-grounding scars on the channel floor — and arrive where she had arrived, the place she had been circling for a decade like a shark around its own wound.

"They sank her," he said. "They sank her on purpose."

"They insured her for triple value two months before she sailed, loaded her with a manifest that doesn't match her draft marks, ran her aground once to test the spot, and then closed the tide window on paper so that when she stuck for real, the record would show pilot error and an owner's greed. Your father's greed. The perfect fraud needs a villain, Sable, and they chose him because he was famous enough to be believable and proud enough to be blamed." She made herself hold his eyes. "And I let them. I took the envelope, I bought my boat, and I built everything you see out of that money, and every year it got bigger and colder and further from the girl who told an old man to keep his mouth shut."

"Why are you telling me now?"

"Because the syndicate owns me." She said it without softening. "Not metaphorically. When the shipping crash came, I refinanced through a Meridian shell. Then another. My fleet, my yard, my house — papered through them, cross-collateralized, structured so that I could never dig out. I am the local partner of record on this salvage because they own the partner. They think I'm theirs. They sent me to find you because they believe I hate you, and I have performed that hatred so long and so well that even I sometimes forget it's a costume." She laughed, briefly, and it was an ugly sound. "But they made one mistake. They put the *Landfall* in front of me. They handed the coward who watched it happen a chance to be present at the exhumation."

"So this is penance."

"This is absolution," she corrected. "Penance I could do alone. Absolution requires a witness. It requires—" she gestured at him, at the whole stubborn architecture of him, "—it requires the son of the man I helped bury. I don't expect forgiveness. I expect to be useful. There's a difference, and I've had ten years to study it."

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, her engineer's radio murmured a weather band, a voice reciting wind speeds for waters they would all soon be fighting in.

Sable stood and walked to the wall of tide tables. He touched the oldest set the way a man touches a grave marker — two fingers, brief, certain.

"Ninety minutes," he said. "My father argued the window at the inquiry. They produced the logs and he couldn't counter them. He died with everyone believing he faked his own numbers." His voice cracked on the last word and he mastered it, barely. "You could have testified."

"I could have. I would have lost everything, and they'd have buried me next to him, and the logs would still have won. That's what I told myself, every year, on the anniversary, when I drank alone in rooms with good views." She rose and joined him at the wall, standing a careful arm's length away. "It's a true statement, Sable. It's also a coward's statement. Both things can be true. That's the discovery I'm offering you tonight — that the people we've spent our lives dividing into heroes and villains are mostly just cowards with good timing and bad ones. Your father was brave. I was bought. Teddy was brave and it likely killed him. And you—" she turned to face him fully, "—you have spent ten years being brave in every direction except the one that matters. Running isn't courage. I should know. I invented half your routes."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not softening, nothing so generous, but a recalculation. The rival becoming, if not an ally, at least a mirror: the same grief, worn differently, hers polished into ambition, his worn rough into exile.

"The journal," she said quietly. "If there's anything left in that strongroom, it'll be his field notes. He documented everything — drafts, manifests, soundings. If he suspected, and Teddy thinks he suspected, then it's down there in his handwriting. That's what Meridian is really after. Not the cargo. The paper. They need it to stay lost."

"And you want it found."

"I want it *read*." Her voice finally broke its mooring, and she let it. "I want it read aloud in a courtroom with my name in the transcript as the witness for the prosecution. I want to spend whatever years I have left paying down a debt that compounds daily. I am fifty-one years old, Sable, and I am tired of being the villain in a story I didn't finish reading."

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the anchor light of his own boat bobbed in the dark, and beyond it, invisible but utterly present, lay the shoal water where the *Landfall* waited with her dead and her documents and her decade of silence.

"If you're lying," he said at last, "I will spend the rest of my life making sure yours ends in a courtroom too."

"Fair terms," said Marguerite Voss. "Better than I deserve."

He went down the ladder without another word, and she stood at the rail watching his running lights shrink toward his own boat, and she did not feel cleansed, or forgiven, or even lighter. She felt exposed — flayed open and left in the wind, which was, she supposed, what honesty actually felt like when you hadn't practiced it in ten years.

Below in her cabin, the encrypted phone buzzed against the chart table. Meridian's operations desk, checking in, wanting her assessment of the Sterling boy's intentions, wanting confirmation that her surveillance of his crew remained active.

Voss looked at the tide tables on the wall. Ninety minutes. A whole life, hidden in ninety minutes.

She picked up the phone, and lied to the syndicate with the smoothness of long practice — and for the first time, the lie tasted like the beginning of something rather than the end.
