## 8. First Tide: The Outer Shelf

The charts were wrong.

Sable had known it before they ever cleared the harbor mouth, had felt it in the way Voss's survey packet sat in his hands — too clean, too confident, the contour lines drawn with a certainty that no honest hydrographer would claim for a stretch of coast this treacherous. He'd said nothing. He'd spent ten years saying nothing, letting other people's paper tell him where to go and who to be, and he was tired of it in a way that had gone past anger into something more like bone-deep recognition.

Now, standing at the rail of the *Magpie's Debt* with the outer shelf rising gray-green beneath them through forty feet of cold water, he let himself say it out loud.

"These coordinates are a lie."

Dessa looked up from her tablet, wind whipping a strand of hair across her face. "You want to say that a little louder? The syndicate fleet's only twenty miles out."

"They're welcome to hear it." Sable spread Voss's chart across the deck box and weighted its corners with two shackles and a coffee mug. "Look at it. Whoever drew this put the wreck on the eastern edge of the shelf, hard against the drop-off. Convenient. Easy to find, easy to work, easy to strip in a week if you've got their kind of equipment."

Okafor leaned over the chart, his broad shadow falling across it. "And if it's not there?"

"Then whoever drew this chart wanted us spending our first tide — maybe our only good one — diving sand and rock in the wrong place while their fleet steams up behind us." He tapped the eastern edge once, twice. "This isn't bad surveying. Bad surveying is sloppy. This is *tidy*. Tidy means deliberate."

Pip, perched on the rail with the particular bonelessness of a sixteen-year-old who has never once feared falling, squinted at the water as though the answer might be visible from up there. "So where is it?"

Sable didn't answer right away. He turned his back on the chart and faced the sea instead — the long swell rolling in from the southeast, the way the current bent around the shoal spit to the north, the faint discoloration where the bottom changed from sand to something harder. His father had taught him to read water the way other men read faces. *The sea keeps records, boy. It just doesn't keep them where anyone thinks to look.*

He had hated those lessons. He had hated the early mornings, the cold hands, the old man's patient voice correcting him — *no, look again, you're seeing what you expect to see* — and he had carried all that hatred like ballast for a decade, right up until the moment he stood on a pitching deck trying to find a dead man's ship and discovered that the hatred had worn through, somewhere along the way, and what was left underneath was simply the skill itself, load-bearing, permanent, his.

"The current comes off the shoal here," he said, tracing the line of it in the air. "It bends south around the spit, then it hits deeper water and slows. Anything heavy that came off a breaking hull doesn't scatter evenly — it travels with the current until the current gives up. So you don't look where the ship went down. You look where the debris got *deposited*." His finger moved down and west, into the trough between the shelf and the deeper channel. "There. That hollow. Sand collects there anyway; anything else settling into it gets buried fast, which is why nobody's found it. And the syndicate's charts wouldn't show it because their survey was done from the surface, by people who never asked what the current does after it passes the wreck."

Dessa was already recalculating. "That puts us three hundred meters off the marked site. Maybe four."

"Maybe. Or maybe I'm wrong, and we spend the afternoon looking at an empty seabed." He met her eyes. "Either way, we're burning tide doing it. Get me a line on the bottom."

* * *

The *Magpie's Debt* was not a salvage vessel. She was a coastal freighter that had been retired twice and resurrected both times through Dessa's stubbornness and Okafor's welding, and she handled like a barge with opinions. But she held position well enough in the light chop, and when Sable went down the ladder into the green murmur of the shallows, she was there above him — a dark keel, a smudge of engine noise, a lifeline paying out steadily through Okafor's gloved hands.

The water closed over him and the world simplified.

He had always loved this part, and had always been slightly ashamed of loving it, because it was his father's part too. Down here there was no name to outrun, no reputation to dodge, no bar in some boomtown where someone might recognize the surname Sterling and lean in with that particular expression — pity dressed up as curiosity. Down here there was only pressure and breath and the slow arithmetic of the body, and the seabed unrolling beneath him like a page waiting to be read.

He descended along the shot line and the light went from silver to olive to a deep, grainy bronze. At thirty feet the sand below him resolved into ripples — long, even corrugations running northeast to southwest, sculpted by the same current he'd traced from the surface. He checked his compass, checked his depth, and began to swim the pattern.

*Look again. You're seeing what you expect to see.*

He wasn't expecting anything. That was the strange mercy of it. Ten years of refusing to think about Rivergate had stripped him of expectations; all he had was method, and method, it turned out, had survived the neglect better than he had.

The ripple field changed at the edge of the hollow. It was subtle — the corrugations flattening, spacing widening, the sand growing finer — but it was the kind of subtle that meant something, and Sable's pulse picked up with a steadiness that surprised him. He followed the transition line. Sand became shell hash. Shell hash gave way, in patches, to gravel — and gravel did not belong here. Gravel was ballast stone, or coal, or cargo, and none of those things walked three hundred meters on their own.

He found the first artifact eleven minutes into the dive.

A iron strap, crusted white, half-buried at an angle. Then a fitting — bronze, unmistakably bronze even under the patina, because bronze kept its own color the way nothing else down here did. Then a shape in the sand that his mind refused to name until he'd brushed it clear with the side of his glove: a section of hull plating, torn, the rivet lines still crisp, the metal furred with a decade of growth.

Not scattered the way a wreck scatters. Scattered the way a wreck scatters *when someone has already been through it*.

Sable hung in the water above the plating and felt the cold reach through his suit in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. There were drag marks in the sediment. Modern ones. The syndicate hadn't just drawn false charts — they'd run a preliminary grab at the true site, years ago or months ago, and abandoned it when the silt beat them. They knew where the Landfall lay. They'd always known. The corrupted coordinates weren't meant to hide the wreck from everyone.

They were meant to hide it from *him*.

He should have surfaced then. Protocol said surface, report, re-plan. Instead he swam the hollow's full length, laying it out in his head the way his father would have — grid corners, reference points, the low ridge of bedrock on the western lip that would anchor a search line. By the time he thumbed the lift signal, he had the whole scatter field mapped behind his eyes, precise and complete, and he understood that he could no more refuse to use it than he could refuse to breathe.

His father had given him this. Not the shame, not the name, not the ruined expeditions and the whispered-about death. This. The reading of water and sand and current, the patience, the refusal to see what he expected to see. Everything else had been taken or falsified. This was the inheritance that had waited ten years in his hands, unrecognized, like a tool wrapped in cloth at the bottom of a sea chest.

Okafor hauled him up onto the swim step and pulled his hood back, and the afternoon light hit him like a blow after the bronze dimness below.

"Well?" Dessa called from the wheelhouse rail.

"It's there," Sable said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Three hundred meters west of their mark. Hull plating, fittings, ballast stone — and drag marks. Someone's worked this site before and quit." He climbed aboard, dripping, and stood over Voss's tidy chart without touching it. "Get me the marker buoys. We're setting our own grid."

Pip was grinning like it was Christmas. Dessa was already moving. Okafor clapped him once on the shoulder, hard enough to stagger him, and said nothing, which from Okafor was a speech.

Only Voss, aft by the stern rail, watched him with an expression he couldn't parse — relief braided with something darker, guilt maybe, or fear. She'd given them these charts. She'd have known they were poisoned. Which meant either she'd expected him to fail —

— or she'd expected him to be exactly what he was pretending not to be: his father's son.

"Voss," he said, and she flinched almost imperceptibly. "Who drew this survey?"

"A contractor," she said. "I can get you the name."

"Do that." He rolled the chart closed and handed it back to her, handle first, like returning a weapon. "Because whoever drew it knew the true site. And I want to know why they thought I wouldn't."

She took the chart and held it against her chest, and for a moment — just a moment — she looked less like a rival and more like a woman carrying something heavy for a very long time.

Below them, unseen, the true scatter field waited in its hollow of fine sand, holding whatever the Landfall had gone down with — and whatever someone had gone down trying to prove.

The tide was turning. They had five left.
