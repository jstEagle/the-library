## 9. Pip Overboard

Pip had been told three times, and each time the telling had landed on her like weather — present, unpleasant, and passing.

*You stay topside. You hold the tag line. You do not go in the water.*

Sable Sterling said it the way he said most things, which was to say barely at all, his voice pitched low and flat as a lead weight, and Okafor repeated it with more warmth but no less finality, and Dessa didn't say anything because Dessa was below in the engine room keeping their patched-together boat alive by what sounded like argument and prayer. So it was Sable's version that stuck in Pip's ears as she crouched at the rail with the tag line wrapped twice around her fist, watching the gray-green water of the outer shelf heave and breathe.

The thing about being sixteen and a deckhand was that nobody ever told you *why*. They told you *stay*, the way you'd tell a dog. And Pip understood — she wasn't stupid, whatever the crew thought when they spelled things out slowly around her — that the sea off Rivergate Point was not a forgiving sea, that the wreck of the *Landfall* lay down there in forty feet of cold green murk like a held breath, that the tides through the channel ran hard enough to fold a grown diver in half against the hull plating. She understood all of it. Understanding had never once stopped her from doing the thing anyway.

That was the trouble with Pip, if you asked anyone aboard the *Kestrel's Wake*. That was also, if you asked Pip, the whole point of her.

* * *

The morning had gone wrong slowly, the way mornings did. First dive of the second tide: Sable and Okafor down on the scatter field, mapping, lifting, running lines from the wreck's broken spine to the lift bags Dessa had rigged from salvage-yard castoffs. Pip's job was the surface end of those lines — keep them clear, keep them tensioned, watch for fouling, call depth and drift. A job for hands and eyes and patience. Pip had two of the three.

The water was doing something strange. She'd noticed it an hour ago and hadn't said anything because she didn't have the words for it yet, only the feeling — the way the swell came in longer than it should have, the way the current buoy, a mile north, leaned harder than the tide tables said it ought to. Dessa would have known what it meant. Dessa knew everything about water that Pip knew about engines, which was everything, which was too much, which was why nobody asked.

Then the surge brought up the artifact.

She saw it happen almost directly beneath her, a pale flicker in the murk where the scatter field ended and the sand shelved away toward deeper water. One of the lift bags had shifted — she'd see later, in the terrible slow replay she'd give herself a hundred times, that Okafor had clipped it to a piece of hull plating that wasn't as sound as it looked — and the surge, the long wrong swell arriving exactly on schedule, had rolled the plating over and torn the bag loose. It went tumbling down-slope, trailing its line, and something went with it. Something small and pale and *wrong*, catching light the way nothing down there should catch light, rolling end over end into the green.

Pip knew what it was before her brain finished assembling the picture. She'd spent the last two days cataloguing finds under Dessa's supervision, and she knew every shape that had come off that seabed: concreted iron, crusted brass, the dull lead seals of cargo bales. This was none of those. This was clean-edged. This was deliberate. This was the kind of thing that made Sable Sterling go quiet in a way that wasn't his usual quiet.

It settled on the sand at the edge of visibility, maybe thirty feet down, and lay there gleaming like a coin at the bottom of a well.

Pip looked at the tag line in her fist. She looked at the water. Down below, two diver bubbles broke the surface in lazy silver strings, far off across the scatter field, both of them deep in the wreck's ribs where they couldn't see, couldn't hear, couldn't stop her.

Nobody would even know until it was done.

Later — much later, in the dark, in the bunk she shared with the boat's smell of diesel and salt — Pip would try to explain it to herself honestly, and the honest version was this: she wanted to be the one who found it. Not Sable, with his dead father's instincts and his ten years of running. Not Okafor, who could read a wreck like scripture. Her. The deckhand. The kid they tolerated because she was cheap and cheerful and climbed the rigging like she'd been born in it. She wanted, just once, to put something in Sable Sterling's hands that would make him look at her the way he looked at the sea — like it mattered, like it was real, like it was worth the whole rest of the world.

So she tied off the tag line, checked her weights out of habit rather than sense, pulled the spare mask and the shorty tank from the rack — the emergency rig, the one nobody was supposed to touch without Okafor — and went over the side.

* * *

The cold hit her first, then the noise. Everyone thought the sea was silent down there and everyone was wrong; the shallows roared. The surge that had looked like long swells from above was, from inside, a living thing — a hand the size of the sky pushing her, releasing her, pushing again, dragging her body sideways across the sand in great slow pulses. Her fins found the bottom and lost it. Her mask fogged and cleared and fogged.

She got to the artifact on the third pulse, more thrown than swimming, and closed her hand around it, and understood immediately that she had made a mistake of a size she couldn't yet measure.

It was heavy. Heavier than it looked — dense, cold, ridged along one edge like a tool or a fitting, and she had no net, no bag, no line to clip it to. Just her fist and her grip and thirty feet of open water between her and a boat that was, right now, a small white smudge of light overhead.

And the surge was building. She could feel it in her teeth, in the pressure against her eardrums, the whole ocean leaning shoreward with the tide turning, and between her and the boat lay the wreck itself — the *Landfall*'s broken spine, black ribs rising out of the sand, and the gap between two of those ribs was exactly where the current wanted to funnel through.

Pip was not stupid. Pip had grown up on these waters, stealing rides on harbor tugs since she was nine, and she knew — in the way you know a storm is coming, in your bones, in the animal part of you — that she could not make it back the way she'd come. The surge would take her and the artifact and push them both into the wreck's iron bones, and the iron would win, the way it always won, the way it had won ten years ago against better divers than her.

She turned with the current instead. Let it carry her. Kicked for the gap in the ribs because it was the only way through, and the artifact dragged at her arm like an anchor, and her tank — the little emergency bottle, never meant for this, never meant for *her* — gave a wheeze and a stutter and started giving her less air than she needed.

The gap came at her fast. Too fast. The surge funneled through it and took her like a river takes a leaf, spinning her, slamming her shoulder against plating furred with ten years of growth, tearing her fin half off, and the artifact — she felt her fingers opening, felt it go, felt the pale shape tumble away into the dark on the far side of the wreck —

— and then someone's arm closed around her chest like a band of iron, and someone's regulator jammed into her mouth, and the world became bubbles and violence and one furious heartbeat that wasn't hers.

* * *

They broke the surface together on the fourth wave, gasping, and the *Kestrel's Wake* was fifty yards off and closing, Dessa's engine screaming a note Pip had never heard it make, and Okafor hauled them both over the gunwale with hands that shook so badly he dropped Pip twice onto the deck plates and didn't apologize for either.

For a moment there was only breathing. Pip's own breath, ragged and tasting of copper. Okafor's, beside her, prayers in three languages. The engine note falling away as Dessa throttled down. And Sable.

Sable stood over her dripping seawater, mask pushed up on his forehead, and his face was doing something Pip had never seen it do. Ten days she'd worked for this man, and she'd catalogued his faces the way she catalogued artifacts: the flat one, the distant one, the one that was almost a smile when Dessa swore at the engine. This was none of them. This was a face with the skin stripped off it.

"You were told." His voice came out wrong. Cracked down the middle. "I told you. I *told* you—"

"I saw it," Pip said, and her teeth were chattering so hard the words came out in pieces. "In the surge. It came loose, it was — it was important, I could tell it was important—"

"It was a *fitting*." His voice rose, cracked again, and he turned away and turned back like a man fighting his own body. "It was a piece of pipe, Pip. It was garbage. You went into a turning tide over a wreck site for *garbage*—"

"It might not have been—"

"It might have been your *body*!" 

The words came out of him like something breaking, and the whole boat went silent. Even the sea seemed to lean in. Sable Sterling never shouted. In ten days Pip had heard him angry exactly once, quietly, at a chart, and mostly she'd heard him be nothing at all — competent, absent, a man piloting himself through his own life from very far away. Now he was here, all of him, everywhere, shaking, and Pip realized with a strange floating clarity that she had done this. That underneath all his flatness and his rules and his leaving-before-dawn, there had been this the whole time, packed down like cargo in a flooded hold, and she had cracked the seam.

He crouched in front of her. His hands — steady now, she noticed, steady the way they were steady on lines and charts, the competence coming back like a reflex — took her face and tilted it toward the gray light, checking her eyes, her pupils, pressing two fingers under her jaw.

"Tell me where it hurts."

"My shoulder. I hit the—" She winced as his fingers found the spot. "I'm okay. Sable, I'm okay—"

"Okafor. Blanket. The warm kit." He didn't look away from her. "Dessa! Get us inside the lee of the shoal, we're done for this tide."

"We're done?" Dessa's head appeared in the companionway, wild-haired, grease-streaked, and Pip watched her take in the scene — Pip wrapped in Okafor's arms, blue-lipped, the spare tank lying empty on the deck, Sable kneeling in a puddle of seawater with his hands still cupped around a sixteen-year-old's face. Dessa's expression went through fury and arrived somewhere else entirely. "We're done," she agreed, in a voice that promised this conversation was not over, and vanished below, and the engine changed its tune.

Sable stayed crouched there while Okafor wrapped her, while the boat turned and ran for the sheltered water behind the shoal, while the sun came and went behind clouds. He didn't yell again. He didn't say much of anything. But he didn't let go of her either, not really — one hand resting on her shoulder, the uninjured one, light as a bird, as if he was checking she was still solid, still there, still real.

"I had people once," he said finally, so quietly Pip almost missed it under the engine. "A long time ago. On another boat. I learned what it costs to lose them." His thumb pressed once against her shoulder, a small unconscious pressure, like testing a knot. "I decided it was cheaper not to have any."

Pip's chattering teeth slowed. She looked up at him — this man she'd hero-worshipped from the day Voss's contract showed up, this legend with the dead father and the ten years of running — and saw, for the first time, not a legend at all. Just a man sitting in a puddle on a rotten deck, holding on to her like she was the last dry thing on earth.

"That's stupid," she said.

Something moved across his face. It took her a second to recognize it, because she'd never seen one before.

It was a laugh.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, it is." He stood, scrubbed a hand through his wet hair, and became — not all the way, but partly — the flat, competent Sable again, turning to check the fuel, the heading, the tide tables Dessa had left open on the chart table. But the flatness had a crack in it now, and light was getting through, and Pip sat wrapped in her blanket watching him and understood that she had changed something on this boat today, and that it could not be changed back.

Okafor squeezed her ankle once, gently, and murmured, "Never again, small one. Never. Again." But his eyes were soft.

* * *

They anchored in the lee as the tide turned fully, the water going slack and brown, and Dessa came up from below with tea and a lecture she delivered standing over Pip like a storm front. Pip took the tea and the lecture and the extra blanket and the watch duty she was assigned as punishment — four hours staring at the anchor line — and accepted all of it gratefully, because punishment meant she was still crew, still theirs, still worth the trouble of being angry about.

It was near dusk when Sable came and sat beside her at the rail without being asked.

For a long time he said nothing. Below them the anchor line hummed in the slack current, and beyond the shoal the syndicate fleet sat at the horizon like a row of black teeth, patient, waiting, three lights already showing against the dying sky.

"The thing you went after," Sable said at last. "The fitting. Describe it to me. Exactly."

Pip blinked. "You said it was garbage."

"I said that while my heart was trying to climb out through my throat." He was looking at the horizon, at the black teeth, and his voice had gone quiet and careful, the way it went careful when he talked about his father. "But you've got good eyes, Pip. Better than mine, maybe. So describe it."

She closed her own eyes and put herself back in the green roar, in the surge, in the half-second her fingers had actually held it. "Heavy. Dense. About so long." She measured with her hands. "Ridged along one edge — machined ridges, regular, like a gear or a ratchet. And one end was capped. Sealed. Brass or bronze, cleaned bright, like it hadn't been down there ten years at all."

Sable went very still.

"Sable?"

"A sealed fitting doesn't come loose from hull plating in a surge," he said slowly, mostly to himself. "Sealed fittings are *installed*. Somebody put that there recently." He turned and looked at her, and Pip saw the crack in him widen, and what was coming through it was not grief anymore. It was something hotter. "Somebody's been on that wreck ahead of us. Planting things. Staging the site." His jaw tightened. "Which means the syndicate isn't just racing us to the *Landfall*."

He stood, and the evening wind caught his hair, and he looked down at her with an expression she was going to remember for the rest of her life — the look of a man who has just realized the game is older and dirtier than he thought, and that the kid he nearly lost today might be the only witness to how dirty.

"Pip. Get Dessa. Get everybody. We need to talk about what else down there isn't what it seems."
