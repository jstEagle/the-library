## 10. The Syndicate's Courtesy

Dessa Vann had learned to read engines the way other people read faces, and the engine of the *Kestrel's Grace* had been telling her a story all morning that she didn't like. A harmonic she couldn't place — a faint, arrhythmic shudder in the drive train, somewhere aft of the reduction gear, that came and went like a man clearing his throat before delivering bad news. She lay under the housing with a stethoscope pressed to the mount and let the machine talk, and the machine said: *something is loose, and it is loose in you too.*

"You've been down there three hours," Pip said, dangling legs over the engine room hatch. "Okafor says you're hiding."

"Okafor is a wise man." She slid out, wiped her hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth, and looked up through the hatch at a sky the color of hammered tin. The syndicate fleet sat on the horizon like a row of teeth — six hulls, white and patient, anchored two miles off the wreck site in a formation that was either courtesy or a noose. "Tell Okafor he's right, and that I'll come up when the shudder tells me what it is."

"Or you could come up now," Pip said, "because there's a boat coming."

She heard it before she saw it — the flat, expensive purr of twin diesels tuned by someone who had never had to fix them in a hurry. She climbed the ladder two rungs at a time.

The launch came out of the glare off the water, long and low and black-hulled, cutting a wake so clean it looked drawn with a straightedge. No flag. No markings. Just a man standing at the wheel in a pale linen jacket, and beside him a woman in a gray suit who held a leather case the way lawyers hold them — like it contained something that was legally, if not morally, sharp.

"Sable," Dessa called, and the word went through the boat like current through a wire. "Company."

* * *

They gathered on the aft deck, and Dessa watched Sable the way she always watched him when strangers were near — not because she doubted him, but because she had spent eleven years learning how men carry their fathers, and Sable Sterling carried his the way a diver carries a weight belt: buckled on, never mentioned, always felt.

The launch idled alongside. The man in linen was younger than Dessa expected, with the unlined, well-fed face of someone whose hands had never been in water colder than a hotel pool. The woman in gray was older, and her eyes went over the *Kestrel's Grace* with an appraiser's patience — the patched plating, the jury-rigged crane, Okafor at the rail with his arms folded like a wall, Pip hovering in the companionway pretending not to listen and listening to everything.

"Captain Sterling," the man called up. "My name is Corven. I represent the interests financing the Meridian salvage fleet." He said it the way other men said *good morning*. "May we come aboard? I promise you this will be the most profitable conversation of your expedition."

"That's a low bar," Sable said. "Come aboard."

The woman came up the ladder first, sure-footed, and Dessa noted that — the sure-footedness. Corven came up like a man crossing a lobby. They stood on the deck that Dessa had caulked with her own hands in a boatyard in Port Amity, and Corven looked around at the rust and the patches and the honest, battered competence of the thing, and smiled the smile of a man about to be generous.

"Captain. I'll be brief, because I know your window is short and mine is shorter." He opened the leather case — not the woman, Corven himself, which told Dessa the case was theater and the woman was the act. "You have, by our estimate, four tides left before that channel closes. We have dredges. We have a hard-hat dive team. We have the patience of people who own the paper this whole coast floats on." He drew out a folder, cream-colored, heavy stock. "We also have a check."

He set it on the deck locker, face up, and the wind tried to take it and he weighted it with two fingers.

"Two hundred thousand for your time and your silence. The wreck becomes ours, the site becomes ours, and you become — " he glanced around at the crew, and Dessa felt the glance like a hand on her shoulder — "free. Free to go wherever people like you go. No questions, no liens, no history. It is, I'm told, what Captain Sterling does best."

Dessa heard Okafor's breath change beside her. Heard Pip go very still in the companionway.

"And if we don't?" Sable said. His voice was level. Dessa had heard him level like that before, in Port Amity, when a harbor official had asked to see papers.

"Then we take the site," Corven said pleasantly, "at slack water tomorrow, with or without your cooperation. And in the course of taking it, we will of course be required to notify the maritime authority of certain irregularities we've observed." He turned a page in the folder he hadn't handed over. "Your salvage permit, Captain. It's a beautiful piece of work. The seal is very nearly perfect. The registration number belongs to a vessel that was scrapped in 2019. The signature of the harbormaster at Corvo Bay — a man, incidentally, who has been dead for two years." He looked up, and for the first time the pleasantness dropped away and something older and colder looked out of his face. "You've been operating outside the law for a decade, Captain. We are simply offering to make that someone else's problem. Or to make it yours, very publicly, at the exact moment you're least able to afford it."

* * *

Later — much later, after the launch had gone and the check still sat on the deck locker where nobody had touched it — Dessa stood in the galley with the kettle on and did the arithmetic she always did when the world shifted under her.

She had met Sable Sterling in a jail corridor in Corvo Bay, eleven years ago. He'd been twenty-four, freshly ruined by a verdict he never stopped insisting was a lie, and she'd been thirty-one, freshly fired from the only legitimate marine engineering firm that would ever hire a woman with her record, and they'd recognized each other the way drowning people do. Everything since — the black-market digs, the forged permits, the harbor officials paid in cash, the whole gray economy of competent people the legitimate world had no use for — everything had been built on a shared, unspoken agreement: *the law failed us first, so we owe it nothing.*

And now a man in linen had come aboard and said, in effect: *we know, and we will use it.* The syndicate's justice was not justice. But it was not entirely a lie either. That was the thing that sat in Dessa's chest like the shudder in the drive train — the thing she couldn't place. Because Sable's permits *were* forged. Because the crew *was* outside the law. Because if the syndicate fell tomorrow and the tables turned, there were harbormasters on this coast who would look at the *Kestrel's Grace* and see exactly what Corven saw.

The kettle screamed. She poured two cups, carried one up to the wheelhouse, and found Sable standing at the window with the check in his hand, looking at it the way a man looks at a snake he's been asked to hold.

"Okafor says burn it," she said. "Pip says frame it. I haven't voted yet."

"What do you say?"

She leaned against the doorframe. "I say he knew the permit number before he ever came aboard. That's not surveillance from the fleet. That's someone on this boat, Sable. Someone who's talked to their people."

Sable didn't move for a long moment. Then he set the check down on the chart table, deliberately, face down, as if putting it away.

"Voss," he said. "It has to be Voss. Her debts — she told us —"

"She told us what she wanted us to hear," Dessa said. "Or she's being squeezed. Or it's none of those and it's someone quieter. I don't know yet. But I know engines, and I know when something's loose in the drive train. You can run it for a while. It doesn't fix itself."

He looked at her then, and she saw something she'd only seen a few times in eleven years — the weight belt slipping, just a little, the grief and the fear showing through the competence. "If we take the money," he said quietly, "we're done. Not just the job. Done. I'd spend the rest of my life being exactly what Corven said I was."

"And if we don't take it, and they take the site, we lose everything anyway and go to jail for the privilege."

"Yes."

"So the only move that isn't losing," Dessa said slowly, "is one where the truth comes out on our terms. All of it. The wreck, the manifest, whatever your father found — and ours too. The permits. Everything, in the open, before they can use any of it against us." She heard herself say it and was surprised at how clean it sounded. "You can't fight people who own the law, Sable. But you can make the law too expensive to own."

Sable picked the check up again. He walked to the little coal stove in the corner of the wheelhouse — the one Pip had salvaged from a tug — opened the door, and fed the cream-colored paper into the fire. It caught, curled, and was gone in eleven seconds. Dessa counted.

"Two hundred thousand," she said.

"Cheapest thing I ever burned," Sable said.

But his eyes went to the horizon, to the six white hulls sitting patient as weather, and Dessa saw that the fear hadn't burned with the paper. Nothing was decided. Everything was moving. And somewhere on this small, patched, honest boat, someone had already sold them.

* * *

She found Pip at the bow that evening, sitting with knees drawn up, watching the fleet lights come on one by one across the water — six strings of white lamps, orderly as a hotel.

"They're just *sitting* there," Pip said. "Why don't they just come?"

"Because they don't have to come yet," Dessa said, sitting down beside the kid. "That's what money is, Pip. It's the ability to wait."

Pip was quiet a while. Then: "If they tell the harbormaster about the permits. About us. Do we go to jail?"

Dessa thought about lying, decided Pip had earned better. "Maybe. Some of us. Probably not you — you're a minor, they'd ship you to an aunt somewhere." She watched the kid's face do the arithmetic of that — the aunt, the shore, the end of the boat. "But here's the thing I've learned about the law, having spent a fair amount of time on its wrong side. It doesn't actually care whose fault anything was. It cares whose paperwork was worse. Right now, our paperwork is worse. Tomorrow, if we find what I think is down there, theirs might be."

"You think there's something down there," Pip said. "Something big."

"I think a syndicate doesn't spend six ships and two hundred thousand dollars on a wreck full of nothing." She stood, knees cracking, and looked back toward the stern, where Sable stood alone at the rail in the last light, and beyond him, where Voss's chartered tender lay moored a hundred yards off, its cabin dark, its silhouette small and unreadable on the water. "And I think somebody on this water already knows exactly what it is."

The fleet lights burned steady across the dark. And below her feet, faint as a heartbeat, the *Kestrel's Grace* shuddered once, and went on.
