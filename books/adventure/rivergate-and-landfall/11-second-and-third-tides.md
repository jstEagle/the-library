## 11. Second and Third Tides

The second tide came in gray and mean, a wind off the northeast that put a chop on the water before the sun had cleared the headland. Okafor stood at the compressor with his hands wrapped around the warm metal like a man holding a stove in winter, and watched Sable Sterling come up the ladder out of the dark water looking like something the sea had chewed and decided against.

"Bottom's worse," Sable said, spitting his regulator free. "Silt's moved overnight. The scatter field's buried half a meter deeper where it was open yesterday."

"Then we dig it out again," Okafor said.

"That's what I said." Sable stripped his hood, and Okafor saw the lines around his eyes, the salt drying white in the creases of his neck. Two dives down already and the day barely started. "Dessa! Lift bag's fouled on the port rail — the line's chafed through the outer braid. I want it re-rigged before I'm back in."

"Want, want, want," Dessa muttered from the engine hatch, but she was already moving, a coil of new line over her shoulder, her hands doing the arithmetic of knots before her mouth finished complaining. She'd been at the winch since four. Okafor knew because he'd heard her — the particular rhythm of a woman who didn't sleep when there was a machine that could be made better.

The boat — the *Maren*, Dessa's patched-together, debt-ridden, beautiful ugly boat — rolled on the swell, and Okafor rolled with it, and thought: this is what work looks like when it's honest. He'd done dishonest work. He'd done the kind of diving where you didn't log your bottom times and the boats didn't answer to any flag and the men who paid you didn't want your face remembered. Ten years of it, off and on, before Sable Sterling had looked at him across a dock in a town whose name Okafor had already forgotten and said, *I need someone who can hold his breath past the point of good sense.*

He'd said yes because he'd recognized something. Not desperation — Sable hadn't been desperate then, not the way he was desperate now. He'd recognized the running. The two of them had been running in opposite directions and somehow ended up on the same dock.

Now they were both running toward the same wreck, and Okafor wasn't sure that was better.

* * *

The hull breach opened on the starboard side of the Landfall like a wound that had been trying to heal for ten years and never got the chance. Okafor went in through it on the third dive of the second tide, with Sable behind him and their lights cutting twin cones through water the color of strong tea.

Inside was a cathedral of rot. Deck plating hung in sheets, furred with ten years of soft growth, and the silt lay over everything like gray snow — deep, undisturbed, patient. Their fins stirred it and it bloomed up around them and for a full minute Okafor was blind, hanging in a cloud of his own making, feeling the wreck breathe around him: the groan of plating flexing with the swell transmitted down from far above, the tick and shift of things settling.

Then the silt began to fall, and the lights found the corridor, and they went in.

They worked by touch as much as sight, the way Okafor had learned in the navy and Sable had learned God knew where — probably from a dead man's notebook. Sable moved through the wreck like he'd lived in it. That was the thing Okafor had never said out loud, the thing that sat in his chest on every dive: Sable didn't swim the Landfall like a salvager. He swam it like a son walking through his father's house. He put his hand on bulkheads without looking. He turned into doorways before his light reached them. Somewhere under all that silt was a map of this ship that Sable had been carrying in his body for ten years without knowing it.

They cleared a path aft, bagging what the surge offered — a lantern, a brass fitting, a case of something that turned out to be preserved fruit, which made Pip laugh so hard on the surface he nearly fell in. And at the end of the corridor, past a collapsed section where the deck had folded down like a kneeler, they found the door.

Steel. Sealed. A wheel-lock gone to rust, and stamped into the plate above it, still legible under the growth: STRONGROOM — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

Sable put his hand flat against it and stayed that way long enough that Okafor checked his gauge without meaning to.

"It's intact," Sable said finally. His voice came through the comms strange and flat. "Waterproof seal's probably still good. Whatever's in there—"

"Is in there," Okafor said. "And we're at forty minutes with the swim back. We crack it tomorrow."

Sable didn't answer. Okafor watched him in the glow of the lights — watched him take his hand off the door the way you take your hand off a door you've been knocking on for ten years.

They went up.

* * *

The trouble with the strongroom, Dessa announced over dinner, was the lock.

She had the wheel assembly's twin — a spare from a sister ship, dredged out of a breaker's yard years ago — bolted to a board on the deck, and she'd been fighting it with picks and penetrating oil and increasingly creative profanity since sundown.

"It's not rust that's the problem. Rust I can beat. It's the mechanism." She sat back on her heels, grease to the elbows. "It's a delay lock. Time-delayed release, old insurance-company spec. Even with the right key — which we don't have — it wants six hours between unlocking and opening. Somebody didn't want this room opened in a hurry."

"Six hours," Pip said. He was mending net by lantern light, fingers quick, ears on everything. "So we start it now and it opens at—"

"We don't have the key, Pip. That's the whole— the point I'm making is the key." Dessa pushed her hair out of her face and left a smear of grease across her forehead. "I can drill it. I can drill anything. But I need eight hours of calm water and a steady platform, and I need to not have a syndicate gunboat sitting on top of us while I do it."

The word landed the way it had been landing all day, heavier each time.

Because the syndicate fleet had come.

Okafor had seen them at dusk — three shapes standing in past the outer buoys, lights coming up one by one, deliberate as a town at evening. Not closing. Not threatening. Just *arriving*, the way weather arrives. The largest of them anchored a half mile off their beam, close enough that Okafor could see figures moving on her deck, close enough that when the wind came off the water he could hear, faintly, a generator running.

"They're not going to do anything tonight," Sable said. He stood at the rail with his back to the lights, which Okafor thought was its own kind of statement. "If they wanted the site they'd have taken it while we were diving."

"Then what are they doing?"

"Waiting for us to do the work." Sable's voice was level, but Okafor had spent ten years listening to men lie with level voices. "We find it, we open it, we mark the safe lanes through the wreck — and then they come in and take it off us. That's the arithmetic. We're their survey crew."

"Then we stop surveying," Dessa said.

"We're two tides from the channel silting shut. We don't stop." Sable turned and looked at them, all of them, and Okafor saw the tiredness in him plain now, no longer disguised. "We work faster."

* * *

The third tide began at three in the morning, and it was the worst water Okafor had worked in five years.

The surge came through the breach in long pulses, shoving them against plating, dragging their lines, filling the corridor with silt every ninety seconds so that they worked in a rhythm of blindness — clear, work, whiteout, hold, clear again. Okafor ran the lift lines from above and felt each bag come up heavy and each bag come up wrong, tangled, fouled, and once — for one long, cold minute — not coming up at all, until Sable's voice on the comms said, "Cut it loose, it's a fitting, it's not worth you," and Okafor cut it loose and hated it.

By midmorning they had the corridor cleared to the strongroom door and a work platform rigged in front of it, and Dessa went down with the drill.

Okafor watched her on the monitor — the grainy feed from the helmet cam, her hands steady in the surge, the drill bit biting into the lock housing in a fine gray thread of metal and rust. She worked in the rhythm of the surge too, drilling on the calm pulse, shielding the bit on the whiteout, and her breathing on the comms never changed, never once, and Okafor thought: there is a kind of courage that looks exactly like stubbornness from the outside, and I am looking at it.

"Bit's through the first plate," she said. "Second plate's hardened. This is going to take the rest of the tide."

"Take it," Sable said.

They took it. The day ground down the way tides grind — no single moment, just accumulation. Pip hauled bags until his hands blistered and refused the gloves Dessa brought him because, he said, gloves cost him half his grip. Okafor ran the deck, coiling, rigging, hauling, watching the syndicate ships stand off in the haze with their patience like a held breath. Once, a launch came out from the largest vessel — came out, circled the *Maren* at a distance, and went back. Nobody aboard said a word about it. Everybody aboard thought about it.

By late afternoon the drill was through the second plate and into the mechanism, and Dessa's hands were shaking, and she wouldn't come up.

"Twenty more minutes," she said. "Twenty."

She got fifteen. The surge picked up with the turn of the tide, and the platform shifted, and the drill bit snapped off in the lock with a sound Okafor felt through the deck plates more than heard.

Dessa came up the ladder in silence, pulled her hood, and stood dripping on the deck looking at nothing.

"I can go back in," she said. "I can get another bit in there. Give me—"

"Tomorrow," Sable said. "It's held for ten years. It'll hold one more night."

"That's not the point, Sable. The point is the channel—"

"I know what the channel's doing." He said it quietly, and it stopped her, because they all knew what the channel was doing. The pilot's words had become the clock they all lived inside: six tides, and two of them spent, and the water in the narrows already running browner, thicker, the silt coming down off the river country with the spring tides like the land itself closing its hand.

* * *

Night came down and the syndicate fleet's lights burned across the water, and the crew of the *Maren* ate in the galley because the wind had come around and made the deck miserable.

It should have been a grim meal. Okafor was aware of that — aware of the arithmetic stacked against them, the fleet at anchor, the broken drill bit, the channel silting, the strongroom sealed. And yet.

Pip was telling a story about the preserved fruit, how they should market it, how there was probably a fortune in decade-old ship's peaches, and Dessa was telling him he was an idiot with her mouth while her eyes did the thing they did when she was amused and would rather die than admit it. Sable sat at the end of the bench eating without tasting, but he was *there*, he was in the room, and when Pip's story collapsed under Dessa's assault he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge that hadn't turned in years, but a laugh.

Okafor sat among them and felt the strangeness of it settle over him. He had spent most of his life on crews, and he knew the difference — knew it in his bones, the way he knew water — between a crew that was a payroll and a crew that was a thing. This was becoming a thing. It was becoming a thing out here, in sight of the guns, with the channel closing and the money probably gone and the truth at the bottom of a locked room, and there was no sensible explanation for it. They were not here for the fortune anymore; Okafor didn't think any of them believed in the fortune. They were here because Sable Sterling had finally stopped running, and it turned out that when he stopped, they all wanted to stand where he stood.

Strange peace, he thought. Peace with weather coming.

He went up on deck after midnight to check the lines and found Sable at the rail, looking at the syndicate lights.

"They moved," Sable said. "An hour ago. The small one shifted anchor — came in closer. They're not waiting for us to finish anymore."

Okafor followed his gaze. The nearest ship had indeed closed the distance, a dark mass now sitting less than a quarter mile off, her deck lights doused, her outline just barely readable against the water's last light.

"Slack water's at four," Okafor said. "Dessa can drill in slack water. She can have that lock open by mid-morning."

"Then we open it at mid-morning." Sable's eyes stayed on the dark ship. "Whatever they're planning, they'll do it before we're done. They won't be able to help themselves."

"You sound sure."

"I spent a decade being chased by men like that. You learn their tempo." Sable turned, and in the low light from the cabin Okafor saw his face clearly — the tiredness, and under the tiredness something harder and calmer, something that had not been there a week ago. "Get some sleep. Both tides tomorrow. All of it."

Okafor went below, but he slept badly, and near dawn he heard the drill deck being rigged in the dark, and Dessa's voice low and steady, and the first bite of the new bit into steel — and above it all, through the hull, faint and patient as weather, the sound of engines coming off anchor.
