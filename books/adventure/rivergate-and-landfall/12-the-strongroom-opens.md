## 12. The Strongroom Opens

The strongroom door fought them for six hours.

It was a slab of riveted steel set into the Landfall's aft hold, crusted with a decade of barnacle and rust, and it did not care that the tide was turning or that Okafor's hands were bleeding through his gloves or that somewhere beyond the hull, in the gray-green murk above the wreck, the syndicate fleet sat at anchor like patient carrion birds. The door cared about nothing. It simply held.

"Cutting charge," Dessa said for the third time, her voice flattened by the comm line into something toneless.

"No charge." Sable ran his glove along the seam again, feeling the give and refusal of it. "A charge cracks the frame, and if the frame cracks, whatever's inside floods before we can bag it. We do this slow."

"We do this slow," Pip repeated over the line, not because anyone had asked him to but because Pip had taken up the habit of echoing Sable's decisions back to him like a boy memorizing scripture. It should have irritated him. Instead it landed somewhere under his ribs and stayed there, warm and inconvenient.

They worked the hinges instead of the lock — Okafor's chisels, Dessa's hydraulic spreader jury-rigged off the winch hydraulics, Sable's own hands doing the fine work in the dark, reading metal the way his father had taught him to read seabed: pressure, grain, the story told in how a thing resisted. His father's voice lived in those lessons still. *Metal doesn't lie, boy. People lie. Metal just remembers.* He'd been eleven. They'd been working on the engine housing of a derelict trawler, and he could still smell the diesel and the salt on the old man's sleeves.

The hinge bolts sheared one by one, each with a sound like a knuckle cracking in the deep, and at slack water on the third tide the door came free into Okafor's waiting grip and swung outward with a groan of released pressure, spilling a bloom of silt that turned their floodlights into halos.

Sable went in first.

* * *

He had expected bullion.

Everyone had expected bullion. That was the whole architecture of the thing — Voss's contract, the syndicate's fleet, the forged permits in Dessa's drawer, the six-tide race they were all bleeding against. The Landfall had gone down carrying insured cargo worth more than most ports' annual take, and salvage men did not risk their lives for paper. Salvage men risked their lives for weight. For gold bars clinking in a mesh bag, for coin chests, for the dense bright promise that made a decade of ruin make sense.

The strongroom held no gold.

It held shelves. Steel shelving, bolted to the frame, and on the shelves — sealed. Watertight document cases, the kind survey offices used, the kind his father had used, stacked in careful rows with wax-sealed seams and lead tape over the closures, packed the way a man packs things he intends to survive. Dozens of them. Beside them, wrapped in oilcloth and wired shut, a single long case the size of a rifle scabbard.

Sable hung in the doorway of the strongroom for a long moment, his lights playing across the shelves, and felt the bottom drop out of every assumption he had carried down here.

"Dessa," he said. His voice came out strange, distant from itself. "Come look at this."

She came through the hatch beside him, bubbles streaming, and her light swept the shelves and stopped. Even through the mask he could see her eyes change.

"That's not cargo," she said.

"No."

"Cargo doesn't get packed in archival cases. Cargo doesn't get—" She paused, and he watched her arrive at the same place he had already fallen. "Somebody loaded this room deliberately. Somebody who cared more about these papers than about the ship."

*Somebody who knew the ship was going down.*

The thought arrived whole and cold, and Sable pushed it away the way you push away a hand reaching for you in the dark — fast, instinctive, before you have to see whose hand it is. There would be time. There had to be time.

They worked the rest of the tide hauling cases up the shot line, two at a time in the net sling, Okafor running the winch above with the tenderness of a man handling explosives. By the time the water began to pull toward the ebb, the deck of their patched-together boat held thirty-one sealed cases and one long oilcloth bundle, and the syndicate fleet's running lights burned on the horizon like a string of low orange stars that never moved and never blinked.

Nobody celebrated. A strongroom full of paper made the fortune smaller and the danger larger in the same breath, and everyone on the boat understood the arithmetic without saying it aloud.

* * *

Night came down soft and windless, the kind of night that felt borrowed. Dessa stood the first watch. Okafor slept like a man who had earned it honestly. Pip had been ordered below twice and had obeyed once, and now sat curled in the lee of the deckhouse pretending to sleep, eyes open, watching Sable across the deck.

Sable sat on the hatch coaming with the long oilcloth bundle across his knees.

He had been sitting there for an hour. The lantern was turned low. Around him the boat creaked and ticked as it cooled, and beyond the rail the sea breathed its slow indifferent breathing, and the syndicate ships hung out there in the dark with all their patience and all their guns, and none of it — not the fleet, not the money, not the ten years behind him — moved him as much as the wire wrapped around the oilcloth in front of him.

Because he knew the wrapping. He knew the double twist at the ends, clockwise then counter, the way a man secures wire so it can be undone one-handed in cold weather. He had watched that twist done a thousand times on a thousand jobs. He had done it himself, badly, until his father put the old man's scarred hands over his small ones and showed him again.

*Twice around, twice back. So it opens when your fingers are too cold to think.*

His father had packed this case. His father had wired it shut. Which meant his father had been aboard the Landfall before she sank, or had sent this ahead, or —

Or.

Sable cut the wire. He couldn't remember deciding to; his hands simply did it, the way they'd been trained to, twice around, twice back, and the oilcloth fell open along a seam worn soft by water and time.

Inside, nested in tarred canvas, lay a field journal.

Waterproof stock, bound in oiled leather, the corners rounded from years of pockets. A brass clasp, corroded green. And stamped into the cover in the blind-tooling his father had favored because ink washed off and impressions didn't: a compass rose, and beneath it, worn nearly smooth by a thumb that had worried it for twenty years — the initials **E.S.S.**

Elias Sterling Sterling. Elias Sable Sterling. The name Sable had spent ten years refusing to answer to.

He did not open it right away. That was the strange part, the part he would remember afterward — that he sat with the journal closed on his knees while the boat ticked and the watch lamp guttered and Pip's breathing went carefully, artificially even across the deck, and he let himself feel the full weight of the thing before he let it speak. Because he knew, the way you know before a doctor speaks, that whatever was inside would rearrange him. You don't open a door like that standing in the draft.

Then he opened it.

* * *

The handwriting hit him first, harder than any sentence could have. The tight forward slant, the numerals with the crossed sevens, the little hooks he'd mocked as a boy when the old man made him copy log entries for practice — *neat enough to read in a storm, boy, that's the only neatness that matters.* Ten years since he'd seen it and his body recognized it before his mind did, some animal part of him leaning toward the page like a plant toward light.

The first entry was dated eleven days before the Landfall sailed.

*Manifest discrepancy. Landfall listed as carrying 240 tons refined ore, insured through Meridian & Cross. Checked the loading drafts myself at the pier — she's drawing four feet light. Either 60 tons of ore evaporated between the weighbridge and the hold, or the manifest is fiction. Went into the hold at night. Ore is there — but it's slag. Painted slag, stacked to look right from the hatch. Someone has insured sixty tons of rock for the price of silver.*

Sable read it twice. Then a third time, moving his lips without knowing it, hearing the words in his father's voice, which was somehow worse than reading them.

He turned pages. The entries came fast after that, day upon day, the handwriting growing tighter, angrier, the margins filling with the notation style Dessa had found in the leaked Rivergate files — the arrows, the circled figures, the small precise annotations of a man building a case.

*Spoke to the second mate. He signed the manifest under instruction. Says the orders came from above the owner — says the word "syndicate" was used openly, which no honest man does. Paid him to keep a copy of his signing instructions. He's frightened. He should be.*

*Meridian & Cross has written policies on three other vessels in this fleet. If the Landfall's manifest is fiction, I will wager the others are too. This isn't one bad ship. This is a machine for manufacturing shipwrecks on paper and collecting on them in fact.*

*They know I've been asking questions. My berth was cancelled this morning and rebooked under another name. Flattered, almost, that I rate the attention.*

And then, dated the day before the Landfall sailed on her final voyage, the entry that stopped Sable's breath:

*I am going aboard. Not as salvor — as witness. If she goes down, I want to be the man who can say what was really in her hold, and prove it. I've copied the manifests, the drafts, the mate's instructions, the insurance schedules. Everything goes in the strongroom cases, sealed. If anything happens to me, the papers must surface. Whoever finds this: the wreck is not the treasure. The wreck is the evidence. Don't sell it. Finish it.*

*Finish it.*

Sable looked up from the page. The lantern flame stood perfectly still in the windless dark, and the sea made its sound against the hull, and he sat with the journal open in his lap and felt the vertigo come up through the deck plates like cold water rising in a flooded compartment.

His whole life had been built on a story. That was the truth of it, laid bare now in his father's tight slanted hand: the story of Elias Sterling, the salvor who chased the Landfall's treasure too greedily, who pushed his crew past safety, who took the Rivergate expedition down with him and left his son a name like a debt. Sable had carried that story for ten years. Had run from it, outrun it, buried himself in black-market digs and dawn departures rather than stand still anywhere long enough for the story to catch him. Every choice he had made since he was nineteen years old had been made inside the walls of that story.

And the story was false. Had been false before the Landfall ever cleared the harbor bar. His father hadn't been chasing treasure. He'd been carrying evidence — walking onto a doomed ship on purpose, packing the strongroom with proof, planning to testify, planning to burn the syndicate's fraud machine down from the witness stand. And the syndicate had known it. Had altered the tide logs, erased the diver's name, doctored the report, and let a dead man carry the blame for a murder dressed as misadventure.

They hadn't just sunk a ship. They had written his father's epitaph in advance and made the world read it aloud for ten years. Made *him* read it aloud, in every port, in every flinch when his surname came up.

Rage arrived where grief had been living, and it fit the space exactly, the way a replacement part fits a worn one — same shape, colder metal.

"Sable?"

Pip's voice, small in the dark. The boy had given up pretending to sleep and was sitting up, blanket around his shoulders, watching him with those wide unguarded eyes that had no business being on a job like this.

"You should be below," Sable said. His voice came out rougher than he intended.

"You've been reading for two hours. You went white an hour ago and whiter since." Pip hesitated, then, with the terrible directness of the young: "Is it bad?"

Sable looked down at the journal. At the last line he'd read, the instruction left for whoever found it — *don't sell it, finish it* — and understood suddenly that the question had two answers, and that both were true, and that for the first time in ten years he did not want to run from either of them.

"It's my father," he said. "It's everything my father was trying to tell us. And Pip — it means the people on those ships out there didn't kill him for treasure."

He closed the journal gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room.

"They killed him to keep him quiet. And tomorrow, when the fourth tide turns, we're going to show the whole coast exactly why."

Out on the dark water, a light moved on the syndicate flagship — a signal lamp, blinking short and short and long, patient as weather. Watching. Waiting to learn what they'd pulled from the strongroom.

Dessa's head appeared at the companionway, drawn by the voices, her eyes going from Sable's face to the journal in his hands. She didn't ask. She just nodded once, slowly, like a woman recalculating everything.

"Their fleet just signaled," she said quietly. "They know we got it open."
