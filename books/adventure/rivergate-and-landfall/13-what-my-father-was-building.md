## 13. What My Father Was Building

Sable read until the lamp oil ran low.

He had meant to stop after the first pages — meant to close the journal, seal it back in its case, and let the morning bring whatever morning brought. That had been the plan, the same kind of plan he'd made for ten years: a small one, a survivable one, a plan shaped exactly like an exit. But his father's handwriting did something to him that no amount of discipline could counteract. It pulled. The way a current pulls at your fins when you're trying to hold position over a wreck, patient and indifferent, until you realize you've drifted forty meters from where you started and the only honest thing left to do is follow it.

So he followed.

The boat was quiet around him. Dessa had the engine compartment torn open again, chasing a rattle she swore was nothing and worked on like it was everything. Okafor slept in shifts now, always half-dressed, boots by the hatch. Pip was locked in the fo'c'sle by decree after the surge incident, though Sable could hear him not-sleeping up there too — the particular restlessness of a sixteen-year-old who had nearly drowned two days ago and hadn't yet decided how to feel about it.

And Voss sat in the main cabin with her own light burning, her own papers spread out, saying nothing. She had said nothing all evening. Sable understood now that her silence wasn't indifference. It was vigil. She was keeping watch over him while he read, the way you keep watch over a grave, or a fire.

The journal's middle section changed character somewhere around the fourth dive of the Rivergate expedition. His father's early entries were what Sable expected — depths, visibility, bottom composition, sketches of hull plating rendered in that precise draftsman's hand Sable had spent a decade trying not to imitate. But on the page dated the ninth day, the neat columns gave way to something else. A list. Cargo manifest line items, copied out in full, each one annotated in the margin with a single word or phrase.

*Listed: machine parts, 40 crates. Found: none.*

*Listed: copper ingots, sealed hold 3. Found: hold empty, hatches dogged from inside.*

*Listed: bullion, 12 cases, insured twice over. Found: —*

The dash after the last line went on for most of the width of the page, pressed so hard the nib had nearly torn through the paper.

Sable set the journal down. Picked it up again. Set it down.

*Insured twice over.*

He knew the shape of this. He'd known it since Dessa laid the doctored Rivergate files across the chart table and he'd seen his father's notation style bleeding through the official report like a bruise through skin. He'd known it in the way you know a storm is coming before you can name the cloud that's carrying it. But knowing the shape of a thing and standing inside it were different weathers entirely.

His father hadn't been salvaging the *Landfall*.

He'd been auditing it.

* * *

The next entry was longer, written in a hand that had lost some of its patience:

*"Spoke with Meridian's adjuster today. Pleasant man. Too pleasant. Asked me three times whether I'd found the bullion cases, and each time he asked, he watched my hands instead of my face. I have been diving long enough to know what a man looks like when he is hoping I find nothing.*

"The manifest says twelve cases of specie went down with the Landfall. The insurers paid out on twelve cases. If those cases are aboard, someone collected on cargo that never existed, or cargo that was removed before sinking. If they are not aboard, then either they were offloaded before departure — which makes the sinking itself the crime — or they went down elsewhere, which makes the manifest the crime.*

"Either way, somebody needed this wreck to stay unexamined. Somebody has needed it unexamined for ten years. And somebody paid good money to make sure the first survey team came home with nothing but tide logs and a dead diver."*

A dead diver. Sable read the sentence four times. His father had written about himself in the third person there — no, wait. Not himself. Sable flipped back, checked dates, cross-referenced against the crew roster Dessa had reconstructed from the leaked files. The dead diver was Anselm Reyes, lost on day eleven, body never recovered. Official cause: entanglement in the superstructure during a slack-water descent. His father's margin note beside the official finding, copied into the journal:

*"Anselm did not entangle. Anselm dove with a cutting tool on every descent for twenty years. Someone moved the wreck sections, or someone moved Anselm. I am done pretending these are accidents."*

Sable's hands were shaking. He noticed it the way you notice cold — distantly at first, then all at once, everywhere.

He got up and stood at the aft rail for a while, looking at the black water, breathing. The syndicate fleet lay anchored two miles north, their running lights strung out like a string of patient orange beads. Waiting. They had been waiting for ten years; they could wait two more tides.

When his hands steadied, he went back inside and kept reading.

* * *

The journal stopped being a dive log somewhere around day fourteen. It became something else — a brief, a case file, a weapon being assembled one page at a time. His father wrote faster now, the letters leaning forward like a man walking into wind.

*"I have copies. Four sets. Manifest originals from the harbormaster's office in Corvane — the real ones, pre-departure, showing the Landfall loaded with ballast and dunnage, not bullion. Photographs of the holds as we found them. Tide tables annotated in my own hand, which will contradict the 'official' logs by six hours in every instance that matters. And the insurance schedule itself, which I obtained through a friend at Lloyd's who owes me more than he wants to admit: the Landfall was underwritten twice, by two houses, on the same cargo, within nine days of sailing. Nobody insures phantom bullion twice unless they intend to collect twice.*

"Voss's people — the backers behind this new expedition, the ones calling themselves the Meridian Recovery Consortium — are the same names. Same signatures. Some of them have changed their letterhead. None of them have changed their habits.*

"I have retained counsel in the capital. When we surface with documentation, I intend to testify. Full account. Names, dates, ledgers. Let them spend their fortune buying back every copy."*

*Sable —*

The name arrived without warning, mid-page, and hit him like a depth charge.

*"If you ever read this, then I failed to come home, and you have spent however many years believing what they told you about me. So let me say it plainly, in ink, where ink cannot be edited: I did not cut corners on that survey. I did not falsify the logs. I did not lose a ship and three reputations because I was careless or greedy or drunk on ambition, whatever story they sold you. I found something they needed buried, and I refused to bury it.*

"You'll be angry with me for that. You have my father's temper; I recognize it in you even when you try to hide it behind your mother's patience. Be angry. Anger is fuel if you aim it. Just don't let them turn it inward. That's the trick they play — they make the honest man's family carry the shame so the dishonest men can carry the money.*

"I'm sorry for what this costs you. I'd do it anyway. That's not stubbornness. That's arithmetic: one reputation against a fraud large enough to buy governments. There's only one side of that sum worth dying on, and I intend very much not to die on it. Come find me when I'm wrong about that.*

"— E.S.S."*

Sable closed the journal.

He didn't remember deciding to stand. He was standing, the lamp guttering, the little cabin swaying around him, and the rage had arrived — not gradually, not building like weather, but all at once, whole, the way grief had arrived ten years ago at a dockside office where a man with a syndicate lapel pin had handed him a folder and a look of practiced sympathy and told him the inquiry had found negligence.

Negligence.

His father had been building a prosecution. Page by page, dive by dive, photograph by photograph — a careful, methodical, damning case, assembled with the same precision he'd used to teach eight-year-old Sable to tie a bowline, the same patience he'd used to sketch wrecks in the margins of Sable's schoolbooks. And they had killed him for it — or arranged the water that killed him, which amounted to the same thing — and then they had taken the wreckage of his work and bent it into a confession, and they had let a boy carry the weight of it for ten years while they collected on their double-insured ghost cargo and bought their letterheads and their harbormasters and their pleasant adjusters who watched your hands instead of your face.

Ten years of leaving towns before dawn. Ten years of forged permits and cash jobs and sleeping in engine rooms. Ten years of becoming exactly the kind of man they could point at and say *see, the son is no better than the father* — and all of it built on a lie they had manufactured, deliberately, with paperwork.

They hadn't just ruined his father. They had drafted him. Made him the continuing evidence of his father's guilt, year after year, town after town, every dawn departure a signature on their document.

Sable became aware, slowly, that Voss was standing in the cabin doorway.

She had her original dive log hugged against her chest — he'd seen it once, wrapped in oilcloth, and she hadn't let it out of her sight since. Her face in the lamplight was older than it had been in the bar in Corvane, older than it had been on any of the days since. She looked at him the way you look at someone standing at the edge of something.

"You finished the middle section," she said. Not a question.

"He was going to testify." Sable's voice came out lower than he intended, scraped raw. "He had counsel. He had copies. Four sets."

"Yes."

"And they—" He stopped. Started again. "You were there. Day eleven. You were on the support boat when Reyes went down."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — a door opening onto a room she'd kept locked for a decade. "Sable—"

"They didn't just doctor the tide logs afterward." He heard his own voice flatten out, go cold and precise, his father's drafting-hand voice. "The logs were altered *before*. Weren't they. The expedition was given corrupted charts from the start — we found that ourselves, out there, the wrong coordinates. Which means whoever planned the sabotage planned it from the beginning. Which means the sinking of the Landfall wasn't an accident they exploited. It was the operation. The whole expedition was the operation — his expedition, the cover story, the fall guy, all of it designed before the first line ever went into the water."

Voss said nothing. Her silence was answer enough, and they both knew it, and the knowledge filled the little cabin like floodwater.

"My father figured it out," Sable said. "By day nine he'd figured it out. And they let him keep diving, keep documenting, keep assembling his case — because a man who thinks he's building a prosecution dives straight into your hands. Every copy he made, every photograph, every annotation — they just needed him at the bottom of the channel when the time came. And then they needed a story. And the story they needed was *negligent diver loses ship, inquiry confirms, case closed.*" He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "They didn't even have to write it themselves. The inquiry wrote it for them. Respectable men, official paper, a grieving family that couldn't afford to fight it."

"Sable." Voss stepped into the cabin. Up close, he could see she was trembling too — had been trembling, maybe, for ten years. "There's something I have to tell you. Something I should have told you in Corvane, the first night. Something I was paid to do, and have regretted every single day since, and if you hear it and put me off this boat, I will deserve it—"

"Not tonight." He held up a hand, and was surprised to find it steady now. Steadier than it had been all evening. The rage had burned through the shaking and left something harder underneath, something with edges. "Tomorrow. First light. Because whatever it is, I'm going to need it — every document, every memory, every name you're carrying. My father built four-fifths of a case, and they buried it with him. We're going to finish it."

"We?" Voss said quietly.

Sable looked down at the journal in his hands. His father's last words to him were inked inside it, ten years old and still wet: *come find me when I'm wrong about that.*

He hadn't been wrong. About any of it.

"We," Sable said. "Get some sleep, Marguerite. Tomorrow we start building the other fifth."

Outside, far to the north, the syndicate fleet's lights burned on, patient as ever. They thought they were waiting out a salvage crew.

They had no idea they were waiting out a witness.
