## 14. Voss Breaks

Marguerite Voss had spent ten years learning to sleep in rooms that weren't hers, and she had gotten good at it. Good at the small hotels with salt-stiff sheets, good at the berths of chartered boats, good at falling asleep mid-thought the way soldiers and fugitives do — because somewhere in the last decade she had become both. But tonight, anchored in the lee of the outer shelf with the syndicate's fleet a smudge of lights on the southern horizon, she lay awake in her cabin aboard the *Corvus* and listened to the boat breathe around her, and sleep would not come near her.

She knew why. She had known since Sable Sterling had come up the ladder from the strongroom two nights ago with his father's journal wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like something rescued from a fire.

She heard his boots on the deck above. Not pacing — pacing was what she did. This was deliberate, weighted movement, a man walking toward a decision. The footsteps stopped outside her door.

For a long moment there was nothing. Then three knocks, spaced evenly, the way you'd knock on a door you had every right to open but were choosing not to.

"Voss." His voice came through the wood, low and stripped of everything he usually wore over it. "I need to talk to you."

She sat up in the dark. Her heart was doing something old and stupid, hammering like a girl's. *This is it,* she thought, and found that some traitorous part of her was relieved.

"Come in," she said. "It's your boat's fuel I'm burning, after all."

The door opened. He filled the frame with the lamp behind him, and for a second he was just a silhouette, and she thought: *that's how he'll remember me, if this goes badly. A shape in a doorway.* Then he stepped inside and shut the door, and the lamplight from the passageway caught his face, and she saw that he wasn't angry. That was worse. Anger she could have fought. What she saw instead was the expression of a man who had read something and needed to hear it said aloud by someone who'd been there.

He didn't sit. He stood with his back to the door, which told her he'd thought about whether to leave himself an exit and decided against it — or decided she deserved to feel cornered. Both, probably. He was his father's son down to the bone.

"You were on Rivergate," he said.

"Yes."

"You were on the *Landfall* the week before she went down. You dove the site. You logged the tides." He recited it flatly, without inflection, the way Dessa read data. "Your name is on the crew manifest. Third diver, survey team."

"I was twenty-four," she said. "Youngest diver they'd take."

"And then your name disappears from the report."

"It does."

"The tide logs are altered. Dessa found the originals in the leaked files — the harbor authority's raw readings versus what went into the final report. Someone moved the slack water by ninety minutes. Someone made it look like my father chose to dive a window that didn't exist." Sable's voice stayed level, but she could hear the effort of it, the way you can hear a hull working in heavy seas. "Someone who understood tides well enough to falsify them convincingly. Someone on the survey team."

The cabin was very quiet. Through the hull she could hear the anchor chain ticking against the hawsepipe, a small patient sound, like a clock.

"Sable—"

"Don't." He held up one hand, not sharp, just final. "I've spent ten years building a picture of that expedition out of grief and rumor, and I'm not going to let either of us make this easier than it is. I read the journal. I know what the syndicate did to the manifest. I know my father was going to testify and that somebody made sure he never got the chance. What I don't know — what I have been standing in this room trying not to say — is where *you* were when it happened. Because you've had ten years to tell me, Marguerite. Ten years of dinners and rivalries and that business off Cayo Blanco where you outbid me by four thousand dollars out of pure spite, and not once — not once — did you say *I was there*."

"I couldn't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only true one I have," she said, and swung her legs off the bunk, and sat facing him with her hands loose between her knees, an old woman's posture though she was barely forty. "But you're right. It isn't enough. So I'll give you the rest, and you can do whatever you want with it afterward. Throw me off the boat. Turn me over to the investigators with the rest of them. I've earned all of it."

His jaw tightened. "Start at the beginning."

* * *

"The beginning," she said, "is that I was broke and ambitious, and those are the two conditions the syndicate looks for. You know how they recruit. They don't threaten people first. They *help* them. They pay off a debt, fund a survey, put your name on a paper you're proud of. And then one day the paper comes due, and you discover that what you signed wasn't a contract, it was a leash."

She looked at her hands. Diver's hands, scarred at the knuckles, the nails cut short. Hands that had once held a slate and written numbers that killed a man.

"I'd done two seasons with the consortium before Rivergate. Survey work, mostly legitimate. My mother was sick — the kind of sick that eats money faster than any salvage can earn it — and the syndicate paid her bills. I told myself it was a salary. I told myself a lot of things. And then the Landfall job came through, and Aldous Renner — you know the name?"

Sable's face went still. "Renner Maritime. They hold the insurance claim on the wreck."

"They held more than that. Renner ran the whole expedition's financing, and Renner answered to the syndicate, and the syndicate wanted the *Landfall* found and then lost. Do you understand what I'm saying? They never intended for her cargo to be recovered. The ship was carrying documentation — bills of lading, insurance certificates, correspondence — that proved a decade of fraud. Overvalued cargoes, phantom losses, ships scuttled for the payout. The *Landfall* was the archive nobody was supposed to open. Renner's plan was elegant: fund an expedition to locate the wreck, establish the coordinates officially, get the site surveyed and then legally sealed as a hazard. Bury the evidence under maritime law instead of silt."

"And my father found out."

"Your father," Marguerite said, "was the only person on that expedition who read everything. Everyone else treated the manifests as background noise. Elias Sterling sat in the chart room every night cross-referencing cargo declarations against port records, and by the third week he'd found discrepancies that made no sense unless the paperwork was fraudulent from the start. He came to me — I want you to know that. He came to me because I was young and he thought I was honest, and he asked me, very quietly, whether I'd noticed anything odd about the tide schedules we'd been issued."

Her throat closed. She waited, breathed past it the way she'd learned to do at forty meters, and went on.

"I lied to him. I told him the schedules came straight from the harbor authority. They didn't. They came from Renner's office, already adjusted, and my job — the job I'd been paid a retainer I never questioned — was to transcribe them into the official survey log in my own hand. My handwriting, Sable. My certification number. When the inquiry came, the tide logs bore the signature of the most junior person on the team, and everyone senior walked away clean except your father, who took the blame for diving a window that had been moved ninety minutes specifically so that it would kill him."

Sable hadn't moved. He stood against the door with his arms folded, and his stillness was terrible, and Marguerite understood suddenly that this was what he must have looked like as a boy — a child holding absolutely still so that no one would see him break.

"He asked me twice," she whispered. "He asked me if the logs were true, and I said yes, and eleven days later he was dead, and I signed the final survey report with the same hand."

The anchor chain ticked. Somewhere above them, Pip's footsteps crossed the deck, light and quick, and were gone.

"Why?" Sable said at last, and his voice cracked on the single syllable, and he hated it, she could see him hate it. "Why tell me now? Why fund this salvage? You could have died knowing this. Most people would have."

"Because I tried that." She laughed, and there was nothing in it. "I tried being silent. I got rich. I got famous. I beat you to three wrecks and put my name on two books, and every night I closed my eyes and saw Elias Sterling in the chart room asking me a question with his whole face open, trusting me, and me lying to him. You want to know what hatred is? Hatred is looking at yourself in the mirror for ten years and recognizing, finally, that the person looking back is the one who did it. Not Renner. Not the syndicate. Me. I moved those numbers. Nobody held a gun to my head. They held my mother's hospital bills, and I let that be enough."

"So the salvage. The fortune you offered us."

"A lever," she said. "The only one I had. The syndicate owns my debts — all of them, still, after everything; that's how these things work, you never finish paying — and they ordered me to lead a competing salvage and fail slowly. Instead I hired the one man alive with a reason to dig past the lies. I knew you'd refuse. I counted on you refusing, and then I counted on your crew overruling you, because I've watched you work, Sable, and I knew the truth about you even if you didn't: you don't run from the *Landfall*. You run toward it. You've been running toward it for ten years in a circle."

He unfolded his arms slowly. "You used my father's death to hire me."

"I used the only thing I had left that was worth anything," she said. "And I'd do it again. That's the part you should hate me for — not the logs. The logs were cowardice. This was calculation. I looked at your grief and I spent it."

The silence stretched so long she heard the diesel generator cycle down in the engine room, and the boat went quieter around them, as if it too were listening.

Then Sable pushed off the door and crossed the small cabin and sat down across from her, knees almost touching hers, and looked at her the way his father must once have looked at her in the chart room — open-faced, waiting for the truth.

"The journal needs corroboration," he said. "Dessa says the manifest fraud is provable from the documents alone, but the tide alteration — that's the hinge. That's what turned an accident into a murder charge that landed on my father. If the original tide readings exist anywhere, unaltered—"

"They exist." Her heart was slamming now, and she let it. "They exist because I'm a coward in one way and a professional in another. The night before the final report was filed, I made a copy of my actual dive log. Raw entries. Real times, real depths, real tide states — including the ninety-minute discrepancy, noted in my own hand, dated, initialed. I kept it for ten years in a waterproof case, moved from safe to safe, because I couldn't destroy it and I couldn't use it. Destroying it would have made me certain of what I already suspected I was. Using it would have meant testifying, and the syndicate owned my mother's care, and then, after she died, they simply owned *me*."

"Where is it?"

"Where no syndicate auditor would ever think to look," she said, "because it's been in plain sight the entire time. In the frame. Behind the photograph in my cabin — the one of the Rivergate team on the deck of the survey vessel. The one I've kept on every boat I've owned for ten years, including this one, including the boat you're sitting on. I look at it every day, Sable. Every single day. That was my penance — keeping the proof of my crime where I had to see it."

He stared at her. "You brought it on the expedition."

"I brought it the moment I decided to hire you. Which was the moment I decided I was done being the woman in the photograph."

* * *

They went to her cabin together, and she took the framed photograph down from the bulkhead with both hands, the way you lift something that has been screwed into place by grief. The picture showed eleven people squinting into tropical sun on the aft deck of the survey vessel, ten years younger, one of them dead, several of them liars. Sable's father stood at the center, laughing at something outside the frame, and Marguerite watched Sable look at him and saw the boy surface in the man's face for one unguarded second before he mastered it.

She worked the backing free with a pocketknife. Behind the cardboard, flat against the frame's plywood, was a slim case of grey waterproof polymer, sealed with wax, unopened long enough that the wax had yellowed.

She held it out to him.

"This is my original dive log from Rivergate. Times, depths, tide states, and a dated entry noting that the tide schedule issued to the survey team did not match the harbor authority's published readings — with the discrepancy quantified. There's also a memo Renner's office sent me, which I was stupid enough to keep, instructing me to 'reconcile' my log with the official schedule before filing. Reconcile." Her mouth twisted. "Their word for falsify."

Sable took the case the way Okafor handled recovered artifacts — two hands, no sudden movements, as though it might dissolve. He weighed it. He looked at her.

"You understand what this does," he said. "Once Dessa scans it and it goes into the broadcast, you're finished. The syndicate will bury you. The investigators will charge you. Falsifying expedition records — you'll lose your license, your boats, probably your freedom. Everything."

"I lost everything ten years ago," Marguerite said. "I've just been renting the wreckage since. Take it."

He didn't move for a moment. Then he said, quietly: "My father trusted you. That's the thing I can't get past. He looked at you and saw someone worth telling, and you were the syndicate's instrument, and he died for it. I have spent ten years hating the sea for taking him and the world for blaming him, and it turns out the person I should have been angry at has been buying my dinners."

"Yes."

"And I'm not going to forgive you tonight. Maybe not ever. Forgiveness isn't mine to give anyway — it was his."

"I know."

"But." He exhaled, long and slow, and she watched him decide something, watch the decision move through him like weather. "My father trusted you once, and he was the best judge of people I've ever known. Maybe he saw something in you that took ten years to arrive. So here's what happens now: you give Dessa that log tonight, you sit for a recorded statement, and when we transmit, your name goes in the broadcast with the rest — confession included, all of it, unedited. No deal-cutting, no softening. You stand in the light with the rest of them."

"That's all I've wanted," she said, "for ten years. To stand somewhere true."

He nodded once and turned to go, and at the door he stopped, his hand on the latch, and spoke without turning around.

"He asked you if the logs were true," Sable said. "And you said yes. Tonight, when Dessa records your statement — say it again. Say *no*. Loud enough that he hears it wherever he is."

The door closed behind him.

Marguerite Voss stood alone in her cabin with the empty photograph frame in her hands, and behind her the grey case was already gone, already Dessa's, already becoming testimony. On the bulkhead, the picture hung lighter than it had in a decade, eleven young faces squinting into the sun, and she looked at her own among them — twenty-four and already bought — and felt the strangest sensation, unfamiliar and terrifying and clean.

It took her a moment to recognize it, because she had not felt it since she was a girl.

It was the feeling of having chosen a side.

Above her, through the deck plates, she heard the boat come alive — Dessa's voice calling for Okafor, the scanner warming, Pip's quick feet crossing to the radio shack. And beneath it all, far to the south, dark against the last of the dusk, the running lights of the syndicate fleet had begun to move.
