## 15. Fourth Tide: The Fleet Moves

Dessa felt the wrongness before she could name it. That was the thing about engines — they taught you to trust the part of your mind that noticed a rhythm break before the ear could explain why. She'd been below since before dawn, re-torquing the pump mounts for the fourth time in three days, and somewhere in the middle of the third bolt her hands had gone still on their own.

The generator note was fine. The compressor was fine. It wasn't the boat at all.

It was the water. Slack tide should have come at 06:40 by her calculations, and slack meant silence — that dead, glassy half hour when the current forgot which way it was going and every buoy out past the shelf stood perfectly upright. But through the hull, against her palm flat on the engine room plating, she felt a long, low shudder that had nothing to do with anything aboard.

She climbed the ladder two rungs at a time and came up into light the color of skimmed milk. Fog sat on the channel like a lid. And through it, from the northeast, came the sound she'd been dreading since Voss first said the word *fleet* in that dockside bar: diesel engines, plural, running heavy and unhurried, the way ships ran when they knew exactly where they were going and didn't care who heard them coming.

"Sable." Her voice came out flatter than she wanted. She tried again. "Sable! Deck, now!"

He was already there. Of course he was — the man slept like a cat with one ear open, ten years of leaving having burned the habit of vigilance so deep into him that he surfaced at the first change in the air. He stood at the rail in his shirtsleeves, hair wild, binoculars up, and Dessa watched his shoulders do something she'd never seen them do in all their years together.

They dropped.

"Three of them," he said. "Maybe four behind the fog line. Running lights off."

Okafor came up from the dive platform with his suit half peeled, black rubber hanging from his waist like a shed skin. Pip scrambled out of the wheelhouse, and Dessa saw the kid's face go through hope and confusion and land somewhere cold. "Is it the syndicate?" Pip asked. "This early? Tide's not even turned—"

"That's the point," Sable said quietly. "Nobody raids on slack water. Nobody *can* work a wreck on slack water. They're not here to salvage."

Dessa did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. Slack water meant no current to fight, no vis churned to milk — but it also meant the *Landfall* site was at its most fragile, silt banks balanced like stacked plates, and it meant their own divers were between dives, gear staged, tanks half-charged. If you wanted to take a site without working it, if you wanted to drive people off it and strip it while they watched from too far away to stop you —

"They're here to take everything," she said. "And they knew exactly when to come."

The words hung there, and then, because she was who she was — because she'd spent twenty years around machines that never lied to her and had learned to hate the creatures that did — she said the rest of it.

"Somebody told them our schedule."

* * *

The accusation didn't have a shape yet. That was the worst of it. She watched it move through the five of them like smoke, settling nowhere, touching everyone.

There were only so many people who'd known the plan. Sable. Herself. Okafor. Pip. Voss, who kept to her own chartered tender anchored two hundred meters off, close enough to consult, far enough to be deniable. And Teo.

Teodor Brandt had come aboard at Rivergate town six days ago, a big quiet man with rope-scarred hands and a reference letter from a harbor master Dessa hadn't bothered to verify because the season was short and good deckhands were scarcer than honest money. He hauled lines without being asked. He'd fixed the winch brake that had been grinding for a week. Pip liked him. That was the part that made Dessa's stomach turn now — she replayed every easy laugh, every time Teo had stood beside her in the engine room asking patient questions about the compressor, and she couldn't find the seam. There was no seam. That was what professionals looked like.

"He was on watch alone last night," Okafor said. He'd dressed properly now, and he stood with his arms folded, and his voice had the careful evenness of a man holding something down. "Radio log shows a transmission burst at 02:10. Short. Encrypted or just compressed — I can't tell. I assumed it was weather traffic."

"You assumed," Dessa said, and hated how it sounded, and said it anyway.

"I assume things about this crew every day," Okafor answered, not blinking. "That's what trust is. You spend it and hope nobody robs you."

Sable hadn't moved from the rail. Through the thinning fog the lead ship had resolved into a gray slab of a vessel, converted trawler, crane arm folded along its deck like a sleeping insect. Behind it, two more shapes. On its bridge, someone raised a hand — not a wave. A signal.

"Sable," Dessa said. "What do we do?"

For a moment he didn't answer, and she understood that he wasn't deciding; he was grieving. She'd seen him grieve like this once before, years ago, over a boat — over the last boat, the one he'd sold rather than keep, the way he sold everything rather than keep it. He was doing the math on how much of himself this job had cost already and how much more it was about to ask.

"We get the journal off this boat," he said finally. "Everything else is replaceable."

* * *

It went wrong the way things go wrong at sea: fast, sideways, and with a logic you could only reconstruct afterward.

The syndicate lead ship closed to four hundred meters and put out a launch — eight men, rifles slung across backs, moving with the bored competence of people doing a job they'd done before. Sable ordered everyone to hold position, no provocation, and Dessa took the wheel while he went below to the strongroom cache they'd cracked on the second tide: the oilcloth-wrapped field journal, the sealed document tubes, the falsified manifest pages, all of it packed into a single waterproof case no bigger than a sea chest.

Which was when Dessa saw Teo move.

Not toward the launch. Toward the stern. Toward the dive platform where the case would come up, where the transfer to Voss's tender was supposed to happen if it came to that. He moved like a man doing deck work, unhurried, coiling a line as he went, and if Dessa hadn't spent six days memorizing his rhythms she might have missed that he was coiling the same line twice.

"Teo," she called down. "Off the platform."

He looked up at her and smiled, and it was the same smile he'd been giving them for six days, warm and unremarkable, and that was somehow the most frightening thing she'd ever seen on a human face. "Just securing the gear, miss. Rough water coming."

"There's no rough water coming."

"Not yet," he agreed pleasantly, and stepped onto the platform ladder, and the launch hit them.

Not alongside — Dessa gave the syndicate helmsman that much credit — but under the counter, a fender-bump that was really a grapple, and then boots on the hull, and then the world became noise. Okafor met the first man at the rail and went down with him in a tangle of limbs and rifle straps. Sable came up out of the hatch with the case strapped across his back like a bomb he intended to deliver personally, and two syndicate men converged on him, and Dessa lost sight of everything except the wheel in her hands and the gray wall of the trawler swinging to block their escape lane south.

"Pip!" she shouted. "Get below! Stay below!"

Pip did not stay below. Pip was sixteen and had been raised, as far as Dessa could tell, entirely by salt water and stubbornness, and Pip did the thing that Dessa would spend the rest of her life trying to decide whether to be furious about or grateful for.

The case strap broke.

Later, everyone would tell the story differently. Sable swore a grapple hook caught it. Okafor said a rifle butt. What Dessa saw, from the wheelhouse, was simpler: Sable spun by a shoulder strike, the strap tearing, the case skidding across the wet deck, sliding for the scuppers, for the open black water where it would sink into forty fathoms and take a dead man's testimony down with it.

Teo was closest. Teo reached it first. And Teo — big, reliable, rope-scarred Teo — picked it up and threw it overboard.

No hesitation. No performance. Just an arm like a capstan and the case arcing out over the rail, and Dessa screaming a sound she didn't know she could make, and then Pip.

Pip went over the rail after it.

No suit. No tank. A knife in one boot and a lifetime of diving the cold shallows off Rivergate town in the body, and the kid cleared the rail in a flat clean arc that would have been beautiful in any other context, and hit the water where the case had gone in.

The syndicate men froze. Even they hadn't expected that. In the half second of their surprise, Sable moved — not toward the case, not toward the water, but into the gap, putting his body between the rail and the men, arms spread, buying seconds the way he'd bought everything his whole life: with himself as the price.

Dessa threw the wheel hard over and brought the boat's stern around, engines screaming, and grabbed the throw ring, and leaned out over the churning wake, and waited for a head that did not come up.

One second. Three. Five. The water was nine degrees and the current was turning and Pip had gone down after a sinking case in the shadow of a wreck they'd barely mapped.

Then a hand broke the surface, gripping the throw ring's line, and Dessa hauled, and Okafor appeared beside her without her seeing him arrive, and together they pulled Pip over the gunwale like a landed fish — small, blue-lipped, coughing seawater, and clutching the oilcloth-wrapped journal to his chest with both arms like it was the only warm thing left in the world.

"The case sank," Pip gasped. "Strap — I cut the strap — got the book —"

"You absolute idiot," Dessa said, and her voice broke in the middle of it, and she didn't care. "You stupid, brave, impossible child—"

"Got the book," Pip said again, and grinned through chattering teeth, and passed out.

* * *

They found Teo in the engine room.

Not hiding. Sitting on the deck plates beside the compressor with his hands open on his knees, waiting, while above them the syndicate launch withdrew and the gray trawler held its station four hundred meters off like a verdict deferred. When Dessa came down the ladder with Okafor behind her and a wrench in her hand that she was honest enough with herself to admit she'd picked up for reasons beyond utility, Teo looked up without fear.

"The documents are insured," he said. "The fleet will recover the case from the seabed within the week. Nothing personal, miss. It's a wage. I've got a daughter in Corvane with lungs that don't work right, and the syndicate pays for the doctors, and you pay in promises."

"So does everyone," Dessa said. "Everyone always has a reason."

"Yes," Teo agreed. "That's what makes it work."

Okafor took the radio log printout from his pocket and laid it on the deck plates between them, the 02:10 transmission highlighted in his own careful pencil. "How long?"

"Since before I came aboard. They picked my berth for me." Teo's eyes went to the ladder, to the sounds above — Sable's voice, low and ragged, calling for hot water and blankets for Pip. "The girl saved the journal, you know. That wasn't in anyone's plan. They'll adjust."

"They'll come again," Dessa said.

"At slack water tomorrow," Teo said simply. "Or they won't need to. Check your fuel lines before you run anywhere. That's free advice, and it's the last I'll give you." He put his wrists out, almost courteously. "Well. Tie me up or throw me over. I'd prefer the tie."

Above them, faintly, Dessa heard Pip's voice — weak, indignant, alive — insisting that they hadn't lost the manifest pages, that the important ones were copied, that Sable shouldn't look at her like that. And beneath the relief, beneath the fury still burning in her chest like an untended fire, Dessa felt the other thing settle into place, cold and clear as a bearing line:

Someone on this boat had been theirs for six days, and they had never once checked the seams. Whatever came next — the fifth tide, the fleet, the choice Sable was going to have to make — it would begin with locking the engine room door from the inside.

She picked up the wrench after all. Not for Teo.

For the padlock hasp she was about to weld shut.
