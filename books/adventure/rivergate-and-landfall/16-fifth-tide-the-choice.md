## 16. Fifth Tide: The Choice

They came at slack water, when the sea went flat as poured pewter and the boat sat motionless on her own reflection.

Sable saw the launch first — a black-hulled tender running without lights out of the syndicate fleet's anchorage, cutting a long white seam across the glass-smooth water. It came straight for them, no hesitation, no pretense of passing by. Behind it, five miles off, the fleet sat at anchor in the last light like a row of pulled teeth: three salvage vessels and a supply ship, all of them patient, all of them waiting, all of them close enough now that he could read their names through Dessa's binoculars if he'd wanted to.

He didn't want to. Names were how they made themselves seem legitimate.

"Company," Okafor said from the rail, unnecessarily. Everyone on deck had already seen it.

"Everyone below except Dessa," Sable said. "Pip stays below. That's not a request."

Pip opened his mouth — Pip always opened his mouth — and caught the look on Sable's face and closed it again. He went down the companionway with his bandaged arm cradled against his chest, and Sable watched him go and felt the old familiar tightening behind his ribs that had nothing to do with the syndicate and everything to do with how quickly he had learned, over the past two days, to count heads.

The launch swung alongside with practiced ease. Fenders went out before Sable could object, which told him something: they knew his boat. They knew her freeboard, her rub strake, the quirks of her hull. Someone had studied the survey records. Someone had studied *him*.

Three men came up the ladder. Only one mattered — a broad, silver-templed man in a linen jacket too clean for this coast, moving with the unhurried confidence of someone who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered. The other two were furniture. Big furniture.

"Mr. Sterling." The man smiled like a handshake. "Aldous Crane. I represent certain interests involved in the Rivergate matter."

"There is no Rivergate matter," Sable said. "There's a wreck, and you're trying to steal it."

"And there it is." Crane sighed, genuinely regretful, or good enough at pretending that the difference stopped mattering. "The famous Sterling bluntness. Your father had it too, you know. I met him once. A remarkable man. Stubborn in exactly this way."

Something moved through Sable's chest, cold and slow, like water finding a crack in a hull. Ten years he had built his life around never hearing that name spoken casually, and here was a man in a linen jacket saying it like they were two widowers sharing memories at a funeral.

"You have two minutes," Sable said.

"I'll need more than that, but we can start there." Crane glanced around the deck — at the patched rail, the dive gear drying on lines, the scarred deck plates — with an appraiser's eye. "May I?"

"No."

Crane smiled again and didn't sit anyway. "Then I'll stand. Mr. Sterling, let me be direct, because I suspect directness is the only currency you accept. You have in your possession documents recovered from the *Landfall*. We know this because our people observed your recovery operations, and because we've known what was in that strongroom since before your father ever found it." He paused, letting that land. "Yes. Since before. Hold onto that thought; it will matter shortly. What we are prepared to offer is simple. Walk away. Give us the cache, take your crew, and receive in exchange a sum that will make Voss's contract look like pocket change — and, more valuably, a full public rehabilitation of Elias Sterling's name. Officially. Certified. The Rivergate verdict overturned, the blame reassigned where it belongs, your father remembered as the honest man he was."

The words went into Sable like a blade going between ribs — clean, precise, aimed at the exact place he kept the thing he never talked about.

*His father's name.* Said aloud. Offered back to him like a ransom note.

"That's not yours to give," Sable said quietly.

"Everything is ours to give, Mr. Sterling. That's rather the point of the last thirty years." Crane folded his hands. "Here is what you must understand about your position. If you broadcast those documents — and I assume that's the plan, it's always the plan with idealists — then yes, some of it sticks. An inquiry. Headlines, perhaps, for a season. But inquiries are slow, records are lost, witnesses retire, and my principals have survived four governments and two wars. What your father learned, we will survive learning again. And you—" he gestured gently at the boat, the crew, the whole small stubborn enterprise of them "—you will have spent the fortune, burned the bridges, and gotten a paragraph in the maritime gazette. Whereas if you deal with us, your father is cleared *now*. Cleanly. With money enough that no Sterling ever has to dig in the mud for another man's secrets again."

* * *

Dessa came up the ladder while Crane was still talking, and Sable let her, because he wanted a witness and because she would hear every word of it later anyway. She stood by the wheel with her arms crossed and her face carefully blank, which was Dessa's way of shouting.

"Your engineer looks skeptical," Crane observed.

"My engineer has good instincts."

"Then let me address her skepticism directly." Crane turned to include her, generous as a host. "You believe we're villains. Perhaps we are — the word depends on whose ledger you read. But consider what we're offering that even your maritime authorities cannot: certainty. Justice, Mr. Sterling, is a process. It takes years, and processes can be appealed, delayed, settled, forgotten. What we offer is *immediate*. Your father died with his name in the mud. I am authorized to lift it out today. All it costs you—"

"The truth," Sable said.

Crane tilted his head, pleased. "You say that like it's a loss."

"It is the only thing he left me."

For a moment — just a moment — something flickered behind Crane's pleasant expression. Not guilt. Sable doubted the man owned any. Something more like recognition: the look of a man who has made this pitch a hundred times and knows, from experience, exactly which words break people, and is briefly annoyed to find the wrong word coming out of his own mouth.

"Sentiment," Crane said softly. "Very well. Let me put it without sentiment." He produced a slim folio from inside his jacket and set it on the deck box between them, weighted against the breeze. "Inside: the transfer instrument for the payment, and a draft statement clearing Elias Sterling, already signed by two officials who owe us more than they owe their oaths. You sign, we transmit, and by morning your name opens doors instead of closing them. Or refuse, and understand what refusal means. Our fleet takes this site at the next tide. Your permits are forged — yes, we know; we checked the moment Voss filed her survey declaration — and we will see you charged, fined, and barred from these waters for life. Your crew's livelihoods go with you. And the documents, Mr. Sterling? We will get the documents regardless. The only variable under your control is whether your father's name gets cleaned in the process."

He let the silence stretch. Slack water held the boat so still that Sable could hear the tick of the cooling engine below, the creak of the fender lines, Pip's footsteps overhead — no, not overhead; below, pacing, unable to help himself, listening through the deck.

"Take the night," Crane said. "Not longer. The tide won't wait for grief." He nodded to Dessa, nodded to Sable, and went down the ladder with his furniture following, and the black launch drew away across the pewter water toward the fleet, leaving its wake to widen and flatten and disappear as though none of it had happened.

Except that the folio stayed on the deck box, white and patient in the dying light.

* * *

They didn't open it for an hour. Sable sat in the stern with it unopened beside him, watching the fleet's anchor lights come on one by one as dusk dropped, and the crew gathered around him the way they always did — Okafor leaning on the rail, Dessa at the chart table, Voss apart from all of them near the bow, arms wrapped around herself, watching him with an expression he couldn't read and wasn't sure he wanted to.

Finally Dessa said, "Well?"

Sable opened the folio.

The transfer instrument was real. He could tell that much even without Dessa's expertise — the account codes, the escrow structure, the kind of paper that moved fortunes without ever being photographed. And beneath it, the draft statement. *In re: the expedition vessel Rivergate, loss dated—* He stopped reading after the first line, because his father's name was in it, spelled correctly, printed in official type, followed by the word *exonerated*, and Sable had to set the pages down on his knee and look at the water for a while.

Ten years. Ten years of dockside bars and black-market digs and leaving before dawn, ten years of a name he'd shortened and a surname he'd almost stopped using, and here it all was, undone, in two pages, for the price of a locked strongroom's worth of truth.

"It's real," Dessa said quietly, reading over his shoulder. "The escrow's funded. I can see the structure. They'd pay."

"They'd pay," Sable agreed.

"So what's the catch?" Okafor asked. "Because there's always a catch, and usually it's got teeth."

"The catch," Voss said from the bow, without turning around, "is that the statement clears Sterling by naming *someone else* guilty. Read the third paragraph."

Sable read the third paragraph. His mouth went dry.

*...responsibility for the falsified manifest and subsequent loss resting with the late diver M. Voss, whose alterations to the tidal records—*

"They clear my father," Sable said slowly, "by feeding Marguerite to the inquiry. Alive. She signs a confession, takes the blame for the record tampering, does whatever time or ruin comes with it, and the syndicate's part in it never surfaces at all. The fraud stays buried. My father walks out of the mud clean, and everyone else stays under it."

"Now you understand the shape of the offer," Voss said. She still hadn't turned around. Her voice was level in the way that cost something; Sable had heard that levelness before, in men holding pressure at depth. "It's a good offer, Sable. Take it. God knows I'd sleep better knowing I'd finally paid for it properly."

"No," Sable said.

"Sable—"

"*No.*" He stood, and the folio slid off his knee onto the deck, and he didn't pick it up. "You don't get to volunteer for a lie on my behalf. None of us do. That's the whole point of what my father died doing — he wouldn't bury the truth even when burying it would have saved him. Even when burying it would have saved *me*." He heard his own voice crack on the last word and let it. "I spent ten years thinking he abandoned me for a wreck. He didn't abandon me. He was building something, and they killed him for it, and if I trade the journal for a signed piece of paper, then they win twice. Once in '09 and once tonight."

The deck was quiet. Somewhere below, Pip's pacing had stopped; he was pressed against the companionway hatch, Sable realized, listening to every word.

Dessa picked up the folio, squared its edges, and set it on the chart table. "For the record," she said, "I want it noted that this is the single worst financial decision of my career, and I've worked with you for six years, which is saying something."

"Noted," Sable said.

"For the other record." She looked at him, and her face, usually so guarded, was open in a way that startled him. "My mother spent nineteen years waiting for an inquiry to clear my uncle's name off a similar list. It never came. Processes get lost. Witnesses retire. He said that, the linen bastard, and he's right. Which means the only version of justice that ever arrives on time is the kind you make yourself." She tapped the journal's waterproof case, sitting on the chart table beside the folio like two opposing counsel. "So we don't sell it. We don't trade it. We finish it."

Okafor grunted — his entire vocabulary of agreement — and said, "Fleet moves at next tide. That's maybe fourteen hours. Channel silts at the sixth. We're going to be squeezed between them."

"Then we'd better not waste the squeeze," Sable said.

* * *

He took first watch alone, because he needed it, and because there was a page of the journal he hadn't shown anyone yet — the last written entry, the one he'd read eleven times since the strongroom opened and still couldn't read calmly.

*If they come before I surface, the duplicate is where I told Marguerite it would be. Tell my boy the work mattered more than the name. Tell him the name was only ever borrowed.*

Tell my boy. As if his father had known, in the last hours, that someday a man in a linen jacket would climb aboard a boat and offer the name back at a price. As if Elias Sterling had seen the whole shape of it from inside the flooded dark of the *Landfall* and left instructions anyway.

Sable sat in the stern with the page in his hands and let himself, for exactly as long as one cigarette lasted, imagine taking the deal. Not for the money — the money meant nothing, less than nothing — but for the morning-after of it. Waking up with his father cleared. Walking into a port office and giving his full name and watching the clerk's face do nothing at all, no flicker of recognition, no pity. Ten years of flinching, ended by signature. It would be so *clean*. That was the horror of it, he thought. Not that the offer was corrupt, but that it was kind. Cruelty he could have refused in his sleep. Kindness with a hook in it took all night.

But the kindness was bought with Marguerite Voss's confession, extracted and shaped, and sealed around a fraud that would go on sinking ships and ruining divers for another thirty years. His father's name laundered through his father's murderers. There was no version of that morning he could live inside.

He flipped the cigarette into the water and watched the spark die.

Below him, the boat breathed — the tick of metal, the slap of slack water against the hull, Dessa's radio murmuring weather bands she was monitoring with increasing frequency. He'd noticed that. Squall activity building northwest, she'd said at dinner, saying it lightly, the way she said things that weren't light at all.

At midnight the wind arrived ahead of itself — a long low breath out of the northwest that set the halyards chiming and turned the fleet's anchor lights into smears. Sable stood up. Out past the buoys, the sea had begun to move again, the fifth tide gathering itself in the dark, and somewhere in that dark the syndicate's launches were doing the same arithmetic he was: hours left, channel closing, everything unresolved.

He went below to wake the crew. Whatever the sixth tide brought, they would meet it moving.

Above the chart table, pinned under the lamp, the white folio waited with its two signatures and its purchased mercy, and nobody looked at it again — but nobody threw it overboard either, and Sable understood why. Dessa kept it as evidence. He kept it as a reminder.

Some offers you refuse once. The dangerous ones, you have to keep refusing all night.
