## 17. Storm Glass

Okafor felt the storm before he saw it — a pressure behind his back teeth, the particular hush that fell over water when it was deciding something. He had grown up on a lagoon where the old men read weather in the behavior of fish, and thirty years later he still trusted that instinct more than any barometer Dessa had bolted to the wheelhouse bulkhead.

The barometer agreed with him anyway. It had been falling all morning, slow and steady, like something sinking.

He stood at the rail of the *Magpie's Debt* and watched the horizon south of the channel markers. The sky out there had gone a color he didn't like — not the bruised purple of an honest squall but a flat, greenish pewter, the light bending wrong. The syndicate fleet sat at anchor two miles off, four hulls in a loose line, patient as moorings. They hadn't moved since dawn. They didn't need to. Everyone on the water knew what everyone else knew: the tide window was closing, and whoever wanted the Landfall's secrets would have to take them soon or never.

"Okafor." Pip's voice from the deck below, pitched high with the effort of sounding casual. "Dessa wants you. The glass."

"I see the glass."

"She says you won't believe the glass."

He went down. The wheelhouse smelled of coffee gone bitter on the burner and the iodine tang of Pip's bandaged forearm — the boy had taken a gash during the raid at slack water, stitched by Dessa with sailmaker's thread and a steadiness that had frightened Okafor more than the wound itself. Pip sat on the chart table now, legs swinging, pretending the arm didn't hurt. Sable stood at the window with his back to the room, watching the fleet, and Okafor had learned in the last five days to read the set of those shoulders the way he read weather. This morning they were wrong too.

Dessa had the barometer log spread out beside the tide tables, her finger on a column of numbers that descended like stairs.

"It's not just falling," she said. "Look at the rate. Three millibars an hour for the last six hours. That's not a passing cell. That's a line."

"How far out?"

"Squall signature on the coastal radar feed before we lost the relay — it was sitting off Cape Verrell at midnight. If it holds its bearing, it makes the channel mouth by—" she checked her watch, did the arithmetic against the tide tables, and Okafor watched her face do something he almost never saw it do, which was flinch. "By fourteen hundred. Maybe earlier if it's moving fast."

Okafor looked at the tide tables himself, though he already knew them by heart. Six tides, the harbor pilot had said, and then the channel silted shut until the spring series. They were deep into the fifth now. The sixth tide — the last one, the only one left — began its flood at 1530 and gave them perhaps four hours of workable water before the ebb started dragging the river's sediment load back across the bar like a curtain being drawn.

"And the storm closes it early," Okafor said.

"The storm closes it early," Dessa confirmed. "A squall line like that piles a surge ahead of it. Surge means current through the channel means the silt starts moving hours before the ebb even turns. We could lose the window at noon. We could lose it at eleven."

Silence in the wheelhouse except for the burner hissing. Out the window, the syndicate fleet's anchor lights burned pale in the flat light, four small suns that refused to rise.

Sable turned from the window at last. Okafor had seen this man refuse Voss's contract without blinking, dive a corrupted wreck site alone, hold a drowning boy's head above surge water with his own arms shaking — and through all of it he had worn the same expression, a kind of armored neutrality, a man keeping his face closed because he'd spent ten years practicing. But now, looking at the tide tables, something moved behind the armor. Not fear exactly. Arithmetic. A man counting what he had left.

"So that's it," Sable said. "One tide. Half a tide, maybe. And the strongroom's still half-flooded, and the cache isn't recovered, and there are four armed hulls sitting between us and open water."

"There's also a storm coming," Pip said, and there was something in the boy's voice Okafor couldn't place at first — and then he could. Hope. "Storms ground aircraft. Storms foul radios. Storms make big ships want to be somewhere else."

"Storms also drown divers," Okafor said gently.

Pip's chin came up. "I know what storms do."

"You do," Okafor said, and meant it. The boy had earned that much and more.

* * *

They gathered on the aft deck because Sable asked for it — asked, which was itself new; a week ago he'd have simply announced. The *Magpie's Debt* rolled on the long swell coming in ahead of the weather, and Okafor leaned against the rail with his arms folded and watched the four of them arrange themselves into the shape of a decision. Dessa with her tablet under her arm like a sidearm. Pip perched on the winch housing, bandaged arm cradled, eyes on Sable with that fierce borrowed certainty he wore like a hand-me-down coat. Marguerite Voss standing slightly apart, as she always did, a woman who had not yet decided whether she belonged on this boat or in its brig.

And Sable in the middle, holding his father's field journal against his chest with one hand, the way another man might hold a child.

"You've all seen the numbers," Sable said. "So I'll skip to the part that matters. The sixth tide is our last. The storm is going to cut it short — maybe to three hours, maybe less. The strongroom cache isn't fully recovered. There are documents still down there, sealed cases, enough to finish what the journal started. And the syndicate knows it. The moment that squall passes, if we haven't broadcast, they'll have every reason and every opportunity to come take what's left and bury it deeper than the channel ever could."

"So we run," Dessa said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation. She said it the way an engineer states a load rating. "We take what we have — the journal, Voss's log, the manifest pages — and we run for port now, ahead of the weather, and we broadcast from inside the harbor where their jamming can't reach and their guns can't follow. It's the smart play. It might even be the right play."

"But?" Sable said.

"But the cases in the strongroom are the difference between a story and a verdict." Dessa's jaw worked. "The journal proves fraud. The sealed cases are the original manifests, the insurance filings, the correspondence. With just the journal, they call it a forgery, a grieving son's fantasy, and ten years from now they're salvaging that wreck in peace. With everything, they go to prison." She looked down at her tablet. "I've been running this problem since dawn. Every solution ends with us staying past the point where staying is safe."

Okafor heard it then, underneath her words — the thing she wasn't saying, the thing none of them were saying. That safety had stopped being the measure sometime around the fourth tide, and nobody had marked the moment it happened.

"They'll offer you money again," Voss said quietly. "Before the last tide. They'll offer you everything, Sterling. They offered me enough, once, to buy a life. I took it. I'm telling you the price of taking it, because I'm still paying."

"I know what they'll offer," Sable said.

"Do you?" Voss's voice cracked open, just a hair. "Because what they offer isn't money. What they offer is your father's name, cleaned up, quiet, official — in exchange for the truth staying buried. They will let you walk away believing you've won. That's the trap. That's always been the trap."

The swell lifted the boat and set it down. Somewhere below, the hull ticked and groaned, the old sounds of a patched vessel doing its best, and Okafor found his throat tight with an affection for the ugly little ship that surprised him.

"My father dove that strongroom knowing the syndicate would kill him for what he carried out of it," Sable said. "Or they killed him before he could. Either way — he didn't finish. Ten years, and the job's been sitting down there in the dark, waiting for somebody to pick it up." He looked at each of them in turn, and Okafor saw him doing it deliberately, the way a man forces himself to look at a wound. "I'm not going to order anyone. I'm asking. Last chance to say run. Nobody thinks less of—"

"No," Okafor said.

Sable stopped. "Okafor—"

"You were about to give us permission to leave you," Okafor said. "I heard it in the shape of the sentence. So let me save us the time." He pushed off the rail and stood, feeling his knees announce fifty years of diving and deck work, feeling none of it matter. "I have been on boats my whole life. Good boats and bad boats and boats that sank. And I will tell you what I know, which is this: a crew is not people who share a wage. A crew is people who have decided, each one alone, in the dark, that some things matter more than getting home dry. I decided a long time ago that I'd found my boat. I just didn't know it was this one."

Pip was grinning. Okafor pointed at him before he could speak. "You don't get to vote. You're sixteen and stitched together with sail twine."

"I'm seventeen in March," Pip said, "and I saved the journal, and I'm the fastest swimmer on this boat, and if you leave me here I'll steal the dinghy."

"He will," Dessa said. "I've seen him practice."

"Pip," Sable started, and the boy cut him off, which no one aboard had dared in a week.

"You went in after me." Pip's voice had lost its bravado entirely; what was underneath was rawer and truer. "Surge running, zero visibility, and you came in after me without even stopping to be angry first. You can't take that back by pushing me ashore now. I'm voting stay. I'm voting finish it. I'm voting—" he faltered, found it again, "—I'm voting whatever you vote, but I want it on record that I voted first."

Dessa closed her tablet. "For the record, I ran the numbers and the numbers say run. For the other record — the one that counts — I've spent six years fixing engines for men who paid me and never once looked at what we were carrying. I'd like to carry something that matters. Stay."

All of them turned to Voss.

She stood apart at the rail, hands white-knuckled on the metal, and for a long moment she said nothing at all. Okafor watched her look at Sable — really look, the way you look at a mirror when you've avoided mirrors for a decade.

"On Rivergate," she said finally, "there was a moment. After. When they pulled us out and debriefed us and told us what the report would say. I could have spoken. One sentence, that's all it would have taken, one sentence about the tide logs, and I calculated instead. I was twenty-four and scared and they made the math very easy." Her voice was steady in the way of something held together with effort. "I have done that math every day for ten years. I am done doing it. Stay. Finish it. Whatever it costs."

The wind arrived then — not the storm itself but its outrider, a long cool breath out of the south that smelled of rain and distance, and the flag halyard began its soft irregular chiming against the mast. Out beyond the buoys, the syndicate fleet's lights still burned, but Okafor noticed, now, movement on the nearest deck. Cranes being checked. Boats being uncovered. The weather was coming for all of them, and the syndicate had decided, as he had, that the last tide was the last chance.

Sable looked at the four of them for a long time. Okafor watched something happen in the man's face — the armor not coming off, exactly, but opening, a visor raised, letting himself be seen deciding.

"My father used to say a crew votes with its feet," Sable said. "That nobody stays on a boat by obligation. They stay because the boat is going somewhere worth going." His hand tightened on the journal. "Ten years I've made sure every boat I was on was going nowhere. You four just outvoted me on that. So." He straightened, and Okafor heard the change in his voice, the running man finally turning to face the country behind him. "We stay. We dive the sixth tide, whatever's left of it. We pull every case out of that strongroom, and we broadcast before the silt takes the channel — storm or no storm, fleet or no fleet. Dessa, I need the generator shielded and every radio we've got hot. Okafor, rig the lift bags for heavy loads in surge conditions. Pip—"

"I'm on the surface line," Pip said. "Don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue," Sable said, and something passed across his face that on any other man Okafor would have called a smile. "Voss — you know these waters better than any of us. I want you on the pilotage. When the channel starts to choke, I want us out ahead of it, not in it."

Voss nodded once, and Okafor saw her shoulders drop a fraction, as if a weight she'd carried ten years had shifted to somewhere she could bear it.

They scattered to their work, and the boat came alive around Okafor — the clatter of gear, Dessa's voice rising from the engine room in cheerful profanity, Pip coiling line with his good arm and humming. Okafor stood a moment longer at the rail, looking south, where the squall line had become visible at last: a low dark wall on the horizon, rain veiling beneath it like curtains, coming on with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Behind him, Sable joined him at the rail, journal still in hand.

"Your father," Okafor said. "Would he have stayed?"

Sable considered it honestly, which Okafor respected. "He'd have already been in the water," he said. "But he'd have made sure everyone aboard got a vote first. I think—" he paused, and Okafor let the silence hold, "—I think this is the first time I've understood him. Standing still."

The first drops came spattering across the deck, fat and warm, harbingers. To the south the wall of rain swallowed the horizon whole, and above the rising wind Okafor heard, faint and clear, the sound of the syndicate fleet weighing anchor.

The sixth tide was coming. So was everything else.
