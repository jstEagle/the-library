## 18. Sixth Tide: The Last Dive

The water over the Landfall had turned the color of old pewter.

Sable stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Debt* and watched the sixth tide begin its work — the great slow exhale of the channel, dragging the sea off the flats and pulling silt up from the bottom like a curtain being drawn across a stage. By nightfall the passage would be a sandbar with delusions of navigation. By morning it would be nothing at all.

Above him, the sky was doing what Okafor had promised it would do. The squall line had come down from the northwest overnight, not the full storm yet but its outriders — low gray cloud moving fast, wind with teeth in it, the kind of weather that made even the syndicate fleet restless. He could see them from here: four hulls anchored in a crescent two miles out, lights burning through the murk, patient as creditors. They'd stopped pretending to be salvage ships an hour ago. Their launches were in the water. Their cranes were rigged for lifting things that weren't cargo.

"They'll be on us inside forty minutes," Dessa said beside him. She had the radio handset in one hand and her other hand closed around it so tightly the knuckles had gone white. "Maybe thirty. The harbor authority is 'reviewing the situation,' which means they're waiting to see who wins."

"Then I'd better be quick."

"Sable." She turned to face him fully, and he saw that she'd done the arithmetic everyone on this boat had been doing since dawn, and she hadn't liked the answer any better than he had. "The strongroom's at fifty-one meters. You went down yesterday and you came back up shaking so hard Pip had to help you out of your rig. The flooded compartment alone—"

"I know the numbers, Dess."

"The numbers don't care that you know them." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated that, he could tell; she dragged a breath through her nose and rebuilt herself out of pure stubbornness. "Okafor says the current on the descent will run two knots against you until you clear the mast step. Pip has your backup bottle staged at the breach. Voss is going to hold the shot line at fifteen meters whether anyone tells her to or not. And I—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm going to be on this radio every second you're under, and if you miss a check-in by ninety seconds, I am sending Okafor down after you, and if the syndicate wants to argue about it they can take it up with his crowbar."

Sable looked at her — this woman who had signed onto a black-market dig for money and stayed for reasons none of them ever said out loud — and felt something in his chest that wasn't fear and wasn't grief and took him a moment to name.

It was being known.

"You'll get your check-ins," he said.

"Damn right I will."

* * *

They suited him up on the aft deck because it was sheltered from the worst of the wind, and because Pip had already laid everything out there in the exact order Sable used, learned from watching, and neither of them mentioned it. The boy worked the buckles of his harness with quick, fierce hands, checking each one twice, three times, as if thoroughness could be converted into safety through sheer application.

"I could come partway," Pip said. "To the breach. Just to the breach."

"No."

"I did okay last time. After. With the journal."

"You did more than okay." Sable caught the boy's wrists and held them still, and made himself meet those eyes — sixteen years old and already carrying more salt water in his lungs than any kid should. "Which is exactly why you're staying up. I can't think about anything else down there except what my father left me. If I have to think about you too, I'll make a mistake. Do you understand? Staying on this deck is the job. It's the hardest job on the boat today, and I'm giving it to you because you're the only one I trust to hate it properly."

Pip's jaw worked. His eyes were bright and furious and terrified, and he blinked all of it back down into whatever place teenagers keep such things. "Ninety seconds," he said finally. "Dessa said ninety. I'm counting."

"Count fast. It makes the time go quicker."

Okafor helped him over the gunwale — the big man's hands steady on the tank rig, his face unreadable in the way it got when feeling ran too deep for his vocabulary. "The river takes what the river takes," he said quietly. It was the closest thing to a prayer Sable had heard him offer. "But it does not take what we carry back up. Remember which one you are."

Then the water closed over Sable's head, and the world became what it always became: pressure and cold and the long green silence, and his own breathing, loud as a confession.

* * *

He'd dived wrecks for ten years. He knew their grammar — the way a hull spoke in rust and collapse, the way silt lied, the way a shipwreck was never really a thing but a process, a slow argument between iron and ocean that the ocean was always winning. He'd told himself once, in another life, that he dove because the work was good and the pay was cash and nobody in the drowned dark asked about your family.

He'd been lying. He'd known he was lying for most of a decade. Every wreck he'd slipped into had been a rehearsal for this one, and every year of running had been the approach swim, long and cold and inevitable, toward the stern of the *Landfall*.

He descended along the shot line into water that thickened around him like ink poured into ink. At twenty meters the light gave out entirely and his torch beam carved a tunnel through the gloom, and there she was — the *Landfall*, lying on her port side in the seabed's cold embrace, her starboard plates furred with a decade of growth, her superstructure collapsed into itself like a fist slowly unclenching. He'd been inside her twice now. He knew the way: over the fallen crane arm, through the tear in the hull where the syndicate's own sabotage had opened her like a tin can, down the corridor that had been a corridor once and was now a throat of silt and shadow.

But today he paused at the breach, hanging in the black water with his bubbles streaming upward like a handful of escaping thoughts, and let himself look at her properly.

His father had died here.

Not in the abstract way people died in reports and courtrooms and other people's careful sentences. Here. In this water. In this dark. Aldous Sterling, fifty-one years old, a diver good enough to teach his son everything the son would spend ten years pretending he hadn't learned, had swum into this same breach with evidence in a waterproof case and the syndicate's fraud in his head and — Sable understood it now, with terrible clarity — no intention of coming back out. Because coming back out meant testifying, and testifying meant names, and names meant his crew, his employers, his son, all of them exposed to men who had already sunk one ship to bury a lie.

The journal had said it in his father's flat, precise hand, the notation style Sable had recognized in the Rivergate margins like a voice from a locked room: *If I do not surface, the cache speaks for me. Tell Sable the tide tables were wrong on purpose. Tell him I was proud of him every day he was gone.*

He hadn't read that line until two nights ago. He still hadn't told anyone he'd read it. Some cargoes you carried alone until you found the person worth sharing the weight with, and he was beginning to suspect — with the same clarity, the same terrible gift his father had apparently possessed — that the persons were waiting for him on the deck of the *Kestrel's Debt*, counting seconds.

Sable finned through the breach.

* * *

The corridor to the strongroom was worse than yesterday. The storm swell above was transmitting itself down through the water column in long, muscular pulses, and each pulse moved the wreck — not much, a centimeter, two, but enough that sediment breathed off the surfaces in slow brown ghosts, enough that somewhere forward of him metal groaned like a sleeper turning over. His torch beam jittered across bulkheads furred white with bacterial mat, across a doorframe bent inward, across the number stenciled on a plate that his father must have passed a hundred times: 7-C.

Fifty-one meters. Bottom time measured in minutes now, not hours. The strongroom hatch stood open where they'd cut it, and beyond it the flooded compartment waited exactly as he'd left it — shelves bolted to the walls, the document cases lashed down in his father's neat webbing, the whole room arranged with the methodical care of a man building a testimony out of paper because paper was the only witness the syndicate couldn't drown.

Except they'd tried to drown it anyway. That was the thing Sable kept circling back to, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. They hadn't just sunk the *Landfall*. They'd falsified the manifest before she ever loaded, altered the tide logs after, erased a diver's name from the record, and let a dead man carry the blame for all of it into history. Ten years of reputation-ruin and rumor and a son running from a shadow that had been painted on the wall by the very people pointing at it.

He worked fast. The cases came free of their lashings one by one — manifests, correspondence, photographs, ledgers in a hand he didn't recognize and one ledger in a hand he did. He packed them into the recovery bag with hands that wanted to shake and didn't, because his father had taught him that too: *fear is fine underwater, boy, panic is not, and the difference is whether your hands remember their training.* The bag went heavy against his chest, twenty kilos of proof, a decade of someone's lies rendered suddenly, wonderfully fragile.

And then, wedged behind the last shelf, in a space too small to be deliberate and too deliberate to be accident, he found the second journal.

Smaller than the first. Thinner. A field notebook, the cheap kind surveyors carried, its plastic cover scarred and its pages swollen with water that had gotten in anyway, over years, despite everything. The handwriting on the cover had faded almost to nothing.

*S. Sterling — personal.*

His own initial. His own name.

Sable hung in the flooded strongroom of the *Landfall*, fifty-one meters down in the last hour of the last tide, and did not move for a long time. His regulator hissed. His gauges glowed. Somewhere far above him a syndicate launch was crossing gray water toward his boat, and somewhere closer than that his crew was counting seconds, and none of it — not the fleet, not the fortune, not the fortune's opposite — reached him at all.

He put the notebook in his suit's inner pocket, against his chest, where a man carries the things he cannot afford to lose and cannot bear to lose differently.

Then he checked his air, turned for the exit, and began to climb out of his father's last minutes.

* * *

The ascent tried to kill him three times.

The first time was the current. It had turned while he was below — the tide's final exhalation meeting the squall's first push, and the collision of the two had filled the water column with a churning lateral drag that grabbed the recovery bag and swung him hard against the wreck's flank. He caught a stanchion, held, felt the metal flex under his grip with a groan he felt through his gloves and into his teeth. For five full seconds he simply hung there, pinned, the wreck alive beneath him in some vast slow way, and understood that the *Landfall* was finishing her argument with the ocean tonight, and that he was inside her when she did.

The second time was the silt. As he rose past the collapsed superstructure, the storm-swell pulses shook the sediment loose in curtains, and his world shrank to a meter of visibility, then half that, then nothing but brown and the glow of his own torch reflected back at him. He navigated by feel and memory — the mast step on his left, the crane arm overhead, count the fins, trust the compass, trust the training, trust the man who taught you. *Fear is fine. Panic is not.* His air gauge read a third. Then less.

The third time was at fifteen meters, where the shot line should have been, and wasn't — or rather, where it was, but moving, whipping in the surge, and tangled with it, holding it steady with both fists and her whole body braced against the pull, was Marguerite Voss.

She'd gone in without being asked. Of course she had. She'd spent ten years paying a debt in silence and had apparently decided that silence was finished. Her mask caught his torchlight and her eyes behind it were wide and ferocious, and she pointed up, and pointed at his gauges, and pointed up again, and together, her hand locked around his harness strap, they rose through the dying light of the sixth tide.

The surface hit him like a slap — rain, wind, gray chaos, the *Kestrel's Debt* heaving at her anchor with her running lights smeared sideways in the rain. Hands pulled him over the gunwale. Pip's hands, Okafor's hands, Dessa shouting his check-in interval into his ear as though volume could restart his heart. Someone stripped his mask. Someone else was laughing or crying or both.

Sable knelt on the deck, coughing salt water, the recovery bag still strapped to his chest, and looked up at his crew through the rain.

"Get it below," he rasped. "All of it. Dry it, photograph it, copy it. Everything."

Dessa hauled the bag open and stared at the documents inside, and then at him. "It's enough?"

"It's more than enough." He pulled the small wet notebook from inside his suit and held it a moment before handing it to her too — carefully, the way you hand someone a live coal. "There's a broadcast set on this boat. There's a press frequency list in the chart table, and maritime authority channels, and every port office from here to Cape Meridian. We're not selling this, Dess. We're not trading it, not hiding it, not spending it."

The syndicate's lead launch was visible now through the rain, a quarter mile out and closing, its searchlight swinging toward them like a slow accusation. Forty minutes, Dessa had said. Maybe thirty. Behind it, the fleet was weighing anchor.

Sable got to his feet on the pitching deck of the boat he had nearly lost, in the rain, in the wind, with his father's name in his pocket and his father's pride in his chest, and smiled — the first real one in ten years.

"Start transmitting."
