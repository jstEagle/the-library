## 19. The Broadcast

Dessa had built the transmitter out of spite.

Not entirely — there was engineering in it too, and she was honest enough with herself to admit that the engineering was the part she loved. The boat's long-range set had been a salt-corroded ruin when they'd bought her, three seasons back, and Dessa had rebuilt it piece by piece in the cramped radio room behind the wheelhouse: new capacitors scavenged from a drowned buoy tender, a hand-wound coil because nobody on this coast sold her the parts she needed without asking questions, an antenna run she'd rerouted twice to keep it clear of the mast step where lightning liked to find them. She knew every solder joint on that rig the way other people knew their own scars.

But the last hour of work — the part where she'd pulled the casing off the frequency limiter and bypassed the port authority's registration handshake so the signal would go out raw, unregistered, untraceable for as long as it took — that part was spite.

She sat back on the stool and looked at what she'd made, and wiped her hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth, and let herself feel it for exactly four seconds before Sable's voice came through the bulkhead.

"Dessa. How long?"

"Ten minutes to alignment. Fifteen if you want me to be careful."

"I don't."

"Yeah," she said. "Figured that."

* * *

The wheelhouse smelled like coffee gone bitter on the burner and like the iodine Pip kept refusing to let anyone change on his bandaged forearm. He was sitting on the chart table with his legs swinging, holding the journal's waterproof case against his chest with his good arm like it might be taken from him, which, Dessa thought, was not an unreasonable fear given the last forty-eight hours. Okafor stood at the forward windows with binoculars, watching the syndicate fleet sit on the horizon like a row of black teeth. Four hulls now. They'd been joined overnight by a fifth, a fast patrol boat flying no colors at all, which told Dessa everything she needed to know about how nervous the backers were getting.

"They're moving boats between ships," Okafor said. "Transfer activity. Could be nothing. Could be boarding parties staging."

"It's not nothing," Sable said. He was at the helm even though the boat wasn't going anywhere, hands resting on the wheel like touching it helped him think. "They've stopped pretending to wait us out. Voss says the jamming gear went active on their lead ship an hour ago — she can see the arrays from here."

"So we go first."

"So we go first." Sable turned. "Status."

Dessa held up one finger, then two, ticking through it because Sable liked lists when he was scared, and he was scared — she could see it in the way he stood too still, the way he hadn't touched the coffee. "Transmitter's hot. I've got line-of-sight relay through the coastal tower at Grayhaven, which means everything north of the Head is covered. South coast I'm bouncing off a weather buoy, which is insultingly easy because the syndicate pays for that buoy's uplink and never changed the access codes." She allowed herself one thin smile. "Arrogance is a gift that keeps giving."

"And the package?"

"The package is clean." She nodded at the case in Pip's arms. "Scans of the journal, all ninety-one pages. Voss's original dive log, photographed front and back including the tide tables in her own hand. The falsified manifest with the comparison overlay I built against the Rivergate official report. And the affidavit Voss recorded yesterday — audio and transcript. Everything's compressed, encrypted with a key I'll publish openly once it's out, and addressed to—" she counted again, because the number still gave her a small electric thrill every time, "—maritime authority regional office, the coastal press consortium, eleven harbor masters' offices, the insurers' fraud bureau, and a public archive mirror in three jurisdictions. Once it lands, it doesn't land once. It lands everywhere."

Pip shifted on the table. "And they can't stop it?"

Dessa looked at him — sixteen years old, stitched up from the raid, still holding the thing he'd bled for — and decided he deserved the real answer, not the reassuring one.

"They can try. Jamming works by drowning a frequency in noise. But I'm not sending on one frequency. I'm splitting the transmission across six bands and hopping between them faster than their operators can chase, and each packet carries its own checksum, so even if they shred half of it, any single receiver that catches enough pieces can reconstruct the whole. It's like trying to burn down a library by tearing pages out of books that have already been copied." She shrugged. "The only way they stop this broadcast is to take this boat in the next fifteen minutes. And if they do that, they do it in front of witnesses, with the fleet impounded by nightfall anyway, because Okafor already sent our position to the authority cutter on a separate channel they don't know about."

Okafor didn't lower the binoculars. "I did."

"You did," Dessa confirmed. "You absolute paranoid saint."

"Somebody on this boat has to assume the worst," Okafor said mildly. "It's a full-time position. I don't get holidays."

* * *

There were nine minutes left when Voss came aboard.

Dessa watched her come up the ladder from the skiff — Marguerite Voss, who ten days ago had been a name Sable spat like a bad tooth, climbing onto their deck with her dive bag over one shoulder and nothing else, having surrendered her own ship, her salvage contract, and whatever remained of her standing with the people who owned her debts. She moved like a woman who had put something very heavy down and hadn't yet learned how to walk without it.

She stopped at the wheelhouse door and didn't enter until Sable nodded.

"Jamming's live," Voss said. "Full spectrum. They'll start sweeping for your carrier wave within minutes of your first transmission. You'll have less time than you think."

"Then we send the most important thing first," Sable said.

Dessa had expected an argument about this — some debate over sequencing, over strategy, over whether to hold anything back as leverage. That was what she'd have argued. Leverage was how she thought; information you didn't spend was information you kept. But Sable shook his head before she could open her mouth, and she realized he'd already settled it, maybe days ago, maybe years ago, in some interior country she was only visiting.

"No leverage," he said. "All of it, all at once, everywhere. If we hold a page back, they'll spend the rest of their lives buying people to bury it. If we give away everything, there's nothing left to buy." His jaw worked. "My father tried to trade with them. Quiet testimony for quiet money. Look where it got him."

"That's not what the journal says he tried," Voss said quietly.

Everyone looked at her.

"He wasn't trading," she said. "He was building a case. He wanted it public from the start. It was the rest of us — me, the expedition backers, everyone who signed his reports without reading them — we're the ones who taught the world that Rivergate could be handled quietly." Her voice didn't break; Dessa noticed that. Ten years of hating yourself apparently either breaks you or sands you smooth, and Voss had been sanded. "So make it loud. Please. Make it so loud they can't sand it down again."

Sable looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at Dessa. "Send it all."

* * *

The first packet went out at 14:07 local time.

Dessa felt it leave — not physically, though she'd swear sometimes she could feel a good transmission in her teeth, in the fillings, a faint pressure like weather changing. What she felt was the meaning of it: ninety-one pages of a dead man's handwriting leaving the flooded strongroom where it had waited ten years, leaving the waterproof case a sixteen-year-old had shielded with his body, leaving this boat, and becoming something no one could ever take back. Truth, she thought, feeding the hop sequence, watching the checksums confirm green across six bands, truth was the only cargo in the world that gained weight the more hands it passed through.

The manifest went second. Then Voss's log. Then the affidavit, her flat, steady voice describing the night she'd altered the tide tables, the envelope of cash, the name of the man who'd handed it to her — a name Dessa recognized from the leaked files, a name that sat three rungs below the syndicate's board and one rung above the men currently anchoring on their horizon.

On the fourth packet, the noise started.

Dessa heard it before her instruments flagged it — a rising wash of static on band two, then band five, broad-spectrum garbage flooding the airwaves like silt choking a channel. She glanced at the receiver gain and saw the needle pinning. Out on the water, the syndicate lead ship had come alive, its array masts bristling, pouring interference into the strait with the cheerful indiscriminate malice of a machine that had been told to stop a message and didn't care what else it drowned.

"They're jamming," Okafor reported, unnecessarily. The whole wheelhouse could hear it, a low oceanic roar under everything.

"They're jamming blind," Dessa said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, because her heart was doing something complicated — fear and fury and a wild, singing joy all fighting for the same breath. "They're flooding every frequency in range, which means they're also jamming their own fleet comms, the harbor pilots, the weather service, and the authority cutter that's currently steaming toward them with our position. Every minute they keep this up, they look guiltier to everyone with a radio." She fed the hopper a new pattern. "And meanwhile my packets are already ashore."

Because that was the thing — the beautiful, irreversible thing. The first three packets had landed before the jamming came up. She'd watched the acknowledgments tick in from Grayhaven, from the buoy relay, from the archive mirror two hundred miles south. Somewhere in a server rack in a coastal town, a copy of Elias Sterling's field journal was sitting in a directory, timestamped, checksummed, waiting. Somewhere a harbor master's inbox had a falsified manifest in it. The syndicate could jam the air over this strait until the sun burned out; they couldn't reach into those machines. Not legally. Not quietly. Not anymore.

"Band four's solid," she said aloud, mostly to herself. "Two and six are marginal but the checksums are rebuilding from partials. Keep talking to me, Okafor — what are they doing?"

"Launching," Okafor said. "Two fast boats off the lead ship. Coming this way. Fast."

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that we should stop admiring your handiwork and think about whether we're still here when they arrive."

Sable was already moving. "How many packets left?"

"Three. The big ones — the full journal scans, the overlay, the affidavit file. Six minutes at current throughput, longer if the jamming eats more bandwidth."

"We'll give you your six minutes," Sable said. "Pip — engine room, cold-start the main, don't spin it up till I call. Okafor, get the fenders in and the deck secured, we may be taking wakes. Voss—" he paused, and Dessa saw him choose the word, "—stay. I want a witness who was there the first time."

Voss stayed. She stood by the chart table with her hands folded, watching the transmitter's lights with an expression Dessa couldn't read and didn't try to, and after a moment she said, to no one in particular, "He would have liked this. Your father. He'd have hated the danger and loved the machine. He always said information was the only salvage that didn't rust."

Dessa's hands stuttered on the keys for half a second. Then she locked the next hop and sent the fifth packet into the roaring dark, and somewhere past the jamming wall, a receiver she'd never see caught the pieces and began, patiently, to rebuild a dead man's testimony.

* * *

The syndicate boats were seven minutes out when the last acknowledgment came in.

Dessa watched it arrive — the final green checkmark, the archive mirror confirming receipt of the complete cache, all files verified, all hashes matching — and she did something she almost never did in the wheelhouse. She put both hands flat on the console and bowed her head over them, just for a moment, the way you do at a graveside or a finish line.

"It's done," she said. "Everywhere. It's done."

The wheelhouse went very quiet, except for the jamming, which roared on outside like a storm that had arrived too late to matter. Pip came up from the engine room with grease on his chin and triumph all over his face. Okafor lowered the binoculars at last. Voss pressed the back of her hand to her mouth and breathed.

Sable stood at the wheel and looked south, toward the two fast boats knifing through the water toward them, and Dessa watched him understand it — really understand it, watch it land in his shoulders and his stance. They could take the boat. They could take the crew, the transmitter, the original documents, everything physical they had. It wouldn't matter. The thing they'd come to bury was already loose in the world, multiplying in inboxes and archives and newsroom queues along eight hundred miles of coast, and every hour the syndicate spent chasing this boat was another hour the story grew roots.

"They can't repossess it," Dessa said. She meant it as engineering assessment. It came out sounding like a benediction.

"No," Sable said. "They can't."

The lead fast boat was close enough now to see faces at its rail. Behind it, absurdly, the syndicate's jamming continued, flooding frequencies that no longer carried anything but weather reports and fishing chatter, a fortress shelling a town its army had already abandoned.

Then Okafor said, quietly, "Contact. North. Two points off the bow."

Dessa raised her head. Out past the buoys, coming down the strait with the authority cutter's grey hull throwing a bow wave wide enough to read from here, were ships — two of them, then a third behind, running lights burning in the afternoon haze, closing on the syndicate fleet at flank speed.

Somebody, somewhere ashore, had opened the files.

Sable's hand tightened on the wheel, and for the first time in ten years, Dessa thought, he looked like a man who was exactly where he intended to be.
