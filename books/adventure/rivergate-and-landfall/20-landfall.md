## 20. Landfall

The morning the syndicate fleet was impounded, Sable Sterling woke before dawn out of habit and then, for the first time in ten years, had nowhere to be.

He lay in his bunk aboard the *Kestrel's Grace* and listened to the harbor breathe. Gulls working the fish market two piers over. The groan of mooring lines. Somewhere below, Dessa's engine ticking as it cooled from the night's run — she never could leave an engine alone until it told her what it wanted. The sounds of a boat at rest, not a boat about to flee. He'd forgotten there was a difference.

Through the salt-hazed portlight he could see them: six hulls riding at anchor under the maritime authority's pennant, gray and patient as headstones. The *Corvane Meridian*, the syndicate's flagship salvage vessel with its crane arm folded like a broken wing. The fast launches that had shadowed them for five tides. The tender that had delivered their "courtesy" offer wrapped in a lawyer's smile. All of it impounded, sealed, chained to its own anchors by writs Sable had watched being served through binoculars an hour after the broadcast went out.

The backers hadn't waited for the writs. By midmorning yesterday, three of the syndicate's named directors had resigned by press release — statements so carefully worded they admitted nothing while confessing everything between the lines. One of them, a shipping magnate whose face Sable recognized from charity galas in ports he'd worked under false names, had simply vanished from his penthouse, leaving behind a wife, a yacht, and a paper trail the investigators were already pulling apart thread by thread. Money, Sable was learning, scattered faster than rats. It had somewhere else to be. People had to stand where they stood.

He got up quietly, because some habits deserved to survive even when their reasons didn't, and climbed to the quarterdeck with a mug of coffee Pip had left warming on the galley plate — the boy had started doing that without being asked, another small sign of how wrong things had gone, how right.

The light was doing something to the water that Sable's father would have called *reading weather*. Gold running in long seams east to west, the kind of flat calm that meant either nothing or everything. Out past the buoys, where the channel bent toward the shelf, the sea lay so still you could see the shadow of the Landfall's broken mast on the bottom, forty feet down, like a sundial marking a different hour.

Sable stood at the rail and did not leave.

It should have been the easiest thing in the world, and it was the hardest thing he'd ever done. Ten years of leaving. Ten years of wrapping a job, taking his pay, and being gone before the town woke up — before anyone could learn his face well enough to match it to the photograph that had run beside every story about Rivergate, the son of the man who sank the Landfall. Leaving had been a discipline, like diving. You practiced until it cost you nothing.

This morning it cost him everything not to do it. His duffel sat packed in the foot of his bunk. He'd packed it last night without deciding to, hands moving on their own, and this morning he looked down at it through the companionway hatch and understood that the bag wasn't for going anywhere. It was a funeral. You buried the old ritual the way you buried anything — with your own hands, and it took longer than you thought.

"You're up early." Okafor came up the ladder with the rolling gait of a man whose body still thought it was on a deck in a swell. He settled against the rail a respectful distance away, the way he always did, giving Sable room the way you gave a wild thing room, though neither of them called it that anymore. "Couldn't sleep?"

"Could have," Sable said. "Didn't see the point."

Okafor grunted. Below them, on the main deck, Pip was coiling line with the fierce concentration of someone performing surgery, and Dessa had her head inside the engine housing again, arguing with it in a low voice. The morning moved around them like water around a stone.

"They'll want statements today," Okafor said. "Investigators. Probably the press too, if the investigators can't keep them off the pier."

"I know."

"And Voss."

Sable said nothing. The name sat in the air between them like a dropped tool.

"She asked for you specifically," Okafor said. "When she surrendered. Wouldn't give her statement until someone sent for you. The investigator's launch has been sitting off our stern since first light." He nodded toward it — a white government hull, official and slightly absurd in all that gold light, flying the little blue pennant of the coastal maritime commission.

Sable looked at it for a long time. Marguerite Voss. His father's rival, his own tormentor, the woman who had walked into a dockside bar with a contract and a lie and then, tide by tide, traded the lie away piece by piece until all that was left was the truth and whatever a person became after carrying it for ten years. She had altered the tide logs. She had let a dead man take the blame for a fraud she'd been paid to bury. And she had handed Sable her original dive log with shaking hands and said, *Tell it straight. Whatever else you do with me, tell it straight.*

He had spent a decade hating his father's ghost for dying wrong. He was only now learning what it cost to hate someone who was still alive to look back at you.

"Tell them I'm coming," Sable said.

* * *

They brought him alongside the impounded fleet first, because the investigators wanted him to see it, and because — Sable suspected — the lead investigator, a broad, gray-templed woman named Castellanos who had the unhurried manner of someone who had already read every document twice, wanted to watch his face when he did.

The *Meridian* was a different ship in defeat. Her decks, which had bristled with salvage gear and quiet menace, were littered with evidence tags. Men in coveralls moved through her holds cataloguing crates, and Sable caught glimpses of what they were finding: dredging equipment rated far beyond any legal survey, sealed manifests with the syndicate's house stamp, and — stacked in a hold like cordwood — replacement plates stamped with the Landfall's original hull numbers. Plates manufactured decades after the sinking. Proof that someone had built the pieces needed to fake a wreck site, and had done it in advance.

"They've been preparing for this salvage for years," Castellanos said beside him. "Not recovering the Landfall. *Replacing* her. If your crew hadn't broadcast the journal when you did, they'd have raised fabricated wreckage, 'proven' the official report, and closed the file forever." She glanced at him. "You understand what you did, Mr. Sterling? Not the legal part. The other part."

"I'm starting to," Sable said.

They put him ashore at the commission pier, where a temporary office smelled of wet rope and cheap coffee, and that was where they told him Voss was waiting.

She sat in a straight-backed chair in an interview room with a window, because she'd refused a closed one — Okafor relayed this later, having heard it from a junior officer who found it worth repeating. She'd surrendered her passport, her salvage licenses, her stake in the Voss fleet, everything, in an envelope she'd apparently prepared days ago, before the raid, before the storm, maybe before the sixth tide. A woman packing for a long stay.

Sable came into the room and she looked up, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

Up close, without the dockside armor of expensive coat and colder voice, Marguerite Voss looked exactly like what she was: a diver who had been holding her breath for ten years. There were lines around her eyes that hadn't come from sun. Her hands, folded on the table, were steady in the way of hands that had decided to stop pretending.

"Sterling." Almost a smile. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy's fleet."

That earned a real breath of laughter, short and surprised out of her, and then it was gone and they were back in the deep water where they lived.

"The investigators say I can choose my counsel, make my statement, negotiate cooperation agreements," she said. "Reduced exposure. They use words like *mitigation*. As if I fell into this." She turned her hands over, palms up, as if showing him they were empty. "I altered the tide logs, Sterling. Page by page. I was twenty-six years old and they owned my certification, my debts, my father's medical bills, and I told myself it was just ink. Just numbers. Nobody dies of ink." Her voice didn't waver; it had clearly done all its wavering years ago, alone, somewhere, and come out the other side flattened and hard. "Then your father went down with the truth in his kit bag, and the ink killed him anyway, and I learned that everything is ink. Every record. Every verdict. Every name on a memorial plaque. Somebody writes it, and somebody lets it stand."

"Why are you telling me this?" Sable asked. "You know I read the journal. You know I know."

"Because there's a difference between knowing a thing and hearing it said to your face by the person who did it, and you of all people have earned the second one." She leaned forward. "And because I need to ask you for something, and I need you to hear the asking before you decide whether to grant it."

"Ask."

"When this goes to the inquiry — and it will, publicly, they've already announced it — they're going to tell my father's story." She stopped. Corrected herself, and Sable saw the correction cost her more than anything else in the room. "*Your* father's story. Elias Sterling's story. The diver who flagged the falsified manifest, who documented the fraud, who died for it. They'll tell it with me in it as the woman who helped bury him." She held his eyes. "And they'll be right to. But there's a version of that story where I'm only a coward, and a version where I was also twenty-six and terrified and human, and I am not asking you to tell the second one instead of the first. I'm asking you to tell both. Straight. The whole weight of it. Can you do that?"

Sable stood at the table and felt ten years rearrange themselves around him.

He thought about the postcard in his duffel — the impossible postcard in his dead father's handwriting that had started all of this, that had turned out to be the first thread of a letter the postal system had finally, absurdly, delivered across a decade of delay. He thought about his father's field journal, the tight slanted script documenting fraud in the margins of a salvage log, a man writing the truth in the spaces left for lies. He thought about what it meant to tell a story straight when the story had nearly drowned everyone who touched it.

"My father kept records," Sable said slowly. "Everything in its place, everything dated, everything signed. He used to say a log entry was a promise to whoever comes after you." He looked at her. "So here's mine. I'll tell it straight. All of it. What you did, and what it cost, and what you carried, and why. No mitigation. No mercy either. Just true."

Voss closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and she let them be, which Sable understood was the hardest thing she'd done in the room.

"That's all I ever wanted," she said. "From anyone. From myself." She extended her hand across the table, and after a moment Sable took it. Her grip was a diver's grip — strong, brief, letting go first. "Your father would have hated me," she said. "But he'd have logged it honestly."

"He would have," Sable agreed. "It's the family trade."

* * *

The rest of the day happened to him rather than through him, which was itself a novel sensation.

Statements, given in a room that smelled of the same coffee, recorded by officers who treated his words as if they might matter — and discovering, with a strange vertigo, that they did. The journal pages spread under glass on a worktable, Dessa's photographs of each leaf projected on a wall while Castellanos's team cross-referenced them against the recovered manifests. Pip, bandaged along one forearm from the raid, sitting in on everything because he refused to leave and nobody had the heart or the legal grounds to make him, taking notes in a waterproof notebook he'd bought with his first real wages and labeled, in block capitals, *EVIDENCE (MINE)*.

By late afternoon the story had escaped containment entirely. A coastal radio station ran the manifest forgery as its lead. A wire service picked it up. By evening there were boats idling off the commission pier carrying people with cameras, and Castellanos posted two junior officers at the gangway with instructions that Sable recognized, with some amusement, as the maritime equivalent of *no comment*.

Dessa found him at dusk on the quarterdeck, where he'd gone without deciding to, the way rivers find the sea.

"Engine's happy," she reported, settling beside him. "For once. Ran her through the full diagnostic and she only lied to me twice." She was quiet a moment. In the failing light the impounded fleet had become a row of dark shapes, their riding lights reflected in long smears on the water. "You know what I keep thinking about? The night we voted. In the bar. When you told us no and we took the vote anyway."

"I remember."

"You looked at us like we'd betrayed you." She said it without accusation, just inventory, the way she'd inventory an engine. "And I've been thinking — that wasn't it. You looked at us like we'd *found* you. Like we'd done something to you that couldn't be undone." She shrugged. "Took me this long to figure out that's what it was. We didn't take the job, Sable. We took you. Off your own hands."

Sable looked at her. Dessa had rebuilt his boat's engine with parts scavenged from three wrecks and a stubbornness that bordered on theology. She had dug through leaked files until she found the erased name. She had never once, in all the years and all the ports, asked him why he always left. She had just made sure the boat was ready whenever he decided to.

"Dessa," he said.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Whatever sentence starts with my name right now, save it. I'm an engineer. I run on maintenance schedules, not speeches." But she was smiling as she stood, and she squeezed his shoulder once, hard, the way you test a weld, and went below.

* * *

Night came down soft over the harbor, and Sable stayed on the quarterdeck because he had nowhere else he wanted to be, and the strangeness of that sentence still hadn't worn off.

Pip came up around nine with two blankets and no apology, draped one over Sable's shoulders like it was the most natural act in the world, and curled into the other against the rail. For a while the boy watched the lights of the commission pier, where the investigation offices still burned late.

"Do you think they'll name him right?" Pip asked eventually. "In the inquiry. Your dad."

"They will if I have anything to say about it."

"Not what I asked." Pip was fifteen and had the terrifying precision of fifteen-year-olds. "I asked if you think they will. Like — on their own. Because it's true. Or only because you made them."

Sable considered it honestly, because the boy had earned honesty and mostly because lying to Pip had become physically difficult. "Both," he said at last. "Truth doesn't defend itself, Pip. That's the whole lesson of the last ten years. Somebody has to stand next to it and refuse to move. My father stood next to it alone, and it killed him." He pulled the blanket tighter. "We're not going to do it alone anymore. Any of it."

Pip nodded slowly, absorbing this, filing it wherever fifteen-year-olds file the things that will decide who they become. Then, because he was also still a kid: "Dessa says if the survey contract comes through, she wants first pick of the equipment budget. Okafor says he wants hazard pay. I haven't decided what I want yet."

"Take your time," Sable said. "We're not leaving tomorrow."

The words came out of him and hung in the air, and Pip turned to look at him — really look, the way he'd been looking at Sable since the first day, hero-worship and something sharper underneath — and grinned.

"Yeah?" the boy said.

"Yeah," Sable said. "We're staying."

Below them, the harbor settled into its night sounds. Out past the buoys, the Landfall rested on the shelf in forty feet of clear cold water, her secrets given up, her hull numbers soon to be confirmed by divers working under warrant, her name destined at last for the record books in the company of the truth. Somewhere in the commission offices, a clerk was opening a file that had been closed wrongly for ten years and writing, in the space reserved for the cause of sinking, a new entry.

Sable stood his watch — not a watch, really, nothing to guard, no one pursuing, just a man standing on the quarterdeck of the boat he had nearly lost with the people he had nearly lost it with — and when the moon rose he counted the impounded ships' lights out of habit and found he didn't need to count anything anymore.

In his bunk below, packed and waiting, the duffel bag sat like a headstone. Tomorrow, he thought, he'd unpack it. Hang things up. Put the postcard — the letter, his father's handwriting crossing a decade of silence to reach him at last — somewhere it could live instead of travel.

There was a hearing in three weeks. There was a story to tell straight, all of it, the whole weight. There was a survey contract pending, and a crew that had chosen him twice, and a channel that would silt shut behind them and open again in spring, the way channels do, the way doors do, if you're the kind of person who stays long enough to see them open.

Sable Sterling stood on the quarterdeck and did not leave.

And in the morning, when the gulls started and the fish market woke and Pip came up the ladder with coffee already warming, he was still there — standing his ground at the rail of the *Kestrel's Grace*, watching the light come up over the water his father had loved and died in, ready at last to inherit it.
