## 1. The Water Keeps Its Dead

The river gave up its dead the way it gave up everything else: slowly, grudgingly, and only when it felt like it.

Elodie Galloway hung forty feet beneath the brown-green skin of the Rivergate stretch, her lamp cutting a cone through water thick with silt and old secrets. Below her, the corvette *Vessant* lay on its side in the mud like a sleeping animal, its hull furred with forty years of weed, its gunports gaping. She had been diving this wreck for three seasons now. Brass fittings, copper sheathing, the occasional porthole glass gone opalescent with age — enough to keep Tamir's daughter in medicine and Sefa fed, if never quite enough to get ahead of anything.

She worked the seam where the officers' quarters had collapsed inward, her gloved fingers reading the metal the way a blind woman reads a face. The monsoon was still days out, but the river already knew. It moved differently down here — heavier, more deliberate, as though it were gathering itself for something.

Her lamp caught a shape that didn't belong.

Not brass. Not copper. Wood — dark wood, dense with saturation, bound in iron straps that had somehow refused to rust entirely. A chest, wedged beneath a fallen beam near what had been the captain's cabin, half-swallowed by mud but unmistakably deliberate in its placement. Someone had put it there to be found by no one.

Elodie's heart did the slow, heavy knock it always did when the river offered something up. She checked her air gauge out of habit — plenty — and then checked it again, because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

The chest came free with a groan of shifting sediment that bloomed across her vision like ink in water. It was heavier than it should have been, or maybe everything down here weighed more than it should. She got her lift bag under it, feathered the valve, and watched the chest rise past her into the grey light above, trailing mud like a shroud.

* * *

Tamir was waiting at the surface, leaning over the gunwale of their battered workboat, the *Magpie*, his broad face already arranged into the expression he wore whenever Elodie brought up something that wasn't obviously sellable.

"Tell me that's ballast," he said.

"Tell me you've ever once listened when I said that."

He hauled it aboard with a grunt, and they both stood over it, dripping, while the river slapped against the hull in its patient way. The wood was black with age. The iron straps were stamped — not corroded away, not worn, *stamped* — with a mark Elodie knew from salvaged coins and one cracked dinner plate she kept on her shelf because nobody would buy it.

Two stars, interlocked. One slightly larger than the other, as though one had swallowed the light of its twin.

The double-star of Vessia.

A country that had been erased from every map before Elodie learned to read them. A name that appeared in old contracts only as a smudge, an inked-out word, a hole in the record. Forty years ago there had been a war, or something shaped like a war, and afterward Vessia simply wasn't anymore. No borders. No flag. No nation. Just salvage divers occasionally pulling up artifacts "of foreign origin," which was the phrase the permit office used, which meant *don't ask*.

"That's not foreign origin," Tamir said quietly. "That's Vessian."

"I know what it is."

"Do you? Because I know what it means." He crouched, ran his thumb along the seal without touching it. "It means the navy sank this ship on purpose, Elodie. Corvettes don't go down in calm water forty feet deep with their officers' quarters sealed. And it means whatever's in that box was worth killing a country over."

"It's worth money."

"It's worth *trouble*. There's a difference, and the difference is usually measured in how many people are standing around you when the trouble arrives." He straightened up, wiping his hands on his thighs. "Throw it back. We finish the season on fittings and glass, we pay Mira's debt down another month, and nobody's name goes on any list."

Elodie looked at the chest. The mud was drying on it in pale streaks, and the interlocked stars seemed darker than the wood around them, as though the stamp itself had been cut deep enough to hold shadow forever.

Her instinct said money. Her instinct had fed her for fifteen years in the drowned streets of Rivergate, and it had rarely lied to her, and right now it was saying that whatever sat inside this box was worth more than every fitting she'd pulled from every wreck since she'd first strapped on a tank.

But there was another voice underneath, older and less greedy, that said: *the river keeps its dead, and it kept this one hidden for a reason.*

"We crack it in the boathouse," she said. "Tonight. If it's coin and silverware, we're rich for a season. If it's paper—" She shrugged. "Paper burns."

Tamir looked at her for a long moment. "Paper doesn't burn clean," he said. "Paper leaves ash. Ash has names in it."

Sefa appeared at the stern then, silent as she always was, a thin woman with rope-scarred hands who could rig a lift bag in the dark and hadn't spoken ten full sentences in the three years she'd crewed for them. She looked at the chest, and then at the sky upstream, and Elodie watched her go very still.

"What?" Elodie said.

Sefa pointed.

Upstream, where the river bent around the drowned church spire, a boat was coming down toward them. Grey hull. Low profile. No running lights, though the afternoon was dimming toward early dusk. Moving slow — not searching slow, not patrolling slow, but *deliberate* slow, the way a man walks toward a door he already knows he's going to open.

A navy patrol boat, unannounced, in a stretch of river where the navy hadn't shown its face in years except to collect permit fees through a clerk in an office two towns over.

"They don't come down here," Tamir said. His voice had gone flat, careful. "They haven't come down here since—"

"Since anyone had a reason to," Elodie finished.

The chest sat between them on the deck, shedding the last of its river water through some seam in the lid, dripping onto the planks in small dark drops. Elodie threw the tarp over it, more to stop the dripping than to hide it, and told herself the gesture wasn't fear.

The patrol boat came on, unhurried, its wake spreading wide and white behind it in the failing light. Somewhere on its deck, glass caught the last sun and flashed — binoculars, maybe, or a rifle scope, impossible to tell at this distance and pointless to guess.

"Get below," Elodie said. "Both of you. Act like it's any other evening."

"And you?" Tamir asked.

Elodie watched the grey hull grow larger, watched the river carry it toward them with all the patience of water, which is the patience of things that always arrive eventually.

"I'm going to stand here," she said, "and look like a woman who just finished a long day's honest work."

The tarp-covered chest dripped softly behind her, keeping time, and the patrol boat turned its bow toward the *Magpie*, and the river went on carrying its dead.
