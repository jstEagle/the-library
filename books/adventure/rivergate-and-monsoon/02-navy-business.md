## 2. Navy Business

Lieutenant Corvane had been on the river for three days before he saw Rivergate, and in that time he had learned to read the water the way his instructors at the naval academy had never taught him — not as a chart, not as a depth sounding, but as a ledger of everything the country preferred to forget.

The river ran brown and patient between its levees, carrying silt from the highlands and, beneath the silt, carrying other things. Corvane stood at the rail of the patrol boat *Vigilant* and watched the banks slide past: drowned orchards with their dead trees standing in rows like congregations waiting for a sermon that would never come; the rusted crown of a grain elevator; a church spire leaning at an angle that suggested it had given up arguing with the current decades ago. Forty years ago this valley had been a different nation. Now it was just water with opinions.

"Slow ahead," he said, though the helmsman had already slowed. The *Vigilant* drew little enough water to nose up the flooded main street of what remained of Rivergate — the upper town, the part that had survived the dam and the deliberate flooding that followed, where salvage families lived in houses built on stilts above a harbor full of drowned neighbors.

He had read the file twice on the journey upriver. It was thin, which told him more than a thick one would have. Thin files meant someone had spent effort making them thin. *Hydrographic Survey, Section Nine*, the folder said, and inside were eleven years of dredging reports from a stretch of river that appeared on no survey schedule Corvane could find anywhere else in the fleet. Eleven years of quiet government tugs dragging magnets and grapples along the riverbed around a wreck officially listed as *position unknown, significance nil*. Somebody had been looking for something for a decade, and somebody senior had decided the looking should be invisible.

And now the looking had failed, or succeeded — the orders did not specify which — and so the navy had sent him. A lieutenant with a polite manner, a briefcase of permits, and instructions written in the passive voice, which in Corvane's experience was always the voice of shame.

The harbor at Rivergate smelled of diesel and wet rope and the green rot of things the river was slowly digesting. Houseboats clustered along a sagging boardwalk. Nets hung drying like funeral banners. Children watched him from a walkway with the flat, unblinking assessment of children who had learned early that uniforms came to take things.

Corvane stepped onto the boardwalk and let the permits do the introductions. He wore his dress whites pressed to regulation sharpness, because he had found that men who dressed carefully were trusted slightly longer than men who didn't, and trust, however shallow, bought time, and time was what soft questions needed.

* * *

Elodie Galloway's boathouse sat at the end of the boardwalk on pilings gone silver with age, and she received him standing in the doorway with her arms crossed, as if the door itself were a bulkhead she was deciding whether to open.

"Lieutenant Corvane. Naval Salvage Administration." He offered the folder. "Permits for survey work in this reach. I'm told you hold a commercial salvage license."

"I hold whatever keeps the tax office off my back," Elodie said. Her eyes went over his shoulder to the *Vigilant* moored in the channel, then back to him. "You're a long way from any survey I've ever heard of."

"The river is long," Corvane said pleasantly. "May I?"

She stepped aside without answering, which he took as permission, which he suspected she intended as contempt. Inside, the boathouse smelled of tar and brine and engine oil. A diving helmet hung on the wall, old brass gone green at the seams. Charts papered one side of the room, annotated in a woman's small, fierce handwriting. He noted, without appearing to note, the chart nearest the door: the reach downstream, the drowned town proper, and — circled twice, crossed out once, circled again — a set of coordinates that matched, to the digit, the wreck of the corvette *Vessant*.

So. She had found it already. Or she had been circling it for years the way the dredges had, drawn to the same hole in the river's memory.

"You'll have noticed," Corvane said, laying the permits on her workbench, "that the government conducts periodic hydrographic surveys through this section. Channel maintenance. Navigation safety. Routine."

"I've noticed the dredges," Elodie said. "Eleven years of them. Funny kind of routine, dredging the same two hundred meters of riverbed every season and never touching the rest of the channel." She leaned against the bench, unhurried. "Funny kind of navigation safety, when nothing but catfish and my crew ever comes through here."

Corvane smiled the smile he used at inspections, the one that conceded intelligence while conceding nothing else. "You've been paying attention."

"It's my river."

"It is," he agreed, "in the way that all rivers belong to the people who drown in them." He let that sit a moment, then opened the second folder, the thinner one, the one with money in it. "The administration offers a finder's fee. For artifacts of foreign origin recovered within the survey zone. Recovered and surrendered, that is. The fee is generous. The alternative—" he paused, delicately, the way one pauses before a word one does not wish to say aloud— "the alternative involves statutes I'd rather not recite in a lady's boathouse. Unregistered recovery of state property. Interference with a government operation. There are older laws too, about the material of the former Vessian territories. Most people don't know they still exist. They do."

"Foreign origin," Elodie repeated. Her voice was even, but Corvane had interrogated enough sailors to hear the keel under the words. "Rivergate's under forty years of water. Half the harbor is foreign origin by your definition."

"Which is why the fee is per item," Corvane said, "and assessed case by case. By me." He took out a card, heavy cream stock, absurdly official against the tar-stained bench, and wrote a number on the back. "Anything bearing certain marks. A double star, for instance. Seals. Insignia. Documents." He watched her face on the word *documents* and saw — barely, a flicker, a rudder shifting half a degree — that he had scored. "Bring them to me and there will be no questions about how they were found. No paperwork. Just payment, and the administration's gratitude, which is worth more than the payment."

"And if I bring you nothing?"

"Then the river keeps what it keeps," Corvane said, "and we are both poorer, and no one is arrested, and everyone is happy." He gathered his folders. At the door he stopped, and allowed himself one question that was not soft at all, though he delivered it softly. "The wreck of the *Vessant*. Officially she's unlocated. Unofficially, my predecessors spent eleven years dragging for her. You're the best diver on this reach, Galloway. Everyone says so. If anyone found her—" he turned, and met her eyes, and made sure she saw that he saw— "it would be you. And if you had found her, and found something aboard her, I want you to understand something clearly, because I am going to say it only once, and I am not a man who enjoys saying it twice."

He held her gaze.

"There are people above me who have wanted that ship to stay lost for forty years. They sank her themselves. Whatever she carries, they killed to put it at the bottom of this river, and they will not hesitate to kill again to keep it there. The finder's fee is real. So is the alternative. But the third option — keeping what you find, selling it, running with it — that option doesn't exist. It only looks like it exists, right up until it kills everyone you love. I've read the files. I know how it ends."

Elodie said nothing for a long moment. The river slapped against the pilings below them, patient as a debt collector.

"Was there anything else, Lieutenant?" she said finally.

"No," Corvane said. "That was everything. Except—" he paused on the threshold, the light behind him turning him briefly into a silhouette, a shape without a face— "the dredging reports list a secondary site. Two hundred meters downstream of the primary. Something was moved off the *Vessant* before she settled, or after. A smaller object. If it surfaces, the fee doubles."

He stepped out into the sun and walked back down the boardwalk, feeling her stare between his shoulder blades like a hand.

* * *

Aboard the *Vigilant*, Corvane poured himself the coffee that passed for coffee in the river fleet and stood at the stern rail, watching Rivergate shrink behind them.

His ensign, a boy named Pell, asked the question junior officers ask. "Sir, do you think she has it? The thing we're looking for?"

"I think," Corvane said, "that she looked at my chart coordinates before I ever mentioned them, which means she's already been down there. And I think she lied to me about nothing, which means she's smart enough to know lying is the first thing we check." He drank. "Yes, Pell. She has it. Or she knows where it is, which amounts to the same thing."

"What happens to her if she doesn't turn it in?"

Corvane watched the drowned orchards slide past, the dead trees standing in their flooded rows.

"That depends," he said, "on whether the people who wrote my orders care more about the object or about the silence. With that crowd, Pell, it's usually the silence."

He had not told Elodie the whole truth, though he had told her more of it than his orders contained. He had read the annexes — the ones a lieutenant was technically cleared for and technically not supposed to study — and he knew what the *Vessant* had carried in her final voyage: not cargo, not contraband, but people. Officials of a drowned nation, promised repatriation, executed instead, and the papers proving it locked in a strongbox that had been supposed to go down with them and had instead gone down beside them, two hundred meters off, where the current wanted it.

He knew also that the names in that strongbox did not end in 1943. They ran forward, year after year, into the present, into ministries and admiralties, into the signature blocks of the very orders now sitting in his briefcase.

Corvane finished his coffee and set the cup down with great care, as if it might detonate.

Behind him, Rivergate dissolved into the haze. Ahead, the river bent toward the next reach, brown and patient, keeping its accounts.

He had given the diver three days to come to him. Privately, watching the way she had stood in her doorway — rooted, unafraid, a woman who made her living going down into dark places and coming back — he gave her less than that.

And privately, in the part of himself he did not show to ensigns or admirals, he admitted what the visit had really been. Not a warning. Not a threat.

A test. His own. To see whether the man he still believed himself to be could deliver a message like that and walk away clean.

The river slid on. Somewhere beneath it, a strongbox waited with a double-star seal, and somewhere above it, in a stilted boathouse, a woman was already deciding what to do with the truth — and Corvane understood, with the particular dread of a man reading his own orders properly for the first time, that whatever she chose, the ending had already been written by people whose names he would find, eventually, in exactly the sort of ledger he had been sent to recover.
