## 3. What the Chest Holds

The boathouse smelled of tar and old rain, and the chest sat in the middle of the floor like a thing that had been waiting forty years to be looked at.

I'd left it there overnight, which I never did. Salvage came up, got sorted, got sold or scrapped — that was the rhythm that kept us fed. But this one I hadn't touched after Tamir hauled it aboard, hadn't even run a hand over the lid. It sat in the lamplight with the river still weeping out of its seams, and the double-star stamped into the brass plate caught the flame and threw it back, two points of gold in all that dark.

Sefa wouldn't come near it. She'd made herself busy at the far bench, mending a net she didn't need mended, her back a closed door. Tamir sat on an upturned crate with his elbows on his knees, watching the chest the way you watch a dog you're not sure is friendly.

"You slept?" he asked.

"Badly."

"Me neither." He rubbed his jaw. "I keep hearing it. Under the floor. Like it's knocking."

"It's not knocking. It's wood settling."

"Wood settles," Tamir said. "Chests don't."

He was right, and we both knew it, and neither of us said anything else for a while. Outside, the river ran high and brown with the first push of the monsoon coming down from the hills, and the pilings groaned in a way I told myself was just the current.

Finally I got up and knelt in front of it.

The lock was Vessian work, which meant it was good work, which meant it fought me. I went at it with picks and then with a heated knife and then with plain patience, sweat running down my spine in the boathouse heat, and Tamir talked the whole time — not to me, exactly, more to the room, the way he did when he was nervous.

"Corvane's patrol boat sat out there all night. You see the lantern? Didn't move. Just sat. That's not a boat, Elodie, that's a statement." A pause. "We could still put it back. Weight it, drop it at the wreck, done. Nobody ever knew."

"Corvane knows."

"Corvane suspects. There's a difference, and the difference is a finder's fee."

The lock gave with a click I felt in my teeth. The lid came up stiff, hinges screaming after four decades underwater, and the smell that rose out of it was not river-smell. It was oilcloth and ink and something faintly sweet, like the inside of a church.

I lifted out the top layer and my hands went still.

Documents. Dozens of them, wrapped in oiled silk that had kept the water out better than the navy had ever kept anything in. Letters with Vessian seals. A folio bound in grey leather, its spine cracked but its contents dry. And beneath those, wrapped separately, a ledger — real leather, brass corners, the kind of book that gets carried by men who sign things that matter.

"Elodie," Tamir said quietly. "What is it?"

I didn't answer. I opened the folio.

* * *

The first document was a manifest. I could read enough of the old Vessian script to get the shape of it — names, ranks, a date. The date was forty years and two months ago. The names numbered eleven.

The second document was an order. It was written in our own language, our own navy's hand, and it was short enough that I read it three times without it getting any shorter.

*The passengers are to be transferred at Rivergate anchorage. No repatriation is authorized. Disposition of passengers at the discretion of the commanding officer. No record to be kept.*

No record to be kept. Except the man who wrote it had kept one, or someone had — because the third document was a report, and the report said, in the flat bureaucratic language of men describing terrible things in clean sentences, that the transfer had been *completed successfully* and the passengers *did not survive the operation*.

Eleven names on a manifest. Eleven exiled officials, fleeing a country that was about to be wiped off every map, who had put themselves in the hands of a navy that promised passage — and been taken off the *Vessant* at the Rivergate anchorage, in the dark, and killed.

Not lost. Not drowned in the storm the histories talked about. Executed, on our water, by our flag, and then the whole town drowned on top of them like a lid coming down.

The boathouse was very quiet. Sefa had stopped pretending to mend the net. She was standing at the edge of the lamplight, and I realized she'd been reading over my shoulder, and that her hands were pressed flat against her apron like she was holding herself down.

"Open the ledger," Tamir said.

"Why?"

"Because a manifest is a story and a ledger is a bill. I want to know who paid."

I opened it. The pages were dense, columns of entries in two hands — one Vessian, one ours. Payments. Dates. Names.

I knew some of the names.

Not from history books. From the news sheets. From the ceremonial broadcasts. One of them sat in the capital now, a minister, his face on every anniversary parade. Two others held admiralties. There were judges. Port authorities. Men who had taken Vessian money — or given it, or moved it — in the years before the erasure, and who had helped decide, in smoke-filled rooms, that the cleanest solution to a drowned nation was to make sure none of its government survived to complain about the drowning.

The ledger didn't just name them. It named what they'd done, entry by entry, in a hand that got shakier toward the back, as if the man writing it had known he was building a bomb and kept building it anyway.

"Minister Halloway," Tamir read over my shoulder, and whistled low. "That's — Elodie, that's the man they put on the stamps."

"I can read, Tamir."

"Then read it again, because I don't think it says what you want it to say." He straightened up, and I watched something move across his face — the thing I'd been afraid of since the chest came over the rail. Not horror. Arithmetic. "Do you know what this is worth? Not to a museum. Museums pay nothing. To a newspaper in the capital — no. To the right buyer. Someone across the border, someone with an interest in Vessia. There are people, Elodie. Exiles. Old families. They'd empty vaults for this."

"They'd kill for it," Sefa said.

Her voice was so level it took me a second to understand she'd spoken at all. She came forward into the light, and her face was pale but her eyes were steady, and she pointed at the manifest without touching it.

"Those eleven people trusted the navy and it killed them. You think the people who want this ledger are better? You think anyone who wants a book full of names like this wants it for the truth?" She shook her head slowly. "They want it for power. Same as the navy wanted the passengers gone for power. It's the same hand, just a different glove."

"So we burn it," Tamir said. "Is that your answer? Forty years of — of this—" he gestured at the ledger, at the names, at the whole drowned machinery of it, "—and we burn it so nobody's feelings get hurt?"

"I said burn it because I don't trust anyone to hold it. Including us." Sefa looked at me. "Elodie. The navy has been dredging this stretch for years. Years. They sent a lieutenant with soft questions and a finder's fee. You know what happens next. They'll stop asking softly."

Tamir turned on her. "And you know what happens to us if we do nothing? We're three salvagers in a leaky boathouse, Sefa. I've got Mira's debt sitting on my chest like a stone — you know the number, you've helped me count it. This chest is the first thing that's ever come up out of that river that could actually pay it. Actually change something. And you want to feed it to the fire because you're scared?"

"Yes," Sefa said simply. "I'm scared. Scared is the correct response."

They both looked at me then, and the lamplight hissed, and the river groaned against the pilings, and I understood that whatever I said next was going to cost something, because there was no answer in that chest that didn't.

* * *

I made them put it away before we talked. All of it, back in the chest, lid down, and I dragged my workbench over and set it on top, which was theater and we all knew it, but theater helps.

"Here's what I know," I said. "Corvane didn't come here by chance. The navy's been dredging this reach for years — that's not a search, that's a sweep. They knew the *Vessant* was here. They've always known. They sank the town on top of their own crime and they've been patrolling its grave ever since, and last week a corvette's hull finally shifted in the current and a chest came loose, and now they're frightened."

"Frightened men pay well," Tamir said.

"Frightened men with gunboats pay in bullets." I held up a hand before he could answer. "I'm not saying no. I'm saying we don't decide tonight, and we don't decide loud. Sefa — the chest stays under the floor ballast, wrapped, and nobody breathes a word. Not to the harbor master, not to the priest, not to your cousin who runs the ferry. Tamir—"

He was already shaking his head. "You're going to tell me to sit on the only fortune I'll ever see."

"I'm going to tell you that the men in that ledger have admirals and ministers, and the first thing they'll buy isn't the ledger. It's silence. And the cheapest silence is three drowned salvagers." I let that sit. "If we sell, we sell right — to someone who can protect us, at a price that includes our backs turned and walking away. Not a whisper to Corvane, not a letter across the border. Understood?"

Tamir's jaw worked. He looked at the floorboards where the chest was hidden, and I watched him do the arithmetic again, Mira's debt against the odds, and I knew I hadn't won. I'd only bought time.

"Understood," he said, and it was the tone of a man agreeing to a ceasefire, not a peace.

Sefa put her hand on my arm as she passed. "Thank you," she whispered, and I hated it a little, because her thanks felt like a debt I might not be able to pay.

* * *

They left after midnight. I stayed, the way I always stay, doing the rounds of the boathouse with the lamp turned low — lines checked, bilge sounded, the river talking its endless talk against the hull.

I knelt once by the loose floorboard and put my palm flat on the wood, over the hidden chest, and I thought about eleven names who had trusted a flag and died for it, and one name in the ledger — the shakiest hand, the last entries — and I wondered who he'd been, and why a man building his own funeral had bothered to keep accounts.

The lamp guttered. Outside, far upstream, where the navy patrol boat had sat all night without moving, I saw a lantern blink.

Twice. Pause. Twice again.

Not river-talk. Not fishing boats — no fisherman signals twice-and-twice at one in the morning.

Somebody out there in the dark was talking to somebody on shore. And the only thing on this stretch of river worth talking about was sleeping under my floorboards.
