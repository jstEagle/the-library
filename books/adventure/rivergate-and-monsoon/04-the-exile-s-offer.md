## 4. The Exile's Offer

The letter arrived the way bad weather arrives in Rivergate: first a smell, then a pressure, then the thing itself.

Ilse Marchetti read it twice by lamplight in the back room of the Saltcellar, where old Dov Hark ran his smuggling correspondence between barrels of salted herring and crates of nothing at all. The paper was heavy, cream-colored, watermarked with something she couldn't make out without holding it to the flame. The handwriting was careful, educated, the kind of hand that had learned its letters from tutors rather than ledger clerks.

*Madam — I am told you are a woman who understands that some things are worth more than their weight. I have come a long way regarding a matter of Vessian interest. I will be at the Saltcellar tomorrow evening. Bring discretion; I will bring gratitude.*

No name. No seal. But the phrase *Vessian interest* sat on the page like a fishhook under the skin, and Ilse Marchetti — born Ilsabet Marchetti-Vessin, last surviving daughter of a house that no longer existed on any map worth trusting — felt the hook set.

She had spent forty years being no one in particular. A merchant's widow in Corvane's capital, then a widow again in the river towns, then simply a woman with good boots and better manners who asked questions in the right taverns and paid for her answers in coin nobody could trace. The network called her the Seamstress, because she stitched together what the flood of history had torn apart: names, loyalties, the scattered threads of a nation that had been drowned twice over — once in water, once in ink.

And now someone in Rivergate had pulled something out of that water. Dov Hark's letters were usually reliable, and this one was practically singing.

* * *

Rivergate smelled of rot and diesel and rain that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it. Ilse walked its plank streets in the late afternoon with an umbrella she didn't open, letting the humid air press against her face like a damp cloth. She had dressed down for the occasion — gray wool, plain brooch, the wedding ring of a fictional husband — but she carried herself the way she always did, spine straight as a mast, and she knew how that read in a place like this. Out of place. Worth watching.

The Saltcellar was a low-beamed tavern built on pilings over the brown water, and it stank of tallow and pipe smoke and the particular sourness of men who drank before noon because the morning had already beaten them. Dov Hark himself was behind the bar, a slab of a man gone soft at the edges, wiping glasses that never got clean.

"You're not my usual trade," he said when she took a stool.

"Your usual trade doesn't pay."

"That's true enough." He leaned in. "You know what this is about?"

"I know what your letter said."

"My letter said what they told me to say." He wiped the bar in slow circles. "Woman named Galloway. Salvage diver. Pulled a chest off the *Vessant* two weeks back. Navy's been crawling up and down this stretch ever since, asking soft questions with hard eyes. There's a Lieutenant Corvane running permits through every dock office between here and the falls." He paused. "You know the *Vessant* was supposed to be somewhere else entirely. Official record says she went down off Cape Merren in the war year. Somebody moved the wreck. Somebody wanted it forgotten."

"I know," Ilse said quietly. "I've known for thirty years where it wasn't."

Dov grunted. "Then you know more than me. All I know is Galloway's crew is spooked. And there's another thing." His voice dropped further, until she had to lean across the sticky wood to catch it. "There was a diver. Season before last. Man named Oskar Bren. Worked the deep channels south of here. Came up one day with something in his bag and a look on his face like he'd seen his own ghost. Sold his boat inside a week. Told people he was going inland, going to family."

"And?"

"They found him in the reeds off Marsh Landing. Spring floods brought him up. Navy called it drowning." Dov set the glass down. "Oskar Bren swam these rivers since he was six years old. He didn't drown in three feet of reed water, madam. Somebody held him under."

Ilse kept her face still, the way she'd learned to keep it still through funerals and interrogations and one memorable night in a border town where soldiers burned the inn around her. But inside, something cold turned over. Oskar Bren. She remembered the name now — a name that had come through the network eighteen months ago, a diver who might have found documents, a lead that had gone silent. They had all assumed he'd simply lost his nerve.

The navy had assumed nothing. The navy had made certain.

"Thank you, Dov," she said, and put coin on the bar, more than the information was worth, because she wanted him to remember her as generous rather than memorable.

"Galloway drinks here sometimes," he said as she rose. "Evenings. Quiet woman. Hard hands. You'll know her by the way she watches the door."

Everyone in Rivergate watched the door, Ilse thought. It was the ones who watched the windows you had to fear.

* * *

Elodie Galloway came in at dusk, and Ilse knew her instantly — not by Dov's description but by something older, something in the set of her shoulders. Salvage divers carry themselves like people who have made peace with the dark. They don't flinch at shadows; they've been inside them, working, breathing through a hose while the black pressed on all sides. Elodie crossed the tavern floor the same way, unhurried, her eyes doing a slow circuit of the room before she allowed herself to sit.

Ilse waited until the woman had a drink in front of her, then rose and crossed the floor and sat opposite her without asking, because asking would have given her time to refuse.

"Elodie Galloway," she said. It wasn't a question.

"You've got the advantage of me."

"Ilse Marchetti." She let the name sit there, watching for recognition. There was none — the Marchettis were a merchant family, unremarkable, exactly as intended. "I'm told you're a woman who found something in the river."

Elodie's expression didn't change, but everything behind it did. Ilse watched the shutters go up, brick by brick, behind those river-gray eyes. "I find things in the river most weeks. Bottles. Chain. Once a piano."

"A chest," Ilse said softly. "With a double-star stamped on the lid."

For a long moment the only sound was the creak of the pilings beneath them and the mutter of the river against the pilings' barnacled skirts. Then Elodie said, "Whoever told you that told you wrong. Or told you a story they wanted to believe."

"Madam Galloway." Ilse leaned forward, and now she let a little of the other voice come through, the voice she hadn't used since she was a girl in a palace that was later used as a granary and then a barracks and then a ruin. "I am going to speak plainly, because we don't have the time for anything else, and because I think you are tired of being lied to by everyone with a uniform. That chest belonged to my country. My country was erased. Not defeated — erased. Its maps were pulped, its records were burned, its name was struck from treaties like a stain scrubbed out of linen. And the people who did the erasing are the same people whose navy is currently crawling up this river looking for what you pulled out of the mud."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Ilse agreed. "But you *did*, the moment I said double-star."

* * *

They talked for two hours, moving once to a corner table when the tavern filled, and Ilse gave her only as much truth as would serve. She was Vessian nobility — that much was true, or true enough; the blood was real even if the title was ash. There were others like her, scattered across three borders, keeping faith with a dead nation. They had money. They had reach. They had waited forty years for a chance to bring Vessia back into the world, and the chest in Elodie Galloway's boathouse was the key to the door.

"We can move it safely," she said. "Across the border, into hands that will honor what it is. You'll be paid — properly paid, not salvage scrap prices. More than you'd make in ten years of dragging corpses out of the mud. And you'll be protected. When the navy comes — and they will come, madam Galloway, they are already coming — our people can make you invisible. New papers. New name. A life on the far side of the map."

"And if I say no?"

Ilse hesitated. This was the part she hated, the part that tasted like a threat even when it was meant as mercy. But Dov Hark's story about Oskar Bren needed telling, because it was the only honest currency she had left.

"Then I'll tell you what happened to the last man who found what you found," she said. "A diver. Oskar Bren. He worked the southern channels. Two seasons ago he came up with Vessian papers in his bag, and within the month he was dead in the reeds off Marsh Landing. The navy called it drowning. He'd been swimming these waters since boyhood." She held Elodie's gaze. "They killed him for less than you're carrying. They will kill you and your whole crew and call it drowning too, or fire, or bandits, and no one will ask twice, because no one asks questions about people who touch Vessian things. That is what you're holding, madam Galloway. Not treasure. A lit fuse."

Something moved behind Elodie's eyes — not fear, Ilse noted, or not only fear. Calculation. The look of a woman weighing weights.

"You talk about protection," Elodie said slowly. "Your people, across the border. Tell me honestly — if I hand that chest over, what happens to me afterward? Do I get my money and my new name and a quiet life? Or do I become a loose end, the same way your navy friends see loose ends?"

It was a knife of a question, and Ilse felt it slide in exactly where it was aimed, because the honest answer was the one she could not give. The network needed the documents. The network did not, strictly speaking, need the diver. She thought of the orders waiting for her in the cipher book in her luggage — *secure the materials by any means; the bearer's welfare is secondary to the cause* — and she thought of Oskar Bren floating in the reeds, and she understood suddenly that she was sitting across from a woman offering to walk into the same trap from the other side.

"The cause comes first," Ilse said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

"Yeah," Elodie said. "That's what I figured." She stood, dropped coins beside her untouched second drink, and looked down at Ilse with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite contempt. "You want to be somebody's instrument, that's your business. I've got a crew to think about. If I decide the chest needs a buyer, I'll find one who wants papers, not martyrs."

She walked out into the blue-dark, and Ilse sat alone at the corner table listening to the river mutter against the pilings, and understood that she had just failed, and understood also — with a clarity that frightened her more than any navy patrol — that she was glad she had failed.

Then she finished her wine, went upstairs to her room, and opened the cipher book, and sent the message that would bring others. Others who were not glad. Others who did not ask.

By morning, Ilse knew, the network's answer would be moving down the river toward Rivergate. And whatever Elodie Galloway decided about buyers and instruments, the choice was no longer going to be hers alone.
