## 5. Rivergate Locks Down

Sefa heard the river change before she saw the reason for it.

She was up on the *Kestrel's* deck, oiling the winch cable in the grey half-light before dawn, when the foghorn sounded from the bend at Tarrow's Reach — two long blasts, then a short one, repeated. Not a commercial signal. Not a flood warning. Sefa had grown up on this water and knew its grammar the way other people knew their mother tongue, and what it said now was: *closed*.

By full light there were three navy launches strung across the channel below the old toll bridge, hull to hull, with a chain boom sagging between them and buoys painted in the harsh yellow of quarantine. A fourth launch sat upstream at the weir, and soldiers in wet-weather capes moved along both banks setting up canvas shelters, tripods, signal lamps. The rain had started around midnight, a thin insistent drizzle that made everything glisten and made the soldiers' capes shine like beetle shells.

Sefa stood at the rail with the oily rag forgotten in her hand and watched Rivergate become something it had not been in her lifetime: a locked room.

Below decks, the chest lay open on the chart table where Elodie had left it, and the documents inside it — brittle, foxed, smelling of forty years of cold silt — were stacked in three bundles tied with sail twine. Sefa had looked at them only once, properly. She had read the way some people pray: quickly, silently, asking nothing. What she'd seen had names in it. Names she knew from the news sheets, from the faces on the customs house wall, from coins. That was the trouble with the chest. It didn't hold treasure. It held people, still breathing somehow, still dangerous, drowned men walking around in ink.

Tamir wanted to sell them. Elodie hadn't decided anything yet; Elodie went quiet when she was deciding, like deep water going still before it moves. And Sefa — Sefa wanted them gone. Not sold, not kept. Gone. Burned, sunk, scattered. Some things were too heavy to carry and too heavy to put down, and the only mercy was to let the river have them.

Now the river was closed, and the choice was being taken out of all their hands.

* * *

The notice went up on the customs house door by mid-morning, and Sefa read it twice because the first time her eyes refused to cooperate.

**BY ORDER OF THE RIVER AND HARBOUR AUTHORITY:** All vessels subject to inspection. Any person found in possession of property stolen from a government salvage site will be detained under emergency statute. REWARD: 500 crowns for information leading to recovery of said property. Report to any naval officer.

*Stolen government property.* As if the Vessant had ever been theirs to lose. As if forty years of silence had been stewardship instead of burial. Sefa copied the words into her memory the way she copied channel markers, without opinion, because opinions were a luxury and she had never been able to afford many.

It was Tamir who said it out loud, standing beside her in the market crowd, his breath sour with cheap coffee. "Five hundred crowns." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "That's what we're worth. Each."

"Each?" Sefa said.

"Read it again. Information leading to recovery. There are four of us on that boat." He didn't look at her when he said it. That was how she knew he'd already done the arithmetic about himself.

* * *

They came for the *Kestrel* on the third day.

Sefa was alone aboard. Elodie had gone upriver to see a man about fuel, or so she said, though everyone understood she'd gone to feel out which way the wind blew among the smugglers and fish-buyers who knew things. Tamir was ashore too, and so the knock on the hull — three measured raps, gloved knuckles on wood — found only Sefa, mending a net in the wheelhouse with her hands doing the work while her mind screamed somewhere far above itself.

There were six of them. Four sailors to handle the lines, a young lieutenant with rain beading on his peaked cap, and behind him, filling the companionway like weather coming in, Lieutenant Corvane himself. Up close he was younger than his voice suggested over the loudhailer, and tired around the eyes in a way that no amount of discipline could iron flat. He took off his gloves before he stepped aboard, which Sefa would think about later, long after. A man who removed his gloves to search your home was either courteous or ashamed, and she couldn't decide which frightened her more.

"Routine inspection," the young lieutenant said, showing papers that meant nothing and everything. "Hold, ballast compartments, living quarters. You'll remain on deck."

"Of course," Sefa said.

Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. Inside, her heart was going like a bilge pump in a storm, and her knees had turned to something warm and unreliable, but her voice — her quiet, unremarkable, easily-overlooked voice — held.

Because here is what nobody knew about Sefa, what even Elodie only half-knew: Sefa had spent her whole life being the one nobody watched. Small, silent, quick with rope and engine and nothing else, the crew member visitors forgot within a minute of meeting. She had spent twenty-nine years learning exactly how invisible a person could make herself, and now, with soldiers pulling up her floorboards, she discovered that invisibility was not the same as emptiness. Underneath all that quiet there had been accumulating, all these years, an enormous pressure of things unsaid. And pressure, given a direction, becomes strength.

The night before, while Tamir snored and Elodie paced the deck under the drizzle, Sefa had done the thing she'd known since the checkpoints went up she would have to do. She had opened the ballast limber along the port side — the narrow flooded channel where the scrap iron sat to keep the *Kestrel* honest in the water — and she had wrapped the three bundles of documents in waxed canvas, greased it with tallow until it shone, weighted it with a piece of lead sheeting, and lowered it down among the iron pigs, arranging the scrap over it by hand, piece by piece, until the ballast looked like nothing but ballast. Then she'd pumped a few inches of river water over the top, because river water smells like river water, and river water was everywhere on a salvage boat, explaining everything, excusing everything.

It was not a clever hiding place. It was a true one. The documents were now, genuinely, part of the boat's dead weight — the thing that kept her steady. Sefa had thought about that as she worked, lying on her side in the dark bilge with the smell of iron and tallow in her nose. There was probably a lesson in it. She was too frightened to work out what.

* * *

They searched for two hours.

They went through the forepeak and the lazarette. They pulled the net bins apart and prodded the fuel tanks with a sounding rod. They found the false bottom under the chart table — every salvage boat had one, it was practically a courtesy — and were visibly disappointed to find only tools and a bottle of arrack that the young lieutenant confiscated with a straight face. They made Sefa stand on deck in the rain while they did it, and she watched through the open hatch as strangers' hands moved through the intimate geography of her home, lifting her spare clothes, reading the spines of her paperbacks, opening the tin where she kept her mother's letters.

Something in her chest hurt worse than fear. It was the specific ache of being violated politely.

Corvane did not search. He walked. He moved through the boat slowly, touching nothing, looking at everything, and twice Sefa caught him looking at her — not the way the young lieutenant looked at her, as furniture, but the way you look at a knot you can't quite read. Once he stopped at the mainmast and rested his hand against it, and asked, conversationally, to no one in particular:

"How much ballast does she carry?"

"Nine hundred pounds, scrap iron," Sefa said. "Port and starboard limbers."

"And she sits right?"

"She sits true." *Please,* she thought, with a clarity that felt almost holy. *Please. I have never asked anyone for anything. Ask me for anything else.*

Corvane looked at the waterline for a long moment. The *Kestrel* floated exactly as she always floated, the boot-top even, the scuppers dry. Whatever he saw, he saw nothing wrong with it, because there was nothing wrong with it to see. Nine hundred pounds of iron plus three pounds of paper, wrapped in tallow and lies.

He put his gloves back on.

"You've been diving the Vessant," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"We dive everything out here," Sefa said. "Wrecks pay better than fish."

"The Vessant especially?"

"It pays best. She's shallow and she's rich. Everyone dives her." This was true, and Sefa had learned the oldest trick of lying, which was to hide inside truth the way she hid inside silence. "You'd know that better than us, sir. Your dredges have been working this stretch for years. We've seen your barges."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — quick, quickly buried, but Sefa was a connoisseur of buried things. It looked like confirmation. It looked like a man hearing his own suspicion spoken aloud by a stranger and finding no comfort in it.

"Thank you for your cooperation," he said, and gave the order to go, and then, halfway to the ladder, stopped. Turned. Looked at her once more, directly, and said:

"If someone offered you five hundred crowns for something you had, would you take it?"

Sefa's hands, folded around her mending, did not move. Her knees shook where no one could see them, below the rail, below the waterline, down in the dark where the ballast lived.

"I don't own anything worth five hundred crowns," she said.

"No," Corvane said softly. "I don't suppose you do." And he went down the ladder, and the launch pulled away, and Sefa stood at the rail until it was a smudge in the rain, holding herself upright by the pure mechanical refusal of her legs to quit.

* * *

That night, when Elodie came back and heard the report, she sat down on the chart table across from Sefa and looked at her for a long time.

"You lied to a navy officer," Elodie said finally. "In his face. On my boat."

"Yes."

"To protect the chest."

"To protect the crew," Sefa said. "The chest is just iron and paper. We're the ones who hang if it goes wrong."

Elodie laughed — one short breath of it, surprised out of her — and reached over and gripped Sefa's shoulder, hard, once, the way you grip a hawser you're trusting your weight to. "Load-bearing," she said. "That's what you are now. Don't tell anyone. It'll ruin your reputation."

But later, when the lamp was out and the rain ticked on the deck above, Sefa lay awake listening to the river push against the hull, and beneath her, inches from her spine through the planking, the documents rode in the dark among the iron pigs, patient as sediment.

And she thought about the flicker on Corvane's face. About a man who took his gloves off to search a boat, and asked strange questions, and looked at the waterline like he was checking whether it told the truth.

She thought: *he knows something. And whatever he knows, he doesn't like it.*

And she thought, with a dread that settled into her bones colder than the bilge water: *the monsoon is coming, and when it comes, this river is going to want its cargo back.*
