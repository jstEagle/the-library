## 6. Tamir's Price

The debt came to Tamir the way the river fog came to Rivergate — silently, in the dark, settling into everything he owned until nothing was dry.

He kept the letter folded inside his boot, where he'd carried it for eleven days. He had read it so many times he no longer needed to unfold it, but he did anyway, each night, by candlelight when Sefa slept and Elodie was out on the water or pacing her boathouse with that look she got, the one like a woman listening to a door nobody else could hear knocking.

*Outstanding balance: 4,200 crowns. Failure to remit within thirty days will result in transfer of account to the Sanatorium Recovery Office.*

He knew what the Recovery Office meant. Everyone in the river towns knew. They took the debt out of a person's labor, and a person's labor at the sanatorium was measured in years, and the years were measured out in the wet wards where the fever patients coughed their lungs into linen rags. Mira was nine years old. Nine years old and already owing four thousand crowns because the fever that nearly took her had chosen to be expensive on its way out.

Tamir sat in the stern of the *Magpie* with the ledger open across his knees and let himself hate it — the ledger, the chest, the double-star seal, the whole drowned country that had come up out of the mud to ruin him a second time.

* * *

He told himself it would be simple.

That was the lie he built first, and he built it well, because Tamir had always been good at building things — hulls, cradles, stories. The ledger was only paper. Paper could be copied. A copy was not the thing itself; Elodie would still have her chest, her secret, whatever it was she thought she was protecting. He would sell the navy a photograph of paper, not the truth, and with the money he would pay the sanatorium down to something survivable, and Mira would come home in the spring, and no one would ever have to know.

He waited until Elodie went upriver to meet the smuggler woman's contact — three hours, maybe four, if the wind held contrary. Sefa was ashore buying pitch and lamp oil, moving through the market with that careful quietness, counting coins twice. The *Magpie* sat alone at her mooring, rocking in the brown water, patient as a confessor.

Tamir photographed the ledger pages with the little brass camera he'd bought off a dead man's estate two winters ago, back when he'd imagined a future with more use for it. It was slow work. Each plate needed light and stillness. He spread the pages on the deck boards beneath the awning and knelt over them like a man praying to something he didn't believe in, and the shutter clicked, and clicked, and clicked.

The names looked up at him from every page. Admirals. Commissioners. A harbor master whose signature Tamir recognized from the tax stamps on his own boat papers. Men who had signed their names to executions forty years ago and gone on signing other, kinder documents ever since — fishing quotas, bridge contracts, the permits Lieutenant Corvane carried in his leather case.

Forty years, and the ink hadn't even faded properly.

He finished the last page as the light went copper on the water, and he wrapped the plates in oilcloth and put them in the false bottom of his tool chest, and his hands were steady the whole time, which frightened him more than shaking would have.

* * *

Corvane's message came back within a day, which told Tamir how badly the navy wanted what they wanted. A time, a place, a single word written in a clerk's careful hand: *Alone.*

The place was the customs shed below the coal wharf, the one with the warped door and the smell of creosote soaked so deep into the timber it seemed to sweat it. Tamir arrived early, because a man walking into a trap should at least choose his own hour. Rain had been threatening all afternoon without committing, and the air lay on the river like a wet cloth.

Corvane was already there. Of course he was. He stood by the window with his hands clasped behind him, uniform immaculate despite the heat, looking out at the gray water as though the river were a document he was reading. He did not turn when the door opened.

"Mr. Voss." Corvane used Tamir's boat name, the name painted on the stern, and there was something almost courteous in it. "You'll forgive the venue. I find open-air conversations attract gulls."

"Gulls and witnesses," Tamir said. "I imagine you prefer neither."

Something moved at the corner of Corvane's mouth. Not quite a smile. "You've brought something."

"I've brought an offer." Tamir set his tool chest on the table between them and rested his hand on the lid, not opening it. "Photographs. Every page of the ledger, clear enough to read a signature. You get your copy. The original stays where it is, and the people who found it stay unmentioned. Cash, and a written immunity — for me, my partner, my crew. Signed by someone above your rank."

Corvane turned from the window then, and studied him with those pale, unhurried eyes. "You understand," he said mildly, "that I could simply take the chest."

"You could try. And then you'd have to explain to your superiors why a salvage crew fought back, and why half the river heard it, and why the story got bigger instead of smaller." Tamir had rehearsed this part on the walk down, and it came out steadier than he deserved. "Your whole business here is quiet, Lieutenant. I'm offering to keep it quiet. That's worth more than paper."

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. Then he crossed to the table, drew out a chair with exaggerated care, and sat, folding his hands. "Let us suppose," he said, "that I am authorized to discuss terms."

* * *

It was going well. That was the terrible thing Tamir realized later — it was going exactly as well as he'd planned, numbers being spoken, figures being written, Corvane's pen scratching with bureaucratic patience while somewhere in Tamir's chest a small voice kept saying *Mira, Mira, four thousand crowns, Mira home in spring.*

Then Corvane reached into his case — not for the immunity paper, but for a printed notice, still smelling of fresh ink, and laid it flat on the table to use as a blotter beneath his signature.

Tamir's eyes went to it the way a drowning man's eyes go to light.

**REWARD — STOLEN GOVERNMENT PROPERTY.** The header was official, the border ornamental, the type dense. His gaze slid down it hunting for the figure, the sum, anything — and snagged instead on the lower section, where the notice listed persons sought for questioning.

Three names.

Not one. Not *Tamir Voss.* Three.

Elodie Galloway. Sefa Renn. Tamir Voss.

Printed. Distributed. Already nailed to customs posts from here to the delta, most likely, ink drying on a hundred boards while he sat here negotiating. There had never been an immunity to buy. There had never been a negotiation. Corvane had been filling out paperwork this whole time — the polite, patient paperwork of men who intended to bury a problem, and knew exactly how many shovels they'd need.

"Something interests you?" Corvane asked, without looking up from his signature.

"The reward notice," Tamir said. His voice sounded far away to himself, calm and strange, like hearing his own shout underwater. "It lists three names."

"It lists persons of interest." Corvane finished the signature, blotted it with care, and lifted his eyes. And there it was — the thing under the courtesy, the thing Tamir had been too desperate to see coming. Not cruelty, exactly. Something worse: procedure. "Mr. Voss. May I speak plainly?"

"I think you're about to regardless."

"When this matter concludes, there will be a version of events that requires no witnesses. That is not a threat. It is an observation about how governments survive." Corvane slid the signed immunity across the table, and Tamir saw now that it was real, genuinely signed, genuinely binding — binding Corvane to protect a man the navy had already decided to erase. "Take it. Take the money. In exchange for the photographs, you will be the only name that comes off that list. I can do that much. I have latitude for exactly one name."

"And theirs?"

Corvane's silence answered better than words.

Tamir looked at the paper. Four thousand crowns stood behind it, and behind the crowns stood Mira, small and fever-bright, asking him in her last letter whether the river had given them anything good lately. *Anything good, Papa?*

He thought of Elodie standing in the boathouse doorway, saying *we decide together or not at all.* He thought of Sefa hiding the documents in the ballast with hands that shook and never once fumbled. He thought of the ledger's signatures, forty years old, and how the men who'd made them had all, every one, told themselves a story first.

Everyone betrayed somebody. That was the whole architecture of the world. The only choice left was how many.

Tamir closed the tool chest. The click of the latch was very loud in the customs shed.

"No sale," he said.

Corvane watched him, and for just a moment something flickered behind the pale eyes — not respect, Tamir thought, but recognition, the look of a man watching another man step off a ledge he himself was standing on. "You understand what happens now."

"I understand what happens now."

He picked up the chest and walked out through the warped door into the thickening dark, and behind him he heard Corvane say, quietly, to no one, "So do I."

* * *

The rain finally came as Tamir climbed the wharf road, fat drops striking the dust like thrown stones. He didn't hurry. He walked through it with the empty tool chest under his arm and the unsigned nothing in his heart, and by the time he reached the *Magpie* he was soaked through and shaking — not from cold.

There had been a moment, kneeling over the ledger with the camera clicking, when he had felt powerful. Clever. A man solving his problems with his hands, the way he fixed engines, the way he patched hulls. Now he understood what he'd actually been doing: drilling a hole in the bottom of the boat and calling it maintenance.

He had to tell them. Tonight, before Corvane's patience ran out, before the checkpoints tightened, before the reward notices sprouted like rot on every post in Rivergate. He had to stand in front of Elodie and Sefa and say *I photographed the ledger, I met the lieutenant, I meant to sell you both* — and watch their faces do what faces do.

Sefa would forgive him. That was worse than anger.

And Elodie — Elodie would look at him the way she looked at the water when it gave up its dead, and she would decide something, and whatever she decided, the crew would follow, because that was how it worked, because trust once cracked only holds if someone binds it fast and keeps bailing.

Tamir stowed the chest, lit the lantern, and sat down to wait for her boat to come around the bend, watching the rain stitch silver holes in the black river.

Upstream, faint through the rain, he heard it: engines. More than one. Running heavy, running slow, the way boats run when they are carrying something they don't want the river to hear.

They weren't Corvane's patrol craft. Tamir knew those engines like his own heartbeat.

These were strangers' engines, and they were headed for Rivergate.
