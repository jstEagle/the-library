## 7. Monsoon Rising

The river told you before the sky did. Three days out from the monsoon, and already the current had changed its mind about Rivergate — running faster, browner, thick with silt it had stolen from the high country. Elodie stood on the deck of the *Maren* and watched a whole tree come down the channel, roots first, spinning slowly like something drowning, and thought: *that could be us.*

"Two days," Tamir said beside her. "Maybe three. The rapids at Sorrow's Bend will go from bad to suicide by tomorrow night."

"Then we run them tomorrow night."

He looked at her the way he always did when she said something reckless — not with argument, because he'd learned that argument came later and cost more, but with the flat, tired patience of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "The chest goes with us."

"The chest goes with us."

"And after the rapids?"

She didn't answer. That was the truth of it: she had a plan for the running and no plan for the arriving. South past the checkpoints, down to the delta, and then — what? Sell it to Marchetti and trust a woman whose smile never reached her eyes? Burn it and spend the rest of her life wondering what she'd drowned? Every road out of Rivergate led through somebody's hands, and every pair of hands wanted the chest for a different reason, and not one of them wanted *her*.

Below deck, Sefa was sewing lead shot into canvas bags, adding weight low in the hull where the ballast sat. The documents were in there now, wrapped in oilcloth and tarred canvas, tucked between the ingots. If the navy boarded them, the chest on deck was a decoy — old brass fittings, a false bottom, nothing inside but river stones and a Vessian double-star painted fresh to catch the light. Sefa had painted the stars herself, kneeling in the bilge, tongue between her teeth, and when she'd finished she'd looked up at Elodie and said, "I hate that I'm good at this."

The sky over the drowned town had gone the color of old pewter. Somewhere upstream, thunder spoke in a language Elodie almost understood.

* * *

Marchetti found her at the Salt Latch, the dockside tavern where the lamps burned low and the rain had started — not the monsoon, not yet, but a rehearsal, a long gray drizzle that made everything smell of rust and wet rope.

Ilse Marchetti sat in the back booth like she owned the deed to it. Silk the color of storm water, a ring on one finger worth more than the *Maren*, and that same patient smile. Elodie slid into the seat across from her and put her hands flat on the table where Marchetti could see them.

"You look tired, Captain Galloway."

"I look like a woman with a navy lieutenant counting her breaths and a river about to eat her boat. You look like a woman who's about to make me an offer I should refuse."

Marchetti laughed — a real laugh, brief and warm, and that was the frightening thing about her. She could be warm. She chose it the way she chose everything. "The border crossing is arranged. Two hundred miles south, past the last checkpoint, there's a ferry landing at Vell's Ford. My people will meet you there. You'll be paid in full — more than full. And you and your crew will have papers, new names, passage anywhere in the world. No navy. no debts. No Rivergate."

"No Vessia either," Elodie said. "Because there isn't one. There's just you, and whatever you're planning to build out of forty-year-old bones."

The smile thinned but held. "A nation isn't bones, Captain. It's a claim. And a claim needs proof."

"Proof." Elodie leaned forward. "Here's what I can't stop turning over, Marchetti. The Vessant carried exiles. Officials. People your government-in-exile has been mourning for forty years. And the navy executed them instead of sending them home. That's a crime. That's a *scandal*. So why does everyone who touches those papers end up dead or hunted? If the truth serves your cause, why does the truth have a body count?"

For a moment — just a moment — the warmth went out of Marchetti's face like a lamp turned down. What was underneath was older and much less kind.

"Because," Marchetti said, "the ledger doesn't only name navy men."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere behind the bar, a bottle broke and nobody cheered.

"The collaborators," Elodie said slowly. "The ones who helped the navy find the Vessian officials. The ones who signed the orders, or looked away, or took the money. Some of them—"

"Were Vessian," Marchetti said. "Yes. Some of the names in that ledger are names my people have been using for forty years. Honored names. Names on our committees, our funding letters, our claim to legitimacy." She turned the ring on her finger, once, a full rotation, like a woman winding a watch. "If those papers come to light the way they are — unedited, unexplained — they don't just hang the navy. They hang us. So they won't come to light the way they are. Certain pages will be removed. Certain names will be... clarified. And the rest will burn Vessa back into existence on the strength of the navy's guilt alone."

"And the crew of the *Maren*? The divers who pulled it up? The partner, the deckhand, the man with the sick daughter?"

"They'll be compensated. Generously."

"That's not what I asked."

Marchetti met her eyes, and Elodie had to give her this: she didn't lie. She simply told the truth the way a knife tells the truth. "Captain. You've seen what the navy does to people who know. You've seen the checkpoints, the reward notices, the diver they pulled out of the water at Graymoss with his pockets full of nothing. You are a woman who knows things. Knowing things is a condition. It doesn't go away. The only question is whether the condition is managed... or whether it manages itself." She spread her hands, almost gentle. "I would rather manage it. I'm offering you a life. My colleagues would offer you a shorter alternative. I'm the moderate in this room, Elodie. Understand that. I am the *kind* one."

Elodie sat very still. The tavern noise went on around her — dice, argument, the hiss of the lamp — and she felt the bottom drop out of the last thing she'd been holding. There was no buyer. There had never been a buyer. There was only the navy, which wanted the truth dead, and the exiles, who wanted it *edited*, and her crew caught between them like the tree in the current, spinning, going under.

"And if I refuse?" she asked. "If I run the rapids tomorrow night and keep the chest?"

"Then you're a woman running rapids at night in monsoon water with the two most dangerous organizations in the world behind you," Marchetti said. She stood, smoothed her silk, dropped a coin on the table — too much, always too much, the generosity of people who spend other people's lives. "I'll make one more offer at Vell's Ford. After that, Captain, the offer stops being mine."

She left. Elodie sat in the booth a long time, watching the rain crawl down the window, and felt something in her chest stop wobbling and set, like a bone going back into joint. Fear had been running her for days — fear of Corvane, fear of Marchetti, fear of the water. But fear was just another current, and currents could be read.

There was no safe buyer. Fine. Then the truth would have no buyer at all.

* * *

She told them in the boathouse, by lamplight, with the rain finally coming down hard enough to drum the roof like a fist. Tamir. Sefa. Old Piet from the next slip, who'd heard everything and pretended he hadn't, and who now pulled his chair into the circle without being asked, because Piet had never once pretended not to be curious.

"Marchetti will kill us," Elodie said. "Maybe not tomorrow. Maybe she pays us first and we drown in a canal a year later. But she'll kill us, and worse — she'll use the papers to build her country out of lies, and she'll call it justice while she does it. The navy wants the truth dead. The exiles want it wearing a mask. And I'm done choosing between two ways of losing it."

Sefa spoke first, quiet as always, her needle still moving through canvas. "So we burn it."

"No." Tamir, and his voice cracked on it, and everyone let him take a breath before he went on. "No. I've seen what's in that ledger. I've read the names. My daughter's doctor is on it, Elodie. The man who fixed her heart took Vessian money to point at his own neighbors, and he sits in a clinic in the capital and people *thank* him. If we burn it, that man dies old and admired." He looked around the lamplight, jaw working. "I was going to sell a copy. To Corvane. I was going to—"

"We know," Sefa said gently.

"—and I didn't, because the reward notice had three names on it and one of them was mine, and I understood something standing in that office. Nobody buying this truth wants it whole. But the river doesn't want anything. The river doesn't edit." He turned to Elodie. "You said the river doesn't edit?"

"I said it last night. Drunk."

"I'm saying it sober." Tamir leaned forward, and there was something in his face Elodie had never seen there — not desperation, not debt, but conviction, arrived late and all the harder for it. "Copy the three worst names. The ones even Marchetti can't explain away. Keep them. Mail them slow, from far away, one town at a time, so nobody knows where they came from. Then feed the rest to the flood. Let the navy lose its secret and the exiles lose their mask, and let the three names do their work in the dark for the next twenty years. Nobody owns the truth. But three names — three names can *leak*."

The rain hammered. The lamp guttered and steadied. Piet said, into the silence, "Sorrow's Bend, tomorrow night, at flood stage. In the dark. With the navy upstream and the exiles at the ford." He cracked his knuckles, one hand, then the other. "I've run worse in better weather. Well. Once."

Sefa set down her needle and looked at Elodie. "The ballast holds the papers. The decoy holds the stars. What do you want the river to hold?"

Elodie looked at each of them — Tamir with his debts and his new spine, Sefa with her steady hands, Piet with his one worse run — and felt the strange, hard peace of a decision already made, just waiting for her to catch up to it.

"The river holds everything," she said. "Tomorrow night we give it the whole truth. All of it. And then we run."

Outside, the thunder that had been speaking all evening finally said something in a language everyone understood, and the rain came down like the sky meant to finish what the navy started — and Elodie Galloway began, in her head, counting the hours to Sorrow's Bend.
