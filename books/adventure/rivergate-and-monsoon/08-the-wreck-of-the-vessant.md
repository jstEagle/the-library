## 8. The Wreck of the Vessant

The order came down in a sealed pouch, as the worst orders always did, and Corvane read it three times before the words stopped rearranging themselves into something he could live with.

*Demolition authorized. Site to be rendered unsalvageable. All persons with knowledge of recovery operations to be accounted for.*

Accounted for. In forty years of reading navy paperwork, he had never once seen that phrase mean anything good. It was the language men used when they wanted a thing done and wanted their names nowhere near the doing. The pouch also contained a slim folder he had not requested — a courtesy, or a test, or a trap — stamped with the double-star of the old Vessian republic, its wax broken and resealed so carelessly that whoever had done it had not even bothered to match the halves.

He should have burned it unread. That was the correct procedure. That was what Lieutenant Commander Aldous Renner, the man he had been at twenty-six, would have done: feed the file to the stove, sign the receipt, and sleep the sleep of the obedient.

Corvane was fifty-one now, and he read it.

* * *

The file was thin. That was the first obscenity. Forty years of history — a nation drowned in ink as thoroughly as its officials had been drowned in the river — and it fit in twenty pages. He read it by lamplight in the wardroom of the *Kestrel*, while outside the rain came down in long gray sheets and the sentry's boots squelched past the porthole on his rounds.

The *Vessant* had not been lost. That much he had already suspected, standing in Elodie Galloway's boathouse with his soft questions and his finder's fee, watching her eyes do arithmetic behind a face that gave him nothing. But suspicion was a fog, and the file was a lamp. The corvette had been intercepted on the night of the fourth of Stormmonth, carrying eleven Vessian officials who had surrendered under guarantee of repatriation. Guarantee given. Guarantee withdrawn by morning signal, on authority of a name Corvane knew — a name that now sat in the capital, old and honored, on committees, on portraits, on the currency itself.

The *Vessant* had been taken to the deep channel and scuttled with her passengers aboard. The divers who did the work had been transferred to a station that no longer existed. The logbook of the intercepting vessel had been burned, except that a clerk with a conscience had copied the relevant pages before the burning, and the copy had traveled, hand to hand, exile to exile, until it came to rest in a strongbox lashed to the wreck itself — the one place on earth the navy could be certain no honest searcher would ever look.

Until a salvage diver with strong hands and a stronger instinct had gone down into the dark and brought it up into the light.

Corvane set the file down. His hands were steady. He noticed that, and was ashamed of noticing it, the way a man is ashamed of noticing his own heartbeat during a funeral.

*Accounted for.* Galloway. Her partner, Tamir — a name that had surfaced in his inquiries, a man with debts. The deckhand, Sefa. And the woman Marchetti, whose network he had been ordered to ignore, which was its own kind of confession.

He had spent his career believing the navy was a rough instrument that nonetheless pointed true — that behind the blunders and the politics, the thing itself was sound, a keel laid straight. He had told himself this the way men tell themselves anything: often, and in a comfortable voice, and always just before sleeping.

The keel was rotten. It had been rotten for forty years, and he had been caulking it with his obedience.

* * *

They went out at first light, or what passed for it under the monsoon — a bruised half-dark, the river the color of gunmetal, rain hissing on the water like oil in a pan. The demolition party was four men and a launch, and Corvane commanded it because he had insisted, because he would not send boys to do a thing he could not watch with his own eyes.

The wreck lay in the deep channel below the old ferry landing, forty feet down in water so thick with silt it was less water than weather. The divers — his divers, navy-trained, faces gray above their masks — planted the charges along the hull with the unhurried competence of men laying out a table. Corvane stood in the stern and watched the buoys mark the pattern: a ring of death, neat as a garden bed.

*Rendered unsalvageable.* The *Vessant* was already a grave. Eleven people had died in her, bound or drowned or both, and now the navy would blow the grave apart so that no one could ever read the headstone. He thought of Galloway's hands — he had seen them once, scarred and capable, wrapping a rope — bringing up the chest through forty feet of cold dark, and he thought: *she carried them up. She carried the dead up out of the water, and we are sending them back down.*

"Charges set, sir," said the bosun. "Ready on your word."

The rain came down. The river moved under it, patient, enormous, keeping everything it was ever given.

"Stand by," Corvane said.

He looked at the detonator box in the bosun's hands. He looked at the water. Somewhere under that gunmetal skin lay the proof that his country had murdered eleven men under a flag of truce, and that the man who ordered it had a statue in the capital square, and that Corvane himself had spent three decades polishing the statue's boots.

"Sir?" the bosun said. "Word, sir?"

And that was when the shooting started.

* * *

It came from the camp ashore first — three flat cracks, then a ragged volley, then the shriek of a whistle and men shouting, and Corvane turned to see smoke rising from behind the naval storehouse, gray against the gray, and figures moving on the ridge above the camp with the loose, practiced gait of people who had done violence in many countries. Marchetti's people. They had not come to negotiate. They had come while the garrison was thin, while the launch and its best men were out on the water, and they had come armed for a war the navy had not believed possible.

"Sir, the camp —"

"I know." His mind was suddenly, horribly clear, the way it went clear in the worst moments, all the fog burned off. "Half of you, back to the launch. We're going in to support."

"And the charges, sir?"

He looked at the buoys. The ring of them, bobbing in the rain. Under them, the *Vessant*. Above them, the truth, and the order to destroy it.

Every instinct he had, every year of drill and deference, said: *blow it. Blow it now, while the camp burns and no one watches, and the order is fulfilled, and you are a man who follows orders in a navy that has orders to follow. Then go ashore and fight, and perhaps die, for the honor of the flag that buried the dead twice.*

The file lay in his cabin aboard the *Kestrel*, and he thought of it sitting there in the lamplight, twenty pages thin, heavier than any cargo he had ever carried.

"Leave the charges," Corvane said.

The bosun stared at him. "Sir?"

"The demolition is suspended. That's an order. If anyone asks, the weather made the operation unsafe — current's too strong for accurate placement, and I'll sign whatever paper says so." He was already moving to the launch's wheel. "Get us ashore. Our people are dying."

The bosun looked at the buoys, at the captain, at the rain, and — this was the thing Corvane would remember all his life — the bosun simply nodded, and turned the detonator box over to the deckhand, and took his place at the lines. As if some part of him had been waiting for the order. As if obedience, once it breaks, breaks clean.

The launch came about through the rain. Behind them the buoys bobbed in their ring, guarding a grave they would not open and would not close, and Corvane stood at the wheel with the river spray in his face and understood that he had just chosen, in the space of a single sentence, which man he would be for the rest of his life — however long the navy, or Marchetti, or the truth allowed it to be.

Ahead, the camp burned. And through the smoke, on the water below the ridge, he caught a glimpse of something that stopped his breath: a salvage boat, running dark, slipping out from the boathouse channel toward the rapids — Galloway, running the river on the worst night of the year, with whatever she carried lashed below decks.

Marchetti's people would have seen her too.

"More speed," Corvane said, and pushed the throttle to its stop.
