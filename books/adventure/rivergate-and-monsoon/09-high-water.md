## 9. High Water

The monsoon did not arrive. It *landed* — a wall of sound and water that came down the valley like something with intent, and by midnight the Rivergate rapids had stopped being rapids and become a single roaring throat.

Elodie stood at the wheel of the *Maren* and could not see the river. She could hear it — a sound like the whole drowned town speaking at once, every cellar and church bell and sunken street giving up its voice — but the rain erased everything past the bow lamp, which threw a cone of yellow into the dark and found nothing in it but water moving too fast to be believed.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Sefa shouted from beside her, both hands white on the rail.

"Because the navy seals the river at dawn," Elodie shouted back, "and because Marchetti's people are ashore right now shooting at Corvane's, and neither of them is looking at the water."

That was the truth, or most of it. Through the rain, upstream and to the east, muzzle flashes stuttered along the navy camp's perimeter — orange blooms swallowed instantly by the downpour. The exiles had come for their documents the same night the storm came for the town. Two wolves at one door, and the *Maren* slipping out through the flood while they tore at each other.

The chest sat amidships under an oilcloth, lashed to the deck where the ballast hatch opened. Tamir crouched over it like a man guarding a coffin. He hadn't spoken since they cast off. His daughter's name was written inside his hatband, and Elodie had seen him touch it twice before they cleared the boathouse, the way you'd touch a charm you no longer quite believed in.

"First chute in four hundred meters!" she called. "Everyone low! Sefa — kill the lamp when I say!"

The river took them before she finished the sentence.

* * *

There was no steering in it. That was the thing no one told you about high water: you didn't run the rapids, you *surrendered* to them and made small corrections, the way a leaf makes corrections. The *Maren* rose on a swell that shouldn't have existed this far inland, hung at the top of it with her propeller singing free, and dropped.

Water came over the bow in a solid green sheet. It hit Elodie like a collapsed roof, tore the wheel half out of her grip, filled her mouth with the taste of silt and drowned timber. Somewhere below them lay Rivergate itself — the streets she'd walked as a girl, now forty years under this same river — and for one disorienting moment she felt the boat was diving toward them, that the whole town would rise up around her, lit windows and all, and ask her what she'd brought back.

"Bollard!" Sefa screamed, pointing.

A navigation piling from the old channel, black and splintered, standing out of the flood like a broken tooth. Elodie hauled the wheel hard over. The *Maren* answered with a groan of strained planking and slid past it close enough that Tamir could have touched it — did touch it, flinging his arm out as if to push the whole river away.

Then the second chute, and the third, and Elodie stopped counting and started simply surviving, reading the water by feel through the wheel, by the way the hull shuddered before each drop, by the sound Sefa made — a thin continuous note, not quite a scream — that turned out to be the most reliable depth gauge aboard.

They came through the last chute sideways, spun twice in the boil below it, and then the river widened, slowed by a full degree, and there was a moment — just a moment — where the rain thinned enough to show them open water ahead and the far bank a gray suggestion through the murk.

Elodie laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her like the air out of a diver's lungs, ragged and enormous.

"We're through—"

The searchlight hit them like a fist.

* * *

It came from downstream, from the navy picket boat that had been holding the lower channel all week — the one boat Corvane couldn't call off, because Corvane wasn't answering his radio anymore. The light pinned the *Maren* against the rain, and through it Elodie saw the patrol boat swing its bow toward them, saw figures on its deck, heard the amplified voice crackle and dissolve in the storm:

"—heave to—this is a naval vessel—heave to or we will fire—"

"They'll sink us," Sefa said. Not a question. "They can't let us reach the border with that chest."

Tamir moved first. He didn't move toward cover; he moved toward the chest, throwing himself across the ballast hatch, spreading his body over the oilcloth as if his spine were worth more than the wood beneath it. Which, Elodie understood in the same instant, was exactly what he thought.

"Get down—" she started.

The first shot went wide and sheared off the rail post a hand's width from Sefa's head. The second didn't go wide.

Tamir jerked once, like a man remembering an old injury, and folded down over the hatch. He didn't cry out. He made a sound Elodie had only ever heard from drowning men — a wet, surprised exhale — and then he was lying very still with blood already diluting pink in the rainwater washing the deck.

"TAMIR." Sefa reached him first, hands pressing the wound in his shoulder, in his side — two wounds, three, Elodie couldn't count them through the water sheeting everywhere. "He's breathing. He's breathing, Elodie, get us out of the light—"

Elodie put the wheel over and drove the *Maren* straight at the near bank, into the shadow of the drowned willows that lined the old towpath, killing the lamp as she went. Behind them the patrol boat's searchlight swung, hunting. A third shot cracked somewhere above her head and was gone.

And then a different engine note entered the world — deeper, closer, coming fast from downstream — and the patrol boat's light swung away from them entirely.

* * *

Later, Elodie would try to reconstruct what she saw through the rain, and never fully succeed. But this much was certain: the second boat came out of the dark flying no lights at all until the last possible moment, and when it finally showed itself, it put itself directly between the *Maren* and the navy picket, and its single lamp blinked three times. Slow. Deliberate. A signal she'd seen river pilots use for forty years and had never once expected to see from a naval officer.

Corvane.

His voice came over the loudhailer, stripped of all its careful softness, raw as an open wound: "*Maren*, run. Don't look back. Run."

What followed was not a battle so much as a collision of institutions. The picket boat fired on Corvane's launch; Corvane rammed it. In the confusion of the flood — two steel hulls grinding against each other in water running at fifteen knots, both crews fighting the river more than each other — the *Maren* slipped past in the willow shadow, close enough that Elodie saw Corvane at his own wheel, saw him glance once toward her, saw him nod.

Not salute. Nod. The way one working person nods to another across a shared understanding.

She ran. She hated herself for running, and she ran.

Behind them, the searchlight swung wildly, found them, lost them, found the bank instead. There was a sound like a door slamming underwater, and a geyser of spray lifted off the picket boat's stern — Corvane had put them aground on the towpath shoal, bought minutes with tonnage and nerve. Then his launch backed water, turned, and placed itself squarely in the channel behind them, broadside to anything that might follow.

Blocking the door.

"*Go*," the loudhailer said one last time, almost gently, and then the rain closed over everything, and Elodie took the *Maren* down the flooded middle channel at full throttle with Tamir bleeding under Sefa's hands and the chest lashed to a dead man's back, and did not look back, because looking back would have meant seeing whether the door held.

It held long enough. They heard the firing fade. Then they heard nothing at all but the river.

* * *

Two miles downstream, in water deep and slow and brown as tea, Elodie brought the boat about and let her drift.

Sefa looked up from Tamir, who was conscious now — gray-lipped, groaning, alive, his hand still resting on the hatch as if letting go might cost him something. "Why are we stopping? *Why are we stopping?*"

Elodie didn't answer. She knelt at the chest and cut the lashings, and the lock gave way to the pry bar she'd carried since she was nineteen, and the lid came up on everything the drowned town had kept for forty years.

The orders. The execution reports, signed and countersigned, the Vessian officials of the *Vessant* listed like cargo — *repatriation declined, disposition authorized*. The ledger with its columns of names, collaborators, senators, admirals, men whose signatures she'd seen on public buildings all her life. Enough paper to hang a government. Enough paper to crown a new one.

Marchetti wanted it. Somewhere behind them, wounded or victorious, the exile network wanted it badly enough to kill a crew for it. And the navy wanted it buried so completely that they'd mined the wreck of the *Vessant* rather than let it speak.

Both of them were wrong. Both of them had always been wrong. It had taken her the whole length of the river to understand it, and she understood it now with a clarity that felt like fury and freedom in the same breath.

This truth belonged to no flag. It belonged to the people who'd been lied to — and the only way to give it to them without handing it to a new set of masters was to make it impossible for anyone to own.

"Elodie," Sefa whispered. "What are you doing."

"What the river's been trying to do for forty years," Elodie said.

She lifted the first armful of documents — the execution orders, the ones that mattered most, the ones that named the crime — and held them out over the gunwale into the rain.

The river took them. It didn't tear them away so much as accept them, the pages spreading and darkening and sinking, ink blooming off them in gray ghosts, signatures dissolving into the same water that had held Rivergate's dead for four decades. She fed it the ledger next, page by page, tearing them free so they'd scatter wide, so no single net could ever gather them all back. Names she'd risked everything for, names Tamir had bled for, names Corvane had died for — she gave every one of them to the current, and the current carried them north toward the delta and the sea and the whole unowned world.

It should have felt like loss. It felt like setting something down that she'd been carrying since the day she pulled the chest from the *Vessant*'s hold.

When the last page was gone she sat back on her heels, soaked through, shaking, empty-handed, and started to laugh — or maybe to sob; in the rain there was no telling the difference.

Sefa was staring at her, Tamir's blood on her hands. "You just threw it all away."

"No," Elodie said quietly. "I scattered it. There's a difference."

She reached into her coat and drew out the oilskin packet she'd sewn flat against her ribs three nights ago, when the plan had still been escape instead of surrender. Inside it: three names from the ledger, copied in her own hand. Three names, three envelopes, three towns on three different roads.

She looked south, toward the border Marchetti's people would be watching. Then north, toward the delta, the sea, the open water where a woman could take a new name as easily as a new coat.

"Hold course north," she said. "We've got mail to send."
