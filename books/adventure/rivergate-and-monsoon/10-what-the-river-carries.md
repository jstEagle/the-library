## 10. What the River Carries

The river gave back nothing. That was the first thing Elodie understood, standing on the bank at Greywater Bend three days after the flood crested, watching the brown water slide past with the patience of something that had already eaten what it wanted. No paper. No ink. The monsoon had taken the chest's contents the way it took everything—completely, without malice, without memory.

She had fed forty years of secrets to a river, and the river had said thank you and moved on.

Tamir sat behind her on an overturned crate, his shoulder bandaged where the bullet had gone through meat instead of bone. The field doctor in Greywater—a woman who asked no questions for double pay—had dug the slug out by lamplight while Tamir bit down on a leather strap and Sefa held his hand. He'd live. He'd dive again by autumn, maybe sooner if he was stubborn about it, which he would be.

"You're brooding," Tamir said.

"I'm thinking."

"Same thing, worse posture."

Elodie almost smiled. Almost. Her hands still smelled of river mud and gunpowder, no matter how many times she washed them in the pump basin outside the rooming house. She kept seeing the moment above the flood—the chest open on her knees, the boat bucking through white water, pages lifting into the rain like pale birds—and she kept waiting to feel certain that she'd done the right thing. The certainty hadn't come. Maybe it wouldn't. Maybe doing the right thing felt exactly like drowning and you only knew afterward, when you surfaced somewhere new.

Sefa came out of the rooming house carrying three cups of chicory coffee and the morning's newspaper, folded to a story on page four. NAVY DENIES RIVERGATE INCIDENT; INVESTIGATION PROMISED. The word *promised* doing all the work a body could ask of it.

"They're calling it a training accident," Sefa said. "The patrol boat. Corvane's boat." He set the cups down carefully, as if they might detonate. "They didn't even name him."

"He'll be a line item," Elodie said. "A budget entry. *Vessel lost, cause undetermined.*"

"And us?"

"We were never there."

* * *

They stayed in Greywater nine days, long enough for Tamir's fever to break, not long enough to become familiar. Familiarity was how names got remembered, and remembered names got reported. Elodie had learned that arithmetic from the navy herself, back when she'd thought permits and paperwork were protection instead of bait.

On the fourth day she bought paper, envelopes, and ink from the stationer on Cord Street, and sat up half the night copying three names out of memory. She had burned the ledger copies before they ran the rapids—all but one page, torn small and swallowed in pieces over two days of travel—but memory was a salvage tool like any other, and hers had never failed her underwater. Three names. Three signatures she'd traced by lamplight in the boathouse, back when the chest was still a mystery instead of a funeral.

An admiral. A port director. A magistrate whose signature appeared beside the word *repatriation* spelled out in careful bureaucratic language that meant execution.

She mailed them separately, weeks apart, from towns that shared nothing but distance. The first went from Greywater, addressed to a newspaper editor in the capital, tucked inside a letter complaining about dredging fees—boring enough to be opened, plain enough to be believed. The second she posted from Millbrook Junction, wrapped around a recipe her mother used to make, sent to a retired judge whose name she'd found in an old Vessian trade directory. The third she left in a church poor-box in a river town so small it didn't appear on navy charts, trusting that some curate's conscience would do what couriers couldn't.

No return address. No handwriting anyone could trace—she'd learned to write left-handed years ago, for signing for other people's cargo. Each name traveled alone, deniable, slow as sediment. Justice wasn't a rescue dive. You didn't go down once and bring everything up. You marked the spot and came back season after season, and eventually the current did your work for you.

"The exiles will figure it out eventually," Sefa said, watching her seal the last envelope. "Marchetti, whoever's left of her people. They'll know someone kept copies."

"Let them look," Elodie said. "They'll be looking for a chest. People always look for the chest."

* * *

The new boat was called the *Kestrel*, though that wasn't the name painted on her stern. She was a river tramp, thirty feet of patched hull and honest rust, bought cheap from a widow in Greywater who wanted her gone before the navy came asking about her late husband's debts. They re-caulked her seams, replaced the ballast—Sefa insisted on inspecting every stone personally, remembering what those spaces had carried—and repainted her a color that existed nowhere in any naval registry: a green so dark it was nearly black, the color of deep water under shade.

They took new names the way you take on ballast: quietly, evenly distributed, because the boat needed it to sit right in the water.

Tamir became Tomas Ferro, a widower with a bad shoulder and a talent for engines. Sefa became simply Sefa, because he had spent his whole life being overlooked and saw no reason to surrender the advantage. And Elodie Galloway—salvage diver, finder of drowned things, keeper of a dead nation's last testimony—became Mara Voss, of nowhere in particular, licensed for light salvage and river work, references available.

The license was forged. The references were real people who owed her favors. It balanced.

"Do you miss it?" Sefa asked one evening, their third week on the water. They were moored in the reeds downstream of a town whose name Elodie had already forgotten, and the fireflies were coming up off the water like sparks rising from a drowned fire. "Rivergate. Your name. All of it."

Elodie considered the question honestly, which took longer than she expected.

"I miss the water I knew," she said finally. "Every bend of it. I could've navigated that river blindfolded, by feel, by the pull of the current against my hand." She turned her cup slowly. "But Rivergate was a town the government drowned on purpose and then pretended had never existed. I lived my whole life on top of a lie and called it home. This—" she gestured at the dark river, the nameless banks, the stars coming out thick as spilled salt—"this is just water. Nobody's lying about it. Nobody's hiding anything in it except what sank by accident."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

* * *

In the capital, six weeks later, a newspaper printed a story on its inside pages about irregularities in naval procurement records dating back four decades. The story named no one. But a retired judge wrote a private letter to the Ministry of Marine asking pointed questions about a corvette called the *Vessant*, and a magistrate in the southern district suddenly requested extended leave, and an admiral's office began shredding documents with such urgency that two clerks noticed and started keeping copies of their own.

None of this reached Elodie directly. News reached her the way everything reached her now—slowly, sideways, filtered through dock gossip and old smuggler contacts who traded information the way they'd once traded brandy. But she heard enough. Enough to know the seeds had taken. Enough to know that somewhere in the capital, men who had signed their names beside the word *repatriation* were sleeping badly.

It wasn't vindication. Vindication was loud, public, triumphant. This was quieter and better: the truth moving through the world like water through limestone, patient, invisible, dissolving things.

* * *

They worked the river that summer and the next. Light salvage, mostly—lost cargoes, sunken skiffs, a wedding ring off a bridge piling that Elodie recovered for a young widow who wept on the bank and paid them in preserves. Tamir's daughter's debt got paid off in installments, quietly, through intermediaries, until the letters stopped coming. Sefa learned navigation and turned out to have a gift for it, reading water the way he read rooms, sensing trouble before it showed itself.

Once, in a port town far downriver, Elodie saw a woman across the market square with Marchetti's bearing, Marchetti's way of standing, and her heart slammed against her ribs for ten full seconds before the woman turned and proved to be a stranger. After that she stopped looking over her shoulder. Not because the danger passed—because she decided that a life spent flinching was just another kind of drowning, slower, and she had already done her time underwater.

Sometimes, on flat evenings when the current ran gentle, she dove alone in whatever water they were moored on. Not for salvage. Just to feel it—that silence, that weightlessness, the world reduced to breath and pressure and the pale geometry of light from above. Down there she was nobody's instrument, nobody's witness, nobody's fugitive. Down there she was just a body in the water, and the water kept its dead and kept its secrets and kept its promises, all three at once, and asked nothing of her except that she leave before her air ran out.

She always left before her air ran out. She'd learned that much.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the flood, they were moored below the ruins of an old ferry crossing when Tamir brought her a letter that had been forwarded through three hands and two false names before finding her. The envelope was cheap paper, unmarked. Inside was a single sheet, unsigned, in a hand she almost recognized:

*The river gives back more than you think. Ask at the Lantern House, Corvane's Landing, if you ever come this way. Some of us kept what mattered.*

Corvane's Landing. A town she'd never heard of, named for a family she'd never known—or perhaps for a man the official record listed as lost, cause undetermined, vessel and all.

Elodie read the note twice, then held it over the galley stove and watched it curl. But she folded the *name* away in her memory, the way she'd once folded away three others, and that night she charted a course downriver, past the flooded ruins, past the checkpoints that no longer stood, toward a town on the map that shouldn't exist and a lantern burning in a window for reasons she intended to discover.

The river carried everything somewhere. It was only a matter of going to meet it.
