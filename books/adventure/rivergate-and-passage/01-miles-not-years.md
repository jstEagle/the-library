## 1. Miles, Not Years

The morning Odile Lockridge found the cache, she had been measuring the world in miles for one thousand and ninety-four days.

She did not count the days. That was the point. She counted the miles instead — the chain lengths run along dead track, the stations re-sighted through a theodolite older than she was, the kilometers of rail that no train would ever ride again but which she walked anyway, stake by stake, because a line abandoned was not a line erased, and someone had to keep the numbers honest. Three years since the washout on the Kestrel Grade. Three years since Wren. She had never once said the number aloud. But she knew to the tenth of a mile how much track lay between her cabin door and the place where the river had taken him, and she walked it every Sunday, and on Sundays only, she let herself think of it as a pilgrimage rather than a survey.

Rivergate in the last week of summer smelled like dust and cold water. The valley town itself sat below her route — slate roofs, mill smoke standing straight in windless air, the freight yard where four engines slept under tarpaulins like patient animals. Above it, the mountains went up in stages: pasture, pine, then the grey shoulders of shale where nothing grew and everything slid. The closed lines ran up into all of it, stitched across slopes that had shrugged them off in places, buried them in others. The government had sealed the high country three years ago — *pending reassessment*, the notices said, nailed to every tunnel mouth — and pending had become permanent, and permanent had become hers alone.

She left before dawn, as always. The kettle's whistle she muffled with a cloth so as not to wake the silence; the silence was a thing she kept, the way other people kept dogs. Her pack held the transit level, two staves, a notebook bound in oilcloth, bread and hard cheese, and the small tin flask of coffee she permitted herself at the turnaround point of each day's walk. On her hip, the chain. In her chest pocket, folded soft as a letter, the photograph she never looked at anymore because looking at it cost more than carrying it.

The old spur climbed north out of Rivergate toward the Shoulder — that long granite ridge the maps named with such confidence and the rock contradicted at every turn. It had carried ore once, then timber, then nothing but weather. Rails still showed in the cuts, furred orange with rust, holding their gauge faithfully for no one. Odile walked the centerline without thinking about it, boots finding the ties by memory, and let the day assemble itself around her: the light coming down the valley in a slow gold flood, the first chill off the glacier-fed creek crossing under the culvert at mile four, the marmots whistling from the talus like sentries announcing a visitor who never came.

At the sixth-mile cut she stopped to shoot an elevation, mostly for the ritual of it. The instrument settled on its tripod; she leveled it, sighted down the old grade to the benchmark she'd painted herself two summers ago — a small blue X on the cut wall, the only signature she allowed herself now — and read the rod. Forty-one meters above the valley floor, give or take the patience of her hands. She wrote it in the notebook in her narrow surveyor's hand, each figure standing straight as a tie. Numbers didn't grieve. Numbers just were. That was why she loved them, though she'd have denied loving anything.

It was a quarter past nine when she came around the shoulder of the cut and saw, fifty meters ahead, the canvas.

Her first thought was bear. Bears got into things, dragged things, made messes of things. But bears did not fold tarps over corners with stones, and this tarp was folded — tucked, even — over a low rectangular shape wedged into the shadow of the cut wall where the rock overhung. Someone had wanted it hidden. Someone had wanted it findable. Both intentions lived in the same clumsy knotwork, and the contradiction of it stopped her with her hand already on the flap of her own pack, where the little hatchet lived.

She stood a long moment in the cut, listening. Wind in the scree above. Water talking to itself in the ditch. Nothing else. The marmots had gone quiet, which she noted and did not like.

"Hello the camp," she said, because the words were habit even if the meaning had rusted out of them years ago.

Nothing answered. She gave it a count of thirty, watching the slope above the cut for any movement that wasn't wind-shimmer, then crossed to the tarp and pulled it back.

Survey gear. Government survey gear — she knew the issue pattern at a glance, the grey-green cases stenciled with the crown-and-rail mark of the Provincial Works Department, the same make as the instruments locked in the freight office at Rivergate behind a door only Administrator Hale's office could open. A theodolite in its fitted case, lens cap on. Two range poles, banded. A wooden ammunition box that rattled when she shifted it — inside, wrapped in waxed paper, a dozen sticks of blasting powder and a coil of fuse, dry and untouched. A leather satchel, buckled. And beneath all of it, half-tucked under the theodolite case as if it had been shoved there last of all, a canvas rucksack with a bedroll still strapped to it, the roll compressed flat by use, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and of someone else's sweat.

Odile crouched and did not touch any of it for a full minute. This was a discipline her father had taught her before he taught her letters: *look until looking runs out*. So she looked.

The gear was clean. Not new — the theodolite case bore the scuffs of real work, the range poles were chalked with elevation figures in a hand that wasn't hers — but clean, wiped down, packed with the care of people who expected to come back for it. The tarp had been drawn over the pile against rain. The powder box was latched. Nothing was scattered, nothing gnawed, nothing spoiled. And yet the rucksack had been left, and no surveyor alive left his rucksack, because the rucksack was the surveyor — food, shelter, spare socks, the whole portable self of him.

Someone had meant to return. Someone had been certain they would return. And then they hadn't, and whatever had interrupted that certainty had done it suddenly enough that the packing had stopped mid-thought — the satchel's buckle hung open, and when she finally leaned close enough to look inside without touching, she saw papers in it, squared and stacked, with a pencil lying crosswise on top as if set down between one entry and the next.

The unease arrived then, not all at once but the way cold water finds a boot seam. She had worked these lines alone for three years and had learned every species of absence the mountain offered: the absence of trains, the absence of voices, the great deliberate absence the government had stamped across the high country with its notices and its seals. She had made a home inside all that emptiness and called it peace. But this was a different absence. This absence had a shape. It was the exact negative impression of human urgency — a camp packed to be resumed, a pencil laid down mid-sentence, a tarp tucked against weather by hands that believed there would be weather to tuck against tomorrow.

*Someone was here,* she thought. *And left in a hurry.*

And underneath it, quieter, in the voice she kept locked below the numbers: *the way Wren would have left. The way anyone leaves when the mountain changes its mind.*

She looked up the cut then, really looked, following the line of the old grade as it bent north into the shadow of the Shoulder. The shale slopes above were pale and unbroken, holding the morning light like a held breath. No smoke. No birdsong where there should have been — and there should have been; the cut at this hour usually rang with pipits working the scree. She noticed their absence the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue.

She unbuckled the satchel after all. Discipline only runs so deep in a person, and curiosity in Odile Lockridge had always run deeper than discipline, though she dressed it up as thoroughness.

Papers. Survey forms, the standard provincial stock — station logs, elevation sheets, a sketch map of the spur drawn competently enough that she paused to admire the linework before she caught herself. All of it covering ground she knew better than the government did. And clipped to the back of the stack, a field journal in a stiff cover, its pages swollen from damp and dried again, warped like sea-driftwood.

The journal was not hers to open. She knew that. She opened it anyway, or rather she lifted it, felt its weight, and read the name inked inside the front cover in a careful draftsman's hand:

*A. Reeve, Party Chief — Provincial Boundary & Works Commission.*

Reeve. She turned the name over. There had been a Reeve in Rivergate talk, back in the spring — a government party come up to do the reassessment everyone had stopped believing in, four men and a cook, staying at the Station Hotel, buying boots and bacon and asking questions about the high lines that the locals answered with shrugs. They'd gone up the pass in June. Nobody remembered them coming down. Nobody had thought about it much either, which was its own kind of answer, and Odile had filed the whole affair under the same heading as everything else the government started and abandoned: *not my line, not my problem.*

The journal fell open where the pencil marked it. The handwriting was small and upright, the entries dense, and the last written page — the page the pencil lay across — began mid-observation, in the flat declarative voice of a man recording facts because facts were the only thing keeping his hand steady:

*—elevation confirms third reading. There is a settlement in that valley. Fences, orchards, a schoolhouse. Bell rang at noon. This is not on any chart I was issued, and when I raised it by wire, the reply from the Administrator's office instructed us to strike the readings and proceed to the pass. Struck nothing. If we are recalled, this book goes to the archive regardless. Let them explain a bell.*

Below that, nothing. No date completed, no signature closing the entry. Just the pencil, lying where a hand had set it down, and the blank rest of the page waiting with a patience that made her skin crawl.

Odile sat back on her heels in the cut with the journal in her hands and the wind moving cold down off the Shoulder, and she understood, in the way one understands a grade failure — all at once, from the toe of the slide to the crown — that the quiet she had built her three years inside of was not empty after all. It was occupied. Something had happened up here among the dead rails, something with bells in it and struck readings and men who packed their camps to return, and the mountain had folded it away the way the government folded its tarps: carefully, deliberately, expecting no one to look.

A marmot whistled, far up the scree. One sharp note, then another. The sentries announcing a visitor.

But the sound came from above the cut, from the shale, from the direction no road and no trail ran — and Odile Lockridge, who had spent a thousand and ninety-four days learning exactly what belonged on her mountain and what did not, closed the journal, buckled the satchel, and looked up the old spur toward the pass with her heart going like a pilot lamp.

* * *

She told herself she was only going to walk to the bend. The bend in the cut was a hundred meters on, where the grade swung north and the view opened up the whole lower flank of the Shoulder — a natural checkpoint, any surveyor would say the same, standard practice to sight your route before committing to it. She said it to herself twice, in the tone she used for figures that wouldn't balance, and walked to the bend, and stood there with the pack straps creaking as she shifted her weight from foot to foot like a woman arguing with herself in a language of posture.

From the bend she could see a kilometer of the old spur running straight and true into the mountain's grey shoulder, and beyond it, where the rails gave out and the shale began, the pale slopes climbing toward the pass in long unbroken sheets of stone.

Unbroken. That was the word her eye snagged on, the way a flange snags a frog in the track. She had walked this view a hundred times. The shale above the spur was always scored — with goat paths, with meltwater channels, with the slow grey scars of slides. Today it showed none. Today it lay smooth as fresh paper, as if something had passed over it and smoothed it after, or as if her memory of a hundred mornings had been quietly rewritten overnight.

No. Not rewritten. She checked herself, checked the horizon, checked the sun's angle against the notch of the pass to fix her bearings. The slopes were simply bare of tracks because no one had crossed them recently — that was all, that was the ordinary reading, and she clung to it the way you cling to a handrail in the dark.

Except that the cache below argued otherwise. Somebody had come down off that shale lately. Somebody had come down fast, leaving gear behind, leaving a pencil on an unfinished sentence, leaving a journal that spoke of bells in valleys where the charts said there was nothing at all.

The wind shifted and brought her a smell she couldn't immediately place — woodsmoke, very faint, cold almost, the ghost of a fire burned hours ago somewhere upwind. From the direction of the pass. From the direction of nowhere, because there was nothing up there to burn. She had surveyed every hut, every adit, every ruin on this flank. She knew the mountain's inventory the way she knew her own hands.

Odile stood at the bend a long time, the journal heavy in her pack against the small of her back, heavier than paper had any right to be.

Three years she had kept to her rule. Measure the miles. Don't measure the years. Don't go looking for what the mountain took, because the mountain keeps what it takes, and grief is a load you don't carry uphill. She had said it to herself so many times it had worn smooth as the shale.

But the mountain had never before left something *for* her. Gear packed to be resumed. Words written to be read. A bell, rung at noon, in a valley that wasn't there.

She turned up the spur and began to climb, and the miles started counting themselves.
