## 2. The Team That Left No Tracks

The cache had been left in a hurry. Odile could read haste the way other people read handwriting—in the angle of a dropped pry bar, in the way the canvas tarp had been thrown rather than folded, corners flapping loose like a bird with a broken wing. She crouched at the edge of the old spur and studied it for a long moment before touching anything, because three years alone on closed lines had taught her that objects told the truth about the hands that abandoned them.

The morning was thin and bright, the kind of high-altitude light that made everything look sharper than it deserved. Frost still clung to the north faces of the shale boulders, and her breath hung in front of her in small white flags that dissolved as she watched them. Below, the Rivergate valley lay in its usual morning haze, the town's chimneys drawing straight gray lines into the cold air. Above, the pass waited, indifferent, wearing its first dusting of seasonal snow like a man wearing a hat he hadn't decided on yet.

She circled the cache twice before kneeling.

Government issue. That much was certain from the stenciling—*PROVINCIAL SURVEY CORPS* in faded block letters, the seal of the administrator's office stamped beneath it in ink that hadn't been authorized for field use in years, if ever. A theodolite in its fitted case, brass fittings green with disuse. Three crates of ranging rods, strapped and never opened. A signal lamp with its lens cracked clean through. And beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth with a care that contradicted the haste around it, a leather ledger case, buckled shut, water-stained but intact.

No bodies. No blood. No note pinned under a stone.

Odile stood, wiped her palms on her trousers, and looked up the slope. The shale fields began thirty yards above the spur, a great gray apron of broken stone fanning out from the shoulder of the mountain, and beyond them the switchbacks climbed toward the pass in long, patient diagonals that she had walked, measured, and memorized over three years of self-imposed exile. She knew every culvert, every retaining wall, every place where the old roadbed had slumped into the drainage like a tired animal lying down.

Someone had carried this equipment up from the valley. Someone had cached it here—or lost it here—and gone on. Or been taken.

She unbuckled the ledger case, weighed it in her hand, and did not open it. Not yet. Some things you read in the right light or not at all, and this light felt wrong, too clean, too honest for whatever was written inside. Instead she shouldered her own pack, took up the ledger case and the theodolite—the rest she covered again with the tarp, weighted with stones, marked with a cairn of three flat rocks in the pattern she used for her own caches—and started up the shale.

* * *

Shale is honest country. It doesn't pretend. Every step announces itself, every stone you dislodge goes somewhere and stays there, and a trained eye can read a slope the way a farmer reads a field. Odile had spent three years learning to read these slopes, and by midmorning she was reading one now that made the skin crawl along her arms despite the cold.

There were tracks. Of course there were tracks—shale records everything, holds every boot-heel and skidded stone in its loose gray memory—but they were wrong. They went up. Six sets of them, maybe seven, climbing in the loose stagger of laden men, heading for the shoulder and the pass beyond. And then, two-thirds of the way up the first long pitch, where the slope steepened and the stone ran out into a shelf of bare bedrock—

They stopped.

Not faded. Not scattered. Stopped. The last prints were as crisp as the first, edges sharp, weight evenly distributed, no sign of hurry, no sign of struggle, no sign of anything except six men standing still on a shelf of rock and then ceasing to be there. Beyond the shelf the bedrock ran smooth and unmarked for forty feet to the scree field above, and the scree above was unbroken, undisturbed, holding its frost in unbroken sheets.

Odile stood at the edge of the last footprint for a long time.

The wind came down off the pass and moved her hair off her forehead, and she let it, because moving felt suddenly like an admission of something. She thought about turning back. She thought about it seriously, the way you think about a door: here is the way out, here is the valley, here is your cabin and your stove and your careful miles. Three years she had kept herself alive by knowing when a line was dead and leaving it alone.

But her brother had also known when a line was dead, and he had walked into it anyway, and no one had ever told her why.

She stepped onto the shelf of bare rock and crossed it, and her boots rang faintly against the stone, and behind her the six sets of prints stood in the sun like a sentence with its ending torn away.

* * *

The camp sat in a hollow below the second switchback, tucked against a windbreak of stacked stone that someone had built with more skill than urgency. From twenty yards it looked almost peaceful—a cluster of four tents, a cook shelter, gear racked neatly along a rope line. From ten yards it looked like a photograph of a camp. From five yards it looked like a held breath.

Everything was there.

Boots stood outside the tents in pairs, laces tucked in, the way men do when they expect to put them on again in an hour. Wool coats hung from the rope line, sleeves swinging gently in the wind. A mess kit sat on a crate beside the cook shelter with food still congealed on the plate—porridge, by the look of it, gone to gray rubber in the cold. The stove in the cook shelter held ash, and Odile knelt and put her hand near it, not quite touching, and the ash was days old, dry as flour, cold as the stone around it.

She moved through the camp the way she moved through a wreck site: slow, systematic, touching nothing until she understood it.

Four tents. Seven bedrolls, one of them unused, still rolled tight. Seven men, then, or six and a spare. Personal effects everywhere—pipes, a harmonica, letters half-written and abandoned mid-sentence, a tin of tobacco with the lid off. One man had been mending a sock; the needle still stood in the wool. Another had a book face-down on his cot, spine cracked, a pencil marking his page.

Nobody had packed. Nobody had fled. Nobody had been dragged—there were no drag marks in the shale, no scuffs, no blood, nothing. It was as if seven men had finished their porridge, set down their needles and their books, walked out of their boots, and evaporated.

Except.

On the crate beside the stove, squared to the edge with a precision that didn't match anything else in the camp, lay a journal. Black oilcloth cover, government stamp, the kind issued to survey team leaders for official record. Beside it, arranged with the same deliberate neatness, a brass compass, a folding rule, and a single ranging rod planted upright in the ground at the exact center of the hollow, plumb-true, its red-and-white bands facing the pass.

A message, or a marker. Or both.

Odile looked at the rod for a while. Then she picked up the journal, and her hands were steadier than she expected them to be, and she carried it to the cook shelter where the wind couldn't flip the pages, and she sat down on an overturned crate among the ghosts of seven men, and she read.

* * *

The early entries were ordinary. Elevation figures, weather notes, supply tallies in a small, disciplined hand. Anselm Reeve, team leader, wrote the way engineers write—compressed, precise, allergic to adjectives. She found herself liking him a little, in the way you like a stranger whose habits are legible. He complained about the biscuits. He praised his instrument man. He noted, on the third day, that the mule had gone lame and been sent back down with the packer, which meant the team had come up the mountain on foot, which matched the tracks.

Then, on the sixth day, the hand changed.

It didn't deteriorate. It *tightened*, the letters growing smaller and more vertical, as though the man writing them were pressing them flat against some internal resistance. The entry for the sixth day read:

> *Ran the transit line up the east drainage as scheduled. At station 14 the readings will not reconcile. Checked the instrument twice. Checked my own arithmetic three times. The elevation of the valley floor does not agree with the provincial sheet. It does not disagree slightly. It disagrees by a valley.*
>
> *There is a valley on the east side of the shoulder that does not appear on any map I have ever seen, including the ones in my own office. The drainage is real. The water is real. I can hear it.*

The seventh day:

> *Descended into the east drainage against protocol. Found a fence. Wire, posts cut from local timber, stretched across better than a mile of ground. Beyond the fence, orchards in rows. Apple, by the leaf. The trees are old. Old trees mean old planting means generations. There is a road, graded and maintained. There is a schoolhouse with a bell in the cupola.*
>
> *I have surveyed this range for eleven years. I know its drainages the way I know my own hands. There has never been a valley here. There has never been a Bellbrook.*

He'd crossed something out after that—struck through so hard the nib had torn the paper—and written beneath it, smaller:

> *I did not write that name. I do not know where that name came from. It was in my mouth before I knew it.*

Odile sat very still. Somewhere above the camp, wind worried at the scree fields, a sound like slow applause from an audience that would not stop.

The eighth day's entry was the last full one:

> *Sent Kessler down with a report for the administrator. Told him to take the west trail and speak to no one. I have been thinking about the bell. This afternoon we heard it. All of us. Four miles from the schoolhouse, across open shale, with the wind against us, we heard a bell ring twice. Voss says it was a trick of the terrain. Voss has stopped sleeping.*
>
> *I have begun to suspect that our instruments are not malfunctioning. I have begun to suspect that they are functioning perfectly, and that the maps are what is broken, and that I am the first man in eleven years to hold a level over ground that someone went to a great deal of trouble to make disappear.*
>
> *If anything happens to us, the ledger will explain what the instruments saw. The ledger cannot explain what we heard. For that I recommend the reader go and stand in the east drainage at dusk and wait.*

Beneath that, a final line, dated the ninth day, written fast, the discipline cracking at last:

> *Men came up from the valley last night. Not ours. Hale's colors. They say they've come to "consolidate" the survey. Reeve out.*

And then nothing. The pages after that were blank, except for the ghost of an impression on the next leaf, as though the journal had lain open beneath another piece of paper on which someone had written a single word and pressed hard.

Odile turned to the back cover, where the impression showed best against the stiff leather. She tilted it toward the light, angling the page until the shadows pooled in the grooves of the vanished ink.

One word: **CONSOLIDATED**.

* * *

She stayed in the camp until the light went long and amber, and she did her work the only way she knew how, which was thoroughly. She inventoried the tents, noting the serial numbers stamped inside the flaps. She photographed nothing, because she owned no camera, but she drew—fast, accurate sketches in her own notebook, the camp laid out, the gear positions, the boot pairs, the plate of ruined porridge. She checked the stove ash again and confirmed what she already knew: cold for days, no fuel added, no fire built since.

Seven men had eaten breakfast here. Six sets of tracks led up the shale and ended on a shelf of bare rock. The seventh man—Kessler, sent down with a report—had presumably reached the valley, or not. Either way, no one had come looking. No search party had passed on the trails; she'd have seen the signs, the cut brush, the discarded rations. The provincial government had lost a survey team on its own mountain and had responded by sending, apparently, no one.

Or by sending exactly the wrong people.

She thought about the word pressed into the blank page. *Consolidated.* It was a clerk's word, a filing word, a word for merging accounts and closing redundant offices. It was not a word for men. But it had been written hard enough to emboss leather, and whoever had written it had wanted it to stand for the whole team—one entry, one stamp, one line drawn through seven names.

Outside, the light was failing. The first stars were out over the pass, hard and white, and the temperature was falling the way it did up here, all at once, like a trap springing. She should go down. She should take the journal, go down to her cabin, bar the door, and decide in daylight whether she had found a mystery or a grave.

She was gathering her pack when she heard it.

Faint. Impossibly faint. Four miles distant, maybe more, across the black shoulder of the mountain, from the east drainage where no valley was supposed to be.

A bell. Twice.

She stood in the empty camp with the wind moving through seven men's hanging coats, and she listened until the sound had entirely gone, and then a while longer, the way you listen for a heartbeat you're afraid has stopped.

Anselm Reeve had recommended standing in the east drainage at dusk and waiting. Dusk was nearly spent. But dawn was coming, and Odile Lockridge had spent three years measuring distances instead of time, and she knew exactly how many miles it was to the head of the east drainage, and she had never once in her life backed away from a number.

She buckled the journal into her pack, pulled her collar up against the cold, and started down through the dark toward the valley that did not exist.
