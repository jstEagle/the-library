## 3. What the Ledger Wouldn't Say

The cabin took an hour to warm, and Odile spent that hour not warming it.

She stood at the window instead, watching the last light fail over the shale slopes where the survey team had vanished, her hands wrapped around a tin mug of tea she had forgotten to drink. The stove ticked and settled behind her. Outside, the wind came down off the shoulder of the range in long, even breaths, the way it always did after dark, and somewhere below the treeline a branch cracked under the weight of nothing she could see.

Three years she had lived in this cabin, and in three years she had learned every sound the mountain made. She knew which groans were ice and which were rock. She knew the difference between the wind moving through the pines and the wind moving through something the pines had grown around. She had catalogued them all the way other people catalogued birthdays and anniversaries — because miles were what she counted now, not years, and a woman who measured distance for a living could not afford to leave any of it unmeasured.

Tonight the mountain sounded ordinary. That was somehow worse.

She drank the tea at last. It had gone bitter and lukewarm, tasting of tin and pine smoke, and she drank it anyway, standing, because sitting down felt like an admission that she intended to stay the night, and some part of her was still up on that slope with the empty camp, with the boots lined by the cold stove, with the ledgers stacked square as headstones.

She had taken one ledger. It sat on her table now, wrapped in the oilcloth she used for her own field books, looking smaller than it had any right to look. A government-issue survey ledger, bound in grey board, stamped with the provincial crest and a requisition number. *Reeve, A.* written inside the front cover in a hand so neat it looked printed.

Odile hung her coat on its peg. She lit the lamp, turned the wick low, and sat down across from the ledger without touching it, the way you might sit across from a stranger on a night train.

Then she opened it.

* * *

The first forty pages were exactly what they should have been. Chainage figures. Backsights and foresights. Elevations reduced to datum, each column ruled true, each sum carried correctly. Reeve had been a careful man; she could tell from the way he wrote his eights, never closing the loop entirely, leaving them open like doors. His reductions were clean. His error closures were tight. Whoever Anselm Reeve was, he had been good at his work, and Odile felt the small, involuntary kinship that one honest measurer feels for another, even a stranger, even a missing one.

It was page forty-one where the terrain stopped matching the paperwork.

The team's route, reconstructed from the chainages, ran up the old spur — her spur, the line she had walked two hundred times — and then cut west off it into a valley she knew well enough to draw blindfolded. The valley of the upper Aldery. She had surveyed its mouth twice. The provincial rolls listed it the way they listed most of the high country: *uninhabited. No recorded settlement. No water rights of record.*

Reeve's elevations ran down into that valley and kept going.

And between the numbers, in the margins, in that same printed-neat hand, were notes.

*Fence line, post-and-rail, weathered but sound. Ran my tape along it: 340 links to the corner post.*

*Orchard, north-facing slope. Apple, by the bark. Trees pruned within the last two seasons. Counted forty-one standing.*

*Chimney smoke at dusk. Two columns. Did not approach.*

*Schoolhouse bell. Cast bronze by the sound. Struck nine times at nine o'clock precisely.*

Odile read the bell note three times.

She got up, stoked the stove, sat back down, and read it again, as though the words might have rearranged themselves while her back was turned. They hadn't. *Struck nine times at nine o'clock precisely.* A schoolhouse bell, in a valley the province insisted was empty. A bell rung on schedule, by someone, for someone — children, presumably, which meant families, which meant houses, which meant a town. Fences mended recently. Orchards pruned within two seasons. Chimney smoke in columns, meaning green wood, meaning a household burning what it had.

None of it appeared on any map she owned. And Odile owned maps the way misers owned coin — originals, revisions, provisional issues, the whole grey lineage of the Rivergate surveys going back forty years. In every edition, the upper Aldery was blank space with contour lines and a notation: *uninhabited.*

But the land itself — she had stood in that valley. She had run her levels down its floor. And here was the thing that made her set the ledger down and press the heels of her hands against her eyes: the land had matched. Not the maps. The land. There had been a fence rotting along the lower meadow when she surveyed it, and she had noted it in her own field book as an obstruction, *old fence, origin unknown,* and thought no more about it. There had been apple trees gone wild on the north slope, and she had logged their canopy as vegetation interference and moved on. There had been a foundation near the creek bend that she had recorded as *possible former structure, unverified* — because the maps said there was nothing there, and Odile Lockridge trusted her instruments absolutely, but she had trusted the maps too, and when the two disagreed she had assumed the fault lay in her own reading.

She had assumed the land was lying to her.

She opened her own field books, the ones from two springs ago, and laid them beside Reeve's ledger, and went through them entry by entry, and the two records agreed with each other perfectly and disagreed with the province completely. Fence. Orchard. Foundation. A track worn into the turf that she had called a game trail because game trails were what unmapped valleys contained.

*Uninhabited,* said the map.

*Nine strokes at nine o'clock,* said the man who had gone up to check.

* * *

Her brother had trusted the maps too.

Wren Lockridge had been the better surveyor, if she was honest — quicker, looser, willing to trust an eye-level estimate where she would have set up the level and shot it properly. He used to tease her about it. *You'd measure your own funeral, Odi. You'd get the depth right and miss the point entirely.* He had come out to Rivergate two summers before his death, full of restless energy, wanting to see the closed lines she talked about, wanting to walk the high country before the province let it go back to wilderness. She had given him the spur. She had drawn him a route herself, off her own maps, marked in her own hand, and sent him up on a clear morning in June with a day pack and her spare compass, telling him to be back by dark.

He had not come back by dark.

They found the compass three days later, on the shale above the tree line, its needle swinging free. They found his boot prints going up. They did not find boot prints coming down. The searchers — twelve men, then twenty, then the provincial constabulary with dogs — worked the slope until the rain came and washed the shale smooth, and then the report was written, and the report said what reports say: *lost, presumed deceased, cause undetermined.* Her mother had needed a body to bury and never got one, and had died the following winter anyway, and Odile had come back to Rivergate after the funeral and asked the district office for the closed-line contract nobody else wanted, and had been measuring distance ever since.

Because here was the arithmetic she had never said aloud to anyone: Wren had disappeared in country that the maps described one way and the ground described another. He had gone up following her route, drawn from her maps, and somewhere between the last reliable benchmark and the shale, the world had stopped agreeing with the paper. She had told herself for three years that he had simply stepped wrong, that shale gives way, that mountains keep people. All true. All probably true.

But tonight, reading a dead-or-missing man's tidy marginalia about a town that officially did not exist, she felt the other possibility open under her like a bridge deck giving way. If the maps could be wrong about a valley — not mistaken, not outdated, but *wrong*, deliberately, systematically, in every edition for years — then what else could they be wrong about? What else had she walked past, measured over, dismissed as interference or obstruction or game trail, because the paper told her what she was allowed to see?

Wren had gone up into that blank space on the map carrying the map. What if the blank space was not blank? What if it was *occupied* — by fences, orchards, bells, by people who did not appear in any record — and what if the people in it had reasons of their own for wanting a stranger with a compass to keep walking?

No. She pushed the thought away, and it came back, and she pushed it again. She would not build a murder out of grief. Grief would build anything you gave it; she had learned that much in three years. But suspicion — suspicion was just measurement applied to the past, and measurement was the one thing she trusted.

So she measured.

She cleared the table, pinned down Reeve's ledger and her own field books side by side, and began to reconcile them the way she would reconcile any two surveys: station by station, elevation by elevation, discrepancy by discrepancy. She worked through the evening with the lamp trimmed low and the stove fed twice, and the pattern emerged slowly, the way a photograph emerges in a developing tray — first shadows, then shapes, then something unmistakably human.

The discrepancies all ran one direction. Every feature Reeve recorded that the province denied — fence, orchard, structure, track — sat inside a single contiguous area of the upper Aldery, roughly four hundred hectares, bounded on the north by the creek and on the south by a ridge she had always known as the Red Shoulder. Within that boundary, the official contours were subtly wrong. She checked them against her own levels and found the error was not random, the way drafting errors are random. It was consistent. Someone had redrawn that valley — shifted the creek, flattened the meadows, erased the bench of flat ground where a town would sit — carefully enough to survive casual inspection, thoroughly enough to survive hers.

You did not redraw a valley by accident. Draftsmen made mistakes; conspiracies made *editions.*

And at the bottom of page fifty-eight, the last page with writing on it, pressed hard enough to dent the next three sheets, Reeve had written a single line that was not a measurement:

*Asked the office why the Aldery shows vacant. Told to log natural features only. Requesting clarification in person. Returning to Rivergate by the pass road.*

Dated eleven days ago. Eleven days before Odile found his camp empty on the shale, with the boots lined up and the stove cold and no tracks leading out.

Returning to Rivergate by the pass road. The pass road ran within two miles of her cabin. She had lived on it for three years and she could not remember seeing a government party come down it — not once, not ever, not in any season. Supplies came up by rail to the depot and stopped there. Whatever went over that road went over quietly.

Odile sat back. The stove had burned down to a red breathing glow, and the cabin was cold at her shoulders and warm at her hands, and outside the wind had changed, coming around to the north the way it did before weather. Somewhere far off — so far off she might have invented it, so faint she would deny it tomorrow — she heard a sound she had no business hearing at this altitude, this distance, this hour.

A bell. Bronze-toned, unhurried, striking once, twice, three times, and stopping.

Midnight, if anything up there kept midnight.

She looked at the ledger, at Reeve's open eights, at the dented line about returning to Rivergate, and she understood that she was no longer holding a curiosity. She was holding evidence. Evidence of a falsified survey, of an erased town, of a missing team that had asked one question too many — and evidence, possibly, though she could not yet bear to finish the thought, of a boy with a spare compass who had wandered into the same blank space six years early and never walked out.

Someone had gone to great trouble to make a valley disappear. Someone had gone to equal trouble to make a survey team disappear. And tomorrow morning, if the weather held, Odile Lockridge was going to walk up the pass road to the district office in Rivergate proper, and ask, politely, in the language of requisition numbers and filing codes that bureaucrats understood, who had signed off on the upper Aldery being empty.

She banked the stove. She did not sleep so much as lie awake listening, and once more, near dawn, the bell rang — five strokes this time, soft, almost apologetic, like something calling home a child who had stayed out too late.

At first light she packed her rucksack. On top of the instruments and the spare oilcloth, she put Reeve's ledger.

And that was when she saw, through the frost-feathered window, a figure coming up the pass road through the thin grey snowlight — not a government party, not a supply train, but a single small shape, moving fast and low along the fence line like something that had learned to travel unseen, and it was heading straight for her door.
