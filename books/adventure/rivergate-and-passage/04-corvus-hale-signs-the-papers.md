## 4. Corvus Hale Signs the Papers

The office of the Regional Administrator smelled of cedar oil and cold iron, and Corvus Hale liked it that way. Cedar because it covered the smell of the river, which in autumn ran low and mineral over the stones below the government house, and iron because he had ordered the window fittings replaced with cast brackets that did not rattle in the wind off the pass. A man who intended to hold a region for thirty years could not afford rattling windows. Small sounds betrayed large intentions.

He sat at his desk with the morning's light coming through the east glass at an angle that flattered no one, and read.

The reports from Survey Team Nine lay before him in their buff-colored folders, tied with the regulation gray tape, each page initialed at the corner by the team leader, Anselm Reeve, whose handwriting was small and upright and irritatingly precise. Hale had met Reeve once, two years ago, at a function in the capital where the wine was bad and the conversation worse. He remembered a lean man with sun-scoured knuckles and the particular squint of someone who spent his days looking at horizons through instruments. The kind of man who believed that what he measured was true.

That belief had been useful to the province for eleven years. It was about to become inconvenient.

Hale turned a page. Reeve's field notes were exemplary — barometric readings cross-checked against aneroid and mercurial, sight lines logged with station numbers, even marginalia noting the flowering state of shrubs along the traverse, as though the man could not pass a blossom without filing it. A thorough mind. Thorough minds were the most dangerous kind, because they left records.

*Station 44,* Reeve had written, in the entry dated the ninth of last month. *Elevation 2,140 feet. Valley floor opens southward. Fences in good repair, post-and-rail, cedar. Orchards in rows of nine, apples past harvest. Schoolhouse on the rise, bell in tower. Smoke from four chimneys at dusk.*

Smoke from four chimneys.

Hale set the folder down and looked out the window at the town of Rivergate proper, its slate roofs stepping down toward the rail yard, the government water tower standing above everything like a headstone. Four chimneys smoking in a valley that, according to every document in the provincial registry, contained no settlement, no ratepayers, no roads, no wells, and no name. A valley listed — in the great ledger of the territory, the one kept in the capital archive under seal — as *uninhabited; unsuitable for habitation; no recorded improvements.*

Which was true. It was perfectly, immaculately true. He had made it true himself, six years ago, one quiet afternoon in this same office, with three forms and a pen he still owned.

The trouble was not the valley. The trouble was that Reeve had walked into it with a theodolite and a notebook and the constitutional innocence of a surveyor, and had written down what he saw. And now Team Nine had gone silent eleven days ago, somewhere on the high spur, and silence from a survey team was a sound all its own — a sound that traveled, if one wasn't careful, all the way to the capital, to men who asked questions in the mild voices of accountants and never raised them again.

There came the knock he had been expecting: two taps, soft, at the door's lower panel, where a knock would be heard but not remembered.

"Enter."

His secretary, Pell, was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the gift of being forgettable, which Hale paid him well to cultivate. Pell carried a tray with the morning correspondence and set it down without looking directly at anything.

"The wire from the field office," Pell said. "And the gentleman from the Water Commission is waiting, sir. He has an appointment."

"He can wait another quarter hour. Close the door."

Pell closed it. Hale broke the field office wire's seal and read it twice. *No contact with Team Nine since the eleventh. Search party dispatched up the spur. Weather deteriorating. Request instructions.*

Request instructions. As though there were a form for this. There was, in fact — Hale had checked, years ago, out of general prudence — and it was Form 114, *Disposition of Records Following Loss of Personnel in Field Operations*, and it required, among other things, that all materials recovered from a lost team be forwarded to the regional administrator for review before any further distribution. He had written that clause himself. It had seemed like such a small thing at the time, a rivet in a bridge no one would ever inspect.

He took out a fresh sheet of the heavy cream paper he reserved for orders that mattered, and began to write in his own hand, because a thing in one's own hand could be burned, whereas a thing typed could always be duplicated by someone else's machine.

*To the Field Office, Rivergate District.* The nib whispered. Outside, a coal dray ground through the square, and somewhere a gull argued with the wind off the reservoir. *Re: Survey Team Nine. All instruments, notebooks, maps, journals, and personal effects recovered from the expedition are to be conveyed to this office under seal, per Form 114. No copies are to be made. No inventory is to be published. Families of the missing are to receive the standard notification of loss, citing weather conditions. The search is to continue until the first snowfall, at which point it will be suspended as impracticable. This office regrets the necessity.*

He signed it. *C. Hale, Regional Administrator.* His signature was a fine thing, taught to him by a tutor who had charged his father a season's income for the privilege: the C looped like a hook, the H squared like a gate. A signature should look like something, he had been told. His looked like a gate closing.

* * *

The gentleman from the Water Commission was named Aldous Vane, and he arrived smelling of train smoke and bay rum, with a leather case under his arm and the expression of a man who had rehearsed his grievances in front of a mirror. Hale received him in the smaller room, the one with the map of the province on the wall — the official map, the corrected one — and poured him a glass of the decent sherry he kept for exactly these occasions.

"You'll forgive my directness, Administrator," Vane said, "but the Commission is puzzled. The allocation schedules for the western watersheds were published in spring, and they show the Graywater basin drawing down forty percent below its historical yield. Yet our engineers can find no corresponding reduction upstream. The river is running full. Where has the water gone?"

"A fine question," Hale said, "and one I have asked myself." He gestured at the map with his glass, a lazy arc that began at the mountains and ended at the valley that was not on it. "The answer, I'm afraid, is old habit. For decades the schedules assumed consumption by settlements that no longer exist. Ghost towns, Mr. Vane. Paper towns. Allocations drawn up for communities that dried up or moved away or were never more than a clerk's optimism, and no one ever struck them from the rolls. So the arithmetic says the water goes to Bellbrook—" he let the name sit in the air for half a second, watching Vane's face, which showed nothing, which pleased him— "and Millhollow, and the others, when in fact those places have no pipes, no weirs, no people. The water flows on. The books say otherwise. Forty percent, vanished into clerical error."

"And you've done nothing?"

"I've done everything short of a miracle," Hale said mildly. "Consolidation of obsolete allocations requires archival confirmation from the capital — the original charters, the original rolls, proof that the entries are defunct. You know how the archive is. Winter closes the pass, the archive answers at its leisure. I have petitions pending. I expect resolution within the year."

Vane drank his sherry and frowned at the map. "Within the year. The Commission wanted it resolved before the dry season ends. There's talk of a new smelting concern on the lower Graywater, Administrator. Jobs. Revenue. They want firm numbers."

"They shall have them." Hale refilled the glass unasked, a small tyranny dressed as courtesy. "I give you my word the schedules will be corrected. Cleanly. Legally. Every ghost struck from every roll, and the water flowing where living people need it. That is the whole of my ambition, Mr. Vane — to make the paper agree with the world."

It was the truest sentence he spoke all day, though not in the sense Vane understood. Hale had long ago concluded that the world was a disorderly, sentimental, badly surveyed place, full of valleys that insisted on containing orchards and chimneys and schoolhouse bells regardless of what the registry said, and that the only honest work available to a serious man was to bring the world into agreement with the paper. Everyone else simply lacked the nerve. They called it falsification when a clerk moved a boundary line for money, and called it history when a government moved one for order. He had chosen order. Order paid better, and slept soundly.

Vane left at half past eleven, mollified, promising discretion. Hale stood a while at the map. In the corner of the western sheet, where the valley sat beneath its blankness, the engraver had put a small conventional symbol for scree slope, and beneath it nothing at all. Six years ago there had been a name there, and roads, and a dotted line of parcels. He had watched the proofs come back from the printer with the space wiped clean and had felt something he had never managed to feel in church: a sense of power exercised so quietly it resembled grace.

* * *

In the afternoon the reports came up from the search party, and with them a crate.

The crate had been carried down from the spur by four men hired for the purpose, men who did not ask questions because asking questions was not in their contracts. Inside, packed in straw, were the effects of Survey Team Nine: two theodolites, a ranging pole snapped in half, tin plates, a spirit stove, boots — six pairs, some of them still laced — and the notebooks. Eleven notebooks, plus the leader's journal, plus a canvas roll of charts.

Hale dismissed everyone and unpacked the crate alone, which was against procedure and therefore necessary. He lifted each item and set it aside. The boots troubled him most. Men did not walk out of camp barefoot into shale country, and yet here were six pairs of boots, and the search party reported no tracks leading away, none at all, as though the team had been lifted off the mountain like figures from a chalkboard. He did not believe in the supernatural — belief was for people who could not arrange outcomes — but he noted the fact, and filed it in the drawer of facts he did not yet understand, a drawer that had grown heavier these six years and that he expected, someday, to cost him.

Then the journal. Reeve's hand again, upright, patient. Hale read it by the afternoon light with a growing stillness, the way a man reads a letter that begins politely and ends with a knife.

Reeve had found things. Not just fences and orchards — those might be squatters, might be explained, might be handled — but stone. A charter stone, Reeve wrote, set into the hillside above the valley, sealed behind rockfall, bearing the provincial seal and a date sixty years old and words cut deep enough to survive weather and chisels both: *the Town of Bellbrook, incorporated, its rights of water and passage perpetual.* Perpetual. The word appeared three times in the journal, each time underlined, and beside the third underline Reeve had written, in the margin, small and terrible: *This cannot be consolidated. This is not a clerical matter. Someone must see this.*

Someone must see this. Hale closed the journal and held it flat between his palms, feeling the cheap paper's warmth, and considered the sentence the way an engineer considers a crack in a dam.

A charter under seal, predating the registry revisions, predating his own corrections, lodged in living rock where no fire of his ordering could reach it. If it reached the capital, the whole architecture wavered — not collapsed, perhaps, not immediately, but wavered, and structures that wavered invited inspectors, and inspectors invited inquiries, and inquiries had a way of pulling threads until the entire garment of a career came apart in their hands. Five valleys. Six years of schedules realigned, of revenues redirected, of a province quietly re-plumbed to serve the living at the expense of the remembered. All of it resting on the simple proposition that no one would go and look.

Reeve had gone and looked.

Hale rose and went to the window. Below, the river ran low and bright over its stones, carrying toward the lowlands water that the books said belonged to ghosts. On the far side of the square, a woman was sweeping her doorstep, unhurried, her life untouched by any of this, and he envied her nothing. He had never wanted ease. He wanted correctness — the deep satisfaction of a ledger that balanced, a map that matched, a world with nothing in it that hadn't been entered properly.

He returned to the desk and drew the cream paper toward him again. Three orders this time, brief as surgical notes.

First: the recovered materials were to remain in his custody, unlogged. Second: a discreet inquiry was to be made regarding the whereabouts of anyone else who might have come upon the team's route — prospectors, poachers, the solitary surveyor who haunted the closed lines, a woman named Lockridge, of whom the district file said little except that she knew the high country better than the men employed to know it. Third — and here his pen paused, hovering, before moving with the smooth finality of a gate swinging shut — arrangements were to be made for the charter stone. Rock could be fallen. Roads could be washed out. Mountains, given sufficient explosive and sufficient silence, could be persuaded to keep secrets.

He signed all three. Then he fed Reeve's journal page by page into the iron stove, and watched the margins curl, and the underlined word *perpetual* glow briefly orange before going to ash, and felt the day's work settle into him with the calm of a level bubble finding its center.

Outside, far to the north, the first thin cloud was building over the shoulder of the range, weeks early, the color of dirty wool. Winter was coming down to argue with the calendar. Somewhere up in that graying country a girl was tending goats or a widow was stacking wood or a surveyor was reading a dead man's notebook by lamplight, and none of them yet knew that the machinery had begun to turn — slow, oiled, patient, the machinery of consolidation, which asked no hatred of anyone and required only that the paper be obeyed.

Hale banked the stove, straightened the folders until their edges aligned, and rang for Pell.

"Send word to Dree," he said. "Tell her the high country needs tidying before the snow."
