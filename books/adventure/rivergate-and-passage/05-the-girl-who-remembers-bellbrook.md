## 5. The Girl Who Remembers Bellbrook

The morning Odile met the girl who remembered a town that did not exist, she was measuring the frost line on the old Kettle Spur with a steel tape and a stub of carpenter's pencil, because that was what she did instead of living.

Three years she had been at it. Three winters in the cabin above the Rivergate yards, three springs walking the closed lines with her level and her chain, recording elevations for no one, filing reports to an office that had stopped answering, keeping a ledger so meticulous it could have shamed a cathedral. The work was the point. The work was a way of counting that asked nothing of her. A chain was sixty-six feet; a mile was eighty chains; a day was however many miles her boots could argue out of the shale. Miles, not years. She had learned to think that way after Wren, and she guarded the habit like a coal carried in cupped hands.

The Kettle Spur was a dead branch off the main grade, abandoned before the war, its rails pulled up somewhere south of the third culvert so that only the roadbed remained—a long gray scar stitched through the scree, straight as a ruled line. Odile liked dead lines. They didn't pretend. A working line lied to you constantly about where you were going; a dead one just said *here I am, going nowhere*, and let you walk alongside it in peace.

She was forty chains from the cabin when she smelled woodsmoke.

Not her own—hers was birch and coal, banked low since dawn. This smoke came riding down the valley on a thin north wind, sweet with pine pitch, and it was wrong in every particular. Wrong direction. Wrong fuel. Wrong season for anyone to be up here at all, with the dry season dying and the first hard weather maybe two weeks out. She stood on the roadbed with her tape still unspooled between her hands and listened the way she'd taught herself to listen in these hills: not for sounds but for absences. No hammer. No axe. No voice. Just smoke, thin as a rumor, threading down through the larches.

Odile coiled her tape, clipped it to her belt, and went toward the smell with her thumb hooked under the strap of her surveyor's bag, where the little hatchet rode.

* * *

She found the camp—or rather, the camp declined to be found until it chose to be—in a fold of ground below the fourth trestle abutment, where a spring seeped out of the hillside and kept a pocket of grass green well into autumn. It was the kind of place Odile herself would have chosen, which annoyed her, and it was hidden the way animals hide things: not concealed, exactly, but arranged so that your eye slid off it. A lean-to of tarpaper and larch poles. A fire ring built small and hot, burning dry pitchwood that gave light without much smoke. Snares hung along the seep, four of them, set by someone who understood water the way trappers understand it—which is to say, better than most engineers.

And there were things. That was the word her mind supplied, standing at the edge of the green pocket with the wind at her back: *things*. A tin plate polished bright. A comb carved from antler. A row of river stones laid out in a curve, each one painted with a single white dot. A child's collection grown strange and deliberate, the way a magpie's hoard becomes architecture if the magpie lives long enough.

"You're standing on my sightline."

The voice came from above and behind, and Odile did not flinch, which cost her something. She turned slowly.

The girl was crouched on the abutment's shoulder, ten feet up, barefoot on cold stone in late October, wearing a coat made of two coats sewn together and a wool cap with the earflaps cut off. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Dark hair chopped short with a knife. Eyes the flat amber of creek water over sand, watching Odile with the total, unhurried attention of a hawk deciding whether a thing is food or weather.

"I wasn't aware this was your line," Odile said.

"It isn't." The girl tilted her head. "It's nobody's. That's why I'm here."

"That's not how ownership works."

"It's exactly how ownership works. You own what somebody will come and take away from you if you don't defend it. Nobody comes here." She said it without bitterness, as a fact of physics. "So it's mine the way the moon is mine. Which is plenty."

Odile looked at her a long moment. Feral children turned up on the margins of the rail country every few years—kids whose families had failed or fled or been failed, raised partway by the woods and partway by the maintenance sheds. Usually they were furtive and half-starved and dumb with fear. This one was none of those things. This one was fed, and warm, and looking at Odile like Odile was the anomaly. Which, standing there in her government-issue canvas with her steel tape and her ledger, Odile supposed she was.

"You're the one in the cabin past the switchback," the girl said. "The surveyor. You burn birch. You walk the same eight lines every month and write in a book. You talk to yourself when you're leveling, but only numbers."

A cold thread went down Odile's spine, entirely separate from the wind. "How long have you been watching me?"

"Since the second summer." The girl hopped down from the abutment, landing soft as thrown cloth, and Odile saw that her feet were calloused like boot leather. "You never saw me. You see rails, mostly. And distances." She said it without malice, and somehow that was worse. "I'm Tamsin."

"Odile Lockridge."

"I know. Your name's stenciled inside your instrument case. You left it open once, at the third culvert, for eleven minutes while you chased a marmot off your lunch." Tamsin smiled then, quick and crooked, and for a moment she was only a kid, and the wildness in her face rearranged itself into something almost shy. "I've been wanting to talk to you for a year. But you always looked like talking would break you."

Odile had no answer for that, because it was true.

"I smelled your fire," she said instead. "You're burning pitchwood. It's early for winter camp. If you're staying up here past the rains, you'll want drier stores than—"

"Bellbrook burned pitchwood," Tamsin said.

The name landed in the green pocket and sat there.

"What?"

"Bellbrook. That's where I'm from. We burned pitchwood because the orchard prunings made too much ash, and ash in the air settled on the drying racks and ruined the fruit leather. Papa used to say we were the only town in the range that smelled like a candle factory all September." She said it easily, the way you'd recite your own address. Then she frowned, reading something in Odile's face. "What?"

"There's no Bellbrook," Odile said. "In this range. I've walked every named settlement on the provincial rolls and half the unnamed ones. There's Rivergate, Corrie Mills, Ashford Halt, the mining camps at Dunlow. There is no Bellbrook."

Tamsin looked at her the way you look at someone who has just insisted, politely and with great confidence, that the sky is below the ground.

"Yes there is," she said. "I was born there."

* * *

They sat at the edge of the fire ring, at Tamsin's invitation, which had been issued with the grave courtesy of someone hosting tea in a parlor rather than a stranger in a hollow under a dead trestle. Odile told herself she was staying for the warmth. She told herself she was gathering intelligence about the vanished survey team, whose trail she had followed up the shale two days earlier and lost among stones that held no prints. She told herself several things, and underneath them all ran a single unquiet sentence: *she said fences, orchards, a schoolhouse bell, and Reeve's ledger said fences, orchards, a schoolhouse bell.*

Because that was the thing. Two nights ago, hunched over Anselm Reeve's recovered journal by lamplight, Odile had read elevation readings for a valley listed in every official document as barren, uninhabited, unwatered—and beside those readings, in Reeve's cramped hand, notes like a man arguing with his own instruments: *stone fence running true for 400 yds, surveyed alignment? apple stock, mature, rows N–S. BELL on ridge, cast bronze, rings at hours.* She had assumed Reeve had gone mad up there, or been drunk, or been testing his juniors. She had closed the ledger and not slept, and told herself in the morning that grief made people credulous, and she of all people knew that.

Now a barefoot girl was telling her about the fruit leather.

"Tell me about it," Odile said. Carefully. The way you approach a snared animal. "Bellbrook. From the beginning."

Tamsin pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them, and her eyes went somewhere else—not vague, not dreamy, but *precisely* elsewhere, like a surveyor sighting down a line to a distant target.

"The town sits in a valley that runs east-west, so the sun comes up the length of the street. Forty-one houses, not counting the mill house and the school. The street's called the Long Row because it's longer than any street needs to be—Papa said the founders wanted room to argue. There's a stone fence on the north side that my great-grandfather built, and it runs true, no dips, because he was a mason and he'd have died before he let a fence wander. The orchard's above the town on the south slope, apples and a few pears, planted in rows north to south so they shade evenly. Under the orchard there's a spring house dug into the hillside, and the water there stays so cold it hurts your teeth even in August."

She was speaking faster now, the words coming up out of her like water finding an old channel.

"The school stands at the west end of the Long Row, and it has a bell—a real bronze bell, not iron—brought over the pass on a sledge before the railroad came, by six men and a woman named Harrow who everyone said was stronger than any of the six. It rings at eight and noon and four, and at any hour when something happens worth ringing for. When I was seven the Kessler twins got lost up the shale and the bell rang until midnight and everyone climbed around with lanterns, and when they found the boys the bell rang again just because it could." Tamsin's voice caught, very slightly, on the next part. "There's a bench under the big pear tree at the orchard gate. Mama sat there. She had a blue scarf she wore even in summer. I can tell you the exact shape of the crack in that bench. I fell asleep on it more times than I can count."

She stopped. Looked at Odile. The firelight moved in her amber eyes.

"Well?" she said. "Do I sound crazy?"

Odile did not answer immediately, because she was doing something she despised: believing.

It wasn't the detail alone—liars could manufacture detail, though rarely this kind, this *load-bearing* kind, detail that interlocked, that answered questions nobody had asked yet. It was the specificity of orientation. North-south orchard rows. East-west valley. A fence running true for a stated distance. These were not a child's memories; these were a surveyor's facts, worn smooth by repetition, carried in a body the way railmen carry grades and curves. And beneath the facts ran something else, something that cracked Odile's cynicism not with force but with precision, like a wedge driven along the grain:

The girl remembered *weather*. She remembered that the valley held the evening light an extra twenty minutes because of its angle. She remembered the smell of the candle-factory Septembers. You could invent a town. You could not invent, unprompted, the way evening light behaves in a valley oriented east-west, unless you had stood in it.

"Nobody's saying crazy," Odile said at last. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. "I'm saying there's no such place on any map I've ever seen. Provincial rolls, railway alignments, postal routes, tax schedules. I've spent three years in this range. I know its valleys like my own hands."

"Then your maps are wrong," Tamsin said simply.

"They're not wrong. They're *mine*. I've measured the ground myself. I've run levels through every drainage in the western range, and there are exactly zero valleys matching what you describe with—" Odile stopped.

With what? With a stone fence running true for four hundred yards. With an orchard in rows north to south. With a spring house dug into a south slope. With a bronze bell.

Reeve's ledger sat thirty feet away in her surveyor's bag, and it might as well have been sitting in her lap, open, its pages breathing.

"Show me," Odile said.

Tamsin blinked. "Show you what?"

"The valley. Where you think it is. Where you remember it being." Odile was on her feet now, and her heart was doing something it hadn't done in three years, which was beating ahead of her caution. "Because I have walked this range with a level and a chain for a thousand miles, and if there is a stone fence anywhere on the north side of any valley in it, I would know. I would have *stepped over it*. So either you're the best liar I've ever met, or—"

"Or the maps are wrong," Tamsin said again, patiently, the way you speak to someone climbing a stair in the dark who has missed a step. She rose too, barefoot and certain, and pointed west-northwest, up the spine of the range toward the high country where the shale slopes began and where, two weeks ago, a government survey team had walked into the mountains and left no footprints behind.

"It's up there. Past the shoulder. In the valley the maps call the Hollow." She glanced back at Odile, and her crooked smile returned, but changed now, older. "Except it isn't called the Hollow. It's called Bellbrook, and it has forty-one houses, and a school bell, and my mother's bench under the pear tree. And I don't know why nobody remembers it except me."

The wind came down the valley and stirred the fire, and sparks went up through the larch branches like a handful of thrown stars, and Odile Lockridge—who had spent three years trusting nothing in this world but steel tape and level bubble and the honest arithmetic of distance—heard herself say:

"First light tomorrow. Bring boots."

"I don't own boots."

"Then bring whatever you've got, because we're going up there, and I'm going to measure every foot of that valley, and one of us is going to find out what's been lying to me—the land, or the maps." She shouldered her bag. "And God help whoever made the difference."

Behind her, faint under the wind, Tamsin began—without seeming to notice she was doing it—to hum. Four notes, rising and falling, patient as a pendulum.

"What's that?" Odile asked.

Tamsin looked surprised at herself. "The bell song. Everyone in Bellbrook knew it. It's what the bell sounds like when it rings for good news." She hummed it once more, committing it to the morning, and the sound went up the darkening valley toward the high country like a message sent ahead of them both.
