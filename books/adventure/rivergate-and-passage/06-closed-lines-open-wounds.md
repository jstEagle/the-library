## 6. Closed Lines, Open Wounds

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, and Odile woke to the particular silence of wet shale drying in the cold—a ticking, settling sound, like the mountain counting its own bones. Tamsin was already awake. Of course she was. The girl sat cross-legged on the cabin floor with the last of the oat bread in her lap, tearing it into pieces too small to eat and not eating them.

"You slept," Tamsin said. It wasn't an accusation, exactly. It was closer to wonder, as though sleep were a country she'd heard of but never been issued papers for.

"Barely." Odile swung her feet down onto the cold boards. Her survey chest sat open where she'd left it, the brass instruments gleaming dully in the grey light, and beside it lay the ledger she'd taken from Anselm Reeve's abandoned camp—Reeve's careful hand marching across the pages like a column of soldiers who didn't know yet that they'd been ordered into nothing.

She had dreamed about her brother again. Not the fall itself—she never dreamed the fall—but the morning of it, the way he'd whistled while he checked his gauge glasses, three notes rising, the way he always did when the fog was thick on the high cuttings. She pushed the dream down where she kept all such things, in the sealed chamber behind her sternum, and stood up.

"Get your boots," she said. "We're going to test your town."

Tamsin's head came up sharply. "Test it?"

"You told me things last night. A bend in the river shaped like a drawn bow. A shelf of white rock above the orchards. A spring that ran red with iron and stained the well stones." Odile pulled on her coat, working her arms into it with the brisk violence of someone refusing to be gentle with herself. "I have measured this range for three years, girl. Every drainage, every shoulder, every cut. If Bellbrook sits where you say it sits, then I have walked over its grave a dozen times and never once looked down."

"And if I'm wrong?" Tamsin asked quietly.

Odile paused at the door, one hand on the latch. Outside, the valley below her cabin lay in layers of mist, the old spur line vanishing into it like a sentence trailing off unfinished.

"Then you're wrong," she said, "and we've both lost something. But if you're right—" She didn't finish it. She couldn't, not yet. "Boots."

* * *

They went up through the birch scrub first, following a game trail that Odile had never bothered to name because it led nowhere worth naming. That was the thing about her surveys: she had spent three years cataloguing what mattered to the railroad—grade, bearing, load-bearing shale, water crossings—and everything else had passed beneath her instruments like scenery past a train window. She had measured the mountains the way a tax assessor measures a house. Squarely, thoroughly, and without once asking who lived there.

Tamsin walked ahead of her, and Odile watched how she moved. It wasn't a hiker's walk. It was a homing instinct—the girl's body reading the terrain the way Reeve's instruments read elevation, some older instrument running behind her eyes. Twice she stopped at forks in the trail and chose without hesitating, and both times, when Odile checked her pocket compass afterward against the direction they'd come, the girl had chosen the truer line.

"How do you know that?" Odile asked, after the second time.

"I don't know," Tamsin said. "My feet know."

"Feet don't remember."

"These do."

The mist thinned as they climbed, and the range opened up around them in its long grey folds. Odile carried the ledger in her satchel along with her own field books—three years of them, oilcloth-bound, their spines cracked from use. She had brought them almost as a weapon. *Here is my data,* she wanted to say to whatever waited up there. *Here are my numbers. Numbers don't lie.*

But she had already begun, somewhere in the night, to suspect that numbers could be made to lie by the people who printed them. And that suspicion sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"The spring first," Tamsin said. "If the spring's still red, then the rest will be true."

* * *

They found the spring two hours up, in a fold of the hillside where the shale gave way to a seam of older rock. Water seeped from under an overhang and pooled in a stone basin, and the basin's rim was stained the color of dried blood—iron oxide, laid down in rings, season upon season upon season. Some of those rings were old. Decades old. The staining pattern of a spring that had been drunk from for generations.

Odile crouched and put her fingers to the rim. The stone was worn smooth in a crescent, the way stone wears when hands and buckets and lips touch it year after year.

"There's no record of this," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "I surveyed this drainage in my second spring. There's a seep marked here—unpotable, minor flow, no notation of mineral content. No notation of use." She turned her field book to the page; the entry was there in her own handwriting, neat and certain and blind. *"Seep, east fold, negligible."*

"It's not negligible," Tamsin said. "It's the sweetest water on the mountain. Everyone said so. You had to let it stand overnight or the iron got into your teeth, but Mama kept a jug of it on the sill for the feverish ones." She knelt and cupped the water and drank, matter-of-fact, and Odile watched her throat work and felt the ground tilt very slightly under her knees.

Negligible. She had written *negligible* four feet from a spring that a whole town had depended on, and she had written it because the map she'd been given to correct had already decided nothing here needed correcting. She had surveyed inside the borders of a lie and called it thoroughness.

"Show me the bow in the river," she said.

* * *

The river bend took most of the day to reach, dropping down through scree and alder into the valley that every map in her possession called the Hollow Basin—uninhabited, unimproved, unsurveyed except for grade lines run through it twenty years ago by crews whose names had been struck from the index. Odile knew the basin the way she knew a closed room: she had traversed its northern rim twice, shot elevations across it, noted its floor as marshy, marginal, worthless.

From the rim, looking down, she saw it now.

The bend was exactly as Tamsin had described it—a long drawn arc of green water, curved like a bow held ready, and on the near bank, half-swallowed by alder and willow, the shapes of foundations. Not one or two. Dozens. Rectangles of lichen-grey stone laid out along lanes that still held their geometry beneath the years of growth, streets running true to a grid some surveyor had once staked with care. Farther along, a roofless gable stood against the sky, its chimney intact, and beyond it a square of ground fenced in rusted wire that could only have been an orchard—apple trees gone wild and leggy, but alive, still bearing small hard fruit that glinted among the leaves like dropped coins.

And on the low rise above the orchard, a bell tower. Roofless, its frame blackened by weather, but standing. The schoolhouse bell itself had fallen and lay half-buried in the grass at the tower's base, a great bronze mouth filled with earth and moss.

Odile stood at the rim for a long time without moving. The wind came up the valley and moved her hair and she did not feel it.

"I shot a sightline across this valley," she said finally. "Two years ago. From that shoulder—" she pointed west, to a spur she knew intimately, whose every contour lived in her hands like a face, "—to the far ridge. My line passed directly over that tower. Directly over it. And in my field book I wrote *open basin, no structures.*"

"Because your book was printed before you ever opened it," Tamsin said. She said it without cruelty, the way children state terrible things. "That's what happened to us. They didn't burn the town. They burned the paper."

Odile descended the slope on legs that felt borrowed. In the lanes between the foundations she walked slowly, touching things. A doorstone worn into a shallow saddle by feet. A hinge rusted to lace, still screwed to a post that held no gate. A child's slate, unbroken, lying face-down in the weeds; she turned it over and found a single word scratched in the old slate pencil, faded but legible: *arithmetic*, spelled wrong, corrected, spelled wrong again.

Her instruments hung at her hip and she did not take them out. What would she measure? The town was real. The town had always been real. The only fiction was the page in her field book, written in her own honest hand, fed to her by maps that had been laundered before they ever reached her.

She thought of her brother then—not the fall, not the whistle in the fog, but something he'd said once, laughing, his boot on a rail: *You can't cheat a level, Odile. Water finds its own height. That's the whole beauty of it.* And she understood, standing in the dead town's street, that Corvus Hale had done precisely the opposite. He had cheated the level. He had poured the landscape into new channels on paper and let reality run dry in the old ones, and everyone downstream of his pen—including her—had measured the flood and called it the truth.

The vertigo came on her all at once. She put her hand flat against a foundation stone to steady herself, and the stone was warm from the afternoon sun, and that warmth—that dumb, patient, physical fact—was the only thing holding the world together. The land was real. The land had never lied. Only the records had, and records could be fought.

"Tamsin." Her voice was rough. "How many towns? How many places like this?"

The girl considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "Bellbrook," she said. "And Grandmother said there was a place up the Vane water that used to be on the maps. And a mining camp past the Shoulder. She said the government men came twice—once to count us, and once to stop counting us."

*Once to count us, and once to stop counting us.* Odile looked back at the bell in the grass, at the orchard glittering with its untended fruit, at the lanes running straight toward a future that had been amputated on paper. Three years she had spent measuring these mountains, believing herself the last honest set of eyes on them. And all that time the truth had been sitting in valleys she'd traversed, patient as water, waiting for someone to read it at its own height.

"We go back to the cabin," she said. "Tonight. I'm putting your memory against every page I own, line by line, mark by mark. And tomorrow—" She stopped. Tomorrow was a shape she hadn't let herself look at yet, but it was coming into focus now, hard-edged and inevitable, like a summit against a clearing sky.

"Tomorrow what?" Tamsin asked.

Odile picked up the fallen schoolhouse bell's rope—still hanging, rotted to a frayed cord—from its beam, and let it lie. Some bells you didn't ring until you were ready for what answered.

"Tomorrow I start drawing a map they can't erase," she said. "One copy. Hand-drawn. Hidden where paper fires can't reach it."

They climbed out of Bellbrook as the light went long and copper, and behind them the valley settled back into its official silence. But Odile's stride had changed, and she knew the girl noticed, because Tamsin lengthened her own steps to match it without being asked—as if they were both, now, walking on a line that had finally been staked.

It was full dark when they reached the cabin, and the dark was wrong. Down the slope, at the bend of the old spur, lantern-light moved where no lantern had any right to be—three lights, maybe four, swinging slow and deliberate, working their way uphill toward her door.
