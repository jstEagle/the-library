## 7. Hale's Men on the Spur

The mule came down the spur road an hour before dawn, and Odile heard it before she saw it—not the soft clop of a pack animal but the iron-shod strike of a government mount, shod for hard miles and indifferent to the quiet. She had been awake anyway. She was always awake at that hour, the hour when the mountain exhaled its cold into the valley and the windows of her cabin wept condensation down the glass. Three years of solitary surveying had tuned her ears like instruments: she could tell a deer from a man by the rhythm of his footfall on frost, could hear the difference between wind moving through the pines and wind moving through something the pines had to bend around.

This was neither. This was four sets of boots on shale, deliberate, spaced like men who had done this before.

She did not light her lamp. That was the first discipline of the old lines: never show a light you cannot account for. Instead she stood at the window with the shutter cracked two fingers' width and watched them come up the switchback through the grey, their lanterns hooded, their breath pluming. Four of them, and leading them on the mule a woman who sat her saddle the way Odile sat a transit stool—perfectly still, perfectly economical, as though motion were a currency she refused to waste.

Odile knew the silhouette without knowing the name. Government issue coat, cut long, collar up. A leather document case slung across the body like a bandolier. And riding not in the saddlebags but strapped flat against the mule's flank, wrapped in oilcloth, something with the rigid rectangular geometry of a strongbox.

Her hand went, without her deciding it, to the shelf where Reeve's ledger lay.

It had been three days since she'd decoded the elevation columns, three days since the word *orchard* had surfaced from between the numbers like a body rising in still water. Three days of telling herself that a vanished survey team was a tragedy, perhaps an accident—rockfall, weather, the mountain doing what mountains do—and that a ledger full of impossible readings was grief playing tricks with arithmetic. Her brother had taught her better than that. *Numbers don't lie, Odie,* he used to say, tapping the page with a pencil stub. *People lie about numbers. Learn the difference and you'll never be fooled twice.*

She had been fooled once already, by the silence after his train went off the closed section above Kestrel Cut. She would not be fooled twice.

The riders halted at the bottom of her path. The woman dismounted first, and even in the pre-dawn murk Odile could see the economy of her: medium height, no wasted line anywhere, hair bound back so tight it looked poured rather than grown. She said something to her men—Odile read it from the shape of her mouth more than heard it through the glass—and two of them peeled off toward the woodshed while the other two came up the path itself, unhurried, the way officials walk when they are certain the door will open.

It would open. It always opened. That was the whole architecture of power in Rivergate: they never had to break anything, because everything broke for them in advance.

Odile took the ledger from the shelf. It weighed almost nothing—a surveyor's field book, canvas-bound, its corners furred soft from Anselm Reeve's pocket. But it felt in her hands like a live rail, humming with everything written inside it: elevations for a valley that officially held no one, fences where no fences were charted, a schoolhouse bell rung in a town that had been subtracted from the world. Somewhere under those numbers was her brother's ghost too, because every closed line in this range ran through the place he died, and she had measured that ground so many times she knew its faults the way other women knew their own hands.

Not the cabin. They would search the cabin; cabins were the first thing anyone searched, and hers had exactly one room and nowhere a mouse could hide that a man couldn't. The culvert, then—the old stone drain where the spring melt crossed under the abandoned spur, forty paces below the house, its mouth screened by alder and blackberry gone feral over fifteen summers. She had crawled into it once, years ago, chasing a dropped chaining pin, and remembered the dry chamber behind the first bend where the masons had left a gap in their rubble. Cold, dark, dry. A grave for paper, if paper must be buried.

She wrapped the ledger in the oilskin she kept for instrument covers, pressed it flat against her stomach beneath her coat, and went out the back door into the blue-black cold as the knock came at the front.

* * *

They let themselves in anyway. Of course they did. She heard the door open behind her as she rounded the alders, heard boots on her floorboards, heard one of them say, "Ma'am's out back," in the toneless voice of a man reporting weather. Running would have been worse than walking. She walked.

The woman waited by the woodpile, and up close she resolved into particulars that Odile would carry afterward like splinters: eyes the color of creek ice, a scar splitting one eyebrow into a small white Y, hands bare despite the cold and utterly steady. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had learned that quiet frightened people more thoroughly than shouting ever could.

"Miss Lockridge." Not a question. "Dree. Provincial administration. We're conducting a records audit along the closed sections."

"At dawn."

"The mountain doesn't keep office hours." Dree's gaze moved past her shoulder to the cabin, then came back with the unhurried weight of a level bubble finding center. "You've been surveying these lines for some time. Alone. Unlicensed."

"Closed lines don't require licenses. They require tolerance, and I have that in writing from your predecessor's predecessor."

"Administrations change." Dree smiled, and it was the worst thing yet, because it reached her eyes and made them friendly while remaining, underneath, exactly as cold. "May I see your field books?"

"They're in the cabin."

"Then let's go in out of the cold, and you can show me what three years of trespassing bought you."

Inside, the search was already well underway, conducted with a courtesy that made it worse. One man stood at her shelves lifting each tin of negatives and setting it back precisely where it had been, as if the violation were somehow smaller if the objects returned to their exact coordinates. Another had her desk drawer open and was reading her brother's last letter—the only letter, the one that had come with the box of his effects—holding it in both gloved hands like a specimen.

"Put that down." Her own voice surprised her. It came out flat and hard, a spike driven clean.

The man looked at Dree. Dree looked at Odile. For a moment the cabin held nothing but the tick of the stove cooling and the sound of Odile's own blood.

"Sensitive document?" Dree asked mildly.

"It's my brother's death notice. If your audit requires reading dead men's mail, I'd like the regulation number."

Something moved behind Dree's eyes—recalculation, brief and quick as a bird turning in flight. She crossed the room, took the letter from her man, and set it back in the drawer herself, squaring its edges with two fingers. "We're not here for grief, Miss Lockridge. We're here for paper. Recent paper. Survey records, correspondence, anything touching the upper pass or the—" a fractional pause, smooth as a rail joint, "—the eastern tributaries."

*The eastern tributaries.* Bellbrook's valley, named by its absence. Odile felt the ledger's oilskin bulk against her ribs and made her face do what faces in Rivergate had learned to do around administrators: nothing at all.

"My recent work is all on the western spurs. Grade surveys. You're welcome to copy anything you like; I'll want a receipt."

"You'll get one." Dree turned a slow circle in the middle of the single room, taking in the transit case, the rolled charts, the cot, the photograph of two children on a locomotive pilot that Odile had never been able to take down. "You know what strikes me about you, Miss Lockridge? You live like a woman who's decided no one is coming. No neighbors, no letters, no church, no market runs. Just you and the numbers." She stopped, facing her. "That kind of solitude is either a wound or a strategy. My experience says usually both."

"And what does your experience say happens next?"

"Next," Dree said, "I give you a warning, because the administrator is a merciful man and prefers warnings to incidents." From the document case she drew a printed sheet, heavy stock, embossed seal, and laid it on the desk with the care of a woman dealing cards she already knew the value of. "By order of the regional administration, the high country above the Kestrel watershed is closed to all private travel pending resurvey. Effective immediately. Violators will be detained."

Odile looked at the order and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, like cold water finding a crack, that the paper in her coat was now worth more than her life. They were sealing the range. Whatever had happened to Anselm Reeve's team, whatever Hale was erasing, the erasure was entering its final phase—and she was standing in it holding the only unaltered record of what had been there before.

"I'll need a receipt for the inspection," she said. "And your name, spelled properly, for my own records."

Dree wrote it out without hesitation—*M. Dree, Warden-Inspector, Third District*—in a hand so neat it looked typeset. At the door she paused, half-turned, and for just a moment the official mask slipped sideways, showing something older and wearier underneath, something that might once have known a valley and a bell.

"Burn your lamp early tonight, Miss Lockridge," she said. "A lit window tells people you're home. Home is safe." Then she was gone, and the boots went down the switchback, and the mule's iron shoes struck sparks of sound off the frozen rock until the morning swallowed them.

* * *

Odile did not burn her lamp early. She burned it late, and dim, and only after she had spent two hours being, very carefully, a woman doing nothing.

She washed her dishes. She split kindling within sight of the road, letting any watcher see the ordinary choreography of a solitary life. She re-hung the shutters that didn't need re-hanging. And all the while her mind ran the numbers the way it always ran when fear got loud: distance to the culvert, forty paces; time since the riders left, three hours, maybe four; probability of a watcher left behind, high—they had struck her as thorough—but probability of a watcher patient enough to sit in the alder cold all day, lower. Bureaucracy worked in office hours. Even its wolves knocked at dawn and went home to tea.

At full dark she went out the back with a sack of seed potatoes over her arm, in case the shape of a woman carrying potatoes read differently than the shape of a woman carrying secrets. The frost had hardened; her breath hung behind her like a scarf. At the culvert she knelt, parted the blackberry canes with a gentleness that surprised her, and wormed into the stone throat of the mountain on her elbows.

Inside, the dark smelled of cold granite and old water. She found the masons' gap by touch, eased the oilskin bundle into it, and packed rubble around it until the wall looked like nothing but itself. Then she stayed a moment longer than she needed to, forehead nearly touching the stone, and spoke aloud in the smallest voice she had used in three years.

"Hold," she told it. "Whatever you are—hold."

Crawling out, she stood in the alder shadow and looked up at her own cabin, lamplit now, one warm square of yellow in all that blackness. *A lit window tells people you're home. Home is safe.*

Dree had meant it as instruction. Odile received it as declaration of war.

Because here was the thing the warning had shown her, the way a flash flood shows a farmer where his land actually drains: she had spent three years believing her invisibility was a shelter, and it had never been a shelter at all. It had been a cage she'd mistaken for one. They had let her measure her lonely miles because measuring dead lines threatened no one—and the moment her numbers touched something living, four riders came up the spur before sunrise. Invisibility wasn't safety. It was simply the interval before being noticed, and the interval had ended.

She thought of Anselm Reeve's careful columns. She thought of a schoolhouse bell ringing in a town that no longer existed, and a girl she hadn't met yet who would swear she'd been born there. She thought of her brother, somewhere under the snowfields of the upper pass, waiting three years for someone to come asking the right questions.

Eleven days, maybe fewer, until the autumn rains closed the pass. After that, snow, and after snow, spring—and by spring, whatever papers remained in the capital archive would be ash or amendment, and Bellbrook would be gone the way the others had gone, dissolved into administrative silence, unmourned because unrecorded.

Odile went inside, banked the stove, and pulled her charts across the table. Not the ones she'd shown Dree—the real ones, the working set, the alignments she'd drawn by lamplight over three winters, accurate to the chain, honest to the degree. She spread them out and began, with a pencil worn to a stub, to plan a route through the high country that the government had just declared closed to her.

Outside, far up the valley, the wind changed and brought the first smell of coming rain.

She counted the days on her fingers, and for the first time in three years, the counting felt less like mourning and more like a clock.
