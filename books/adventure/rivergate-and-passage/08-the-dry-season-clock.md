## 8. The Dry Season Clock

The almanac hung on its nail by the door like a thing under arrest, and Odile had been avoiding its eyes for three days.

She knew why. The almanac didn't lie, and she had spent three years building a life out of the things it didn't say. Rain gauges, frost tables, the little printed curve of the dry season's decline — she had never needed them before. Her work had no seasons. A closed line was a closed line in February and in August alike, and she walked it when she walked it, and the miles did not care what month the miles were in.

But the ledger lay open on her table now, Anselm Reeve's cramped figures marching across its pages like a column of ants that had somewhere urgent to be, and the ledger cared very much what month it was. Because the ledger was not the end of anything. It was the beginning of a journey, and journeys — she remembered this dimly, the way you remember a language you spoke as a child — journeys ran on time.

She made herself stand up and take the almanac down.

The paper was soft at the corners from three years of hanging untouched. She flattened it on the table beside the ledger, weighted its corners with her dividers, a rail spike, and the tin cup Tamsin had drunk from that morning and then, in the manner of the girl, simply set down wherever her hands had been when her hands were done with it.

Outside, the light had that particular quality Odile had learned to distrust. It came slanting through the smoke haze off the eastern fires, amber and forgiving, making everything look like a painting of itself. The dry season's last kindness. She had seen it every autumn of her life on the range and had never once thought of it as a countdown. It was just the way the light got before the weather changed. Things got to be beautiful right before they got to be dangerous. Her brother used to say that about rivers.

She did not let herself think about her brother. Not yet. First the arithmetic.

* * *

She worked the way she had been taught to work, which was the way her father had worked, which was the way the old alignment men had worked before either of them: everything on paper, nothing in the head, the head being a liar with excellent penmanship.

The dry season, per the almanac, ended on the twenty-sixth. That was not a guess; that was sixty years of gauge readings kept at the Rivergate station house, and the rains came within two days of the twenty-sixth nine years in ten. The rains lasted — she checked the marginal notes, the fine pencil annotations of some long-dead stationmaster who had clearly loved the numbers more than the trains — between four and nine days. After the rains, the first snows. On the high passes, sometimes the first snows came *with* the rains, and then the range simply closed, the way a book closes, and did not open again until late spring.

Eleven days of safe travel. Perhaps twelve, if the twenty-sixth came late. Perhaps nine, if it came angry.

She wrote *11* on the back of one of her own alignment sheets and looked at it for a long while.

Then she measured the distance, and this was where the arithmetic turned from arithmetic into something with teeth.

The capital archive sat beyond the range, in the river city of Corvane, four days by rail on the open southern line. But the southern line was Hale's line, ran through Hale's junction towns, past Hale's men, and she had a face that was probably already on a description board by now and papers that would not survive a second look. The southern route was a trap with a schedule.

That left the range itself. The old survey route. The one Reeve's team had gone in by and not come out of.

She spread her charts — her own charts, three years of them, the truest record of that country that existed anywhere, because she had never had a reason to falsify anything and no one had ever thought her work worth checking. She traced the route with her finger and let the numbers come.

To the shoulder of the range by the maintenance traverse: two days, hard going, the shale slopes where no tracks had led out of the empty camp. Through the shoulder — the old tunnel, if it was passable, and her charts said *unverified* in her own hand, which meant she had looked at the portal and decided she would rather not know: one day if it went, three if it didn't and they had to climb over. Then the high traverse along the spine, exposed, waterless in the dry season and suicidal after it: three days. Then the descent to the pass road and the last stretch to Corvane: two days more, if the pass road was open, if the rains hadn't taken a culvert out, if the world cooperated, which the world had never once done for her in thirty-eight years.

She added it up. Seven days of everything going right. Eleven days of everything going right being possible.

Four days of margin. Four days in which every river crossing held, every rockfall had already fallen, every boot held together, every body did what it was asked.

She sat back. The smoke-hazed light had moved across the table while she worked, and now it lay across Reeve's ledger, and the elevation figures for the uninhabited valley caught the sun — the figures that were not uninhabited, that had fences in them, orchards, a schoolhouse bell, a town called Bellbrook that Tamsin carried around inside her skull like a coal carried in cupped hands.

*Eleven days,* she thought. *And then the range shuts, and whatever proof there is stays shut inside it until spring. And by spring, Hale will have had a winter to finish the job. To find the girl. To find me. To burn whatever the archive still holds.*

She looked at the number *11* again, and she understood that she had already decided. She had probably decided the moment she decoded the ledger. The arithmetic wasn't a decision; it was a confession. It was her mind catching up to what her feet had been saying for three days, which was: *pack.*

The strange thing — the thing she sat with for a long moment, the tin cup cool under her hand — was that the feeling wasn't dread. She had expected dread. Dread was the honest response to the numbers on the page. But what she felt was closer to what she imagined other people felt on the first morning of a journey they'd chosen. Something in her chest unclenching that she hadn't known was clenched. Three years of measuring miles because miles didn't accumulate into anything, miles just lay end to end like sleepers on a track, going nowhere, meaning nothing. And here was a distance that *meant* something. Here was a distance with a clock on it.

She got out her pack and began, methodically, to lie to herself about how little she could carry.

* * *

The lie collapsed by evening, as such lies do. The pack sat on the floor by the door looking like what it was: the entire portable contents of a life, and not a light one. Instruments she could not justify and would not leave. The ledger, wrapped in oilcloth and then in her spare shirt. Charts. The good rope. The little brass spirit level her father had given her, which weighed nothing and which she carried everywhere anyway, the way other people carried saints' medals. Dried meat, hard bread, the last of the coffee hoarded against a morning exactly like the ones ahead. A storm shell that had never been worn and would very likely be worn to destruction.

She was re-wrapping the dividers for the third time — a task that had nothing to do with the dividers and everything to do with her hands needing employment while her mind circled the thing it was afraid to say — when she heard the step on the porch.

She had heard it every evening for two weeks now. Light, deliberate, a walker's step, not a sneak's. Tamsin never knocked. Knocking, Odile had gathered, was a town behavior, and Tamsin's relationship with towns was complicated at best, and currently consisted of one town that did not exist and everyone in it.

The door opened and the girl stood in it, backlit by the last amber of the day, a brace of something feathered hanging from her belt and a look on her face that Odile had learned to read as *I have been thinking, and this is dangerous for both of us.*

"You've packed," Tamsin said.

"I've packed."

"You're going over the range."

"I'm going to Corvane. The range is between here and there."

"That's what I said." The girl came in without invitation, which was also a settled matter between them, and put the birds down inside the door with the unconscious tidiness of someone raised by a mother who had cared about such things, in a house that had had a kitchen, in a town that had had a name. She looked at the pack. She looked at the almanac lying open on the table. She looked at the number Odile had written and never erased: *11.*

"Eleven days," Tamsin said.

"Give or take."

"The rains come on the twenty-sixth. My mother said the rains always came on the twenty-sixth, and my father said that was nonsense, and the rains always came on the twenty-sixth." She said it flatly, the way she said everything about Bellbrook — no performance of grief, no asking for sympathy, just the facts of a place, offered like survey figures. Odile had stopped wondering how the girl could bear it. She had begun to understand that the bearing was the whole point. Tamsin kept Bellbrook alive by refusing to make it tragic. Tragic things got buried. Bellbrook was not going to be buried.

"Then we're agreed on the calendar," Odile said. "That's more than most survey parties manage."

"I'm coming."

And there it was. The thing her mind had been circling all afternoon, and the girl had simply walked up to it in the doorway and put her hand on it.

"Tamsin."

"You knew I was going to say it. You've known for two days. That's why you've been weird about the dividers."

Odile looked down at the dividers, betrayed by her own hands. "The range is no place—"

"The range is *my* place." Not loud. Just final, the way bedrock is final. "You've been walking those lines for three years and you still take the low fork at Miller's Cutting because a map told you to, and the low fork floods every spring, and the high fork is dry and safe and doesn't appear on any map because the people who walked it for two hundred years didn't write maps, they wrote *paths*, and I know the paths." She pulled the birds' feathers absently, one at a time, thinking. "You want to get to Corvane before the snow. You have seven days of route and eleven days of weather. You need every hour, and you need the hours that aren't on your charts. Those are mine."

Odile opened her mouth to construct the argument — the danger, the age of her, the responsibility, all the respectable scaffolding of a no — and found that she couldn't. Not because the arguments weren't there. They were, all of them, load-bearing. But because she had spent the afternoon discovering that she was not going to Corvane out of duty. She was going because a town had been stolen and a girl was its last living record, and if the record burned with the archive, or drowned in a cutting, or froze on a traverse because Odile had miscalculated a margin, then the theft was complete, and Odile Lockridge would have spent three years measuring a country and understood none of it.

And a girl who had survived alone on a closed mountain line since she was — how old? Twelve? Thirteen? — did not need Odile's protection. She needed Odile's instruments. The protection ran the other way, and they both knew it.

"You walk," Odile said at last. "All day. You don't complain, you don't slow the party, and when I say a slope is unsafe, we go around it, even if your paths say otherwise. My instruments have been right about this range for three years."

"Your instruments have been right about the *land*," Tamsin said. "The land isn't the thing that's been lying."

It was, Odile thought, the most precisely aimed sentence anyone had ever said to her.

"First light," she said. "We make the shale slopes by midmorning, camp at the tree line. Two days to the shoulder if the weather holds."

Tamsin nodded, once, and then did something Odile had not seen her do before. She crossed the small cabin and stood at the table, over the almanac and the ledger and the number 11, and she put her finger on the elevation figures for the valley that was not uninhabited, and she read them. Not the way a child reads. The way an engineer reads.

"Two thousand one hundred and forty feet at the schoolhouse," she said. "That's right. The bell tower was the high point; you could see the whole valley from the belfry. My mother rang it on the twenty-sixth, too. Not for the rains. For the first frost. She said frost and rain were the two clocks God kept, and everything else was gossip."

She took her finger off the page.

"I'm going to ring that bell again," she said. "Or I'm going to make somebody in Corvane wish I could."

* * *

Odile did not sleep. She told herself she was checking the pack, and for a while she was, but past midnight the checking became a pretext and she let it go and sat in the chair by the cold stove with the last inch of coffee, doing the thing she had forbidden herself on the almanac morning.

She thought about her brother.

He had gone onto these same lines in the wet year, with a party, with papers, with permission — everything she had, everything Reeve's team had had, everything the range required on its face and ignored underneath. And the range had taken him, or something on the range had, and the inquiry had written *misadventure* in the beautiful indifferent hand that inquiries used, and she had come up to Rivergate to measure the miles where he had stopped measuring anything, and had stayed, because miles were the only unit of grief that didn't accumulate.

Now she was going in with eleven days on the clock, and the difference between her and him, she thought, staring into the dead stove, was not courage or preparation or the girl asleep — she'd checked, twice, pretending it was about the fire — curled in the corner with her back to the wall and her boots on, the way you sleep when you have learned what nights are for.

The difference was that she finally had something to carry *toward*.

It was not a comforting thought. It was a terrifying one, and she held it anyway, the way you hold a spirit level on an unstable footing: carefully, with both hands, knowing the bubble would not settle until the ground did.

* * *

First light came up gray and windless, the smoke haze thicker, the light gone from amber to a dull pewter that made the whole eastern sky look like unpolished steel. The dry season, spending its last coin.

They stood by the door in the pewter light, packs on, breath showing faintly. Odile looked back once at the cabin — three years of exile, one room, everything squared away because there had never been anyone to mess it up — and found she felt nothing she could name, except that it looked smaller from outside than it ever had from in.

Tamsin was already facing the range.

"Which way?" Odile asked, and heard, as she said it, that she had already surrendered the navigation, and that it was the right thing to do.

The girl pointed — not at the shale slopes, not at the route Odile's charts laid out, but north of them, at a fold in the ridgeline that Odile had looked at a hundred times and never once considered, because no map had ever put anything there.

"The paths," Tamsin said. "We'll beat the shale slopes by half a day. And Odile—" she turned, and in the pewter light her face was very young and very old at once, "—there's something you should know before we're up in it. About why the team didn't leave tracks. I've seen that slope. I know what's up there."

The wind moved down off the range, carrying the first cold smell of the season, and Odile shouldered her pack and stepped off the porch onto the path that appeared on no map, and said, "Then tell me while we walk."
