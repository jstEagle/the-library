## 9. Water Over Rails

The river found them before they saw it.

They heard it first as a change in the air—a low, grinding hush beneath the birdsong, the sound of a great weight of water moving with patience rather than violence. Then the trees thinned along the old right-of-way, and Odile stopped at the lip of the cutting and understood why no survey crew had ever bothered to re-grade this section.

The Kestrel had come down out of its gorge and taken the railway for its own.

The cutting ran a half mile through solid rock, and where the trackbed should have been there was only river—brown-green, muscular, sliding over the rails themselves in long glassy sheets. The water stood two feet deep over the ballast in the shallower stretches and deeper where the grade dipped toward the old culverts. The rails still showed in places, dark lines under the current like the spines of drowned animals, catching the morning light and throwing it back broken.

"Can we go around?" Tamsin asked. She had her boots slung over her shoulder already, barefoot on the warm stone, reading the water the way she read everything—instinctively, without apology.

Odile crouched at the edge and studied it. "Around means up and over that ridge." She nodded east, where the slope climbed steep and brush-choked into country she hadn't surveyed. "Half a day lost, maybe more if the going's bad. And we'd lose the line. The whole point of following the rail is that it goes where we need to go."

"So we go through."

"So we think about going through." Odile pulled her notebook from her coat, licked her thumb, and flipped to the pages she'd made three summers ago on this very stretch—the last time she'd walked this line before the closure orders came down. Her own handwriting looked strange to her now, younger somehow, more certain of itself. *Cutting 14. Grade falls 1:80 westbound. Culvert at chainage 6,400, stone-arched, clear of debris as of inspection.* She'd noted the culvert because stone arches were rare on these mountain spurs; most of them were corrugated steel, cheap and temporary, meant to be replaced by crews that never came.

She looked up at the water again and did what she always did when the world got difficult: she measured it.

Two feet over the ballast in the flats. The current ran westward, same direction they were traveling, which helped—but not much, because the cutting narrowed twice, and where it narrowed the water would speed up, and moving water at knee depth could take an adult off their feet without effort. She'd seen it happen. She'd nearly seen it happen to herself, once, on the Alder Fork crossing, and the memory of that cold grip around her calves still lived somewhere below her ribs.

Her brother had been better in moving water than she was. That thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts of him did, and she set it aside carefully, the way you set down something sharp.

"There's a rhythm to it," she said aloud, mostly to herself. "Flooded cuttings aren't uniform. The water pools in the dips and runs fast over the crowns. If we can read where it's shallow and slow versus shallow and fast—" She stopped. Tamsin was looking at her with an expression Odile couldn't parse, something between amusement and recognition.

"You talk like my father," Tamsin said.

"I don't know your father."

"No. Nobody does anymore." She said it without drama, the way she said most true things, and waded to the edge of the water and put one foot in. "It's warm near the edge. It won't be warm in the middle."

"It won't be anything in the middle but fast." Odie laced her boots tight, tied her pack's straps down, and wrapped her notebook in oilcloth, then in a second layer of oilcloth, because some things were not negotiable. The ledger went against her spine, inside her shirt, where it would stay dry or drown with her. "Rules. We cross on the upstream side of each rail, never downstream—the downstream side scours out and you'll step into a hole that wasn't there last spring. We keep contact. Hand on pack strap, always. And if the water takes either of us, the other does not chase. You get to the far side and you walk to the capital and you tell them what I told you."

Tamsin considered this. "You'd leave me?"

"I'm telling you not to leave me. There's a difference, and the difference is that I've done this before and you haven't."

"I've crossed the Kestrel before," Tamsin said. "At the ford above the mill."

"The mill."

"The Bellbrook mill. Before they took it apart." She pointed upstream, to where the cutting bent north behind a shoulder of rock. "There was a footpath. The mill workers used it when the rail flooded—it went up through the birches on the south wall, above the high-water line, and came back down past the second culvert. My mother walked it every spring. She said the railway people forgot it existed because it wasn't on any of their maps."

Odile felt something shift in her chest, a small tectonic event. "Show me."

* * *

The path was there.

Not obvious—never obvious, because a path that wanted to survive erasure learns to look like nothing—but there. A seam of lighter stone where feet had worn the lichen off the rock. A birch root polished smooth at hand height. A flat shelf of ledge running laterally along the south wall of the cutting, thirty feet above the flood, wide enough for one person, narrow enough that Odile's engineer's eye kept calculating fall distances and making her stomach tighten.

Tamsin moved along it like she'd been born on it, which, Odile reflected, in a sense she had been. The girl didn't hesitate at the places where the shelf pinched down to eighteen inches. She didn't test the footing. She simply knew, the way Odile knew grades and curves—not from measurement but from inheritance, from a body of knowledge passed hand to hand until it became instinct.

"This is how they knew the water would come," Tamsin said over her shoulder, walking. "The mill people. They watched the snowpack on the high shoulders and counted days. My mother said the railway men laughed at them for it—said you couldn't predict a river by counting. But they were right every year. Righter than the gauges."

"Gauges fail," Odile said quietly. "People who live with a river don't."

They came down off the shelf where Tamsin said it descended, past the second culvert—and there it was, exactly as promised: a stone arch, dry-throated, the flood passing beneath it in a smooth brown rope. Odile put her palm against the archstone and felt the faint vibration of all that water doing its work, patient as geology. Her inspection note from three years ago sat in her memory beside the living thing, and the two matched perfectly. The map in her head and the map in Tamsin's head, laid over each other, agreed.

It was the official maps that disagreed with both of them.

"We'll follow the culverts," she said. "There are four more before the cutting opens out. Each one marks the low point, and each one will have a shelf or a bench above it if the masons were careful." She hesitated, then asked the question she'd been carrying since the birch path. "Tamsin. How many paths do you know like that? Paths that aren't on any map?"

The girl shrugged, which was not an answer, and then gave one anyway, because she was Tamsin. "All of Bellbrook's. The town had a hundred years of people walking places before anyone drew it. And then—" she made a small gesture, a closing of fingers, "—they drew it out instead. But the ground kept the paths. Ground doesn't forget. Only paper forgets."

* * *

The third crossing nearly killed them anyway.

The cutting narrowed at chainage 7,100—she knew the number without checking, the way she knew her own pulse—and the river, compressed, forgot its manners. What had been a sheet became a torrent, white-capped over the crown of the grade, and the birch path on the south wall ended in a slide scar where the whole hillside had let go sometime in the last two winters, dumping a fan of rubble straight into the flood.

They stood at the top of the scar and looked at the water and the water looked back.

"We could climb out," Tamsin said. "Go over the top."

"And lose the afternoon, and come down into country I haven't surveyed, with weather building." Odile tipped her chin at the western sky, where clouds were stacking themselves over the high shoulders with the unhurried confidence of a season arriving on schedule. Dry season's end was a countdown now, and every hour spent climbing was an hour subtracted from the pass. "No. We cross here. But we cross it my way."

She knelt at the edge of the torrent and read it properly this time, giving it the full discipline of her trade. The flood ran fastest over the crown—that was physics, immutable, the water accelerating where the bed rose. But the crown was also the shallowest point, and depth was the killer, not speed alone. Speed plus depth plus footing. You could defeat any two if you respected the third.

"Rope," she said, and uncoiled the length from her pack. "We anchor to that pine. I go first, diagonally downstream to the crown, then angle back up to the rail on the far side of the narrows. The rope gives you a handline so the current can't have you even if your feet go. You come second, and you keep three points of contact at all times, and you do not look at the water. Look at me. Look at the rope. The water is a liar—it tells you it wants to play."

"That's a strange thing for a river to say."

"They all say it." She rigged the anchor with a tensionless hitch around the pine's base, tested it with her whole weight, tested it again with a jerk, and paid out the line. "Tamsin. Before I go in. If I go under and the rope fouls, there's a knife on my left hip. Cut it. Don't be brave about it."

The girl's face did something complicated. "You keep telling me how to lose you."

"I keep telling you how to survive me. It's different." Odile stepped down into the flood.

The cold hit her like a struck bell. Not mountain-cold—this was low-country water, warmed by miles of valley sun—but powerful, insistent, a hand around her thighs that immediately began negotiating for her knees. She leaned on the current the way she'd taught apprentices to lean on a grade, took two steps, three, felt the bed of the cutting firm and familiar under her boots through the churn—ballast stone, good ballast, laid by men who'd known what they were doing—and reached the crown where the water broke white around her waist and tried very seriously to become the only fact in the world.

She did not look at the water. She looked at the far rail, a dark line under the foam, and she walked to it, and the current released her by degrees, grudgingly, like a creditor.

On the far side she breathed, once, deliberately, and turned. "Your turn. Three points of contact. Look at me."

Tamsin came down the scar rubble light as water herself, took the rope, and entered the flood.

And here was the thing Odile would remember afterward, in the nights when she took the journey out and examined it piece by piece: the girl crossed differently than she did. Where Odile had fought the current in disciplined increments, Tamsin *read* it—tilted her body, shifted her weight a half-second ahead of each surge, used the eddies behind the larger stones as rest stops, as though she'd grown up swimming in rivers that talked. Halfway across, a surge hit her broadside that would have folded Odile like a chart, and Tamsin simply wasn't where the surge expected her to be. She flowed around it.

She came up the far bank dripping and grinning, and it was the first time Odile had seen her grin, and it rearranged something in the older woman's chest.

"You fight water," Tamsin said, wringing out her braid. "My mother said townspeople do that. Rail people too. You treat the river like a problem with an answer."

"Doesn't it have an answer?"

"Sure. The answer is: it's stronger than you, and it's going where it's going, and the trick is asking nicely." She held out her hand, and after a moment Odile took it, and the girl pulled her up the bank with surprising strength. "You got us across the narrows, though. Your rope and your numbers. Mother would've liked you. She liked things that worked."

* * *

They dried in the sun on the flat above the fourth culvert, eating cold oats and hard cheese, their clothes steaming gently in the strengthening light. Below them the river ran on through the cutting, reclaiming its territory inch by inch, indifferent to maps and memories alike.

Odile got out her notebook while her hands were steady and marked the crossing: date, chainage, depth estimate, the slide scar, the condition of the culverts. Survey work. The small liturgy of her life. But halfway through the entry she stopped, pen hovering, and looked at Tamsin sitting cross-legged on the warm stone, picking apart a seedhead with great concentration.

"Teach me one," Odile said.

"One what?"

"A path. One of yours. The way your mother taught you." She closed the notebook. "I've spent three years learning this range with instruments. Every grade, every curve, every chainage. And this morning a twelve-year-old showed me a road I never found because nobody paid her to draw it. I'd like to know one thing the way you know it. Not measured. Inherited."

Tamsin was quiet for a while. The river filled the silence.

"All right," she said finally. "But you have to learn it the real way. Not from me telling you. From walking it until your feet know it and your eyes stop needing to check." She pointed back the way they'd come, upstream, to a fold in the south wall where a shadow suggested a seam. "There's a shortcut from the fourth culvert up to the shepherd's bench on the ridge. It saves two hours when the cutting floods, which is every spring. My mother's mother used it. Probably her mother before that." She stood, shook out her skirt, and grinned that rearranging grin again. "Come on, then. Feet first. Numbers after."

They climbed, and Odile let the girl lead, and somewhere on the third switchback—on a stretch of trail that existed nowhere on paper, worn into the world by generations of unnamed feet—she realized she had stopped counting the distance since the last milestone. She had stopped, in fact, counting anything at all. She was simply walking, in company, through country that was teaching itself to her through her soles.

It was the closest thing to ease she'd felt in three years, and it lasted until they topped the ridge and she raised her binoculars toward the pass road, out of habit, the way a sailor checks the horizon.

Dust. Far to the west, on the capital road, a column of it—vehicles, several, moving fast, and moving not toward the pass but away from the capital, toward the mountains. Toward them.

Odile lowered the glasses slowly. Beside her, Tamsin had gone very still, the way animals go still.

"Somebody's coming," the girl said.

"Yes," Odile said, and began, already, to calculate.
