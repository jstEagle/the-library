## 10. Dree Closes the Gap

The rain came in off the western shoulder of the range in long gray sheets, and Dree Voss stood in the doorway of the Lockridge woman's cabin and let it hit her face until it hurt.

She had been in the cabin for an hour. She had gone through it the way she did everything—methodically, without hurry, without pleasure. The bed was made with a soldier's corners, which told her the engineer had been regular once, in some other life, before the mountains got her. The desk held nothing but a cold pipe, a tin of tobacco gone stale, and a stone paperweight that had no business being a paperweight—river-smoothed, pale, the kind of thing a child picks up and keeps. There was a second cup on the shelf above the stove, chipped, gathering dust, and Dree had looked at it for a long time without deciding what it meant. Company, sometimes. Company, rarely. A cup kept out for a dead man, maybe. She knew about that. She had one herself, in a house she no longer lived in, in a town that no longer existed.

The papers were gone, of course. The girl had told her they'd be gone. *She'll hide them close and low, near water, because she thinks like water*—that was what the girl had said, that strange feral child on the spur, and Dree had filed it away the way she filed everything, in the drawer that never stuck.

The culvert was forty paces below the cabin, where the drainage ran under the old service road. Dree found the oilcloth bundle in nine minutes, wedged in the throat of the pipe where the water couldn't reach it, and she crouched in the wet gravel and held it in both hands and did not open it.

That was the moment she understood she was in trouble.

Because a loyal officer of Administrator Hale would have opened it. Would have checked the ledger, confirmed the alignment charts, counted the pages, and burned the rest. A loyal officer would have carried the bundle back down the mountain in her teeth if she had to. Dree Voss had been many things—constable, survey guard, the administrator's long right arm—but she had never once, in eleven years of service, hesitated over a bundle of paper.

She put it back in the culvert.

She told herself it was tactics. Leave the cache, let the woman believe it was undiscovered, let her lead them to whatever else she'd found—Reeve, probably, if Reeve was alive, and the girl was proof that someone up here had been talking. It was good tactics. It was the kind of thing she could write in a report with a straight face, and she had a gift for straight faces; she had worn one through the whole of the consolidation hearings, eleven years ago, when a man with a soft voice had read out a list of names and asked her, gently, whether she recalled a place called Bellbrook.

*I recall a valley,* she had said. *I recall that it was unproductive.*

The soft-voiced man had smiled and stamped something, and that had been that. That had been the whole of it. A valley, unproductive. Four hundred and some souls, unproductive. A schoolhouse bell, unproductive.

She had not thought of the bell in nine years. Now she could not stop thinking of it, and it was raining, and the girl's voice was in her head like a splinter: *she thinks like water.*

* * *

They had a day's lead on her, maybe less. The engineer was good—Dree had read her file twice on the ride up, and the file was thin in the way files were thin when a person had made themselves deliberately small. Odile Lockridge, assistant engineer, third class, transferred to the closed-lines survey after a family incident. The phrase *family incident* did a great deal of work in that sentence. Dree knew what it meant to make a file thin. She had thinned her own.

But good as the woman was, she was traveling with a child, and children left sign whether they meant to or not. Not tracks—the girl was too canny for tracks—but other things. A snare reset with green alder where the engineer would have used wire. A handful of rowan berries stripped from a bush at exactly a child's height. At the second camp, a flat stone by the fire ring with three scratches on it, fresh, and Dree had stood over it for a long moment before the meaning came: not a message for pursuers. A counting. The girl was counting days. Children counted days the way engineers counted miles—because the alternative was to count what you'd lost.

Dree knew what the girl had lost. That was the obscenity of it. Dree knew, better than anyone alive, because she had been the one who'd walked the length of Bellbrook's main street in the last autumn of its existence, and she had seen the orchards going heavy with apples, and she had heard the bell ring the hour, and she had signed the census roll with her own name—*Dree Voss, constable, Bellbrook District*—and then she had let the soft-voiced man fold the district into the Kestrel Water Compact like a letter into an envelope, and she had never once opened it again.

They had offered her the choice, in the end. That was what people didn't understand about Hale's machine. It was not cruel. Cruelty was inefficient. It offered choices, clean ones, and it kept its word about them, and that was worse than cruelty because you couldn't hate it properly afterward. You could only hate yourself, quietly, for the rest of your life, which was a form of efficiency too.

*You can forget,* the soft-voiced man had said. *Or you can be forgotten. Either way, Bellbrook comes off the rolls. The water has to go somewhere, Constable, and it cannot go to a town that isn't there.*

She had chosen to forget, and the forgetting had been real—she would swear to that, she had sworn to that, before the inquiry boards, before her own mirror. It was not that the memories were taken. It was that they had been *filed*, sealed behind a decision she had made herself, and a person could live an entire life on the far side of their own decision if the decision was heavy enough. She had done it for nine years. She had been very good at it.

And then a girl had stood on the spur in the rain, thin as a rail, wild as a hawk, and said the name *Bellbrook* out loud in the open air where anyone could hear it, and the seal had cracked like ice in March.

* * *

She caught their first true sign at the flooded cutting, two days out, and it stopped her cold.

The river had taken the trackbed—she could see that from the ridge, the whole cutting turned to brown violence, water shouldering through the gap where the engineers had once cut the mountain open. No one crossed that. No one sane. Her crew would lose two days going around by the Kestrel road, and she had already sent them around, because Dree Voss did not spend her people on gambles.

But someone had crossed here. She read it in the small things, the way she read everything: a length of rope left coiled on the downstream boulder, wet still, deliberately left—*for whoever comes after*, which meant the engineer knew she was being followed and had chosen to leave a lifeline anyway, and Dree did not know what to do with that, so she put it in the drawer that never stuck, next to the chipped cup and the bell.

She crossed on the rope. The water hit her to the waist and the cold of it went through her like a blade of winter finding a gap in a door, and halfway across, with the river shoving at her hips and the rope humming, she had a memory that was not a memory—a body-memory, her hands knowing the pull-angle of a wet rope over stone, her boots finding a ledge below the surface that her eyes had never seen. Bellbrook had a ford. Bellbrook had a ford at the willow bend, and the constable had pulled two drowned lambs and one drowned boy out of that water in her three years there, and her hands had learned the river's grammar, and nine years of forgetting had not unlearned it.

She stood on the far bank, streaming, shaking, and laughed—one short, ugly sound that the river took and shredded.

*You didn't forget anything,* she said to herself. *You just stopped answering when it called.*

The thought should have frightened her. It did something worse. It made her curious. And curiosity, in Dree Voss's experience, was the one thing in the world that no drawer could hold.

* * *

The radio call came at dusk, from the Kestrel road, her sergeant's voice flattened by static and distance.

*Trail's cold on the road side. Crew's asking for orders. Also, Administrator's office wants a schedule. They want a date, Dree. They want it by tomorrow.*

She looked at the fire she hadn't lit. She looked at the dark coming down off the shoulder of the range, and the first hard stars, and the black seam of the old tunnel where the mountain had folded in on itself years ago—the only route forward now, unless the fugitives had grown wings.

*Tell them three days,* she said. *Tell them I'll have the engineer and the papers in three days.*

*And the girl?*

Dree was quiet long enough that the static filled it up.

*The girl wasn't in the order,* she said finally. *I read the order twice. The order says the engineer and the documents.*

*The Administrator doesn't write things down he doesn't want written down. You know that.*

*I know that,* Dree said. *Three days.*

She signed off and sat in the dark with the radio cold in her lap, and she made herself look at the thing she had been not looking at since the culvert.

Here was the truth, laid out plain, the way she liked all truths: Corvus Hale had sent her up this mountain to retrieve paper and silence a witness. The order had been verbal, in the administrator's office, with the shutters drawn—*Miss Lockridge has taken materials that belong to the province; recover them; she has been under a strain, I fear; see she comes to no harm, but see that she comes*—and every word of it had been a smooth stone over deep water. *Comes to no harm* meant what it always meant. And the girl, the girl who was not in the order, the girl who was a witness with a living memory of a town that officially had never existed—the girl was the one thing on this mountain that could not be allowed to reach the capital, and Dree had known it when she said *the girl wasn't in the order*, and she had said it anyway, out loud, on an open channel, so that it would be in the log. So that there would be a record. A thin file. A place to stand later, if there was a later, when the inquiry boards came around again with their soft-voiced men.

She was building herself an alibi in advance. Eleven years of service, and she had never once needed an alibi, and now she was stitching one together out of radio logs like a woman sewing a parachute in the air.

*You're going to let them run,* she said to the dark. *That's what this is. You've already decided.*

But that wasn't quite it either, and Dree Voss did not lie to herself in the field; the field was the one place she kept honest. The truth was stranger and more dangerous than mercy. The truth was that she wanted to see the papers. She wanted to see the ledger with her own eyes—the ledger that recorded fences and orchards and a schoolhouse bell in a valley marked *uninhabited*. She wanted to know whether the numbers still held. Whether the land, which did not answer to administrators, had gone on being what it was while the maps said otherwise. Because if the land remembered—if the terrain itself was a witness that could not be consolidated—then the forgetting she had chosen nine years ago was not a door closed but a door painted on, and everything she had filed away was still in there, waiting, patient as water, for the seal to crack.

The bell. She could almost hear it. Not the sound—she'd lost the sound, that was gone, that had been the price—but the *weight* of it, the way the hour settled over the valley at noon like a hand on the shoulder, telling every soul in Bellbrook: *you are here, this is here, this counts.*

She banked the thought. She did not feed the fire. She lay down in her coat with the radio for a pillow and the rope-burn on her palms stinging, and she set her mind to the practical work, because the practical work was the only prayer she knew.

Three days. The tunnel tomorrow—they'd have to take the tunnel, there was nothing else, and the tunnel was a black throat with a collapsed belly, and the engineer would have to lead them through it blind, by measurement and memory, and Dree knew what lived in that tunnel's dark for a woman with a thin file and a family incident. Grief. The tunnel was made of grief. She would let the tunnel do half her work.

And on the far side, above the snowline, there was the maintenance hut at Mile Forty-One, and if Anselm Reeve was alive—and the girl was proof that someone had been talking—then he was in that hut, and the engineer was going to him, and Dree would be one day behind her the whole way, close enough to take them, far enough to pretend she was trying.

*Three days,* she told the sergeant, and it was a lie, and it was a promise, and she could not have said which part was which, and that frightened her more than the dark of the tunnel ever could.

Above the ridge, the stars were going out one by one behind the coming weather. The dry season was dying. Somewhere ahead of her, a woman who thought like water and a girl who counted days were climbing toward the pass with a dead town in their keeping, and behind her, down in the warm lit offices of the capital, a soft-voiced man was already reaching for the matches.

Dree Voss closed her eyes and, for the first time in nine years, let herself hear the bell.
