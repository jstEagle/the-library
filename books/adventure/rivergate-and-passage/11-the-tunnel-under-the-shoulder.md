## 11. The Tunnel Under the Shoulder

The mountain had a shoulder, and the old line went through it rather than over it, because the men who built the Rivergate lines believed—rightly, in most places—that rock could be trusted more than weather. The tunnel was called the Shoulder Bore on Odile's charts, though no chart she'd ever seen admitted to its existence anymore. It was two hundred and forty meters of granite and timber, driven through the ridge at a grade of one in ninety, and it had been closed for eleven years, since the spring collapse that sealed its northern mouth behind a fan of shattered stone.

Odile stood at the southern portal and looked into it the way one looks into a well.

They had come up from the flooded cutting that morning, wrung out and silent, Tamsin's boots squelching with every step until they'd stopped at a shelf of dry granite and built a small fire to steam themselves back toward something like warmth. Now it was past noon. The light came down the valley at a long slant, gilding the scree, and the tunnel's mouth was a black rectangle cut into all that brightness, breathing cold air out at them like something asleep.

"There's no way around," Odile said. She didn't need to say it; she'd already walked the eastern flank as far as the talus would allow, and the western side fell away in cliffs banded with ice. "Not before the snows. Not without losing three days we don't have."

Tamsin said nothing. She was looking at the tunnel too, but her face held none of Odile's dread. To her it was only a dark place, and dark places were where you slept when the wind was high.

"The roof's bad in the middle third," Odile went on, mostly to herself, because saying the numbers aloud kept her hands steady. "Timber sets rotted or crushed. There'll be sections where the muck has come down to within a meter of the ballast. We go through single file, I go first, we keep our lamps low and our voices lower. If anything shifts, you don't run—you get under the arch crown, against the wall, where the old timbers still hold."

"You've been through before," Tamsin said.

It wasn't a question, and Odile couldn't lie to her. "Once. Partway." She adjusted the strap of her pack. "Three years ago."

Tamsin waited. She was very good at waiting; it was one of the wild things about her, the patience of someone who had learned early that answers came slow or not at all.

"My brother was on a maintenance crew," Odile said. "Eleven years ago. The same spring the north end collapsed. They were doing drainage work inside when the mountain let go. Four men came out. Wren didn't." She heard herself say his name and was surprised how easily it came now, like a coin worn smooth by handling. "They never recovered him. The collapse buried the bore too deep. The company paid out a settlement, closed the line, and put the whole thing under seal. That seal is why your town got forgotten so cleanly, maybe. Everything up here stopped being worth looking at."

"And you came back anyway."

"I survey closed lines. It's what I do." She almost smiled. "I told myself it was work."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment, then shouldered her own pack—the small one, holding the dried fish and the oilcloth-wrapped ledger—and stepped toward the black mouth of the tunnel as if it were simply the next thing, which, Odile thought, was exactly the right way to treat it.

* * *

Inside, the world ended at the edge of their lamplight.

Odile had walked enough tunnels to know their particular grammar: the way sound flattened, the way air moved in slow tidal breaths, the way the darkness pressed not like absence but like presence, a weight against the skin. But this bore had been dead eleven years, and dead tunnels had their own character. Water seeped somewhere ahead, ticking off the seconds against stone. The rails still ran into the dark, rusted to a dull red-brown, and she found herself walking between them out of habit, her boots finding the ties by feel, each one a measured step, each step a number.

Two hundred and forty meters. At a careful pace, twenty minutes. She gave herself forty.

The first fifty meters were intact. The timber sets stood square and true, and the rock above them was dry, furred here and there with pale mineral bloom. Then the light touched the first failure—a set bowed inward like ribs around a wound—and Odile slowed, raised her lamp, and read the damage the way she read everything: in loads and vectors, in what the mountain was doing and what the timber was asking of it.

"Roof's holding," she murmured. "Stay close to my lamp."

Behind her, Tamsin hummed.

It was barely a sound at first, three notes rising and falling, tuneless almost, the kind of thing a child makes without knowing. Then it resolved, and Odile realized it was not tuneless at all. It was a melody with a shape she almost recognized—a falling phrase, then a rise, then a long held note that seemed to hang in the cold air like smoke.

"What is that?" she asked quietly.

"Bellbrook's bell song." Tamsin's voice came soft out of the dark behind her. "The ringer played it at closing. Every night. You could hear it all down the valley if the wind was right. Mama said the mountain learned it too—that's why the echo comes back wrong sometimes. The mountain sings it back with its own words."

"It's lovely."

"It's mine." A pause. "You can borrow it if you want."

Odile did want. She hadn't known how much until the melody started again, three notes, falling and rising, and she found her steps falling into its rhythm, tie after tie after tie, and the darkness pressing less heavily than it had.

At eighty meters they reached the middle third, and the tunnel became a ruin.

Here the collapse had done its worst—not the great northern break, but secondary failures, places where the shattered strata had never finished settling. Timber sets lay snapped like green sticks. In one place the entire crown had come down in a slab the size of a barn door, and the muck and rubble above it had poured through to within arm's reach of the ballast, leaving a gap you might crawl through if you were willing to bet your life on the friction of loose stone.

Odile stood before it with her lamp raised and felt the old fear open beneath her like a trapdoor.

Because she had been here before. Three years ago, partway, with a lantern and a rope and no plan beyond the going. She had gotten this far—to the first great fall—and she had stood exactly here, and she had called out. Just once. Just his name, into the deep of the bore, into the direction of the sealed north end where the maintenance crew had been working when the mountain came down.

*Wren.*

And the tunnel had given her back her own voice, distorted, doubled, wrong—*ren, ren, ren*—and she had understood that no answer was coming, had always known it, and the knowledge had gone into her like a spike driven home. She had turned around and walked out and not spoken his name aloud again for three years. She had measured miles instead of years because miles ended and grief didn't, because a chain had a fixed length and a loss did not, because if she just kept counting distance she would never have to count time—time since the last morning she'd seen him, time since the letter, time since the knock at her door and the man with his hat in his hands.

Now she stood in the same place, and the girl who remembered Bellbrook was waiting behind her in the dark, humming, and Odile found she could not move.

Her lamp hand shook. The circle of light shivered on the rubble. Somewhere water ticked. Somewhere, impossibly far, the mountain breathed.

"Odile." Tamsin's voice, very quiet. "Your light's dancing."

"I know."

"Is this the place?"

"This is the place."

Tamsin came forward until Odile could see her at the edge of the glow—small, wiry, mud-stained to the knee, her eyes catching the lamplight like an animal's. She looked at the rubble wall, then at Odile, and she did not ask what the place was. Instead she began, softly, to sing.

The bell song rose through the ruined bore, and the tunnel took it and gave it back changed, as Tamsin had promised—as her mother had promised—with its own words underneath, a low harmonic in the stone itself, and Odile stood in the heart of her oldest fear and listened to a dead town's evening bell sung by its last living memory, and something in her chest that had been clenched for eleven years loosened by a single degree.

"Wren liked music," she heard herself say. "He couldn't carry a tune in a bucket. He used to whistle anyway. All day, on the crews. Drove the foreman mad."

Tamsin kept singing.

"He'd have liked you. He'd have taught you card games and cheated at all of them." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "He was under the north end. Two hundred meters that way. Eleven years. And I never—" The breath went out of her. "I never said goodbye. Nobody did. They just sealed it. Like your town. Like a line item. *Closed, unrecovered.*"

The melody wavered, then steadied. Tamsin reached out and took the hem of Odile's coat, not pulling, just holding, an anchor line in the dark.

"The mountain sings it back wrong," Tamsin said. "But it still sings it. That's what Mama meant. Things don't stop being true because they're buried. You just have to be the one who remembers the tune."

Odile wiped her face with the back of her glove—there was no one to see, and it mattered anyway—and raised her lamp to the crawl gap in the rubble.

"Forty minutes," she said. "Keep singing."

* * *

They went through the broken country slowly, and the tunnel tested them the way tunnels do.

Twice the ceiling groaned—a long, grinding exhalation of settling stone that stopped both their hearts—and twice Odile stood with her hand flat against a timber set, reading the vibration, judging, waiting, then moving on. Once Tamsin's foot broke through crusted fill into a void beneath, and Odile caught her wrist and hauled her out and they lay together on solid ballast for a full minute, hearts hammering, before either spoke.

"Deeper than it looked," Tamsin managed.

"Everything is." Odile marked the spot with chalk on the rail web—for whoever came after, if anyone ever did. "Step where I step."

At a hundred and sixty meters the bore bent slightly eastward, and around the bend the air changed—colder, moving, carrying a smell of snow and living pine. The north portal was near, and it was not fully sealed. The collapse had left a throat of daylight high in the rubble cone, a jagged lens of white sky perhaps four meters up, and gray light spilled down the talus inside like water finding its level.

They climbed toward it over fallen stone, and near the top, wedged in a crevice where the flood of eleven years ago had deposited it, Odile's lamp found a shape that stopped her breath.

A lantern. Brass, green with age, its glass smoked black. And beside it, half-buried, a canvas tool roll, and a boot—just one—upturned, sole toward the light.

She knew the boot. She had bought those boots. She had teased him about the price of them for a month.

Odile knelt in the gray light falling through the broken crown, and the tunnel was silent, and Tamsin stood a few paces off with her head bowed, humming very softly, giving her the room the way you give a grieving animal room.

"We can't take him out," Odile said at last. Her voice was steady, which surprised her. "Not through this. Not with what's coming. But I'm not leaving him unmarked." She worked with cold fingers, quick and precise: she chalked the crevice, drove a piton into sound rock above it, hung from it a strip of her own red signal cloth, knotted the way railroad women had knotted memorial markers for a hundred years—three knots for a life, laid down in service. "Wren Lockridge. Killed in the line. Eleven years late, but recorded."

The red cloth turned slowly in the draft from the broken crown, bright as a coal against all that gray.

Tamsin touched it once, lightly, with one finger. "There," she said. "Now the mountain knows somebody remembers."

They went out through the throat of the collapse into afternoon light so sudden and clean it made their eyes stream. Below them the northern valley opened—high meadows going gold, the pass road a thread to the northwest, and beyond the ridgelines, faint with distance, the capital's haze.

Odile stood in the wind and felt lighter than she had in three years. Not healed—healed was a word for other people—but carried. Shared, the way a load shared across good timber will hold where a single span fails.

Then Tamsin, shading her eyes, went rigid.

"Smoke," she said.

Odile followed her gaze. Far to the south, back along the line they had crossed, beyond the flooded cutting, beyond the shoulder of the mountain itself—a thin gray plume rising against the blue, straight in the still air. Too much smoke for a campfire. Too deliberate.

"That's not us," Tamsin whispered. "That's my cabin hollow. Or yours."

Odile thought of Dree, methodical, patient, thorough. Thought of the culvert where the ledger lay hidden, and whether a culvert was hiding enough. Thought of eleven days, and how many of them she had just spent underground.

"No," she said quietly. "That's a signal fire. Somebody wants us to know they're behind us."

The wind shifted, and the smell of woodsmoke reached them across the miles, thin and cold as a warning.
