## 12. Anselm Reeve, Alive

The snowline came up to meet them the way a tide comes up a shore—imperceptibly, then all at once. One hour Odile was walking through scree still warm from the sun, the next the world had gone white at its edges, frost furring the rails of an abandoned switch-stand and silvering the wire grass until it looked like something grown rather than grown over. She had measured this altitude on a dozen surveys. She had never felt it before: the particular hush of air too thin and too cold to carry sound very far, so that her own footsteps seemed to arrive a half-second late, as though the mountain were considering them before admitting them.

Tamsin walked ahead, and Odile watched her the way she watched instruments—with a professional's attention and a growing, unprofessional attachment. The girl read the slope the way Odile read a gradient chart. Where Odile saw white noise, Tamsin saw a path.

"Smoke," Tamsin said, and stopped.

Odile caught up. "There's no smoke."

"Not smoke. Warmth." Tamsin crouched and pressed her palm flat against a slab of shale, the way Odile had seen her test river stones. "The rock's breathing wrong. There's a hollow under here with something alive in it. Heat comes up through cracks." She looked up, and in the flat grey light her eyes were the color of the old bell bronze she'd described to Odile three nights ago, singing in a town that did not exist. "Reeve said in his journal the team was heading for the maintenance hut at the shoulder. The one they built for the snow crews, back when the pass line ran year-round."

"The Kestrel Hut," Odile said. "It's on my charts. Decommissioned forty years ago." She heard herself say it and felt the sentence wobble, the way all her sentences about Bellbrook had wobbled for a week now. *Decommissioned* was a word from the maps. The mountain had its own records.

"How far?"

"Two miles, maybe less. But the last mile—" She turned and looked up the slope, where the traverse narrowed to a shelf of broken stone with a drop on the left hand side that her instruments, three summers ago, had measured at ninety meters to the frozen creek below. "The last mile is the mountain's business, not ours."

"Then we'd better ask it politely," Tamsin said, and started climbing.

* * *

The shelf traverse took them an hour and cost Odile more than she wanted to count. It was not the drop that troubled her—she had worked above drops her whole career, had dangled from trestles on a bosun's chair with a level in her teeth. It was the fact that the shelf was *wrong*. Her memory of the survey said the shelf was six feet wide. Her feet said it was barely three. Twice she stopped and took out her notebook and checked the figures against the terrain, and twice the terrain refused to match the figures, and each time the mismatch ran in the same direction: the land was narrower, steeper, more dangerous than anything she had written down.

"They shaved it," Tamsin said, when Odile muttered this aloud.

"What?"

"The mapmakers. When they took the valley out, they took the edges off everything around it too. Like cutting a photograph." Tamsin said it simply, without drama, the way she said everything, and that simplicity was the most frightening thing about her. She had never known the maps, so she had never had to unlearn them. "You keep expecting the world to be the shape of the lie. It never is."

Odile put the notebook away. She would re-measure everything when this was over, she promised herself. She would re-measure the whole range, every cut and fill and shelf, and she would write the true numbers down in a hand so clear no clerk could ever claim to misread them.

The hut appeared out of the white the way things appear in snowfall: not gradually but suddenly, as if it had been waiting for them to be ready. It was a low stone building with a tin roof, half-buried on its uphill side, a black stovepipe leaning out of the snow like the neck of a submerged animal. The door had once been green. There was a rusted handrail, and beside the door, bolted to the stone, a plaque that Odile could only read because Tamsin brushed the frost from it with her sleeve:

*KESTREL MAINTENANCE HUT — ELEV. 2,840 M — RIVERGATE & PASSAGE RY.*

The old railroad's name. The name from before the consolidation, before the government lines, before Hale's era of tidy paper. Odile touched the letters. They were cast iron. They had been here the whole time, at 2,840 meters, keeping a record no administrator could amend.

"Hello the hut," Odile called. Her voice went out into the snow and came back thin. "Anselm Reeve. If you're in there, I'm Odile Lockridge. I found your camp. I found your ledger. I've come up from Rivergate, and I'm not with the administrator."

Silence. The wind moved the frost off the stovepipe and it fell in a soft sigh.

Then, from inside, a voice like dry paper tearing: "Prove the last part."

* * *

He had barricaded the door with a snow-crew stretcher and an iron cot, and it took them ten minutes to shift it, during which Odile talked steadily through the gap—her name, her survey numbers, the serial on the theodolite she'd recovered from his camp, the date the team's supply cache was abandoned on the spur. She gave him the ledger back through the gap, page by page, and heard his breathing change when the pages touched his hands.

The door opened on a man who had been handsome once and was now a topographic problem: all hollows and ridges, cheekbones like cut banks, a beard gone to ice at the edges. His left leg was bound in splints made from a survey stave and a belt, and the smell of the hut told her the rest—blood gone bad, a wound fighting him and losing. He was propped against the cold stove with a pistol in his lap, and he did not put the pistol down until he had looked at both of them for a long, silent minute.

"A surveyor," he said at last. "And a child."

"A witness," Tamsin said.

Reeve's eyes went to her and stayed there, and something moved across his ruined face that Odile could not at first name. Then she could. It was recognition—not of the girl, but of what she represented. Hope, arriving somewhere he had boarded up.

"Lockridge," he said. "There was a Lockridge on the old through-freight rosters. Rivergate division."

"My father. And my brother, after him." She knelt by the stove, which held a few embers, and began feeding it the splintered remains of a survey stake she found by the door. "Anselm. Four men went up this mountain with you. The camp at the shale was left like a held breath—boots by the bedrolls, the stove cold, the instruments wrapped. No tracks out. I've spent eleven days telling myself there was an explanation that didn't end in a grave."

"There's no grave," Reeve said. "That's the one mercy in it." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him double, and when he straightened there was blood on his lip and his eyes were wet. "They didn't die, Lockridge. That's what I have to make you understand, because it's worse than dying, what happened to them. They were *removed*."

* * *

The stove took the edge off the cold, and Odile boiled snow in a dented kettle she found on the hut's single shelf, and they sat in the iron smell of the old maintenance hut while Anselm Reeve told it.

"We were a mapping team," he said. "Official. Commissioned under the Provincial Reconciliation Act—Hale's own office signed the warrant. Our brief was to survey the closed alignments above Rivergate and certify them for decommissioning. A paperwork errand. That's what we were told. Four of us: me, Voss, the Calloway brothers. Good men. We came up the spur in the last week of summer."

He held his hands out to the stove. They were shaking, and he looked at them as though they belonged to the missing.

"The first wrong thing was the fences. We came down into a side valley—unmapped, listed as bare rock and scree in every sheet the archive holds—and there were fence posts. Old ones, locust wood, but a fence is a fence. Then apple trees gone feral, still bearing. Then a foundation, and another, and a schoolhouse with its bell still hung in the cupola. A whole town, Lockridge, sitting in a valley that does not exist." He looked up. "You've seen it. I can tell by your face. Tell me the name."

"Bellbrook," Tamsin said quietly.

The name went through him like current through a rail. He closed his eyes. "I never heard it spoken aloud until now. We found it carved on the schoolhouse lintel. Bellbrook, pop. 312. And under the floor of the schoolhouse—Voss pried up a flagstone, don't ask me why, surveyors are magpies—we found a sealed tin. A charter. The original incorporation charter, with the provincial seal, signed and dated sixty years back. The town had been chartered, taxed, and recorded. And then it had been taken off the rolls, and not by fire or flood. By *ink*."

"The water rights," Odile said. "The valley feeds the headwaters."

"The valley feeds everything," Reeve said. "And on paper it feeds no one, because no one lives there, because there is no there. Hale's office has been quietly reassigning the flow for years. We didn't know that yet. We only knew we'd found a town the government had erased, and a charter that proved it, and we did what survey teams do." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Odile had seen since her brother's coat came back without her brother. "We wrote a report."

* * *

"They came at dusk," Reeve said. "Six men, no insignia, but they had the administrator's warrant folded in a leather case and they showed it to us like a priest showing a relic. Hale's seal. Hale's signature. The warrant said we had entered a closed administrative zone and that our findings were *prejudicial to the orderly conduct of provincial business*. That's the phrase. Prejudicial to orderly conduct. I've had six weeks to turn it over and I still can't decide if it's the most evil sentence I've ever heard or just the emptiest."

Odile's hands had gone still around the tin cup. "Go on."

"They took the report. They took the charter. They took our instruments' memory cards and our field books and Voss's camera. And then the man in charge—quiet fellow, spectacles, apologized twice—explained our options. We could sign a certification stating the side valley contained no structures, no improvements, and no inhabitants, and go down the mountain with our pay and our pensions intact. Or we could decline, and the team would be dissolved. His word. Dissolved." Reeve's voice had gone very flat, the flatness of a man reading elevations off a dead level. "I signed. God forgive me, I signed, and I told the others to sign, because I have a daughter in Rivergate and Voss had a mother, and the Calloways were twenty-two and twenty-four years old. We signed the lie and started down."

He stopped. The stove ticked. Outside, the light was failing into that blue-grey hour when the snow gives back no color at all.

"But the Calloways," Odile said.

"The Calloways were honest," Reeve said. "Young and honest, which is the same thing. Somewhere on the shale slope, the younger one—Piet—said he couldn't do it. Said he'd seen the bell. Said a bell that size doesn't get hauled up a mountain for no one. And the man with the spectacles said, very kindly, that he understood, and that the team would be dissolved after all." Reeve's hands had stopped shaking. That was worse. "They took the boys down the slope ahead of us, and Voss and I were made to walk on toward the pass road, and there was a truck waiting with its engine running, and I heard—I will hear it until I die, Lockridge—I heard the boys laughing. Nervous laughing. The laugh men do when they've decided to be brave and want it over with. Then the truck drove off, and the men with us walked back up the slope, and that was the last of the Calloway brothers in the world."

"And you and Voss?"

"Voss ran. He was the strongest of us, and he ran for the high country, and they chased him, and I crawled into a rockslide crevice with a broken leg from a fall on the shale and stayed there two days listening to men with lamps calling my name in friendly voices." He took a long breath. "When they gave up, I dragged myself here. Kestrel Hut. Nobody remembers it exists—it's not on the current sheets, it was decommissioned, erased, same as everything else up here. The mountain's full of places that were unmapped, and it turns out that's the safest country there is. The erasure cuts both ways." He laughed again, briefly, horribly. "I've been here five weeks, eating the emergency stores and melting snow, waiting for my leg to mend or me to end. And writing in my head the report I couldn't file. Over and over. Every night. In case anyone ever came."

"Someone came," Tamsin said.

Reeve looked at her again, and this time he asked the question Odile had been dreading and longing for. "Who are you, child? Truly. You say the name like you were christened in it."

"I was born in Bellbrook," Tamsin said. "In the house on the mill lane. I'm the last one who remembers it, near enough. And I've come to put it back."

* * *

Odile stepped outside while Reeve slept, because the hut had grown too small to hold what she was feeling. The snow had begun—fine, dry, the first grains of the season's real snow, ticking against the tin roof like static. Above the shoulder the peaks were going out, one by one, as the cloud deck rose to meet them. She stood on the shelf with her notebook against her chest and let the cold have her face.

*Mortal.* That was the word the day had handed her. For three years she had lived as though she had already died, walking the closed lines, measuring distances because distances could not break her heart the way years did. She had thought the danger ahead of them was weather, altitude, Dree and her crew, the calendar of the dry season. All true. But Reeve had shown her the true shape of it: there were men with a warrant and a truck, and the Calloway brothers had been twenty-two and twenty-four, and the mountain had swallowed them so completely that even their boots had been collected. This was no longer a survey. It was a war over what was real, and the other side had already killed to win it.

And yet. She looked down the darkening slope, toward the pass road, toward the capital forty leagues beyond it, where the provincial archive held—*if it still held*—the original rolls, the tax ledgers, the charter registry. Reeve's testimony was a man's word, and a man who had signed a false certification at that; it would not survive a bureaucrat's shrug. Her own charts showed fences and foundations in a valley listed as bare rock, but charts could be called forgeries. What the inquiry would need was the paper trail itself, the unaltered record, the thing Hale could not dissolve because it was already filed.

Eleven days, she had calculated. Now the snow was starting early.

Behind her, through the stone wall, faint as a heartbeat, she heard Tamsin singing to the injured man—the bell song, the one from the cupola, the tune that was the last living record of a town. Reeve's ragged breathing slowed under it. The girl had a gift for anchoring people in the dark; Odile had felt it herself, in the tunnel, and did not examine it too closely because examining it felt like the beginning of love, and love was a liability with a price on their heads.

She went back inside. Reeve's eyes were open, fever-bright, fixed on her.

"Lockridge. Before you decide anything—there's something you have to know about the charter. When the man with the spectacles took it, he said something I didn't understand until this week." He gripped her wrist with surprising strength. "He said, *the original is already where it can do no harm.* Already, Lockridge. Not *we will destroy it*. Already. Which means the charter was never in Bellbrook's keeping at all—it was filed. It's in the archive. In the capital. And so is every other town's paper that he's erased, because he can't burn what's already filed without an inquiry asking why, not yet, not unless he's forced to." His grip tightened. "He's forced to *now*. Because I'm alive, and he'll know it soon, and when he knows it, he will burn the archive to bury me. You have to get there before he does. Do you understand me? You have to get there *first*."

Outside, the snow thickened, and far down the valley—so far it might have been imagination, but Odile's whole life had trained her not to dismiss what her instruments and her instincts agreed on—she saw a light moving on the pass road. A vehicle's headlamps, climbing.

Coming up.
