## 13. What Hale Erased First

The maintenance hut sat above the snowline like a thing the mountain had tried to swallow and failed. Its timbers were silvered with weather, its roof weighted down with stones against a wind that never stopped up here, and its single window held a pane of glass so old it had gone violet at the edges, turning the world beyond it into something seen through water.

Anselm Reeve lay on a cot inside it, his left leg splinted with survey staves and wire, his face the color of the shale outside. He had been alone in this hut for nineteen days, he said—long enough to run through his rations, long enough to watch the light change on the shoulder of the range and know that no one was coming for him, because no one had been sent to find him. The men who had come to silence him had assumed the fall would finish what their orders began. They had not counted on him crawling three miles through scree with a broken leg to reach a hut he had helped build as a younger man, back when the government still maintained things instead of unmaking them.

Odile sat on an overturned crate near the stove, her hands wrapped around a tin cup of snowmelt she had boiled over Reeve's hoarded coal. Tamsin perched by the window, knees drawn up, watching the cloud deck below them the way sailors watch the sea—for movement, for threat, for weather.

"You said Hale ordered you silenced," Odile said. "Say the rest of it."

Reeve looked at her for a long moment. Up here, away from the ledgers and the instruments, away from everything that could be measured, he seemed smaller than the man whose journal she had decoded in her cabin—the man who had written *the valley is inhabited* in a hand pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

"You found my ledger," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Every page."

"Then you know about Bellbrook." He shifted against the cot, and the pain of it moved through him like weather through a valley. "You know the elevation readings don't match the official surveys. You know there were fences. Orchards. A schoolhouse bell." His mouth twisted. "A town of two hundred and eleven souls, according to the last honest census, which is to say the last census anyone bothered to take before Corvus Hale arrived in Rivergate Province."

"And you went looking because—"

"Because I'm a surveyor." He said it simply, the way another man might say *I breathe*. "My team was contracted to re-survey the closed lines. Routine work. Recertification of alignments that had been mothballed since the flood years. But you can't survey a line without surveying what's beside it, and what was beside the line—" he gestured vaguely eastward, toward the pass, toward the country they had crossed to reach him "—was a town that did not exist. Officially. Fences that weren't there. An orchard that wasn't there. A bell tower that wasn't there, ringing noon over fields that weren't there, worked by people who had never been born."

Tamsin's head came up from the window. She didn't say anything, but her eyes fixed on Reeve with an intensity that made the air in the hut seem to thin.

"I filed a report," Reeve continued. "Standard procedure. Discrepancy noted, recommendation for field verification. And then I received a visit from Administrator Hale himself—which is unusual, Ms. Lockridge, very unusual, administrators do not climb mountains—and he was so reasonable. So courteous. He explained that the discrepancy was a cartographic error, decades old, self-perpetuating. That the 'town' was a phantom created by a mislabeled alignment chart. That if I corrected my report, the matter would resolve itself quietly, and my team would be commended for thoroughness." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the shale. "And then he asked me, very gently, whether I understood how much paper it takes to build a province. And how much paper it takes to unbuild one."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves.

"What did you tell him?" Odile asked.

"I told him I'd correct the report." Reeve stared at the ceiling. "I lied. I kept the original readings. I kept the photographs. And then I started doing what you're going to ask me about right now, so let me save us both the time." He turned his head and looked at her directly, and Odile saw that behind the exhaustion and the pain there was a mind still working, precise and cold, a mind that had spent nineteen days alone above the snowline with nothing to do but think. "Bellbrook isn't the first."

The words dropped into the hut like a stone into a well.

"There were four others," Reeve said. "I've spent two years reconstructing it, off and on, whenever the contract work brought me into the high country. Four settlements, dissolved into administrative silence before Bellbrook. Each one the same pattern. Each one sitting on or near water."

He began counting on his fingers, and his voice took on the cadence of a man reading from a ledger, because in a sense he was—he had carried these names in his head for two years, guarded them like instruments, checked them nightly against memory the way Odile checked her charts.

"Lowmere. A mill town on the Alder Fork. Population ninety-odd. The millpond fed an irrigation channel that ran through three downstream districts. In the spring of the year Hale took office, Lowmere's water rights were reassigned to the Corrow Basin development. By autumn, Lowmere had ceased to appear in any provincial record. Not destroyed—*dissolved*. The tax rolls show the parcels consolidated under a holding company. The parish registers were transferred to a diocesan archive that, when I wrote to inquire, replied that no such registers had ever been deposited. The school records—" he shook his head "—there are no school records. There never were. You understand? It isn't that the documents were lost. It's that the documents were *made never to have existed*."

He raised a second finger.

"Carrow Green. A crossing settlement where the old coach road met the river. Ferry rights, bridge tolls, a market charter older than the province itself. Gone the same way. Third, Dunlow—a hamlet in the western cut, six families, a spring that fed half the grazing land on that side of the range. Fourth, Mereside." His voice caught slightly on the name, and Odile watched him master it. "Mereside was larger. A lake town. Two hundred people, maybe more. My grandmother was born there."

The stove ticked again. Tamsin had turned fully from the window now, her back against the wall, her arms wrapped around her knees.

"They erased your grandmother's town," she said.

"They erased everyone's town," Reeve said. "That's the point of it. That's the elegance of it. Hale doesn't burn anything. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. He doesn't even falsify much—that's the genius. Falsification can be detected, compared, contradicted. No. He simply *removes*. He lets time do the work. A town that appears in no record, referenced by no document, remembered only by people too few and too poor to matter—within a generation, such a town becomes impossible. A rumor. A story grandmothers tell. And stories," he added bitterly, "are not admissible evidence."

Odile set down her cup. Her hands were steady, but she could feel her pulse in her throat, feel the vertigo rising again—the same vertigo she had felt the day Tamsin first described Bellbrook's streets and every landmark matched terrain Odile herself had measured. The maps were altered, not the land. And now the alteration had a history. A *pattern*.

"The water rights," she said slowly. "Each town sat on water. And each dissolution coincided with a reassignment."

"Downstream development," Reeve confirmed. "Corrow Basin. The Vane irrigation scheme. The new industrial leases along the lower river. Every acre of reclaimed water needed a legal origin, and every legal origin needed the prior claim to vanish. You cannot reassign a right that exists. So the right stops existing. And the town holding it—" he spread his hands "—stops existing with it. Hale has spent eleven years converting living towns into blank space on the map, and blank space into contracts, and contracts into money. He has never forged a signature. He has never burned a file. As far as any inquiry will ever see, nothing has happened at all, because *nothing* is precisely what the record shows."

Odile stood and walked to the window. Below them the cloud deck stretched white and featureless to the horizon, and she thought: *that is what his maps look like. That is what five towns have been turned into.* Weather without shape. Country without memory.

She thought of her brother, dead on these same lines, and pushed the thought down—not gone, never gone, but set aside, the way you set a heavy tool down carefully because you'll need both hands for what comes next.

"The archive," she said.

Reeve nodded grimly. "Now we arrive at the difficulty."

"The provincial archive in the capital holds the original records. Pre-consolidation. The tax rolls, the charters, the censuses—everything predating Hale's administration would have been deposited there under the Records Continuity Act. He can dissolve a town from the *current* rolls, but the historical deposit is sealed. Legally sealed. Even an administrator can't touch it without an order of inquiry."

"Correct," Reeve said. "Which is why Bellbrook still has a ghost of a chance, and why the other four towns do not—or rather, why their chance sleeps in the same vault. The original charter of Bellbrook. The Carrow Green market charter, which is nearly four hundred years old. The Mereside incorporation papers. The Dunlow common-rights grant. If those documents exist in the provincial archive, then Hale's entire edifice is built on sand, and a competent inquiry—one subpoena, one sworn testimony, one authentic seal—collapses it all."

"But," Odile said.

"But." Reeve's laugh was hollow. "You've met the man. Do you imagine a man this careful leaves the one vault in the province that can destroy him untouched? I have asked myself, for nineteen days, lying on this cot with my leg in pieces: why did he send men after us instead of simply letting us freeze? Why does a man who has erased five towns so cleanly need to chase three witnesses across a mountain range?" He looked at her, and his eyes were very tired and very clear. "Because we are the first people ever to come asking. Because in eleven years, no one has ever followed the blank spaces. And because I believe—I cannot prove it, but I believe it the way I believe in elevations I have personally measured—that somewhere between his office and that vault, arrangements are already being made. A fire inspection. A humidity failure. A roof leak. Archives are delicate things, Ms. Lockridge. Documents crumble. Records degrade. It would be tragic. It would be investigated, briefly, and concluded unavoidable. And then the last paper proof of five towns would be ash, and the only remaining evidence would be—" he nodded at Tamsin "—memory. And memory, as I said, is not admissible."

The wind rose outside, pressing at the hut, and somewhere far below them, down in the white, the world went on believing whatever its maps told it.

Odile stood very still. She thought about the eleven days. The dry season clock ticking in her head since the culvert, since Dree's crew, since the decision at her cabin that had seemed enormous and now seemed almost quaint. Eleven days to cross the range ahead of the autumn rains. Eleven days to reach the capital archive before winter sealed the pass.

But eleven days meant nothing if the vault was already being emptied. The race wasn't against the weather anymore. Or rather—it hadn't only ever been against the weather. Somewhere in the capital, a man who unmade towns with paperwork might be, at this very moment, signing the requisition that would turn five towns' worth of history into an unfortunate accident.

"We go faster," she said.

Reeve looked at her. "Even at your best pace, you cannot arrive before word of my survival does. Dree will report it. Hale will act within hours of receiving the report. You are three days behind the news and four days from the capital, and the news travels by telegraph."

"Then we're not racing the weather," Odile said. "We're racing a telegram."

Tamsin slid off the windowsill. She had been quiet through all of it, listening the way she listened to everything—with her whole body, the way children listen when the subject is whether the world they remember is real. Now she spoke, and her voice was small in the hut but absolutely certain.

"The charter's not only in the archive."

Both adults turned to her.

"Bellbrook's charter," she said. "The original. Papa kept a copy. He said the province keeps its copy in a vault in a city full of strangers, but a town keeps its own heart at home. He copied it out himself, in his own hand, and he read it to me every Founding Day until I knew it better than my own name." Her chin lifted. "It's in Bellbrook. Or it was. Before they moved us out."

Silence filled the hut, thick as wool.

Odile looked at the girl—at the feral, impossible girl who remembered fences and orchards and a bell song, who was the last living record of a town that officially had never existed—and felt something shift in her chest, some load-bearing assumption giving way. If Tamsin was telling the truth, then the archive was not the only vault. There was a second repository, written in a child's memory, carried out of an erased town on two feet, walking around in the world wearing boots and defiance.

But a memorized charter was not a sealed document. A recitation was not a signature. Against Hale's clean, empty paper, it might be worth nothing at all—unless it stood beside the originals, corroborated them, gave the inquiry a living witness to bind the dead paper to the living truth.

They needed both. The archive *and* the girl. The measured fact *and* the remembered word.

And the archive might already be burning.

Odile turned back to the window. Far to the west, past the shoulder of the range, past the pass they still had to cross, the capital lay invisible beneath the cloud—but she found she could see it anyway, could see the vaults and the shelves and the quiet corridors where a man's careful orders traveled faster than any traveler on foot.

Three days behind the news. Four days from the capital. Eleven days of dry season, minus three already spent.

She picked up her pack and began checking the straps.

"We leave within the hour," she said. "Before the light goes. Reeve—can you travel?"

"I can travel," Reeve said, "badly."

"Badly will do. Tamsin—" she looked at the girl, and something passed between them that neither of them named, the pact of two kinds of memory, measured and lived, bound now to the same errand. "Stay close. We're going to outrun a telegram."

Outside, the first flakes of the season's early snow came sifting down through the violet light, fine as sifted flour, settling without sound on the shale, on the hut, on the blank white map of the world below—

and Odile, shouldering her pack, thought of the capital, and the vault, and the man who erased towns, and wondered, with a coldness that had nothing to do with the altitude, whether they were already too late.
