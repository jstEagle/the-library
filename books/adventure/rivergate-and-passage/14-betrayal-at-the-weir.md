## 14. Betrayal at the Weir

The weir announced itself before they saw it—a low, constant thunder under the morning fog, the sound of water being made to obey. Odile smelled it too: cold stone and churned silt and the green rot of weeds torn loose by the current. Three days above the snowline had scrubbed her senses raw, and now every sensation arrived with an almost painful clarity, as though the mountain were determined that she miss nothing.

They came down through a stand of silver-barked pines onto the old service road, and there it was: the Bellbrook weir, or what remained of it. A broad concrete spine crossed the river gorge, its face streaked black with decades of mineral bleed, its walkway railing rusted to lace. Below it the river fell in a white curtain into a churning pool, and above it the reservoir lay flat and gray as hammered tin, holding the sky upside down.

"Reeve said there was a maintenance hut on the near abutment," Odile said. She kept her voice low out of habit now; sound carried strangely up here, bouncing off the gorge walls, arriving from directions that didn't make sense.

Reeve walked ahead of them, favoring his bound ribs, his breath whistling through the splint Tamsin had fashioned from a ski pole and strips of shirt. He had been quiet all morning—too quiet, Odile thought, though she had told herself it was pain. Pain made men silent. So did other things.

"There." Tamsin pointed. The hut crouched at the end of the abutment like something that had crawled there to die, its roof furred with moss, its single window glazed with grime. But beside the door, bolted to the wall in a bracket gone orange with rust, sat a radio cabinet—the old line-maintenance kind, hand-cranked, built to outlast everything around it.

Odile felt the hope rise in her chest and hated how easily it rose. Three years alone had not cured her of hope; it had only taught her to distrust it. "If the line's still live, we can raise the Rivergate relay. Get word to the capital inquiry board directly. Skip Hale's office entirely."

"That's what I thought," Reeve said.

Something in his tone made her look at him properly for the first time since dawn. He was standing very still, hands hanging at his sides, and he was not looking at the radio. He was looking downriver, along the service road that followed the gorge toward the low country, toward Rivergate, toward help—or toward whatever else might come up that road.

"How long," Odile said quietly, "have you been thinking about that road?"

* * *

It had started in the night, though none of them knew it yet. While Odile slept in shifts against her pack and Tamsin slept the boneless sleep of the young, Reeve had lain awake listening to his own body fail him. The ribs were bad. Worse was the fever climbing his spine like water finding a level, the wound in his thigh gone hot and angry at its edges despite Tamsin's poultice. He knew infection when he felt it. He had watched a rodman die of exactly this, two seasons ago, on a cut so remote they'd had to carry the man eleven miles in a canvas litter.

He did not intend to die on this mountain. That was the whole of it, the simple animal arithmetic underneath everything else. He had spent thirty years making careful measurements, and here at the end he could measure only one thing honestly: the distance between himself and a doctor, and the hours he had left to close it.

The radio cabinet's crank turned with a groan of dry bearings. In the dark of the hut, hunched over the set with his coat draped over his head to kill the lamp-glow, Reeve keyed the channel Dree's crew used—the frequency he had memorized from their own expedition orders, because Anselm Reeve forgot nothing, and it occurred to him now, with a bitter taste like copper, that forgetting was the one skill that might have saved him.

"Dree's element, Dree's element, this is Reeve." His voice came out cracked, barely his own. Static. Then, faint as a moth against glass: *Go ahead.*

"I'm at the Bellbrook weir. I'm hurt. I want extraction."

A pause long enough to hold a whole winter. Then the voice again, calm, female, unhurried—the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it. *You're offering me something, Mr. Reeve. Otherwise you'd have called three days ago.*

Reeve closed his eyes. Behind them he saw the ledger, the elevation readings, the schoolhouse bell noted in his own neat hand. He saw Odile's face when she'd decoded it, the way grief and fury had braided together into something harder than either alone. He saw the girl asleep by the dead stove, small and fierce, carrying a whole town inside her skull like a lantern.

"They're crossing the shoulder tomorrow," he heard himself say. "The high traverse. There's a notch above the cirque—they'll aim for it. Odile Lockridge has the original alignment charts. The girl has the charter. Word for word. All of it."

Another pause. When the voice came back it had warmed by a single degree, which from Dree was the equivalent of an embrace. *Extraction at the weir. Tonight. You did right, Mr. Reeve.*

Had he? The question floated up and he pushed it down, drowned it, the way you drown a thing that keeps surfacing. Outside, the river went on falling over the weir with its patient thunder, indifferent as arithmetic.

* * *

Tamsin had gone looking for firewood.

That was all. That was the innocent beginning of it. The pines near the hut had been picked clean on their arrival, so she'd ranged downstream along the abutment, gathering windfall branches, her arms full, her hood up against the fine drifting mist that came off the falls. She was halfway back when she heard Reeve's voice through the hut's thin wall—not the words at first, just the shape of them, low and urgent and wrong.

She stopped. Set down the wood without a sound, branch by branch, the way she did everything, the way you learn to do things when you've lived four years alone in country that has officially forgotten you exist.

Then the words resolved, and one of them was *Lockridge*, and another was *the girl*, and another was *tomorrow*, and the world went very simple and very cold.

She did not cry out. She did not run. She stood in the mist with the waterfall's spray beading on her lashes and listened to a man sell them, and what she felt was not surprise. That was the terrible part. Some deep, unsentimental chamber of her heart had been waiting for this since the tunnel, since the night Reeve flinched every time Dree's name came up, since she'd watched him count the exits in every shelter they entered. Trust, for Tamsin, had always been a loan made against evidence, and the evidence had finally come due.

But then—underneath the cold, beneath even the anger—grief. Because she had liked him. Because he had taught her the names of stars on the traverse, because he'd given her half his rations without being asked, because he was the first adult from the vanished world who had spoken Bellbrook's name aloud like it was real. And now he was trading that world away a second time, and this time she was the collateral.

She gathered her wood again, quietly, and carried it back to the hut, and put on her face the way she'd learned to put on her coat: quickly, completely, against weather.

* * *

Odile found them both at the hut door—Reeve gray-faced and sweating, claiming the fever had driven him to check the radio for weather traffic, and Tamsin standing beside him with her arms full of wood and her eyes fixed on some middle distance where Odile could not follow.

"Weather traffic," Odile repeated.

"The front's moving faster than I thought." Reeve wiped his mouth. "We should move tonight instead of morning."

Everything in him was wrong. The sweat was fever-sweat, but the rest—the darting eyes, the hands that wouldn't settle, the way he stood between Odile and the radio like a man guarding a confession—was something else entirely. Odile had spent three years reading terrain for a living, reading the lie in a contour line, the falseness in a map drawn by someone who'd never walked the ground. People were just another kind of map. She could read this one.

But she said nothing. Not yet. Not with Tamsin watching her with that flat, waiting look, as if the girl were handing her a knife and watching to see what she'd do with it.

They ate cold rations on the abutment while the light failed. The reservoir turned from tin to lead to black. Somewhere across the water a loon called, once, twice, the sound so desolate it seemed to come from inside Odile's own chest. Reeve excused himself early, pleading the fever, and they heard him moving about inside the hut, and then not moving, and then—very faintly, if you weren't listening for it you would have missed it—the creak of the radio cabinet's bracket as something was lifted from it and put back.

Tamsin looked at Odile across the small fire. Neither of them spoke. The fire popped. Down in the pool below the weir, the river boiled white and endless, grinding the mountain into the sea one grain at a time.

* * *

It was past midnight when Odile made her decision, and she made it the way she made all her decisions now: by measurement.

She measured the way Reeve had looked at the downriver road. She measured the missing hour of his evening, the bracket-creak, the fresh boot-scuff on the hut floor pointing not to the bunk but to the radio. She measured the fact that Dree's crew had found them at the flooded cutting within a day of their passing, after three weeks of losing them entirely—and that the finding had begun only after Reeve joined them.

She did not want it to be true. That was the thing she measured last, and most carefully, and found it weighed nothing against the rest. Wanting had no mass. It never had.

"Tamsin," she whispered, in the dark beyond the fire's last coals. "Walk with me."

The girl came without question. They walked out along the abutment until the hut was a smudge behind them and the weir's thunder filled the world, and there, with the spray cold on their faces, Odile said: "Tell me what you heard."

Tamsin's shoulders dropped a fraction, as if she'd been carrying the words all evening and could finally set them down. "The high traverse. The notch above the cirque. He told her we'd cross tomorrow. He told her about the charts and the charter." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "She said extraction tonight. At the weir."

Odile stared out at the black water. Above the dam the reservoir lay perfectly still, holding whatever stars the clouds allowed, and below it the river tore itself apart, and between those two conditions—that stillness and that violence—the whole night seemed suspended.

"He gave us to her," Tamsin said. "After everything. After the tunnel. After—" Her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in stone. "Bellbrook trusted people too. That's how it worked, you know. Nobody erased us with soldiers. They erased us with paper, and everybody believed the paper, because believing is easier than checking." She turned to Odile, and in the darkness her eyes were wet and furious. "Are you going to believe him?"

"No," Odile said. "I'm going to use him."

* * *

They confronted him at first light, and Odile chose the ground the way she'd choose a survey station: with the abutment rail at his back, the drop to the pool below, nowhere to go but the truth.

Reeve denied it for exactly ninety seconds. She counted. Then his body betrayed him the way bodies do—shoulders sagging, eyes sliding to the rail, to the water, to anywhere—and the denial collapsed into something worse than anger: justification.

"You don't understand what this fever is doing to me," he said, and his voice climbed, cracking on the altitude of it. "I've seen this kill a man, Lockridge. Slowly. In a place like this. I have maybe two days of walking left in me and you're asking me to spend them climbing toward a blizzard so a girl can recite a charter to a board that Hale may already have bought. I gave you everything—the ledger, the testimony, the names of the other towns. Doesn't that buy me a stretcher?"

"It buys you a trial," Odile said. "Where you'd testify. Instead you sold the only two witnesses who can end this."

"They'll extract me and let you go!" he shouted. "Dree said—"

"Dree said what you needed to hear." Odile stepped closer, and her voice dropped to the register she used on the line crews, the one that carried over machinery and meant no argument survived it. "Anselm. Look at me. You found Bellbrook's charter sealed in the rock. You wrote down a schoolhouse bell in a valley that isn't supposed to exist. You are the last honest measurement that man's machine ever produced, and the moment you're in their hands, you become a problem to be solved. They didn't silence your team because you failed. They silenced it because you succeeded."

The words landed. She watched them land, watched his face work through the stages—anger, bargaining, and then the long gray country of understanding, where there was nothing left to trade.

"They promised me a doctor," he said, very quietly.

"And I'm promising you a reason to need one," Odile said. "Live long enough to see Hale fall. Live long enough to see your readings read back into the record. That's the extraction worth having."

Tamsin stood apart, arms folded, saying nothing. When Reeve looked to her—looking, Odile realized, for absolution—the girl met his eyes steadily and said only: "You taught me the stars. I'll remember that. I'll remember this too. Both are true. That's how memory works." It was not forgiveness. It was something more exacting: a sentence passed and a sentence served, simultaneously.

Reeve sat down heavily on the abutment rail's concrete footing and put his face in his hands. The fever burned in his cheeks. Below him the river fell and fell.

"I told them the notch above the cirque," he said at last, hollow. "They'll be moving on it now. First light. If you take the traverse, you're walking into them."

Odile felt the plan turn over in her mind like a switch thrown, one route dying and another lighting up. "No," she said slowly. "If they're watching the notch, then the notch is exactly where someone should be seen going." She looked east, where the range shouldered up into cloud, and west, where the service road ran down toward Rivergate and the enemy. "How much does it cost them to chase a sick man down a road they already control?"

Reeve lifted his head. Understanding dawned in his ruined face, and with it something that was almost relief—the relief of a man offered a way to be useful that doesn't require forgiveness.

"You want me to be the decoy," he said.

"I want you to be the surveyor," Odile said. "Walk the line they expect. Loudly. Let them take you if they take you—but make them spend the morning doing it, and make them believe the traverse is empty." She held out her hand. "And when it's done, get yourself to Rivergate Hall any way you can. Testify. That's your extraction, Anselm. Take it or leave it."

For a long moment the only sound was the weir, pouring the mountain's patience into the pool. Then Reeve took her hand, and his grip, fever-weak as it was, held on a beat longer than a bargain required.

Tamsin had already turned away, shouldering her pack, her face set toward the high country. But as Odile moved to follow, the girl spoke without turning, her voice nearly lost under the water's thunder:

"He wasn't the worst thing that could happen to us." A pause. "Believing him was."

Above the cirque, unseen, the clouds had begun to assemble themselves into the season's first true storm—and somewhere down the dark service road, engines that had been idling all night shifted into gear, and began to climb.
