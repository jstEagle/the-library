## 15. Splitting the Range

The maintenance hut sat in a fold of the mountain like something the rock had swallowed and half-digested—walls of gray timber gone silver with weather, roof sagging under years of snowload it had somehow survived. Odile stood at its doorway and looked down the two ways out, because there were only ever two ways off a shoulder this high: the low road and the high one.

The low road followed the old service grade, switchbacking down through scree and stunted pine toward the river valley. It was wide enough for a mule train, gentle enough for an injured man, and visible from three miles in every direction. The high traverse clung to the mountainside itself—a surveyor's path she had cut into her own charts years ago, following bench after bench of exposed shale above the treeline, narrow as a handshake, dropping into the capital watershed only where the last ridge allowed.

Dree would take the low road. Dree would have to. It was the only route her crew could move fast on, and speed was what they'd been paid for.

Which meant Reeve had to be on it too.

She turned back inside. Anselm Reeve lay propped against the cold stove with his leg splinted from a ski pole and a length of lashing, his face the color of the ash in the firebox. He'd been hiding up here for nine days on melted snow and a tin of crackers, and it showed in him the way rot shows in a sill—quietly, structurally, everywhere at once. Tamsin sat beside him, cross-legged, whittling a stick into nothing. She hadn't spoken since dawn. She hadn't spoken since the radio.

"You understand what I'm asking," Odile said.

Reeve didn't look up. "You're asking me to be bait."

"I'm asking you to walk down a road."

"Down a road," he repeated, "with a broken leg, carrying your spare charts, while a woman who used to sign my expense reports hunts me with four men and a rifle." He laughed then, a dry sound with no floor under it. "Anselm Reeve. Team leader. Twelve years of government service. And my final assignment is to be a decoy."

"Yes."

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, the wind moved through the pass with that thin, glassy note it made before weather—the sound of the mountain clearing its throat. Odile had learned to read that note the way other people read letters from home. It meant the dry season was ending early. It meant the eleven days she'd counted had become eight, maybe seven, maybe fewer, and every hour she spent standing still was an hour the snow took from her.

"They'll catch me," Reeve said finally. Not a protest. A calculation.

"No." She crouched so their eyes were level. "They'll find your tracks going down. They'll find the charts I give you—real ones, partial ones, enough to make them think you're carrying everything. They'll follow you because you're slow and loud and exactly what they're looking for. And by the time they realize you're not us, we'll be above the treeline where their rifles can't reach and their mules can't climb."

"And when they realize?"

Odile held his gaze. "By then you'll have reached the weir station. There's a telephone line still strung to it—dead officially, but the copper's good. You call the capital. Not Hale's office. The inquiry clerk. Name of Sorrel Vane. You tell her everything you told us. The charter in the rock. The order to silence you. The four towns before Bellbrook. You put it in the record before anyone can stop you."

Reeve stared at her. "You've thought about this."

"I've had nothing else to think about since the culvert." She straightened. "You tried to sell us, Anselm. I haven't forgotten that. But you also crawled nine days up a mountain on a broken leg rather than go back to him, which means some part of you is still worth the risk. This is how you pay for both."

Tamsin looked up from her whittling. "He'll do it," she said. "He's already decided. He just wants someone to watch him decide."

Reeve flinched as if she'd touched the splint. "How old are you?"

"Thirteen."

"That's—" He shook his head. "That's exactly what the girl in Bellbrook would have said. We interviewed survivors, you know. After. There weren't supposed to be any, but there were always a few, and they all talked like that. Like they could see the inside of you." He closed his eyes. "Fine. Give me the charts."

* * *

They left at first light, or what passed for first light under a sky gone the color of wet slate. Odile divided the documents with a surveyor's precision: the true alignment charts—the ones drawn in her own hand, corrected against terrain no map acknowledged—went into oilcloth and then into the pack on her own shoulders. For Reeve, she bundled duplicates of the elevation readings, the ledger pages, and a folded sheet that looked, if you didn't know her drafting, exactly like the master chart of the pass. It wasn't. It was the alignment for a spur line abandoned forty years ago, and it led precisely nowhere.

"Memorize which pocket," she told him. "If they search you, let them find the left one. Fight for the left one. Then stop fighting."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's a working plan. Terrible plans are the only kind that survive contact."

Tamsin stood apart, facing east, where the clouds had begun to build over the far ridges—not the scattered white of fair-weather cumulus but a solid, bruised mass with a flat anvil top, stacked and patient. Storm clouds in the high country gathered like debt. These were early. These were wrong.

"That's coming tonight," Tamsin said. "Maybe tomorrow morning. My mother used to say you could smell the iron in them." She turned. "Can we beat it?"

Odile measured the traverse with her eyes, the way she measured everything—bench by bench, hour by hour, the arithmetic of feet gained and hours spent. "If the shale holds. If the wind doesn't pin us against the wall. If we make the saddle before dark tomorrow." She shouldered the pack. "Three ifs. Surveyors work with worse."

They walked Reeve down as far as the fork, because it seemed wrong not to. His gait was a bad joke—hopping on the good leg, swinging the splinted one, leaning on a staff cut from mountain ash—but he kept moving, jaw set, and Odile found herself revising her estimate of him upward with each hundred yards. Cowardice was easy to diagnose from a distance. Up close, fear and courage wore the same coat.

At the fork, where the low road bent away toward the valley and the high path climbed into cloud, Reeve stopped and leaned on his staff and looked at each of them in turn.

"The charter," he said. "When you get it read into the record—when they ask who else knew—" He hesitated. "My team. Marek and Osei and little Fenn. They didn't run. They were taken. I want that in the record too. Names. All three."

"It will be," Odile said.

"Fenn was nineteen." His voice cracked on it, and he didn't try to hide the crack. "He hummed when he worked. Drove everyone mad." He straightened as much as his leg allowed. "Go. Get above me before they see us together."

Tamsin stepped forward suddenly and pressed something into his hand—a smooth gray stone, river-worn, the kind a child keeps. "For the road," she said. "Bellbrook people carried them. So the mountain knows whose side you're on."

Reeve looked at the stone, then at her, and something in his face came undone and reassembled itself into something older and better. He closed his fist around it. "Whose side is the mountain on?"

"Nobody's," Tamsin said. "But it remembers."

They watched him descend until the switchbacks took him—small and limping and stubborn, a man-shaped question mark moving down into the country where Dree's crew was hunting. Then Odile turned uphill, and the wind met her full in the face, and it smelled, faintly and terribly, of iron.

* * *

The first bench went well. The second went less well.

The traverse was everything her charts promised and nothing her charts could convey: a shelf of shattered shale barely wide enough for her boots, angling across a face of naked stone with the valley falling away below in a haze of green distance. Above them the cliff rose two hundred feet, seamed and dripping. Below, the drop was not a number she permitted herself to calculate. Tamsin moved behind her in Odile's own footsteps, silent, sure-footed in the way of children raised by wild country, one hand trailing the rock.

"Don't look down," Odile said, without turning.

"I'm not looking down. I'm looking at where you put your feet. That's different."

"It is different," Odile admitted. "Keep doing that."

The wind arrived in gusts now, hard and horizontal, carrying grit that stung like thrown sand. Each gust she counted between—one breath, two—and the intervals were shortening. Behind them, the storm front had eaten the eastern ridgelines whole. Ahead, the saddle they needed was still two benches up and a mile across, wrapped in cloud that thickened even as she watched.

Halfway along the third bench, the mountain spoke.

It was a deep grinding groan, felt in the boots before the ears, and then a section of the shelf ahead simply let go—a slab the size of a barn door peeling away from the face and cartwheeling down into silence, taking a yard of the path with it. Dust boiled up. Pebbles rattled past their ankles and dropped.

Tamsin's hand closed on the back of Odile's jacket. "Tell me there's another way."

Odile studied the gap. Four feet of missing path, and beyond it the shelf continued, but the shale there was fresh-fractured, hungry. Her mind ran the numbers the way it always did—grade, load, cohesion—and the numbers came back the way numbers sometimes do when you need them most: possible. Barely.

"We cross it one at a time. Fast weight, no talking, no stopping. I go first, test it. If I go through, you go back to the fork and down Reeve's road, and you don't stop until the weir station."

"If you go through, I'm not going anywhere."

"Tamsin—"

"You said the mountain remembers." Her voice was steady, which frightened Odile more than screaming would have. "Well, I'm what it remembers. You don't get to leave me behind. That's the whole point of me."

There was no time to argue, which was fortunate, because Odile had no argument. She crossed the gap in six strides, weight rolling heel-to-toe, arms out, feeling the shelf flex beneath her like a living thing deciding whether to object. It held. She turned and held out her hand.

Tamsin crossed the way she did everything—without ceremony, without hesitation, light as a bird on a wire—and then they were both through, and neither of them mentioned the moment, because some moments are only survivable if nobody names them.

* * *

They made the saddle as the light failed.

Not sunset—there was no sunset, only the storm front closing overhead like a lid, the last gray draining out of the sky until the world was the color of pencil lead. The saddle itself was a shallow notch between two shoulders of black rock, floored with old snow patches gone dirty and hard, and on its lee side stood the thing Odile had been counting on since the fork: a survey shelter, pre-fabricated, government-stamped, one of the emergency caches her own department had stocked along the high lines a decade ago. Whether anyone remembered it existed—whether anyone besides her still had it on any chart—was another question. That was precisely why she'd chosen it.

Inside: a bunk platform, a small iron stove, half a cord of split wood under oilcloth, and a window of thick glass crusted with mineral bloom. Odile got the stove lit with hands that wanted to shake and refused to. Tamsin spread the sleeping rolls. Neither spoke much. The altitude had taken their breath and given back silence.

Through the window, as the last light died, they watched the storm arrive.

It did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a decision—cloud descending the far valleys in slow gray tongues, erasing ridge after ridge, swallowing the low road where a limping man with a stone in his fist was, right now, either fooling hunters or being found by them. Lightning flickered inside the cloud mass, silent at this distance, like lamps being tested in a house down the street.

"He'll make it," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite a statement. It was the voice she used for things she refused to doubt.

"He'll make it," Odile agreed, because the alternative was not sayable aloud.

She unrolled her charts on the bunk platform and weighted the corners with stones from her pocket, and by stove-light began drafting tomorrow's route: the descent into the capital watershed, the abandoned flume grade that would save them six hours, the last ridge. Her pencil moved steadily. Outside, the wind rose to a long continuous roar, and the first flakes came against the glass—not drifting but flying sideways, hard and dry, the vanguard of the season's end.

Then Tamsin, sitting with her knees drawn up, watching the window, said quietly:

"There's a light on the low road."

Odile crossed to the glass. Far below, tiny as a struck match, a point of brightness moved along the valley grade—swinging, bobbing, hurrying. A lantern. One lantern, moving fast, heading down-valley toward the weir station.

One lantern meant one man.

"One lantern means one man," Odile breathed. "Reeve's clear. He's ahead of them."

But Tamsin had gone very still, her face pale in the stove-light, her eyes fixed not on the single light below but on the darkness beyond it—on the flank of the mountain opposite, where, high up near the treeline, two more lights had appeared. Small. Steady. Unhurried.

Moving along the base of the high traverse.

"Not all of them took the bait," Tamsin whispered.
