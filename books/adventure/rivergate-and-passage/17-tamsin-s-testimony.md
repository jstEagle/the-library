## 17. Tamsin's Testimony

The storm came down on them an hour after dark, and it came the way Odile had always been told mountain storms came in her grandmother's stories—sideways, all at once, like a door thrown open onto winter.

They had made it as far as a shelf of rock above the tree line where an old section house clung to the shoulder of the range, its roof half-collapsed, its walls of dry-stacked stone still standing because stone has nowhere better to be. Odile had found it on her surveys years ago and marked it *shelter, marginal* in her neat hand, never dreaming she would one day be grateful for the word *marginal*. Now she wedged the door shut with a length of broken rail while the wind tried its lock again and again, patient as a burglar.

Tamsin sat against the far wall with her knees drawn up, watching Odile work by the light of a candle stub. The girl's face was gray with cold and something else—the same gray that had been settling over her since the weir, since Reeve's voice crackled through the radio static offering them up like coins from a pocket. She hadn't spoken more than ten words since they'd left him on the lower path with his bandaged leg and his bargain.

Odile got the small stove going with the last of their resin-pine kindling. The flame caught, guttered, caught again. Heat began to pool around them like water finding its level.

"We can't stop long," Odile said, though they both knew the storm had already decided that for them. "Snow's early. A week early, maybe more."

"I know." Tamsin's voice was flat. "You've said."

Odile fed another stick to the stove. Outside, the wind found a gap in the eaves and played it like a reed instrument, a thin rising note that set her teeth on edge. She thought about the smoke they'd seen two days ago, rising off the capital road in a column too wide and too black for any cookfire. The archive. Or what had been the archive. Every chart, every roll, every original alignment she had pinned her hopes to—possibly ash before she'd ever laid eyes on it.

She had not said this aloud either. But Tamsin had watched her face when the smoke appeared, and Tamsin missed nothing.

"The papers are gone," the girl said now, simply, the way she said most things. "Aren't they."

"Maybe some survived. Archives are built of stone for a reason. Fires eat paper faster than stone."

"You don't believe that."

Odile looked at her. In the candlelight the girl seemed younger than her fourteen years and older than Odile herself, both at once, the way children who have lost things get stretched between their ages. "No," Odile admitted. "I don't believe it. I think Hale burned what he could reach, and what he couldn't reach he'll have people searching for right now, in cellars, in attics, in the hands of anyone who ever held a Bellbrook deed."

"Then we have nothing to show the inquiry." Tamsin said it without drama, without tears. A statement of inventory. "My word against his maps."

"Your word against—" Odile stopped. Something moved behind her ribs, some old surveyor's instinct, the feeling she got when a contour line didn't close and she knew there was a truth hiding in the gap. "What did you say? At the inquiry. What would you say?"

"That I was born in Bellbrook. That it had forty-one houses and a grange hall and a schoolhouse with a bell cast in Rivergate in the year the line came through. That my mother kept bees and my father mended clocks and the water rights ran to every parcel along the creek, equal shares, written down in a charter that hung behind glass in the grange hall." Tamsin recited it like a freight manifest, uninflected, exact. "That Corvus Hale's grandfather signed it as witness."

The wind screamed through the eave-gap. Odile sat very still.

"Tamsin. When you copied that charter. How old were you?"

"Seven. Maybe eight." The girl pulled her coat tighter. "It was a game. My mother said a person ought to know what they're owed, so she made me copy it out three times, and then when I could do it without looking, she made me say it back to her every Sunday after supper until I left for the summer pastures." A pause. "I never went home from the pastures. You know that part."

Outside, snow ticked against the stones like fingers testing a window. Odile felt the vertigo come back, the one from weeks ago on the ridge when she'd first understood that the maps lied and the land told the truth—but deeper now, stranger, because this was not terrain she was hearing described. This was law. Words set down by men dead fifty years, witnessed and sealed and hung behind glass, and now living in exactly one place in the world: inside the head of a girl sitting three feet from a dying candle.

"A charter is a document," Odile said slowly, working it through the way she'd work through a bad gradient. "If the original's burned and the copies are burned, the charter doesn't stop existing. It stops being *located*, that's all. It exists wherever a true copy exists."

"There aren't any copies."

"There's you."

Tamsin looked up. For the first time since the weir there was something in her face besides the flat gray endurance—something startled, almost frightened, the look of someone shown a door in a wall they'd believed solid.

"They won't take my word," she whispered. "I'm nobody. I'm not even *on* anybody's rolls. He made sure of that. That's the whole point of what he does—he makes sure there's no paper to say you exist, and then your word is worth nothing, because everyone knows a person without papers is a person making things up."

"He'll say that. The board may want to believe him." Odile leaned forward, and the heat of the stove pressed against her shins. "But here's what I know about documents, Tamsin, and I know it better than any man in the capital. A document isn't the paper. Paper is just where a document *parks*. A document is testimony that's been fixed—made stable, made repeatable, made checkable. That's all a survey chart is. It's me, fixing what I saw so other people can test whether I saw it. And a charter—" she tapped her own temple, awkwardly, unused to the gesture, "—a charter fixed in a memory that can be recited whole, checked line by line, is a document. An odd one. An inconvenient one. But if it's true, it will bear checking, and if it bears checking, it stands."

"And if they laugh at us?"

"They laughed at my contour lines once. A chief engineer, twenty years my senior, told me a woman's elevations ran high." Odile allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "I showed him my field book and my level and my arithmetic, and by the end of the afternoon he was using my numbers. Truth is stubborn. It outlasts the laughing."

Tamsin was quiet a long moment. The storm hammered. Somewhere under the floor, wind moved through the old culvert of the foundation with a sound like breath.

"It's long," the girl said at last. "The charter. It takes most of an hour to say properly, with all the parcels and the easements and the names of the signers. Mama used to time me with the kitchen clock." Her chin trembled, just once, and was mastered. "Nobody's asked me to say it since she died."

"Then say it for her," Odile said. "And say it for me, because I need to hear every word of it before we stand in front of that board. If I'm going to stake my name—what's left of it—on your testimony, I need to know it the way I know my own alignments. Word for word. So that when they ask me how I can vouch for a child's memory, I can tell them: because I checked it against itself, twice, through a blizzard, on a mountain, and it never varied by so much as a syllable."

Something kindled in Tamsin's eyes then—not hope exactly, nothing so gentle. More like the moment a cold stove finally draws, when the fire finds the draft and stops being a struggle and becomes a fact.

"All right," she said. She unfolded from the wall, knelt upright on the stone floor as if it were a church, folded her hands. Closed her eyes. When she opened them again she wasn't looking at the room at all; she was looking at something forty miles away and eleven years gone.

"'Charter of the Town of Bellbrook, in the Valley of the Alder Water,'" she began, and her voice changed—it deepened, steadied, took on the cadence of formal language learned young and kept warm, "'being freely assembled by the qualified inhabitants thereof, this ninth day of the harvest month, in the year of the line's arrival…'"

* * *

She spoke for a long time.

Odile listened the way she read instruments—with total attention, with a pencil moving. She had no paper to spare, so she scratched the structure of it into the frost on the inside wall of the section house, a skeleton of headings and numbers: *forty-one parcels, equal shares; the creek easement; the school lot; the grange; the mill race; the witnesses.* Around the skeleton the girl's voice laid flesh.

And it was not the content alone that convinced Odile. It was the texture. A forged or coached story runs smooth, but Tamsin's ran *specific*—it snagged on oddities the way real things do. The charter reserved the north bank alders for firewood "for widows and the infirm, without fee, in perpetuity." It set the bell-ringing times by the train schedule rather than the clock, "so that the town and the road may keep each other honest." One signer's name was given twice, once spelled with a final *e* and once without, followed by the dry annotation, *as he himself wrote it both ways.*

"No forger keeps the misspellings," Odile murmured, when Tamsin paused to wet her throat with melted snow. "A liar cleans things up. Only a true copy preserves the dirt."

"Mama said that too." Tamsin's mouth curved faintly. "She said the dirt is how you know it's real. 'Law that's too tidy is law somebody's been through with a broom.'"

The candle burned down. Odile lit the second stub, then the third, hoarded against exactly such a night, and the shadows swung on the walls while outside the storm built its cathedral of white. Twice the wind gusted hard enough to make the whole structure shudder and the girl faltered—and both times Odile said, steadily, "Again, from the clause," and both times Tamsin drew a breath and returned to the exact sentence, unerring, as though the charter were a rail she could find with her foot in total darkness.

Somewhere past midnight, in the middle of the water-rights schedule—the long middle stretch, forty-one parcels named one by one, Miller's Flat, the Hurn place, the orchard terraces, the constable's quarter-acre—Odile heard a name that stopped her pencil in the frost.

"*…the Dree holding, being four rods frontage on the mill lane, with rights appurtenant…*"

"Dree," Odile repeated.

Tamsin opened her eyes. "The constable's family. Mara Dree was constable when I was little. She had a whistle she'd blow when the river rose, two notes, and everyone knew to move the stock up." The girl frowned slightly, chasing something. "She left the year before the erasing started. Nobody said where. Constables are appointed from the capital sometimes; Mama said maybe she was transferred." A pause, and then, quieter, with the particular weight Tamsin gave to facts she didn't yet understand: "Mama cried when she left, though. Mama never cried about transfers."

Odile said nothing. She thought of a woman with a rifle and a crew of hired men, tracking them across the range with a patience that had never once smelled of money. She thought of the way Dree had shouted across the cutting at the flooded trackbed—not *surrender*, not *halt in the administrator's name*, but *you're off the path, girl, the high shelf gives way in rain*—a warning, wasted on fugitives, offered anyway. She filed it away next to her other unexplained readings, the anomalies she'd learned never to erase, only to mark and return to.

"Keep going," she said gently.

Tamsin kept going.

* * *

Near dawn the storm eased from fury into the long steady fall of a settled snow, and the candle stubs were done, and they sat in the gray light with the stove ticking as it cooled. Tamsin had recited the charter through four full times. Odile had checked it against itself, clause by clause, and found it constant as bedrock. Her frost-scratchings covered the wall like a strange new survey of an invisible country.

Her hands ached. Her eyes burned. She had not felt this way in three years—not exhausted in the manner of miles, but worn in the manner of *mattering*, and she found, to her surprise, that she had missed it.

"Do you know what you are?" Odile said quietly.

"A girl with a lot of words in her head."

"You're the archive." Odile looked at the frost-covered wall, at the skeleton of Bellbrook written there in her own hand. "Hale burned the paper because he believes paper is all a record is. He thinks a town is a file, and a file can be closed. But a town is also a bell song, and a beekeeping mother, and a constable's whistle with two notes, and a charter in a child's memory kept warm for eleven years. He can burn every sheet in the province and he cannot reach a single word of what you said tonight. That's why he'll lose. Not because we're clever. Because he made one mistake no bureaucrat ever thinks to guard against."

"What's that?"

"He left a witness alive who loved the place enough to memorize it." Odile stood, joints cracking, and went to peer through the gap in the shuttered window. The world beyond was white and featureless—but she had spent three years learning to read featureless country, and she could see beneath the snowfall the shape of the pass, still open, still passable, perhaps two days' climb. Beyond it, the capital. Beyond that, a hall full of men with maps, waiting to be told what was real.

Behind her, Tamsin rose too, and stood at her shoulder, small and steady.

"They'll ask me questions," the girl said. "At the inquiry. Hard ones. They'll try to trip me."

"Let them try. You've been cross-examined by worse than a board of administrators." Odile glanced sideways. "You've been cross-examined by a blizzard, and the blizzard got nothing out of you but the truth."

For the first time in days, Tamsin laughed—a short, cracked, real sound, swallowed quickly by the storm, but real.

Then Odile's eye, sweeping the white slope below the shelter out of pure habit, caught movement. A figure. Two. Four—no, five, strung out along the lower traverse, roped together, moving with the unhurried competence of people who knew this mountain and were paid to be on it. At their head walked a shape she would have known anywhere, in any weather: broad-shouldered, deliberate, climbing out of the storm toward them like something the mountain itself had sent to collect a debt.

Dree. And she had not gone down after Reeve. She had climbed.

Odile stepped back from the window, heart hammering, mind already running gradients and escape lines. Below the shelter, cut into the mountainside, lay the last waystation before the pass—an old snowshed, roofed, walled, defensible, and directly in the path of the climbers. Whoever held the snowshed held the pass.

"Tamsin," she said, reaching for the girl's arm. "Get the charter ready to run."
