## 18. Snowline Standoff

The storm found them before the shelter did.

It came down the pass like something poured, a white slurry of wind and snow that erased the world in long horizontal strokes. Odile walked with her head down, one hand on Tamsin's pack strap, the other holding her compass against her chest as if it were a small warm animal she could keep alive through the night. The hand-drawn alignment charts were wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, pressed against her ribs, and every few steps she checked them with her elbow, the way a person checks a wound.

"Shelter's close," she shouted over the wind, though close was a relative word now. She had measured it that morning in rods and chains, back when distance still meant something fixed: two miles below the summit cairn, an old section house built for track crews when this line had been alive. In her charts it was a rectangle with a roof symbol and the notation *stone, sound*. She had drawn it herself three years ago, standing in summer light, and had not thought once about who might need it after.

Tamsin didn't answer. The girl had stopped talking somewhere above the tree line, conserving herself the way Odile conserved matches. But her grip on the strap tightened twice—once at a gust that nearly took them both off the traverse, and once, later, for no reason Odile could see except that the girl wanted to be held to the world.

They found the shelter the way you find anything in a whiteout: by walking into it. One moment there was nothing but the roar, and then Odile's outstretched hand struck timber, and she fumbled along the wall until her fingers found the door frame, and then they were inside, falling against each other in the dark, breathing hard, their breath immediately frosting everything within reach.

The stove was cold but whole. Odile got a fire going from the last of their dry kindling, feeding it splinters first, then splits, until the little iron box ticked and glowed and threw a circle of amber into the room. There was a bunk frame, a table bolted to the floor, a window gone grey with frost. Through it, nothing. The world outside had been withdrawn.

"We're here," Tamsin said at last, her voice cracked and small. "We actually made it."

Odile nodded and did not say what she was thinking: that making it here meant very little if Dree Voss had made it too. The woman had been behind them since the weir—Odile was sure of it now, sure in the way she was sure of grades and curves, by the evidence of things measured. A boot print half-filled with snow at the last water crossing. A tin ration label, recent, snagged on gorse. The sense, twice on the traverse, of being watched by the mountain itself.

She fed the stove another split and sat down across from Tamsin at the table. Between them they spread the oilcloth and unwrapped the charts, checking them for damp, and beneath the charts, wrapped separately, the pages Tamsin had written out over two nights in the tunnel hut and the high camp: the charter of Bellbrook, copied from memory, in a child's careful hand grown into a young woman's steady one.

"Say it again," Odile said quietly. It had become a ritual, these last two days. Not because either of them doubted—but because saying it aloud made it real in a way paper alone did not. "From the beginning."

Tamsin straightened. Her chapped hands flattened on the table. And in the small warm circle of stove-light, with the storm howling like a live thing at the walls, she began:

"*Be it known to all persons, that the town of Bellbrook, in the valley of the Alderwater, is duly incorporated under seal of the provincial survey, this ninth day of harvest month, in the year—*"

The door opened.

There was no knock. There was barely a sound—the latch lifting, the hinge turning—and then the room filled with cold and wind and three shapes in grey mountain canvas, snow crusted on their shoulders like ash. The tallest stepped through first and stood with his back to the weather, and Odile knew him before he spoke: Brannic, Dree's second, the one with the patient hands and the rifle carried low.

Behind him, filling the doorway, came Dree Voss herself.

Odile had seen her only at a distance, at the cabin, at the spur—a broad-shouldered figure giving orders, face shadowed under a hood. Now, up close, in the stove-light, Odile saw her plainly for the first time: a woman perhaps fifty, weathered as a bridge piling, with a face that seemed assembled for disapproval and eyes that were doing something else entirely. They moved over the room—the stove, the charts, the girl—and stopped on Tamsin with an expression Odile could not read, because it looked less like triumph than like grief arriving late.

"Sit down," Brannic said, and gestured with the rifle. "Both of you. Hands where we can see them."

The third man closed the door and barred it. The storm cut off mid-roar, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Odile put her hands flat on the table. Under her palms, the charter pages. She thought: *they don't know what's under my hands.* She thought: *eleven hundred feet below us, the pass road. Above us, the summit, and beyond it the capital, and the archive, and whatever Hale has or hasn't burned.* She thought, absurdly, of her brother, and of how he had always said that in any standoff the side with the better map wins.

"You've been following us since the weir," she said.

"Since the cutting," Dree said. Her voice was low and even, a voice used to being obeyed without volume. "You crossed the flooded trackbed on the old culvert line. Nobody living knows that crossing. Nobody living should." She took a step forward, and her eyes never left Tamsin. "Where did you learn it?"

"I grew up here," Tamsin said.

"There's no here here," Brannic said. "This valley's uninhabited. It's on no roll."

"That's what I keep hearing," Tamsin said, and there was something in her voice then—not fear, not quite defiance, but the particular steadiness of someone standing on ground she knows better than anyone in the room. "You're standing in it. This shelter. My father helped build it. He cut the stone for the foundation. There's a date scratched inside the chimney breast—harvest month, the incorporation year. Go look."

Brannic glanced at Dree. Dree didn't move. But something in her jaw shifted, the way a structure shifts when a load it wasn't designed for comes onto it.

"The girl talks," the third man said. "Administrator wants them brought down. Alive, preferably. Dead's acceptable if the weather turns."

"The weather's turned," Odile said.

"Then dead's acceptable."

"No," Dree said.

It was quiet, but it stopped the room. The third man looked at her; Brannic's rifle dipped a degree. Dree Voss stood in the middle of the shelter with snowmelt running off her canvas coat and pooling around her boots, and she was looking at Tamsin with those late-arriving-grief eyes, and Odile understood suddenly that she was watching a woman stand at the edge of something she had spent years not looking at.

"Dree," Brannic said carefully. "Orders are orders."

"Whose?" Dree asked. "Hale's? Hale's orders come off no lawful roll. You know what we've been doing, Brannic? Do you? Three towns before this one. Four. Men paid to carry ledgers out of the archive and burn them in the dry wash. Clerks reassigned so fast their own families lost the thread. And us—" her voice caught, just slightly, and resumed, flatter—"us keeping the high country clear of anyone who might remember otherwise."

"You took the pay same as the rest of us."

"I took the pay," Dree agreed. "And I took an oath before that. And I have spent eleven years telling myself the oath was to the province, and the province is whatever paper says it is." She turned fully to Tamsin. "Girl. What's your name?"

"Tamsin."

"Tamsin what?"

"Tamsin Alder. Of Bellbrook. Alderwater valley." She said it the way other people recited lineage, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "My mother kept the orchard rows above the millrace. My teacher was Mistress Corren, who rang the schoolhouse bell at eight and noon and four, and made us memorize the town charter our first winter, all of us, every child, because she said paper burns but memory doesn't, if you start them early enough."

Dree made a sound. It might have been a laugh. It was not.

"Mistress Corren," she repeated. "Elspeth Corren. She'd have been—what—sixty when you were born?"

"Fifty-eight. She died when I was nine. We buried her above the bell tower. Everyone came. Even the constable came, and the constable never came to anything."

The stove ticked. Snow slid off the roof in a long hiss. Odile watched Dree Voss's hands—big, capable, scarred across both knuckles—and saw them begin, very slowly, to shake.

"What was the constable's name?" Dree asked. Her voice had gone strange. Stripped. As if she were asking about the weather on a day that had happened to someone else.

"I don't remember her name," Tamsin said. "I was little. She was tall, and she wore her hat brim pinned up on the left, and she taught us river signals in case of flood—three whistle blasts means get to the church rise. She had a scar—" Tamsin frowned, reaching, "—here, through the eyebrow. From a rope, my mother said. A mooring rope, off the ferry barge, the spring it broke loose."

Dree raised one hand, slowly, to her left eyebrow. To the thin white seam that ran through it.

The room went absolutely still.

"She chose to forget," Reeve had told them, in the maintenance hut above the snowline, his leg bound and his voice bitter with fever. "That's what Hale offers people like that. Not money. Oblivion. A clean ledger of the self. She was Bellbrook's constable—I found her name in the sealed charter file before they took me. Voss. She signed the original rolls. And then one day she signed something else instead, and everything before it stopped existing for her. That's the trick of it, Odile. He doesn't erase towns. He erases witnesses. The towns are just where the witnesses live."

Now Odile watched the forgetting fail. It failed the way a dam fails—not all at once, but in a single structural surrender, a groan through the whole mass of it. Dree Voss stood in a shelter her own neighbors' hands had built, in a valley that officially contained nothing, being remembered by a child she had sworn to protect, and eleven years of clean paper came apart around her like wet newsprint.

"Bellbrook," Dree said. Just the word. Testing whether it would hold weight.

"Bellbrook," Tamsin said. "Population two hundred and six, last census. Incorporated ninth of harvest month. Charter sealed in the rock vault under the assay office, with a duplicate lodged at the capital archive." She paused, and then, gently, terribly: "You attended the sealing. You carried the duplicate yourself, on the ferry, the autumn the barge broke loose. That's how you got the scar."

Dree's hand dropped from her eyebrow. She looked at it as though it belonged to someone else.

Then she turned to Brannic, and everything in her had changed. The grief was still there, but underneath it now was bedrock—the thing she had been before she let herself be hollowed, the officer, the constable, the woman who had once stood between a town and its troubles.

"Bar the door properly," she said. "And give me that rifle."

Brannic stared at her. "Dree—"

"Eleven years, Brannic. Eleven years I've been his broom. I swept four towns into nothing and I told myself it was administration." Her voice rose, not loud, but with a force that made the frost on the window seem to lean away. "I'm done sweeping. Lower it, or point it at me, but I'm telling you now that I will stand in front of these two until the snow closes this pass, and if you want to explain to the inquiry why a section-house shelter full of witnesses froze to death on your watch, you go ahead and shoot."

The third man's hand drifted toward his belt. Dree moved faster than Odile would have believed possible for a woman her age in soaked canvas—two steps, a twist, and the man's knife was out of its sheath and clattering on the floorboards, and his arm was bent behind him at an angle that made him yelp.

"Sit," Dree told him. "On the bunk. Both of you sit. Brannic—you knew Elspeth Corren's sister married into the Ferryman line. You told me that story yourself, two winters ago, drunk, on the spur road. You remembered. You just didn't let yourself finish remembering."

Brannic's rifle sank until the muzzle pointed at the floor. His face worked. "They'll send others," he said hoarsely. "When we don't report."

"They'll send others," Dree agreed. "Which is why nobody's sleeping tonight. We hold this shelter. Storm seals the pass behind us and ahead of us both—that cuts Hale's men off as much as it cuts us off. Come first clear light, these two go over the summit. And we—" she looked at Brannic, at the man on the bunk, at her own scarred hands— "we go with them. To the inquiry. To testify."

"On whose authority?" the third man spat from the bunk.

"Mine," Dree said. "Constable of Bellbrook, duly sworn, ninth of harvest month." She drew a breath, and Odile heard in it eleven years leaving a body all at once. "An office doesn't stop existing because a clerk burns the paper. I'm proof of that. She's proof of that." She nodded at Tamsin. "Memory holds the office. Memory holds the town. All we have to do is carry it over that pass alive."

Odile let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the weir. Slowly, deliberately, she lifted her hands from the table and turned back the oilcloth, revealing the charts, and beneath them the charter pages in Tamsin's careful hand.

"Then you'd best hear the rest of it," she said. "Because there's a fire burning in the capital, and a man named Hale who thinks paper is the only place truth lives. Tomorrow we show him otherwise."

Outside, the storm redoubled, hammering the shelter like a fist on a door. Inside, five people who should have been enemies pulled the table to the center of the room, laid out maps and memory side by side, and began—together—to plan the crossing of the pass.

Above them, unseen through the frosted glass, the snow thickened on the summit cairn. Below, far down the white throat of the pass road, a single lantern swung through the dark: riders coming up from the valley, too many, moving too fast, driving straight into the teeth of the closing season.

Whatever Hale had decided to do about the survivors of his own silence, he had decided to do it tonight.
