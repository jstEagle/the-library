## 19. Over the Pass Before the Snows

The snow came down the mountain to meet them.

Odile had watched weather her whole working life—had learned to read cloud over ridgeline the way other people read faces—but she had never seen a storm arrive with such intent. It did not drift in. It descended, deliberate as a verdict, filling the col between the two shoulders of the pass with a white that was not white at all but a churning gray, the color of paper burning from the edges inward. Somewhere above them, the summit they had climbed three days to reach had ceased to exist.

"Wind's out of the northwest," she said, and heard how flat her own voice sounded inside the hood of her coat. "Thirty miles an hour, maybe more. It'll be fifty on the saddle."

Tamsin stood beside her with her chin up and her eyes narrowed against the first stinging grains, and Odile thought—not for the first time—that the girl looked less afraid of the storm than offended by it. As if the sky were another map someone had altered without asking her permission.

"And Dree?" Tamsin asked.

They turned together. Dree Voss sat against the leeward wall of the last shelter—a stone hut built by track crews forty years dead, its roof half caved, its doorway facing away from the wind like a man who had learned which way trouble comes from. She had pulled off her left boot and the wool sock beneath it, and she was binding the ankle with strips cut from her own shirt hem, her jaw set in that way Odile had come to recognize as the only expression Dree permitted herself: pain converted directly into function.

The ankle had gone bad two days ago, on the scree below the weir, when Dree's boot had found the one loose slab on an entire mountainside of loose slabs. She had not cried out. She had sworn once, quietly, with real artistry, and then kept walking. But the joint had swollen to twice its size overnight, and this morning she had accepted Odile's arm without comment, which from Dree Voss was the equivalent of a signed confession of helplessness.

"It won't hold for the descent," Odile said. Not a question. She had watched the woman favor it all morning, had counted the extra seconds each step cost.

Dree finished the knot, tested it, and looked up. Snow caught in her cropped hair and did not melt. "It'll hold."

"The descent is four hours of broken rock under new snow. If you go down on it—"

"I know what happens if I go down on it." Dree pulled the boot back on, laced it with short vicious tugs. "I've put men in the ground for less than what I'm about to ask my body to do. It knows better than to fail me now."

It was the closest thing to humor Odile had ever heard from her. Tamsin made a sound that might have been a laugh, quickly swallowed, and Dree's eyes flicked to her—there and gone, like a fish surfacing—and Odile saw again what she had seen at the standoff in the shelter below: something moving behind the enforcer's face that had no name yet, or rather had an old name, buried so deep even Dree hadn't known it was there until a fifteen-year-old girl stood in a snowstorm reciting the charter of a town that officially had never existed.

*I, Anselm Bell, do grant unto the freeholders of Bellbrook these waters entire...*

Dree had wept. Odile would carry that image to her grave: the woman who had hunted them across half the range, standing with her rifle lowered and snow melting on her cheeks, mouthing words along with Tamsin as though the syllables were rising out of her own chest after twenty years underwater.

*Constable of Bellbrook.* That was what she had been. Before Hale had found her, or made her, or whatever verb applied to the erasing of a person. Before she had chosen forgetting over grief because grief was a country with no exit.

Now here they all were, three people the maps had no room for, about to walk into a blizzard.

"We wait," Odile said, "and we lose the light, and then we lose everything. We go now." She unrolled her satchel against the shelter wall, shielding it with her body, and drew out the thing she had been protecting since Rivergate: her alignment charts. Eleven sheets of drafting linen, hand-ruled, every contour of the range drawn in her own ink during three years of walking lines no one else would walk. The government maps showed none of it correctly. The government maps, she understood now, had never been meant to show anything correctly. They were instruments of forgetting, drawn by men paid to forget.

Her charts remembered.

She spread the top sheet and weighted its corners with stones. The linework swam in the failing light, but she knew it the way she knew her own hands. "Here." Her finger found the saddle. "The summit ridge runs east-northeast for nine hundred meters, then breaks at the notch—the old survey called it the Kettle, because the wind does this." She demonstrated with a circling finger. "On the far side, the trail drops through switchbacks cut into the shoulder. The charts show a drainage culvert at the third switchback. If we can reach the culvert, we're below the worst of the wind, and from there it's graded bed all the way to the archive road."

"And if we miss the notch," Tamsin said.

Odile met her eyes. The girl deserved the truth; she had earned it a dozen times over. "Then the wind takes us off the ridge, and nobody finds any of us until spring, and by spring it won't matter."

Tamsin nodded slowly, the way she nodded at everything—as if filing it somewhere permanent. Then she said, "My father walked this pass in worse than this. He told me. He carried a lamb over his shoulders the whole way because it was born wrong-footed and he wouldn't leave it." She paused. "He said the trick is you don't look at the storm. You look at the next ten feet. Ten feet is always survivable. You just do ten feet, over and over, until there isn't any mountain left."

Odile felt something loosen in her chest—an old knot, tied the day her brother went into the white and did not come back. *Ten feet.* All those years she had measured her life in miles because miles could be surveyed, verified, ruled in ink, while years were terrain that shifted underfoot. But maybe the girl was right. Maybe survival was just the smallest unit of distance, repeated.

"Ten feet," Odile said. "All right. Rope's too stiff to trust in this cold, so we tie off with the survey cord—it's rated for instrument loads, it'll hold a person. I lead. Tamsin, you're middle, and you keep your eyes on my heels and nothing else. Dree—" she hesitated, hating the sentence before she said it— "you take my arm on the descents or you stay roped at full length behind us. Your choice. But if you fall, you call out, and we anchor, and we come back for you. Nobody gets erased today. Not by the mountain."

Something crossed Dree's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Understood," she said, and Odile heard in the single word the ghost of an oath, older than any she'd ever administered herself.

They stepped out of the shelter and into the mouth of the storm.

* * *

The world ended ten feet away, exactly as promised.

Beyond that boundary there was only motion—snow moving sideways, snow moving upward, snow arriving from directions snow had no business arriving from. The wind did not blow; it shoved. It found every gap in clothing and gear with the patience of water finding cracks in stone. Within a hundred meters Odile could no longer feel her face, and she stopped trying to, because feeling was a luxury and navigation was not.

She navigated by counting.

Paces, first—she kept them short and even, a surveyor's pace, sixty-two centimeters, the same unit she had laid down across three years of empty country. Then compass bearings shouted backward over her shoulder to Tamsin, who repeated them aloud so the words would not be lost, so that somewhere in the roar there would remain a human voice holding numbers the way a bell rope holds a note. Then slope, judged by the pull in her calves. Then the charts themselves, consulted in snatches: she would stop, turn her back to the wind, open the oilskin wrap for four seconds—no more, she could not afford frost on the linen—and fix the next leg in her mind before sealing it away again.

East-northeast. Nine hundred meters. The Kettle.

Twice the wind knocked her down. Both times the cord snapped taut and both times she felt, through the line, the small fierce weight of Tamsin leaning back against her fall, braced, boots dug in, holding. The second time, when Odile got her feet under her again, the girl shouted something she couldn't hear, and Odile read her lips through the ice crusting her own lashes: *Ten feet.*

Somewhere behind them, Dree walked. Odile could not see her—could see almost nothing—but the cord stayed live in her peripheral awareness the way a rail stays live under a passing train, and she took a savage comfort in it. The woman who had come to erase them was now tethered to their survival, bound by eighty meters of braided hemp and the memory of a bell.

The Kettle announced itself before they reached it. The wind changed character—stopped shoving and began spinning, a horizontal cyclone screaming through the notch, and Odile understood they had found it only because nothing else on the ridge could make that sound. She pressed into the lee of the break, dragged Tamsin in after her, hauled the cord hand over hand until Dree materialized out of the white, gray-faced, rimed head to foot, a statue that breathed.

They had made the notch. Odile checked her watch against the elapsed time and her pulse against her watch, and allowed herself four seconds of triumph, which was all the storm would sell her.

Then the descent.

* * *

The switchbacks were worse than she had feared, because the charts could tell her where they were but not what the new snow had done to them. Each cut platform was a shelf of rock perhaps four meters wide, and the snow lay on them in drifts that hid the outer edge completely. One step too far and the mountain simply stopped being there.

Odile went first on every traverse, probing with her staff, finding the edge by the hollow change in sound when the iron tip struck air instead of stone. At each turn she planted the staff upright as a marker for the others and waited, counting heartbeats, until Tamsin's hand closed on her sleeve and then Dree's heavier tread followed, uneven, favoring the ankle, swearing under her breath in a low continuous stream that Odile suspected was prayer in disguise.

At the second switchback, Dree fell.

Not far—a meter of slide on the drift edge, boots skidding, body tipping outward toward the drop—and the cord went taut and Odile threw herself flat and wrapped it twice around her forearm, and the burn of it went up her arm to the elbow, and Tamsin was suddenly beside her with her hands over Odile's hands, adding her whole slight weight, and for three seconds the three of them hung in a geometry of trust above four hundred meters of nothing.

Then Dree's good hand found rock, and she pulled, and she came up over the edge with snow packed in her collar and blood on her lip where the wind had thrown her against the slope, and she lay there a moment, breathing, before she said:

"My fault. I looked past ten feet."

Odile laughed. It tore out of her before she could stop it—a raw, cracked sound, half sob, entirely involuntary—and Tamsin laughed too, high and startled, and even Dree's mouth twitched at one corner, and the three of them knelt in the blizzard laughing at the edge of a cliff because the alternative was to be afraid, and fear had become, sometime in the last hour, a country none of them could afford to live in anymore.

"Third switchback has the culvert," Odile said when she had her breath back. "Two hundred paces. Then we're under the wind."

Two hundred paces took an hour. The culvert, when they reached it, was exactly where her chart said it would be—a great stone throat bored through the shoulder of the mountain to carry meltwater under the old grade—and it was dry, and it was dark, and it was the most beautiful thing Odile Lockridge had ever seen. They crawled in until the wind became a distant rumor, and there they stopped, all three of them, and ate snow, and shared the last of the biscuit and the heel of hard cheese, and did not speak for a long time because speech had become expensive and they were saving.

It was Tamsin, eventually, in the black, who started to hum.

Odile knew the tune by now. Three nights of storm camps had taught it to her—the bell song of Bellbrook, the little descending phrase the town's schoolhouse bell had rung each evening, that Tamsin's mother had taught her, that Tamsin had taught to the dark of tunnels and the silence of shelters, keeping it alive the way you keep a coal alive in cupped hands. In the culvert the stone gave the melody back, round and resonant, and Dree's breathing changed, slowed, steadied, and Odile closed her eyes and let the sound move through her and thought: *this is what the maps cannot hold.* Every line she had ever ruled was a record of what the land looked like. This was a record of what it *meant*. And meaning, it turned out, could survive being unmapped. Meaning could walk out over a pass in a blizzard carrying itself.

When the girl's voice finally spoke, it was practical, almost funny in its smallness against the dark. "How much farther?"

"To the archive road? Four hours, if the grade holds true. To the capital, two days after that." Odile paused. "To making them listen?" She thought of the inquiry board waiting in Rivergate Hall, of the falsified sheets in the provincial vaults, of the smoke she had seen rising from the archive road two days ago and refused to believe was the end of anything. "That part I don't have a chart for."

"You don't need one," Tamsin said. "You've got me. And I've got the charter. Whole thing, word for word." A pause, and then, with the gravity of a girl quoting scripture: "*These waters entire, unto the freeholders and their heirs, in perpetuity, witnessed under seal.*" The old formal words rang strangely in the stone throat, and Odile felt them settle into her bones like ballast. "Hale burned the paper," Tamsin went on. "But paper was never where it lived."

* * *

They came out of the culvert's lower mouth into a world remade.

The storm had spent itself against the summit; here on the eastern flank it fell gentler, fat unhurried flakes dropping through air gone suddenly, eerily still. Below them the grade emerged from the white in long clean curves, the old railbed running downgrade through stands of pine bowed under fresh load, and beyond the trees—far below, blue-gray in the last light—the valley opened toward the capital road, and Odile saw the lights.

Not the town lights. *Work* lights. Lanterns strung along a stretch of the road perhaps two kilometers distant, and figures moving among them, and the dark rectangular shapes of wagons being loaded.

Dree saw them at the same moment. She went very still, and Odile watched the old reflexes war with the new allegiance across her face.

"That's a records convoy," Dree said at last, quietly. "Salvage detail. They're loading what's left of the archive onto wagons—Hale will want the remains moved to the administrative annex tonight, before the inquiry convenes. Out of public reach. Where things can be... consolidated." Her jaw worked. "I've ordered that operation twice before. Two other provinces. I stood in the rain and watched the wagons roll and told myself it was housekeeping."

"And now?" Odile asked.

Dree turned and looked at her, and then at Tamsin—small, snow-crusted, indomitable Tamsin, whose whole inheritance fit inside her skull—and something in the ex-constable's face finished its long journey home.

"Now," Dree said, "we're going to go down there and stand in front of those wagons. And when they ask me by whose authority, I'm going to give them my name. My *real* name. Voss, of Bellbrook, constable of the freeholders, sworn under seal." She flexed her ruined ankle, testing it, and grimaced, and grinned—a terrible, wonderful grin. "In perpetuity."

Below them, the lanterns swung. Above them, the last light failed. And Odile shouldered her satchel of hand-drawn truths and started down the grade, ten feet at a time, toward whatever remained to be saved.
