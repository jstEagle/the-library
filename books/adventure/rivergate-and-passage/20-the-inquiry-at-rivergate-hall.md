## 20. The Inquiry at Rivergate Hall

The capital smelled of coal smoke and wet stone, and Odile hated it within the first hour.

She had been in cities before—had grown up in one, in fact, in the rail yards south of Rivergate where her brother learned to whistle through his teeth and she learned to read elevation tables off freight manifests. But three years of mountain air had thinned her tolerance for the close press of buildings, for streets that funneled instead of opened, for the way sound came back at you off brick as if the city were correcting your pronunciation. Rivergate Hall rose at the center of it all like a paperweight set down on a map to keep the wind from moving anything: granite columns, bronze doors, a pediment carved with figures whose faces had weathered into anonymity. Justice, presumably. Or Commerce. At this scale they looked the same.

Tamsin walked beside her with her chin up and her hands jammed in the pockets of a coat Odile had bought her two days ago from a pawnshop near the station—the sleeves still too long, the cuffs rolled four times. The girl had not stopped looking since they came down off the pass. She looked at everything: the gas lamps, the omnibuses, a man selling roasted chestnuts, the pigeons that strutted between the cobbles with the confidence of creatures who had never once been erased. She looked the way someone drinks after a long thirst, greedily, without pretending otherwise.

"You're staring," Odile said.

"I'm memorizing," Tamsin said. "In case."

Odile didn't answer that. There was no answer that wasn't a lie or a promise, and she was saving what remained of both for the room ahead.

Dree came last, on a stick Odile had cut for her above the snowline, her left leg bound and splinted below the knee where the rockfall had caught her during the standoff. She had refused a stretcher at the pass road station and refused a doctor until Odile threatened to describe her injuries in detail to the inquiry board herself, which Dree seemed to consider a fate worse than amputation. She moved now with the stiff economy of a woman who had spent her whole life being watched and had decided, somewhere on the mountain, to stop caring about it. Her crew's statements were already in the board's hands—signed, witnessed, damning. Hale's men had scattered like spilled ball bearings the moment the blizzard broke and the telegraph lines came back up. Two had been taken at the pass road. The rest would be found; men like that always were, eventually, because they only knew how to follow orders and orders had stopped coming.

The doors of the hearing chamber were oak, banded in iron, tall enough to make a person feel surveyed. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs checked their names against a list, and Odile watched his pen hover over Tamsin's line.

"Residence?" he asked.

"Bellbrook," Tamsin said. "Upper Valley district. Population forty-one when I left it. Thirty-eight when I got there." She said it evenly, without performance, and the clerk's pen stopped entirely.

"There is no Bellbrook in the provincial rolls," he said, but gently, the way one corrects a child.

"Not anymore," Tamsin said. "That's rather the point of all this."

* * *

The chamber itself was smaller than its doors promised—a semicircle of tiered benches facing a raised dais where five board members sat behind a curved table of dark wood. Behind them, mounted on the wall like trophies, hung maps. Provincial maps, district maps, the great master sheet of the Rivergate watershed with its blue-veined rivers and its careful hatching of elevations. Odile recognized the cartography immediately. She had been reading maps since she was nine years old, and she knew a lie drawn by a competent hand when she saw one. The Upper Valley was blank. Not empty—blank. There was a difference. Empty country showed its emptiness: contour lines wandering, spot elevations scattered like dropped coins, the honest confession of terrain nobody had bothered with. This was smooth. Deliberately, expensively smooth, as if the land itself had been ironed flat.

Corvus Hale sat at a separate table with two advocates in gray silk and a stack of documents squared so precisely it looked machined. He wore charcoal wool and a watch chain of dull gold, and when Odile entered he did not look at her—not a flicker—which was itself a kind of statement. She had expected contempt, or at least curiosity. What she got was the look a man gives a filing error. She was a discrepancy in his ledger, nothing more, and discrepancies could be reconciled.

"State your name and profession for the record," said the chairman, a dry-voiced woman named Aldous with spectacles on a chain and the posture of someone who had spent thirty years not being impressed.

"Odile Lockridge. Railroad engineer. Survey grade one, licensed under the old Mountain Lines Act."

"You surveyed the closed lines around Rivergate for—" Aldous consulted a paper "—three years. Without commission?"

"Without pay," Odile said. "I commissioned myself."

A ripple went through the gallery. Somewhere a journalist's pencil scratched. Odile kept her eyes on the chairman.

"And why does an uncommissioned surveyor come to sit before this board?"

"Because I measured something that isn't supposed to exist," Odile said, "and I'd like to know who made it stop existing, and how many times they've done it before."

* * *

They let her present first. Hale's advocates had argued—she heard them in the anteroom, low and fluid, like water finding cracks—that an amateur's instruments were inadmissible, that chain and level readings taken alone in the field carried no evidentiary weight. But Aldous had overruled them with a single sentence: *"This board will hear measurement before it hears argument."* Odile suspected the chairman had already read the recovered ledgers. Reeve's journal had reached the capital ahead of them, carried by the pass road riders, and journals written by missing government surveyors have a way of softening procedural objections.

So she stood at the front of the chamber with her alignment charts unrolled across two trestle tables, weighted at the corners with brass, and she did the thing she did best. She taught.

"This is the Upper Valley as I surveyed it in the spring, two years past." Her finger traced the contour lines, dense and confident, the work of a full season. "Elevation at the valley floor, two thousand one hundred feet. Mean annual flow of the river at the narrows, measured across four gaugings: sufficient to serve irrigation for"—she turned a page—"a settlement of approximately fifty persons, with surplus. Here are the terraces. Cut and filled, engineered, maintained. You do not get terraces like these from geology. You get them from people with shovels and grandchildren."

She moved to the second chart. "Here is the same ground as it appears in the current provincial master sheet. Note the absence of contours. Note the classification: 'unimproved alpine basin, no permanent habitation.' Note the date of revision." She looked up. "Fourteen months ago. Signed under delegation authority by the regional administrator's office."

Hale's senior advocate rose. "The board will note that survey methodology from a single unverified source cannot—"

"The board will note," Aldous said, without looking up, "that the witness has not finished. Sit down, Mr. Quill."

Odile almost smiled. She didn't. Instead she brought out the third chart, the one she had redrawn by lamplight in a capital boarding house while Tamsin slept and the rain worked at the windows, and this was the one she had saved.

"Now. Overlay them."

She had traced the falsified master sheet onto translucent paper, and she laid it over her own survey, and the chamber went quiet in a particular way—the silence of people watching something irreversible happen. Because the overlay revealed what alteration always reveals to anyone patient enough to look: the forgery hadn't been drawn from scratch. It had been drawn *from her kind of work*. The river's course on the false map matched the true course exactly for eleven miles—and then diverged, bending east around a blank space, as if the draftsman had carefully routed the water away from the town he'd been ordered to remove. The contour lines resumed above the valley floor precisely where the orchard terraces ended. Someone had erased a settlement and then rebuilt the landscape around the hole, stitch by stitch, and the stitches were visible.

"You don't erase a town by leaving the space empty," Odile said. "Empty space invites survey. You erase it by making the space *boring*. By making sure no engineer ever has a reason to walk in with a level and a chain." She straightened. "Whoever drew this knew surveying. Knew it well enough to fake it. And they used my profession's own grammar to write a lie, which I take personally."

She heard, from the back of the chamber, a short exhale that might have been Dree laughing.

* * *

Hale's defense unfolded through the afternoon like a document being read aloud in a language designed to induce sleep. The delegation authority was proper. The revision process was standard. The vanished survey team was a tragedy of the high country—weather, misadventure—and the testimony of a confessed enforcer was the testimony of a criminal seeking leniency. He spoke rarely himself, and when he did it was in the tone of a man patiently explaining arithmetic to children. Odile watched him and understood, finally, what she was looking at. Hale was not a villain of the operatic sort. He was a man who believed, sincerely, that administration was a form of geography—that reality was whatever the records said it was, and that a sufficiently tidy file was indistinguishable from truth. He had not erased Bellbrook out of cruelty. He had erased it out of *tidiness*, and tidiness, she thought, might be the more frightening motive, because it never doubted itself.

Late in the afternoon, Aldous called the girl.

Tamsin came forward with her rolled cuffs and her too-long coat, and the gallery leaned in the way galleries do toward spectacle, expecting either a trembling orphan or a rehearsed performance. She gave them neither. She stood with her boots planted the way Odile had taught her to stand on scree—weight centered, ready to move—and she waited for the chairman's nod.

"Your name?"

"Tamsin Vael. Born in Bellbrook, Upper Valley, in the spring fourteen years ago. House on the north terrace, third from the lane. My father kept bees. My mother mended the schoolhouse roof twice because the storms came off the shoulder wrong."

"You understand," Aldous said, "that no record in this province corroborates any of that."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "Because you burned the corroboration. That's why I'm here."

Someone in the gallery made a sound. Aldous let it die.

"Then tell the board what you remember."

And Tamsin recited.

She began with the charter—Bellbrook's founding charter, copied out by a nine-year-old's hand onto slate and then into memory when the slate was taken, word by word, clause by clause, the names of the original signatories, the water rights apportioned by season and need, the boundary stones described by their relation to the river bends. She recited it the way Odile read contour lines: steadily, without drama, trusting the content to carry its own weight. Then she went further. She gave the board the school register as she remembered it—thirty-one names, and she apologized twice for being unsure of the spelling of two of them, and Aldous said, quietly, "Uncertainty noted is worth more than certainty invented, child; continue." She described the bell: cast in the capital, shipped up the river, inscribed with the year and the founder's mark, rung at six and at dusk. She described the weir, the mill race, the footbridge with its seventh plank always loose.

And then she did the thing that broke the room open.

She recited the numbers. The water shares. Not from the charter—from the *ledger*, the annual allocation ledger that hung in the Bellbrook meetinghouse, which she had helped her father copy out one winter when her hands were small enough to hold the book flat. Forty-one souls, apportioned by household, adjusted each dry season. She recited three years' worth. And when she finished, Odile stepped forward with the fourth chart—the hydrology survey, her own measurements of flow and diversion at the narrows—and laid it beside the testimony, and the numbers fit. Not approximately. Fit. The intakes Tamsin described sat exactly where the surviving stonework sat. The shares summed to a volume that matched the river's yield to within the margin of her instruments.

Two independent records, Odile told the board—one measured in stone and water, one carried in a child's memory across three years of erasure—and they agreed. "You can forge a map," she said. "You can burn a registry. But you cannot forge agreement between things that never met. That agreement is the truth, and it's sitting in this room."

* * *

Dree testified at dusk, when the light through the high windows had gone the color of old tin.

She declined the chair they offered and stood, weight on her good leg, and gave her name—her real name, Maren Dree Voss, late constable of Bellbrook—in a voice like gravel settling. She told it plainly, without asking for pity: how she had been offered a transfer and a clean file fifteen years ago, how the paperwork had dissolved her post along with her town, how she had signed because the alternative was being erased *with* it, and how she had spent the years since enforcing the silence she had chosen. "I told myself I'd forgotten," she said. "That was the arrangement. Forgetting was the payment." Her eyes found Tamsin in the front row. "Then I heard a child recite an oath I swore on that bridge with the loose seventh plank, and I discovered the arrangement had terms I hadn't read." She turned to the board. "I took Corvus Hale's orders for four years. I can name every file he consolidated, every clerk who processed them, every fire that was scheduled. Ask me."

The board asked her. For two hours, they asked her, and she answered, and Odile watched Hale's advocates stop objecting one by one, because each objection required a factual claim and every factual claim they had left was burning.

It was nearly midnight when Hale himself finally took the stand—not compelled, but choosing it, because a man who believes administration is reality believes he can argue his way out of anything. He spoke for twenty minutes about delegation, precedent, the burden of maintaining accurate rolls in difficult terrain, the regrettable enthusiasm of amateurs. He was good. He was very good. Odile listened to him build his paper world again, plank by plank, and she understood that half the gallery was nodding along, because competence is seductive and fear of chaos is older than law.

Then Aldous removed her spectacles, folded them, and set them on the table.

"Administrator. The charter this witness recited bears the seal of the provincial recorder, dated sixty-two years past. Your office certified that seal's registry destroyed in a fire eight years ago. We have tonight recovered the recorder's duplicate index from the archive annex—unburned, in a box marked for disposal, signed out under your delegation authority eleven days ago." She let the silence stretch. "Eleven days ago, Administrator, was before this inquiry convened. What, precisely, were you disposing of?"

Hale opened his mouth. Closed it. And Odile watched the moment arrive that she had seen coming since the shale slopes—the moment when a man built entirely of files discovers that the file containing him has been pulled. He looked at his advocates. They looked at their papers. He looked at the board, at the gallery, at the great master map on the wall with its smooth, expensive blankness, and for the first time Corvus Hale appeared to understand that the land had been keeping its own records all along—in stone, in water, in the memory of a girl with rolled-up cuffs—and that none of them could be revised.

"I request a recess," he said.

"Denied," said Aldous. "Clerk, enter the charges."

* * *

They came out of Rivergate Hall near two in the morning into a city washed by rain, the gas lamps doubled and blurred in the wet cobbles. Tamsin walked between Odile and Dree's stick, silent for the first time all day, and Odile realized the girl was crying without any noise at all, the way you learn to cry when crying has to be free.

"It's done," Odile said. "It's actually done."

"He'll appeal," Dree said. "Men like him appeal. It's the last file they have."

"Let him," Odile said. "We've got better records than he does now."

Tamsin stopped walking. She looked up at Odile, rain in her eyelashes, and asked the question Odile had been waiting three weeks to hear:

"When can we go home?"

Odile looked down the long wet street toward the rail yards, where the night engines breathed steam into the dark, and felt something in her chest turn over like a switch thrown on a line long dead. Home. The word had a direction again.

"Soon," she said. "There's a train at six."
