## 21. Bellbrook Back on the Map

The new survey stakes went in on a Tuesday, though no one had declared it a Tuesday in Bellbrook for eleven years, because Bellbrook had not officially existed to have days of the week.

Odile stood at the valley's mouth and watched the crew work. There were six of them, sent down from the capital with transit instruments and a case of fresh-painted stakes, and they moved with the careful, almost apologetic manner of people walking into a room where a funeral had been held and the furniture had since been sold. They measured everything twice. They consulted her charts—the original alignment sheets, now bearing the inquiry board's seal in red wax like a wound that had finally been allowed to scab—and they drove their stakes into ground that had never forgotten what it was, only been forbidden to say it.

The first stake read BELLBROOK MUNICIPAL BOUNDARY, and the paint was so bright and the letters so certain that Odile had to look away for a moment, down the valley, at the orchards gone wild, the fences collapsed into silver-gray lacework, the schoolhouse with its bell tower leaning like a man cocking his head to listen for something he half-remembered.

"They'll want your signature on the restoration order," said the crew chief, a round man named Pell whose hands shook slightly, whether from the altitude or the strangeness of his task Odile couldn't tell. "Administrator's office countersigned. Well." He coughed. "The interim administrator."

"Interim," Odile said. The word tasted like clean snowmelt. Hale's office had been sealed the same week the inquiry convened; his maps were spread across the hearing tables at Rivergate Hall like the skins of animals he had skinned himself, and men who had signed things for him for years were suddenly unable to locate their own signatures in their memory. Paper, it turned out, could be unmapped too.

Pell held out the order and a pen. Odile signed. Her handwriting looked strange to her—she had written so many numbers in the last three years, so many elevations and chainages and curve radii, that letters felt like a language she'd set down and only now picked back up. *Odile Lockridge, Engineer of Record.* The ink dried gray in the thin mountain air.

"Eleven years," Pell said, following her gaze up the valley. "You'd think the ground would've changed more."

"Ground doesn't lie," Odile said. "It just gets ignored."

* * *

They came back the way water comes back into a dry channel—first a trickle, then a run, then a river that seemed to have always intended to be here.

The first was a woman named Marta Cade, who arrived on the supply train with two crates of beehive parts and stood a long time in the road where her house had been, because the house had fallen in and the apple trees had grown through the roof, and she wept, and then she laughed, and then she began clearing the branches with her bare hands before anyone could offer her gloves. Odile watched her from the line cabin's porch, three miles off, through a survey glass, and understood that she was watching something that no instrument could measure.

Then came the Hollis brothers, who had been boys when the town was erased and were now men with the particular wariness of people who had spent their adulthood being told they came from nowhere. Then a schoolteacher, gray at the temples, who walked straight to the schoolhouse, climbed the tower, and put her hand on the bell without ringing it, the way you touch the face of someone sleeping.

Tamsin watched them all from the top of the water tower, which was where Tamsin watched everything now, and Odile climbed the rusted ladder one evening to bring her bread and found her sitting with her knees drawn up, very still, in the way she went still when something mattered too much to move for.

"You could go down," Odile said, handing her the bread. "Marta Cade's been asking after you. Half the town has. The girl who kept the name alive."

"I know." Tamsin broke the bread and didn't eat it. Below them, lamplight was coming on in windows that had been dark for eleven years, and the sound of the Hollis brothers arguing cheerfully about a fence line carried up the valley like music from a country that had resumed broadcasting. "That's the trouble."

"Trouble."

"They remember it different." Tamsin's voice was flat, and Odile heard the effort it cost to keep it flat. "Mrs. Cade remembers the bell ringing at noon. It rang at eight and four. I know because I copied the charter in the schoolhouse and the bell schedule was posted beside it, and I sat there so long the teacher made me sweep the floor as payment. Eight and four. But Mrs. Cade says noon, and now everyone will say noon, because she's older and she has a house to come back to and I have—" She stopped. She turned the bread over in her hands. "I have a memory. That's all I came back with. A town is houses and fences and a bell schedule, and I don't have any of it. I'm not going home. I never had anywhere to go home *to*. I had a thing I knew, and now everyone knows it, and I don't know what I am now that I'm not the only one."

The wind moved through the tower's struts and made a low organ note of them. Far below, the bell in the schoolhouse tower caught the last light and held it, dull gold, patient.

"You're the one who was right," Odile said.

"Being right is a terrible thing to be made of."

Odile sat down beside her on the tower's edge, boots hanging over the drop, the way Tamsin sat, because in four months of high country and storm the girl had taught her that much: that the edge was the only place with a view. "When my brother died," she said, "I stopped counting years. I counted miles instead. Miles were honest. A mile is a mile whether you're happy or not. Years were—years had him in them, and after, they had the shape of him missing, and I couldn't bear the shape." She looked out at the valley, at the smoke rising thin and domestic from chimneys that had been cold since Tamsin was six. "I'm telling you this because I want you to know that a person can lose the thing that named her and still get named again. Not the same name. A new one. It doesn't feel like a restoration. It feels like a survey of ground you thought you knew, and then one day you look at the numbers and realize the numbers have been quietly describing a different, larger country all along."

Tamsin was quiet a long time. "What's your new name?"

"I don't have it yet. I think it's still being surveyed." She nudged the girl's shoulder with her own. "But I know what mine is made out of. It's made out of the people who were there when the map ran out. You were there when my map ran out. That's not nothing, Tamsin. That's the whole territory."

Below, Mrs. Cade lit a lamp in her ruined-and-reclaiming house, and the light fell across the orchard grass, and somewhere down there a hive was being reassembled by hands that remembered every part.

* * *

The bell rang on the morning of the restoration ceremony, and it rang at eight, because Odile had written the schedule out herself from Tamsin's recitation and nailed it to the schoolhouse door, and when Mrs. Cade protested—gently, puzzled, certain of her noon—Tamsin had stood in front of the whole assembled town with the charter in her memory and recited the section on municipal observances, word for word, in a clear voice that did not shake, and Mrs. Cade had listened with her hand over her mouth and then said, "Well. Eight and four. Isn't that funny. I can *hear* it now, eight and four," and wept again, and this time the weeping sounded less like grief and more like a door opening.

The ceremony was short. The interim administrator read the order. The survey stakes were painted a second coat. Someone produced a fiddle. The town that had been a rumor for eleven years ate a midday meal off planks laid across sawhorses, and the food was the plain mountain food of beans and bread and a lamb someone had driven down the pass, and it tasted, everyone agreed, like a great deal more than it was.

Odile stood at the edge of it, as she stood at the edge of most things, and let it happen around her. She had been thanked at the hearing; she had been offered a commission, a title, a desk in the capital with her name in gilt on the door, and she had asked for a different thing: the reopening of the mountain lines, all of them, the closed spur and the flooded cutting and the tunnel under the shoulder, surveyed true and run again. Trains through country that had been declared empty. Wheels on rails through the valley, so that Bellbrook would never again be a place you could only reach by disappearing.

Tamsin found her at the fence line, where the old orchard met the new right-of-way, and stood beside her without speaking for a while. The girl had new boots—Mrs. Cade had insisted—and wore them like a treaty between two countries.

"They're all asking me to stay," Tamsin said. "Mrs. Cade wants me in the school. She says I should learn to be a student now, after being the whole archive." She smiled a little. "The Hollises want me to help run the boundary line up to the high pasture. Everyone has a place picked out for me. It's like being a stake. They want to drive me into the ground and paint me."

"Is that so bad? A stake is a true thing. It says: here, exactly here, this is real."

"It's bad if it's all you are." Tamsin turned to look at her, and in the afternoon light Odile could see how much the girl had changed since the shale slopes—filled out, steadied, her eyes holding less of the wild watchfulness and more of something that had a right to rest. "You're leaving tomorrow. The survey train. Up the old spur, the whole closed line, all winter if the tunnels hold."

"The whole line. Every mile of it. And it'll take years." Odile heard herself say the word and let it stand there, unflinching. *Years.* She was counting in years again. It still caught in her chest sometimes, the shape of her brother missing from them, but the shape was changing, filling in at the edges, the way a valley fills in when the water comes back.

"So I'm asking," Tamsin said, and now her voice did shake, just at the edges, just enough. "Don't make me choose between being a stake and being a stray. I've been a stray. I was good at it and I hated it. I want—" She looked down the line, at the rails running west toward the pass, toward the shoulder, toward the black tunnel where a bell song had once pulled them both through the dark. "I want to come with you. I know every path the old maps lost. I know where the water goes before it knows itself. You've got the instruments and I've got the memory, and between us we're the only survey crew in this whole range that can measure what a place *was* and what it *is* and tell the truth about the difference." She stopped, breathless, and finished, quieter: "And I don't want to go back to being the only one who remembers things. I want to be one of two."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Then out at the valley—the bell tower straightened now, the smoke rising, the stakes bright in the low sun, the town that existed again because a girl had refused to forget it and an engineer had refused to stop measuring—and she found that the thing in her chest that had been a wound for three years had become something else, something with weight and warmth, like a stove banked for the night.

"Rule one," she said. "We measure everything twice."

"Rule two," Tamsin said, and her grin was sudden and young and lit her whole face like the lamp in Mrs. Cade's window. "We eat before we argue."

"Rule three." Odile put out her hand, engineer to engineer, and the girl took it, and her grip was strong and rough and sure. "Nobody gets erased. Not towns. Not people. Not ever again. Not while either of us is drawing the map."

They shook on it there at the fence line, between the wild orchard and the new rails, while behind them the bell rang four o'clock, exactly as it was written, and the sound went out across the valley and up the slopes and into the high country where the snow was already thinking about falling—and down the line, far off, the survey train's whistle answered it, one long note, like a voice calling a name it had finally learned to say.
