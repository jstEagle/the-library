## 22. Years, Not Miles

The spring came up the valley the way water finds a channel—slowly at first, then all at once, as if it had only been waiting for permission.

Odile Lockridge stood on the reopened line above Rivergate with her hands in her coat pockets and the wind pushing gently against her back, and she watched the meltwater run silver in the ditches alongside the trackbed. The rails gleamed where the crews had polished them with a season of use. Somewhere down the grade, a locomotive breathed in its yard, patient as livestock, waiting for the day's first run. The sound carried clean through the thin air, and for the first time in three years the sound did not feel like an accusation.

She had come out before dawn, as was her habit, though the habit had changed its shape. Once she had walked these closed lines to measure them—to count ties, to log curvature, to fill ledger after ledger with distances that added up to nothing except more distance. Now she walked them because the morning was beautiful, and because Tamsin would be awake when she got home, and because the kettle would be on, and none of those reasons required a surveyor's justification.

That was the strangest part, still. That a life could be justified by a kettle.

* * *

She had brought the marker with her, wrapped in sacking and slung across her shoulders like a yoke. It was a length of rail cut down to four feet, its head ground flat and smooth, and into that face she had chiseled the letters herself over three evenings at the forge behind the cabin, while Tamsin sat on an upturned crate and read aloud from the recovered Bellbrook charter—not because Odile needed to hear it again, but because Tamsin liked saying it, the way some people liked singing. *Be it known to all who come after.* The words rang in the little forge like a bell being struck softly, over and over, until the iron seemed to absorb them.

Now she set the rail down at mile 114 of the old high line—the stretch her brother Aldous had been walking the night the trestle went, three years ago last autumn. She knew the spot within a pace. She had measured it often enough, in the years when measuring was all she could do: 114 miles and six chains from the Rivergate zero-post, forty-one paces east of the culvert where the snowmelt sang even in August. She had stood here in every weather, telling herself she was surveying, telling herself the numbers meant something, when what she had really been doing was standing vigil at a grave that had no stone.

Aldous's body had never been found. The river below the trestle kept what it took; everyone said so, and eventually she had believed them, or something close enough to belief to let her stop walking the banks. But the track itself had remained, closed and rusting, and so she had remained with it, two abandoned things keeping each other company.

Today she dug the hole for the marker with a spade she'd sharpened the night before, and the earth came up dark and loose and smelling of thaw, and it went easily, almost willingly, as if the ground had been expecting her.

* * *

She worked without hurry. There was no hurry anywhere anymore—that was the other thing the winter in the capital had taught her, watching Hale's paper empire come apart sheet by sheet under the inquiry's patient hands. Corvus Hale had built his whole architecture on the assumption that no one would ever check. That maps were believed because they were printed, that silence was the same as absence, that a town erased from the rolls simply stopped having happened. He had assumed, in the end, that reality was whatever the filing system said it was.

He had never met a girl who memorized a charter because she loved the sound of it.

Odile tamped the ballast around the rail's base, stone by stone, the way you'd set a fencepost, testing it with both hands until it didn't give. Then she stood back and looked at what she'd made. Four feet of iron rising out of the trackbed shoulder, its face catching the early light. The letters were not elegant—she was a surveyor, not a mason—but they were deep, and they were true, and they would outlast the frost heaves and the summers and probably her.

**ALDOUS LOCKRIDGE**
**WALKED THIS LINE**
**THE LINE REMEMBERS**

Tamsin had suggested the last part. Odile had argued that lines didn't remember anything, that memory lived in people or nowhere, and Tamsin had looked at her with that level, unblinking look she had—the one that had stared down an inquiry board at fourteen years old—and said, "Then why did you keep walking it?"

Odile hadn't had an answer then. Standing here now, with the wind moving through the new grass along the embankment and the meltwater talking in the ditch, she thought maybe she did. The line remembered because she had made it remember—had walked it and measured it and refused, for three bitter years, to let it be closed and forgotten the way Bellbrook had been closed and forgotten. Her exile and her grief had been the same act: a one-woman survey crew holding a record open against the erasure that comes with time, which is gentler than Hale's kind but just as thorough if nobody fights it.

She put her palm flat against the cold iron. It was a thing she had done at her brother's empty room, at his tools, at his coat still hanging by the cabin door—a substitute for a hand she couldn't hold anymore. But this time the gesture felt different. Less like reaching for something gone. More like setting something down.

"You'd have liked her," she said aloud, to the rail, to the morning, to whatever part of Aldous the river hadn't managed to swallow. "She's stubborn in ways you weren't and honest in ways I'm not. She hums when she thinks. You'd have driven each other mad inside a week."

The wind moved the grass. The water kept its time in the ditch. It was not an answer, but it was not nothing either, and she had learned this year that not-nothing was most of what the world offered anyone, and that it was enough if you let it be.

* * *

She heard Tamsin coming before she saw her—heard the particular rhythm of her boots on the ties, quick and light, a walk learned on mountain paths where hesitation gets you hurt. The girl came around the curve below with a canvas bag over one shoulder and her hair tied back with the red cord she wore everywhere, the last thing she owned from Bellbrook besides the words in her head.

"I knew you'd come here first," Tamsin said, stopping beside the marker. She read the letters twice, lips moving slightly, the way she read everything—carefully, completely, as if text might change if you skimmed it. After what had been done to the maps, Odile supposed, careful reading was not paranoia. It was citizenship.

"The line remembers," Tamsin said.

"So I'm told."

"It's good." She crouched and touched the base of the rail where it met the ballast, checking the work the way Odile had taught her to check everything—hands first, eyes second, trust third. "It won't shift. You set it deep."

"Deeper than the archive set Bellbrook," Odile said, and Tamsin laughed—one short bark, surprised out of her, the laugh she was still learning to spend freely. A year ago she had hoarded laughter like food in a siege winter. Now she spent it, mostly, on Odile's grimness, which she seemed to find inexhaustibly funny.

They stood together a while. Down the valley the morning smoke rose from the houses of Rivergate, straight columns in the still air, and beyond them the range held its last rags of snow on the high shoulders, white going gray at the edges as the season ate it away. The pass was open. The pass would stay open until October, and next spring it would open again, and the one after that. She had spent so long thinking of the mountains as walls. They were doors. They had always been doors; she had simply been standing on the wrong side of them.

"There's a train at eight," Tamsin said. "Marta said we could ride up to the summit section if we wanted. They're running the first passenger service over since the closure." She glanced sideways. "We don't have to."

"We'll go," Odile said.

"Yeah?"

"I've spent three years walking that grade. I'd like to see it from the window for once. Let somebody else do the engineering." She picked up the empty sacking and folded it over her arm. "Besides. You've never seen your valley from the high traverse. From up there you can see all the way down to where Bellbrook was."

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. When Odile looked over, the girl was watching the low green fold of hills to the south, behind which, thirty miles distant, the restored settlement was waking to its second spring. Survivors had been trickling home since the thaw—eleven families so far, the inquiry's rolls said, drawn back by notices posted in every hall in the province: *BELLBROOK — REESTABLISHED — LAND GRANTS AVAILABLE — ALL FORMER CLAIMS HONORED.* Some had come old and bent and weeping. Some had come with children born in exile who had never seen the orchards their parents described. And some, the rolls admitted quietly, had not come at all, because three years is long enough for a life to grow roots elsewhere, and a map can restore a town's name faster than it can restore a town.

Tamsin had been offered a land grant. She had stood in the inquiry chamber with the charter still warm in her mouth, having recited all nine sections without a single error while the board's stenographer struggled to keep pace, and afterward the chairman himself had asked what she wanted, and she had said, "For it to be true," and then, after a pause, "And to think about the rest."

She was still thinking about the rest. She had decided, for now, to think about it in Odile's spare room, with the window that faced the mountains and the shelf where Odile was slowly building her the beginnings of a library—survey manuals, yes, but also novels, poetry, anything with words worth memorizing. "You never know," Tamsin had said, taking possession of the shelf with a look that brooked no appeal, "what they'll try to erase next. Best to keep copies."

* * *

They walked down together toward the station, and Odile found herself doing something she had not done in three years: counting in seasons.

It was a small arithmetic, but it changed everything downstream of it. Miles were a currency of avoidance—you could always add another, always keep moving, always convert grief into distance and spend it before it could be felt. Seasons could not be converted. A season arrived whether you walked toward it or away, and it stayed exactly as long as it stayed, and then it left, and you were left standing in the residue of it with nothing to do but notice how you had changed. Spring had come, and she had noticed. That was all. That was everything.

Three springs ago, she had been a woman alone in a cabin full of instruments, measuring a dead man's last road. Two springs ago, the same, with the ledgers thicker and the silence thicker too. One spring ago, she had been following a trail of abandoned survey gear up a shale slope, telling herself it was professional curiosity, knowing already that it was a door opening.

This spring, she was walking to a train with a girl who remembered a town, past a marker for a brother who walked his line, toward a summer that needed nothing from her except that she live in it.

At the station, Marta waved them aboard the single passenger car, all fresh paint and smelling of sawdust and varnish, and the conductor—a round, cheerful man who had been a signalman on the closed lines and still treated every reopening like a festival—punched their tickets with unnecessary flourish. The whistle sounded. The wheels took up their song, that slow syncopation Odile had known since childhood, and the platform slid away, and the valley opened ahead of them like a page.

Tamsin took the window seat. Of course she did. She pressed close to the glass as the line climbed, watching the country unroll—the ditches running bright, the orchards in blossom, the far blue folds of the range where the pass cut through—and Odile watched her instead, this child of an unmapped town who had become, without either of them quite deciding it, the fixed point her own life now swung around.

"What are you looking at?" Tamsin asked, catching her.

"The line," Odile said. "Remembering."

The train climbed. Below them, small and certain in the morning light, mile 114 slid past, and the iron marker stood at its post in the trackbed, holding its lettering to the sky, keeping its record open against all the silences to come. Odile did not look back at it. She had spent three years looking back. She leaned her head against the seat, felt the rails humming their steady count beneath her—seasons now, seasons now, seasons now—and rode forward into the green world, home.
