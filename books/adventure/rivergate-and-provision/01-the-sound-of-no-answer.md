## 1. The Sound of No Answer

The radio went quiet at 0600 on a Tuesday, which was the first thing wrong with it.

I want to be clear about that, because everyone else in the office at Provision River Freight treated the silence like weather — regrettable, impersonal, nothing to be done. But I'd been running the upper river for seven years, and I can tell you how radios die. They die in static. They die mid-word, mid-syllable, a man's voice sheared off like a rope under load. They die drunk, when some survey hand knocks the set into the cook pot and swears about it for a week. What they do not do is stop cleanly, on schedule, between one scheduled transmission and the next.

The Callisto expedition had checked in every Sunday and Thursday at 1800 hours, six weeks running. I knew because their supply contract sat on my desk, and because I read contracts the way other men read scripture — looking for the parts where somebody's lying to me. Their last transmission came through Thursday at 1802, two minutes late, which the log noted as *slight atmospheric interference*. Then Tuesday morning the freight office got a call from the sponsor's representative, a smooth-voiced man named Corben who wore his suit like an apology he never intended to make, asking whether we could have the resupply boat ready by Friday.

"Ready by Friday," I said. "The expedition hasn't confirmed they need it."

"They've gone quiet," Corben said, in the tone of a man discussing a delayed train. "Equipment failure, most likely. These remote stations are always losing gear."

"And you're not worried."

"Should I be?"

It wasn't a question. It was a door being closed politely in my face, and I noticed that, and I filed it away, because noticing things is most of what I'm good for. The rest is piloting, which is really just noticing things faster.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about me, since you're stuck with my voice for ten chapters: I don't take jobs like this anymore.

Seven years ago I would have. Seven years ago I took everything — crooked cargo, honest cargo, passengers, salvage, anything that pointed the bow upriver into country where the maps got apologetic. My brother Tomas used to say I had river fever, that I couldn't stand still long enough for trouble to find me twice. He said it laughing, usually, one hand on the wheel of our old boat while I checked the fuel lines, both of us twenty-something and immortal the way men are at that age, which is to say completely by accident and only until further notice.

Tomas died on a Tuesday too. Drowned, four miles below Serrano Rapids, when a load shifted in a crosscurrent and took him over the rail before I could reach him. I was at the wheel. I was always at the wheel; he was always the one who went out on deck, because he liked the water and I respected it, and there is a difference, and the difference killed him. Four miles. I measured it afterward, obsessively, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Four miles of brown water between the spot he went in and the spot they found him, and I have spent seven years putting miles between myself and everything else instead, because miles are honest. Miles don't promise anything. A mile is just a mile, and if you keep enough of them behind you, you never have to hear a silence like the one coming out of that camp radio and wonder whose voice used to fill it.

So no, I don't take rescue jobs. I don't take strange jobs. I take scheduled runs with signed manifests and clients who answer their own telephones, and I go home to a bunk aboard the *Rivergate* that I have made, through rigorous practice, into the least interesting place on earth.

Which is why, Wednesday night, I stood on her deck with Corben's contract in one hand and a kerosene lamp in the other, reading it three times, looking for the reason to say no.

* * *

The contract itself was clean. Too clean, maybe — indemnity clauses stacked like sandbags, the syndicate's name nowhere and its fingerprints everywhere in the language — but clean enough. Fuel, medical stores, dry goods, spare batteries for the radio. Standard resupply weight, standard rates. And then, stapled to the manifest, a single line item that didn't belong:

**One (1) sealed crate, contents undisclosed. Handling per client instruction. Do not open.**

I called Corben at his hotel. It was late, but men like Corben are always awake; sleep is for people without leverage.

"The crate," I said. "What's in it?"

"Survey equipment."

"It weighs forty kilos and the manifest says fragile. Survey equipment doesn't get marked fragile unless it's glass, and glass doesn't weigh forty kilos."

A pause, long enough to pour a drink into. "Captain Ashgrove. You were recommended to me as a man who delivers cargo and asks no questions. Was I misinformed?"

"No," I said. "But I'll tell you what I am. I'm a man who doesn't run sealed crates upriver to camps that have stopped answering their radios. That's not a delivery, Mr. Corben. That's a funeral with extra steps."

Another pause. When he spoke again, the apology had drained out of his voice, leaving something smoother and colder underneath. "The crate goes or the contract doesn't. Your choice, Captain. You have until noon."

He hung up. I stood there in the lamplight with the receiver warm in my hand, and I will be honest with you, because Felix Ashgrove lying to himself is how we all got into this mess: I wanted to say no. I wanted it the way a drowning man wants air. Every instinct I'd spent seven years sharpening told me to walk down to the office in the morning, slide the contract across the desk, and let some younger fool with fewer ghosts take the job.

Instead I climbed down into the engine room, because that's where I go when I can't think, and started checking the fuel lines I'd already checked.

* * *

The *Rivergate* is forty years old and ugly as a mudfight, steel-hulled, shallow-drafted, built for exactly the kind of water the maps apologize for. My father helped lay her keel. Tomas grew up in her engine room the way other kids grow up in kitchens. When he died I thought about selling her — thought about it for one long bad month — and then I realized that selling her would mean admitting the miles hadn't worked, and I wasn't ready for that admission, so I repainted her instead. She's been repainted four times since. The rust always comes back. I've come to respect that about her.

That night in the engine room, with my hands busy and my head finally quiet, I heard him.

Not literally. I want that on the record. I don't believe in ghosts; I believe in memory, which is worse, because at least ghosts have the decency to be someone else's fault. But the engine has a rhythm at idle, a low uneven throb, and somewhere in my seventh year of listening to it, it became his voice. Don't ask me how grief works. Nobody knows. It just moves into your machinery and starts making repairs you never asked for.

*"You're doing the thing,"* the rhythm said. *"Where you decide the safe thing and the right thing are different things, and pick the safe thing, and then spend a month telling yourself they were the same."*

"I'm doing the sensible thing," I said out loud, to an empty engine room, like a madman.

*"You're doing the scared thing. There's a difference."*

"There's people missing up there, Tom. Maybe dead already. Maybe nothing. I run supplies. I'm not police, I'm not army, I'm not anybody's hero."

*"No,"* the engine agreed, and the rhythm softened the way his voice used to soften when he'd cornered me and knew it. *"You're their ride. Somebody's up there hurt or hungry or hiding, and the man who's supposed to bring them fuel and medicine is standing in his engine room arguing with his dead brother. So. Are we going, or are we going to repaint the boat again?"*

I sat on the tool chest for a long time after that, listening to the idle fall back into being just an idle.

Here's the thing about restless duty: it isn't noble. I want to be clear about that too. It's not courage, it's not virtue, it's not any of the words they put on monuments. It's an itch. It's the knowledge that somewhere upriver there's a silence shaped exactly like the one I've been running from for seven years, and that the only way to make it smaller is to drive straight into it. Duty, for me, has always just been self-protection with better manners. If I go, and they're alive, I don't have to wonder. If I go, and they're dead, I don't have to wonder either. Wondering is the thing I can't afford. Wondering is what killed Tomas — not the water, not the current, but the four miles of *what if* I've carried ever since.

At noon I signed the contract. At two I was loading fuel drums, and at four, the crate.

* * *

The crate bothered me the whole time I wrestled it aboard. Forty-one kilos by the scale, pine boards, no markings except FRAGILE stenciled in fresh paint over older paint that had once read something else — sanded and restenciled, sloppy work, the kind of job done fast by men who assumed nobody upriver would ever look closely. I looked closely. Looking closely is my whole profession.

Under the fresh stencil, in the old paint, half-legible: **CAL —** and then nothing. Callisto? Calibrated instruments? Or something else entirely, something the syndicate's people hadn't wanted shipped under its real name?

I put it amidships, lashed it down, and tarped it, and then I stood over it for a minute with my hands on my hips feeling like a man who'd agreed to carry a lit stove into a hay barn.

"You're a box," I told it. "You're forty kilos of survey equipment, and you're going to sit there and mind your business, and so am I."

The box said nothing. Boxes are good at that. It was the first silence of many on this trip, though I didn't know it yet, and unlike the radio silence, at least this one was honest.

* * *

We cast off Friday at dawn, alone, because I run the *Rivergate* solo and prefer it that way. The harbor fog was burning off, the water high and green-brown from last week's rains, everything according to schedule. Two days upriver to the Callisto camp, one day loading whatever needed loading, two days back. Five days, plus margin. In and out. Miles, measurable and finite.

I passed the last settlement at noon, gave a lazy horn to the fisherman drying nets on the point, and settled into the channel with the throttle at cruising and the world reduced to its proper components: water, bank, sky, engine. This is the part of the life I actually love, and I'll defend it — the long middle of a run, when the boat and the river agree on a speed and the mind goes smooth as slack water. For a few hours I almost believed the trip would be ordinary.

Then, near dusk, thirty miles below the camp, I caught the smell of woodsmoke where there shouldn't have been any, and rounded a bend to find a moored launch flying no colors, its crew of four watching me pass with the fixed, pleasant attention of men counting my guns and my cargo. One of them lifted a hand in a friendly wave. All four of them wore the same kind of boots — new, city boots, wrong for the river — and none of them wore the sunburn of men who'd been on the water longer than a week.

Surveyors, their banner said. Mineral assessment, provisional concession, all very official.

I waved back and gave them nothing else, and held my course, and did not slow down. But I watched them in the mirror until the bend took them, and I did the arithmetic I should have done in Corben's office: four men, city boots, thirty miles downstream of a silent camp, arriving *after* the silence started.

Somebody had switched that radio off. And somebody else was already on the river, heading the same direction I was, and I had a sealed crate lashed in my hold that suddenly felt less like cargo and more like a question I'd promised not to ask.

Ahead, the water ran dark toward the hills, and the first stars came out over a camp that had stopped answering, and I opened the throttle, because whatever was waiting up there, I'd rather meet it moving.
