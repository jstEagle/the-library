## 2. Miles Instead of Years

There is a particular smell to a river dock at dawn — diesel and wet rope and the green rot of things the water has decided to keep — and I have come to think of it as the smell of a day that hasn't yet had time to disappoint me. Seven years of dawns like this one. You'd think I'd be bored. You'd be wrong. Boredom requires wanting something else, and I gave up wanting in the same season I gave up answering letters.

The Rivergate sat low on her lines at the Provision wharf, taking on cargo the way an old woman takes on opinions: slowly, with visible disapproval. Fuel drums first, because fuel is honest — it burns, it moves you, it asks nothing back. Then the medical crates for the Verrazano expedition, forty miles upriver past the last map that anyone respectable had bothered to draw.

And then there was the other crate.

It sat apart from everything else on the wharf, pine boards, no markings, heavier than it had any right to be. The client's man — a solicitor's clerk with the complexion of someone who worked indoors on principle — had delivered it with instructions written out twice, once formally and once, apparently, in case I was stupid.

*Do not open. Do not weigh. Do not discuss.*

"I've hauled coffins before," I told him. "At least those came with names."

"Captain Ashgrove." He said my name like he was reading it off a warrant. "The expedition paid in advance. The crate travels as-is."

Everything travels as-is, in my experience. That's the whole trouble with rivers.

I signed. Of course I signed. A man who measures his life in miles doesn't ask what's in the box; he asks how far.

* * *

My brother Tomas used to say I was born with river water where other people kept their patience. He was the patient one. Seven years older, which meant seven years of being the one who held the flashlight while I did something inadvisable, and he never once complained about it, which should have warned me. People who never complain are saving it up.

We ran the *Provision* together, back then — our father's old boat, our father's old debts, our father's old habit of naming things after towns we'd never afford to live in. Tomas handled the engine room because he liked machines that made sense. I handled the wheel because I liked water that didn't.

"You know what your problem is?" he said to me once, maybe six months before the end. We were tied up at Serrano Bend waiting out a storm, playing cards by lamplight while the rain tried its best to drown us out of sheer spite. "You count everything in miles. Miles to Verrazano. Miles since the last port. Miles of channel left. You know what you never count?"

"Days," I said. "You tell me every week."

"Years," he said. "You never count years. Like if you don't count them they can't catch up to you."

He won that hand, too. He usually did. I let him, mostly, because watching him gloat was cheaper than most entertainment and better than all of it.

Here is what I have learned about counting: Tomas was right, and being right killed him deader than any superstition ever could. Because the year we stopped counting — the year I finally agreed to slow down, take the winter refit, stay put through the floods like a sensible man — that was the year the crane cable snapped.

* * *

They tell you grief comes in waves. Liars, all of them. Grief doesn't wave. It pools. It sits in the low places of you and waits, and then some small thing — a card game, a laugh with his exact pitch in it, a crate that won't open — tips over into the pool, and suddenly you're underwater in a place you thought was dry ground.

The cable snapped at 4:17 in the afternoon on a Tuesday in March, and I know the time because I checked my watch afterward and then hated myself for checking, and then checked again anyway, because a mind wants facts even when the fact is unbearable. Two hundred kilos of engine block, swinging on a line that some supplier had certified and some rigger had trusted and I had signed off on, because signing off on things was my job and I was good at my job.

Tomas was under it. That's the whole story. Everything else is just weather.

The inquiry cleared me. Faulty certification, third-party liability, condolences of the court. I sold the *Provision* within the month, bought the *Rivergate* with the insurance money, and pointed her upriver, because downriver led home and upriver led anywhere else.

Seven years. I could tell you how many miles. I couldn't tell you how many Tuesdays.

* * *

Loading finished by mid-morning, and I stood on the wharf doing the arithmetic I do instead of praying: fuel for nine days, water for six, medicine for forty mouths I hadn't met, and one crate of secrets riding amidships like a passenger who wouldn't meet my eye.

Dov appeared around the time the last drum went aboard.

I want to be clear that I did not invite him aboard. He was simply *there*, fourteen years old and all elbows, sitting on my mooring bollard like it was municipal property, watching me work with the frank appraisal of a boy deciding whether a man was worth stealing from or merely robbing.

"You're the supply run," he said.

"I'm a private citizen minding my own commerce."

"You're going up to the Verrazano camp."

"The river goes many places."

"Not today it doesn't." He hopped off the bollard, and I noticed then that he moved like a stray dog — light on his feet, weight always ready to leave. "I'll work. Deckhand. I know knots."

"Who taught you knots?"

"A book." He said it defiantly, as though books were contraband, and maybe for him they were. There was something about him I didn't like, and it took me half a day to identify it: he looked at the *Rivergate* the way I used to look at her. Like a way out. Boys with that look either find a boat or find trouble, and boats are scarcer.

"No," I said.

He nodded as if I'd said yes, which is a trick I respect, and vanished down the wharf before I could reorganize my refusal into something with more syllables. I told myself I'd forgotten him by nightfall. I told myself a lot of things that day; it was that kind of day.

* * *

The client's clerk came back at dusk, which clerks do not do, and that alone should have been enough to make me cast off in the dark and bill him for the inconvenience. Instead I bought him a beer at the Provision Arms, mostly because the alternative was loading cargo alone with my thoughts, and my thoughts and I have a strained relationship built on seven years of mutual avoidance.

"The expedition's gone quiet," he said, into his glass.

"So I'm told. Radios fail. Rivers eat them."

"This isn't radio failure." He turned the glass a quarter-turn, precise, the gesture of a man rehearsing. "The schedule was weekly contact. They missed two. The syndicate's survey teams are moving upriver ahead of their concession surveys, which is — irregular. And my firm would very much prefer that whatever happens next, the *Rivergate*'s manifest remains unremarkable."

"There it is," I said. "The crate."

He didn't confirm it. Confirming things is above a clerk's pay grade. But he looked at me with the flat honesty of a man who has decided I'm already implicated whether I like it or not, and said, "Captain, why do you take these runs? The upper river. Alone. Every contract, every season. A man your age—"

"My age," I said, "is none of my business."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give free."

He left money on the bar and left me with the question, which was rude of him, because I had spent seven years building a life specifically engineered so that no one would ask it. Why the upper river. Why alone. Why miles.

Because miles don't ask anything of you. A mile doesn't need you to be brave or kind or present. A mile doesn't die under a crane cable because you signed a certificate without reading the fine print. A mile is the only unit of distance between a man and the thing he can't outrun, and the terrible joke — the joke I only admit to at dawn, on a dock, when the diesel smell hasn't yet burned off — is that the thing doesn't get tired. It rides along. It sits amidships like an unmarked crate, and it weighs exactly what it weighs, and no manifest ever lists it.

* * *

I cast off at first light, alone, the way I liked it, the way I'd arranged it, the way I'd earned it through seven years of careful, joyless competence.

The *Rivergate* took the current and the current took us both, and for about four hours it was good. Just water and fuel gauges and the long green corridor of the upper channel opening ahead like a sentence I hadn't finished reading. I ate tinned beans on the flybridge. I checked the crate's lashings three times and resented it each time. I did not think about Tomas, which is to say I thought about nothing else, the way an empty room is full of silence.

Then, around the second bend past Marker Rock, I saw the smoke.

Not cooking smoke. Cooking smoke rises lazy and apologetic. This rose straight and thin and dark, the way a signal fire burns when someone wants to be seen from a long way off — or the way a building burns when someone wants it seen falling.

And below it, rounding the point downstream of me, moving fast against etiquette and probably against the law, came a boat I didn't know: steel-hulled, new paint, running lights lit in broad daylight like a man wearing sunglasses at midnight. Three figures on deck. None of them waving.

Surveyors, the clerk had said. Moving upriver. Irregular.

I throttled down and let the current have its say, and watched the strange boat grow smaller ahead of me, heading toward a camp that hadn't answered its radio in two weeks, carrying men whose boat was worth more than my whole year.

Somewhere behind me, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a boy whistling from the reeds — Dov, apparently, having decided that *no* was a word that required further negotiation.

Ahead of me, the smoke.

I counted the miles to the Verrazano camp. Forty-one. I did not count anything else.
