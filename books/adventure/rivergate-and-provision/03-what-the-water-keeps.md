## 3. What the Water Keeps

The river tells you when you're being followed. Not by sight — by sound. A wake carries differently than current, a diesel idles with a smugness that no outboard matches, and somewhere behind me, two bends back, something big and patient was keeping my speed exactly.

I'd noticed it the evening before, at the fuel stop at Puerto Ceniza, where a man in a clean khaki shirt with too many pockets asked me, in the tone of a man making conversation rather than asking questions, whether I was running supplies up to the Verrone expedition.

"Contract work," I said. "I don't discuss cargo."

"Of course." He smiled like a lock turning. "We're surveyors. Mineral assessment, upper basin. You'll probably see our boat around."

I had seen their boat around. It was a river cruiser with a reinforced hull and an engine worth more than my whole vessel, painted the dull gray of official indifference, and it had been ahead of me since dawn — I could tell because the channel markers were freshly repainted on one side only, the side facing upstream traffic, which is the kind of detail a real survey crew never bothers with and a fake one always does.

Real surveyors argue about depth soundings. Fake ones ask about people.

So there were three facts stacked up like cards: the expedition's radio had gone silent on schedule, not accidentally; a well-armed "survey" boat was loitering between me and them; and my client — a logistics firm whose letterhead I hadn't bothered to verify, because the money cleared — had insisted on one unexplained crate, sealed, heavy, and marked FRAGILE in four languages.

Seven years ago I would have turned around. Six years ago I would have turned around faster. But grief is a strange currency, and I had discovered that the only thing it buys reliably is momentum. Turning around meant stopping, and stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant Tomás's voice in my head doing its usual arithmetic: *You can't outrun a thing by measuring the distance to it, Felix. You just end up knowing exactly how far away it is.*

"Shut up," I told him, and opened the throttle.

* * *

The second day upriver, the water changed its mind about me twice before noon.

The first time was at the Narrows of San Telmo, where the channel pinches between rock ribs older than language and the current runs like a rumor through a small town — fast, distorted, and dangerous to repeat. I took it the way I always do, reading the surface the way other men read faces: the V-shaped tongues of smooth water marking safe lines, the dark boils where the river was lying about its depth, the standing waves that meant rock underneath wearing a disguise.

The second time was at a bend called, on the old charts, La Curva del Ahogado. The Drowned Man's Bend. The charts were written by someone with a sense of humor and probably a personal loss.

There was a campfire ring on the gravel bar. Still warm. And beside it, half-buried in the sand where someone had kicked it in haste or contempt, a cigarette butt with a brand I recognized from Puerto Ceniza — the same brand the pocketed man had been smoking while smiling his locked-door smile.

They weren't following me anymore. They were ahead of me. Which meant they knew where I was going, which meant they knew what I was carrying, which meant the crate in my hold wasn't cargo so much as bait, and I was the fish, and somebody had spent real money on the hook.

I stood at the rail for a long moment, listening to the river say nothing, and did the math every captain eventually has to do: the cost of going back against the cost of going on. Going back meant I'd never know. Going on meant I might.

Tomás again, unhelpfully: *Since when do you need to know? Since when is not-knowing not enough for you?*

"Since somebody made it personal," I said out loud, and went below to check the medicine locker, because whatever was waiting upriver, someone there was going to need what I carried, and that much at least I could still be sure of.

* * *

The expedition camp sat on a terrace above the confluence, in the elbow of land where the Río Blanco pours its pale sediment into the darker main channel. From the water it looked almost peaceful — the canvas mess tent sagging, equipment tarped, a wind sock drooping like a tired flag. Then I got close enough to see the details, and the details were wrong in the specific way that details are wrong after violence.

The generator had been shot. Not disabled — shot, twice, methodically, the way you kill a thing you want to stay dead. The radio mast lay across the supply shed with its guy wires cut, and the shed door hung open on shelves that had been searched by someone thorough and angry. The records office — a smaller tent, the kind archaeologists use for paper that matters more than food — was a circle of black ash and curled metal. Someone had burned everything and then burned it again, raking the coals flat, and had done it recently enough that the ash still held the shape of file boxes.

And there was a grave.

It was new — the earth still dark, the marker a piece of tent pole with no name, just a date scratched into the wood with a knife. Three mounds, actually, side by side, dug deep and filled carefully, which told me the digging had been done by friends, not enemies. Enemies don't dig deep. Enemies don't line the graves with stones. Whoever was left had done right by whoever was gone, and then they had run, or hidden, or died, and the camp had been keeping their secret for days.

I counted the tents. Eight sleeping berths in the manifest I'd been given. Three graves. That left five unaccounted for, and none of the possibilities were good.

I was standing over the graves, hat in my hand because some habits are older than caution, when I heard the click.

Not a rifle bolt. A camera shutter. An old film camera, the cheap kind, and it came from the cliff face north of the camp where the limestone folded into a shadow I had taken for geology.

"I have a rifle," said a voice, young and trying very hard to be steady. "I'm not afraid to use it. Probably. Don't come closer."

"Dov," said another voice, weaker, from deeper in the shadow. "Don't."

The boy stepped out anyway — fourteen, maybe, all elbows and defiance, holding a rusted .22 like it was a ceremonial object he'd read about in a book. Behind him, half in the cave mouth, a woman lay propped against a rolled tarp, her left leg wrapped in bandages that had been white once and were now the color of the river in flood. She had a fever shine on her face and eyes that assessed me the way I assess water: fast, professional, looking for the rocks.

"You're not syndicate," she said. It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway.

"No. I'm the supply run that came late." I set my hat back on. "Felix Ashgrove. Rivergate and Provision."

"The Rivergate." Something moved behind her eyes — relief, maybe, or its close cousin, calculation. "Ilsa Reyes. Cartographer. We put in a resupply request six weeks ago."

"It got rerouted," I said. "By your sponsor, I'd guess, straight into the hands of the men who burned your records. There's a gray cruiser working this stretch calling itself a mineral survey. Four men, maybe five. They were at Puerto Ceniza asking about you."

She closed her eyes briefly, the way you do when a diagnosis confirms itself. "Marrow's people."

"You know the name?"

"Everyone who's ever been bought knows the name." She shifted, and the pain of it whitened her knuckles. "Captain, how many days' fuel do you carry?"

"Enough to get home. Not enough to outrun that cruiser in open water."

"Then we have a problem," she said, "and a satchel full of reasons why they can't let us reach home."

That was when I noticed what the boy was clutching. He'd retreated to the cave wall with it when I approached — a leather satchel, old, patched, held against his chest with both arms the way children hold things that are either precious or dangerous, and in his case I suspected both. One corner of a stone slab protruded from the flap, incised with lines too regular to be natural and too confident to be modern.

"What's in the bag, Dov?" I asked.

He looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at me. Somewhere above us a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and the river kept saying nothing, the way it does when it already knows how a story ends and doesn't think it's worth spoiling.

"Tablets," the boy said finally. "From the city. Dr. Amaya said if anything happened to everyone else, they were mine to keep." His chin came up. "And something happened to everyone else."

* * *

We talked while the light went long and gold across the water, and what they told me rearranged the geography of everything I thought I knew about this river.

A terraced city, older than any culture in the national curriculum, older than the colonial maps, older than the empire whose ruins the textbooks start with. Causeways. Aqueducts still carrying water. Stone tablets bearing a script that predated every known writing system on the continent — proof, carved in permanent fact, that the land the mining consortium's concession covered had been a civilization's heart, not its empty frontier. Land with prior owners cannot legally be concessioned as vacant. The syndicate's entire claim rested on the fiction that nobody had ever lived here.

"So they need the site destroyed," Ilsa said, "and the discovery buried, and anyone who saw it buried with it, preferably in order."

"And your own sponsor sold you to them."

"Our sponsor owns forty percent of the consortium." She said it flatly, without surprise, which was somehow worse than tears. "The radio silence wasn't equipment failure, Captain. It was a signal. *Done.*"

Dov had fallen asleep against the satchel, mid-sentence, the way the young and exhausted do — one minute narrating the fall of the expedition, the next gone, his hand still hooked through the strap. Ilsa watched him with an expression I didn't have a name for yet but suspected I'd be learning.

"He stowed away at Puerto Ceniza," she said quietly. "Ran from a detention center. Marrow searched him once, at the camp, before the shooting started. He didn't find the tablets because Dov had already cached them in the cave. But it means—" 

"It means they know the tablets exist," I finished. "And they know someone has them."

"Yes."

I looked downriver, toward the narrows, toward the gray cruiser somewhere on the water between me and everything safe, and I felt the old familiar pull — the one that said *this is not your fight, Ashgrove, drop them at the nearest post, take your fee, keep your miles and your silence.* Seven years of practice at not belonging to anything larger than a hull and a channel.

But the boy was asleep against a bag full of the oldest writing on the continent, and the woman with the fever had handed her survival to a stranger because there was no one else on the river, and three fresh graves sat in the dirt behind me because some people had chosen money over the truth, and Tomás's voice, for once, wasn't telling me to measure anything.

It was just saying: *Well?*

I crouched by the cave mouth and laid out the map of the next hundred miles in my head — the shallows, the locks, the one place the syndicate couldn't easily follow.

"Here's what happens now," I said. "We load tonight. Medicine first, then you, then the boy. And then we're going to introduce those surveyors to a stretch of water they can't read and I can."

Ilsa studied me through the fever shine, and something in her face shifted — trust, extended like a hand across a gap.

"Captain," she said, "you should know they've blocked the lower channel. The lockmaster at Vado Seco is theirs. Every legal route home is closed."

I looked at the darkening water, and then at the boy, and then at the woman who had decided I was worth betting a fever on.

"Then we go the illegal way," I said. "Have you ever heard of the Serrano Rapids?"
