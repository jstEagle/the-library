## 4. Ilsa's Map

The fever comes in tides. That's the first thing I want to say about being shot, the thing nobody tells you: the pain is manageable, it's the fever that dismantles you. It arrives like the river arriving somewhere flat, spreading out over ground you thought was solid, and suddenly the camp is burning again, or it's raining inside the cave, or my mother is asking me why I never write, and I'm trying to explain that I do write, constantly, that I've covered three notebooks with contour lines and the names of dead colleagues, and she just looks at me with that patient disappointment until I wake up.

I wake up because someone is humming.

Not singing — humming, tuneless, the way a man hums when he's checking an engine by ear. I know that sound. Seven days on his boat and I hadn't heard him say forty words, but I'd heard the humming, low and constant, like the Rivergate itself had found a voice.

"Captain," I said. My mouth tasted like the inside of a boot.

"Fever's down a little," Felix Ashgrove said. He didn't look at me. He was doing something to my bandage with the concentration of a man defusing ordnance. "Drink this."

It was water with something bitter in it. I drank. Above us the cave mouth framed a slice of grey morning, and beyond it the river went about its business, indifferent as a bureaucrat.

"Where's the boy?"

"Sleeping. He sleeps like a cat. One eye open." A pause. "Your leg's ugly. But it'll hold. Bullet went through the meat, missed the bone. Whoever shot you was in a hurry or a bad mood."

"Both," I said. "It was Okafor."

The humming stopped.

I hadn't meant to say it. Or maybe I had — maybe the fever had been holding the door open for three days and my mouth finally walked through it. Felix tied off the bandage, set the scissors down with a small precise click, and finally looked at me. He has eyes like river water over stone: you can see the surface, but what's underneath is anybody's guess.

"Tell me," he said.

So I did. It came out in pieces, the way it always does now, fever or no fever. I'm a cartographer. I'm trained to render the world in order — north at the top, scale in the margin, the legend explaining every mark. But there is no legend for what we found upriver, and I have stopped pretending there is one.

* * *

We were eleven when we went in. Eleven, counting Dov, who we didn't count because he was folded up in the fuel locker with somebody's rain poncho and a stolen bag of dried plantains, and he didn't announce himself until the second night, when he climbed out of the locker like a jack-in-the-box and scared Okafor into the river. We should have turned back. Okafor argued for it. But we were six days up and the water was falling and every day of argument cost us a day of return, and I said — God help me, I said — *he's fourteen, he wanted an adventure, let him have it.* I liked him. He asked questions. Real ones, the kind with teeth in them, about how you know where a river goes when the maps stop. I told him: you ask the water. He wrote that down. In my notebook, actually, which should have been the first sign he was going to be trouble.

The sponsor was Meridian Provision & Survey. That's the name on the contract — Meridian, like a line drawn through the middle of the world. Dr. Halloway got the grant three years ago. Three years of permits, of supply runs, of a syndicate mining concession sitting on the upper basin like a lid on a pot. The concession is the whole reason we went, if you want the ugly truth. Halloway wanted to find something under that lid worth lifting it off. Something that would make it impossible for the consortium to strip-mine the basin and call it legal.

We found it.

You come around a bend at kilometer two hundred and eleven — I know the number, I'll always know the number — and the valley opens and there it is. Terraces. Nine of them, cut into the hillside, stone, dressed stone, fitted without mortar, running up the slope for nearly a kilometer. A city. Not a village, not a mound some culture built on its way to somewhere better. A city, with causeways and cisterns and a plaza the size of a cathedral, and not one shard of pottery, one glyph, one technique that matches anything in any museum I have ever stood in. It predates everything. The Olmec are children of it. The river was old when the river people came, and the river people built this, and then the river took the valley back and kept it for two thousand years, and the jungle grew over it like a bandage.

Halloway cried. I want that on the record somewhere, in whatever account of this eventually gets written: Dr. Edmund Halloway, who once made a graduate student cry over an inaccurate scale bar, sat down on a terrace step and cried like a man at a funeral and a christening at once.

For nine days we worked. Dov turned out to be useful — small hands, endless patience, no fear of the dark chambers. He found the first tablet. The tablets were in the cistern chamber, stacked like records in a crate, forty-one of them, stone, dense with script nobody on earth can read yet but which is *writing*, I'd stake my life on it, I have staked my life on it. A civilization with a written language, two millennia before we thought writing came to this basin, and every tablet is a page of a book we didn't know existed.

On the ninth day the radio crackled and a voice I didn't know asked for Halloway by name. Asked how the survey was progressing. Asked, very politely, for our coordinates.

Halloway gave them.

I want to be fair to him. He thought Meridian was Meridian. He thought the man on the radio was a supply check. It was three days later, when the second boat came — the one with the new outboards and the men who didn't look like surveyors, because surveyors don't carry that many guns and surveyors don't smile like that — that Okafor went through the satellite uplink logs and found the messages. Meridian Provision & Survey, our beloved sponsor, had been corresponding with the consortium's land office for eight months. Our permits, our route, our findings — all of it forwarded, all of it priced. Halloway's discovery wasn't a discovery to them. It was an obstacle. A terraced city two thousand years old sitting on top of a copper seam, and under the concession law, an unregistered archaeological site can be *cleared* — that's the word, cleared, like it's brush — before extraction begins. All they needed was for no one official to ever know it was there.

So they came to clear it.

Okafor got the tablets and Dov into the cave in the first minute. He got me as far as the cave entrance in the second. The bullet caught me going in. He didn't make it to the third minute. Neither did Halloway, or Chen, or the Okonkwos. They burned the records, the photographs, the survey notes — everything that could prove the city existed — and they put what was left of us in a grave by the waterline, and they would have put me in it too, but Okafor had already wedged the cave's rockfall loose so it would come down behind us, and it did, and here I am.

Here I am. With a satchel of stones and a head full of contour lines and a fever that keeps showing me the plaza at dawn, the light coming up the valley and catching the top terrace, and the city looking exactly like what it is: the oldest human thing on this river, and the only witness to what we were before we were who we are.

* * *

When I finished, the cave was quiet except for the river. Felix hadn't moved. I don't know how long he'd been sitting there — long enough that the light through the cave mouth had changed.

"They'll come back for the tablets," he said.

"They'll come back for anything that floats." I laughed, and it turned into a cough. "They think everything's burned. If they knew about the tablets, they'd have dug us out of this cave already."

"Maybe they know more than you think."

"Then we're dead, and the fever doesn't matter." I said it flat, and he didn't argue, which I appreciated. I was starting to understand Felix Ashgrove. He doesn't waste words on things that can't be fixed. He fixes what can be fixed and stands very still in front of what can't, like a man deciding whether a river is fordable.

I made myself sit up. The leg screamed its opinion. I ignored it, the way you learn to, and I reached under the folded shirt that had been my pillow and brought out the notebook — my third one, the one they didn't burn because it never left my body.

"This is the field map," I said. "The real one. Not the copy I filed with Meridian — I filed a false one, old habit, never trust a sponsor. This is the valley, the terraces, the cistern chamber, the river approach, all of it. From memory, since the originals burned. Every line in here is true."

He didn't take it.

That's the thing I'll remember about this moment, I think, when I'm old, if the fever lets me get old. I held out the one true map of the oldest city on the river, and this man — who had run supplies to us for a year, who had come up a dead river on a hunch and a schedule, who was at this moment keeping a fourteen-year-old fugitive and a bleeding stranger alive on his own fuel and his own nerve — looked at the notebook like it was a live coal.

"I'm a supply captain," he said. "I haul diesel and penicillin."

"You hauled a crate upriver that your client wouldn't let you open," I said. "You hauled yourself into a massacre site. You're hauling a stowaway the syndicate would kill on principle." I pushed the notebook at him. "Take it."

"Why me?"

"Because you came." My voice cracked and I hated it, fever or no fever. "Because eleven of us went up that river and the world will never hear a word about ten of them unless someone carries it down. Because you have a boat and a river-reading head and the look of a man who owes the dead something, even if you won't say what." I was shaking now, and not only from the fever. "I can't walk. I can't row. I can't even hold a rifle steady. All I am right now is this map and the mouth that can explain it. So take the map. Please. Before the fever takes it somewhere I can't follow."

He took it.

He held it the way he holds everything — carefully, like it might be the last of its kind. He opened it to the first page and looked at my contour lines for a long moment, and something moved behind his face, some door opening a crack on a room I wasn't shown.

"Forty-one tablets," he said. "Where are the other thirty-three?"

"Hidden. Okafor's idea. He split them before the shooting started — hid most of them in the chamber and re-stacked the rubble. Dov's satchel is the sample. The proof of concept." I managed a smile. "The boy is carrying the argument, Captain. The rest of the evidence is still upriver, waiting for someone to come back with a permit and a press pass and an army of grad students."

"And until then?"

"Until then, we get downstream alive, and the map gets to people who can't be bought." I lay back. The fever was rising again, the tide coming in, and I let it, because I had done the one thing that mattered. The map was out of my hands and into the hands of a man who hums at engines and doesn't waste words, and that felt — I don't have a cartographer's term for it. It felt like north.

He was standing to go, tucking the notebook inside his jacket, when Dov spoke from the back of the cave. I hadn't heard him wake. Cats, I told you.

"Captain," the boy said, and his voice was wrong — flat and fast, the voice of a kid who has spent his whole life learning to read danger. "There's a boat coming up the channel. Two engines. They're not even trying to be quiet."

Felix went still, the way he does. Then he looked at me, and for the first time since I'd met him, he asked a question he didn't already know the answer to.

"How fast can you be moved?"
