## 5. Dov

The captain didn't sleep. I'd figured that out by the second night, watching from the wheelhouse doorway the way you watch anything you're not supposed to want. He sat up with the engine ticking under him like a big slow heart, drinking coffee that had gone the color of the river, and every hour or so he'd stand, look at the water, and sit back down again. Like checking a lock you'd already checked.

I knew a lot about locks. That was the whole problem.

"You're staring," he said, without turning around.

"I'm observing. There's a difference. Staring is rude."

"Observing, then." He took a sip of the dead coffee and made a face like it had personally wronged him. "Find anything?"

"Your port bilge pump sounds wrong. It hiccups every fourth cycle. Also you hold your cup in your left hand but you steer with your right, which means you broke your right wrist once and it never healed right, and also you haven't slept in—"

"That's enough observing."

Ilsa was sleeping in the cabin he'd given up for her, which meant the whole boat had rearranged itself around her fever the way water rearranges around a rock. Her fever had broken that morning — I'd heard him say it to her, quietly, like it was a secret from the river — but she was still sleeping eighteen hours a day, and the satchel sat under my bunk like a sleeping dog nobody wanted to wake.

Fourteen stone tablets. I'd counted them so many times I could count them in my sleep, and I did, some nights, when the dreams about Marrow got loud. Fourteen slabs of gray stone with lines cut into them older than anything, older than the country, older than the idea of a country, and I was fourteen years old, which felt like the universe making a joke at my expense.

The captain turned around in his chair. "Dov."

"Yeah."

"You want to tell me why a kid who's been on this boat four days knows what a bilge pump hiccup sounds like?"

"Because I listen," I said. "It's the thing I'm good at. Listening. Hiding. Memorizing." I shrugged, which is a thing I'd practiced in the mirror of a detention center washroom until it looked like it cost me nothing. "I had a lot of time to get good at things."

He looked at me for a long moment. He had a way of looking at you like he was reading water — like there was something under the surface and he was waiting to see how it moved before he put his hand in.

"Detention center," he said.

It wasn't a question, which meant Ilsa had told him, which meant she'd been awake longer than she'd let on, which meant I owed her a conversation about professional courtesy. Or maybe the captain had just done the math. Fourteen years old, alone, no school records, no family coming to collect him, flinched when a door slammed. People like me are easy arithmetic.

"Three years," I said. "Since I was eleven. It wasn't — I didn't do anything. My mother went into the hospital and there wasn't anybody, so the state said it would take care of me, and the state's idea of taking care of is a building with bars on the windows and a man named Hult who took your shoes at night so you couldn't run." I kept my voice flat. Flat was a skill too. "When the expedition came through provisioning at the river town, they needed a runner. Somebody small to carry crates through the market. I carried the crates and then I didn't get off the boat."

"You stowed away on an archaeological expedition."

"Nobody checks the hold of a supply skiff. Everybody checks a boy's papers." I looked at him. "You checked, though. First day. You went through my bag while I was getting Ilsa water."

"I did," he said, not even sorry about it.

"Did you find them?"

"No."

"Because I'm good," I said. "I hid them in the flour tin. Nobody searches flour. Hult taught me that — he searched everything except what he thought was beneath him. That's the trick with people like that. They've already decided what's beneath them, and you just have to be it."

Something moved across the captain's face — not quite a smile, more like the shadow of one, gone before it committed to anything. "You're fourteen."

"Fifteen in March."

"You're fifteen in March and you're talking like a career criminal."

"I'm talking like a survivor. There's a difference." I heard myself and winced, because that was Ilsa's sentence, one of hers, the way she talked when the fever was highest and she was explaining the tablets to me over and over so I'd have them memorized too, in case. I'd been carrying other people's sentences around for years. It's what happens when nobody asks you for your own.

* * *

He came to find me the next afternoon, which surprised me, because captains don't come to find you. Captains stay in the wheelhouse and let the boat do the talking.

I was sitting on the cargo deck with my legs through the rail, watching the banks go by. The river up here ran green-brown and slow, all its violence stored up for the rapids downstream, and the banks were thick with trees that leaned over the water like they were eavesdropping.

"Lesson one," the captain said, sitting down next to me without asking, which I decided not to hold against him. He nodded at the river. "Tell me what you see."

"Water."

"Try again."

"Green water. Slow water. Trees. A heron. Sandbar on the left, maybe."

"Maybe?"

"Maybe," I said, stubborn. "It's a sandbar or it isn't."

"Nothing on a river is a maybe." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Look at the line where the current slides off the point. See how it bends? If that bar were deep, the water would run straight over it, flat and dull. It bends because the water's arguing with something shallow. The river tells you everything. You just have to listen with your eyes."

"Listen with my eyes," I repeated. "That's the worst sentence I've ever heard."

"And yet." He pointed. "The bend in the current, the color change — darker means deeper, lighter means something's under it. The V of a rock shows itself as a seam of white. A snag shows itself as a smooth tongue of water sliding over it, like it's trying to keep it secret. And where the water looks calmest, right against the bank—" he flicked a pebble out over the rail, and it landed in water that looked like glass, and something down there went *thunk* — "that's where the deadheads live. Logs that sank years ago, standing on end, waiting for a boat like ours to come along and introduce itself."

I looked at the calm water for a while. It looked back, innocent as a liar.

"Why are you teaching me this?" I asked. "I'm cargo. Cargo doesn't get lessons."

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wasn't going to answer. Downriver somewhere, a bird made a sound like a rusty hinge opening.

"Because in about two days," he said finally, "we're going to be running water that doesn't forgive mistakes, and I can only steer one thing at a time. If something happens to me — if I go over the side, if I'm knocked out, if I'm busy with a line — somebody on this boat needs to be able to look at that river and know what it's saying." He glanced at me. "Ilsa can't stand up for ten minutes without the deck tilting. That leaves you."

"That leaves me," I said. My chest did something complicated. I filed it under *anger*, because that was the safe drawer.

"You don't have to like it."

"I don't have to like anything. That's the whole point of me." But I leaned forward anyway. "Show me the seam again. The rock one."

* * *

We did it every afternoon after that. He never called it a lesson, and I never thanked him, and we both understood this was the correct arrangement. He'd point at the water and I'd tell it back to him — the arguing current, the lying calm, the dark tongue of a deep channel sliding between the bars — and when I got it right he'd say nothing at all, and when I got it wrong he'd say *again*, and somehow *again* started to sound less like a failure and more like a door that stayed open.

It was on the fourth afternoon, with the sun going orange and the banks narrowing, that I told him about Marrow.

I hadn't planned to. But he'd asked me to read a stretch of water coming into a bend, and I'd read it right, and something about getting it right loosened the bolt.

"There's a man," I said. "Big. Gray coat. Voice like gravel settling. He came to the camp two weeks before everything went wrong. Said he was with a survey company doing mineral assessments. Ilsa didn't like him. Professor Anselm didn't like him. But the sponsor's man vouched for him, so he stayed."

The captain's hands went still on the wheel.

"He searched the camp. Officially it was a 'safety inspection.' Unofficially he went through everything. Tents, crates, the professor's notes. And one night he came to the skiff where I was sleeping and he searched me." I kept my eyes on the water, because it was easier than the captain's face. "Went through my bag. Made me turn out my pockets. I'd moved the tablets by then — Ilsa made me, the day he arrived, she took one look at him and started moving things — so he didn't find them. But he found the *string*."

"String?"

"I'd been wrapping them. Cloth and cord, for carrying. I'd missed one piece of cord. Just a loop of it, in my pocket, and he held it up and looked at it, and he didn't say anything, he just — smiled. Like he'd been handed a receipt." I swallowed. "He knows something was found. He doesn't know what, exactly, or how many, or that a kid has them. But he knows the expedition found something worth wrapping. And men like that don't stop looking until the receipt is paid."

The captain was silent for the length of a whole bend in the river. Then he said, in a voice I'd only heard him use once before, talking about the lockmaster: "What's his name?"

"Marrow."

"Marrow," he repeated, like he was setting the word down somewhere he could find it again.

"He's the one who shot Ilsa."

"I know."

"He's the one who burned the records. I watched from the tree line. He stood there while they burned and he checked his watch, like it was an appointment." My voice cracked on the last word, which infuriated me, and I stared harder at the water. "He's going to come for the tablets. He's going to come for us. And I'm the one who left the cord."

"Hey." The captain's voice changed — not soft, exactly, because I don't think soft was in his inventory, but lower. Steadier. "You hid fourteen stone tablets from a professional searcher when you were fourteen years old. The cord was one loop. You did the impossible part."

"The impossible part doesn't matter if the possible part kills us."

"It matters," he said, "because it means you're the reason any of it survived. The professor's dead. The records are ash. Everything that city was is sitting in a flour tin under your bunk, and it's sitting there because of you." He looked at me then, straight on, and it was worse than Marrow's smile and better than anything, because Marrow had looked at me like a thing to be searched and this man looked at me like a person who'd *done* something. "So we're going to get it downriver. All of it. You, the tablets, Ilsa, the truth — all of it goes home."

"We," I said. "You keep saying we."

"You're crew," he said, like it was obvious, like it had been obvious for days and I was the last one informed. "Crew is we."

I turned back to the river fast, because my eyes were doing something I didn't authorize, and there, right where he'd want me to look, the current bent around the point in a long clean arc, arguing with the shallows, telling anybody who knew how to listen exactly where the deep water ran.

"Sandbar on the left," I said. "Deep channel hugging the right bank. And there's a deadhead off the point — see the smooth water? It's trying to keep it secret."

The captain looked at where I was looking. And this time, when the shadow of the smile crossed his face, it stayed.

"Lesson two," he said. "Tomorrow I'll teach you the sound the engine makes right before the pump gives out. And then you'll know this boat better than I do, and I'll finally be able to sleep."

He didn't sleep that night either. I heard him up past midnight, and I heard something else too — the far-off, wrong-sounding cough of an engine that wasn't ours, somewhere upstream, running without lights.

I sat up in the dark with my hand on the flour tin and listened with my eyes, and my ears, and every skill I had.

Somebody was coming down the river.
