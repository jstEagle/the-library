## 6. Downstream of Trouble

The lock at Verrada sat in the river like a rotten tooth — concrete gone the color of old bone, weeds shouldering up through the expansion joints, a chain-link fence that had once meant something to somebody. I'd locked through it maybe forty times over seven years, and every one of those times the lockmaster had been a heavy, sweating man named Osgood who waved you through with the enthusiasm of a man waving through traffic he resented.

This time he was standing on the catwalk above the gates, and he wasn't sweating.

"Felix Ashgrove," he called down, before I'd even killed the engines. "Rivergate. That right?"

"It's on the hull," I said. "Twice."

"Need to see papers."

I'd never been asked for papers at Verrada in my life. The lock took a fee in cash and a wave, same as it had since before I owned a boat. I looked up at him and did the arithmetic that rivermen do without knowing they're doing it: new shirt, clean shave, a radio handset clipped to his belt that hadn't been there in June. Somebody had bought Osgood recently, and bought him whole.

"Since when do you need papers?"

"Since now." He said it flat, no apology in it, which told me more than an apology would have. A man embarrassed by his own corruption hedges. A man who's decided he's a different kind of man now just states things. "Syndicate directive. All vessels transiting southbound get inspected for archaeological materials."

"Archaeological materials," I repeated. "On a fuel barge."

"Inspection takes an hour."

An hour. Long enough for whoever Osgood had sold us to arrive. I looked past him, down the long straight reach below the lock where the channel ran wide and brown and empty toward the lowlands, and I noticed what my eyes had been trying to tell me for a full minute: there was a boat tied up at the downstream mooring buoy. White hull, twin outboards, the kind of clean that doesn't come from work.

Marrow's people. Or Marrow himself. Either way, an hour was a gift I couldn't afford.

"Osgood," I said, and I kept my voice friendly, because there were probably binoculars on me from that white boat, "you know what happens to lockmasters when the water drops? They become very expensive men to bribe and very cheap men to replace." I let that sit. "I'm going to turn around now, go back upstream, and take the Serrano cut. You can tell your new friends whatever gets you through the night."

His face did something complicated. Fear, mostly, but under it — and this surprised me — something like relief. A man who'd been waiting seven years for someone to give him permission to not be the worst version of himself.

"Ashgrove." He lowered his voice, leaned over the rail. "Serrano's at eleven-two and falling. You want my advice—"

"I don't."

"—you run it tonight or not at all. By Thursday she'll be under nine. Nobody runs Serrano under nine."

I threw him a salute I didn't mean and swung the Rivergate's bow upstream, and behind me I felt his relief like heat off a stove, and I hated him a little for making me the hero of his private redemption, and I understood him completely, and both of those things made me tired.

* * *

Ilsa found me at the chart table an hour later, back at the mouth of the Serrano feeder, where I'd anchored in the shadow of a bluff to think — or to fail to think, which is most of what thinking is.

"You're going to run it," she said. Not a question. She moved carefully these days, favoring the shoulder, but her eyes worked fine, and they went straight to the chart spread open on the table — the chart with the great blank space where Serrano should have been, annotated only with a surveyor's note from 1931: *impassable at all stages; three lives lost.*

"The syndicate knows we're coming," I said. "They've got the lower channel. The bypass Ilsa—" I stopped, corrected myself. "The route the old pilot described adds two days. We don't have two days. Marrow will have the trading post burned and the bypass watched by tomorrow noon."

"So it's the rapids."

"So it's the rapids. At night. At eleven-two feet and falling." I tapped the blank space on the chart. "There's no survey. There's no map. There's a dead man's note saying don't. And the water is leaving."

Dov appeared in the doorway behind her, the way he did — silently, suddenly, like a cat deciding whether you're worth the trouble. He looked at the chart, then at me.

"How fast does the water leave?" he asked.

"Six inches a day, maybe more with no rain. Which means the rocks that are dangerous tonight are lethal by Friday, and the ones that are merely annoying tonight didn't exist last week." I closed the chart. "The river isn't a thing, Dov. It's a negotiation. And Serrano is a negotiation where the other party changes its mind hourly."

"Then we go tonight," Ilsa said.

I wanted to argue. I want you to understand that — I sat there with every argument lined up like barges waiting to lock through: too dark, too low, too little known, too much cargo, a wounded woman, a boy who shouldn't be aboard at all. Seven years of Felix Ashgrove would have laid those arguments out neatly and then turned the boat around and delivered his fuel and gone back to measuring his life in miles.

But seven years of Felix Ashgrove had also gotten exactly nowhere, and somewhere between the burned camp and the cave and the satchel of stone tablets sleeping under my bunk, I had apparently become a man who ran rapids at night for people.

"Tonight," I agreed. "And here's how it works. Dov — searchlight. You'll stand at the bow and put the beam where I tell you, when I tell you, and nothing else matters except that. Not what you see coming at us. Not the noise. The light goes where I say."

He nodded, and there was something in his face that wasn't fear, exactly. It was the look of a boy who had spent his whole life being handled and had just been handed something.

"Ilsa — you're on the depth sounder and the stopwatch. I'll call marks, you call depths. If the numbers go wrong, you say so loud, and nobody argues with anybody until we're through the drop."

"And you?" she said.

"Me, I read water." I almost smiled. "By ear, if I have to."

* * *

We entered the Serrano cut at 9:40 p.m., with the moon down and the sky doing that thick tropical black that swallows a searchlight beam at forty yards. The Rivergate came off plane and settled into displacement speed, and the sound changed — that's the first thing about running rapids that nobody tells you. It's not a visual problem first. It's an auditory one. Smooth water whispers. Broken water argues. And water going over a ledge makes a sound like the world clearing its throat before it says something terrible.

I stood at the wheel with the engine telegraphs in my hands and listened.

"Light left," I said. "Hold on the wall."

Dov's beam found the canyon wall sliding past, close enough to touch, wet stone shining like the flank of some animal. Good. Wall on the left meant the main channel hugged it — the 1931 note had said as much, in the way dead men say things.

"Depth," I called.

"Fourteen feet," Ilsa answered, steady as a metronome. "Thirteen. Twelve-six."

"Coming to something," Dov said, and his voice cracked only a little.

"I know."

I knew because I could hear it — a long, low argument ahead, water fighting itself, the sound of a shoal building across the channel. I brought the port engine back and swung the bow right, away from the wall, against everything the note had said, because the note was ninety years old and the river had renegotiated.

"Depths dropping fast," Ilsa warned.

"They're supposed to. Ride it out." I could feel the bottom through the hull now, that faint shudder of water compressed between steel and stone, the river telling me exactly how much room it was willing to sell me and at what price. "Light right. Find me the far bank."

The beam swept, caught white foam standing up like smoke, then caught the far shore — gravel, low, maybe sixty yards of channel between us and it. Sixty yards of channel with something broken in the middle of it.

"Hard right," I said, and put both engines into it.

The Rivergate heeled, cargo shifting below with a groan, and we threaded the gap between the shoal and the bank with maybe a boat-length to spare on either side, and the sound of the broken water went from an argument to a roar to — nothing. Deep water again. Quiet enough to hear Dov breathing at the bow.

"That," he called back, and his voice had a grin in it whether he wanted it to or not, "was awful."

"That was the easy part," I said, and I wasn't lying to toughen him up. The easy part is always the part where you can still see.

* * *

The bad water came at midnight, the way bad water does.

It announced itself as a change in the air pressure more than a sound — a deep, rhythmic compression, like the canyon itself breathing — and then the light found it and even Ilsa, fever-hardened and shot-through and furious at the world, said a word in a language I didn't speak and didn't need translated.

The river dropped. That was the whole horror of it. Ahead, the channel simply ended, poured itself over a granite sill into a churning pit eighty yards downstream, and the only way through was a slot on the far side where the water folded back on itself in a standing wave — runnable, barely, at high water, at daylight, by someone who'd done it before. We had none of those things.

"Stop the boat," Ilsa said.

"If I stop, I lose steerage, and if I lose steerage, the current decides."

"Then don't stop. But Felix—" She turned in the chair, and the searchlight caught half her face, and I saw that she wasn't afraid for herself. "If we hit the sill wrong, Dov needs a line on. Now. Before, not after."

So that's what we did, in the last hundred yards of calm water we would see for an hour: I talked Dov into a life vest and a tether clipped to the jackstaff, and I talked Ilsa into lying flat on the deck plates with her bad shoulder braced, and I talked myself into believing that seven years of running safe water had left anything in me worth betting three lives on.

Then I put the wheels over and drove at the slot.

The world became noise. The bow climbed, hung — I remember the hang, that weightless moment when the propellers sing free and you know the next second belongs entirely to the river — and then we were down the other side of the sill with the hull slamming and green water coming over the foredeck in sheets, and Dov's light was whipping around the sky like a lighthouse in an earthquake, and I was shouting "Left! LEFT!" into a roar that ate words whole.

The light found the wall. I steered for it. Something struck the hull aft — a rock, a log, the river reaching up to take its fee — and the starboard engine note changed, went ragged, went wrong, and the Rivergate began to drift sideways down the chute with one good screw and a rudder biting water that didn't want to be bitten.

"Starboard's hurt," I shouted to Ilsa. "Soundings!"

"Eleven! Ten! Nine-nine—"

Nine-nine. Under nine, Osgood had said, nobody runs Serrano. The river was giving us its last inch and asking for a decision.

I gave it one. Port engine hard ahead, starboard in reverse to swing the stern, using the damage instead of fighting it — letting the crippled side fall away and the good side drive us through the last quarter-mile of broken water like a man walking on a broken leg, ugly and off-balance and moving.

And then, all at once, quiet. The canyon opened, the water smoothed, the stars came back out as if they'd been waiting politely behind a curtain. The Rivergate floated in the lower pool, listing slightly to starboard, taking water somewhere aft, alive.

Dov unclipped his tether with shaking hands. Ilsa sat up slowly, holding her shoulder, and looked at me across the wheelhouse with an expression I couldn't read in the dark and didn't try to.

"We're through," Dov said. "We're actually through."

"We're through," I agreed, "and sinking. Get the pump. Both of you — pump first, questions after."

Because that was the other thing about Serrano, the thing the 1931 note hadn't mentioned: the river doesn't charge you when you enter. It charges you when you leave. And as I knelt on the deck plates with a flashlight in my teeth, tracing the crack the rock had opened along a seam aft, listening to the water come in faster than any honest boat should leak, I heard it.

Engines. Far upstream, back at the head of the rapids — more than one, big ones, idling.

Marrow hadn't sent anyone around through the night. He'd sent them to the top of the Serrano cut, to wait for the sound of a boat coming through, and to follow the damage downstream.

The river had charged us our fee. Now it was going to collect the rest.
