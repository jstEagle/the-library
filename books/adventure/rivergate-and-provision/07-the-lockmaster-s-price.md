## 7. The Lockmaster's Price

Fever is a strange cartographer. It draws you maps of places that don't exist, then erases the ones that do. For three days after the camp — after the grave and the burned records and the boy with the satchel pressed to his chest like a second heartbeat — I drifted in and out on a tide of Felix's cheap aspirin and river water boiled in a dented kettle. When I surfaced, the Rivergate was moving. When I sank, I dreamed of terraces.

Not nightmares. That was the strange part. I'd expected nightmares — Marrow's men, the crack of the rifle, Tomás folding into the mud with his hand still reaching for his notebook. Instead my sleeping mind kept returning to the city itself. The morning light coming down the gorge in a long gold blade and striking the first terrace, and every one of us standing there silent, even Dr. Voss, who had never once in eleven years been silent about anything. The stone steps rising out of the jungle like the spine of something enormous that had lain down to sleep and not yet decided to wake.

We had stood there for maybe four minutes without speaking. I timed it afterward because timing things was my job, and because I couldn't bear not to know exactly how much wonder we'd been given before it was taken away.

* * *

I woke properly on the fourth day, to the smell of engine oil and the sound of an argument conducted at the volume of two people trying very hard not to argue.

"—saying we can't just knock on the door of every retired pilot on this stretch," Felix was saying. "Half of them will sell us to Marrow for the price of a case of beer."

"And the other half?"

"The other half are dead."

I pulled myself up on one elbow in the wheelhouse bunk where someone — Dov, judging by the careful clumsiness of the blanket tuck — had installed me. Through the open window I could see the river running wide and brown-gold, and on the left bank, set back among fig trees, a house on stilts with a signal mast beside it, flying no flag at all. A man sat on the porch in a chair that had clearly been chosen decades ago and defended ever since.

"Who's that?" I asked.

Both of them jumped. Felix recovered first, which annoyed me more than the jumping did.

"Emmerich Vane," he said. "Retired pilot. Ran the Serrano for thirty-one years before the company built the locks downstream and put him out of business. He knows a bypass around the lower channel."

"Then we're knocking on his door."

"We're *considering* knocking on his door."

"Felix." I swung my legs down and immediately regretted the swing; the world tilted politely and righted itself. "The lockmaster at Corrientes Bend has been paid. You told me. Which means Marrow owns the only legal route south. Every hour we sit here deciding whether to be polite is an hour his boats get closer. Knock on the door."

Dov appeared in the doorway with a coil of rope over his shoulder like a bandolier, wearing the expression of a boy who had recently discovered that adults were optional. "I already knocked," he said. "He says if the captain wants to talk, he can come say so himself, like a person."

Felix closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when he realizes the crew has developed opinions. "Fine," he said. "But I do the talking."

"You'll do fine," I said. "You talk to the river all day. One old man is easier than water."

"That," said Felix, "is demonstrably untrue."

* * *

Vane received us on his porch with coffee that tasted of tar and history, poured from a pot older than any of us. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a bollard, with hands so scarred they looked braided. He listened to Felix's offer — money, real money, for the location of the bypass and whatever advice would keep a loaded boat off the rocks above it — with the expression of a man listening to a weather report for a country he no longer lived in.

"No," he said finally.

"It's a fair price."

"It's a fair price for fuel or flour. You're asking me for the Serrano bypass. Thirty-one years I ran that water. Lost two friends to it and one marriage, though the marriage was probably going anyway. You think I keep that knowledge in a drawer to hand over for cash?" He sipped his coffee. "Cash spends. Knowledge about that river is a debt. You don't pay debts with money."

Felix looked at me. It was the first time since I'd met him that he'd looked at anyone for help, and I understood what it cost him, and I loved him a little for it, in the way you love a locked door that has just rattled.

"What would you take, then?" I asked.

Vane turned to me properly for the first time. His eyes were pale river-gray and entirely unfooled. "You're the one from the expedition," he said. "The one that went quiet. Radio stopped three weeks back, and everyone on this stretch assumed fever, or pirates, or the usual. But you didn't look surprised when your captain mentioned trouble. You looked *guilty*. So." He set down his cup. "What did you find up there? And don't tell me pottery shards. Nobody burns records over pottery shards."

The fever had left a tremor in my hands. I watched it as I wrapped them around the cup, and thought: here it is again, the choice. Tell or don't tell. I had been telling no one for weeks — telling Voss's widow nothing yet, telling the sponsors' lawyers everything and hating it, telling myself the truth was safer in my skull than on paper. And look where silence had gotten us. Tomás in the ground. The records ash. Marrow's boats behind us like a closing fist.

"They found a city," I said.

Vane's eyebrows rose a millimeter. In him, that was a shout.

"A terraced city, in the gorge above the Serrano headwaters. Older than anything in the national museum by a thousand years, minimum. Stone causeways, aqueducts still carrying water, a plaza big enough that our survey instruments couldn't find its far edge through the trees. A civilization nobody has ever written down. Not pre-colonial — *pre-everything*. And the consortium that holds the mining concession up there—" my voice caught on the tremor and I pushed through it, "—the concession is only legal if the land is classified as empty. No significant historical sites. So they sent men to make sure it stayed empty. They burned our records. They killed our director. They are going to strip-mine the valley, and when they're done there will be no proof it was ever anything but rock."

The porch was very quiet. Somewhere below the stilts, river water slapped the pilings in a slow rhythm, like something patient counting.

"I have the map," I said. "In my head, mostly, because the paper copy burned. I've been redrawing it from memory, mile by mile, terrace by terrace. And I am offering it to you — not selling it, offering it — because you asked what we found, and because I'm done letting the answer die with the people who found it."

Vane looked at me for a long time. Then he looked past me, at the river, and something in his face moved the way light moves on water when a cloud goes over.

"My grandfather," he said, "used to talk about stairs in the jungle. Old man's stories — steps cut in stone going up the gorge, way above any high-water mark. Everyone laughed at him. Said he'd been drinking the cargo." He laughed shortly, without humor. "He died thinking he'd seen a ghost. You're telling me he saw a civilization."

"He saw the top of one."

Vane nodded slowly. Then he stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and went inside, and came back with a roll of oilcloth tied with wire. He laid it on the table between the coffee cups and unrolled it: a hand-drawn chart, ink faded to the color of weak tea, showing the lower channel and — branching off above Corrientes Bend — a thread of blue no official map had ever admitted to. The Willow Cut. Backwater, side-channel, a twisting fifteen miles of drowned forest and sandbar that rejoined the main stem below the locks.

"Nobody runs it anymore," Vane said. "Too shallow for the barges, too twisty for anyone in a hurry. But your boat's not a barge, and if you're in a hurry, shallow and twisty beats blocked and searched." He tapped the chart. "Three things. One: enter at the old ferry stake on the north bank — miss it and you're on the Gravel Teeth inside a minute. Two: the middle six miles flood at dawn and drop by noon; run it mid-morning or don't run it. Three—" he fixed Felix with those gray eyes, "—whoever's on your bow sounding depth better be somebody you trust with your life, because that's exactly what they'll be holding."

"Dov," said Felix, before he seemed to decide to say it. Then, quieter, almost to himself: "Dov."

Vane rolled the chart and pushed it across the table. He refused the money twice, and the third time Felix stopped offering, which I suspect was the point.

"For my grandfather," Vane said simply. "Tell the world about the stairs."

* * *

We were casting off when Dov came up from the bow at a run, and I knew before he spoke, because the smoke was already visible past the fig trees — a black column standing up straight in the windless afternoon, the way smoke does when it has plenty to eat.

"The trading post," Dov said. "Marta's post. It's burning."

Felix ran the Rivergate in close enough to see, and none of us spoke for a long while. Marta's post had sold us diesel and eggs and, on my first lucid evening, a jar of honey that Dov had eaten half of with his fingers. Now its roof was a skeleton and its dock was char, and nailed to the last upright piling was a sheet of tin, and painted on the tin in letters you could read from the water:

**NEXT TIME IT'S THE BOAT.**

"Marrow," Felix said. Not a question.

"Marrow," I agreed. My hands had stopped trembling, I noticed. The fever was gone and what had replaced it was colder and steadier and I suspected permanent. "He's telling us he doesn't need the lock. He's telling us he knows about the bypass, or thinks he does, and he'll burn everything ahead of us until we stop."

"He burned a woman's livelihood," Dov said. His voice was flat in a way fourteen-year-olds should never learn. "For a *message*."

"Yes," I said.

Below us, the river went on sliding past, brown and gold and indifferent, carrying its secrets the way it always had. I had spent my whole career believing that institutions worked — that you reported, you filed, you published, and the machinery of law ground slowly but it ground. I had believed it right up until the machinery's representatives put a bullet in Tomás and a match to our records.

There was no institution coming for Marrow. There was only us, and a backwater channel, and a boy on the bow with a sounding pole, and a captain who talked to rivers, and a truth that would burn as easily as a trading post unless somebody carried it fast enough.

"Run the Cut tomorrow, mid-morning," I said. "And Felix — when we're through, we don't go quiet. We go loud."

Felix looked at the burning post a moment longer. Then he rang the bell for departure, and the sound went out over the water like a promise.

"Mid-morning," he said. "And Dov — get your pole. You're about to learn why old pilots die stubborn."
