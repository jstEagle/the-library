## 8. Serrano at Night

The Serrano Rapids announce themselves an hour before you see them, the way a fight announces itself in a bar — a low grumble under everything else, a sound like the river clearing its throat. I'd heard them twice in my life, both times from a safe distance, both times with the firm intention of never hearing them closer.

Now I was steering toward them on purpose, at night, because the alternative was a lockmaster named Peralta who'd sold his soul for a syndicate pension and a channel that would have us in irons by dawn.

"Water's down to what?" I asked.

Ilsa stood at my shoulder, one hand braced on the wheel housing, the other holding her field map against the binnacle light. The fever had broken that afternoon but it had taken something out of her; she looked like a woman who'd been wrung out and hung back up slightly wrong. Her voice was steady, though. Steadier than mine felt.

"Meter and a half below seasonal mean. Maybe more." She traced a line on the map with her finger. "The survey team sounded the main channel in June. Left-hand side past the first island, then hard right at the split rock. But that was June."

"June was another country," I said. "June had water in it."

Behind us, Dov climbed into the bow with the searchlight's control cable coiled over his shoulder like a pet snake. The boy had spent two days learning the lamp — its quirks, its temper, the way the filament dimmed when the generator lagged — and he'd done it the way he did everything: silently, completely, as if his life depended on mastery. Which, I suppose, it did.

"Ready when you are, Captain," he called back.

"Don't call me Captain. It makes me feel responsible."

"You *are* responsible."

"That's what makes me feel it."

Ilsa almost smiled. Almost. Then the grumble ahead deepened into something with teeth in it, and we all shut up and became professionals.

Here is what nobody tells you about running rapids at night: it isn't bravery. Bravery is a story you tell afterward, sitting somewhere dry, with a drink in your hand and your pulse back down around ninety. In the moment it's just arithmetic performed very fast by a terrified man. Depth against draft. Current against power. The width of a gap against the width of your hull. You do the sums and you do them again and you keep doing them until either the numbers work or the river decides they don't.

I throttled down to half and let the current take us in.

* * *

The first mile was almost polite. Water sliding over shelves of rock, the Rivergate lifting her bow and settling, lifting and settling, like a horse testing unfamiliar ground. Dov's searchlight swept the channel ahead in long arcs, and each time the beam crossed white water I read it — the color telling me depth, the texture telling me speed, the shape of the foam telling me where the rock sat underneath, patient and permanent and entirely indifferent to our plans.

"Split rock coming," Ilsa said. "Half a mile. Hard right after it."

"I remember."

"You said that about the last bend too."

"The last bend forgave me. This one might not."

The searchlight found the split rock and held it: a fin of black granite rising out of the channel like the dorsal ridge of some drowned animal, the river tearing itself around it in two ragged streams. The right-hand gap was wider. The right-hand gap was also, according to every instinct I owned, hiding something.

"Left," I said.

"Felix—"

"Left. Look at the foam on the right. It's folding back on itself. There's a reversal in there big enough to swallow us."

She didn't argue. That was the thing about Ilsa — she'd hand you her map, her trust, her life, and then she'd let you carry them without checking your pockets every ten seconds. She turned the page and said, "Left channel narrows to maybe eight meters past the rock. After that it opens again."

"Eight meters is plenty."

"For what?"

"For a boat that's exactly seven meters wide if you include my mistakes."

Dov laughed from the bow — a short, startled bark of a laugh, the kind you can't help — and then caught himself, because laughing in rapids feels like tempting something. But I filed the sound away. Seven years I'd been measuring my life in miles, in contracts, in the clean arithmetic of distance between me and everything that could hurt. And here was a kid laughing at my joke in the teeth of the Serrano, and I found I wanted to keep the sound.

We went left. The rock slid past close enough that I could have reached out and touched it, could have chipped off a souvenir of my own stupidity. The hull shuddered once as the current grabbed the stern and tried to swing us broadside, and I gave it full throttle and the old girl dug in and answered, because she always answered, because she was the one thing in my life that had never lied to me.

Then the light died.

* * *

No drama to it. No explosion, no smoke. The filament just went from white to orange to nothing, and the night came in through the windows like floodwater.

"Dov?"

"Generator's lagging!" His voice came thin over the engine noise. "It did this before — give it thirty seconds—"

"We don't have thirty seconds." I killed the wheel lights so my eyes could start their slow, useless adjustment. Ahead there was nothing. Behind there was less. The river made its sounds in the dark — that low continuous roar, and under it, sharper, the individual voices of the water. A hiss like sand poured on sand: shallow gravel bar, port side. A deeper thrumming, rhythmic: standing wave, dead ahead, big one. And somewhere to starboard, a hollow booming, regular as a drumbeat, which meant water hitting the inside of a rock cavity, which meant rock, close, waiting.

Seven years of reading this river by daylight, and now the river was going to make me read it blind, by ear alone, the way my brother used to say real pilots did. Tomas. Who'd been dead seven years because I'd misread a crossing current in full sun with every instrument working. If I'd been half the pilot he thought I was—

*Stop.* I put him away. Not gone. Just away, in the pocket where I kept him, where he'd ride along quiet until the water gave me room to grieve properly.

"Felix." Ilsa's voice, right at my ear. "Tell me what you hear. I'll hold the map in my head. We'll do it together."

And that — that simple sentence, *we'll do it together* — hit me harder than any wave had. Because I'd run this river alone for seven years. Alone was safe. Alone meant no one to drown, no one to bury, no one whose face I'd have to live inside of forever. Alone was the whole architecture of my life, load-bearing wall and all, and this woman with a bullet scar and a head full of maps had just proposed to knock it down in the middle of the worst water in three hundred kilometers.

"Hissing gravel, port side, keep us off it," I said. My voice came out level. Some part of me was proud of that. "Deep thrum dead ahead — standing wave, we'll punch through it. Booming to starboard means rock, stay center-left. Talk to me, Ilsa. Every hundred meters, talk to me."

"Center-left. Hundred meters."

The Rivergate climbed the standing wave and I felt her whole hull leave the water for one endless half-second, Dov's weight shifting in the bow, Ilsa's hand locking on my shoulder, and then we came down and the impact went through my spine and out through my boots and the boat kept going, kept answering, kept being the truest thing I knew.

"Gravel's louder," I said. "Fall off to starboard a touch. Not much. The boom's still out there."

"Starboard a touch. Felix, the narrows — the June chart has a bend after the wave, ninety degrees, cliff on the outside—"

"I hear the cliff."

"How do you hear a cliff?"

"It stops echoing. Sound goes into it and doesn't come back. Dead air on the right, maybe forty meters."

"Forty meters," she repeated, and I could hear her translating it into map-space, laying my ears over her memory. "Then the channel turns left. Trust the turn, Felix. Don't fight it."

Don't fight it. Seven years of fighting everything — the grief, the current, the whole idea that a man could belong to people instead of distances — and here was a woman telling me the way through was surrender. Turn with the river. Let it carry you where it's going anyway.

"Generator!" Dov shouted, and the searchlight stuttered back to life, threw one wild arc across the black water — and lit the cliff.

Forty meters. Maybe thirty-five. White water exploding against its base, spray hanging in the beam like a curtain of glass beads, and beyond it, exactly where Ilsa's memory said it would be, the channel bending away to the left, open, breathing, alive.

I turned with the river. All of it — the wheel, the throttle, the seven years, all of it surrendered into the turn — and the Rivergate swung her stern away from the stone and ran the bend clean, and for one moment, one whole shining moment, there was nothing in the world but the four of us: me, the woman, the boy, the boat, moving together through the dark like a single animal that had finally remembered how its legs worked.

The roar began to fall behind us. The water smoothed. Somewhere in the bow, Dov started to laugh again, and this time he didn't catch himself, and this time Ilsa laughed too, a real one, cracked and disbelieving, and I realized my own face hurt and discovered I was smiling, actually smiling, the muscles rusty and astonished at being asked to work.

"Again tomorrow?" Dov called.

"If you ever touch that generator again, we walk."

But I said it soft, and he knew, and I knew he knew.

* * *

We anchored at dawn in a backwater below the rapids, willows trailing in the current, mist coming off the water like breath. Ilsa slept in the pilothouse chair. Dov slept wherever boys sleep, which is anywhere, instantly, mid-sentence.

I stood at the rail with coffee I wasn't drinking and did the thing I hadn't let myself do in seven years. I took Tomas out of the pocket. Not the accident — I'd carried that part long enough, and it suddenly seemed lighter than it used to, worn smooth like river stone. Just him. His laugh. The way he'd stand beside me at a wheel like this one and say, *you don't steer a river, little brother, you negotiate with it.*

"You'd have liked them," I told the mist. "The map woman. The kid. You'd have liked them a lot, and you'd have been insufferable about it."

The mist didn't answer. It didn't need to. For the first time since the accident, the silence where his voice used to be felt less like a wound and more like company.

Downstream, maybe six hours at cruising speed, the channel narrowed toward the last confluence — the place where the Serrano branch met the main stem, the only road home, the one piece of ground Marrow and his syndicate money couldn't afford to leave unguarded. I'd been telling myself we'd slip past in the dark, quiet as silt. Standing there in the willow-smell and the morning steam, watching the river run easy and green toward whatever waited downstream, I finally admitted what I'd known since the split rock:

Marrow wouldn't be guarding the confluence.

He'd be waiting at it. With his boat, his men, and an offer he thought I couldn't refuse.

I finished the coffee after all. It had gone cold, but cold coffee is honest coffee, and I figured a man about to choose his family over his freedom ought to start the day with something honest.

I rang the engine bell twice — wake-up, get moving — and turned the Rivergate's bow downstream.
