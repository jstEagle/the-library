## 9. Marrow's Bargain

The last confluence announces itself the way bad news does — quietly at first, then all at once. The Serrano spits us out of its gorge in a long brown exhale, and there ahead is the wide meeting of waters: our river sliding in from the north, the main channel sweeping up from the south, and between them a fan of shallows where the two currents argue and never settle. Every pilot on this stretch knows it as Three Fingers. Nobody calls it that politely.

I've been running rivers for twenty years, and I can count on one hand the times I've come around a bend and known, before I saw anything, that I was expected. This was one of them.

The syndicate boat sat anchored off the southern point like a statement of account. Steel hull, twin diesels idling low, a man standing at the rail with a rifle held in the lazy way of someone who doesn't expect to use it because he's already been paid not to have to. Behind him, the morning fog was still burning off the water, and through it I could see the smoke from the trading post — old Tomás's post — still rising, thin and gray, two days after Marrow put it to the torch. A message written in somebody else's livelihood.

"He's waiting," Dov said from beside me. He'd been quiet for an hour, which for Dov was either exhaustion or strategy. His hand rested on the satchel of tablets like it had grown there.

"Waiting men are patient men," I said. "Patient men are usually wrong about something."

"That's not wisdom, Captain. That's just you talking."

The boy had learned more from me than river-reading, apparently. God help him.

Ilsa came up from below without being called. She moved better now — ten days past fever, the bullet wound in her shoulder down to an angry pink seam — but she still favored her left side, and she still scanned the bank before she scanned the sky, which told me everything about what her weeks with the expedition had taught her. She looked at the syndicate boat, then at me.

"How much channel do we have?"

"Enough to turn around. Not enough to pretend we didn't see him."

"Then don't turn around."

I hadn't planned to. That was the trouble with the last seven years of my life: I'd built them out of turns. Turn away from the accident, turn away from the inquiry, turn away from every dockside conversation that lasted longer than a fuel receipt. Miles instead of years, miles instead of names, miles instead of grief. And here was Marrow, anchored across my only exit, offering to sell me back every one of those turns at once.

* * *

He came alongside in a skiff, alone, which was its own kind of threat. Men like Marrow don't travel alone unless they're certain. He stepped aboard the Rivergate with the unhurried care of a man entering his own kitchen, and he looked around my wheelhouse — at the chipped compass binnacle, the coffee ring on the chart table, Dov's river-reading notes pinned above the throttle — with an expression I couldn't read until I realized I could. It was appraisal. He was pricing my life.

"Captain Ashgrove." His voice was gravel poured slow. "You've cost me two boats, a lockmaster's pension, and most of a season."

"You're welcome."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Interest. That was worse. "Here's what I know about you," he said. "No registered home port worth mentioning. No family listed since seven years ago. No debts, no memberships, no photographs anywhere. You've spent a decade making yourself into a rumor with a boat. That takes discipline. I respect it." He set a leather folder on my chart table. "So here's my offer. You give me the tablets and the woman's silence, and tomorrow morning none of this ever happened. Your name stays clean. Your boat stays yours. You go back to being nobody, comfortably, forever."

And there it was. The thing I'd been running toward for seven years, gift-wrapped by the man who'd burned Tomás's post and put a bullet in Ilsa's shoulder and chased a fourteen-year-old boy across half a country for a box of stones.

I looked at the folder. I thought about how easy it would be. That's the honest truth, and I'll write it down even though it shames me: for three full seconds, I wanted it. The river ahead, empty and anonymous. No syndicate, no inquiries, no museum men asking questions I'd have to answer under my own name. Just miles. Beautiful, weightless, meaningless miles.

Then Dov shifted behind me — just shifted his weight, the way boys do — and I heard Ilsa breathing evenly in the doorway, and I understood that the three seconds were over and had been over since the night I pulled them out of a cave and felt something in my chest unclench for the first time since my brother died.

"No," I said.

Marrow tilted his head. "You haven't heard the terms."

"I heard them fine. You're offering me my old life back." I picked up the folder and handed it back to him. "My old life was a smaller boat than this one."

For a moment neither of us moved, and the river did what rivers do, which is keep going whether or not anyone aboard has made up their mind.

* * *

He didn't reach for a weapon. I'll say that for him. He went for the boat instead — turned on his heel, dropped back into the skiff, and gave a short signal to the steel hull, and I heard both diesels come up out of idle with a sound like a door closing.

"They'll run us down in open water," Ilsa said. She was already at the chart table, hands flat on the Serrano survey she'd redrawn from memory. "We can't outrun them downstream. Hull speed won't allow it."

"We're not going downstream."

Dov looked at me. Ilsa looked at me. Even the compass seemed to look at me.

"The confluence shallows," I said. "Three Fingers. Two currents fighting over a sand fan — the draft changes hour to hour, day to day. No chart is current because no chart *can* be. It's the one piece of water on this river that belongs to whoever reads it best." I put my hand on the throttle. "They've got twelve inches more draft than we do and a helmsman who's never run this stretch. We've got a cartographer who memorized the 1911 survey, a stowaway with good eyes, and a captain who's been listening to this river since before your syndicate had a concession."

"And if you misread it?" Ilsa asked.

"Then we find out together."

I want to remember that sentence exactly as I said it, because it was the first time in seven years the word *together* had come out of my mouth without flinching on the way past.

* * *

What followed took four minutes and lasted approximately nine years.

I brought the Rivergate hard to starboard and drove her straight at the mouth of the shallows, and the syndicate boat came after us the way money always does — fast, direct, and convinced of its own right-of-way. The steel hull drew a straight line across the water. I drew a crooked one.

"Brown line off the left point!" Dov called from the bow, one hand on the rail, pointing where the water changed color from tea to milk. "It's curving south — the sand's moved!"

"Sand's moved," I confirmed, and swung inside the curve where the deeper cut had migrated overnight. The keel sighed over the bar with maybe a hand's width to spare. Behind us, the syndicate boat's helmsman hesitated — I saw it, the fractional pause of a man consulting a chart instead of the water — and committed to the old channel.

"Marrow's boat is following the 1911 line," Ilsa said, and there was something in her voice that wasn't fear anymore. It was the sound a cartographer makes when reality agrees with her.

"Then Marrow's boat is about to learn what rivers think of archives."

I didn't ram them. Let the record show that. What I did was simpler and crueler: I crossed their bow at the exact moment the two currents met, threw my wake into the seam where the Serrano's cold water slid under the main channel's warm load, and let the river do the arithmetic. Their bow wave collapsed into the confusion. Their helm overcorrected — twelve inches of draft, remember, and a sand fan that had spent the dry season rearranging itself precisely to punish men who bought their maps instead of earning them.

The steel hull hit the bar at eleven knots.

The sound carried across the whole confluence — a grinding, tearing groan, metal surrendering to geology — and then they were hard aground, listing, engines screaming against nothing, and Marrow stood at their rail watching us go while his own wake slapped against his stranded hull like slow applause.

I should have felt triumph. What I felt instead was colder and older. Because the current that had trapped him wasn't finished. The main channel, cheated of its usual bed by the season's low water, was shouldering past the grounded hull with new force, scouring the sand out from under his stern, and I watched — everyone watched — as the boat began to pivot, slowly, stern swinging downstream, taking Marrow's footing with it.

He went over the rail trying to reach the skiff. The river took him mid-reach.

I had the Rivergate turned and backing water before I'd decided to. That's the truth of it — my hands moved before my grief or my judgment could vote. Ilsa threw the buoyant cushion. Dov leaned so far over the bowrail I grabbed his belt. And for perhaps thirty seconds we searched that churning seam of water for a man who had burned a trading post, murdered a surveyor, hunted a child, and offered me my loneliness back at a fair price.

The river kept him. Rivers are impartial that way; it's their one virtue and their one vice. The current that drowned him had been dammed, dredged, bribed, and sold by men like him for forty years, and it took him without malice and without memory, the way it takes everything, the way it took my brother, the way it almost took me.

Dov was crying. Not loudly. Ilsa put her hand on the back of his neck, and I put the wheel over, and we stood off from the wreck until it was clear there was nothing left to do but leave.

* * *

We ran the rest of the confluence in silence, past the grounded hull settling into the sand like a monument nobody would build, and when the deep water finally took us again I found my voice had gone somewhere and come back changed.

"I knew him," I said, mostly to the wheel. "Not Marrow. My brother. When the river took him, I swore I'd never let it take anyone else I—" I stopped. Seven years of not-finishing sentences, and I could feel this one wanting to be finished.

Ilsa waited. Dov waited. The river, for once, waited too.

"Anyone else I loved," I said.

It hung in the wheelhouse air, plain and unarmored, the first true thing I'd said aloud since the funeral. Ilsa reached over and touched the back of my hand on the wheel, once, and said nothing, which was the correct navigation.

Ahead, the channel bent north toward home, toward the lock, toward the city where a map drawn from fevered memory and a satchel of stone tablets were going to end a mining concession and start a war of a very different kind. There would be reporters. There would be hearings. There would be my name, spoken out loud, attached to things, permanent as a carving in stone.

Seven years ago I'd have turned at that bend and let the world keep its secrets.

I opened the throttle, and the Rivergate ran for the light.
