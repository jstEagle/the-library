## 10. Rivergate and Provision

**FELIX**

The dry season beat us home by four days.

I knew it before we rounded the last bend above Rivergate itself — the channel markers standing in air where they should have stood in water, the mud along the banks cracked into a mosaic like something shattered and never repaired. The *Rivergate* drew too much now for the cuts she'd run in spring. We came down the outside of the channel the whole way, slow, careful, me reading the water the way my father taught me and my brother never learned, and Ilsa at the wheel for the last stretch because her hands had healed steadier than mine.

Four days late on a contract that no longer existed. The syndicate's concession was dead by then; we'd killed it somewhere over the Serrano Rapids, or rather Dov had, mailing three copies of everything from the trading post at Two Forks while Marrow's boat was still upside down in the confluence shallows. Journalists. The national museum. A university archivist whose name Ilsa had memorized years ago, the way some people memorize prayers.

So when we tied off at the Provision dock, there was nothing to come home to except home.

I stood on the deck a long time after the lines were made fast, listening to the hull tick as it cooled. Seven years I'd measured my life in miles. Miles upriver, miles downriver, miles between me and the memory of Tom's voice on a radio that went quiet one afternoon in exactly the wrong way. I used to think if I kept moving fast enough, the arithmetic would hold — that distance could be converted into safety, that safety could be converted into a kind of peace.

It doesn't convert. I can tell you that now with authority. It just accumulates.

"You're doing the thing," Dov said from the gangway, "where you stand very still and look tragic."

"I'm assessing the dock."

"You're looking tragic at the dock."

The boy had been aboard nine weeks and had already developed the insufferable precision of a good deckhand. He came down the planks two at a time, satchel empty now — the tablets were in a museum vault behind three locks and a curator who wept when he unwrapped them — and stood beside me with his hands in his pockets, fourteen going on forty-five.

"They're saying," he said, "that there'll be a hearing. About the concession fraud. And that they might want us to testify."

"Us."

"Well. You." He shrugged, elaborately casual, which is how teenagers say *please say yes*. "Ilsa says you hate cameras."

"Ilsa says a lot of things."

"She also says you're staying."

I looked at him then. He was watching the water, not me, giving me the same courtesy I'd given him the night I found him in the cave — the pretense of privacy, which is sometimes the only kindness available.

"The boat needs a crew," I said. "And I'm short-handed."

"You're not short-handed. You have me."

"I have a stowaway with delusions of employment."

"Delusions of *wages*," he corrected, and grinned, and went below to argue with Ilsa about whose turn it was to scrub the bilge, an argument they'd been having cheerfully since the rapids and would presumably still be having when we were both old.

I stayed on deck until dark. Then I did something I hadn't done in seven years: I sat down at the little table in the wheelhouse, took out the logbook, and wrote an entry that wasn't mileage.

*Home,* I wrote. *All hands accounted for.*

***

**ILSA**

They gave me back my map. That's the part nobody warns you about — that after the museums and the hearings and the men in suits saying *invaluable* in reverent voices, someone hands you a photocopy of your own fever-drawn lines and asks you to sign it, and your hand shakes because the thing that saved your life looks so small on paper.

I signed it anyway. Felix watched from the doorway of the hearing room with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, and afterward he said, "You realize you're famous now," in the tone of a man describing a disease.

"So are you."

"That's different. I was trying to stay anonymous."

"You rammed a syndicate gunboat at a river confluence in front of six witnesses. Anonymity was never on the table, Captain."

He almost smiled. With Felix, almost is a whole conversation.

We walked down to the docks together through the evening market, past the fish stalls and the fuel depot and the bulletin board where someone had pinned a newspaper clipping — CONCESSION REVOKED, RIVER CIVILIZATION CONFIRMED — with a photograph of the terraced city rising green out of the gorge, taken from the cave mouth where I'd lain bleeding and watched the light move across stone older than every name we had for it. My city. Our city, maybe. It belonged to whoever came next, which was the entire point.

At the *Rivergate*, Dov was on deck repainting the nameboard, badly, tongue between his teeth. He'd added a second word underneath in letters twice the size: PROVISION. Not the town. The promise.

"He's decided we're a company now," Felix said.

"Are we?"

Felix looked at the boy, then at me, then out at the brown water going gold with evening, and I watched him do the arithmetic he'd been doing his whole adult life — miles against miles, loss against loss — and then, visibly, deliberately, stop.

"Yeah," he said. "I suppose we are."

I'd spent eleven weeks learning this man: how he grieved in measurements, how he loved in maintenance schedules, how he said *the boat needs* when he meant *I need*. I'd drawn maps of harder country. So I did what I do with unmapped channels — I named the hazard plainly.

"I'm staying too," I said. "Not as cargo. As partner. Half the boat, half the decisions, half the blame when you ground her."

"Fifty percent of the blame is generous."

"It's fifty percent of *everything*. That's what partnership means. Take it or swim."

Dov, without looking up from his painting: "Take it, Captain. She's better at the wheel than you anyway."

And Felix Ashgrove, who had spent seven years being the only fixed point in his own drifting universe, put out his hand and shook mine like signing articles, solemn as a christening, while a fourteen-year-old snickered at us both.

**DOV**

Here's what nobody tells you about having a family: it's mostly chores.

I thought it would be more dramatic. In the detention center we used to talk about families like weather systems — distant, dangerous, something that happened to other people. What actually happened to me was that a grumpy riverboat captain taught me to read water, a feverish cartographer taught me to draw it, and now I spend my mornings scrubbing a bilge that produces slime at a rate I consider scientifically insulting.

Worth it, though.

Because here's the other thing: when Marrow's people burned the trading post at Two Forks, I understood for the first time why Felix measures everything. When the world takes something from you, numbers feel like a floor you can stand on. But I've watched him stop counting. Last week Ilsa asked how far it was to the museum dock and he said, "An hour, give or take," and didn't convert it to miles, and didn't flinch, and Ilsa caught my eye over his shoulder and we both pretended not to notice, which is also family. Family is mostly pretending not to notice things while quietly building a shrine to them.

The tablets are in the museum now. I visited once. There's a plaque with the expedition members' names on it, including the ones who died at the camp, including Dr. Voss who sold them all out and got exactly what the river gives everyone eventually. My name isn't on the plaque. I was never officially on the expedition.

But there's a display case near the entrance with a photograph of a stone tablet in it, and under the photograph, small print: *Recovered from the upper river by private vessel, autumn of the great drought.* Private vessel. That's us. That's the *Rivergate and Provision Company*, population three, fleet size one, bilge slime production rate unacceptable.

I asked the curator if I could touch the tablet. He said no. I touched it anyway, quick, when he turned around, and it was cold and heavy and real, and it felt like proof. Proof that things buried aren't always lost. Proof that some things get carried out of the dark by people who shouldn't have survived and didn't plan to care about each other and did anyway.

***

**FELIX**

On the anniversary — Tom's, not the accident's; I've stopped marking the day the radio went silent and started marking the day the voice existed at all — I took the boat out alone at dawn. Not far. Just up to the bend where the current runs clean over gravel, the place my brother used to call the front porch.

I brought two cups of coffee out of habit, and drank them both out of choice.

"Seven years," I told the water. "You'd have liked them. Well. You'd have liked the last eleven weeks. The rest were just mileage."

The river said nothing, the way rivers do, which is its own kind of answer. Somewhere upstream, under terrace and vine and ten thousand years of patient silt, a city waited to be known again. Somewhere downstream, lawyers were dismantling a syndicate clause by clause. And somewhere behind me, on the dock at Provision, a boy was arguing with a woman about paint colors for a nameboard that now read, in letters visible from the far bank:

*RIVERGATE AND PROVISION.*

Provision, Dov explained to anyone who asked, meant two things. It meant supplies — what a boat carries, what a river provides. And it meant providing for someone. Making sure they eat. Making sure they're counted. Making sure that when the radio goes quiet, somebody comes looking.

I turned the boat for home.

I didn't check the odometer. For the first time in seven years, I honestly didn't care how many miles it was.
