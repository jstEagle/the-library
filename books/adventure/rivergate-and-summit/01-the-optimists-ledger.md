## 1. The Optimists' Ledger

The supercell had been beautiful for exactly eleven minutes, and Sebastian Thornbury had spent all of them working instead of watching.

She stood in the bed of the Kestrel's old chase truck—she'd never renamed it, couldn't bring herself to—and fed sensor cable through her gloved hands while the mesocyclone turned green-black over the wheat. The anemometer on the mast screamed a steady ninety knots. Hail the size of golf balls hammered the truck cap in a rhythm like applause, which was the kind of joke her brain made when she was running on four hours of sleep and vending machine coffee.

"Reading's clean," Wren called from the cab, voice half-swallowed by the storm. "Client wants the full package. You good for another pass?"

Sebastian looked at the wall cloud, at the scud ripping sideways beneath it, at the lightning strobing inside the core like something trying to get out. She'd chased storms for fourteen years. She knew what they were: heat and moisture and shear, nothing more, nothing that wanted anything.

"Good for another pass," she said, and climbed down to drive.

The data run took forty minutes and cost her a cracked windshield. The client—a weather derivatives outfit out of Denver that paid cash and asked no questions—wired eleven thousand dollars before she'd finished packing the probes. Eleven thousand. She sat in the driver's seat afterward, in the sudden enormous quiet behind the storm, and let herself feel it for ten seconds: the specific, chemical relief of money arriving.

Then she did the math she always did. Eleven thousand in. Interest out. Fees out. The number didn't shrink. The number never shrank. It had been four years since the number had shrunk.

"Seb." Wren's voice, careful. "You're doing the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have a face. It's the one where you're dividing."

Sebastian put the truck in gear. "Let's go home."

* * *

"Home" was a fuel stop outside Salina with showers, a diner, and—because the universe had a sense of humor and it was a cruel one—a man in a pale yellow suit sitting at the corner booth like he'd been grown there from seed.

Sebastian saw him through the glass before she saw the sign for the showers, and her whole body did the thing it did: shoulders down, chin down, the old instinct to become smaller, harder to bill. Wren saw him too. Wren's jaw went tight, and he reached across the bench seat and put two fingers on her wrist.

"I can wait in the truck," he said.

"No." She exhaled. "If I go in alone, he'll enjoy it more."

Mr. Feliz—everyone called him Mr. Feliz, and he seemed genuinely delighted by it—was a large man with a round, open face and the eyes of someone who had never once been disappointed in anything, because disappointment required expectations and Feliz simply adjusted reality to suit the ledger. The Vane Syndicate didn't send threats. Threats were for people who might default. The Syndicate sent Feliz, and Feliz sent fruit baskets.

"Sebastian!" He stood, arms wide, like she was a daughter home from college. The booth was set for two, but there was a third plate anyway—pancakes, butter already melting. He'd ordered for her. He always ordered for her. "Sit, sit. You look terrible. Storm work is so hard on the complexion."

"Feliz."

"Please. Merrick." Nobody called him Merrick. He beamed anyway. "Eleven-two hundred on the Denver run. I saw the wire. I want you to know that everyone at the office is so proud of you. Marisol printed it out and put it on the board."

"The board."

"The board." He nodded solemnly, as if this were an honor. It was, in the way a scar is an honor. The Vane Syndicate kept a board of its debtors' earnings, updated weekly, in the lobby of a building in Wichita that didn't appear on any map worth trusting. Being on the board meant they were watching. Being watched by the Syndicate was the closest thing to being loved they offered, and it was worse than any collection call Sebastian had ever endured. "Which is why it pains me—genuinely, physically pains me, I saw a doctor about it—to bring you the revised figures."

Sebastian sat. The pancakes steamed between them. She didn't touch them. Eating Syndicate pancakes felt like signing something.

"The figures were revised in March," she said.

"And April," Feliz agreed happily. "And now. You know how it is. Currency fluctuations. Administrative reassessment. There was a fire in the records annex—no one was hurt, which we're all thankful for—but some of the original documentation for your principal was, well. Gone. And when documentation is gone, we have to reconstruct, and reconstruction is an art, Sebastian. Marisol is an artist."

"Reconstruct it from what?"

"From what's fair." He spread his hands, and his rings caught the diner light, ten small suns. "You had the Kestrel years. Equipment depreciation, opportunity cost, the value of the routes you're sitting on. The Rivergate material alone—" he paused, and for half a second, the delight in his face sharpened into something with edges— "well. We'll get to that."

Sebastian's hands went flat on the table. "The Rivergate material is not collateral. It was never collateral."

"Of course not." Feliz's smile returned, warm as a heating pad. "Nothing is collateral, Sebastian. That's the beautiful thing about our arrangement. We don't want your truck or your sensors or your little map. We want *you* to keep working, keep earning, keep being the best storm reader on the continent. Everything else is just... accounting." He slid a single sheet of paper across the table. Cream stock. The Syndicate's seal, a stylized V like a bird in descent. "The new number."

She looked at it. She'd known she would look at it; she looked every time, because not looking felt like losing a different way.

Four hundred and twelve thousand. Last month it had been three hundred and eighty-six.

"That's more than the interest," she said. Her voice came out level, which she was proud of. "That's more than the principal ever was. You've added a *reconstruction fee* larger than the original loan."

"We added a *clarity* fee." Feliz picked up his coffee, sipped, sighed with pleasure. "Debt is foggy, Sebastian. Numbers drift. We're just trying to bring things into focus for everyone. You'll notice we also adjusted your earning projections upward—your Denver run was very impressive—and so your *capacity* has increased, which means your *obligation* has increased, because we believe in you." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register he probably thought of as intimate and she thought of as the sound a trap makes when it closes. "That's the whole philosophy of the firm. We are optimists. We believe every debtor can pay. We believe it *so hard*, Sebastian. We will never, ever stop believing."

The pancakes had gone cold. Outside, the storm's outflow reached the parking lot, and the wind chimes over the door began their idiot cheerful clatter.

"I'll need the payment schedule," she said.

"You'll need *opportunity*." Feliz produced a second envelope, thinner, and set it beside the first with the tenderness of a man laying down a winning card. "And I have wonderful news. Someone is going to call on you. Very soon. A colleague. An old friend, I'm told. The firm has encouraged her to reach out, and we'd be so pleased if you took the meeting."

Sebastian stared at the envelope. "Who?"

"I'm not at liberty—it's not my department." He stood, buttoned his yellow jacket, and beamed down at her with the full wattage of a man who had never carried a debt he couldn't transfer. "Eat your pancakes. You've earned them. And Sebastian—" he paused at the booth's end, and for a moment the cheerfulness thinned, like fog over something with teeth, "—the firm is rooting for you. Whatever you decide. Whatever you *have* to decide. We're rooting."

He left a tip that was too large and a fruit basket that had been sitting in his car, and the door chimed behind him, and the diner exhaled.

Sebastian sat for a long time with the two envelopes in front of her, the number and the nameless invitation, the fog and the focus. Wren came in from the truck, read her face, and sat down across from her without asking. He looked at the cream-colored paper, then at her.

"Seb," he said quietly. "What did they give you?"

She turned the thinner envelope over. No seal. Just a handwritten line, in a slanted, elegant script she hadn't seen in six years and would have known anywhere, the way you know the shape of a scar:

*Sebastian. You know what I'm selling. Call me. —O.M.*

The wind chimes clattered. Somewhere west, over the plains, another storm was building, and for the first time in four years, Sebastian Thornbury was afraid of something that wasn't weather.
