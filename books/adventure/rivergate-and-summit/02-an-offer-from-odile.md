## 2. An Offer from Odile

The diner sat where the highway bent around a dry wash, all chrome and faded red vinyl, the kind of place that survived on truckers and people hiding from something. Sebastian fit the second category. She'd been nursing the same cup of coffee for forty minutes, watching dust devils walk the flats outside, trying to do arithmetic that refused to come out even.

The debt figure from last night sat in her head like a stone in a boot. *Revised terms. Interest recalculated. Congratulations on the successful chase — Mr. Vane is pleased.* Pleased. The Syndicate was always pleased. That was the horror of them. They'd send a man in a linen suit to stand in your kitchen, and he'd beam at you like you'd won something while he slid the new number across the counter, and the number was always bigger, and his smile never moved.

The bell over the door rang.

Sebastian didn't look up right away. She'd learned to read doors — the weight of them, the way wind came through. This door opened slow, deliberate, the way someone opens it when they want you to have time to turn around.

She turned around.

Odile Marchetti stood in the entrance with the late light behind her, tall and dry-eyed, wearing a field jacket worth more than Sebastian's truck. Her hair was shorter than it had been five years ago. Everything else was exactly the same: the posture of a woman who had never once entered a room she didn't intend to own.

"Thornbury," Odile said. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive." Sebastian turned back to her coffee. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

"I'm not selling. I'm buying." Odile crossed the diner, took the seat across from her without asking, and set a slim gray folder on the table between them. "Your coffee's gone cold. Order another. This takes ten minutes."

"I know what takes ten minutes. Contracts take ten minutes."

"Then you know I came prepared." Odile's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Funded expedition. Full crew, full equipment. I need a guide into the Rivergate basin, and there are exactly two people alive who can read that country, and one of them is me."

Sebastian looked up. "And the other one?"

"You're looking at her, and we both know it."

The word landed in Sebastian's chest like a dropped wrench. *Rivergate.* Five years since anyone had said it to her face. Her old crew had a rule about it — not spoken, never spoken, but absolute as gravity: *we don't go back.* They'd sworn it over cheap whiskey in a motel room in Amarillo, six of them, and one of them dead, and one of them staring at a wall like it had something written on it that only he could see.

"No," Sebastian said.

"I haven't made an offer yet."

"The answer's no before the offer. It was no when you walked in."

Odile nodded slowly, as if Sebastian had said something interesting. "You know what I've always admired about you? You commit. Right or wrong, you commit all the way down." She tapped the folder once with one finger and left it there. "I'm going to the restroom. When I come back, you'll either have opened that folder or you won't. Either way, I'll know everything I need to know about you, and I'll act accordingly."

She stood, adjusted her jacket, and walked to the back of the diner like a woman with all the time in the world.

Sebastian looked at the folder.

She didn't touch it. She looked at the window instead, at the dust devils, at the long light going amber over the flats, and she thought about the linen-suited man in her kitchen, and his smile, and the number that grew while she slept. She thought about the *Kestrel* — and stopped thinking about the *Kestrel*, the way you snatch your hand off a hot stove.

The coffee was cold. She drank it anyway.

When Odile came back, the folder was still closed, and Sebastian was surprised at how much it cost her to keep it that way.

"Interesting," Odile said. She picked up the folder. "Truly. I'd have bet on the other outcome." She slid it into her jacket. "Well. I'll leave you my number, in case—"

"How much."

Odile paused. She turned back, and this time the smile arrived all the way, and it was worse than the linen suit's smile, because Odile's was real.

"Enough," she said, "to clear the Vane ledger. All of it. With the interest. With the *revised* interest." She let that sit. "Plus twenty percent, which you can spend on anything you like, or nothing at all, just to feel what it's like to owe no one."

The diner noise — the fryer, the radio, the truckers — seemed to fall away. Sebastian heard her own pulse.

"That's not possible," she said. "Nobody pays that for a guide."

"Nobody pays that for a guide," Odile agreed. "They pay it for *you*."

* * *

Wren Okafor was in the truck outside, feet on the dash, working the route board on her tablet with a stylus she held the way other people held cigarettes. She'd been navigator for Sebastian for six years, which meant she'd learned to read two weathers: the one in the sky and the one in Sebastian's face. Right now the second one was reading *storm cell, rotating, undecided.*

Through the diner window she watched the tall woman in the expensive jacket stand over Sebastian's booth. Wren didn't know her name, but she knew the *type*. Old life. Old debts. The kind of person who showed up in your story right before the chapter where everything caught fire.

Sebastian had gone still in the way she went still before a chase — not frozen, *focused*. Wren had seen that stillness maybe four times. Twice it had ended in the best driving of her life. Once it had ended with Dov's hand in a winch. Once it had ended with Sebastian not speaking for two days.

The woman left the booth. Through the glass, Wren watched Sebastian sit alone for a long moment, staring at nothing, then press both palms flat against the table like she was bracing for turbulence.

Wren put the stylus down.

Here was the thing Wren had never said out loud: the debt was killing them. Not fast — the Syndicate didn't kill anything fast, that was their whole method — but surely. Every chase they ran now, they ran for the ledger. Every good season got eaten before it landed. Wren had watched the joy drain out of the work year by year, watched Sebastian chase weather she didn't love for money she'd never see, and the worst part, the part that sat in Wren's chest at three in the morning, was that Sebastian never complained. She just got quieter. Quieter and quieter, like a radio losing signal.

Wren wanted her to say yes. She didn't even know to what. She just wanted the number to be one that ended the story.

The truck door opened and Sebastian dropped into the driver's seat, and Wren read her face and got nothing. Blank sky.

"Who was that?" Wren asked.

"Nobody," Sebastian said, and started the engine.

"Seb."

"Old rival. Odile Marchetti. She's putting together an expedition." Sebastian pulled onto the highway, eyes on the road. "Rivergate."

The name went through the cab like cold air through a cracked window. Wren had never been to Rivergate. She'd joined the crew two years *after* — after Amarillo, after the whiskey, after the rule nobody said. But she knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a room where something happened before you moved in. She knew that Dov went quiet at the word, and that Ilsa had once thrown a coffee mug at a wall over it, and that the old route map lived in the truck's lockbox and had never once been opened in her presence.

"You told her no," Wren said.

"I told her no."

"Okay." Wren watched the flats roll past. The light was failing. Somewhere out there, a storm was building that neither of them would chase, because chasing cost money they didn't have, and not chasing cost something else entirely. "What was the number?"

"Wren."

"I'm navigator. Numbers are my whole job. What was the number?"

Sebastian was quiet for a mile. Two. When she answered, her voice had a strange flatness to it, like a road with no curves.

"Enough," she said. "All of it. The whole ledger. Plus twenty."

Wren turned that over and felt something in her chest she refused to name, because naming it would make her complicit, and she was trying very hard not to be complicit. She was loyal. Loyalty was the one thing she'd never had to think about.

"Rivergate killed people," Wren said carefully.

"Rivergate didn't kill anyone. Whatever happened there—" Sebastian's jaw worked. "We don't know what happened there. That's the whole point. That's why we swore off it."

"You were there."

"I was there." A beat. "Parts of me were there."

Wren didn't know what that meant. She'd stopped asking, years ago, what Sebastian meant by the things she said about the old crew — the gaps in her own stories, the way she'd talk about a storm chase and skip a week like a record with a scratch. She'd filed it under *trauma* and left it alone, because you leave a person's scars alone.

But a number like that. A number like that wasn't a job. A number like that was a door, and doors only got offered once.

"Seb," Wren said. "I've never asked you for anything. I'm not asking now. I'm just going to say one thing, and then I'll never say it again."

"Say it."

"The ledger doesn't sleep. The Syndicate doesn't sleep. Every year we're slower and they're bigger, and one day the number gets bigger than any storm can pay down, and they come collect, and they'll be *smiling*." Wren kept her eyes on the road ahead, on the last band of daylight. "I'd rather find out what's in Rivergate than find out what's in that future."

Sebastian's hands tightened on the wheel. The truck hummed. The flats went violet, then dark.

"You don't know what you're asking," Sebastian said finally.

"No," Wren agreed. "I don't. But I know what I'm asking *for*."

They drove in silence for a long time. When Sebastian finally spoke, she didn't answer. She said something worse.

"She left the folder number on the receipt," she said. "Under my coffee cup. She knew I'd look."

Wren glanced over. In the dark cab, she could just see Sebastian's face — and there it was again, that stillness. That focused, terrible stillness.

"Did you look?" Wren asked.

Sebastian didn't answer. She reached into her jacket pocket, and Wren heard the dry whisper of paper, and knew.

"Tomorrow," Sebastian said, to the road, to the dark, to herself. "Tomorrow I burn the map."
