## 3. The Sworn-Off Map

The route burned in a coffee can on the tailgate of the Kestrel's replacement—a battered chase truck Sebastian had bought with Syndicate money and named nothing, because naming things you expected to lose was a habit she'd broken along with several others.

The paper curled, blackened, and let go of itself in flakes. Eleven years of ink. Elevations she'd memorized so thoroughly that burning them was theater—she could draw the whole basin from memory on a napkin, blindfolded, drunk. The map wasn't information anymore. It was a confession with contour lines.

"You know that doesn't actually delete anything," Wren said from the passenger seat, feet up on the dash, watching the fire with the flat expression she wore when she'd already lost an argument and wanted credit for having made it.

"It deletes the copy."

"It deletes the *copy*."

"That's what I said."

Wren didn't laugh. That was the thing about Wren—she laughed at funerals and held silence for jokes, an inversion Sebastian had never fully mapped. She watched the last corner of the paper go gray and said, quietly: "You signed it anyway. The contract."

"I signed it because the alternative was Vane's people repossessing the truck while we slept in it."

"You signed it because the number had four zeros more than anything we've seen since—" She stopped herself. They both knew how the sentence ended. *Since the Kestrel.* Since the year the money stopped making sense to either of them.

Sebastian fed another page to the can. This one was hand-annotated, her own cramped script noting safe crossings, dead zones, a ridge line marked with three Xs and no explanation. Three Xs. She remembered drawing them. She did not remember why.

That was the shape of it now, eleven years on—not holes but seams. Places where the fabric of those months had been stitched over with something smooth and wrong, like scar tissue. She knew the disaster had happened. She knew Marisol hadn't come back. She knew Teo had come back without the year that contained it, sitting in a hospital hallway asking, gently, repeatedly, who these people were who kept crying at him. What she did not know was her own signature on any of it, because there was no signature left in her to find.

* * *

Dov came at dawn, as promised, towing a trailer full of equipment he refused to explain on the phone. He was a broad, deliberate man who spoke about engines the way priests spoke about grace, and he took one look at the burned coffee can, still sitting on the tailgate like an accusation, and said nothing about it at all.

"Fuel range?" he asked instead.

"Rivergate's four hundred miles past the last reliable pump."

"Then I'm bringing six hundred miles." He started loading without waiting for permission, which was why Sebastian had called him. Dov had been crew on the old team—the one before, the one that still had all its members—and he'd heard about the Kestrel the way everyone had: in fragments, in the negative space of conversations that swerved away. He'd never asked. That was his religion too.

Ilsa arrived at noon and stood by her car for a full minute before walking over, which told Sebastian everything about how the recruitment call had gone.

"I read the contract," Ilsa said. "Twice. There's a clause about atmospheric anomalies. 'Anomalies' is doing a lot of work in that sentence, Sebastian."

"Yes."

"And the location appendix was sealed. I had to sign just to learn where we're going, and I still only learned it as a *region*."

"Odile's lawyers are thorough."

"Odile's lawyers are cowards with stationery." Ilsa dropped her bag on the asphalt. She was the best meteorologist Sebastian had ever worked with, and she had quit fieldwork after the Kestrel season—not because of the Kestrel itself, though nobody believed that, but because of the week after, when the data came back from the basin and every instrument reading was beautiful and useless and wrong. "Tell me one thing honestly. Is this a recovery mission or a salvage mission?"

Sebastian thought about lying. It would have been easy; Ilsa wanted a reason to say no, not a reason to doubt.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the honest answer. Odile knows something's down there. I don't know what."

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her pick up her bag. "Fine. But I'm carrying my own instruments. Yours lie in that basin. Everyone's do."

* * *

They rolled out at first light two days later, four vehicles strong once Odile's specialists joined the column at the highway junction—six quiet people in matching technical gear who introduced themselves by first name only and checked their satellite uplinks with a frequency that suggested they expected the satellites to stop answering.

Wren rode with Sebastian, navigating out of habit even though the first leg was four hours of straight road. Around mile ninety she said, "Show me the route."

"Burned it."

"Sebastian."

"I have it memorized."

"I know you have it memorized. Show me the *route*." And when Sebastian glanced over: "You've been weird about this since the diner. You burned your copy before you even told us yes. People burn things they're ashamed of, or things they're hiding. Which one is this?"

The road unspooled ahead, brown and patient. Sebastian drove.

"Wren—"

"Don't." Wren's voice cracked, just once, and she fixed it immediately, the way she fixed everything, with effort visible only if you'd known her ten years. "Don't give me the voice you use on clients. I've been your navigator since before the Kestrel. Before the debt. I followed you into a tornado alley in '19 with a fuel gauge I told you was broken and you went anyway. So you tell me. Right now. What is in Rivergate that you already know about?"

The honest answer was *nothing*, and it was true, and it was also the most dishonest thing Sebastian could say, because the nothing had edges. She could feel them from here, eighty miles out, the way you feel a missing tooth.

"There's a ridge," she said finally. "Kestrel Ridge. We lost—" Her hands stayed level on the wheel through long practice. "We lost people there. I don't remember the details. Not avoiding them. I mean I don't *have* them. There's a gap where those weeks should be, and I've stopped trying to dig in it, because the one time I tried, I got migraines that lasted nine days and a nosebleed in both directions."

Wren was silent for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice was navigator-flat, coordinates-only. "A gap."

"A gap."

"You went to a clinic. After. I drove you. I thought it was for the migraines."

"It was for the migraines."

"Sebastian." Wren turned to look at her fully, and Sebastian kept her eyes on the road because she was a coward about exactly one thing and they were both looking at it. "What did you have them take?"

"Nothing. I don't know. Maybe nothing. Maybe the treatment records were—" She exhaled. "I signed releases, Wren. I signed a lot of releases that month. Whatever's gone, I authorized it going."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Wren turned back to her window and didn't speak for forty miles, and when she did, it was to call a turnoff that Sebastian had already seen, and the correction—gentle, automatic, unnecessary—landed between them like a small formal apology neither of them had agreed to make.

* * *

They camped that night on a bluff above the basin's northern rim, close enough that the fog below looked like a held breath. Odile's specialists ran cables and antennas and spoke in low clusters. Dov torqued something essential on vehicle three. Ilsa sat apart with her own instruments, frowning at readings she kept recalibrating, as if the atmosphere might simply apologize and behave.

Sebastian built a small fire she didn't need and found Wren already sitting at it.

"Tomorrow we go in," Wren said. "Through the window you read off the pressure maps that made Ilsa swear in two languages."

"Something like that."

"So this is the last night it's just a job." Wren poked the fire. Sparks went up and died young. "I keep thinking about the map. Your copy. You burned it the same day you signed."

"Habit."

"No." Wren shook her head slowly. "I've decided it wasn't. I think someone gave you that map, or you wrote it, and either way it's the truest document you own, and you couldn't stand having it in the truck with us. Which means whatever's written in the margins is worse than what's written in the contract."

Sebastian looked into the fire and said nothing, and Wren nodded as if the silence were an answer, which it was.

"Eleven years, Seb. I earned better than a gap."

Before Sebastian could answer—which was fortunate, because she had no answer—headlights swept the bluff. A single vehicle climbing the access road, running dark until the last hundred yards, when it flared its highs and pulled alongside the convoy with the confidence of something arriving exactly on schedule.

Odile Marchetti stepped out into the firelight, coat collar up, holding a folded sheet of paper like a man holding a winning hand he intended to play gently.

"Beautiful spot," she said. "Very close to the old approach corridor." She unfolded the paper and held it toward the firelight, and Sebastian's stomach dropped through the bluff, through the fog, through the basin floor itself.

It was the route. Her route. Her handwriting, her annotations, the three Xs on the ridge line—photographed, enlarged, crisp as the day she'd drawn it.

"Where did you get that."

"Your old crew's insurance archive," Odile said pleasantly. "Filed the season after the Kestrel. You really should read what you sign, Sebastian. You of all people." She smiled, warm as a heat lamp. "There's an annotation on the reverse side I'd very much like you to explain before we descend tomorrow. In your own words, if possible."

She turned the paper over.

The back was blank.

"That's the problem," Odile said softly. "Whoever catalogued it noted writing on the reverse. Faded past recovery. Something you wrote the night of the disaster—and then paid to forget."
