## 4. Into the Basin

The storm window opened at 4:17 in the morning, and only I saw it.

That was the whole reason Odile had hired me, though she'd dressed it up in the contract with phrases like *navigational expertise* and *regional meteorological acumen*. What she meant was: the Rivergate basin sits inside a collar of high ground that chokes off every approach but one, and that one approach exists for maybe ninety minutes at a time when a supercell's outflow boundary collides with the basin's own thermal inversion and pries the atmosphere open like a can opener. Everyone else who'd ever tried to drive into Rivergate had met a wall of shear that flipped vehicles like toys. My old crew had gone through the window once, eleven years ago, and we had never spoken of what we found on the other side.

I stood on the roof of the lead vehicle in the blue-black dark, watching lightning stitch itself along the ridge line to the north, and I did the math in my head the way you do arithmetic in a language you swore you'd forgotten.

"Sebastian." Wren's voice from below, careful. "Talk to me."

"Window opens in forty minutes," I said. "Closes around six. After that, nobody goes in or out for three days."

Nobody said anything about three days being exactly how long it would take the Syndicate's people to figure out where we'd gone. Nobody had to.

* * *

We went in single file—Odile's two support trucks, then my rig with Wren navigating and Dov riding shotgun with his boots on the dash, then Ilsa alone in the instrument vehicle because she'd asked for solitude and Odile, in one of her rare moments of actual generosity, had granted it.

The first twenty minutes were the best driving of my life. The outflow rolled over us cool and clean, the pressure dropped like an elevator, and the collar of shear that should have shredded us parted instead, a corridor of eerie calm threading between two walls of rotating cloud. Lightning walked across the ridges on both sides of us, close enough to taste the ozone, and above the corridor the clouds had opened into a ragged eye through which we could see stars.

Dov whistled low. "Okay. Okay, this is—" He didn't finish it. There wasn't a word.

Wren kept her eyes on her screens. "Instruments are nominal," she said, and then, quieter, "for now."

"For now," I agreed, and hated how much weight we both put on it.

Because here is what the brochures don't tell you about Rivergate, if there were brochures, which there aren't, because the people who come back don't write them: the basin doesn't want you. That sounds like poetry until you're inside it. Then it sounds like a diagnosis.

The fog found us at the bottom of the descent road. Not valley fog—that has texture, edges, a logic to it. This rose up out of the ground itself in the hour before dawn, unseasonal, unexplained, thick as wool and warm, which fog has no business being in April at altitude. Our headlights pushed cones into it and got nothing back. Wren called out our position twice and both times the GPS disagreed with itself by more than a hundred meters, drifting, correcting, drifting again, like a compass needle near a magnet.

"Dead reckoning only," I said. "Log every turn."

"You think it's interference?" Dov asked.

"I think it's the basin," I said, and let them chew on that.

* * *

By full light the fog had thinned to a gauze, and we saw the river.

The Rivergate runs through the center of the basin the way a spine runs through a body, and it is wrong in a way that photographs never capture and witnesses always doubt. In three places—and I remembered all three, my body remembered them before my mind caught up—the water flows uphill. Not an illusion of gradient. You can stand at the lip of one of those reaches and watch the current climb, steady and unhurried, a quarter mile of river gaining elevation like it has somewhere better to be. Ilsa stood at the bank with her anemometer hanging dead in her hand and her mouth slightly open.

"It's not possible," she said. "There's no mechanism. There's no—"

"There isn't," I said. "Welcome to Rivergate."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she hadn't since signing on. "You've been here before."

It wasn't a question. Odile had told her crew that I knew the region, nothing more. I watched Ilsa do the same math Wren had done weeks ago in a diner booth, arriving at the same answer: *knew* was doing a lot of work in that sentence.

"We'll set camp at the bend," I said, and walked away from the river before it could ask me anything else.

* * *

Camp went up in the lee of a gravel bar, and that was when I started watching Odile's specialists.

There were four of them, and they were too good. Field teams have a rhythm—grumbling, improvisation, someone forgetting stakes, someone else's radio dying. These four moved like a surgical team. They had the tents up in nine minutes. They unpacked equipment I didn't recognize: ground-penetrating arrays, a drilling rig compact enough to fit in a duffel, sealed cases they carried two-handed and never set down in the dirt. And they talked to each other in a shorthand that wasn't quite jargon and wasn't quite code, glancing our way when they thought we weren't looking.

One of them—a broad woman with a sunburned neck whose manifest listed her as a hydrologist named Perrin—caught me watching and smiled at me with real warmth. That was the part that put ice in my stomach. I know performed smiles; I've spent years under the Vane Syndicate's, and those are bright as billboards and empty as parking lots at night. Perrin's smile was genuine. She was glad to be here. Whatever they were looking for, she expected to find it, and soon.

"They're not surveying," Wren murmured, coming to stand beside me at the tailgate. We both faced outward, casual, two people admiring a view. "They've been here four hours and they haven't taken a single soil sample from open ground. Everything's pointed at the riverbank. That cutbank east of camp."

"I noticed."

"And their drill rig is rated for forty meters."

"I noticed that too."

Wren was quiet a moment. The fog was burning off the water behind her, and for a second the light made her look very young, which she wasn't, and very loyal, which she was, and both of those things hurt.

"Seb. What's under the riverbank?"

"I don't know," I said, and it was mostly true. I knew there was something. Eleven years ago there had been something, and my crew had come out of the basin changed—one of us dead, one of us hollowed out, me carrying a gap in my head the size of a summer. But whatever lived under that bank, I had paid good money to forget its address.

Wren studied my face the way she read weather, looking for the front behind the front. "You'd tell me," she said. It wasn't quite a question either. It was a down payment on a question, and we both knew the balance would come due.

"When I know something worth telling," I said, "you'll be the first."

She nodded slowly and walked away, and I felt the fracture line from the diner—the one I'd hoped time and miles would seal—open another millimeter.

* * *

At dusk the instruments died.

Not drifted. Died. Every radio on the expedition, every GPS unit, Ilsa's entire instrument array, all within the space of four minutes, as if the basin had reached up and switched itself off for the night. Odile's specialists didn't panic. That was the worst part. They checked their watches, exchanged a look of pure satisfaction, and began moving crates toward the cutbank by lantern light.

Ilsa came to my tent with her dead equipment cradled in her arms like a wounded animal. "This is impossible," she said. "EM interference doesn't discriminate by device class. This is selective. This is—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Sebastian, this is directed. Something is doing this on purpose."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

Because eleven years ago, on the last night, the radios had died the same way, in the same order, and my captain had laughed and said the basin was just shy, and by morning he was dead. I said none of that.

"Get some sleep, Ilsa."

She didn't move. Behind her, through the tent flap, I could see lanterns bobbing along the cutbank, and Odile standing apart from them, phone in hand despite the dead networks, staring at it as if willpower alone might raise a signal. As if she needed to report something. Or receive something.

"Odile's expecting company," Ilsa said quietly. "Aren't they."

"Sleep, Ilsa."

* * *

I took the last watch myself, sitting on the hood of my rig with a thermocouple thermometer and a wind meter that still worked because it was purely mechanical, no electronics to kill. The basin breathed around me. Somewhere upstream, the river climbed its impossible grade, patient as arithmetic.

And at 2 a.m., the fog parted over the eastern rim for maybe thirty seconds—long enough for starlight, long enough for me to see what sat on the far ridge against the sky.

Vehicles. Four of them, maybe five, parked in convoy formation with their lights killed. Sitting there. Waiting. Nobody drives to the rim of Rivergate and waits unless they already know the window opens again in three days, and nobody knows that except people who've been given my numbers.

I got my binoculars up, hands steady, heart not. The lead vehicle was a Syndicate transport—I'd know that silhouette anywhere, had seen it parked outside enough diners and courtrooms and hospital rooms. Painted on its flank, cheerful as a greeting card, the little stylized V that meant *we're so pleased you're making payments.*

They weren't coming in. Not yet. They were holding at the rim like creditors outside a locked office, smiling, patient, certain the door would open.

Behind me, down at the cutbank, a lantern flared, and I heard the bite of Odile's drill rig engaging, chewing down through the earth toward whatever the river had kept.

Three days until the window reopened. Three days inside a basin that didn't want us, with a rival who'd sold the door key, and a hole in my own memory sitting directly beneath our camp.

I keyed the intercom to Wren's tent, soft. "Wake up. Bring Dov. Don't wake anyone else."

The river ran uphill in the dark, going somewhere it shouldn't, and so, it seemed, did we.
