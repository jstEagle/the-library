## 5. What the River Kept

Wren had learned to read Sebastian's silences the way other navigators read contour lines. There was the silence of concentration, flat and workmanlike. There was the silence of anger, which hummed. And there was the silence she'd been hearing for three days now, since the fog closed over the basin and the compasses started lying — a silence with weight to it, like a held breath that had been held for years.

This morning the river gave them the reason.

It was Dov who spotted it, because Dov noticed everything that was broken. They'd stopped to winch the second truck up the cutbank, the one where the water ran uphill in a thin, glassy ribbon that Ilsa kept photographing like it might confess to something. Dov crouched at the base of the bank, wiped mud off a curved gray surface with his sleeve, and said, in the tone he used for rusted frames and cracked manifolds:

"Seb. This isn't rock."

It wasn't. It was composite paneling, river-smoothed, maybe thirty years buried, and it curved down into the bank in a long deliberate arc — not wreckage, not debris, but architecture. Something had been built here, and the river had swallowed it, and the river had kept it.

Sebastian stood over the exposed panel for a long time without touching it. Wren watched her hand come up, hover, and withdraw.

"Odile," Sebastian said finally, and her voice was too even. "Your specialists want to see this."

* * *

Odile arrived with the two hired specialists — Voss and Adeyemi, who had spent the whole expedition being politely vague about their credentials — and Wren noticed that neither of them looked surprised. Not even a little. Voss ran a scanner along the panel like a man checking a box on a list he'd written months ago.

"Subsurface structure's intact," Adeyemi said. "There's an access shaft forty meters downstream. It should still be sealed."

"*Should* be," Dov said.

"Sealed against weather," Adeyemi said, "not against people who know what they're looking for."

Sebastian looked at Odile. Odile looked back with that expression Wren had come to think of as her ledger face — pleasant, patient, arithmetic running quietly behind it. "You knew," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question.

"I knew there was a reason to fund an expedition," Odile said. "I didn't know what we'd find. That's what expeditions are *for*, Sebastian."

Wren had spent enough years around both of them to know when two people were having a different conversation than the one they were speaking aloud. She didn't like it. She liked even less the way Sebastian's jaw set — not with anger, but with something older and more tired, like a woman being shown a bill she'd already paid once.

"Open it," Sebastian said.

* * *

The access shaft took Dov four hours and most of his patience. The seal was magnetic, which meant power, which meant that somewhere under the riverbank a station that had been buried since before Wren got her first license was still drawing current from something. When the door finally exhaled — an actual pneumatic sigh, dust and cold air rolling out like the station had been holding its breath along with everything else — the specialists went in first, and Sebastian went in second, and Wren followed because following Sebastian was the only job she'd ever wanted and the only job she'd ever been bad at quitting.

The station was small. Three rooms under the river, lit by strips that woke as they walked, the air cold and mineral and impossibly clean. Banks of equipment stood in stasis-sleep, screens dark, cables coiled with the neatness of people who had expected to come back. There was a galley with mugs still on hooks. There was a door with a stenciled designation — **RIVERGATE FIELD STATION 3 — KESTREL PROGRAM** — and beneath the stencil, in marker, someone's handwriting:

*If you're reading this, check the date on everything.*

Wren felt the cold of the place settle into her shoulders. She'd heard the name Kestrel exactly once in six years of working with Sebastian, on a night in a motel bar in Amarillo when Dov had said it and Sebastian had stood up, put a twenty on the table, and walked into the rain without her jacket. Nobody had said it since. Nobody had needed to. It lived in the crew the way a scar lives in a body — unmentioned, unexamined, load-bearing.

"Storage room's this way," Adeyemi said, and her voice had changed. Softer. Almost reverent. Wren thought: *she's read something about this place. She's known the shape of it before she ever saw it.*

The storage room held racks of media — physical drives, tape canisters, sealed cases — and a terminal that woke at Voss's touch with a chime that sounded, in the dead air, almost like a voice.

"Index is intact," Voss said. "Field recordings. Logs. Launch reviews." He paused, fingers hovering. "Kestrel expedition, full archive."

Wren watched Sebastian.

She watched because she had learned, over six years, that Sebastian Thornbury's face was a landscape you could navigate if you paid attention — that the flatness was not emptiness but weather, that storms moved across it in ways most people never saw. And what moved across it now was not surprise.

That was the thing Wren would carry out of the station, later, turning it over in the dark: Sebastian had not looked surprised. She had looked like a person arriving at a place she had been before. Like the land itself had come back to her.

"Play the first one," Odile said.

"Odile." Sebastian's voice was quiet. "Don't."

"It's data, Sebastian. It's why we're here."

"It's why *you're* here." Sebastian hadn't moved from the doorway. "I'm here because you paid me to drive."

The two women looked at each other across the humming racks, and Wren understood suddenly that the whole expedition had been a question asked in the wrong direction — not *what's in the station* but *what's in Sebastian*, and Odile had spent a fortune to find out.

Voss, with the delicacy of a man stepping between two large animals, touched the terminal anyway.

* * *

The first recording was ambient. Wind, mostly — the enormous wind of the plains, but wrong, Wren thought, wrong in the way everything in the basin was wrong, too rhythmic, too steady, like the land breathing in its sleep. Then voices, and the voices were young, and laughing, and one of them was—

Wren's stomach dropped.

One of them was Sebastian.

Not the Sebastian she knew — this voice was lighter, quicker, a woman talking with her hands even through a microphone, saying, "—tell Ilsa the shear profile's a gift, tell her she can thank me at altitude—" and another voice answering, warm, male, familiar in a way Wren couldn't place until she realized she'd heard its echo in the way Dov sometimes hummed when he worked, a cadence passed from crew to crew like an inheritance.

"That's Kestrel crew," Dov said softly, behind her. "That's— that's Marisol. And that's—" He stopped.

"That's me," Sebastian said.

The recording played on. The young Sebastian laughed at something, made a joke about the fog, said the basin was the most honest weather she'd ever stood in. The male voice — the one Dov hadn't finished naming — said, "Thornbury, if you're wrong about this window, I'm writing you up," and young Sebastian said, "I'm never wrong about windows," and everyone laughed, and the laughter went on a half second too long, the way laughter does when everyone in the room knows the window is marginal and has decided, together, not to say so.

Wren looked at Sebastian's face.

It was doing the thing it never did. It was open. Not soft — Sebastian's face didn't do soft — but unguarded, the way a locked room looks when you find the door standing ajar. She was listening to a dead woman joke about shear profiles and a man whose name Dov wouldn't say, and she was listening like a person hearing her own voice for the first time, and Wren understood, with a certainty that arrived all at once and fully formed, that Sebastian was not remembering this.

Sebastian was *learning* it.

The recording ended. The terminal offered the next file. The next file was titled, in clean administrative type: **LAUNCH REVIEW — FINAL CALL SIGN-OFF — S. THORNBURY**.

"Stop," Sebastian said.

Voss stopped.

The station hummed. The river moved somewhere above them, thirty years of it, patient as arithmetic. Wren became aware that she had taken one step toward the terminal without meaning to — one step, her hand half-raised, her whole body leaning toward the file like a plant toward light — and that Sebastian had seen her do it.

That was the moment. Wren knew it was the moment. Six years of navigating by this woman, six years of trusting the flatness, the silences, the way Sebastian would burn a route rather than speak a name — and here was a door standing open with Wren's own name on the other side of it, because whatever was in that file was about Sebastian, and Sebastian was Wren's person, and people were allowed to have locked rooms, weren't they, weren't they allowed—

Sebastian's eyes came to hers. Gray, level, and underneath the levelness, for the first time Wren had ever seen, something asking. Not *don't*. Something more frightened than *don't*.

*Please.*

Wren lowered her hand.

"Power's unstable down here," Wren said, and her voice came out steady, which she would be grateful for later. "We shouldn't run their hardware cold. Dov, can we put a meter on their supply before we pull anything else?"

Dov looked at her. Dov had been in the crew for the Kestrel name; Dov knew what that file title meant as well as she did. He looked at her for a long second, and then he said, "Yeah. Give me an hour," and turned to the cable runs like a man who had been given an order he'd been waiting all his life to receive.

Odile's gaze moved between them, pleasant and patient and arithmetic. "Of course," she said. "We have time."

They didn't have time. Wren could feel it — in the fog pressing down on the river, in the way the station's hum had deepened since they'd arrived, in the barometer Ilsa had carried down the shaft and now held up without a word, its needle pinned hard against the stop like a small animal trying to escape.

But that wasn't the hook in Wren's chest. The hook was simpler, and it was this: she had chosen, in front of everyone, to protect Sebastian from a file with Sebastian's own name on it. And Sebastian had let her.

And Sebastian had looked, in the half second before she'd looked away — relieved.

Not grateful. *Relieved.* Like a woman whose hiding place had held.

Wren stood in the cold of the buried station, next to the person she trusted most in the world, holding the door shut with both hands — and for the first time in six years, she wasn't entirely sure which of them she was protecting, or what, exactly, she was keeping inside.

Above them, faint through thirty meters of riverbank, the first thunder of the evening rolled across the basin, and it sounded, Wren thought, almost like a voice clearing its throat.
