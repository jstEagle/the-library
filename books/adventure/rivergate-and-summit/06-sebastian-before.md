## 6. Sebastian, Before

The station's lights came up in stages, like something waking.

First the corridor strips, amber and patient. Then the workroom overheads, white and merciless. Then the screens along the far wall, six of them, flickering through boot sequences that had been waiting years for someone to ask. The air tasted of dust and cold metal and, underneath it, the faint mineral smell of the river that ran somewhere above their heads, patient as a creditor.

Sebastian stood in the doorway of the archive room and did not go in.

"You found the master index," Odile said behind her. Not a question. Odile Marchetti stood with her hands in her coat pockets the way she always did, as if the world were a briefing she'd already read.

"The console was warm," Sebastian said. "Somebody kept a trickle of power running. Somebody wanted this place findable."

"Somebody wanted it *findable by you*."

The screens finished booting. A directory unspooled: dates, designations, audio files, video logs. And at the top of the list, in plain institutional type, an expedition name that hit Sebastian like a hand on the back of the neck.

KESTREL-7. FIELD RECORDS. UNRESTRICTED ACCESS.

"Turn them off," Sebastian said.

"No," said Wren.

Wren had come up beside her without Sebastian noticing, which never happened. Wren was the navigator; noticing things before they arrived was the whole job. She stood with her arms crossed, jaw set, and she looked at the screens the way she looked at weather she intended to drive through anyway.

"Wren—"

"Six years, Seb." Her voice was low, even, terrible in its gentleness. "Six years you've flinched at the word *Rivergate*. Dov won't say Kestrel out loud. Ilsa thinks we all took a vow of silence for fun. I have followed you into every ugly sky you ever pointed at, and I never once asked why you stopped pointing at this one." She turned to face Sebastian fully. "I'm asking now."

The screens waited. The river hummed its one long note above them.

Sebastian reached past Wren and pressed play.

* * *

The first file was audio only. Wind, mostly—the particular layered roar of a supercell's inflow, a sound Sebastian knew better than her own heartbeat. Then voices, young and certain, moving through static.

"—inflow's at forty knots and climbing, we're not going to get a better window—"

"Window's not the issue. Radar's showing rotation tightening two miles northeast of the pad. That's *toward* the pad."

"That's why we launch *now*, Marisol, before it eats the corridor—"

Sebastian knew those voices. That was the whole of it, the whole horror: she knew them. Calloway's easy drawl. Marisol Reyes, sharp as a dropped blade. And the third voice, the one making the argument, steady and reasonable and full of that specific confidence that comes from having been right so many times you've forgotten what doubt feels like—

It was her own voice.

She did not remember recording it. She did not remember the day it described. But she knew the cadence, the little upward lilt she got when she'd already decided and was just bringing the room along. She had heard it in playback a hundred times in her twenties, reviewing her own calls, proud of herself.

"—I'm signing off on it. Kestrel-7 launches in twenty minutes. If the meso shifts, we scrub on my authority. Nobody else carries this."

On my authority. Her voice again, sealing it like wax.

Static. Wind. Then, distantly, the sound everyone in the room recognized and no one named: the rising shriek of something enormous deciding where to come down.

The file ended. The next one began automatically. Video this time—grainy, handheld, a tent interior lit by a single lamp. Marisol Reyes sat close to the camera, rain hammering the canvas behind her, and she was crying without any interest in hiding it.

"If anyone finds these," she said, "the launch call was Thornbury's. I want that on record. Not because I hate her—I don't, God help me—but because she'll try to carry it alone, and she can't, none of us can, and—" The image shuddered. "Calloway's hurt. The east vehicle's gone. We're going to try for the ridge at first light. If we don't make it, tell her it wasn't her fault. Tell her until she believes it."

The timestamp in the corner read a date Sebastian had never been able to explain to herself: the date she'd woken up in a Billings clinic with three days missing and a doctor's note recommending rest.

Three files later there was a final entry. No video. Just her own voice again, flat and stripped, recorded somewhere small and quiet.

"This is Sebastian Thornbury. I've retained Dr. Ansel Voss for a full elective wipe, effective tomorrow. Scope: the Rivergate expedition, the launch decision, and the aftermath. I understand the risks. I understand I'll lose more than they're taking out. That's the point. I've read the last entries. Marisol's right—I'd try to carry it, and I'd drown under it, and then I'd be no use to anyone, including the people who are still alive because I got them out of the basin that night." A long pause. Static breathing. "Tell me nothing. If I come looking, tell me nothing. Let me be somebody who hasn't done it yet."

The screen went dark. The room went dark inside Sebastian too—a clean vertical drop, the floor giving way all at once, six years of self standing on nothing.

She had signed the call. She had killed them. And then she had chosen to forget, which was somehow worse, because the dead didn't get to forget.

"Seb." Wren's hand hovered near her elbow, not touching. "Seb, sit down before you fall down."

"I signed it." Her voice came out sanded flat. "I heard myself sign it."

"You also got people out," Wren said. "You said so—you said some of them lived because of you."

"Some of them." Sebastian laughed, and hated the sound. "That's the arithmetic, isn't it? That's the ledger I've been balancing since Billings without knowing there was a ledger."

Dov stood in the corridor with his wrench forgotten in his hand, face gray. Ilsa had both fists pressed to her mouth. They had all known there was a wound. None of them had known Sebastian had cut it out herself and called it healing.

Odile let it stand for exactly as long as it needed to. Then, softly: "Now you know why I needed you specifically."

* * *

Sebastian turned around slowly.

"Say that again."

Odile had moved to the master console, and she stood there with the dark screens reflected in her eyes, and for the first time since the diner she wasn't smiling. That was how Sebastian knew how bad it was. Odile's smiles were armor; without one, she was just a woman who had planned something cruel and executed it well.

"There's a party interested in this station's data," Odile said. "Atmospheric research with applications they don't publish. They funded this expedition down to the last fuel drum. Their condition was simple: the station's archives could only be opened by someone with prior authorized access. Biometric legacy credentials, old Voss-clinic protocol." She tapped her own temple. "The wipe didn't erase your access, Sebastian. It erased your memory of having it. You were the lock and the key in the same body. All I had to do was walk you back to the door."

"The debt," Sebastian said. "The Syndicate. The revised figures. Every wall closing in until Rivergate was the only open road."

"I bought your paper from the Vanes eight months ago," Odile said. "They were delighted to sell. They think in ledgers; I think in doors." She spread her hands, and there it was at last—not quite an apology, but its fossil record. "I knew about the launch call. I've known for four years. I also knew you'd wiped it, and I decided you'd survive learning it better than you survived carrying it. I may have been wrong. I've been wrong before."

"You used a dead crew's grave as a keyhole."

"I used *you*. The crew was already dead." Odile's voice stayed level, which was its own kind of confession. "And before you decide what I am, ask yourself what you'd have paid to know the truth. Because the answer is: everything. It's always everything with you."

The room was very quiet. Above them the river ran its uphill way, indifferent. On the dark screens, faintly, the directory still glowed—two hundred files, a whole buried year, Marisol's face frozen mid-sentence in Sebastian's memory.

Wren stepped up on Sebastian's left. Dov on her right. Ilsa behind. Nobody said anything. They didn't have to; the formation said it for them, the same way it always had, the same way it would in any sky on earth.

"We're leaving," Sebastian said.

"With the data?" Odile asked. "Or without it?"

Sebastian looked at the screens—at the archive that could clear her debt, buy her freedom, fund ten lifetimes of chasing—and felt the old temptation arrive wearing its familiar face, offering her the same bargain she'd taken once before in a clinic in Billings: *forget, move on, be somebody who hasn't done it yet.*

Outside, the wind changed.

It came down the corridor as a pressure shift, a low thud in the ears, and every instrument panel in the station lit at once—not amber now, but red. Ilsa was at the weather terminal in four strides, and the color drained out of her face by degrees, like a photograph developing in reverse.

"Barometric drop of nineteen millibars in six minutes," she said. "That's not a storm cell. That's the basin itself. Something down here is *feeding* it."

Through the station's thick walls, muffled and vast, they all heard it: thunder with no sky to explain it, rolling up from beneath the earth.

And on the radio, crackling through static, a voice with cheerful, unhurried confidence that Sebastian would have known anywhere in the world:

*"Storm chasers in the Rivergate basin, this is Vane recovery flight one. Lovely weather you're having. Stay right where you are—we're coming to collect."*
