## 7. The Syndicate Arrives

I heard the helicopters before I saw them—a stutter in the fog above the ridge line, rotors chewing air that had no business holding anything aloft. The basin's instruments had been dead for two days. Mine still worked, the old analog barometer Dov called "the liar" because it had never once lied to him. The needle was falling like it had somewhere to be.

"They're early," Odile said behind me. She didn't sound surprised. She never sounded surprised. That was the thing about Odile Marchetti—she treated the future like a reservation she'd already made.

I turned. She stood in the mouth of the research station, silhouetted against the emergency lighting inside, and for the first time since the diner she looked less like a rival and more like a woman watching weather she couldn't outdrive.

"How early?" I asked.

"Twelve hours." She checked her watch anyway. "Give or take the storm."

"What storm?"

I regretted the question before it finished leaving my mouth, because I already knew the answer. The station's machinery had been humming under the riverbank for three days now, waking up section by section as Wren coaxed the old systems back online. And the basin had been answering. The fog that wouldn't burn off. The river running uphill in the shallows past camp. The pressure dropping in slow, deliberate steps, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

I'd been telling myself it was coincidence. Storm chasers are professional liars on that one subject. We spend our whole careers insisting the sky doesn't care about us, because the alternative is a kind of knowledge nobody survives intact.

The sky cared. The sky was coming.

"Wren!" My voice cracked across camp. "Kill the station. Everything. Now."

She came out of the access tunnel at a run, headlamp bouncing, and I watched her understand before I finished the sentence—watched her go pale in the green-white glow of the lamp. She'd been the one who wanted to press deeper into the recordings. She'd been the one who said the station deserved to be understood. But Wren had never once confused understanding with permission.

"On it." She was already gone.

Odile hadn't moved. "It won't matter," she said quietly. "The systems are designed to complete their cycle. Your navigator is very good, but she's rearranging furniture in a burning house."

"Then you'd better tell me who's in those helicopters, Odile, because if you say *surveyors* I'm going to put you on the first raft down the river."

She looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere above us the rotor stutter grew louder, then split—two aircraft, maybe three, spreading out along the ridge.

"You know who they are," she said. "You've known since the diner. You just kept hoping the number in your ledger was money."

The Vane Syndicate didn't send enforcers. That was the thing nobody understood about them, the thing that made them worse than any loan shark with a bat. They sent *optimists*. People who genuinely, warmly believed everything was going to work out—for them—and who could smile through anything, including the exact moment the arithmetic stopped being about dollars.

I'd owed them for six years. I'd assumed the debt was financial because the statements said so, because the cheerful monthly letters said so, because Mr. Aldous Vane himself had once shaken my hand at a memorial service and said, *We're patient, Ms. Thornbury. Patience is just faith with a payment schedule.*

But I was a storm chaser with a dead crew and a wiped memory and a basin I'd sworn off, and I had just led a funded expedition straight to the one place on earth the Syndicate couldn't reach on their own.

My debt had never been money. It was me. My eye. My instinct. My ability to read a storm window into Rivergate that no satellite, no model, no hired meteorologist could parse. Odile hadn't bought a guide. She'd bought the key, and the Syndicate had paid for the door.

"They've been funding the expedition since the start," I said. It wasn't a question.

"Through three shell foundations," Odile said. "I told you the money was clean. I never said whose."

* * *

They landed on the gravel bar a mile downstream at 4:17 in the afternoon, in fog so thick the aircraft seemed to assemble themselves out of it—first the sound, then the running lights, then the blunt dark shapes. Four helicopters. Two of them hauled equipment pods. The other two carried people, and I counted them as they fanned out across the bar with the unhurried confidence of a company that had never once been told no by the weather.

Thirty-one. I counted thirty-one, and at the center of them, in a coat too good for the mud, a small round man I recognized from a photograph that had been stapled to six years of cheerful correspondence.

Mr. Aldous Vane.

He walked up the riverbank to our camp like a man arriving at a family reunion, arms slightly open, face lit with genuine delight.

"Sebastian." He said my name like a gift he was giving me. "Look at this place. Look at what you've found. I told you patience would pay off. Faith with a payment schedule—I say that to everyone, don't I? And here we are, and the account is *settled*."

"Nothing here is yours," I said.

"Everything here is mine." He beamed at the fog, at the station entrance, at Wren and Ilsa and Dov standing in a loose line behind me. He beamed at Odile, who gave him a small nod between professionals. "The station, the data, the recordings—and you, of course. You've always been the asset, Sebastian. The rest was bookkeeping." He turned that smile on my crew, and I watched him do the arithmetic on them, the warm, terrible arithmetic of a man deciding what things cost. "Your people can come along, naturally. The Syndicate rewards loyalty."

"The Syndicate buries it," I said.

"Sometimes both. That's what makes it loyalty."

The barometer in my jacket pocket had gone soft and slack, needle pinned below the last marked line. I'd felt pressure like that exactly once before, and I'd spent six years not remembering it. The fog above the ridge had begun to move against the wind—drawing *inward*, toward the center of the basin, the way air moves when something below it is pulling.

The station's cycle. Odile's burning house. And thirty-one optimists standing on a gravel bar in the open.

"We need to move the vehicles," I said. "Now. All of them, up onto Kestrel Ridge, chained down. Everyone off the river flats inside the hour."

Vane laughed—a real laugh, delighted, awful. "There it is," he said. "That instinct. That's what we paid for, right there. Odile, you see it? She reads the sky like a ledger."

"Move the vehicles, Sebastian," Odile said, and for once she wasn't smiling. "I've seen your face do that before. On the Kestrel footage."

* * *

Dov got the trucks halfway up the ridge before the basin closed its fist.

I've secured vehicles in downdrafts, in hail, in rain coming down so hard it registered as a solid. This was none of those. This was the air itself turning traitor—wind arriving from four directions at once, fog peeling off the flats in ropes, lightning walking the ridge line in slow, silent steps, the thunder arriving late and wrong, like an echo from somewhere else. The station's machinery was doing something to the storm, feeding it, tuning it, and every rule I'd spent my life learning was being quietly edited.

Dov was on the winch line at the second truck when the gust hit. Not a gust—a *shove*, a wall of moving air with a solid object in it, some piece of the basin's own debris, and I heard the crack of the truck door slamming on his forearm from forty meters away through the roar.

We got him into the lead vehicle. Ilsa held his arm steady while I wrapped it, and he never made a sound, which was worse than screaming. Dov talked constantly—that was Dov, a running commentary on bolts and bearings and the moral failures of modern fuel injectors. Silence from Dov meant the pain had gotten ahead of his vocabulary.

"Broken," Ilsa said. "Bad one. Maybe two. He's not driving."

"I can ride shotgun," Dov managed, gray-faced, teeth clenched around a grin. "Just point me at the road and don't make me shift."

"Nobody's driving anywhere yet." I looked out the rain-streaked window at the flats below. Vane's people were still down there, helicopters lashed and bucking on the gravel bar, and the fog was coming off the river now in a way I recognized from the inside of my own erased memories—a way that meant the storm window was closing, and closing fast.

Wren put her hand over mine on the wheel. She'd been quiet since the recordings, since the floor gave way under everything she thought she knew about me. She'd had every right to walk into the fog and keep walking.

"Sebastian." Her voice was steady. "Whatever you did six years ago. Whatever's on those tapes." She squeezed. "You're the one who told us to chain the trucks. You're the one who got Dov off the flats. Whatever you were, you're the one who's here."

"The tapes—"

"Are for tomorrow." Her eyes didn't leave mine. "Tonight you're our driver."

Ilsa, wrapping the splint, not looking up: "Tomorrow you're explaining. Tonight you're driving."

Dov, through his teeth, delirious with pain and loyalty: "Tonight you're driving *fast*."

I looked down at the flats one more time. Vane's crews were scrambling now, the optimism finally cracking, helicopters trying to lift in air that wouldn't hold them. One of them lifted, lurched sideways, and I watched the basin take it—not destroy it, just *move* it, set it down on the riverbank like a toy a child had tired of.

And above the ridge, through a tear in the fog, I saw the storm's heart. A wall of rotating cloud, descending, organized, deliberate, centered on the station.

On us.

The window was closing. In maybe forty minutes, everything in this basin would be inside it—Vane's crews, Odile, the station, the truth on those tapes, and my family in a convoy with one working arm between them.

There was one way out. A line through the eastern breaks that I'd read off the land on our way in and never told a soul about, because it was the kind of route that only worked if you drove it once, perfectly, the first time, with no second attempt.

I put the truck in gear.

"Hold on," I said. "All debts come due tonight—and we're paying in speed."
