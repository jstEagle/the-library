## 8. The Choice at Kestrel Ridge

The water came up through the floor of the station like something rising to meet them, patient and certain, and Sebastian understood in one cold flash that the basin had been holding its breath for years and had finally decided to exhale.

"Seventy seconds," Ilsa shouted from the doorway, rain sheeting past her into the corridor. "Maybe less. The river's found the old intake shafts."

Sebastian stood at the archive console with the drive in her hand.

It was small. That was the obscenity of it—small enough to close her fist around, small enough to fit in a pocket and walk out of her own history a second time. Every recording. Every log. The launch call, timestamped, her own voice authorizing it in a register she didn't recognize as hers. The medical consent form, signed S. Thornbury, electing erasure. The whole architecture of the woman she'd built on top of the ruin.

And somewhere above them, rotors were cutting through the storm. The Vane Syndicate had come smiling, the way they always did, and Odile had sold them the door.

"Sebastian." Wren's voice, low, close. "We have to move."

"I know."

"The Syndicate will take the station whether we're here or not. If you've got that drive, we run now, we're clear by dawn, and your debt—" Wren stopped. Sebastian watched her do the arithmetic she'd been doing since the diner: debt cleared, past erased, family intact because the family would never know what they'd carried out of the ground together.

It was a good plan. It was the plan Sebastian had been living inside for six years, just larger.

* * *

She thought about the first time she'd woken up after the wipe. Not the hospital—the van, afterward, Dov's hands on the engine, Ilsa reading instruments that no longer meant anything to her. She'd asked who Kestrel was and watched three faces go carefully blank, and she'd felt the shape of the hole in herself and decided, with the pragmatism that had made her a good chaser and a better liar, that a person could navigate fine without a past. You only needed the sky in front of you.

She'd been running ever since. From the Syndicate, yes. But mostly from the console in front of her.

Above, through the flooding corridor's grate, thunder rolled down the ridge—not weather thunder. Machinery thunder. The station's array was still cycling, agitating the basin's storms into something the plains hadn't seen in a century, and the Syndicate's aircraft were dropping into weather that even Sebastian wouldn't have chased for money.

"Sebastian." Odile stood in the far doorway, soaked, unhurt, infuriatingly composed. Behind her, two of her specialists held cases already packed with copies of whatever they'd salvaged. "The water's at your knees. Decide."

"You knew," Sebastian said. "From the diner. You knew what was down here and you knew why I was the only guide who could find it."

"I knew you'd signed off on the launch," Odile said. No apology in it. There never would be. "I knew you'd cut it out of yourself rather than carry it. Which made you the only person alive whose instincts could read this basin without flinching. You navigated around your own grave for six years, Thornbury. That's a skill money can't buy."

"And the Syndicate?"

"Are buyers. The data has value. You have debts. Everyone leaves this room paid." Odile glanced at the water climbing Sebastian's shins. "Take the drive. Hand it over on the ridge. Walk away clean. It's the only version of tonight where everyone gets what they wanted."

"That's not true," Wren said quietly.

Odile looked at her.

"It's the only version where *she* gets what she wants," Wren said. "The rest of us get to keep loving someone who's still hiding."

The water surged. Somewhere deep in the station, a bulkhead let go with a sound like a whale dying.

* * *

Sebastian looked at the drive.

Six years of invisibility weighed exactly as much as a stick of gum. That was the trick of it—that was what nobody told you about erasure. It didn't cost anything to keep. It cost everything else. Every friendship built on the flattened ground where the truth used to be. Every morning waking up as a woman assembled from spare parts. Every mile of open sky that never once felt like freedom because freedom requires somewhere you're not fleeing from.

Dov appeared behind Odile, arm bound, face gray, leaning on Ilsa. He looked at Sebastian the way he'd looked at her across a hundred storm fronts: waiting for the call. Her call. It had always been her call. That was the disease of it. Six years ago she'd made a call and buried it, and every day since, people had been trusting her judgment without knowing what it had already cost.

"Seb," Dov said. "Whatever you pick. We're with you."

That was the unbearable part. They were with her either way. That was what made silence theft instead of mercy.

She set the drive on the console.

Then she opened the broadcast panel beneath it—the old station's uplink, still live, still hungry, designed to push data to satellites that had stopped listening decades ago but whose successors, she'd bet her life, were listening now. The Syndicate's aircraft overhead confirmed it. Someone had kept the frequency warm.

"What are you doing," Odile said. Not a question.

"Everyone gets what they wanted," Sebastian said. "Just not privately."

She keyed the full archive. Unedited. Timestamps, signatures, her voice making the call, her signature consenting to forget it. Everything the Syndicate wanted to sell in the dark went up into the storm in the light.

"No!" One of the specialists lunged; Wren stepped into his path, small and immovable, and Ilsa put a hand on his chest like a toll gate.

The progress bar crawled. Water hit Sebastian's waist, cold as a confession. On the ridge above, she heard the Syndicate's lead aircraft bank hard—someone aboard realizing that the asset they'd come to buy had just become evidence, public, unownable, worthless to anyone who'd wanted it quiet.

The bar filled. The console died with a sound like a sigh.

Sebastian looked at Odile across the black water. "Your buyers just lost their monopoly. My debt was expertise, you said? Consider my expertise spent."

Odile stared at her for a long moment, rain hammering the flooded corridor between them. Then she did something Sebastian had never seen her do in fifteen years of rivalry: she laughed, short and genuine, almost admiring.

"You absolute disaster," Odile said. "Run."

* * *

They ran.

The basin was coming apart around them—the river reclaiming its lowlands, the fog burning off in sheets as the agitated storms stacked higher, lightning walking the ridgelines on legs of white fire. The vehicles sat where Dov had lashed them before his arm got wrecked, half-submerged, engines drowned.

Except one. Because Dov, half-conscious on a stretcher of tarps, had spent his last good hour rigging the chase truck's intake above the waterline, and when Ilsa cranked it, it caught on the third try with a roar that sounded like forgiveness.

"Everyone in," Sebastian said, and took the wheel.

There was no road anymore. The route they'd driven in on was under two feet of moving river. But Sebastian had spent six years learning this basin without knowing she was learning it—her hands remembered what her mind had surrendered, every arroyo, every shelf of high ground, every place the water couldn't follow fast. She drove by instinct older than her memory, and the truck climbed out of the floodplain as the station's lights went dark below them, swallowed whole.

Behind, the Syndicate's aircraft fought their way back up through weather that had turned hostile, one of them streaming smoke, all of them leaving. Whatever deal Odile had cut, it was void now. Whatever leverage the Syndicate had held over Sebastian, it had gone up on an open frequency along with her worst self.

Ahead, the ridge road. Kestrel Ridge, named for nothing, named for everything, and beyond it the plains opening wide and ordinary under a sky that was finally, finally just weather.

Wren rode shotgun, soaked, silent, watching her.

"You heard all of it," Sebastian said. "The broadcast. My voice. The consent form."

"Yeah."

"I'm not going to ask if you forgive me. That's not mine to ask yet."

Wren was quiet for a long time. Lightning lit her face, and Sebastian braced for the verdict.

"You navigated us out," Wren said finally. "Through that. Nobody else alive could have. So whatever you erased—you kept the part of you that doesn't leave people behind." She looked forward again. "Drive, Seb. We'll do the rest at home. Slow."

Sebastian drove.

Behind them the basin collapsed into itself, taking the station, the recordings' original copies, and the last sealed chamber of her old life down into the water. Ahead there was only road, and rain, and a crew that knew everything now and had gotten in the truck anyway.

For the first time in six years, Sebastian Thornbury was completely visible—and completely free.

Then the radio crackled, and a bright, cheerful voice she knew too well said: *"Well, well. Quite the broadcast, Ms. Thornbury. The Syndicate would love a word about your outstanding balance."*

The storm wasn't done with her yet.
