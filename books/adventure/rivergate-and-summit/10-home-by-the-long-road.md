## 10. Home by the Long Road

We took the long way home because nobody suggested otherwise.

The interstate would have put us back in cell range in four hours, and none of us wanted to find out what waited there. So Dov pointed the convoy west on the old farm roads, two lanes cracked like riverbed, and we let the country unspool at its own pace. Storms stacked up behind us on the horizon all afternoon—green-black towers, fat with rain we'd never feel—and for once I didn't chase them. I just watched them in the mirrors until the light went amber and they sank out of sight.

Dov rode in the back of the lead truck with his arm in a sling and his boots up on a cooler, sleeping in fits. Ilsa had claimed the passenger seat and spent it doing something she called "post-event atmospheric review," which as far as I could tell meant staring at her laptop and muttering. That left Wren beside me at the wheel, navigating by paper map because every instrument in the basin had taught us not to trust them.

"You missed the turn," she said, an hour after there hadn't been a turn to miss.

"I know."

"Okay." She folded the map along a crease that was already soft from handling. "Just checking you knew."

The compass sat on the dash between us. It wasn't mine originally—it had come out of the Kestrel station, off a shelf in the archive room, brass-cased and older than both of us, the kind that didn't need batteries or satellites or anything but the earth's own stubborn pull. I'd picked it up without thinking, the way you pocket something at a funeral, and Odile had watched me do it and said nothing. It pointed north with a small, honest tremble, and I found I liked having it where I could see it. Proof that some things still told you the truth without being asked.

We stopped at dusk outside a town so small the gas station doubled as the post office. Dov ate half a gas-station burrito and pronounced himself healed. Ilsa bought three bags of ice and a newspaper from 2019 and read it cover to cover like scripture. Wren and I stood by the trucks while the sky over the plains did its slow purple business, and I felt the strangest sensation building in my chest, and it took me a while to name it.

It was lightness. The specific, disorienting lightness of owing nothing to anyone.

I kept waiting for the Vane Syndicate's cheerful voice in my ear—*Sebastian! Great news!*—and it never came. The broadcast had gone out raw and unedited to every feed that would carry it: the station recordings, the launch call, my signature on the authorization, my face afterward, gray and hollow, signing the consent forms for the wipe. Everything. The whole ugly architecture of what happened at Rivergate, laid open for the world to walk through.

I'd expected ruin. Instead, by morning, the messages were strange in a new way. Meteorologists who'd spent a decade calling the Kestrel disaster an act of God were rewriting their papers. Two networks wanted interviews. A woman whose sister died on the Kestrel sent me a single line: *Thank you for saying her name.* And somewhere in the noise, a lawyer's letter informed me, with regrettable good cheer, that the Vane Syndicate considered my contract "resolved through alternative consideration," and wished me continued success in all future endeavors.

They'd smiled while they cut me loose. Of course they had. But for the first time, the smile cost them more than it cost me.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stand very still and look at the horizon like it owes you an explanation."

"It usually does."

She bumped her shoulder against mine. Below her collarbone, where the basin fog had gotten into everything, she still smelled faintly of river water. "Come on," she said. "Dov's about to tell the burrito story again. We should be present for it. He counts the laughs."

* * *

We drove into the night because Dov said his shoulder hurt less sitting up, and because none of us said the real reason, which was that stopping meant the trip was over, and the trip being over meant deciding what we were now.

Somewhere past midnight, with Ilsa asleep against the window and Dov snoring in counterpoint, Wren said, quietly, "Tell me about them. The old crew. The parts that weren't on the tapes."

I'd known the question was coming since the ridge. I'd been rehearsing answers I didn't have. Now, in the dark, with nothing between us but the hum of the road, I found I didn't want a rehearsed answer.

"There was a woman named Cass," I said. "Cass Okafor. She ran comms on the Kestrel. She used to say instruments lie but people lie better, so always trust the one that beeps." My throat tightened around it, but I made myself keep going. "She's the one whose name was in the last recording. The one who didn't make it to the shelter. She'd have hated how it happened. She always said if the weather was going to take her, it should at least have the decency to be interesting weather."

Wren didn't say anything. She just drove, hands easy on the wheel, giving me the room the way she always did.

"And there was Tomas. Our meteorologist before Ilsa. The one whose memory went." I watched the compass tremble north. "Everyone assumed the wipe broke him—that he chose it like I did and it went wrong. But that's not what I remember now. What I remember is that he never wiped anything. He just decided to stop talking about it, and then he decided to stop talking altogether, and eventually everyone treated him like he'd forgotten." I shook my head slowly. "He remembered everything. He just couldn't carry it and carry us too. So he set us down."

"That's worse," Wren said.

"Yeah. It is." I turned the compass a quarter-turn on the dash, watched it swing back true. "And there was a kid, Priya, nineteen, first season, who thought chasing storms was the most romantic thing a person could do. She was right, actually. That's the part nobody says out loud. It is romantic. It's also the thing that killed Cass, and broke Tomas, and turned me into someone who signed a piece of paper and paid to forget she'd held the pen."

"Do you remember holding the pen?" Wren asked. "Now? Since the station?"

I searched for it the way I'd been searching all week—in the dark behind my eyes, in the place where the recordings had reattached themselves to me like grafts onto a tree. "I remember the weight of it," I said finally. "Not the moment. Not the decision. Just—the weight. Like my hand remembers even though I don't. Some mornings I wake up and my fingers ache, and I think that's what that is."

"Is that enough?" she asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely wanting to know.

"No," I said. "But it's what I've got, and I'm done pretending there's a fuller version hidden somewhere that would make it okay. There isn't. There's just what I did, and what it cost, and the fact that I'm still here." I looked over at her. "You should know I understand if that's not—you know. If the crew you signed up for isn't the crew you got."

Wren was quiet for a long mile. Then another.

"When I was twelve," she said, "my family's place flooded. Not dramatically. No rescue helicopters. Just water in the house for nine days, and everything we owned going soft and gray. And afterward my mother threw out almost nothing. She dried things out and kept them. Photos with warped corners. Books that smelled like the river forever. I asked her why, and she said—" Wren's voice caught, steadied. "She said, *because it's still ours.*"

She glanced at me, eyes wet in the dashboard glow.

"So no, Sebastian. You're not the crew I signed up for. You're heavier than that. But you're still ours."

I didn't trust myself to answer. I reached over and rested my hand briefly on her shoulder, and she leaned into it for a second, and then we both pretended that was a normal amount of feeling for two people in a truck at one in the morning, and the road carried us on.

* * *

We crossed out of the plains on the third day, up into hills that smelled like pine and cold stone, and the storms finally lost our scent and wandered off eastward. At a overlook above a valley full of morning fog, we stopped and got out and stood together in the cold, five people—four and a half, counting Dov's arm—who had gone down into a place that eats memories and come back carrying more than we'd left with.

Odile was long gone. She'd left before dawn the day after the broadcast, no goodbye, just a note tucked under the wiper of my truck in her sharp, unhurried handwriting: *You were always the better chaser. I was only ever the better liar. —O.* I'd burned it at the first fuel stop, and then, halfway through burning it, wished I hadn't, and stood there watching the ashes go. That felt about right for her. For us.

"So," Ilsa said, breath fogging. "What now? We're famous. Or infamous. There are literally forty-one unread emails asking for your quote, Sebastian. One of them is from a university."

"They can wait."

"The Syndicate—"

"Is someone else's storm now." I said it and felt it land true, like the compass finding north. "Let them chase it."

Dov shifted his sling and grinned. "So we keep the trucks."

"We keep the trucks."

"And the name?" Wren asked. "Rivergate and Summit. People are using it already. The broadcast, the run out of the basin—they're calling it that."

I looked at the word written across everything now—on feeds, in headlines, in the mouths of strangers. Our worst day and our best one, fused into a single name that belonged to all of us whether I liked it or not.

"Keep it," I said. "Both halves. The river and the ridge. We don't get to keep just the part that flatters us. That was the whole problem."

Ilsa wrote it down, because Ilsa wrote everything down. Dov saluted with his good arm. Wren just smiled, small and sure, and got back in the driver's seat, because she'd decided somewhere in the last hundred miles that this was her job now and she was done asking permission.

I took the compass off the dash and held it in my palm. Brass worn smooth. Needle trembling toward home, wherever we ended up defining that. In my other pocket, folded soft as the old maps, the truth—the whole truth, mine now, no longer something I paid storage fees on.

For the first time since I was nineteen years old, I owed nobody anything.

The road bent downhill toward the valley, toward towns with names I didn't know and storms I hadn't met yet, and I got in the truck, and we went.
