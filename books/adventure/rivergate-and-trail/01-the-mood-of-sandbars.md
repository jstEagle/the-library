## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The river told you its mood through the sandbars, if you knew how to listen.

Mirabel Vane knew. Nine years on the Rivergate had taught her the grammar of it — the way a bar's crest went from dun to bone-white when the water dropped below the safe line, the way the current braided itself into thin silver threads over a shoal that would gut an unwary hull like a fish. This morning the bars were pale as old teeth, and the Sparrowhawk's wake curled off them in long lazy question marks.

"Shoal water," she said, mostly to herself, one hand resting easy on the wheel. "Left channel's lying today."

Odji looked up from coiling rope amidships, his brow furrowing. "Lying?"

"They all lie in low water." She tilted her chin toward the left bank, where the current seemed to run smooth and inviting between two gravel spits. "See how it looks deep? That's the sand telling you what it was last month, not what it is now. River doesn't keep promises. It keeps *moods*."

He grunted — Odji's conversations were largely conducted in grunts and shrugs, with occasional words surfacing like carp — and went back to his coils. That was why she kept him. He didn't need her to talk.

The Sparrowhawk slid downstream through the morning haze, her patched sails doing little work in the light air, the current doing most of it. She was a cargo boat of no particular beauty: sixty feet of river-cedar and stubbornness, deck scarred by thirty years of crates and coal sacks, hull caulked with more hope than pitch this season. Her name was painted on the stern in letters that had faded from gold to a color like weak tea. Mirabel had repainted them twice and stopped bothering after that. The river knew who she was. That was enough.

Ahead, the gorge country began to gather itself — hills folding up out of the floodplain, the valley narrowing by degrees, the water quickening as though it too felt the walls coming. Mirabel read the reach ahead the way other people read a page: the leaning dead cedar on the right bank marking the head of Halloway's Bend; the submerged wing-dam below it, invisible but for the boil of confused water; the cutline where the old ferry landing had rotted down to pilings.

She did not look at the cutline longer than she needed to.

"You're standing off," Odji observed, appearing at her elbow with two tin cups of chicory. He handed her one. It was his way of buying conversation.

"I'm steering."

"You're standing off. Three boat-lengths wide of every mark. You could shave Halloway's Bend close enough to kiss it and we both know it."

She took the cup. "Kissing marks costs paint. Paint costs money."

"Costs nothing today. Water's low everywhere. You're not avoiding the sandbars, Mira. You're avoiding the banks."

Mirabel drank her chicory and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer, and Odji had been aboard long enough — four years now — to accept it. He leaned against the rail beside her, a lean dark man gone grey early at the temples, hands like knotted rope. He'd come up from the delta ports with no history he cared to share, which suited her fine. Two people with no pasts made a clean crew.

Behind them, forward of the cargo hatch, Tamsin Corrigan was running the deck crew through the morning's work — though "crew" flattered them this season. There was Tamsin, first mate, red-haired and freckled and built like she'd been assembled from dock timber; there was Odji; there were the Pell brothers, Ferro and Sam, who'd signed on at Coppermouth in spring and whose main qualifications were that they showed up sober more often than not. Five souls and a boat. In better years the Sparrowhawk had carried eight and a full hold of salted fish and ironmongery down from the upper settlements. These were not better years. The Authority's tolls had climbed three seasons running, the paddle-steamers had taken the scheduled freight, and what was left for an independent sailer was the awkward cargo, the urgent cargo, the cargo nobody else would carry at prices nobody respectable would ask.

Which suited Mirabel too. Awkward cargo asked no questions about why its captain never lingered.

The bend came up and she took it without touching the bank, threading the boil over the drowned wing-dam so smoothly that Sam Pell, hauling on the jib sheet, never even noticed they'd crossed danger. Only Tamsin glanced back at the broken water sliding away astern, then at Mirabel, and gave the small nod that meant *I saw, and I'll say nothing, because saying something would jinx it.*

That nod was worth more than wages. Mirabel had learned that too.

* * *

They tied up at noon at the Gullet — not a town so much as an argument between a trading post, a fuel depot, and a tavern, all clinging to the left bank where the river pinched between bluffs. The post's dock was a rickety arrangement of planking and apology, and Mirabel brought the Sparrowhawk alongside it with a touch so soft the fenders barely sighed.

"Two hours," she told Tamsin. "We take on water, take on bread if the price is honest, and we're gone before the afternoon wind fills. I want the Narrows behind us by dusk."

"The Narrows at dusk with this water?" Tamsin pushed her hat back. "Mira, be reasonable. Run the Narrows in the dark on a falling stage and you're gambling the whole boat on your memory."

"My memory's the best-charted thing on this river."

"That's not comforting. That's terrifying. Those are different things."

But she went to see to the water casks, because arguing with Mirabel Vane about the river was like arguing with a mule about walking. Odji went for the bread. The Pells went, inevitably, to the tavern, with instructions — Mirabel's instructions, delivered at volume — that involved the words *two hours*, *sober*, and *I will leave without you*, all of which everyone understood to be negotiable except the last.

Mirabel stayed aboard. She always stayed aboard when they tied up. The boat was safest with someone on it, she told herself, and it was true as far as it went, but it didn't go far enough and she knew it. The truth was simpler and uglier: docks were where questions lived. Docks were where people stood on the planking with their hands in their pockets and watched you make fast, and some of those people had eyes that lingered, and mouths that carried names upstream and down. *Vane,* they'd say. *Sparrowhawk Vane. Doesn't she go anywhere twice?*

So she stayed aboard, in the shade of the tiller canopy, and pretended it was seamanship.

It was nearly an hour later, when the shadows had swung and the bread had come back wrapped in oilcloth, that Odji climbed aboard carrying the bread and something else — a folded newspaper, river-damp, three weeks old.

"Postmaster gave me this," he said. "Thought you'd want the shipping news."

"Shipping news is gossip with numbers."

"Gossip with numbers pays us." He settled onto the hatch coaming across from her, uninvited, comfortable, and unfolded the paper with the care of a man handling something explosive. "Tolls up again at Lock Seven. Steamboat *Coriander* lost a barge string at Widow's Teeth — nobody drowned, small miracle. And—" he paused, and Mirabel, who had spent nine years learning to hear pauses the way she heard currents, felt the water change under her before he said another word, "—there's a piece here about Trail's Landing."

The name went through her like a splinter going in: quick, small, deep, and certain to fester if she worried at it.

"What about it?"

"Rebuilding. Slow. Stilt houses going up where the old town stood. Some relief society out of Rivergate City raising funds." He turned the paper toward her. There was a drawing — one of those engravings that made everything look nobler than it was — showing half-drowned foundations and new houses on poles above them, and a headline in type that had probably been meant to inspire: *THE LANDING RISES AGAIN.*

Mirabel looked at it for exactly as long as it took to appear indifferent. "Good for them."

"Mira." Odji folded the paper slowly. "Four years I've sailed with you. We've stopped at every landing, hamlet, and mudflat from the delta to the head of navigation. Except one." He tapped the engraving. "You'll beat up into the gorge, you'll take the Narrows at night, you'll run cargo past the place in fog, in flood, in ice — but you will not stop at Trail's Landing. Not once. Not ever. I've watched you alter course around that mooring like it's a plague buoy."

"It's a bad harbor. Silting since the flood. Half the pilings are rotten—"

"I've seen the charts. The outer basin's still good deep water. Better than this stick pile we're tied to now." His voice wasn't accusing. That was the worst part. Odji never accused; he only observed, patiently, the way he observed everything, until the observation became unbearable. "So it's not the harbor."

Mirabel set down her cup. She looked out at the river, low and glittering and full of lies, and she reached — automatically, the way a man reaches for a tool worn to the shape of his hand — for a story.

"There was a man," she said.

Odji waited. He was good at waiting. It was half his personality.

"Years back. Before your time. A trader at the Landing — decent sort, or I thought so. We ran together two seasons. Then he took my cargo manifest and my charter book and sold them to a rival line, and I lost a season's profit and very nearly the boat." She shrugged, a small economical shrug she'd practiced until it looked real. "I don't stop where I've been robbed. Bad luck clings to places. Any working pilot knows that."

It was a good story. It had the right shape — betrayal, money, superstition dressed as prudence. It had just enough specific detail to satisfy and not so much as to invite questions. She'd built it years ago, the way you build a false channel mark, and she'd maintained it, and it had held against every current.

Odji looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded slowly, folded the newspaper, and set it on the hatch beside him.

"All right," he said.

"All right?"

"That's the story you're keeping." He stood, gathering the bread. "I won't trouble it."

And he went aft to stow it, leaving Mirabel alone under the canopy with her chicory going cold and the lie sitting between them like a third passenger.

*Keeping.* Not believing. Keeping. As if her stories were cargo he'd agreed to haul without opening the crates.

She stared at the river and hated him a little, and hated herself more, because the truth was sitting right there beneath the waterline of her mind where it had sat for nine years, and she was not going to raise it. She was not going to raise it the same way she never raised the sunken things she passed on this river — the drowned skiffs, the dead men's barges, the whole silent population of the Rivergate's mistakes. You let them lie. That was the bargain you made with moving water: you kept going, and it kept your secrets, and neither of you ever, ever stopped.

* * *

The Pells came back forty minutes late and only moderately sober, and Mirabel charged them each a day's wage for it, which was fair and which they accepted with the resigned air of men who had priced the entertainment beforehand. By mid-afternoon the Sparrowhawk was again in the current, running down the narrowing valley with the wind finally filling in from the southwest, enough to give her canvas a voice.

The Narrows came at dusk, exactly as planned.

It was the finest stretch of piloting on the whole river and Mirabel loved it the way you love a dangerous animal — with respect, with attention, with no illusions about who would eat whom if her concentration slipped. The gorge closed to less than a hundred yards of green water between walls of grey rock, and the current ran hard and deep in the center, and along both sides the eddies turned slow circles that could swallow a boat and hold it, turning, while its timbers opened. There were three rocks in the Narrows that showed at low water and none that showed at high, and there was a ledge off the left wall that no chart had ever gotten right, and Mirabel knew all of it the way she knew the backs of her own hands.

She took them through at speed, because speed was control in the Narrows — the rudder biting, the current flowing honestly past the hull instead of playing games with it. Tamsin stood at the bow calling the leads out of pure ritual, though Mirabel didn't need them. Sam worked the sheets. Ferro watched the walls slide past close enough to touch and made a low continuous noise of religious alarm.

Then they were through, and the gorge opened, and evening came down purple and gold over the widening water, and the tension went out of the deck the way it always did, into jokes and pipe smoke and the smell of supper starting in the galley.

Mirabel held the wheel through the first watch anyway. She liked the night river. At night the banks dissolved and there was only the channel, only the water, only the work — a ribbon of black glass with the stars doubled in it, and her hands on the wheel, and nothing anywhere that knew her name.

"You should sleep," Tamsin said, coming up around midnight with tea. "I have the conn."

"In a while."

"You said 'in a while' yesterday. And the day before. You know what the doctor at Coppermouth said about a woman who doesn't—"

"The doctor at Coppermouth also wears a hat indoors. I weigh his counsel accordingly." But she took the tea, and Tamsin took the wheel, and Mirabel stayed leaning on the rail rather than going below, because below was where the dreams were, and the dreams were the one cargo she couldn't jettison.

From the bow, faintly, she heard Odji's voice, low, talking to Tamsin. She caught only fragments on the moving air:

"—good story, mind. Well-built. Right shape."

"Whose story?"

"The captain's. About the trader at the Landing."

A pause. Then Tamsin, dry as August: "There was no trader."

"No," Odji agreed. "There wasn't."

The river ran on, black and patient, carrying them downstream through the dark, and Mirabel stood at the rail and did not move, and told herself the cold in her chest was just the night air off the water.

Because that was the arithmetic she'd lived by for nine years: keep moving, keep the hull tight, keep the story patched, and the past stays sunk. Every mile downstream was another fathom of water over it.

She was so busy watching the miles go by that she almost missed the light.

It appeared astern, upstream, where no traffic should be at this hour — a white lantern swinging at the end of a dock, burning steady in the dark. And beneath it, riding the current down toward them, came the shape of a small boat: a skiff or gig, running light, oars working, making straight across the river for the Sparrowhawk with the kind of purpose that meant a message, or money, or trouble.

At this hour, at this reach, in this water — it meant trouble wearing one of the other two as a coat.

"Tamsin," Mirabel said quietly. "Light on the stern. Company coming."

Tamsin turned. A long pause. "Who in the hell would be chasing us *up* the river at midnight?"

"That," said Mirabel, reaching for the signal lamp, "is exactly what I intend to find out."
