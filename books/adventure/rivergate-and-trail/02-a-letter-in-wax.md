## 2. A Letter in Wax

The trading post at Halfway Bend announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, drying fish, and the sweet rot of melons going soft in the heat. Mirabel brought the *Sparrowhawk* in against the current with her hands barely moving on the wheel, a touch here, a breath there, letting the river do most of the work. Nine years had taught her that the Rivergate rewarded patience at exactly the moments it seemed to demand violence.

"Starboard a hair," Odji said from the bow, though there was no need. He liked saying it. She let him.

The dock at Halfway Bend was built on pilings driven into a shelf of gravel, and it groaned when anything heavier than a cat walked its length. Mirabel took the lines herself rather than trust the mooring to the post's hired boy, who was eleven if he was a day and spent more attention on the gull he was feeding than on knots. She wrapped the bow line twice around the bollard, tested it, and stood a moment with her palm flat on the rope as if she could feel the boat's pulse through it.

The hull was talking again. She'd heard it all morning below the waterline — a soft, wet insistence, like someone whispering through cupped hands. The pumps had run twice since dawn. Neither time had she mentioned it to Tamsin.

"You're frowning," Odji said, coming down the gangplank with the cargo manifest rolled in his fist. "You only frown at docks."

"I frown at everything."

"Not at sandbars. Sandbars you smile at. I've seen you do it." He squinted up at the trading post, a long low building of grey timber with a veranda sagging like a tired shoulder. "We're not staying long, are we?"

"Long enough to sell the cotton, buy salt, and leave before anyone asks me my mother's name." She said it lightly, and Odji laughed, because he was supposed to, because half the crew believed she was joking and the other half knew better than to say so.

Halfway Bend sat almost precisely midway between Rivergate City and the drowned country above — close enough to Trail's Landing that on clear evenings, the old-timers claimed, you could smell the flood-stink off the flats where the town used to be. Mirabel had never smelled it. She made sure never to be on deck on clear evenings.

* * *

Tamsin found her in the post's back room an hour later, where the factor kept his scales and his ledgers and his opinions about other people's business. The cotton had sold well; the salt was being carried down to the boat in baskets; and Mirabel was drinking tea she didn't want because refusing it would have required conversation.

"There's a letter," Tamsin said.

"For me?"

"Wax seal. Heavy paper. The factor's been sitting on it three days waiting for us to come through." Tamsin set it on the table between them, and Mirabel saw immediately that it was expensive — the kind of paper that got bought in Rivergate City stationers' shops by people who wanted the paper itself to say something before the ink did. The wax was deep green, stamped with a monogram: **C.R.**

Her stomach did something slow and cold, like a stone dropped into still water.

"Cassian Roe," she said.

"You know him."

"Everyone knows him." She turned the letter over once, twice, as if the back might be less dangerous than the front. "He runs salvage out of the lower gorge. Dredging, wrecking, insurance work for the Authority. We've raced cargo twice. He cheats."

"Does he win?"

"He has bigger boats." She picked up the factor's letter knife, then put it down again without cutting the seal. "Why is Cassian Roe writing to me? I'm a cargo runner. I don't wreck. I don't dredge. I keep my nose pointed upstream and my name out of ledgers."

"Maybe he admires your nose."

"Tamsin."

"Open it or burn it," Tamsin said, pulling out the chair across from her and sitting with the finality of a woman prepared to wait out a siege. "But open it or burn it while I'm watching, because either way I want to see your face."

Mirabel cut the wax.

*My dear Captain Vane,* the letter began, in a hand so controlled it looked printed.

*I will come directly to the point, as I understand that is your preference. The Rivergate Authority lost a pay-barge in the upper gorge nine years ago this season — the *Meridian*, carrying wages and specie for the Landing works, sunk in the narrows above Trail's Landing during the flood event. Salvage efforts were abandoned as uneconomical. They are no longer uneconomical.*

*I have contracted with interested parties to raise what can be raised before the monsoon seals the upper gorge. I require a pilot who knows that water. There are perhaps four such pilots alive. You are one of them, and in my estimation the best, whatever your cargo tonnage may suggest.*

*I offer triple your current rates, paid weekly in advance, plus a share of recovered specie should your crew wish to participate in the risk. Work begins within the fortnight. The window closes with the rains.*

*I am aware, Captain Vane, that Trail's Landing lies within the operating area. I am aware of certain stories told about you. I do not trade in stories. I trade in wrecks. Consider that a professional courtesy, and consider my offer seriously. The *Sparrowhawk*'s seams, I am told, are singing.*

*Yours in confidence,*
*Cassian Roe*

Mirabel read it twice. The second time was not for comprehension. It was because her eyes kept snagging on the same sentence — *I am aware of certain stories told about you* — the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

"Well?" Tamsin said.

She laid the letter flat on the table and smoothed it with both hands, as though it might behave better under pressure. "It's a trap."

"It's money."

"It's both. Those aren't mutually exclusive." She stood, went to the room's single shuttered window, and looked out at nothing — the blank grey wall of the factor's storeroom next door. "He knows about the hull. How does he know about the hull?"

"River pilots talk. Shipwrights talk louder."

"He knows about Trail's Landing. He says it like it's nothing — *lies within the operating area* — but he put it in the letter. On purpose. He wants me to know he knows." She turned around. "And the *Meridian*. Tamsin, the pay-barge went down in the flood. The same flood."

"Barges go down in floods. That's what barges do."

"Not that flood." Her voice came out lower than she intended, roughened at the edges. "That flood wasn't weather. It was—" She stopped. Closed her mouth. Opened it again on something safer. "It was a bad season. People died. And now Cassian Roe wants to send me back up there to dig coins out of it, and he thinks triple wages will make me forget which stretch of river that is."

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. She was a broad, deliberate woman, first mate by seniority and temperament, and she had a way of letting silence do her arguing for her. When she finally spoke, it was gently, which was worse.

"The caulking's gone, Mirabel. You've known since the shallows below Kestrel Rapids. I've heard the hull too. We're pumping every six hours in calm water. What happens when we take weather?"

"We avoid weather."

"And the monsoon?"

"Avoid that too."

"You can't avoid a season." Tamsin leaned forward, elbows on the table. "I did the arithmetic last night while you slept. Repairs — proper repairs, new planking below the waterline, recaulked seams, the sternpost looked at — that's two hundred and forty crowns at the yard in Halfway, and they'll want half up front. Our float is thirty-one. Triple wages for even six weeks covers it with margin left over. Wages for the crew. Food that isn't salt fish. A season after this one where you're running cargo instead of running from a sinking ship."

"I know the arithmetic."

"Do you?" Tamsin's voice stayed level, but something underneath it had hardened. "Because from where I stand, you've been doing different sums for nine years. Every time the numbers get tight, you point us somewhere new. Every time a port feels too warm, we're gone before dawn. I've followed you up rivers that don't have names because you wouldn't tell me why we couldn't take the named ones. And I've done it because you're the best pilot on this water and because the crew eats. But this—" she tapped the letter, "—this is the river handing you a bill, captain. Pay it or the river takes the boat."

Mirabel looked at her first mate for a long moment. Outside, faintly, came the sounds of the post waking toward evening — a dog, a hammer, someone laughing at the dock. Normal sounds. Sounds from a world where letters arrived with good news inside them.

"You weren't there," Mirabel said quietly. "At the Landing. When it happened."

"No."

"Then you get to think it's just geography. That's a mercy. Don't spend it on me." She gathered the letter, folded it along its original creases with exaggerated care. "The answer is no. It was no before I opened it. Write that down in the log if you like: offered salvage contract, declined, reasons private."

"The crew's shares—"

"The crew keeps breathing. That's their share." She moved toward the door, then stopped with her hand on the frame, not quite turning. "Triple wages, Tamsin. Men like Roe don't pay triple wages for coin at the bottom of a gorge. Coin sits. Coin waits. Whatever's down there with the *Meridian*, it's worth more than silver to somebody, and I am not going to be the pilot who finds out what."

"That's the most sense you've said all day," Tamsin admitted. "So why do you look like you're already halfway up the gorge?"

Mirabel didn't answer. Because the truth was ugly and simple: some part of her, some part she kept locked below the waterline like ballast, had read the words *Trail's Landing* and felt not only dread but a horrible, magnetic pull — the pull of a wound that wants pressing. Nine years of pointing her bow away from that bend in the river, and one letter had turned her head.

She hated Roe for knowing that. She suspected he hadn't even needed to guess.

* * *

They cast off at first light, taking the ebb of the current downstream toward the lower yards, where Mirabel told herself she would find a shipwright, haggle over patchwork, and piece together another season on borrowed luck. The morning mist lay on the water in long white ropes, and the *Sparrowhawk* moved through it like a needle through cloth.

Odji had the wheel for the first watch, and Mirabel stood at the rail doing nothing, which was the hardest work she knew. Behind them, upstream, the gorge waited with its drowned town and its sealed hold and its nine-year-old dead. Ahead, the yards waited with their bills. She had chosen ahead. She had chosen ahead every single day for nine years, and she could feel, today, how much thinner the choosing had worn.

"Captain." Tamsin came up beside her, voice pitched under the wind. "There's a boat following us out of the Bend. Small. Single sail. Been holding our wake since the channel."

"Fisherman?"

"Fishermen don't follow for two hours."

Mirabel watched the little craft in the mist astern — a dark shape, patient, keeping just far enough back to be deniable. Her hand found the rail and gripped it.

Roe's courtesy, arriving to make sure his letter had been received. Or something else entirely.

"Log it," she said. "And Tamsin — ready the pumps. The hull's singing again."

As if the boat had heard her, the *Sparrowhawk*'s seams gave a long, low groan, and the first dark thread of water welled up between the planks at her feet.
