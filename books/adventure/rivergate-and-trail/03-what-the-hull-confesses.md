## 3. What the Hull Confesses

The shipwright's name was Doss Halloway, and he had hands like river stones — smooth where the work had worn them, hard everywhere else. He came aboard the Sparrowhawk at first light without asking permission, which told Mirabel everything about him before he said a word. Men who asked permission before boarding a working boat were men who hadn't spent their lives around boats.

He went down into the hold with a lantern and a mallet, and for two hours the only sounds were his tapping and the small wet complaints of the bilge pump doing its endless arithmetic.

Mirabel waited on deck, arms folded against the morning chill, watching the trading post of Halfway Light wake up around her. Smoke from cook fires. A barge crew arguing over mooring lines in three languages. The river sliding past, brown and patient, carrying last night's rain toward a sea none of it would ever see.

Tamsin brought her tea without being asked and stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. That was Tamsin's way — she didn't ask questions when she could stand next to you instead.

"He's been down there a long time," Tamsin said.

"Seams take time."

"She's not that bad."

Mirabel looked at her. Tamsin was twenty-six and had the kind of face that made lying to her feel like kicking a dog. "She's bad enough."

Below them, the mallet stopped.

* * *

Doss Halloway climbed out of the hold the way a man climbs out of a grave he's just measured for someone. He set his lantern down on the deck planks with exaggerated care, as if even that weight might be too much for what lay beneath.

"I'll say it plain," he said, "because I can see you're a plain woman, Captain Vane. Your name travels faster than your boat, and what travels with it is that you don't stop anywhere long enough for anyone to look at your hull." He wiped his palms on his apron. "Now I know why."

"How bad," Mirabel said. Not a question. She'd learned years ago that questions gave people room to soften things.

"The garboard seam on the port side has opened near the deadwood. Not sprung — opened. That's old movement, old wood, old caulking gone to powder. You've been pumping her dry with a prayer and a half-horse pump, and she's been letting you because rivers are kind right up until they aren't." He crouched and pressed his thumb into the deck plank, and Mirabel heard it — a faint, wet give, like flesh. "The frames around it are soft. I could push an awl through this oak like bread. And it's not just there. She's worked loose along her whole length. Every season you've run her hard in low water, every sandbar you've kissed at speed, every time you've forced a channel that wasn't meant for a boat her draft — it's all come due."

"She made the gorge run last year," Odji said from the rail. He'd come up when Halloway boarded, drawn the way crew are always drawn to news of their own deaths. "Made it fine."

"She made it fine," Halloway agreed. "Boats do that. They make it fine right up until they make it to the bottom. I've pulled forty hulls off this river in thirty years, and every one of them 'made it fine' the season before." He stood, knees cracking. "Here's my confession, Captain, since we're talking about what confesses: I won't recaulk her. Not patch her — recaulk her, refasten her, replace three frames and a length of keelson. That's six weeks in my yard and money I'd want in advance, because I've seen captains like you before. You'll promise anything to keep moving, and then one morning you'll be gone, and I'll have a half-dismantled boat and a story."

The words landed harder than he probably intended, or maybe exactly as hard as he intended. Mirabel felt them go in under her ribs, precise as a caulking iron.

"What would it cost?" Tamsin asked, because Mirabel's voice had gone somewhere she didn't trust.

Halloway named a figure.

It was not an obscene figure. That was the worst of it. It was the honest price of honest work, and it was more than the Sparrowhawk had earned in the last two seasons combined after wages, fuel, food, and the slow tax of existing that the river levied on everything that floated on it.

Silence took the deck. The river kept moving, indifferent.

"We could run her light," Odji said. "Short hauls. Stay out of the gorge."

"And stay out of the money," Tamsin said sharply. "Half our contracts are upstream. We run short hauls, we earn enough to eat and pump and eat and pump until—" She stopped herself, but everyone heard the end of the sentence anyway. *Until she opens up under us in deep water with a full cargo and no bar in reach.*

Mirabel looked down through the open hatch, into the dim hold where the lantern still burned, lighting the black water that seeped up between her ship's bones. Nine years she'd owned the Sparrowhawk. Nine years of motion, of never being anywhere long enough for the past to find its footing. The boat wasn't a boat. The boat was the argument she'd been having with the world since Trail's Landing: *you can't catch me, you can't make me stay, you can't make me be the thing they say.*

And now the boat itself was turning witness against her.

"Thank you for your honesty," she said to Halloway. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "I'll let you know by tomorrow evening."

Halloway gathered his lantern and his mallet. At the rail he paused. "Captain. One thing, free of charge, since I like your boat even if I don't like her chances. There's a man been asking after you at the post office. Been here three days. Says he needs passage upriver and hears you're the only captain who'll take water nobody else will." He shrugged. "I told him I didn't know you. But you'll be wanting to decide what you tell him before he decides what he knows about you."

Then he was gone down the gangplank, and Mirabel stood very still on the deck of her dying ship, and thought: *the river is closing every door but one.*

* * *

She found him at the end of the post-office dock, because of course she did. He sat on a coil of rope with a leather case open across his knees, drawing. Not writing — drawing, quick sure strokes of a pencil, and when she got closer she saw he was sketching the river itself: the bend below Halfway Light, the way the current frayed white over the shoal, the drowned willow leaning out of the water at an angle that told you exactly how the bank had failed.

"You're Vane," he said, without looking up. "Sparrowhawk. Forty-one feet, shallow draft, twin screws, and a reputation for running channels that make grown pilots cry." He finished a line, blew graphite dust off the page, and looked up. He was younger than she expected — early thirties, sunburned, with the lean, weathered look of someone who'd spent years outdoors and liked it better than rooms. His eyes were brown and very steady. "Ilan Reyes."

"Who told you I'd talk to you?"

"Halloway told me you wouldn't. That's usually how I know someone will." He closed the case. "I need passage into the upper gorge. Past the Narrows, all the way to the headwaters if the season allows. I'm mapping the river."

"Mapping it." She said it flatly. "The Rivergate's been charted for sixty years."

"The river that was charted sixty years ago doesn't exist anymore." He stood, unhurried, and unrolled a section of chart on the rope coil, weighting the corners with pebbles. It was beautiful work — fine linework, soundings in a neat hand, depths marked in dates. "Look. Here's the government survey from nine years back. Here's mine from last season. The channel's moved four hundred yards east below Redstone Bend. The shoal that drowned the *Cormorant* in year five wasn't on any chart, because it didn't exist in year four. The whole middle river is reorganizing itself since the flood." He tapped the chart, tapped again. "Every pilot above Rivergate City is navigating by memory of a river that's gone. People die that way. I'm trying to draw the river as it actually is, before it changes again."

Mirabel looked at the charts longer than she meant to. It was good work. It was the kind of work she'd have wanted done, if she were the kind of person who wanted things for other people.

"Why me? Halfway Light is full of captains who'd take your money."

"Because the ones who'd take my money are the ones who believe the old charts. I need someone who already knows the river lies." He smiled slightly. "Also, I asked about you specifically. You ran the Widow's Teeth at flood stage two years ago with a cargo of live cattle and no fatalities. Nobody else has done that. Nobody else will try."

"That was luck."

"It was reading. Luck doesn't read water." He rolled up the chart. "I'll pay for the passage. Fair rates plus hazard. And—" he paused, and she watched him choose his next words with the same care he gave his linework, "—I understand you have repairs that can't wait. I'd cover those too. Consider it a condition of the contract. I can't have my cartographer's boat sinking under her in the Narrows."

There it was. She should have felt anger — the man had been asking after her, buying information, timing his approach to her desperation like a man fishing a hole he knew held fish. Instead she felt something colder and more useful: clarity. This was what cornered looked like. This was the shape of it. And the only question left was whether the trap in front of her was worse than the one behind.

"Who's paying for your mapping, Mr. Reyes?"

"Nobody. Me. Savings, and the sale of my late mother's house, and whatever I can earn selling charts to the pilot associations." He said it simply, without the little flourish most liars added. "I'm not rich. I'm solvent. There's a difference, and the difference is I can afford your hull and nothing else."

"And why the gorge? The upper river's empty. Nothing up there but ruins and rock."

Something moved behind his eyes — fast, gated, gone. "That's where the river's changing fastest," he said. "That's where the map matters most."

It was a true sentence wrapped around a false center, and Mirabel had spent nine years becoming an expert in exactly that construction. She used that language herself, daily. She knew its grammar cold.

But the hull was open. The money was real. Monsoon season was six weeks out, and after monsoon season the upper gorge would seal itself behind brown water and debris until autumn, and the Sparrowhawk would spend those months rotting at a dock Mirabel couldn't pay for, going nowhere, being somewhere — being *findable*.

"Terms," she said. "You ride as passenger, not crew. You follow my orders on the water without discussion. If I say we turn back, we turn back, and you don't get your money back for the miles we didn't run." She held up a finger. "Second. Whatever you're really doing up there — and you are doing something, so don't insult us both — if it puts my crew or my boat at risk, the contract dies on the spot and you swim. Third. Repairs are paid direct to Halloway, today, before we cast off. I don't take promises upriver."

Reyes considered her for a long moment. On the dock behind him, the trading post went on with its morning — crates, voices, the smell of tar and frying fish — and Mirabel felt the strange doubled vision that had haunted her for nine years: the world proceeding normally while underneath it, unseen, the water rose.

"Agreed," he said. "All of it." Then, as they shook hands, his grip dry and firm: "One term of mine, Captain. When we pass Trail's Landing, you don't have to stop. But I'd take it kindly if you didn't pretend the place isn't there."

Her hand went stiff in his. She made herself not pull it away.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," Ilan Reyes said gently, gathering his charts. "I imagine you don't. Shall we say Halloway's yard at noon?"

* * *

They signed papers in the post office among the flypaper and the pigeonholes, and by the time the ink dried, the repair money had changed hands and Doss Halloway's crew was swarming the Sparrowhawk's hull like ants on a fallen fruit. Mirabel watched from the dock as they hauled her out of the water — her boat, her nine years, her whole strategy of survival — and hung her naked above the mud, seams gaping, ribs showing, every hidden weakness suddenly public.

Tamsin found her there. Stood beside her. Didn't ask.

"We're going into the gorge," Mirabel said. "Up past the Narrows. Under contract to a stranger who knows more about us than he's telling."

"Roe's offer was triple wages," Tamsin said carefully. "This is just passage money."

"This is passage money and a seaworthy boat, which Roe never offered, because Roe wants us desperate, not healthy." Mirabel turned from the sight of her ship in the slings. "We're taking Reyes's terms. And when we're up there—" she stopped.

"When we're up there," Tamsin prompted.

Above the trading post, the sky had begun to change. Clouds building in the southwest, fat and blue-black, weeks ahead of any season Mirabel remembered. The river below the dock slid past, higher than it had been yesterday. Higher than it should have been.

"When we're up there," Mirabel said, "we do the job, we keep our heads down, and we come home. That's all. That's the whole plan."

Tamsin looked at her the way she looked at damaged cargo — assessing, deciding whether to argue or lash it down. She chose to lash it down. "I'll tell the crew."

She went, and Mirabel stayed on the dock alone, watching the clouds build over the direction they'd be sailing, and felt the river rising beneath everything, patient and certain, coming for all of it — the boat, the name, the lie she'd lived inside for nine years.

At noon, they'd cast off. Upstream of everything.
