## 4. Terms of Passage

Ilan Reyes came aboard at first light, when the mist still lay on the Rivergate like a sheet drawn over a body, and he brought with him a leather case, a roll of banknotes bound in oiled paper, and the particular patience of a man who had already decided how a conversation would end.

Mirabel met him in the stern, where she could keep the water in view. She'd learned long ago that negotiations went better when she could see an exit.

"You slept on it," Ilan said.

"I slept on my boat. The thinking was separate." She poured coffee from the dented pot into two tin cups and handed him one, mostly so her hands would have something to do that wasn't counting his money. "You want passage into the upper gorge. That's forty miles of water I haven't run in nine years, most of it through country where the river changes its mind every season. You're offering to fix my hull for it."

"Recaulk and re-seam, new garboard strake on the port side, ironwork refastened. Your shipwright gave me his list." He sipped the coffee without flinching at its bitterness. "I've already paid him a deposit."

"That was presumptuous."

"That was efficient. You'd have said no if you had time to think, and you don't have time. Your hull has maybe six weeks left in it, and monsoon closes the gorge in five."

The truth of it sat between them like a third passenger. Mirabel looked past his shoulder at the trading post's gray palisade, the moored barges, the gulls picking at the mudflats as the river dropped another handspan overnight. Low water everywhere. Everything exposed this time of year — sandbars, snags, sunken things. She'd built her whole life on the principle that nothing stayed hidden forever; she'd just planned to be somewhere else when her own secrets surfaced.

"What's in the gorge?" she asked. "For you. Cartography doesn't pay for garboard strakes."

"Maps pay poorly," he agreed. "But the gorge is unmapped since the flood changed its course. Every chart older than nine years is a lie now. Someone has to draw the truth." His eyes were steady, brown, unremarkable, and entirely too calm. "You know something about old charts being lies, Captain Vane."

"Do I."

"Everyone who runs this river does. It's not an accusation. It's a trade."

She set down her cup. "Here are my terms. You ride as passenger, you work as crew. You don't touch the wheel, you don't give orders to my people, and you don't ask me about Trail's Landing."

A flicker crossed his face — not surprise exactly. Confirmation. "I haven't mentioned Trail's Landing."

"You didn't have to. Nobody pays to go up there unless they've got business with what happened. And nobody looks at me the way you do unless they think they already know the story." She stepped closer, close enough to smell the ink and damp wool on him. "So we understand each other. You're buying distance, not conversation. If you start asking questions I don't like, I'll put you ashore wherever we happen to be, repairs or no repairs, and you can walk your maps home."

"And if I ask questions you do like?"

"Then I'll answer them," she said, "and hate myself after."

He almost smiled. He put out his hand, and she took it, and his grip was dry and firm and held on perhaps half a second longer than a bargain required.

"One more thing," he said, releasing her. "Roe's contract. Brack will come aboard today with the salvage gear. Triple wages, bonus on recovery. Are you taking it?"

She had known this question was coming since dawn, had turned it over all night like a stone with something alive under it. *Take the job,* Tamsin had argued, and Tamsin was rarely wrong about money. *The hull is dying, Odji hasn't been paid proper in two months, and Roe's gold spends the same whether you like him or not.*

"It's only business," Mirabel said.

"Yes," Ilan said mildly. "It usually is, right up until it isn't."

* * *

Brack arrived mid-morning with two hired men and a barge-load of gear: diving suits with brass helmets gone green at the seams, air hoses coiled like sleeping snakes, winch chains, grappling irons, a small crane bolted to a sled, crates of blasting powder stenciled with the Rivergate Authority's mark. Mirabel watched it all come over the rail and felt the Sparrowhawk settle lower in the water, heavier already before they'd even cast off.

"Where do you want it?" Brack asked. He was a wide man with a foreman's habit of standing too near, and he smelled of tar and chewing leaf.

"Away from my galley. Away from my rigging. And the powder goes in the forward hold, lashed, nowhere else."

He looked at her a moment too long, the way men did when they were deciding how much obedience a woman captain required. Then he nodded and shouted orders at his men, and the loading began.

Tamsin found Mirabel at the rail an hour later, watching the last crates swing across. Her first mate had a manifest in one hand and an expression Mirabel distrusted on principle.

"We're rich," Tamsin said. "Or we will be, if the wreck cooperates."

"We're solvent. Rich is different."

"Solvent's a word I can live on." Tamsin leaned beside her, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd stood through nine years of weather. "You want to tell me why you said yes?"

"Because you were right about the money."

"I'm always right about the money. That's not what I asked."

Mirabel watched Odji below, showing one of Brack's men how to stow chain without fouling the bilge line, patient and careful, a boy who deserved wages more than she deserved her pride. "Because the hull dies without it. Because winter's coming and the crew eats either way. Because Cassian Roe paying triple to raise a pay-barge above Trail's Landing is strange enough that I'd rather see it from inside than wonder about it from downstream."

"So it's curiosity."

"It's arithmetic."

"Mm." Tamsin folded the manifest into her pocket. "Arithmetic. Is that what we're calling it when you steer toward the thing you swore off? Because I've been keeping books on this crew for six years, Captain, and I've never once seen you take a contract out of curiosity. Fear, yes. Pride, twice. Hunger, often. But curiosity would be new."

"Then log it as hunger."

"For what?"

Mirabel didn't answer. Below them, the last of the powder crates disappeared into the hold, and the Sparrowhawk rocked gently against her lines, patient as ever, carrying whatever Mirabel asked her to carry without complaint. Nine years of that. Nine years of asking.

"Tamsin," she said finally. "If I told you there was a place I'd sworn never to go back to, and going back might cost me everything I've built — would you tell me to stay away?"

Tamsin considered it honestly, which was the trouble with her. "No. I'd tell you that places don't cost anything. People do. And I'd ask which you were more afraid of — the place, or finding out you can survive it." She pushed off the rail. "We cast on the afternoon tide?"

"Afternoon tide."

* * *

Ilan stood at the trading post window with his leather case open on the sill, adding a line to the chart he kept rolled inside it. The river's new channel, surveyed over three seasons, ran where the old charts showed solid bank. The old town landing — Trail's Landing — lay beneath four feet of water and nine years of silt, though the charts still drew it proudly, a lie repeated until it looked like truth.

He wrote in the margin, small and precise: *Vane. Daughter of Emeric Vane, engineer of record, sluice works, upper gorge. Does she know? Does she suspect? Watch.*

He thought of her hands on the wheel yesterday, bringing the Sparrowhawk through the shallows — the same economy of motion he'd seen described in his brother's letters, the same way of reading water as if it were text. *There's a pilot upstream of the Landing,* Joss had written, the last spring of his life, *a girl named Vane who reads the river like scripture. If I ever learn to run water half that well—*

The letter had stopped there. Everything of Joss's stopped there.

Ilan closed the case and buckled it. He had spent four years and most of his inheritance buying his way toward this gorge, and now the current was finally carrying him, and the instrument of his arrival was a woman lying to herself with remarkable discipline. He could hear it in her voice — *it's only business* — the sound a person makes when the truth is too heavy to lift and they've decided to drag it instead.

He was sorry for that. Genuinely. But sorry wasn't a currency anyone accepted here, and the ledger at the bottom of the gorge wouldn't read itself.

Below, on the dock, the crew of the Sparrowhawk cast off her lines. Brack's men secured the salvage sled. The wind had come around to the southeast, warm and wrong for the season, and the sky over the upper gorge had gone the color of a bruise coming up.

Ilan watched the boat turn upstream, toward the country that had taken his brother, and touched the case once, lightly, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it still hurts.

* * *

They made the narrows by dusk, and Mirabel felt the change in the water before she saw it — a different voice in the current, a colder note against the hull. Upstream country. She hadn't breathed this air in nine years and her body remembered it anyway, the way a scar remembers rain.

Odji came up to the wheelhouse with the evening tea and stood beside her, looking at the darkening gorge walls.

"Captain," he said. "The new man — Reyes. He asked me today how long you'd been running the Rivergate."

"What did you tell him?"

"Nine years. And then he asked where you ran before that." Odji blew on his tea. "I told him I didn't know. Which is true. None of us know. Six years I've been with you, and you've never once said."

The wheel was smooth and worn under her palms. Ahead, the first storm-light flickered deep in the gorge, silent lightning behind the ridgeline, heat lightning, monsoon gathering itself somewhere over the high country like a debt waiting to be called in.

"There's nothing to say," Mirabel said. "Before the Sparrowhawk, I worked other boats."

"That's not what he asked."

"No," she agreed quietly. "It isn't."

Odji drank his tea and didn't press, because he was young and kind, and kindness knew when to leave a person alone. He went below, and Mirabel stood alone at the wheel with the river pulling her northward, toward the Landing, toward the coffin they called a pay-barge, telling herself the oldest lie in her inventory, the one she told so often it had nearly worn smooth:

*It's only business. It's only money. It's only a wreck.*

Behind her, far downstream, the first true thunder rolled along the gorge walls like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and the rain began — soft at first, then steadier, falling on water that had been waiting nine years to give up what it held.
