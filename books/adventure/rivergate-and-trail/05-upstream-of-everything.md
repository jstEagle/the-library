## 5. Upstream of Everything

The gorge began the way grief begins — not all at once, but with a narrowing.

Mirabel felt it before she saw it: the current stiffening under the hull, the banks drawing in like a held breath, the water going from brown to a hard, glassy green. Nine years since she'd steered anything upstream of Marker Forty. Nine years, and her hands remembered it anyway. That was the cruelty of rivers. They forgave nothing and forgot nothing, and they expected the same of you.

"Stand off that point," she said, though no one had asked. "There's a rock shelf runs out under the willows. You won't see it till you're on it."

Tamsin relayed the order forward without looking at her twice. Good crew did that — took a pilot's warnings as weather, as fact, as gravity. Odji stood at the bow rail with the lead line coiled in his fist, and every few minutes he swung it out and called the depth in his flat, unhurried voice, and Mirabel folded each number into the map she kept behind her eyes.

Behind her, at the rail by the wheelhouse, Ilan Reyes was watching the country go by.

She'd caught him at it three times since dawn. Not watching the river — anyone watched the river. Watching *her*. The way his gaze came back to her hands on the wheel, to her face when the banks pinched tight, patient and cataloguing, like a man reading a document he'd already memorized and checking for alterations.

She put him out of her mind and put the Sparrowhawk into the first real rapid.

* * *

It was water she could have run blindfolded, once. That was the thing she'd never told anyone: not that she'd avoided Trail's Landing because she feared the place, but because she knew it too well. Every eddy above it, every seam of slack water below the bluffs, every rock that showed its head in August and drowned its crown in April. A pilot's knowledge was a kind of intimacy, and she had been intimate with this stretch of river the way you are intimate with a house where something terrible happened — you know which floorboard creaks, which door sticks, which window looks down on the yard where they buried the dog.

The gorge walls rose now, red shale streaked with white mineral tears, and the river ran fast and deep between them. The Sparrowhawk climbed it in long laboring beats, her old engine turning over like a man climbing stairs who pretends he isn't winded.

Memory came up on her the way snags come up on a boat in flood water — no warning, just suddenly there, right under the keel.

A burning warehouse. She hadn't thought of it in years, and here it was, whole and lit: the chandlery on the Landing's north bank going up one October night, flames standing straight off the roof in the windless dark, orange reflected double in the river so the fire seemed to burn underwater too. She'd been fourteen. Her father had carried her on his shoulders through the crowd so she could see over the men, and she'd felt his laughter through his chest more than heard it — *nothing to be done for it but watch, little bird* — and the whole town had watched together, shoulder to shoulder, warm-faced in the cold. It had seemed then like the worst thing the river town would ever see.

Her father's hands on a wheel. Bigger memory, older: the wheel of the *Maren's Luck*, the little packet boat he piloted before the Authority bought him a desk. His hands were broad and scarred at the knuckles, and he steered the way he did everything, without flourish, without hurry, correcting small and early so the corrections never showed. *Anyone can fight the river, Mira. The trick is agreeing with it before it insists.* She'd built her whole life on that sentence and told herself it was hers.

And a gate. Just a gate — iron, tall as two men, set in a concrete throat where the river narrowed above the town. She'd walked past it a hundred times as a girl. It had always been shut. She'd never wondered much what it opened onto, the way you don't wonder about the bones in a wall.

Mirabel realized her grip had gone white on the spokes and made herself loosen it, finger by finger.

"You've run this water before."

Ilan. He'd come up beside the wheelhouse without her hearing him, which on her deck was nearly impossible, and that alone annoyed her enough to answer honestly.

"I ran everything, once. Rivergate to the headwaters and back, four seasons." She fed the wheel a half-spoke as the channel bent around a fallen cottonwood. "Then I got particular about my routes."

"And this route fell out of favor?"

"Routes don't fall out of favor. Pilots do." She glanced at him. "You paid for passage into the gorge, Reyes, not conversation."

He smiled — that mild, unbothered smile that made her want to put him ashore on a sandbar with a week's rations. "I'll stand somewhere else."

He didn't. He moved exactly far enough to be polite and stayed there, at the corner of her eye, watching the water and watching her read it, and after a while she understood that he wasn't studying her skill. He was studying her recognition. Every time the river showed her something she knew, something crossed her face before she could school it, and he was collecting those somethings the way other men collected coins.

* * *

They made the Narrows by midafternoon, where the gorge pinched to less than a hundred yards and the current ran like spilled mercury. This was the water that decided whether you were a pilot or a passenger. There was no line through it you could take twice; the river rearranged itself nightly, moving gravel bars like furniture, and you read it fresh or you read it wrong.

Mirabel read it fresh.

She stood at the wheel with the engine telegraphed to slow-ahead and let the current do most of the work, feathering the Sparrowhawk along the tongue of green water where the deepest channel ran, using the boils and the back-eddies the way a climber uses handholds. Tamsin stood silent at her shoulder, which was how Tamsin said *I trust you*, and Odji called depths from the bow, and for eleven minutes there was nothing in the world but water and wood and the next hundred yards.

Coming out the far end, into the sudden widening calm, she heard Ilan exhale behind her — not relief. Something closer to satisfaction.

"That marker," he said quietly, pointing to a rusted iron post on the left bank, leaning drunk against a boulder. "Forty-two. Your charts stop at forty."

"My charts stop where I stopped."

"Why?"

The question landed clean between her shoulder blades. She kept her eyes on the channel. "Because I found better work downstream."

"Mm," said Ilan, in the tone of a man filing a document.

* * *

They tied up that night in a cutbank hollow three miles above the old town — close enough that Mirabel chose it deliberately, wanting the last approach in full light, wanting to arrive rather than creep. Supper was beans and salt pork on the foredeck, and the crew ate in the easy silence of people who'd worked hard and were paid for it, and the gorge stood over them black and enormous, full of river sounds.

Odji sat down across from her with his plate. He was the only one aboard who ever did that uninvited; he'd been with her six years and had earned a kind of diplomatic immunity.

"Water's talking tonight," he said.

"It's always talking."

"Not like this." He nodded upstream, toward the dark. "High water coming early. You feel it? River's already up a foot from yesterday. Monsoon's ahead of schedule, and when it comes down this gorge, it'll come like a debt collector."

"We'll be done and gone before it collects."

Odji chewed. Looked at her. He had a way of looking at people that made lying feel like a waste of good material. "You knew this water," he said. "Back before. I can tell. You read it like your own handwriting."

"Every pilot reads water."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Other pilots read water. You *greeted* it. Back at the Narrows, before that big hole below the second bend — you flinched. Little flinch, right there." He touched his own cheek. "Like seeing an old dog that bit you once."

Mirabel looked out at the black river. Somewhere upstream, past the dark bends, a gate stood open or shut over drowned ground, and a town of stilt houses held people who would know her name.

"There was a fire," she said, "when I was young. Warehouse went up on the north bank. Whole town turned out to watch." She gave him the true story, the safe story, the one that cost nothing. "I haven't been back. Rivers change. Didn't figure I'd know the place anymore."

Odji listened the way stone listens. When she finished he scraped his plate and stood, and paused beside her.

"The river doesn't change that much," he said. "Not the parts that matter."

He went aft. Mirabel stayed where she was, listening to the water slide past the hull, and let herself look upstream at last, toward the place the dark was thickest.

Fragments again, unasked-for: the gate, iron and dripping. Her father's hands — not on a wheel this time, but on paper, signing, his pen pressed so hard it tore. And the warehouse burning, except in this version the crowd wasn't warm-faced, the crowd was screaming, and the fire was on the water, and the water itself was the thing burning through the town—

She stood up sharply. Enough. Tomorrow she'd moor below the ruins and face whatever faces were left to face, and she'd do it as a professional doing a job for pay, and when it was done she'd take Roe's money and point the Sparrowhawk's bow downstream and never —

"You should sleep," Tamsin said from the wheelhouse door. "Big day tomorrow."

"In a minute."

Tamsin lingered. "That man Reyes asked me today how long you'd crewed the upper river. Before your time, he said. Wanted to know if you'd worked out of Trail's Landing."

Mirabel turned. "What did you tell him?"

"Told him your history's your own." Tamsin's jaw was set. "But Mira — he didn't ask like a curious man. He asked like a man checking sums."

Downstream, faint on the night air, came a sound Mirabel hadn't heard in nine years and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a voice from a dream: the low, carrying toll of the old Landing church bell, rung by some survivor's hand, marking the hour over water that had taken everything else.

Tomorrow, the town.
