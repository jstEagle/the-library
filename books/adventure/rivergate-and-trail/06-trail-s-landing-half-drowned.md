## 6. Trail's Landing, Half-Drowned

The gorge opened like a throat.

Mirabel felt it before she saw it — the way the current leaned against the Sparrowhawk's hull, patient and enormous, the way the canyon walls rose on either side until the sky was a gray ribbon stitched with rain. Nine years. Nine years of running water everywhere else in the world, and now she was pushing upstream into the one stretch she had sworn off, and the river seemed to know it. It pushed back harder here. Or maybe that was only her hands on the wheel, sweating through her gloves.

"Half a point to starboard," Odji said from the bow, sounding the depth with his pole. "Shoaling on the left. There's a house down there — I can see a roof."

"I see it." She didn't look. She couldn't look yet. The drowned roofs of Trail's Landing were things she had rehearsed not seeing for nine years, and now they were rising out of the brown water on either side of her channel like questions she'd refused to answer.

The town had been built on a bend where the river widened and slowed, a place for barges to tie up and take on timber. When the flood came, it hadn't washed Trail's Landing away. That was the cruelty of it. The water had simply risen over it and stayed — not a disaster, an amendment. The river had taken the ground floor of the world and kept it, and the people who survived had climbed.

They lived there still. Mirabel could see them as the Sparrowhawk crept past the old warehouse district: stilt houses on the high ground above the flood line, ladders instead of staircases, walkways strung between rooftops like rigging. Laundry flapped between two chimneys. A man stood on a roof repairing shingles. Smoke rose from a stovepipe, ordinary as morning anywhere else on the river.

"They never left," Tamsin said quietly beside her.

"People don't leave," Mirabel said. "They just move upstairs." She heard how flat her own voice came out and hated it.

The mooring Roe's charts specified was off the old customs wharf, or what remained of it — pilings standing in eight feet of water, the wharf deck long since rotted or stripped. She brought the Sparrowhawk in slow, backing down against the current, and Odji threw the lines to figures waiting on a floating dock that bobbed at the base of a ladder. The whole time, she kept her eyes on her hands, on the wheel, on the fenders. Anywhere but the shore.

She failed anyway. A woman came down the ladder to the floating dock — broad-shouldered, gray-streaked hair tied back with wire, moving with the economy of someone who had spent nine years hauling her life up and down those rungs. Mirabel knew the walk before she saw the face. Some people you could go nine years without seeing and still know by the set of their shoulders.

Maren Oduya stopped at the edge of the dock, took the heaved line, made it fast to a cleat with two efficient turns — and then looked up, straight into the wheelhouse, straight through the glass, straight into Mirabel.

For a moment neither of them moved. The rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere below, Brack was shouting about fenders.

Then Maren Oduya spat. Not at the deck. At the name painted on the Sparrowhawk's bow — *Sparrowhawk, Mirabel Vane, master* — a thin arc of contempt that the rain immediately began to wash away, though everyone on the dock had seen it, and so had Mirabel, and so had Mirabel's crew.

She turned away from the wheelhouse window and found Tamsin watching her with a question she didn't ask.

"Get the gear staged," Mirabel said. "We dive at first light."

* * *

Tamsin Corr had worked cargo boats since she was fourteen, and she had learned early that a crew reads its captain the way sailors read weather: not by what's said, but by pressure, by the direction things lean. And everything aboard the Sparrowhawk had been leaning wrong since they'd passed the first drowned rooftop.

She went ashore that evening because somebody had to, and because Mirabel wouldn't. The captain had barely spoken since the spitting, had eaten nothing, had spent three hours alone in the wheelhouse with the chart of the wreck site unrolled in front of her and her finger resting on it without moving, like a woman touching a scar to check whether it still hurt. It did. Tamsin could see that from across the cabin.

So Tamsin climbed the ladder.

The stilt houses sat on the last shelf of high ground, and the walkways between them creaked under her boots like ship's decking. Children watched her from a doorway — three of them, none older than ten, all born after the flood, all staring at her the way children stare at anything that comes up out of the water. An old man mending a net nodded at her without warmth. Word traveled fast in a town this size. She was from the boat. The boat was from the company. And the company's money was what had brought them here.

Maren Oduya found her at the end of the walkway, where it dead-ended at a rail overlooking the flooded streets below. The water lay dark and flat between the rooflines, and here and there a lamp burned in an upper window whose lower windows were permanently, hopelessly drowned.

"You're the first mate." Not a question.

"Tamsin Corr."

"Maren Oduya." The woman came to stand beside her at the rail. Up close she was younger than her hands suggested, fifty maybe, with a face like a river stone — something hard that water had worked on a long time. "You've come to raise the coffin."

"The pay-barge. Yes."

"We call it the coffin." Maren said it without drama, the way you'd report a depth sounding. "Forty-one people died on that barge when she went down. Not townsfolk — Authority men, clerks and guards, running the payroll out ahead of the flood they claimed surprised them." Her mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "It didn't surprise anyone who lived here. We'd been telling the Authority for a month the river was going to come up. They told us the gates upstream would hold. Then the gates opened, and the payroll barge sank in the harbor basin with forty-one men locked below, and the Authority called it an act of God."

"Locked below?"

"That's what the survivors said. Men hammering on the hatches from underneath while the water came in. The Authority said panic makes people say things." Maren turned and looked at her, and her eyes were the flattest thing Tamsin had ever seen on a living face. "Your captain knows this river well. She ran cargo through here often enough, before."

"She doesn't talk much about before."

"No," Maren said. "I expect she doesn't."

Below them, in the drowned street, something shifted — a fish, or a door swinging on nine years of rust — and the sound carried across the water with terrible clarity.

"Why does your captain hate this town?" Maren asked. "Or is it the other way around? Because I'll tell you what I know, First Mate Corr, and then you can decide whether to tell me what you know. Nine years ago, the night the gates opened, someone sent a warning downriver. A boat, running the flood ahead of the water, banging on doors, getting people up to the high ground. That boat saved maybe thirty lives. Maybe more. Nobody ever learned who was piloting her. But the boat was a Vane boat, and the pilot handled that floodwater like nobody alive could handle it — except, folks said, a Vane. And then Mirabel Vane never came through Trail's Landing again. Not once. Nine years." Maren's voice stayed level the whole way through, which somehow made it worse. "So either she saved us and can't bear to be thanked, or she knows why the gates opened and can't bear to be asked. And I have spent nine years wanting to know which."

Tamsin looked out at the drowned street, at the lamplit upper windows, at the coffin lying somewhere beneath all that dark water with forty-one men in it.

"I've sailed with her six years," she said finally. "She's the best pilot on this river and the worst liar, and she's never once told me why we don't stop here. Tonight's the first time I've heard any version of it at all."

"And which version do you believe?"

Tamsin thought about Mirabel's finger resting motionless on the chart. About the way she'd flinched — actually flinched — at the first sight of the stilt houses.

"I believe she's afraid of this town," Tamsin said. "And I've never once seen her afraid of water."

Maren Oduya was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "Bring her up the ladder tomorrow. Not to apologize. I don't want apologies. I want to watch her face when she stands on ground her family helped drown." She turned and walked back along the creaking boards, and paused once, without turning around. "Dinner's at the big house if your crew's hungry. We feed people here. Even ones we spit at. Especially those."

* * *

Mirabel didn't sleep. She sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low and listened to the rain thicken, and at some point Tamsin came in dripping and told her everything — the coffin, the forty-one men, the hatches hammered from below, and Maren Oduya's story of the warning boat that ran the flood.

Mirabel listened without interrupting. When it was done she said only, "She remembers the boat."

"Everyone remembers the boat, apparently. It's the one good story this town has." Tamsin hung her wet coat on the peg. "Cap. Six years I've followed you into water most pilots won't look at. You don't owe me the truth. But you should know the town already has half of it, and half a truth is heavier than a whole lie. It sinks faster."

Mirabel looked at the chart. At the little penciled cross marking the wreck, sitting in the harbor basin like a period at the end of a sentence nine years long.

"First light," she said. "We dive at first light."

But when Tamsin had gone, Mirabel took out the oilskin packet she had carried since she was nineteen years old — the one thing she had saved from her father's effects, the thing she had never shown another living soul — and held it over the lamp without opening it, watching the light glow through the oilcloth like something drowning.

Down in the black water of the harbor basin, the coffin waited. And somewhere above it, on the high ground, a woman who had every right to hate her was keeping dinner warm.

The rain came harder. Mirabel put the packet away unopened, and did not sleep, and at first light the gorge was the color of iron, and the monsoon was three hundred miles closer than it had been yesterday.
