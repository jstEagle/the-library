## 7. The Coffin Below

Tamsin had dived wrecks before — a grain barge off the Sallow Flats, a pleasure boat that had gone down drunk on the Ossory bend — and every one of them had felt like theft. This one felt like trespass.

She stood at the Sparrowhawk's rail in the grey first light, watching the river slide past Trail's Landing without hurry, as if it had all the time in the world to keep what it had taken. The wreck lay forty feet down, maybe fifty where the channel had scoured since the flood, out in the drowned main street of a town that still had people living in it. You could see the stilt houses from the rail, their lanterns going out one by one as the town woke up and looked back at them. Nobody waved.

"Water's cold this deep," Odji said beside her, working the air pump's crank handle with his palm, testing the give of it. "Cold enough to cramp you if you rush."

"I don't rush," Tamsin said.

"You rushed at the Sallow Flats."

"The Sallow Flats was a grain barge and I was twenty-two." She pulled on the diving glove, flexing her fingers against the canvas. "And nobody had locked a grain barge from the inside."

Odji stopped cranking. "You believe that? Sealed from inside?"

"I believe the hatch bar was set from below, which is what Mirabel saw through the glass yesterday, and I believe Mirabel saw it right, because she sees everything about boats right." Tamsin tightened the last strap. "What I don't believe is that it means nothing."

Below them, the river held its color — brown-green, opaque, patient. Somewhere under that skin lay the pay-barge the locals called the coffin, and Tamsin had spent an uncomfortable night turning over why a town would give a wreck that name when the wreck was full of money. Towns didn't name things after their treasures. They named them after their funerals.

* * *

They went down in shifts: Tamsin first, with Ilan Reyes on the line above, because Mirabel wanted the cartographer's eyes on whatever they found and Brack wanted a Roe man on every rope. That left Brack himself pacing the deck with his arms folded and his foreman's ledger open, writing down everything that came up and nothing that went down, or so it seemed to Tamsin as she settled the helmet over her shoulders and let Odji bolt the breastplate shut.

The world went small and brass-rimmed. Air hissed. The rail fell away beneath her boots, and then the river took her.

Descent was a slow drowning in reverse — light thinning from green to umber to a darkness that wasn't quite dark, the pressure closing around her ears like hands. She fed the line out hand over hand, counting, and at thirty feet the water turned cold enough to feel through wool and oilskin, and at forty her boots found something that was not riverbed.

Deck planking. Silted over, furred with weed, but planking, running fore and aft, wide and sound. She stood on the roof of the drowned thing and the river pressed down on her shoulders like a crowd.

The superstructure rose ahead of her, a low cabin block half-swallowed in silt, its windows gone to empty sockets. She moved along the deck by touch and by the pale coin of daylight far above, following the plan Mirabel had drawn for her on the back of a chart: cargo hold amidships, hatch on the centerline, companionway aft. Find the hatch. Report the condition. Touch nothing.

The hatch cover was there under six inches of silt, and the silt told its own story. It lay smooth. Undisturbed. Nine years of fine grey settling, unbroken by any hand or tool — no pry marks, no diver's drag trails, nothing. Whatever had happened to this barge had happened once, and the river had kept the scene like a pressed flower ever since.

Tamsin knelt and swept the silt clear with the flat of her glove.

The hatch bars were set. Both of them. And the dogs — the iron locking dogs that clamped the cover down against weather — were thrown from below. She knew it the way she knew a knot by feel: the levers pointed inward, toward the hold, their handles accessible only to someone standing underneath. From above there was nothing to grip, nothing to turn. Someone had closed this hatch from inside the hold and barred it behind themselves.

Or someone else had barred it behind *them*, she thought, and the cold of the water moved from her shoulders into her chest.

She worked the signal line twice — the pull that meant *found it, coming to report* — and hung in the dark a moment longer, her lamp beam moving across the hatch timbers. There, gouged into the wood beside the bars, four short strokes and a fifth struck through them. A tally. Or a count of days. Her lamp slid away from it quickly, though there was no reason for hurry, no reason except that counting days on the inside of a sealed hold was not a thing she wanted to imagine clearly, forty feet down, in a town that called this place the coffin.

* * *

On deck, she gave her report with the helmet still dripping around her neck, and watched three faces take it three different ways.

Mirabel took it like a course correction: eyes narrowing, already redrawing the problem. Ilan took it like a scholar handed a disputed text — he actually leaned forward, water-spots forgotten on his spectacles. And Brack took it like a man hearing a figure he hadn't budgeted for.

"Sealed from inside," Brack said. "You're sure of the dogs?"

"Thrown from below. No question." Tamsin wrung out her hair. "And the silt's untouched. Nobody's been down there since she sank."

"People panic," Brack said. "Barge takes water, some fool shuts himself in the hold with the cargo, bars the hatch against it. Happens."

"With both dogs? In a hull that's sinking bow-first?" Mirabel's voice was flat. "The stern flooded first, Brack. Anyone in that hold had hours to come out. Hours. And instead somebody barred the door and scratched tallies next to it."

Brack's jaw worked. "Tallies."

"Five strokes," Tamsin said. "Cut into the hatch frame."

For a moment nobody spoke. Above them the gulls worked the current, and from the stilt houses came the small sounds of a town starting its day — a hammer, a dog, a child's voice carrying flat across the water.

"Well," Brack said finally, and his voice had reassembled itself into foreman's briskness, "it's salvage, not archaeology. We're paid for the cargo, not the sermons. Get the cover off today. I want the hold opened before the weather turns." He tapped his ledger against his palm. "Roe wants manifest and coin by week's end."

"And if we find whoever barred that door?" Odji asked quietly.

"Nine years in a flooded hold?" Brack almost smiled. "You'll find bones, boy, if you find anything. Bones and banknotes. Neither one talks."

But Tamsin noticed he wrote something in the ledger before he said it, and wrote it fast, and angled the page away from the rail.

* * *

The second dive was Tamsin and Odji together, working the dogs loose from above with long bars while Ilan's camera waited in its waterproof housing on the line, ready to be lowered the moment the cover came free. It was brutal work — nine years of rust had welded iron to iron, and every strike of the bar rang through the water and came back to them from the drowned town's foundations, so that Tamsin kept half-expecting the river itself to object.

The second dog gave way near midday with a shriek of metal she felt in her teeth. The first had gone easier. Together they hauled the hatch cover up on the winch line, silt boiling off it in clouds, and the hold of the pay-barge opened its mouth to the Rivergate for the first time since the flood.

The smell should have been the worst of it — dead water, dead wood, the mineral breath of things long drowned. It wasn't. The worst was the orderliness.

Tamsin went down the line first, lamp high. The hold was a long timber box, sixty feet end to end, and it was nearly empty. Not looted-empty, not scattered-empty. *Stowed*-empty. Crates had been stacked aft in a neat wall, roped and chocked, and forward of them the deck was bare but for a scatter of small objects arranged — arranged, she could not escape the word — along the port side. A boot. A tin cup. A child's wooden horse, its paint still holding a ghost of red after nine years underwater.

"Oh," Odji said into his mouthpiece, and the word came out as a bubble and a shape she read more than heard.

"Don't," Tamsin signaled back, hand flat. *Don't say it.* But they were both already thinking it: this was not a cargo hold anymore. Somebody had lived here, at the end, or died here, and had put their few things in a row the way you'd put flowers on a grave.

The strongbox sat amidships, exactly where a paymaster would keep it, lashed to a ring bolt with chain gone to orange lace. It was iron, knee-high, banded, and even under the silt and the weed the stamp on its lid came up clean under Tamsin's glove: a circle, a bridge, and letters she traced one by one.

R.G.A. The Rivergate Authority.

Not a shipping company's seal. Not a bank's. The Authority — the body that owned the locks, licensed the pilots, ruled the whole river from the gorge to the sea. The Authority whose dredge barges she'd saluted her entire working life. The Authority that had, according to every record anyone had ever seen, lost this barge and its payroll to an accident of flood.

Her lamp moved across the lid again. There was a second mark, smaller, stamped beside the Authority's: a date. She rubbed the growth away with her thumb, careful as touching a wound.

The date was three days *before* the flood.

Tamsin knelt there in the cold and the dark and felt the job change shape around her, the way a channel changes shape under a hull — no visible difference on the surface, and suddenly your keel is grinding. A pay-barge sealed from the inside. A strongbox dated before the disaster that sank it. Tallies cut into a hatch frame. A town that named this place the coffin and spat at the boats that came to open it.

She signaled up. *Camera down.*

Ilan's housing descended slow on the line, lamp-lit, and he worked it by remote from above with a patience Tamsin usually admired. The shutter clicked — a soft double knock transmitted through the water — once on the strongbox, once on the stamp, once on the date. Then, before she could stop him, once on the row of small objects along the port side. The boot. The cup. The wooden horse.

*Leave it,* she signaled, too late. The shutter had already knocked.

They came up into late afternoon light and a wind that had shifted north, carrying the first wet-metal smell of the season. Mirabel met her at the rail, and Tamsin told her everything — the dogs, the tally, the stowed crates, the strongbox, the date — in a low voice, watching Brack across the deck the whole time. He was at the far rail with his radio handset cupped to his ear, speaking into it with his back turned, and when he finished he stood looking at the water for a long moment before he wrote in his ledger again.

"He's reporting," Tamsin said.

"He's reporting the coin count," Mirabel said. "That's what a foreman does."

"He's reporting the strongbox. I watched his face when I said the Authority's name. He already knew it would be there, Mirabel. Knew it the way you know a room you've been shown a drawing of."

Mirabel said nothing for a while. The wind picked at her collar. Out in the channel, the river ran brown and heavy, higher than it had been that morning; she could see it eating at the landing stage steps, one step gone since yesterday.

"Tomorrow we lift the crates," Mirabel said at last. "Coin and manifest, like the contract says. The strongbox stays where it is until we've cleared the rest of the hold — that's standard practice, and it keeps Brack happy, and it keeps us legal." Her eyes stayed on the water. "One day, Tamsin. We do this one day at a time, by the book, until we know what book we're actually in."

"And if the book is the kind that gets people barred into holds?"

Mirabel turned then, and for just a moment Tamsin saw something in her face she'd never seen in nine years of sailing with her — not fear exactly, but recognition, the look of a woman reading her own handwriting on a page she didn't remember writing.

"Then we finish the job faster than anyone expects," Mirabel said, "and we are somewhere else when the weather comes." She glanced north, at the bruised sky stacking itself up over the gorge. "Get some sleep. First shift is yours at dawn."

* * *

Tamsin did not sleep. She sat on the foredeck with her back against the windlass and watched the lights of Trail's Landing, and near midnight she saw a boat come out from among the stilts — a small skiff, one lantern, moving quiet as an oar could push it. It circled the Sparrowhawk once, at a distance, the way you'd circle a sleeping animal you weren't sure of. Then it came closer, close enough that Tamsin could see the single figure at the oars, an older woman with white in her hair, who looked up at the dark ship for a long moment before turning back for shore.

As the skiff swung round, its lantern caught the woman's face, and Tamsin saw that she wasn't looking at the ship at all. She was looking at the stern, at the name board, at the word SPARROWHAWK — and her expression, in that one yellow instant, was the expression of someone seeing a debt walk back into town wearing a familiar coat.

The lantern dipped behind the stilts. The river ran on, higher than yesterday, colder than the season owed.

Tamsin sat in the dark and counted what she knew: a sealed hold, a pre-dated strongbox, a foreman whispering into a radio, a town that called the wreck the coffin, and now a woman in a skiff who knew the Sparrowhawk's name. Nine years, Mirabel had run this river without once stopping here. Tamsin had always assumed it was a pilot's superstition, some bad sandbar memory, a stretch of water that had frightened her once.

Watching the dark where the skiff had gone, Tamsin understood, with the slow certainty of rising water, that Mirabel Vane hadn't been avoiding a sandbar at all.

She'd been avoiding a witness.
