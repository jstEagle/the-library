## 8. Nine Years of Water

Maren Oduya had learned to sleep in short watches, the way soldiers and mothers do, and so she was awake when the lamp came down the stilt-walk at half past eleven.

She heard the footsteps first — light ones, careful ones, a woman who had spent nine years reading water and had never unlearned it on land. Then the creak of the third plank, the one that always announced a visitor whether they wanted announcing or not. Maren lay in the dark of her house above the drowned main street and listened to Mirabel Vane stop at the rail outside her door.

The rain had gone soft after midnight, that fine monsoon drizzle that didn't fall so much as hang in the air, and through the open shutter Maren could smell the river rising. It came up under the floorboards some nights now, a cold breath off the water, as if the flood that had taken everything were checking to see what else was left worth taking.

"You're awake," Mirabel said through the screen.

"I'm awake."

"Brack's posted a watch on the wreck. I couldn't sleep with him up there doing whatever he's doing." A pause. "I thought you'd want to know."

Maren sat up. She did not light the lamp. "I've known what Brack is since the day he stepped off Roe's launch and looked at this town like it was inventory. What I don't know is why you came to my door about it."

Silence. The river said something low against the pilings below, the way it always did, patient as a creditor.

"Because you live closest to the water," Mirabel said finally. "And because—" She stopped. Maren heard her hand rest on the rail, the small groan of old wood taking weight. "Because I owed you a knock before I left here without one."

"You owe me more than a knock."

"Yes," Mirabel said quietly. "I know."

Maren got up, unlatched the screen, and stood in the doorway with the lamp still dark between them. She wanted to look at this woman the way she'd looked at her across the dock that first morning — with spit behind her teeth and her father's name burning in her throat — but the truth was uglier than anger, and she'd carried it long enough to know its shape. The truth was that Mirabel Vane standing wet-eyed in the drizzle looked like every photograph Maren's mother had ever kept of the dead: young, capable, ruined by something no one would say out loud.

"Come in or go away," Maren said. "But if you come in, you'll answer me. Not dodge. Not tell me one of your stories with a hole in the middle of it. Nine years I've watched boats pass this town like we were a stain on the riverbank. You're the first one who ever stopped. So either you're braver than the rest, or you've got more to answer for. Either way, I'm collecting tonight."

Mirabel came in.

* * *

The house was one room and a sleeping loft, built on stilts over what used to be the chandlery, back when there was commerce enough on this stretch to need one. Maren lit a single candle and set tea water on the little spirit stove, because hospitality was a habit deeper than hatred and she resented herself for both. Mirabel sat where she was pointed, on the bench by the window, hands around nothing, dripping slowly onto the boards.

Through the floor, faintly, the river moved against the drowned street below. On clear days you could stand at the rail and see the shapes of it — rooflines, a chimney, the arch of the old bridge house — all of it green with silt and weed, a town lying face-down in its own grave. The survivors called the flooded streets the Low Town and lived above them in houses like this one, and children born after the flood played on rope walks between the stilts and never learned to be afraid of falling, only of going down.

"My mother kept a ledger too," Maren said, when the tea was poured and neither of them had touched it. "Grocer's ledger. Every account in Trail's Landing, every debt, every favor. After the water came, she went back for it three times. Third time the current took her." She said it flatly, the way you say a thing you have said to yourself so many times it has worn smooth. "They found her body two miles downstream with her arms still crossed over the bag. That's the kind of town this was. We died holding our accounts."

Mirabel's eyes went to the candle flame. She didn't speak.

"The Authority paid out four shillings on the pound," Maren went on. "Did you know that? Four shillings, and only if you could prove title, and only if you signed a release saying the flood was an act of God. An act of God, Vane. My father signed his release in the tent city at Harker's Bend with river fever in his lungs, because four shillings was the difference between his children eating and not eating. He was dead within the year. Half this town followed him. The other half stayed — why do you think we stayed? Because leaving means agreeing it was nobody's fault."

Still Mirabel said nothing. Her stillness was famous on the river; Maren had heard men describe it, the way the Sparrowhawk's captain could sit through any storm of words without giving up helm or heading. Tonight it looked less like mastery and more like drowning done politely.

"There's a rumor," Maren said. "You knew there'd be a rumor. There's been one for nine years, and it has a shape, and I'll say its shape to your face since no one else will. They say the sluice gates at the head of the gorge were opened the night before the crest. Opened from the works side. Opened by hand. And they say the man who gave the order was named Vane."

The candle guttered in the draft from the shutter. Below them the river breathed.

"They say it in the tent cities," Maren said. "They say it in Rivergate, in the taverns where the Authority men drink. *A Vane opened the gates.* It rhymes, you see. It's the kind of thing that rhymes, which is how you know it's been passed mouth to mouth instead of paper to paper, because paper burns and mouths don't. Your father's name is a song in this valley, Captain. Children sing it skipping rope. *Vane, Vane, opened the gate, washed the Landing to Harker's Strait.*"

Something moved in Mirabel's face then — not a flinch, exactly. More like a door opening a crack onto a room that was already on fire. She set down the untouched tea with great care, as though her hands needed a task or they would do something unsanctioned.

"And what do you say?" she asked. Her voice was level. Almost.

"I say I watched you take this contract." Maren leaned forward. "I say Cassian Roe's money bought your hull and your crew's wages, and you sailed into the one reach of river you've avoided for nine years like a man walking back into a house where he buried something. I say your diver found that barge sealed from the inside and your cartographer photographed an iron box with the Authority's seal on it, and your foreman ordered it left on the riverbed — left, Vane, when salvage crews fight over boxes like dogs over bones. And I say none of that adds up to coin. It adds up to a woman who knows what's in that water, and is afraid of who else will find it."

"You've been watching my crew closely."

"I've been watching this river closely for nine years. Your crew just happens to be on it." Maren stood. The room was small; standing put her over Mirabel, and she hated that she wanted the advantage and took it anyway. "So here is your one question, and I'll only ask it once, and then you can go back to your boat and your silence and your beautiful competent loneliness. Were you here? That night?"

Mirabel looked up at her.

"Were you at the Landing when the water came? Was your father's hand on that wheel? Did you stand on a deck somewhere upstream and feel the river change under you and know what it meant?" Maren's voice cracked on the last of it and she let it crack; she had earned the crack. "My brother was fourteen. He was on the bridge house roof waving a lantern at the boats coming down, because he thought they were rescue. He waved until the water took the roof. So when I ask you, understand what the answer weighs. Were you here?"

For a long moment there was no sound in the room but the river and the rain and the small tick of the cooling stove.

And Mirabel Vane — who could read a sandbar from the taste of the current, who could hold a crew together with a glance, who had run gorges that killed pilots with twenty years' experience — looked at Maren Oduya and said nothing at all.

Not yes. Not no. Just that terrible, eloquent silence, her jaw tight, her eyes bright and fixed somewhere past Maren's shoulder at the wall, at the dark, at nine years ago.

"That's not an answer," Maren whispered.

"No," Mirabel agreed softly. "It isn't."

"Why?"

"Because an answer is a thing you can be wrong about." She rose, unhurried, and Maren saw that her hands were shaking and that she was controlling them the way she controlled everything, by force of will applied like ballast. "Because if I say yes, I give you a target, and if I say no, I give you a lie, and you've had enough lies from the Authority to last the rest of your life. Because there are things in that river older than the rumor, Maren, and when they come up — and they are coming up, soon, whether I raise them or Roe does — you'll wish you'd asked different questions."

"What things?"

Mirabel paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and for a heartbeat she looked young — younger than Maren, maybe, though the river had aged them both in the same currency — and exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with lack of sleep.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my father told me that a river keeps everything given to it, but gives nothing back except on its own schedule. I thought it was just something boatmen say." Her mouth twisted. "It's not. It's a warning. Goodnight, Maren. Keep your shutters latched. The water's coming up faster than the gauge says."

Then she was gone, footsteps soft on the planks, the third board creaking its announcement to the empty walk, and Maren stood alone in the candlelight with her heart hammering and her question unanswered and yet — this was the strange part, the part she would turn over for years — somehow *answered*, the way a locked door answers. Mirabel Vane had not denied it. In nine years of passing boats and passing years, in all the mouths that sang the skipping-rope rhyme, Maren had never once heard anyone connected to that name deny it either. Denial was cheap. Everyone could afford denial.

Silence like that cost something. Silence like that was purchased in advance.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the dead stove while the candle burned down to a coin of wax, and she made herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the Sparrowhawk first dropped anchor above the wreck.

Roe's foreman orders a sealed strongbox left on the bottom. Roe himself comes upstream in person for a pay-barge — a pay-barge, when his company owns steam launches that cost more than the whole sunken cargo. The Authority pays out four shillings on the pound and calls it God's work. And a pilot who won't say yes or no sails straight back to the scene of the worst thing this valley remembers, on the money of the men who profited by it.

It doesn't add up to salvage, Maren thought. It adds up to a burial party. Someone is paying to make sure something stays down there — and someone else on that crew is paying to bring it up, and the two of them are sharing a deck and smiling at each other over supper.

She went to the window. Through the drizzle she could see the Sparrowhawk's riding lights, two dim orange points above the black water, and beyond them, fainter, the lantern of Brack's watch on the wreck buoy. Between the lights, the river ran high and fast and silent, carrying its cargo of drowned rooftops and drowned names toward the sea.

Her mother's ledger had gone down with the town. Every account, every debt, every favor — the whole moral arithmetic of Trail's Landing, filed under six feet of green water. Maren had spent nine years telling herself it was lost, that some things the river simply keeps.

But Mirabel Vane had said *when they come up*. Not *if*. When.

Maren blew out the candle and began, quietly, to dress.

If something was going to surface from that water, she intended to be standing on the bank when it did — and this time, whatever came up out of the river, she would not let go of it.
