## 9. Brack's Arithmetic

The rain came three days early, and it came wrong.

Mirabel felt it before she heard it — a pressure change in the gorge that made her back teeth ache, the same way the river spoke to her through the soles of her boots when a sandbar was shifting beneath the hull. She stood at the rail of the Sparrowhawk in the grey half-light and watched the clouds come down the gorge like something poured, low and bruise-colored, swallowing the ridgelines one by one. The monsoon was supposed to be a week out. The almanacs said so. The almanacs had been written by men who sat in Rivergate City counting rainfall from ledgers instead of standing on a deck learning what the sky smelled like before it broke.

It smelled like iron. Like wet stone and old blood.

Behind her, the camp on shore was already stirring — the salvage crew's tents sagging under the first fat drops, tarps snapping taut, someone shouting for the dive lines to be hauled clear of the water. Above Trail's Landing, the drowned pay-barge waited where it had waited nine years, its hold sealed from the inside, its iron strongbox stamped with the Authority's seal sitting exactly where Brack had ordered it left.

*Left*, Mirabel thought. Not recovered. Not opened. *Left.*

She turned that word over again as she watched the rain thicken, because it still didn't fit. Roe was paying triple wages for a salvage job, and his own foreman had ordered them to leave the single most valuable object in the wreck sitting on the riverbed. Tamsin had called it caution — heavy lift in silty water, unstable wreck, all fair reasons. But Mirabel had run cargo long enough to know that men who paid for caution got caution, and men who paid triple wages wanted something else entirely.

She just hadn't let herself finish the arithmetic until now.

* * *

The morning passed in the rhythm of a camp preparing to batten down. Odji and the others doubled the mooring lines, stowed the dive gear under canvas, argued cheerfully about whose turn it was to dig the drainage trench around the cook tent. Ilan Reyes spent the hours under the awning of the survey tent with his maps spread out, marking the rising waterline against his soundings of the old channel, and every time Mirabel glanced over there he was watching her — not obviously, never obviously, but with that cartographer's patience, the way a man watches a river bend he suspects is about to cut a new course.

Tamsin found her at the rail, pressing a tin cup of chicory into her hands.

"You've got your storm face on," Tamsin said.

"I always have my storm face on."

"No. Your storm face is when you're ready for it. This is the other one." Tamsin leaned against the rail beside her, shoulder to shoulder, the way she'd done through nine years of weather. "This is the face you make when you're doing sums you don't like."

Mirabel drank the chicory. It was terrible. Tamsin made it terrible on purpose, some superstition about good luck, and Mirabel had never once corrected her because correcting it would mean admitting she'd noticed, and noticing things was how you started caring, and caring was —

"Brack," Mirabel said quietly. "Where is he?"

"Shore side. He took the skiff upriver an hour ago, said he was checking the high-water marks on the north bank." Tamsin paused. "He took the radio case."

The cup went cold in Mirabel's hands, though the morning wasn't cold yet.

Every salvage contract carried a radio for weather reports and schedule changes — standard, unremarkable, kept in a green steel case in the foreman's quarters. And foremen checked in with their employers; that was standard too. But Brack had taken the skiff *upriver*, away from the camp's sightlines, into the blind water above the wreck site where the gorge walls rose sheer and no one could see who he talked to or how long he talked.

"He's been taking that skiff every second evening since we raised the first artifacts," Tamsin said. "I told myself it was nothing. Reports, schedules."

"How many evenings?"

"Six."

Six evenings of private transmissions, and a strongbox ordered left on the riverbed, and Cassian Roe's money flowing upstream like everything else flowed toward Trail's Landing eventually. Mirabel set the cup down on the rail with great care, as if it might go off.

"I'm going ashore," she said.

* * *

She did not take the main path. She knew this country the way she knew the Sparrowhawk's seams — by feel, by memory she hadn't asked for — and there was a goat track above the north bank that climbed through scrub pine and came out on a shelf of rock overlooking the blind water. Nine years ago she'd picnicked there with her father, the summer before the flood, when he'd been home between assignments and she'd been fifteen and bored and furious at him for reasons she couldn't have named then and could not stop naming now.

Her boots remembered the way. That was the cruelest part of coming back to a place you'd sworn off: your body had never left.

She reached the shelf as the rain thickened to a steady drumming on the pine needles, and below her, forty feet down at the water's edge, the skiff sat nosed against a fallen tree with the green steel case open beside it. Brack crouched over the radio with his back to the slope, a big shapeless man in an oilskin slicker, and even from above Mirabel could see the set of his shoulders — the particular hunch of a man saying something he doesn't want overheard.

She couldn't make out words. She didn't need to. She watched him transmit, watch him wait, watch him transmit again, and when he finally closed the case and stood, he stood looking upriver toward the wreck site for a long moment, motionless in the rain, before turning back for the skiff.

Mirabel stayed on the shelf until the sound of the oars faded, and then she sat down on the wet rock and let the arithmetic finish itself.

Triple wages. A pay-barge sunk nine years ago, officially written off as lost with all hands' effects, its coin value a rounding error against what Roe spent getting this expedition outfitted. A strongbox with the Authority's seal, ordered left in place. Six nights of secret transmission. And now the monsoon arriving early, sealing the gorge, closing the trap — except the trap had never been for the wreck. The wreck had been bait. The trap was for whatever the Authority had buried inside that strongbox, and Cassian Roe had hired the only crew desperate enough and skilled enough to find it, and Brack's job had never been salvage at all.

Brack's job was to make sure nothing came up that couldn't be controlled.

And if something did come up anyway — if a certain stubborn pilot with a leaking hull decided to raise a sealed box against orders — well. Then the foreman would know exactly whom to tell, and the monsoon would seal the gorge behind them, and everyone in this valley would be exactly as far from help as Roe needed them to be.

Mirabel laughed once, short and joyless, and the rain swallowed it.

Her father had taught her navigation with the same lesson every time: *the current never lies, but it will carry you somewhere you didn't choose if you stop paying attention.* She had stopped paying attention the day she signed Roe's contract. No — earlier than that. The day the shipwright condemned her hull and a stranger with kind eyes offered to fund repairs, and she'd told herself it was only business. Every step downstream had felt like choice. None of them had been.

She got up. Her knees were wet. The rain ran down her collar, cold as river water, and she thought: *nine years I ran from this town, and I rowed straight back into it for money, and the money was always the hook.*

Then she thought about the strongbox, sitting in the dark under forty feet of rising water, holding whatever truth the Authority had drowned along with the town.

Roe wanted it buried. Which meant it was worth raising.

* * *

By the time she got back to camp, the rain had committed itself fully. The river had come up six inches since morning and was climbing fast, brown now, carrying branches and whole trees torn from the upper forests. Odji met her at the landing with wide eyes.

"Water's up a foot since noon, Captain. Tamsin's got the crew doubling lines again. Brack says we should consider pulling out ahead of the crest."

"Does he," Mirabel said.

"Says the gorge'll be unnavigable in two days. Says Mr. Roe will want his people safe." Odji hesitated. "Captain — the locals are moving up to the high houses. Maren Oduya's people sent word down asking if we need shelter when it breaks proper. After everything, they still —"

"I heard you." Mirabel looked past him at the camp, at the tents straining in the wind, at the survey tent where Ilan stood in the doorway watching her with those patient eyes, and she made a decision the way she made all her best ones: fast, without letting herself feel the cost until later.

"Get Tamsin," she said. "Quietly. And get me a diving lamp and the block and tackle off the small winch. Tonight, after Brack turns in — we're going back down to the coffin."

Odji's face went through several expressions in rapid succession. "Brack ordered —"

"I know what Brack ordered." She stepped closer, voice dropping under the rain's drumming. "Brack has been radioing Roe's backers from the north bank every second evening for six days. This job was never about coin. Roe wants what's in that box, and he wants it destroyed or buried, and when the monsoon seals the gorge he's going to want us sealed in with it. So we raise it first, we open it, and we decide ourselves what happens next. Are you with me?"

Odji was a quiet man, careful with rope and careful with words, and he looked at her for a long three seconds. Then he nodded once and said, "I'll wake the second watch instead of the first. Fewer questions."

"Tamsin first."

"She's already rigging the winch," Odji said, and almost smiled. "She saw you come down off the north bank without Brack, and she knows your storm face from your other face."

* * *

They gathered in the hold of the Sparrowhawk as the light died — Mirabel, Tamsin, Odji, and Ilan, who arrived last and stood dripping in the doorway until Mirabel jerked her chin at him. She laid it out flat: the transmissions, the strongbox, the arithmetic. When she finished, the hold was silent except for the rain hammering the deck above and the river gurgling against the hull, higher now, impatient.

"You're certain about the radio," Ilan said.

"I watched him send. Six evenings. Blind water, out of sight of camp."

"That matches the shipping schedules I pulled from the Authority's records." Ilan crouched and unrolled one of his maps across the crate between them — not a map of the river, Mirabel realized, but a chart of the gorge with dates and water levels annotated in his precise hand. "Roe's launch was provisioned at Grayfall Landing eleven days ago. Enough coal and stores for a long stay. His backers chartered a second vessel out of Rivergate the week before that, registered to a shell company I've been chasing for two years." He looked up. "They planned for a long operation. They planned for weather. They planned for you, Captain Vane."

"Then they miscalculated," Tamsin said, "because they hired the best pilot on this river and she's standing right here."

"The best pilot on this river," Mirabel said slowly, "is about to do something stupid."

"They usually go together," Odji murmured.

Mirabel looked at each of them in turn — Tamsin with her jaw set, Odji with his careful hands folded, Ilan with his brother's ghost standing behind his eyes — and felt the strange calm that came over her in the worst water, the calm that was maybe the only honest thing she'd carried out of Trail's Landing nine years ago.

"Here's the run," she said. "Midnight slack, such as it is. Visibility near zero, current up thirty percent and climbing. We take the work punt down to the wreck site, run the lift bags on the strongbox, and bring it up slow. Two divers max — the water's too violent for more. If Brack wakes, Odji stalls him. If the river turns, we drop everything and swim for it, because no box is worth a life. Everyone clear?"

"And after?" Ilan asked. "Once it's up — what's in it might name names. Powerful names. It might name yours."

"It already does," Mirabel said. "My father's. Whatever's in that box, I've been carrying it for nine years without opening it. I'm tired of carrying."

Above them, the rain redoubled, and the Sparrowhawk groaned at her moorings like an old woman in her sleep. Somewhere upriver, in the dark, Brack's skiff would be tied at the north bank, and the green steel case would be silent now — but tomorrow it would speak, and what it said would bring Cassian Roe up the gorge with all the courtesy and menace money could buy.

Mirabel checked the lamp oil, checked the lift bag valves, checked the punt's lines twice, because checking was praying and she'd stopped praying any other way.

At midnight, they pushed off into the black water, and the river rose to meet them.
