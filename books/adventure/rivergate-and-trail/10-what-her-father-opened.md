## 10. What Her Father Opened

Ilan Reyes had spent nine years learning to lie with maps.

It was a trade like any other, he told himself — a cartographer's first duty was to the shape of the land, and the land did not care what you felt about it. He had drawn the Rivergate's shifting channels for the Authority itself, back when the Authority still bothered to commission honest surveys, and he had learned that a river was the most patient of all witnesses. It moved. It forgot nothing. Every sandbar it built and every cut it abandoned was a sentence in a testimony no one had thought to read.

He had come aboard the *Sparrowhawk* with a satchel of instruments and a story about funding repairs in exchange for passage into the gorge, and both things were true, which made the lying easier. The best lies, his brother Tomas used to say, are load-bearing walls built into a true house. Tomas had said a great many clever things before the water took him.

Now, on the fourth night above Trail's Landing, with the monsoon's first rains ticking against the deck above like fingers testing a lock, Ilan sat across from Mirabel Vane in the cramped stern cabin and stopped lying.

* * *

He had chosen the moment carefully. Brack and half the crew had gone ashore to the stilt houses to trade for lamp oil, Tamsin was on watch, and the rain had settled into that steady gray drumming that made men speak lower without knowing why. Mirabel sat sharpening a salvage knife on a whetstone, her movements unhurried, her eyes never quite leaving him. She had been watching him too closely since the gorge began — he'd felt it like sunburn — and he understood why. She knew the river here. She knew it the way a person knows the rooms of a house they burned down themselves.

"You've been waiting to talk to me," she said. Not a question. The whetstone whispered along the blade. "Since Widow's Teeth, at least. Maybe since the trading post."

"I have."

"Then talk. But understand something first." She set the knife down flat on the chart table, point toward him, an accident of geometry that fooled neither of them. "Whatever you're selling, I've been sold things before. I don't buy."

"I'm not selling." He opened his satchel. "I'm returning something."

From the oilcloth wrapping he drew a folder — not paper, not quite; a waterproof document case of the kind river surveyors used, its brass corners green with age. He laid it on the table between them and did not open it yet. Her eyes went to it and stayed there, and he watched her hands go still in a way that told him more than her face ever would. Mirabel Vane's face was a harbor wall. Her hands were the tide.

"What is it," she said quietly.

"Authority records. Surveys, correspondence, work orders. Nine years of them." He turned the case slowly, so the stamped seal caught the lamplight — the crossed keys and current-lines of the Rivergate Authority. "I stole most of them. Some I bought from clerks who wanted to forget they'd ever filed them. One I took out of a dead man's office while his colleagues drank to his memory."

Mirabel looked up at him then, and for the first time since he'd boarded her boat he saw fear in her — not the ordinary fear of a captain reading weather, but something older and deeper, the fear of a woman who has already survived the worst thing and knows exactly how much worse the world can make it.

"Whose dead man," she said.

"Director Halloway's. He signed the flood order."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere forward, the hull creaked — that low, arthritic groan the *Sparrowhawk* gave even in calm water, the sound that had driven Mirabel to take Roe's money, though Ilan suspected by now she regretted every coin of it.

"My father worked under Halloway," Mirabel said. Each word came out level, placed like stones across a current. "Aldous Vane. Chief engineer, upper gorge district. If you've brought me a file with his name in it, I've read worse in my own head every night for nine years. You'll have to do better than a name."

"No," Ilan said. "I won't. Because you haven't read this."

He opened the case.

* * *

There were eleven documents in it. He knew because he had counted them a hundred times, in a hundred rented rooms, in the years when counting them was the only prayer he had. He arranged them on the chart table in the order he had long ago memorized: the correspondence first, the orders second, the survey last — the survey last always, because the survey was the wound.

"This is dated the eleventh of Sowmonth, nine years ago." He turned the first page toward her. "Three weeks before the flood. It's a memorandum from Director Halloway to the engineering staff of the upper district, marked confidential. It concerns the insurance valuation of the Authority's warehousing assets at Trail's Landing."

Mirabel read. He watched her lips move slightly over the bureaucratic language — *depreciation schedule, replacement cost, actuarial review* — and watched the moment the meaning landed, because her whole body changed, the way a boat changes when its keel touches bottom.

"They were insured," she said. "The warehouses. The town—"

"The warehouses. The grain terminal. The pay-barge, once it was loaded." Ilan tapped the page. "Read the next line."

She read it aloud, barely voicing it: "'Current structures are valued at less than forty percent of replacement cost due to age and deferred maintenance.'" A pause. "'A catastrophic loss event would trigger full settlement.'"

"A catastrophic loss event," Ilan repeated. "That phrase appears fourteen times in these documents. Do you know what engineers call a flood you open the gates for? They call it exactly that."

Her hand had gone to the table's edge. Knuckles white. "My father wouldn't—" She stopped herself. Started again, slower. "Everyone says a Vane opened the gates. Maren Oduya says it. Half the Landing says it. I ran from it for nine years because I couldn't prove they were wrong and I couldn't bear to learn they were right. So show me. Show me my father's name on an order to drown a town, if you have it. Show me and let me finally know."

"That's not what the documents say."

"Then what do they say?"

Ilan turned the second sheet. "This is the sluice authorization. Dated the day of the flood. It carries two signatures." He put his finger on the first. "Aldous Vane, chief engineer. Executing officer." His finger moved to the second signature, heavier ink, a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the page. "Cassian Roe, district commissioner. Issuing authority."

The name went into the cabin like cold water into a bilge. Mirabel stared at it. Ilan had imagined this moment for years — rehearsed it on ferries, on levees, alone in the dark — and none of his rehearsals had prepared him for how still she became. Not frozen. *Anchored.* As if some part of her had been drifting for nine years and had just, horribly, found ground.

"Roe," she said. "Cassian Roe hired me. Cassian Roe is paying triple wages to raise that barge before the monsoon seals the gorge." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing. It got quieter, and flatter, and more precise. "He's not salvaging coin. He's salvaging the proof."

"Now you see why I'm here."

"Why *are* you here?" And now she did look up, and her eyes were wet and furious and searching his face for the flaw in him. "Nobody spends nine years and a small fortune chasing a dead man's paperwork out of principle. Who died, Ilan? Who did you lose?"

The rain ticked. Forward, the hull groaned its old complaint.

"Tomas," Ilan said. "My older brother. He was nineteen. We'd both grown up on the Landing — our mother kept a mending stall on Cordwainer Row, you might remember it, it burned. Tomas worked the grain terminal as a tally clerk. The day of the flood he was on the barge, logging the pay chests into the hold." He found he could not look at her while he said the rest, so he looked at the lantern instead, at its small steady flame. "When the water came, someone sealed the hold from the inside. We think he was trying to protect the ledgers — he was meticulous, he loved those books — or maybe he just couldn't get the hatch open in time. Either way, the divers who went down after found the hold shut fast. They never recovered the bodies. The Authority declared the wreck a hazard to navigation and left it. Left *them*. My brother has been sitting in forty feet of cold water above your drowned town for nine years, and the man who signed the order that killed him just paid me passage to the site."

Mirabel's hand came across the table — not touching him, hovering near his wrist, an offer of contact he could take or leave. He left it. Not from coldness. From the old superstition that if he kept moving he would not break.

"I didn't know," she said. "About the hold. About your brother."

"Very few people know anything. That was the design." He drew the third document forward — the survey, the one he saved for last, the one that had rewritten everything he believed. "But there's more, and you need to hear it, because it's about your father, and it isn't what the Landing thinks, and it isn't what you think either."

* * *

She listened. That was the thing he would remember afterward, in the years when this night had become history — that she listened without interrupting, without defending, without fleeing, though every instinct she owned must have screamed at her to run. Running was what Mirabel Vane did. He had watched her do it in miniature a dozen times since boarding: the way she changed subjects when the Landing came up, the way she answered Odji's questions with stories that circled the truth like gulls over a kill. But tonight she sat in the belly of the one place on earth she'd sworn off, in a boat she'd mortgaged to reach it, and she listened.

"The flood order was issued at dawn," he said. "Your father signed it as executing officer because protocol required the chief engineer's signature on any sluice operation. But look at the annotation in the margin — Halloway's hand, we verified the handwriting against three other memos." He turned the page so the lamplight fell across a scrawled line of ink. "*Vane objects. Overruled per directive 7. Proceed without further delay.*"

"Overruled," Mirabel whispered.

"He refused twice. There's a log entry from the gatehouse — the gatekeeper, a man named Sorrel, kept a private diary and his widow sold it to me four years ago for almost everything I had. Your father came to the gatehouse personally, an hour before the opening. Sorrel wrote it down that same night." Ilan recited it from memory, because he had never needed the page: "*Chief engineer Vane attended at the gatehouse demanding the operation be countermanded. Said the upper snowmelt readings made a controlled release impossible — said the gorge would take the whole town, not just the flats. Commissioner's man told him he'd be relieved of post and charged with obstruction if he touched the wheels. Vane asked Sorrel to note the time of his objection. Then he walked out to the wheelhouse and did his job.*"

The knife lay on the table between them, forgotten. Mirabel's gaze had dropped to it and stayed there, as if the steel were the only solid thing left in the world.

"He tried to close them afterward," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a memory surfacing — and Ilan saw it surface, saw the exact moment a fragment she'd carried for nine years as guilt reshaped itself into something else. "I remembered — God, I'd buried it so deep — I remembered my mother saying he came home soaked to the ribs and wouldn't say where he'd been, and he had rope burns on his palms. Rope burns. Like he'd been hauling on something heavy against the current." Her voice cracked on the last word. "He went back to the gates. After. To try to close them."

"Sorrel's diary confirms it. Two hours after the release, Vane returned with two laborers and attempted a manual close. The flood crest had already blown the downstream lock mechanism. It couldn't be done. He stayed at the wheelhouse until the water took the approach road." Ilan paused. "Sorrel wrote one more line. *He stood on the gatehouse roof until dark, looking downriver. I have never seen a man carry so much.*"

Mirabel Vane put her face in her hands and made no sound at all.

That, more than anything, undid Ilan's careful architecture of purpose. He had imagined giving her these documents like a verdict — cold, formal, justice-shaped. He had not imagined her grief, or how much it would resemble his own: the same nine-year-old wound, worn on opposite sides of the same event. Her father had opened the gates and tried to close them. His brother had sealed the hold trying to save the proof. The river had taken both their families and handed each survivor only half a story, and the halves had been pointed at each other like weapons ever since.

"The Landing blames a Vane," he said softly, when she could hear him again. "Because a Vane's hand was on the wheel. And you blamed yourself, because it was your blood on that wheel. But the hand that ordered it—" he touched the embossed signature, the letters pressed deep enough to read by touch, the way a blind man reads, the way the truth should be read, "—was Cassian Roe's. And above Roe, Halloway. And above Halloway, the insurers' men in Rivergate City whose names we're still pulling thread on. Your father wasn't the flood, Mirabel. He was the last man who fought it."

She lifted her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear, clearer than he had ever seen them, the clarity of a woman whose nine years of running had just collapsed into a single fixed point.

"The strongbox," she said. "In the hold. With your brother."

"The Authority's ledger. Shipping marks show duplicate copies were distributed — one set to a downstream magistrate, one unclaimed, addressed to a widow in Rivergate City. If the original in the strongbox matches, we have the whole chain. Signatures, valuations, the settlement claim, the payouts." He met her eyes. "Brack ordered it left on the seabed. Roe is paying to keep it there. Which means it's worth more than gold to someone, and worth more than gold to us for exactly the opposite reason."

Mirabel stood. She crossed to the stern window, where the rain ran down the glass in braided ropes, and beyond the glass the black water of the gorge moved over the drowned town, over the coffin, over Tomas Reyes and whatever else the Authority had locked away and called a hazard to navigation.

"My father's name," she said to the window. "Nine years I let them spit it. I let them spit *me*, because I thought maybe we deserved it." She turned around, and Ilan saw that the runner was gone from her — the woman who stood there was someone new, or someone very old, restored. "Tomorrow night we raise the box. Against Brack's orders. Before the monsoon closes the gorge and Roe's backers close everything else."

"There's a risk—"

"Everything is a risk. Standing still was a risk. I did the arithmetic wrong for nine years." She picked up the sluice authorization and held it a moment, her thumb resting beside her father's signature — gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping man's shoulder to wake him. Then she set it down and squared the documents into their case with a pilot's precision.

Above them, through the deck, the rain suddenly thickened — a hard, driving rush, the monsoon arriving in earnest, hammering the river flat. And beneath the rain, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the landing stage where Roe's foremen kept their gear: the sound of a signal lamp flashing, three long and one short, aimed downstream into the dark.

Someone was talking to Cassian Roe. And the storm had just given them cover — or a deadline.

Mirabel blew out the lantern.

"We move tonight," she said.
