## 11. The Strongbox

The rain came at dusk, a thin gray whisper against the gorge walls, and by full dark it had settled into the steady, patient drumming that meant the monsoon had stopped being a rumor. Mirabel stood in the stern of the Sparrowhawk with her collar up and her hands in her pockets, watching the lantern light from the salvage barge wobble on the black water, and told herself she was only checking the anchor.

She had checked it three times.

Brack had posted a watch on the strongbox site — two of Roe's men with a lantern and a rifle apiece, sitting under an oilcloth on the salvage barge's deck, directly above the spot where the iron box sat in forty feet of green-black water. *Leave it,* Brack had said that morning, in the flat voice of a man reading terms aloud. *Roe's orders. The coin's what we're paid for. The rest is Authority property, and Authority property goes back to the Authority.* He had said it looking at her, not at the water, and Mirabel had heard what lived underneath the words, which was: *and I know you were asking questions.*

She had nodded like a woman who didn't care. She had been lying since she was nineteen years old; it was the one trade she'd mastered before piloting.

Now the watchmen's lantern burned low and orange behind its oilcloth, and the rain thickened, and Mirabel counted the sounds a river made when it was about to change its mind. The gorge had a vocabulary. A low grinding from the upstream bend meant the flood crest was pushing boulders. A hiss along the stilt pilings meant the water was climbing faster than it looked. And silence — the particular, held-breath silence that had fallen over the Landing now — meant the river was gathering itself the way a man gathers himself before he swings.

*Two days,* she thought. *Maybe three, and the whole gorge turns to whitewater, and nothing comes up out of that water for another nine years.*

She crossed the gap between the Sparrowhawk and the salvage barge on a sprung plank, moving light, and found Ilan Reyes already waiting in the shadow of the dive gear, sitting on a crate with his maps rolled at his knee like a man waiting for a train he'd known would be late.

"You couldn't sleep either," he said.

"I sleep fine." She crouched beside him, voice down under the rain. "The watch?"

"Oduya's nephew brought them wine from the Landing. Maren's idea." His teeth showed white in the dark. "The old woman says if we're going to rob the river, we should at least do it sober-handed and the river's men shouldn't be."

"That's not what Maren says. Maren doesn't say anything to me."

"She said it to me. She said, *tell the Vane girl that her father stood on that dock every morning and looked at the water the same way she does now.*" Ilan let that sit between them. "I think it was a blessing. From her, it might be."

Mirabel didn't answer. There was a version of the last two days that she could still have chosen — the version where she rolled out her bedroll, slept the dreamless sleep of a woman who had made her peace with not knowing, and in the morning helped Brack's men load coin and rowed downstream with triple wages and a recaulked hull and the same hole in her where Trail's Landing lived. She had been that woman for nine years. She knew the shape of her own cowardice the way she knew the shape of a sandbar: by feel, in the dark, without looking.

But Ilan had shown her the documents. *Engineer A. Vane, order of sluice release, countersigned above.* Her father's name in a stranger's hand, ordering the water in. And beneath it, in her father's own cramped script, the annotation she had read forty times since: *Object. Gates cannot be re-closed under load. Request written refusal.* And beneath that, the refusal — signed, dated, three days before the flood. Someone above him had ordered it anyway. Someone above him had signed Roe's name to the indemnity.

Her father hadn't opened the gates. He had tried to close them, and been overruled, and drowned with the town he'd tried to save, and Mirabel had spent nine years running from a crime he hadn't committed — while the men who had committed it sat downstream counting insurance money.

"Winch or weights?" Ilan asked quietly.

"Winch. The box is hooked already — Tamsin set the sling this afternoon when Brack was at the Landing buying his men's silence with beer." She stood. "We get one lift. If the sling slips, we let it go and we never speak of it again, because a second dive at night with the river rising is how divers die."

"And if it doesn't slip?"

"Then we're both ruined," Mirabel said. "Probably worse. Are you ready for worse?"

Ilan stood and slung the map roll across his back. "My brother is at the bottom of the bend below the Landing. I've been ready for worse since I was fourteen."

* * *

The winch was the salvage barge's small derrick, meant for hauling sample buckets, and it groaned like a living thing when Mirabel took the crank. She'd wrapped the ratchet pawl in cloth so it wouldn't click. The rain covered the rest. Forty feet down, in water the color of steeped tea, the strongbox hung in its sling of chain and webbing, and every turn of the crank brought it a foot closer to the air and to everything that would come after.

She cranked. Ilan watched the water.

The box broke the surface at the fourth bell of the night — a slow, black, streaming shape rising out of the river like something surfacing to breathe. It was smaller than she'd imagined, an iron strongbox the size of a sea chest, Authority green gone to black with silt, and the seal stamped on its lid caught the lantern light: the Rivergate Authority's crossed keys, worn but legible, and beneath them a number.

*Not coin,* she thought. *Brack knew. Brack knew from the first day, and Roe knew, and this whole salvage was a keyhole they needed my boat to reach.*

They got it over the gunwale together, grunting, and Mirabel threw the tarp over it and sat on it, breathing hard, suddenly aware of how loud her own heart was. Across the water the watchmen's lantern still glowed under its oilcloth. One of them laughed at something. The wine had done its work.

Ilan had a pry bar. He looked at her.

"Not here," Mirabel said. "The Sparrowhawk. If it opens, it opens below decks, with the hatches shut."

They rowed the box across in the tender, which rode low and shivered under the weight, and hauled it down into the Sparrowhawk's hold, into the place where cargo usually rode, and Mirabel lit the single lamp and shut the hatches, and for a moment the two of them stood in the swaying lamplight with the river knocking softly at the hull, looking at the box the way you look at a door you've been afraid of your whole life.

Ilan set the pry bar in the seam.

"Mirabel." He paused. "Once this is open, there's no version of you that goes back to running cargo and not stopping anywhere. I want you to know that before I lift."

She almost laughed. "I've known that since the gorge. Lift."

The seam gave with a crack that sounded enormous in the small space. The lid came up stiff, shedding rust and river, and the lamplight fell inside.

Not coin. Not gold. Not the pay the Authority had claimed went down with the barge — that had been hauled off in a separate run weeks before the flood, Ilan said later; the barge had been sunk *empty*, sunk on purpose, a coffin with a false bottom of public grief. What the lamplight found was paper. Reams of it, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas, packed with the loving density of men who intended it to last: ledgers, dockets, correspondence, sealed in tin boxes inside the iron, and on the topmost tin box, stenciled in Authority lettering:

*TRAIL'S LANDING — FLOOD EVENT — CLAIMS & CAUSE — DO NOT REMOVE.*

Ilan's hands shook as he cut the wire. "Nine years," he said, half to himself. "Nine years at the bottom of the river, and it's in better shape than the town."

The first ledger was the paymaster's — routine, columns of figures, the barge's legitimate cargo logged and discharged upstream before the sinking. Mirabel flipped past it. The second was thicker, bound in green leather gone soft with water that had never quite reached it, and the first page stopped her breath.

*Rivergate Authority — Internal Record. Re: Trail's Landing levee and sluice works. Assessment of structural failure — engineered.*

She read it standing, because if she sat down she wasn't sure she'd get up. Page by page, in the dry bureaucratic language of men protecting themselves, the flood laid itself out: the levee at Trail's Landing was old, the town's land claim was contested, and the Authority's insurers would pay out far more on a *catastrophic failure* — with the town drowned and its claims extinguished — than on any buyout. The sluice gates above the Landing were to be opened under cover of the storm season. The failure would be attributed to weather. The engineering staff would be instructed to comply. Compensation would be handled downstream, quietly.

And at the bottom of the page, the signatures. The Authority's directors, four of them, their names in ink. And the indemnity rider — the document releasing the Authority from liability for the *unforeseeable* failure — signed, in a confident downward-slanting hand she recognized from a hundred freight contracts she'd countersigned over the years:

*Cassian Roe.*

"He wasn't just paid," Mirabel said. Her voice came out strange, flat, like someone else's. "He signed the insurance. He *wrote the flood into the paperwork.*"

"There's more." Ilan had the third document, a single sheet. "The order to your father. And the refusal. And — " he turned it over — "the reply."

The reply was one line, from the Authority's chief engineer's office, in a hand Mirabel didn't know: *Your objection is noted. Open the gates. Further refusal will be treated as dereliction.*

Below it, in her father's cramped script, the last thing he had ever written on the record: *Complying under protest. God forgive us all.*

Mirabel put her hand flat on the page, over the words, as if she could feel the pressure of the pen. Her father had stood in that gatehouse with the storm coming down and the order in his hand, and he had opened the gates because the alternative was ruin and disgrace, and he had believed — he must have believed — that the water would be managed, that the town would be warned, that the men who signed the papers would not actually let a whole town drown for an insurance figure.

And then the water had come down the gorge, and Trail's Landing had gone under in forty minutes, and the Authority had called it weather, and her father had died in it, and the town had spent nine years cursing the name Vane because someone had to be the hand on the gate.

"He tried to close them," she said. "After. He went back up to the gatehouse — Maren told me — he went back up and the gears were jammed, the flood had already — " Her voice broke and she let it. There was no one in the dark hold to perform strength for. "He died trying to close them, and I spent nine years letting the town believe he opened them, because the truth was *heavier than I was.*"

Ilan said nothing. He was crying too, silently, the way men cry who have carried a thing a long time and finally set it down and found it still heavy. He put his hand on the ledger, next to hers.

"He didn't do it," Mirabel said. "He didn't do it, and neither did I, and we both carried it anyway. That's what they *do*, Ilan. That's the whole machine. They make the drowning look like weather, and they make the survivors carry the blame, because the dead can't argue and the living are too busy staying alive."

The lamp swayed. The river knocked at the hull, patient, rising.

Above them, faint through the deck planks, came the sound of boots — quick, deliberate, crossing the salvage barge's deck. Then Brack's voice, muffled, hard: "Wake them. Both of them. And get a lantern down to the water — the sling's empty."

Mirabel closed the ledger. Her hands were steady now — steadier than they'd been in nine years, the way a river runs smoothest right before it drops.

"They'll search the boats," Ilan said.

"They'll search the boats," she agreed, "and they won't find it, because it's not going to be on a boat." She was already moving, pulling the waxed canvas bundles from the tin boxes, dividing them by feel and lamplight. "Tamsin keeps a cache on the north bank — a dry cave above the flood line, from the smuggling days she pretends she doesn't remember. We put the ledger there tonight, split the correspondence between us, and at first light — "

Boots on the Sparrowhawk's deck. The hatch above them darkened with a shadow, and Brack's voice came down through the planks, courteous as a knife laid on a table:

"Miz Vane. Captain Roe's compliments. He's come upstream to see the work — and he'd like a word with you at first light. Bring your appetite."

Mirabel looked at Ilan. Ilan looked at the ledger.

"Tell him," she called up, in the even voice she used for bad water, "I'll be delighted."

* * *

They worked the rest of the night. By the gray hour before dawn the ledger and its tin boxes lay wrapped in oilskin in a cave above the north bank, behind a fall of stone that only Tamsin and Mirabel could have found in the dark, and the empty strongbox sat back in the Sparrowhawk's hold with the lid loose, waiting to be discovered like a promise kept.

Mirabel stood on her deck as the light came up the gorge, gray and then silver, and watched the downstream bend for the smoke of a steam launch.

It came at sunrise — a black feather of smoke standing up against the rain, moving against the current with the easy arrogance of deep pockets and shallow conscience.

Cassian Roe was coming up the river.

And for the first time in nine years, Mirabel Vane stood her ground and let him come.
