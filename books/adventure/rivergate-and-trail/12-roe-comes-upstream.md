## 12. Roe Comes Upstream

The launch came up the gorge at midmorning, when the mist still hung in the trees and the river ran the color of gunmetal. Mirabel heard it before she saw it — a deep, unhurried chugging that did not belong to any working boat she knew. Working boats hurried. This one came the way a creditor comes: certain of being received.

She was on the Sparrowhawk's deck with a mug of chicory going cold in her hand, and she knew, before the bow swung around the bend below the Landing, exactly whose launch it was. There was only one man on the Rivergate who could afford to burn coal like that on a dead-stretch run — steady, wasteful, showing the water who owned it.

"Cassian Roe," she said to no one.

Tamsin came up the companionway behind her, drying her hands on her trousers. "You want me to get the crew below?"

"No. Let them see." Mirabel set down the mug. "Whatever he's selling, I want witnesses to me not buying."

The launch was forty feet of polished mahogany and brass, a cabin aft with curtained windows, a wheelhouse where a hired pilot stood with the rigid posture of a man being watched. Six men on deck, none of them working. They wore river clothes that had never been river clothes — new canvas, new boots, boots that would blister by noon. Brack stood at the rail, and when the launch slid alongside the Sparrowhawk with a fender's-width of precision, Brack would not look up at her. That told Mirabel more than a confession would have. A man who'd done nothing was eager to meet your eye.

Roe stepped off onto the Sparrowhawk's deck as if he'd been invited.

He was older than Mirabel remembered, and she remembered him precisely, because you remember the men who buy things you can't afford to sell. Nine years had silvered him at the temples and softened nothing else. He wore a gray coat cut close, and his hands were clean, and his smile arrived a half-second before his eyes agreed to it.

"Captain Vane." He said her name like a toast. "Nine years, and you haven't lost a step. I watched you come through the Narrows on my way up — that was seamanship. That was poetry."

"You were watching me come through the Narrows." She didn't return the smile. "Most people wait until a boat is tied up before they start admiring it."

"Forgive me. I was eager." He glanced around the deck — at Tamsin, at Odji at the rail, at the salvage gear lashed amidships, at the tarp that covered, badly, the strongbox's cradle they'd built two nights ago. His gaze rested on the tarp exactly as long as it needed to and no longer. "May we speak privately? I promise you it's worth the inconvenience."

"There's nothing on my deck you can't say in front of my crew."

Something flickered in Roe's face — appreciation, maybe, or its counterfeit. "Of course. I'd expect nothing less from a woman who runs cargo alone through the dry season." He turned, and his voice lifted just enough to carry. "Then I'll say it plainly. I've come to make you a rich woman, Captain, and to make it easy."

* * *

He did it over breakfast, because of course he did. He invited her aboard the launch — "my galley makes better coffee than anything north of Rivergate, I'll stake my reputation on it, such as it is" — and she went, because refusing would have been a statement and she wanted, for as long as the conversation lasted, to have no statements on record. Tamsin insisted on coming. Roe waved her aboard with genuine warmth, which was its own kind of threat.

The launch's cabin was paneled in some pale wood and smelled of tobacco and lemon oil. On the table between the settees: coffee in real china, rolls, preserved figs, a dish of butter that had crossed a thousand miles of river without melting because it was kept on ice. Mirabel ate one roll and touched nothing else. Tamsin ate everything, methodically, watching Roe over the rim of her cup the whole time.

"You're wondering why I'm here in person," Roe began.

"I'm wondering why you're here at all. You have a foreman. You have a radio. You have men whose boots haven't been wet yet."

"Because some transactions soil a middleman." He laced his fingers. "Captain Vane, I'm going to tell you a story, and you're going to let me finish it, and then you're going to understand that what I'm offering you isn't a bribe. It's a kindness. The last one anyone will offer you on this river."

"Go on."

"Nine years ago, the Rivergate Authority insured the warehouses, the grain elevators, the docks, and the payroll barge *Resolute* against flood loss. The premiums were ruinous. The Authority was leveraged to the teeth — bad investments, bad weather, bad arithmetic, the usual. And the upper gorge, as you know better than anyone, floods. It was going to flood that year regardless. The monsoon was early. The sluice gates at the head of the valley were old and failing, and every engineer who looked at them said the same thing: one more season, maybe two, and they'd let go on their own."

He said it smoothly, the way a man recites a route he's walked many times.

"So a decision was made. Not by villains — by tired men with ledgers. If the flood was going to come anyway, better it come *managed*. Better the gates open in a controlled release than fail in a catastrophe. Better the losses be total, and insurable, than partial and contested." He shrugged, a small, elegant shrug. "The Authority collected. The town drowned. The engineers called it a gate failure. The insurers called it an act of God. Everyone was paid, and the river was blamed, because the river has no lawyers."

Mirabel kept her face still. Somewhere behind her ribs, her heart was doing arithmetic of its own.

"And the *Resolute*," she said. "The pay-barge."

"The *Resolute* carried the season's payroll and the Authority's contingency strongbox — the documents, the duplicate ledgers, the things you keep close in case the arrangement needs... clarifying, later, among partners. She was moored above Trail's Landing when the water came down. She went under with everything aboard." He smiled again, thinner. "Including, it seems, the strongbox. Which my salvage operation has now, thanks to you, located."

"Has it."

"Captain." He leaned forward, and the courtesy in his voice acquired weight, the way deep water acquires pressure. "I'm not going to insult you by asking what's in that box. You've had it two days. You're a careful woman; you've looked. So let me tell you what's in it, so we can skip the part where you pretend otherwise. Shipping ledgers. Signatures. A schedule of disbursements. Names — my name among them, and others whose names would end this conversation and several careers and, frankly, the Authority itself, if they ever saw daylight. Nine years of interest on all of it. Nine years of men who cannot afford for it to surface."

"And you'd like it to stay under."

"I'd like it *buried properly* this time." He spread his hands. "Here is my offer, and I ask you to hear all of it before you answer. One: the salvage fee, tripled from the contract you hold. Paid today, in draft, before I leave this cabin. Two: a bonus equal to five years of your best season's earnings. Three: the Sparrowhawk rebuilt from the keel up — new seams, new ribs if she needs them, at the yard of your choosing, in my name. Four —" he paused, and his voice gentled, which was the most frightening thing he'd done yet — "a berth. Command of my upriver packet line. A wage that doesn't depend on the water level. A home port. A *stop*, Captain. You'd never have to run again."

Tamsin's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Mirabel looked at her hands. They were steady, which surprised her.

"You've done your reading," she said.

"I make it my business to know the people I work with. A woman who hasn't docked longer than a week in nine years. A hull held together with prayer and pine pitch. A crew owed three months of wages." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "I'm not offering to buy your silence, Captain. I'm offering to buy you *out*. Of the river's debts, of the past, of that box and everything it's chained to. There are women on this river who'd call that salvation."

"And what happens to the box?"

"Stays where it is. Sealed. I have divers coming who'll set a charge and collapse the channel over her — a thousand tons of rock, and no one will ever find her again, and no one will ever need to. The river keeps what the river keeps." He sat back. "That's the whole of it. No threats. I want to be clear about that — no threats at all. Just an old debt, settled clean, and a good captain freed to be a good captain."

* * *

The silence in the cabin lasted long enough for the launch's engine note to change as the pilot corrected for the current. Mirabel became aware of the river against the hull, that patient pushing, the same push that had been against the Sparrowhawk since she'd first taken her wheel at nineteen.

"You said no threats," she said finally. "Let me tell you what I heard instead. I heard that if I don't take your money, the men with the new boots are going to be on this stretch of water for a while. I heard that my crew's wages are a lever. I heard that my boat is a lever. I heard that you think I'm my father."

Roe's expression didn't change, but it *settled*, the way silt settles.

"You knew him," Mirabel said. "That's why you came yourself. Not for the box. You wanted to watch my face while you said his name."

"I knew Aldous Vane," Roe said quietly. "I signed the papers he carried. And I will tell you something no one else on this river will tell you, Captain, because everyone else is either dead or lying: your father was not a monster. He was an engineer who was handed a decision that had already been made above him, and he made the only choice a man in his position could make, and then he lived with it, and then he drowned himself in the brandy bottle inside two years, and then he died. I attended the funeral. There were eleven people there." He let that sit. "He took the bargain, and the bargain destroyed him anyway. That's the part the story leaves out. That's the part I'm offering you a way around."

"You're offering me a way around," Mirabel said, "the same bargain."

"I'm offering you a better one. He got a salary and a silence. You get a fortune and a future." Roe spread his hands. "The flood is nine years old, Captain. It's silt now. It's geology. Nothing you carry up this gorge in an iron box will raise one drowned roof or un-drown one drowned child. But your crew eats this winter, or they don't. Your boat floats, or she doesn't. Those are the only facts on this table that move."

And that was the trap of him, Mirabel thought. Not the money. The *reasonableness*. He'd built the offer out of true things — the leaking seams, the owed wages, the dead who would stay dead — so that refusing it felt like refusing arithmetic itself. Her father had been handed the same kind of ledger, she realized. Tired men, bad numbers, a flood that was coming anyway. *Better it come managed.* The whole architecture of it, dressed as mercy, and the door out of it marked *prudence*.

She thought of Maren Oduya's face on the dock at the Landing, the spit, the grief underneath the hatred like a stone under shallow water. She thought of Ilan Reyes up at dawn with his photographs and his dead brother's name written in the front of every one of his charts. She thought of the strongbox in its cradle under the tarp, and of the ledger inside it, and of the signature she hadn't yet been able to make herself look at twice.

*Better it come managed.*

"No," Mirabel said.

Roe waited. He was a patient man; she could see the patience was a discipline, like his smile.

"The answer's no," she said. "Not *not yet*. Not *name a higher number*. No. Take your draft and your divers and your boots that haven't been wet, and go back down the river, Mr. Roe. The box stays up. The ledger goes where it goes. And the river can keep what it keeps — but it turns out it already kept me, and I'm not for sale either."

Tamsin let out a breath she'd been holding since the rolls arrived.

Roe stood. He did it slowly, without anger, gathering his coat, and for a moment — one unguarded moment — he looked tired, genuinely tired, the way he must have looked at a funeral with eleven people in attendance. Then the smile came back up like a gangplank.

"I hoped it would go otherwise," he said. "I'll confess I did hope. You're your father's daughter in more ways than one, Captain, and that's a hard inheritance." At the cabin door he paused. "I'll be at the Landing through the week. My offer stands until the divers arrive. After that —" he spread his hands, apologetic, helpless, a man surrendering to forces larger than himself — "after that, matters pass out of my hands entirely. I'd hate for you to be standing on this deck when they do."

He went down the ladder, and the hired men cast off, and the launch swung downstream with that same wasteful, unhurried confidence, showing the water who owned it.

Mirabel stood at the rail and watched it go, and her hands, which had been steady all through the cabin, began to shake now that it was over — now that it was *real*.

"Tamsin," she said. "Get Ilan. Get the crew. And get that box off my boat tonight."

"Why tonight?"

"Because he said no threats," Mirabel said, "and because Brack hasn't looked me in the eye since the launch came alongside, and because the divers are coming, and because — " She stopped, listening to her own certainty arrive like cold water into a hull. "Because we're out of time. We've been out of time since we signed his contract. I just didn't want to do the arithmetic."
