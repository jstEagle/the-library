## 13. Mutiny Weather

Tamsin felt the storm before she heard it — a pressure behind her ears like diving too deep, and a smell off the water that was not rain yet but the promise of it, green and mineral and old. She had grown up on the middle Rivergate, where storms arrived politely, building all afternoon before they broke. This one did not build. It simply *arrived*, the way a debt collector arrives, already through the door.

"Get the second anchor down," Mirabel said from the wheelhouse, and her voice was flat in a way Tamsin had learned to read over six years of running cargo together. Flat meant fear. Flat meant the captain had already done the arithmetic and didn't like the sum.

The Sparrowhawk lay moored in the lee of a gravel bar two miles below Trail's Landing, riding at anchor because there was no safe harbor between here and the gorge. The pay-barge's salvage gear was lashed amidships — the strongbox too, wrapped in canvas beneath the deck plates where Brack couldn't see it, though everyone knew he knew exactly where it sat. The ledger was inside it. Nine years of the Authority's dirty arithmetic, signed by men whose names still bought judges.

Tamsin dropped the kedge anchor into the brown flood and paid out line until her arms burned. Above her, the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the river — the river was wrong. It moved in layers now, surface current shoving one direction while something heavier dragged underneath, and the water level had come up a hand's width since noon. A hand's width in an hour, on a river already swollen with early rains.

Odji appeared beside her, hauling the anchor line's slack. "Captain says double-lash everything."

"I heard."

"She also says nobody goes ashore tonight."

Tamsin tied off the line and looked across the deck. Brack stood at the rail near the bow with three of Roe's hired hands — Sef, the Dann brothers — watching the water with an expression she recognized from card tables: a man counting what was his. He'd been counting for two days, ever since the strongbox came up out of the dark and Mirabel ordered it sealed below without opening it in front of him.

"He knows," Odji said quietly.

"Course he knows. He's known since the dive." Tamsin wiped her hands on her trousers. "Question is whether he's patient enough to wait for Roe to come collect it himself, or whether this weather makes him stupid."

The first rain hit like thrown gravel.

* * *

By full dark it wasn't rain anymore; it was the river falling upward and coming back down sideways, and the wind came down the gorge in long shoves that walked the Sparrowhawk's hull from side to side on her anchors. Tamsin took the midnight watch at the bow because she didn't trust anyone else to feel the anchor line properly — you had to hold it bare-handed, feel for that particular tremor that meant the flukes were walking instead of holding.

At half past one, she felt it.

Not a walk. A drag. A slow, grinding give, like a tooth loosening.

She was halfway to the wheelhouse when the lightning showed her the bank — or showed her where the bank had been. The gravel bar's downstream point had gone. The flood had eaten it whole, and the eddy that had sheltered them was gone with it, and the Sparrowhawk was swinging broadside into the current with forty tons of boat and gear and one iron strongbox full of other people's sins.

Mirabel was already in the wheelhouse. Of course she was. She stood at the wheel in oilskins with her hair plastered flat, and when Tamsin burst in she said, "I know," and rang the engine bell twice — stand by.

"We're dragging."

"I know, Tamsin."

"The bar's gone."

"*I know.*" And then, quieter, in the voice she used only when the truth would do damage: "We can't re-anchor in this. We ride it till dawn and pray the second anchor bites clay."

Tamsin looked out the wheelhouse glass at the black chaos of the river and understood what Mirabel wasn't saying: they were one bad hour from being driven onto the ruins above the Landing, onto the drowned rooftops and the dead pilings that had killed better boats than theirs.

That was when the shouting started aft.

* * *

It was Sef's voice first, then the Dann brothers, and Tamsin went down the companionway into the main cabin where lamplight swung wild with the hull's motion and half the crew stood in a knot around Brack, who had climbed onto the mess table so he could be seen over their heads.

"—three hundred each," he was saying, over the drum of the rain on the deck above. "Three hundred, paid the morning we hand it over, and passage home on Roe's own launch. Or you can stay aboard a leaking hull with a captain who's decided she'd rather drown than deliver. Your choice. Storm's making it for you either way."

"You don't have the ledger," Tamsin said from the doorway.

Brack turned. In the swinging light his face had the pleasant blankness of a man who has rehearsed this moment. "No. But I know which bunk it sleeps under, girl, and I know this crew better than you think. Half of 'em have been looking at me different since the box came up. Half of 'em have been doing sums." He spread his hands. "So let's make it simple. Hands up if you're for Roe's money."

Nobody moved. The lamp swung. Rain hammered.

Then Odji — steady, quiet Odji, who had run the Rivergate eleven years and never once argued with a captain — raised his hand. Slowly, not meeting Tamsin's eyes. And after him, Sef, and both the Danns, and young Pellik, who was nineteen and had a mother in debt upriver, and then more, until Tamsin counted seven of thirteen, and her stomach went cold in a way the storm hadn't managed.

Brack smiled. "There it is."

"It's not yours to sell," Tamsin said. "None of it. That ledger belongs to the drowned."

"The drowned aren't paying wages." He stepped down off the table. "And neither is your captain, once this hull opens up. Look at her seams, Tamsin. You've heard her pump. You think the Sparrowhawk sees morning? I'm offering these people a way off a sinking ship. That's not mutiny. That's mercy."

"That's theft."

"That's *arithmetic*." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that was the worst of it — because Tamsin had done the same arithmetic herself, alone, in the dark of the forepeak, and hated herself for the answer. Three hundred each against a leaking hull and a captain running toward something none of them fully understood. Fear made honest men fluent in Brack's language.

Mirabel filled the doorway behind her.

The cabin went silent except for the storm. She was soaked through, having crossed her own deck in that rain to hear it with her own ears, and she looked at the raised hands one by one, and Tamsin watched her captain take the count the way you take a wound — no flinch, just the slow recalibration of a body learning its new shape.

"Anyone who wants off," Mirabel said, "goes off at first light, at the Landing, with a fair share of what's in the wage box. I'll not hold anyone on a drowning boat."

"And the ledger?" Brack asked.

"The ledger isn't cargo. It doesn't get voted on."

"Everything gets voted on eventually, Captain. Rivers vote. Storms vote." He jerked his chin toward the ceiling, where the hull groaned like something living. "You're just losing the count."

* * *

They rode out the rest of the night by pumps and prayer. Tamsin worked the bilge handle in shifts, arms screaming, watching the water in the well rise and fall and rise again a little higher each time. Around four, the second anchor tore free of the clay and Mirabel ran the engine just enough to keep their bow into the flood, burning coal they'd need later, spending tomorrow to buy tonight.

At gray dawn the rain thinned to a steady sheeting fall, and the world resolved: high brown water, ruined banks, the stilt houses of Trail's Landing standing hip-deep in flood with survivors moving along their walkways like herons. The Sparrowhawk had dragged half a mile downstream in the night and hung now on the last anchor, sixty yards off a drowned orchard, holding — barely.

Tamsin found Mirabel at the rail, staring at the town.

"They'll want to go ashore," Tamsin said. "Your seven."

"I know."

"And Brack?"

Mirabel was quiet a long moment. "Brack will want what he always wanted. He'll just stop pretending he needs our permission." She turned, and there was something in her face Tamsin had never seen there before — not fear for the boat, not even fear for herself. Fear of being too late. "Watch him, Tam. Whatever happens today, whatever he offers, whatever he promises — watch him. Men like Brack don't steal things they intend to keep. They steal things they intend to *deliver*. Somewhere out in this weather there's a launch, and somewhere on that launch is Cassian Roe, and Brack's been rowing toward him in his head since the day we signed."

* * *

They went ashore mid-morning, the seven of them, ferried over in the dinghy to the Landing's flooded lower street where Maren Oduya's people received the deserters with the warmth of a dock receiving rats. Tamsin stayed aboard — someone had to — and helped Mirabel and Ilan shift the strongbox deeper into the lazarette, behind the fresh-water tank, wedged with spare rigging.

It was past noon when Tamsin noticed the sound.

Or rather, noticed the absence of one. Brack hadn't come back aboard with the others. She told herself he'd stayed ashore, taken Roe's offer early, good riddance. Then she saw the dinghy gone from its towline, and the lazarette hatch sitting wrong — seated but not dogged, the way a man closes a thing when his hands are shaking — and she was down the ladder before she'd finished thinking.

The canvas was there. The wedging was there. The strongbox was open, and empty, and inside the lid, folded neat as a napkin, lay a page torn from Brack's logbook:

*Gone to settle accounts. Don't follow. Roe pays better than ghosts.*

Tamsin came up the ladder two rungs at a time and out into the rain, and there — far out on the gray flood, small as a beetle, pulling hard for the downstream bend where the gorge narrowed — was the dinghy. And beyond it, faint through the rain-haze, a smudge of smoke that was not a campfire. Too steady. Too deliberate. A launch lying off Widow's Reach, waiting, with steam up.

She ran for the wheelhouse, shouting Mirabel's name, and even as she shouted she understood the shape of the next hour with terrible clarity: the Sparrowhawk could never catch that dinghy in this current — dragged anchor, spent coal, a hull drinking river through her seams. Nobody could make that run.

Nobody but Mirabel.

Tamsin burst through the wheelhouse door. "He's got it. Brack's got the ledger — he's rowing for Roe right now, off Widow's Reach—"

Mirabel was already at the wheel. Already ringing the engine bell. Already turning the Sparrowhawk's nose toward the flooded rapids below the bend, toward water no sane pilot would enter, toward the only road left.

"Tamsin," she said, eyes fixed on the white water ahead, "get everyone below and lash yourself to something."

"What are you going to do?"

Her captain's hands settled on the spokes, and for the first time in nine years, Mirabel Vane smiled at the river like she knew it personally.

"I'm going home the fast way."
