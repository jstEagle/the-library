## 14. Chase in the Gorge

The ledger was gone.

Mirabel knew it before Tamsin said a word — knew it in the way the deck tilted wrong under her boots, in the empty space where the oilskin bundle had been lashed beneath the pilothouse bench, in Odji's face when he came up the ladder white as river foam. Brack's dory was missing from its davits. The rain came down in ropes, and somewhere downstream, in all that gray violence of water, a man was rowing for Cassian Roe's launch with nine years of buried truth in his coat.

"He took the skiff," Tamsin shouted over the storm. "Five minutes ahead, maybe less. Mirabel — the Sparrowhawk can't run these rapids. She's taking water already. You saw the seams."

Mirabel looked at her boat. The old girl lay against her lines like a tired animal, gunwales low, the bilge pump chugging its stubborn rhythm. Below decks she could hear the river whispering through her hull, patient as a debt collector. Every season Mirabel had coaxed out of her had been borrowed. She'd known it since the shipwright ran his thumb along the opened seam at the trading post and said the word *condemned* without looking up.

But the Sparrowhawk had one more run in her. Mirabel could feel it the way you feel a held breath — not strength, exactly. Permission.

"Get everyone off who wants to go off," she said. "I'm running Widow's Teeth."

"You're what?" Odji's voice cracked on it.

"The Teeth funnel at this stage." She was already moving, throwing off the mooring lines with hands that remembered every knot without her. "Flood crest narrows the channel to the eastern throat. A rowboat can't hold that current — he'll be pushed toward the calmer water on the far side, past the second tooth, where the eddy pools behind the rock shelf. That's where I take him."

"That's where the river takes *you*," Tamsin said. "Nobody runs the Teeth at flood stage. Nobody."

"Somebody built those rapids a map once." Mirabel swung into the pilothouse and put her hand on the wheel, and the wood was warm from her palm the way it had been warm for nine years, and something in her chest went quiet and cold and clear. "I did. Every sandbar, every snag, every lie this river tells. It's the only honest thing I own."

Tamsin stared at her through the rain. Then she swore, long and inventive, and started hauling Odji toward the bow. "Then I'm staying. Somebody has to bail while you kill us."

"Get below. Seal the aft hatch. If I shout *drop*, you drop everything and hold on to something bolted down."

Ilan Reyes appeared at the foot of the ladder, soaked through, the strongbox documents strapped to his chest under his coat. His eyes went from Mirabel to the boiling brown water beyond the rail and back again.

"You'll need someone reading the water," he said.

"I need someone alive to carry that ledger if I fail."

"Then we'd both better succeed." He climbed into the pilothouse and braced himself against the chart table, and there was nothing left to argue about, because the current had already taken the bow.

* * *

The Sparrowhawk went into the gorge like a leaf going over a weir.

Mirabel had run bad water her whole life — low-water threads through sandbar mazes, night passages in fog, the kind of channels other pilots called suicide and called her worse. But this was different. This was the river unbound, the monsoon crest coming down out of the high country with the whole sky behind it, and the gorge had become a single muscle of brown water flexing between walls of black stone. Rain hit the surface so hard it bounced. The spray came up in sheets and erased the far bank entirely.

She didn't look at the water. That was the first secret of it, the thing no one ever believed: in the worst rapids, the surface tells you nothing but lies. You read the rocks. You read the way the current bent around them, the smooth glassy tongues where the deep water ran and the ugly broken V's where it didn't. You read the standing waves and counted their intervals, because the river breathed in rhythm even when it roared, and if you found the rhythm you found the door.

There. The eastern throat — a tongue of smoother water threading between two fangs of granite, barely wider than the Sparrowhawk herself, and the whole flood pressing toward it like a crowd through a gate.

"Hold on," she said, though nobody could have heard her.

The bow lifted. For three heartbeats they were flying — actually flying, hull slapping down spray, the gorge walls blurring green-black on either side — and then the current took the stern and tried to swing her broadside, the death-move, the one that put boats on rocks and crews in the water. Mirabel had been waiting for it. She fed the wheel hard to starboard, felt the rudder bite, felt the whole boat pivot on some invisible fulcrum, and brought her nose back around just as the first fang slid past close enough to read the moss on it.

Below, the second tooth. Behind it, the eddy — a great slow whirl of calmer water pooled in the rock shelf's lee, spinning counterclockwise, and there, God, *there*, the dory: a tiny dark shape wallowing in the boil, one man rowing with the desperate flat strokes of a man who had never learned that the river cannot be argued with.

Brack had made it past the teeth. He thought he'd won. He was pulling for the far bank, for the launch that would be waiting below the gorge, and he had maybe four minutes before the eddy spat him out into the lower current and Roe's men took him aboard.

Mirabel brought the Sparrowhawk around the back of the rock shelf, riding the eddy's shoulder, letting the whirlpool do the work her leaking hull couldn't. The boat groaned beneath her. Through the sole boards she could hear the bilge working double-time, and Tamsin's voice from below, counting depth marks in a voice that pretended not to be afraid.

"He sees us," Ilan said.

Brack had stopped rowing. He turned in the dory, and even across forty yards of churning water Mirabel could see him make his calculation — the same arithmetic he'd done with the radio hidden in the cargo hold, the same cold sums. He looked at the Sparrowhawk. He looked downstream. And he pulled an oilskin bundle from inside his coat and held it up over his head.

"No," Mirabel breathed.

He wasn't surrendering it. He was showing her he had it — and then he hurled it, sidearm, out across the water toward the downstream current, toward the black shape of the steam launch waiting below the gorge with its lights burning yellow through the rain.

The bundle hit the water and vanished instantly, swallowed by brown flood.

For one insane second Mirabel considered diving after it. Then the river reminded her where she was: the eddy's edge grabbed the Sparrowhawk's stern and hauled, and she fought the wheel, wrestling the boat back into the calm eye of the pool while the dory drifted toward them, Brack now rowing hard — not away, but *at* them, trying to reach the Sparrowhawk's lines before she could recover, trying to turn capture into negotiation.

"Bring us alongside," Ilan said. "Carefully —"

"I know how to come alongside in an eddy, Ilan."

She did it clean, the way she'd done ten thousand times in ten thousand ports: bow line first, spring line second, the two hulls kissing with a wooden knock that sounded almost gentle in all that roar. Odji and Tamsin were on Brack before he got a boot over the rail — Tamsin had a boathook across his chest and Odji had his collar, and Brack came over the gunwale like landed fish, swearing, thrashing, and stopped dead when Mirabel put herself between him and the pilothouse.

"The ledger," she said. "Where's the copy? There were copies. Ilan said —"

"Roe's got the only one that matters now." Brack grinned through the blood on his lip. Rain hammered his bald head. "You think I threw him the original? I threw him a decoy, woman. Wrapped around a brick. The real ledger's in my coat, and my coat is worth more than your whole rotten boat." He patted his chest, and Mirabel saw the hard rectangular outline under the wet wool, and understood.

He hadn't passed it to the launch yet. He'd been waiting — waiting to be *taken* aboard, waiting for the safety of Roe's deck, because a thing thrown into a flood might drown but a thing handed over was a debt paid.

Which meant the ledger was still within reach. It was standing right in front of her, breathing hard, smiling.

"Take him below," Mirabel said. "Lash him to the mast step. And somebody find me that coat."

Brack lunged. It was a good lunge — big man, desperate, aimed at the rail rather than at her, choosing the river over captivity. He got one leg over the gunwale before Tamsin's boathook caught him across the thighs and Odji landed on his back with his whole weight, and the three of them went down in a tangle on the wet deck, and Mirabel stepped past them, knelt, and cut the coat's buttons off with her belt knife in two strokes.

The oilskin bundle was inside, next to his heart, exactly where he'd kept it.

She stood up holding nine years of Trail's Landing in her hands, and the rain came down, and Brack stopped fighting, because there was suddenly nothing left to fight for.

* * *

They heard the launch before they saw it — the deep steam-chuff rolling up the gorge like distant artillery, and then the yellow lights resolved out of the rain, and Cassian Roe's boat came around the last bend below Widow's Teeth with the flood crest lifting it high and its paddle wheel turning slow and deliberate, the way everything about Cassian Roe was slow and deliberate.

It was bigger than Mirabel remembered. Two decks, brass rails gleaming even in the rain, a dozen men along the rail with lanterns. It came upstream against a current that would have stopped any other vessel, because money buys powerful engines, and it slowed to a stop fifty yards off the Sparrowhawk's beam, close enough to see faces, too far to throw anything.

Cassian Roe stood at the rail under an umbrella held by one of his men. Even at that distance, even through rain, Mirabel could see him smile.

"Miz Vane!" His voice carried easily over the water, warm as a trading-post stove. "You've had an eventful evening."

"Give it up, Roe," she shouted back. "I have the ledger. My people have your foreman. The magistrate in Rivergate has a name now, and so does the widow on Copper Lane. It's over."

"Is it?" Roe tilted his head, considering, and the umbrella tilted with him. "Let me tell you what I see, Miz Vane. I see a condemned hull sitting in floodwater in the middle of a gorge at night. I see a crew that half of them signed on with Brack's arithmetic and the other half are too loyal to admit they're drowning. I see a woman holding a bundle of paper that isn't worth her life unless she lives long enough to spend it." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "The river doesn't care whose truth wins, Miz Vane. It only cares about tonnage. And I am heavier than you."

The launch's engine note changed. Deeper. The wheel began to turn — not toward them, but downstream. Leaving.

And Mirabel understood, with a clarity that felt like the cold water rising through the Sparrowhawk's seams, that he was right about everything except the ending. He didn't need to fight her. He only needed to outrun her to Rivergate City, where his lawyers were already awake, where the magistrate who'd buried the original docket still held whatever influence nine years of bribes had purchased. The ledger in her hands was paper. Paper needed a courtroom. Courtrooms needed time, and time was the one commodity flowing downstream faster than anything else in this gorge.

Unless she beat him there.

She looked at the wheel under her hands. She looked at the flooded channel below Widow's Teeth — the long chute of white water where the gorge narrowed and the current ran eleven knots if it ran one, the stretch the rivermen called the Long Throat, the stretch no pilot had ever run downstream at flood stage, because running *down* meant you couldn't power out of a mistake, you could only read the water faster than it killed you.

Nobody had ever run it. Nobody except the woman who'd mapped every rock in it at low water, who knew that the fang called the Deacon split the current into two tongues and that the left tongue lied, who knew the submerged bar below the church rocks shifted every season but always shifted the same way, leftward, greedy, reaching for hulls.

"Tamsin," she said quietly. "How much water in the bilge?"

"Six inches and gaining. Maybe eight."

"Can she take the Long Throat?"

Tamsin followed her gaze downstream, to the roaring white chute, and Mirabel watched her oldest friend arrive at the same understanding and hate it. "She can take anything for ten minutes," Tamsin said finally. "Question is what's left of her after."

"Ten minutes is all I need."

"And if it isn't?"

Mirabel put the ledger inside her shirt, against her skin, where the rain couldn't touch it. "Then Odji takes the papers and swims for it, and you all tell the story at my wake." She spun the wheel. "Sparrowhawk, answer to her helm. We're going downstream."

Behind them, Brack began to laugh — a hollow, defeated sound, half horror. "You're mad," he called. "That water will tear her apart!"

"She's been torn apart for nine years," Mirabel said, and opened the throttle, and the old boat surged forward off the eddy's shoulder into the dark.

The launch's lights shrank ahead of them, yellow eyes fleeing down the gorge. The first standing wave rose up out of the rain like a wall, taller than the pilothouse, and Mirabel took it at a quarter-off and felt the whole world become water — and beneath her fear, beneath the groaning of the dying hull and the roar of the flood, there was a strange and terrible peace, because for the first time in nine years she was running toward something instead of away, and every rock in this river knew her name.

She gave the wheel a spoke to port, reading the white water ahead, and the Sparrowhawk answered.
