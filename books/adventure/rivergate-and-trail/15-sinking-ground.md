## 15. Sinking Ground

The Sparrowhawk had been dying for hours, and Mirabel had been lying to her the whole time.

"You're fine," she murmured into the wheel's worn spokes, the way she'd talked to the boat since she was nineteen years old and her father first put her hands on the timber. "You're fine, old girl. Just a little water in your boots."

But the bilge pump was running every four minutes now instead of ten, and Tamsin had stopped reporting the sound of it, which meant it had gone past the point where numbers helped. The monsoon rain came down in gray sheets that erased the gorge walls entirely, so that the river seemed to run through cloud, through nothing, through the inside of some drowned god's throat. The flood crest was behind them — they'd outrun it, mostly — but the Rivergate at high water was a different animal from the one Mirabel knew, wider by half, faster by more, its sandbars drowned or moved or simply gone, its channels rewritten overnight.

She knew this stretch anyway. She knew it the way you know the face of someone you loved before you learned to lie.

"Two hundred yards," she called to Odji, who stood braced at the bow rail with a lead line he could barely throw straight in the wind. "There's a bar off the left bank where the current splits. Old-timers called it the Loaf. If we can get her nose onto it—"

"If we can get her nose onto it," Odji shouted back, "we'll be standing on a sandbar in the middle of a flood."

"That's the plan!"

He didn't argue. Nine years he'd crewed for her, and in nine years he'd learned that when Mirabel Vane started shouting plans that sounded like disasters, the disasters were usually the safer option.

* * *

It had happened fast, the way fatal things do.

They'd caught Brack's rowboat at Widow's Teeth — or rather, they'd watched Brack hand the ledger across to Roe's launch in the white confusion below the Teeth, watch the launch turn downstream with the truth of Trail's Landing lashed under its tarpaulin like any other cargo. Mirabel had put the Sparrowhawk hard about to follow, and the boat had answered, because the Sparrowhawk always answered, right up until she couldn't.

The strain of the turn had opened something in her hull that the grounding above the Landing had cracked and the chase had worried wide. Tamsin felt it before anyone — a change in the boat's voice, a new note under the engine's drone, low and wet and wrong. She'd come up the ladder two rungs at a time and said only, "Mirabel. Come listen."

Mirabel had listened. Then she'd stopped chasing Cassian Roe and started fighting for her ship, and there was no question in her mind which fight mattered, not even with the ledger — the ledger, the whole reason for all of it — sliding away downstream behind a wall of rain.

A boat is not a thing. That was what her father had told her, once, in the last year of his life, when she'd asked why he spent his Sundays down at the mooring scraping barnacles off a hull nobody else would have looked twice at. A boat is a promise you keep every day. You keep it or you don't.

She had kept it for nine years. Now the river was calling the debt due.

* * *

"The seam's gone along the garboard," Tamsin reported, coming back from the hold soaked to the shoulders, her face gray in the lamplight swinging below. "I can't reach it. I can't even see all of it. Water's coming in faster than the pump. Faster than both pumps if we had two."

"How long?"

Tamsin didn't answer right away, and Mirabel understood that the silence was Tamsin doing arithmetic she hated.

"Tamsin. How long."

"An hour. Maybe less if we hit anything. Maybe more if the river's kind." Tamsin wiped her face with a sleeve that only spread the water around. "The river isn't kind, Mirabel."

No. It wasn't. It never had been; that was the first lesson of the Rivergate and the last one too. But it was legible, if you'd spent your life reading it, and Mirabel had spent hers doing nothing else. She turned the problem over in her mind the way she turned the wheel — hands-on, no sentiment, just the shape of the water and the shape of her options.

Beach her. That was the only word for it, though it broke something in her chest to think it. Get the Sparrowhawk onto solid ground before the ground became the bottom of the gorge. Save the people. Save what cargo and gear could be carried. Let the boat take the water on a bar instead of in forty feet of black current, where she'd vanish without a trace and maybe take a crew member down with her.

The Loaf. Two miles down, left bank, where the current split around a shelf of gravel that had been there since before the flood and would be there after. She'd grounded on it once at seventeen, learning, and her father had laughed — actually laughed, the only time she remembered him laughing at her mistakes instead of going quiet over them — and said, *Now you know her better than the chart does.*

She knew the Loaf better than the chart did. The chart was nine years old anyway. Everything was nine years old.

"Odji! Get everyone topside. Life vests, the ditch bag, the tools, the strongbox papers — Ilan's satchel, everything dry matters. We're putting her aground."

"Aground?" That was Pell, one of the younger hands, his voice cracking somewhere between question and protest. "On purpose?"

"On purpose." Mirabel hauled the wheel over and felt the Sparrowhawk lean into the turn with the last of her strength, faithful to the end. "Everyone off within the hour, or the river takes us all. Move."

* * *

The next forty minutes were the hardest piloting of her life, and she had run the flooded rapids above Widow's Teeth an hour ago.

High water hid the landmarks. The rain hid the banks. The current ran three knots harder than the same channel did in dry season, and every instinct she'd built over twenty years on this river screamed against the maneuver she was attempting — you did not steer toward a sandbar, you steered away from sandbars, always, forever, that was rule one, carved into every pilot who ever lived long enough to have rules.

But the Sparrowhawk was sinking, and rule one was for boats that intended to stay afloat.

"There!" Odji pointed through the rain. "White water, left side — is that the split?"

Mirabel squinted into the murk. A tongue of broken water, foaming where current tore over shallows, angling out from the left bank. Below it, the faintest difference in texture where the river flowed over gravel instead of depth. Most pilots would have seen nothing. She saw the Loaf the way you recognize a friend in a crowd from behind — by the set of the shoulders, the tilt of the head.

"That's it. Stand by to fend off starboard. When I give the word, everyone forward — shift her weight to the bow, get the stern light."

"Why forward?"

"Because I want her nose buried and her stern dry, and I want her settled before the current decides for us. Go!"

The Sparrowhawk drove at the bar. Mirabel held her there — dead on, no correction, trusting a memory nine years old with everything she had left — and then the world changed under the keel. A shudder ran the length of the ship, bow to stern, the deep grinding shudder of a hull meeting earth. The wheel went heavy, then strange, then useless. The current took the stern and swung it downstream like a door on a hinge, and Mirabel rode it, feeding the engine just enough to keep the swing controlled, walking the boat around onto the bar the way you'd lower a wounded friend onto a bed.

"Forward! Now!"

The crew scrambled. The bow dug deeper. The stern rose a hand's width, then another, as the weight shifted, and the grinding eased into a groan, and then into stillness — the terrible, blessed stillness of a boat that has stopped moving and will never move again.

Silence, except the rain.

Mirabel stood at the wheel with her hands still on the spokes, holding a course that no longer existed, steering a ship that was now, permanently, part of the riverbed.

"Off," Tamsin said quietly, beside her. "Mirabel. Everyone's off but you. Off."

* * *

They got everything that mattered onto the bar: the ditch bag, the tools, the charts — her charts, sodden but salvageable — Ilan's satchel with the ledger's shipping marks copied out in his careful cartographer's hand, the coil of good line, the axe, the lamp oil, the small iron cashbox with the crew's wage fund that Mirabel had guarded through nine lean seasons. The rain hammered down. The flood roared past them on both sides, brown and enormous, carrying branches, carrying a drowned canoe, carrying — somewhere far downstream — Cassian Roe and the ledger and the truth of Trail's Landing.

The Sparrowhawk lay heeled gently to starboard on the gravel, her bow wedged into the bar's crown, her stern just clear of the current's main pull. Water stood shin-deep in her cabin. Her name was visible on the transom, the paint flaking, the letters she'd repainted herself six times over nine years: SPARROWHAWK. She looked, Mirabel thought, like a bird that had landed and forgotten how the sky worked.

"She'll hold till the water drops," Odji said. He'd come to stand beside her, saying nothing else, offering nothing else, because there was nothing else to offer and he knew it.

"When the water drops, she'll be a wreck on a mudflat." Mirabel heard her own voice come out flat and strange, as if someone else were using it. "Her seams are opened. Her timbers will swell and twist drying out. Even if we pumped her, even if we careened her—" She stopped. There was no point finishing the sentence. They both knew how it ended. The shipwright at the mid-river post had told her in September what a full refit would cost, and she hadn't had the money then, and now she had no boat to refit.

Nine years. Nine years of sleeping aboard, eating aboard, of knowing every plank and every leak and every place where the wood had been patched by her father's hands before it was patched by hers. Nine years of being able to leave anywhere, anytime, of never having to stay in a port long enough for anyone to ask the wrong questions. The Sparrowhawk hadn't just been a boat. She'd been the running itself. She'd been the answer to the question *what are you running from?* — because as long as the boat moved, the question never had to be answered.

And now the boat was done, and the question was standing right there in the rain, patient as the river.

Tamsin crouched at the waterline, checking the tie-offs they'd rigged to a snag of driftwood, professional to the last, giving Mirabel the privacy of work. Ilan Reyes stood apart, staring upstream, his jaw tight — already calculating, Mirabel knew, how many days Roe's launch had on them, whether the widow in Rivergate City could be reached in time, whether the truth would arrive before the money buried it. He hadn't said a word about the lost ledger. He didn't have to. His face said it.

Pell sat on the gravel with his head in his hands. Odji counted the supplies again, though he'd counted them twice already. The rain fell. The river rose, slow and implacable, licking higher up the Sparrowhawk's hull.

Mirabel walked the length of the bar alone.

She told herself she was checking the lines. She checked the lines. Then she kept walking, to the bow, where the gravel shelved down into the flood, and she stood looking at the boat from outside for the first time in nine years — really outside, seeing her the way a stranger would see her, a battered old river freighter aground on a bar with her seams open and her working days behind her.

Her father had bought the Sparrowhawk's hull new, before Mirabel was born. He'd named her for her mother, who used to say she had a sparrow's heart — small, quick, always in motion. Mirabel had grown up in this boat's shadow, learned to read water from her deck, learned to lie from her wheelhouse, learned to run from her wake. Every port she'd refused to stay in, every letter she'd burned unread, every year she'd stayed away from Trail's Landing — all of it had been possible because the Sparrowhawk was underneath her, holding her up, carrying her away.

And now the river had taken her, and Mirabel was standing on a strip of gravel in the middle of a flood with nowhere to be carried, and the grief arrived all at once, the way the flood crest had arrived, sudden and total and impossible to stand against.

She didn't decide to cry. It wasn't a decision. It was a structural failure, like the garboard seam — something held too long under too much strain finally letting go. Her knees went first. She sat down hard on the wet gravel, and the sound she made didn't seem like it could come from a person, a raw tearing sound that the rain swallowed whole, and then she was weeping — not the quick hot tears of anger or fear she'd known all her life, but the deep, ugly, full-body grief of a woman mourning a living thing.

For the boat. For the boat, yes — for the warm smell of her engine room, the particular rattle of her cabin stove, the way her wheel spoke to your palms in rough water. For nine years of mornings waking to the river through the porthole. For the only home she'd had since she was fourteen years old.

And for everything else the boat had carried without complaint. For her father, dead nine years, blamed nine years, defended by his daughter's silence for nine years. For Trail's Landing, drowned and half-alive on its stilts. For Maren Oduya's spit and Maren Oduya's grief. For the ledger in Cassian Roe's hold, riding away downstream on the money of men who had opened the gates. For the girl she'd been at fourteen, who had believed her father was innocent, and the woman she'd become, who had never let herself find out.

The crew let her weep. That was the mercy of it. Nobody came. Nobody called out. Tamsin kept checking lines. Odji kept counting boxes. And Mirabel Vane, who had not cried since the day they buried her father in a pauper's plot upstream of everything, sat on a drowning sandbar and cried until there was nothing left in her, while the rain came down and the river rose around the hull of the only thing she'd ever trusted.

When it was over, she was cold to the bone and empty in a way that felt less like loss than like clearing. The way a channel feels after a flood: scoured, changed, but open.

She stood. Wiped her face with both hands. Walked back to the others.

* * *

Tamsin met her eyes and said nothing about the crying, which was its own kind of kindness. "Lines'll hold tonight. Bar's stable — I probed it. Gravel down six feet at least."

"We're not staying on the bar." Mirabel's voice came out scraped raw but steady. "Flood's still climbing. By tomorrow noon this could be six feet underwater. We need the stilt houses."

Odji looked up sharply. "Trail's Landing? After—" He glanced at Tamsin, at Ilan, and lowered his voice. "After how they took to you?"

"After how they took to me." Mirabel looked downstream, toward the ruined town she'd spent nine years refusing to see, where Maren Oduya lived among the survivors of a flood her father's hands had helped open. "We've got no boat, no radio, and no food past three days. And Ilan needs to get those shipping marks to Rivergate City before Roe's lawyers get to the widow. There's a ferry landing at the Landing. Or what's left of one. And there are people there who know these waters better than any chart."

"They hate you."

"Some of them do." She thought of Maren's face in the lamplight, the night by the fire, the questions Mirabel had neither confirmed nor denied. "Some of them might. But Maren Oduya lost more than I did, and she stayed. She rebuilt on stilts over the water that killed her family. She's the strongest person on this river, and I'm asking her for help, and I'll take whatever she gives me — shelter, scorn, both. We don't have another option, and I'm done pretending otherwise."

Ian lifted his head. "The ferry landing," he said slowly. "If there's a working boat—"

"There's a flat-bottomed ferry, if the flood hasn't taken it. Shallow draft. Built for this water." Mirabel looked back once at the Sparrowhawk, heeled on her bar, rain streaming off her deck, her name running in the wet. "We rest tonight. At first light, we walk into Trail's Landing, and I ask Maren Oduya for a boat."

"And if she says no?"

Mirabel picked up her share of the load and settled it on her shoulders.

"Then I'll ask again," she said. "I'm done running from that town. One way or another, I'm going to stand in front of it and be seen."

They started downstream along the bar's spine, five figures in the rain, leaving the Sparrowhawk to the rising water behind them.
