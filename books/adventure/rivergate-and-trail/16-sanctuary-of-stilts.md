## 16. Sanctuary of Stilts

Maren Oduya had learned to read storms the way other people read faces, and the one coming down the gorge that evening wore the expression of something with a grudge. The clouds had been stacking all afternoon behind the eastern ridges — not the gray wash of ordinary rain but a bruised, low-bellied mass that swallowed the light whole. By the time she'd latched her shutters and hauled in the drying racks from the walkway, the first drops were hitting the stilts like thrown gravel.

She was counting her stores by lamplight when the knocking started.

Not on her door. On the posts below — three hard raps, then silence, then more, urgent and arrhythmic, the way people knock when they've stopped being polite about it. She took down the boat hook and went out onto the platform, rain already needling the back of her neck.

Half a dozen figures stood at the foot of her ladder, drenched through, clinging to the stilt house's cross-braces as brown water shoved past their knees. She knew them without wanting to. The Sparrowhawk's crew — Tamsin with her arm around a boy no older than sixteen whose lips had gone blue; the cartographer, Reyes, holding a satchel above his head like an offering; two others she'd seen hauling lines on the wreck. And behind them, half in the water, hair plastered flat against her skull, hands white-knuckled on the lowest rung —

Mirabel Vane.

Maren stood very still. Nine years of rehearsing this moment, and it arrived looking nothing like she'd imagined. In her imagination, Vane came arrogant, dry-shod, asking favors with her chin up. In reality she looked hollowed out, salt-scoured, her eyes red-rimmed in a face gone gray with exhaustion — and there was no boat behind her. Maren scanned the flooded street twice before she accepted it. No Sparrowhawk. Just the river, high and furious, carrying branches and a drowned crate and what might have been a roof panel past the pilings.

"Her ship went down," Tamsin shouted over the rain. "Sandbar below Widow's Teeth. We got everyone off, but we've got nothing — no shelter, no dry goods, and this water's still rising. Please. One night. Maybe two."

The word *please* did something Maren resented immediately.

"You know who she is," Maren said. Not a question. Her voice came out flatter than she intended, scraped thin by the wind.

"I know who she is," Tamsin said. "I also know she ran the Teeth in a sinking hull to save a man who stole from her. I know what I saw."

"And I know what I saw." Nine years of it. The water coming down the valley like a living thing. The bell tower going under. Her brother's name read aloud at the memorial every year because they'd never found enough of him to bury.

Lightning split the dark, close enough that the thunder arrived almost with the flash, and the youngest crew member flinched so hard he nearly lost his grip. The water was at Maren's lower steps now. She could feel the house shuddering faintly on its pilings, the old timber flexing the way it always did when the river got ideas.

*Hospitality is the law of the stilts.* Her grandmother's voice, unbidden. *You feed whoever knocks, because next flood season you're the one knocking.* It had been law once, before the flood, before the town became a ruin with residents. Maren had thought the law died with everything else.

Apparently it hadn't. Apparently it had just been waiting nine years for her to hate keeping it.

"Get them up," she said, and stepped back from the door. "All of them. Boots off at the platform."

* * *

They filled her single room the way floodwater fills a cellar — completely, silently, taking up every space she'd thought was hers. She gave them the lamp oil without comment, set her stove going with the last of her dry kindling, and put on rice to boil because her hands needed work or they'd start shaking. The crew spread out along the walls, dripping, murmuring thanks in voices roughened by cold. Tamsin wrapped the boy in Maren's spare blanket and didn't ask permission, which Maren decided to forgive.

Vane stood last at the threshold, dripping onto the boards, and for a long moment neither woman spoke.

"I can sleep on the platform," Vane said finally.

"You'll sleep where there's a roof, same as the others." Maren turned back to the stove. "The river doesn't care about your penance. Neither do my floorboards."

That shut her up. Maren heard her move to the far corner, the corner farthest from the door, and sit down with her back to the wall like someone accustomed to making herself small. It should have satisfied something in Maren. It didn't. It sat in her chest like a stone she couldn't cough up.

The storm built through the night hours. Rain came sideways off the gorge walls in sheets, and the river rose in visible surges, each one climbing another handspan up the pilings. Somewhere downstream something large struck a house and the whole settlement heard the crack of it. Nobody slept much. Maren kept the stove fed and the rice moving and watched her unwanted guests the way she'd watch weather — constantly, warily, waiting for the change.

It was near midnight, the others finally sunk into exhausted half-sleep, when Vane spoke from her corner.

"My father's name was Edric Vane."

Maren's ladle stopped mid-stir.

"He was the Authority's senior engineer on the upper gates. Everyone here knows that part. What they don't know—" Vane's voice caught, steadied. "What nobody knows is that he filed an objection. Three weeks before the flood. A formal engineering objection, in writing, saying the sluice schedule would overstress the north abutment. He recommended delaying the release. They ordered him to open the gates anyway. He refused. So they relieved him of command and opened them themselves — and when the abutment failed exactly the way he said it would, they wrote his name into the record as the engineer of record. They made him the reason instead of the witness."

The rain filled the silence. Maren found she was gripping the stove handle hard enough to hurt.

"He spent the rest of his life trying to get the record corrected," Vane went on, quieter now. "Petitions, hearings, letters. Every one buried. He died four years ago with the file still marked denied. I never came back here because—" A breath. "Because I thought if the Landing hated a Vane, it was right to. I thought maybe I deserved the running. Tonight I found out the ledger exists. The real ledger. With the orders in it. With Roe's signature among the signatories. It's going to court, Maren. Or it's going to die trying."

Maren turned around slowly.

She had imagined this conversation for nine years too — the confrontation, the accusation, the confession she'd extract like a rotten tooth. She had rehearsed what she'd say when Mirabel Vane finally admitted what her father did. She had not rehearsed for this. For the possibility that the story had a floor beneath it, and that she'd been standing on the wrong level all this time.

"That's a convenient story," she said, because grief doesn't surrender its architecture easily. "A dead man can't argue."

"It's in the ledger," Reyes said softly from the wall, not opening his eyes. "I've read it. The objection is dated, signed, and stamped received. The order overriding it is dated six days later. Both are in the documents going to the magistrate's clerk in Rivergate City." He paused. "Your brother's name is in the casualty register too. Page four. I checked. I'm sorry."

Maren hadn't told anyone on this boat about Dario. She was certain of it. She looked at Vane, who met her eyes across the lamplit room and didn't look away, and understood that the cartographer had simply done what cartographers do — read everything, held everything, given it back when it mattered.

The rice boiled over. Maren cursed and rescued it, and used the small violence of the moment to keep her face together.

* * *

Toward dawn the storm reached the pitch where sound stops mattering — a single vast roar, wind and water and the groan of timber all fused into one pressure. Maren worked the house like a sailor works a deck: checking lashings, bailing the platform, moving stores to the loft. The crew worked beside her without being asked. That was the thing about river people, even strangers — the flood made everyone crew.

It was during the fourth hour of bailing that she found herself next to Vane on the windward side, both of them hauling the canvas cover down over the wood store, shouting things neither could hear. When the gust passed they stood a moment in the relative quiet, soaked to the skin, breathing hard.

"The stilts on the east row are going," Vane said. "I watched them shift on the last surge. Anyone living there needs to be moved tonight."

"There's no one on the east row. There hasn't been since the flood." Maren wiped her face. "You'd know that if you'd ever come back."

"Yes," Vane said. "I would."

No excuse. No deflection. Maren had braced for the smooth deflections — she remembered them from the market days, from the regatta, from every time young Mirabel Vane had talked her way around a consequence. Their absence was somehow worse. It left nothing to push against.

"Why salvage?" Maren asked. "Roe's money. Why take it? You knew what this place was to you."

"Because my hull was dying and my crew needed wages, and I told myself it was only business." Vane looked out at the churning black water. "I'm very good at telling myself things. It's how I survived nine years. Turns out surviving isn't the same as living. I only learned the difference when the Sparrowhawk went down." Her voice frayed on the boat's name. "I cried for her. On that sandbar. Forty-one years old and I cried like the hull was my mother. Because she was the last thing that let me keep moving. You can't run without a keel."

Maren said nothing. But something shifted in her, tectonic and unwelcome — the recognition that she and this woman had spent nine years drowning in different rooms of the same house.

"Dario played the fiddle," she heard herself say. Vane turned. "At the harvest fair, the year before. Badly. Everyone clapped anyway." Her throat closed. She hadn't spoken his name aloud in three years. "Every year at the memorial they read his name and I stand there like it's a stranger's. Like the river took someone I never met." She stared at the water. "If your father tried to close the gates—"

"He did. He rowed out to the north sluice himself the night of the flood. He was still arguing with the duty officer when the abutment let go." Vane's jaw tightened. "He carried that night until it killed him. I carry it too. But I'd rather carry it here than anywhere else on this river. That's why I came back. Not Roe's money. Not anymore."

The storm screamed. Below them the river climbed another inch, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

By full light the worst had passed into a steady, grinding rain, and the east row — empty these nine years — folded into the current with a sound like a long sigh. Maren stood on her platform and watched the houses she'd grown up beside go under for the second time, and this time she felt less grief than a strange, hard clarity. The river was finishing what it started. What remained after this would be what remained for good.

She went inside, where the crew was eating the last of the rice, and stood over Vane.

"When the water drops," Maren said, "you'll want a boat. The ferry's sitting in the boathouse — flat-bottomed, shallow draft, ugly as sin. She'll run the flood crest better than anything on this river except maybe you." She crossed her arms. "Take her. Get to Rivergate City. Get those papers to the widow before Roe's lawyers do."

Vane blinked. "Maren—"

"Don't thank me. Don't apologize either. I've had nine years of anger and I'm not giving it up in one night — it's load-bearing." She allowed herself the ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "But my grandmother had a law about hospitality, and I broke it getting you in, and the only way to make it square is to see you out again alive. So go. Win. Come back and tell the Landing what really happened to their town — standing on our dock, in daylight, where everyone can hear."

Vane rose. For a moment Maren thought she might reach out, and braced herself — but Vane only nodded, once, deep, the way river people nod at graves.

"One condition," Maren added. "When you testify — and you will testify — you say my brother's name. Dario Oduya. Say it in the courtroom, in front of the magistrates and Roe's fine lawyers. Make them hear it."

"I will," Vane said. "Every day they'll let me speak."

Outside, the rain thinned toward the west, and far downstream, past the drowned east row and the ruined bell tower, the road to Rivergate City lay open under the clearing sky — and somewhere along it, Maren knew, Cassian Roe's lawyers were already moving.
