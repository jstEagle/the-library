## 17. The Ledger's Road

Ilan had not slept in the ordinary way for three days. He slept the way a man sleeps in a house where someone is dying — lightly, with one ear open, waking at every sound as if it might be the last one. In Maren Oduya's stilt house, the sounds were many: rain on the tin roof like gravel poured from a great height, the river chewing at the pilings below, Tamsin's boots on the ladder, Mirabel's voice low and sleepless in the next room, arguing with a river that could not hear her.

He sat at the table by lamplight with the strongbox ledger open before him, and he did what he had trained himself to do for nine years, which was to read watermarks the way other men read faces.

The ledger itself was not beautiful. It was a shipping docket, bound in oilcloth that had done its work — the pages were damp-stained but legible, the ink browned at the edges but stubborn. Forty-one entries. Dates running from the spring of the flood year through the following winter. Each entry carried the Authority's cipher stamp, and beneath each stamp, in a clerk's small patient hand, the routing marks: a series of letters and numerals that meant nothing to a man who read them as freight.

But Ilan Reyes was not a man who read them as freight.

He was a cartographer. For nine years he had mapped the Rivergate's changing moods — the sandbars that walked, the channels that died, the oxbows that strangled themselves shut — and in nine years of mapping he had learned one thing above all others: everything that moves leaves a mark, and every mark can be traced backward to its source. Rivers taught you this. A silt plume told you where the bank had failed. A raft of dead reeds told you where the current turned. You worked upstream from the evidence, always, until you found the wound.

The routing marks were a current. He had only to work them upstream.

* * *

The problem, at first, was the cipher. The Authority used a rotating key — he knew this from his brother's letters, because his brother Tomás had been a junior clerk in the Authority's survey office before the flood, before Widow's Teeth, before the water came down the gorge like a falling mountain and took eleven hundred people and one nineteen-year-old boy who had gone out on the levee that morning to measure the gauge because it was his job and he was faithful to his job.

Ilan had spent years hating the Authority in the abstract, the way you hate weather. Now, with the ledger under his hands, the hate had become specific, and specificity was worse. Specificity had names in it.

The key rotated quarterly. He knew the format — Tomás had once shown him, laughing, how the clerks memorized it with a nonsense rhyme about herons. *Heron, eel, drake, crow* — four birds, four quarters, each bird standing for an offset applied to the letter-marks. It had been a joke between brothers, the kind of joke that seems trivial until the day it is the only thing you have left of a person.

Ilan had remembered the rhyme for nine years without knowing why. Now he knew.

The flood year's third quarter would be *drake*. He applied the offset to the first entry's routing mark and the letters resolved into plain words, and his hands began to shake so badly he had to press them flat against the table and breathe, the way Mirabel breathed when she talked about the wreck, slow and deliberate, a woman holding herself together with arithmetic.

The first entry read:

**COPY SET A — HON. E. VASQUEZ, MAGISTRATE, RIVERGATE CIRCUIT — BY COURIER, SEALED.**

Copy Set B went to the Authority's own archive. Copy Set C —

He checked it three times. Then a fourth, applying the *crow* offset instead, in case he'd misjudged the quarter. It held.

**COPY SET C — MRS. ADELAIDE QUILL, 14 CHANDLERY ROW, RIVERGATE CITY — UNCLAIMED, HOLD FOR COLLECTION.**

Unclaimed. Hold for collection. Nine years of sitting in some records room or lawyer's vault, a set of documents addressed to a widow no one had ever come to collect them for, because the man who would have collected them — her husband, presumably, some clerk in the Authority who had copied the ledger before it was buried — was dead or fled or bought.

Ilan sat back. The lamp guttered. Below him the river said what it always said, patiently, endlessly, the way it had said it over his brother for nine years.

A magistrate paid to bury the truth. That was the story everyone at Trail's Landing already believed; it required no decoding, only confirmation. But a widow with an unclaimed copy — that was something else. That was a door standing unlocked in a wall everyone thought was solid. If Adelaide Quill still lived at 14 Chandlery Row, if she still held whatever her husband had sent her, then the truth was not buried. It was merely waiting, patient as sediment, for someone to come dig it out.

And Cassian Roe's lawyers were already moving downstream. Brack had passed the word upriver before they took the ledger off him; Roe's launch had turned downstream two days ahead of the flood crest, carrying a man who now knew exactly what had been raised from the coffin, and men like Roe did not waste time. They would move on Vasquez first — Vasquez was theirs, bought and paid, easy to scrub. But a widow was loose thread. Men like Roe pulled loose threads.

Which meant the race was not metaphorical. It was a race between a ferry and a law firm, between a flood crest and a courier's saddlebags, between nine years of patience and however many days remained before monsoon closed the river or money closed the case.

Ilan blew out the lamp, because his eyes were burning, and sat in the dark and let himself feel it — not hope exactly, hope was too soft a word — but *trajectory*. For nine years his purpose had been a map with no destination: trace the records, find the names, learn the shape of the crime. Shapes change nothing. But a living witness holding physical documents, reachable before the enemy reached her — that was not a shape. That was a road.

He had spent nine years working upstream from his brother's death. Tomorrow, God willing and the river permitting, he would start working downstream toward something else.

* * *

He found Mirabel on the deck of the beached Sparrowhawk at first light, though found was perhaps too generous a word — he climbed the sandbar's spine in the gray drizzle and there she was, sitting on the rail of her dying ship with her boots hooked in the shrouds, watching the river rise over the ground that had killed her boat.

She didn't look at him. "You've got the face," she said. "The one you get when the ledger talks."

"The ledger talks." He climbed onto the deck, careful of the list, and stood beside her because sitting next to her felt like presumption. "Three copy sets. One to Magistrate Vasquez in Rivergate Circuit — that's the one Roe will go bury, if he hasn't already. One to the Authority's own archive, which is ash or lies by now. And one—"

He made her wait for it. Not from cruelty. From the old cartographer's habit of making people see the whole channel before pointing at the deep water.

"—one addressed to a Mrs. Adelaide Quill, fourteen Chandlery Row, Rivergate City. Marked unclaimed. Hold for collection."

Mirabel turned her head slowly. Rain beaded on her eyebrows. "Whose collection?"

"A clerk's wife, most likely. Someone inside the Authority copied the ledger before it went into the ground and mailed it to his own wife for safekeeping. Then either he died or he lost his nerve, and nobody ever came for it." Ilan heard his own voice go quiet. "It's been sitting there nine years, Mirabel. In a city full of lawyers, one street away from the courthouse, there has been a complete copy of the proof this entire time."

Mirabel looked back at the river. The flood had come up another hand's width overnight; the sandbar that had saved the Sparrowhawk was shrinking into a gray tongue, and the water moved past them heavy and brown and certain, carrying branches, carrying a drowned fence post, carrying everything downstream whether it consented or not.

"Roe knows we have the ledger," she said. "Brack told him what's in the box even if he never touched the book itself. He'll assume copies exist. Men like Roe always assume copies exist — it's why they're still rich." She was thinking aloud, and Ilan recognized the mode; he'd seen her navigate this way, reading the water aloud before committing the helm. "Vasquez first. He's the magistrate who buried the original docket — I'd bet my remaining rigging on it. Roe buys him quietly, gets the record sealed properly this time, and then—" she shrugged, a small brutal motion, "—then a widow in Chandlery Row is just a woman with old papers. Easy to frighten. Easier to buy. Easiest of all to burgle."

"So we reach her first."

"With what?" She finally looked at him fully, and he saw the exhaustion in her, the rawness of a woman who had wept two nights ago over a boat and hadn't yet forgiven herself for it. "Maren's ferry is a mudflat with opinions. The crest is coming down behind us. Roe's launch is ahead of us, faster than anything we can borrow, and his lawyers travel light while we travel with half a salvage crew and a cartographer who gets seasick in chop."

"You forgot one thing we travel with."

"What?"

"Me." He said it without pride, which was the only honest way to say it. "I've spent nine years learning every channel, cutoff, and chute between here and Rivergate City. I mapped the flood crests of 'sixteen and 'nineteen. I know what this water does when it hits the lower bends, and I know the routes a steam launch won't take because they're not on any chart worth paying for." He paused. "They're on mine."

Mirabel studied him for a long moment. Rain ticked on the deck around them. Somewhere below, the river gurgled in the flooded hold of the Sparrowhawk, filling the space where cargo used to ride, where Mirabel's whole life used to ride.

"You want to run ahead of a monsoon crest," she said, "in a borrowed ferry, through water that isn't charted, to beat a steam launch and a phalanx of barristers to a widow's door."

"Yes."

"And if we lose?"

Ilan looked downstream, where the river bent away toward everything: the Landing's stilt houses, the drowned fields, the long brown miles to the city where his brother's killers drank wine and signed papers. He thought about how revenge had kept him walking for nine years and how it had never once pointed anywhere. This pointed somewhere. A door. A name. A set of papers with wax seals and a clerk's careful hand.

"If we lose," he said, "then Trail's Landing waits another nine years, and I go back to drawing maps of a river that doesn't care. But we haven't lost yet. The crest hasn't passed us. The launch hasn't reached her. And Mrs. Adelaide Quill has held those papers alone for nine years without knowing what she's holding." He met Mirabel's eyes. "That's not a woman we can leave holding them alone a tenth year."

Mirabel exhaled through her nose — the sound she made, he'd learned, when a decision had already been made and the breath was only ceremony.

"Maren's ferry it is," she said. "Go tell Tamsin to strip her to the bones. Everything that isn't crew, charts, or courage stays on the bar."

* * *

By noon the transformation was done, and it hurt to watch, which Ilan suspected was part of why Mirabel had done it herself, plank by plank, rather than order it done. The little ferry — Maren called her *Patience*, in the flat tone of a woman naming something that had outlasted her patience — was stripped to bare utility: the salvage winch gone, the spare cable gone, two of the four cargo cradles gone, even the cabin's stove dismounted and left dripping on the sandbar. What remained was a shallow-drafted, flat-bottomed, absurdly tough barge-boat with a patched awning, a borrowed compass, and room for five people and a locked oilskin packet that weighed less than a cat and outweighed everything else aboard combined.

Tamsin came down the ladder from Maren's house with the packet slung across her chest like a bandolier and an expression like a drawn bow. "Maren's coming," she announced.

Mirabel looked up from lashing the last crate. "This isn't her fight."

"It became her fight nine years ago, same as ours." Tamsin handed over the packet. "She says the Landing's survivors have waited long enough to watch other people carry their truth around like contraband. She says—" Tamsin's voice caught slightly, and she covered it with briskness, "—she says if a Vane is going to deliver justice to Rivergate City, she wants to stand on the dock and see it with her own eyes."

Mirabel closed her hands around the packet and held it a moment too long. Ilan watched her jaw work. Whatever she said, she said it to the crate, quietly: "Then we'd better not sink."

* * *

They cast off at the turn of the afternoon tide, four of them — Mirabel at the sweep, Tamsin amidships, Odji forward with the sounding pole he'd taken to like a birthright, and Ilan aft with his charts weighted under stones against the wind. Maren sat near the bow with her arms folded, wearing a man's oilskin two sizes too big, looking like a figurehead carved by someone with grievances.

The river received them the way it received everything: without comment.

For the first hour they ran the main channel, and it was almost easy — the crest was still hours behind them, the water high but behaving, and the Patience slid down the current with a steadiness that felt, after the Sparrowhawk's dying list, like mercy. Ilan spread his charts and worked. This was the part he loved, the part that had survived everything else: the folding-out of the river onto paper, the tracing of their position against the accumulated knowledge of nine years, the small private satisfaction of knowing where they were in a world that mostly didn't.

"We cut off at the Sedge bend," he said, marking it. "Main channel swings east around the point — six miles of open water, and open water is where a launch makes up time on us. But there's a cutoff through the old Sedge plantation channel that saves three miles and hides us from the bend. It shoaled to eighteen inches in the dry season. With this water—" he tapped the chart, "—it'll carry us fine, and Roe's pilot won't take it, because it's not on the Authority charts. They stopped surveying the Sedge after the flood. Too much changed."

"Too much drowned," Maren said from the bow, without turning around.

Nobody answered that. There was nothing to answer it with.

They took the cutoff at the second hour, and it was like passing through a curtain. One moment the river was wide and gray and public; the next they were threading a drowned avenue, cypress trunks rising on both sides with the water halfway up their flanks, Spanish moss hanging motionless in the dead air, the surface ahead streaked with rain-holes. Odji poled them through a gap in a fallen fence line — the top rail of it just breaking the surface, a fence built for cattle grazing on land that was now nine feet under the river — and Ilan saw Mirabel look at that fence rail for a long time.

"This is what the ledger is about," she said quietly, to no one, to him, maybe to the fence. "Not paper. This. All of this, that somebody decided was cheaper underwater."

Ilan marked their position and said nothing, because some confirmations are better given by geography than by cartographers.

* * *

Dusk brought the crest within earshot.

You heard it before you saw it — a change in the river's voice, a deeper note underneath the surface noise, like a drum rolled somewhere far upstream and coming closer. Odji heard it first, head cocked, and said, "Listen," and they all listened, and the hair rose on Ilan's arms.

"How long?" Mirabel asked him.

He did the arithmetic against his charts, against the rainfall reports he'd gathered at the trading posts, against the gauge heights Maren had recited from memory the night before. "Four hours to the Sedge confluence. Maybe five to the Narrows. After the Narrows the valley opens and it spreads out — loses teeth, gains width." He looked up. "We want to be through the Narrows before it arrives. In the Narrows, a crest like this comes through as a standing wave and a half. Nothing flat-bottomed survives the standing wave."

"Then we don't stop," Mirabel said, "and we don't miss the entrance to the Narrows channel, and we don't hit the Widow's Teeth shoal in the dark. Simple."

It was not simple, and everyone aboard knew it wasn't simple, and that shared knowledge had a strange effect Ilan had noticed before on boats in hard water: it made the crew quiet, and the quiet made them fast. They ran the last miles of daylight with the awning struck and the rain coming sideways, Mirabel reading the water ahead by the way the current wrinkled it, calling depths to Odji, who repeated them back like a litany. Ilan navigated by memory and lightning — the flashes came more often now, stuttering white over the drowned country, and each flash showed him a landmark he'd drawn from a survivor's description years ago and never expected to see: the church spire of a town that wasn't there anymore, standing alone out of the flood like a grave marker for everything around it.

At full dark they entered the Narrows.

The cliffs came close enough on either side that the Patience's wake slapped back at them, and the river between them ran black and fast and narrow as a throat. Ilan held the lantern low over his charts and called the channel by heart — "starboard of the mid rock, ten yards, then hard to port where the eddy line shows" — and Mirabel made the boat obey, and the boat obeyed, and for twenty minutes there was nothing in the world but black water, white foam, shouted soundings, and the drumroll of the crest growing louder behind them like a pursuer.

They shot out of the Narrows' lower mouth into the open valley with the crest a mile behind and closing, a visible thing now even in the dark — a broad pale line of broken water stretching bank to bank, carrying trees, carrying roofs, carrying the river's whole temper downstream at a gallop.

"Beach us," Ilan said. "High ground, now. Nobody outruns it on the water — you let it pass and then you ride its shoulder."

Mirabel put the Patience into a willow-choked inlet on the left bank and they tied off to trunks four feet above the present waterline, and then they stood in the rain and watched the flood come, because there was nothing else to do and because some things demand witnesses. The crest arrived like a slow explosion, the river rising a foot, two feet, three, in as many minutes, the willows around them bending and thrashing, the Patience surging at her lines while Tamsin and Odji paid them out and hauled and paid again. Maren stood hip-deep in the new water without appearing to notice it, watching the debris field pass — and once, sharply, she reached out and caught something as it went by: a child's wooden horse, chipped and waterlogged, which she held in both hands for a moment before letting the river take it back.

Ilan understood then that he had been wrong about the shape of the race. He had thought they were racing Roe's launch to a widow's door. They were racing something larger — time itself, the flood, the nine years, the willingness of the world to keep this buried one more season, one more year, forever. The crest would pass. Roe's lawyers would not, unless they arrived to find the door already opened and the papers already claimed.

An hour later, the crest behind them, they pushed off into the swollen dark and ran with the flood's shoulder, faster now than the Patience had ever moved in her life, the drowned valley sliding past, and forty miles ahead — two days, if the river allowed; one day, if Mirabel Vane had anything to say about it — a lamp was probably burning in a window on Chandlery Row, behind which a woman named Adelaide Quill sat on papers she didn't know she owned, nine years into a silence she'd been told was safety.

"Hold on," Mirabel called into the rain, and put the bow downstream.
