## 18. Downstream, Running Water

The ferry was called the *Mudhen*, and she was ugly as sin and twice as honest. Flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted, built for hauling livestock and lumber across a half-mile of slack water at the Landing — not for running forty miles of flooded gorge at monsoon crest. Her engine was a converted threshing mill that sounded like a man being beaten with his own boots.

Mirabel loved her instantly.

"She'll do it," Maren said, standing on the dock in the rain, one hand on the ferry's rail as if she were letting go of something more than a boat. "But you bring her back. You hear me? She's the only thing between this town and starvation if the bridge goes."

"I'll bring her back." Mirabel stepped aboard, and the deck shifted under her feet — wrong, all wrong, too wide, too slow to answer, nothing like the *Sparrowhawk*'s quick nervous hull. Nine years of muscle memory argued against her. She let it argue. The *Sparrowhawk* lay beached on a sandbar upstream, drowned to her gunwales, and grief could wait until there was time for it.

Tamsin came down the dock at a run, hauling a coil of line and a canvas bag of charts Ilan had marked. "Ledger's copies are downstream," she said, breathing hard. "Widow's name is Corvane. Rivergate City, Lantern Row. Ilan says Roe's lawyers will need two days to get a writ through the flood courts — we have two days."

"Then we have two days." Mirabel looked upriver once. The gorge had swallowed its own rain; the cliffs were running like wet slate, and somewhere behind that gray curtain Cassian Roe's steam launch was fighting downstream with hired men and a stolen ledger and a captain who had never read this river in flood. "Get aboard. We're leaving before I change my mind about the first rapid."

"There's a first rapid?"

"There's always a first rapid, Tamsin. That's how rivers introduce themselves."

* * *

The Mudhen went into the current like a leaf going over a weir — no dignity, no argument, just surrender with an engine attached. Mirabel stood at the wheel with her feet wide and her weight forward, reading the water the way other people read faces.

Flood changed everything. A channel she had known dry-shod nine years ago was now a brown torrent shouldering through drowned willows; a sandbar that had been a navigation hazard was now six feet under and invisible, waiting to peel a flat-bottomed hull open like fruit. The old marks were gone. The trees were gone. Half the landmarks of her childhood on this stretch were underwater or in motion.

But the river itself hadn't changed. Rivers never did. They only got louder about what they'd always wanted.

She read the boils — those telltale upwellings where deep water climbed over buried bars — and steered around them without thinking. She read the V's of debris riding the surface, each one pointing downstream like a compass needle toward the fastest thread. She read the color: green-brown meant depth, clay-yellow meant shallows dragging, and where the water went white it wasn't showing off, it was warning.

"You're smiling," Tamsin said from beside her, holding the rail with both hands.

"I'm not."

"You are. First time since the sandbar."

Mirabel realized it was true and didn't know what to do about it. "It's the river," she said finally. "It's angry and enormous and it doesn't care who signed what ledger. It's the most honest thing left on this water."

Ahead, the gorge opened into the lower reach, and the first real rapid came into view — Widow's Teeth, which they'd fought upstream two days ago and which was now a different animal entirely. The rocks that had shown their heads above low water were submerged, but not by enough. The flood poured across them in a broken, boiling sheet, and somewhere under that white chaos were the Teeth themselves, patient as ever.

"Ilan's chart says go left," Tamsin said.

"Ilan's chart was drawn in June."

"And your chart?"

"My chart says the left channel runs over a gravel shelf that floods shallow and mean, and the right channel looks like death but has a scour hole under the drop that'll float us through if we hit it square." She turned the wheel. "My chart also says I've run this drop eleven times, and I'm still here."

"That's not a chart, that's a biography."

"It's the same thing, out here."

She took them right. The Mudhen's bow lifted, hung — that terrible floating moment when the boat stops belonging to the water and starts belonging to gravity — and then dropped into the scour hole with a slam that threw Tamsin off her feet. Brown water broke over the foredeck, waist-high, cold as a confession. The engine screamed. And then the hull found the downstream current, and the flood took them, and they were through.

Tamsin picked herself up, dripping, and stared back at the boiling white. "Square," she said. "You said hit it square."

"We did."

"The *bow* hit it square. The rest of us hit it in order of seniority."

Mirabel laughed — actually laughed, short and startled, a sound she barely recognized as hers. Then she saw the smoke.

* * *

Roe's launch showed up an hour later, and Mirabel spotted it before anyone else because she'd been watching the water, and the water had been watching the launch.

Steam launches draw attention even in flood — the beat of their paddles carries, the coal smoke hangs in wet air. But what told Mirabel first wasn't sound or sight. It was the wake. Where the launch ran, the current's smooth brown skin broke into a different rhythm, a churned confusion that no log or drifting tree made.

"They're taking the inside channel below Harker's Bend," she said. "That's a mistake."

"Why?"

"Because Harker's Bend floods crooked. The inside looks calm and it isn't — there's a counter-current along the bank that sets you sideways into the cutbank, and the cutbank's collapsing right now with all this water. They'll lose their steering." She thought about it for three seconds, which was two seconds longer than the decision needed. "Or they'll lose more than that."

Tamsin understood at the same moment she did. "Do we warn them?"

"He stole the ledger."

"He did. And he's got six men aboard, and two of them are boys, Mirabel. I saw them loading coal at the Landing. Sixteen, maybe seventeen."

Mirabel looked downstream at the bend, where the launch's smoke was just visible above the drowned cottonwoods, moving fast and confident and wrong. She thought about Cassian Roe's face on the launch deck two days ago, all courtesy, offering her a fortune to forget. She thought about her father's hands on a sluice wheel, and written orders from above, and nine years of men who looked at drowning towns and saw arithmetic.

She thought about what she'd become if she watched the river do her arithmetic for her.

"Come about," she said. "We can't catch them, but we can be there when the river catches them."

* * *

Harker's Bend announced itself as a wall of noise — the whole flood funneling through a narrowing, slamming into the outside bank, and curling back on itself in great slow coils. The inside channel, where the launch had gone, looked deceptively smooth: a long glassy lane hugging the near shore.

And there, exactly where Mirabel knew it would be, the launch was in trouble.

The counter-current had taken her stern. She was swinging broadside, paddlewheel thrashing air and spray, sliding sideways toward the cutbank where the flood had undercut twenty feet of earth and the river was eating the bank alive. Trees went down as they watched — whole cottonwoods, tilting, sighing, vanishing. The launch's bow swung toward the collapse. Men were shouting, small and thin across the water. Someone was throwing lines at a bank that couldn't hold them.

"She's going into the cut," Tamsin said. "Mirabel—"

"I see it." Mirabel brought the Mudhen around in a wide flat turn, using the main current instead of fighting it, letting the flood carry her toward the bend's mouth. "There's an eddy behind the point rock. If they can get into it, they live. If they can't—" She didn't finish. The river finished sentences like that for you.

The launch struck the collapsing bank with a crack they heard over the roar. Not the hull — the wheelhouse, shearing against a half-fallen sycamore. The vessel slewed, shipped water over her rail, and began to swing again, stern-first now, toward the boil below the point.

Men went into the water. Two of them — swept clear of the wreck, arms windmilling, gone under, up, under again. The rest clung to the listing deck.

"Line!" Mirabel shouted. "Tamsin, the big coil — get a throw ready! I'll put us in the eddy!"

"You're going to rescue him?"

"I'm going to rescue whoever the river spits at me. Starting with whoever's closest." Because the nearest figure in the water, gray-haired and grasping at a floating crate, was unmistakably Cassian Roe himself — and the boy behind him, the sixteen-year-old coal-handler, was already going under.

Mirabel put the Mudhen's bow across the current at the exact angle the eddy demanded — too steep and they'd broach, too shallow and they'd overshoot into the boil. It was the finest piece of boat-handling she'd done in nine years, done in a borrowed flat-bottomed pig-boat with a threshing engine, and she felt every year of her life in her wrists as she held the wheel against the flood's shove.

They slid into the eddy's slack water like a knife into a sheath.

"Throw!" she yelled, and Tamsin threw — a beautiful long arc, the coil unrolling, landing two feet from the boy's flailing hand. He caught it. His weight hit the line and nearly pulled Tamsin over the rail, and then Odji — who had appeared from below decks without anyone remembering he'd come aboard — braced her waist and hauled, and the boy came over the gunwale in a rush of river water and sobbing breath.

Roe was harder. He'd lost the crate. He was ten yards downstream of the eddy's lip, and the current was carrying him toward the boil, and he knew it — Mirabel could see the knowledge in his face, the arithmetic arriving at last, the moment a man who had spent his life buying outcomes understood that this one wasn't for sale.

He looked at her. Across thirty feet of brown floodwater, he looked straight at her, and she saw him understand exactly who held the wheel, and what she could simply choose not to do.

She threw him the second line herself.

It took three throws. The first fell short; the second the wind took; the third landed across his chest and he wrapped it around his forearm and Mirabel belayed it around the bitts and let the river pull while the rope paid out, smoking, until his weight settled and she could haul him in inch by inch with Odji and Tamsin both on the line. When he came over the rail he was coughing, gray-faced, sixty years old suddenly, and he grabbed her wrist with a grip that had terror in it and nothing else.

"You—" he rasped. "You could have—"

"Yes," Mirabel said. "I could have."

She left him there, kneeling in the bilgewater, and went back to the wheel, because the launch was breaking up and four more men were still on her deck, and the day's work wasn't done.

* * *

By dark they had all seven survivors aboard the Mudhen, packed among the cargo netting, wrapped in sailcloth, shivering. The launch was a scatter of debris below the bend. One of Roe's hired men had a broken arm; the boy wouldn't stop shaking and wouldn't let go of the line he'd been pulled in with, so they let him keep it.

Roe sat apart, dried off, wrapped in someone's spare coat, watching Mirabel at the wheel with an expression she couldn't fully read. When she finally sent Tamsin to take the wheel and came aft to check the survivors, he spoke.

"You saved my life."

"Don't make it strange."

"You had every reason." His voice was hoarse from river water. "Every reason in the world, Vane. I'd have taken the ledger and burned it and you'd have spent the rest of your life running. That was the plan. You knew that."

"I suspected."

"And you threw the line anyway." He shook his head slowly. "I don't understand you."

"No," Mirabel said. "You don't. That's been the trouble between us from the start." She crouched so they were eye to eye, and kept her voice level, because the crew was listening and so was the boy. "Here's what I understand, Cassian. The river nearly killed you today, and it wasn't personal. What you did to Trail's Landing *was*. Nine years ago, men like you looked at a town full of people and saw a number worth more than the town. My father signed his name because men like you told him it was engineering. So don't sit there wrapped in our coat talking about reasons. You're alive because I decided the river doesn't get to settle my accounts. I do. In daylight. In court."

Roe held her gaze for a long moment. Rain drummed on the cargo netting. Somewhere in the dark, the flood went on roaring past, indifferent, honest, enormous.

"You'll never prove it," he said quietly. "The magistrate's bought. The lawyers—"

"Are two days behind us," Mirabel said, "and getting further behind by the hour. Because you gave your launch to the river, Cassian, and I've still got a boat." She stood. "There's blankets in the locker. Try to stay warm. You're a witness now, whether you like it or not — everything you say from here on gets remembered."

She walked forward to take the wheel back from Tamsin, and behind her she heard the boy ask Roe, small-voiced, "Sir — is what she said true? About the town?"

And Roe said nothing at all.

* * *

They ran through the night watch by watch, Mirabel and Tamsin trading the wheel while Odji fed the insatiable engine and the flood carried them downstream faster than any boat had a right to move. Toward dawn the rain thinned. The river widened, slowed, spread out into its lower valley like a man unclenching his fist, and ahead of them, gray-gold in the first light, the towers and levees of Rivergate City rose out of the mist.

Tamsin came to stand beside her, following her gaze. "Lantern Row's on the far side of the customs basin. We can be at the widow's door within the hour."

"Then the race is ours," Mirabel said.

But even as she said it, she was looking at the levee — and at the crowd already gathering along its top despite the early hour, drawn by the flood, watching the water climb the stone. And at the far end of the levee, where the customs basin opened its mouth, a government cutter was tying up, and men in good coats were coming down its gangway with document cases in their hands, moving fast, moving with purpose, moving toward the city's law courts before the city was properly awake.

Roe's backers hadn't waited for a writ. They'd come ahead of it.

Mirabel rang the engine bell and put the Mudhen's blunt bow straight at the heart of the city.
