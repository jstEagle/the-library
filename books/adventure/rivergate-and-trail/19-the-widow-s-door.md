## 19. The Widow's Door

The flood had followed them all the way to Rivergate City.

Maren Oduya had not seen the capital in nine years, and it had grown fat on the river's misfortune — new warehouses stacked along the levee like coins in a miser's fist, cranes swinging over the docks, steam launches shouldering through the brown water as if the monsoon were a festival rather than a funeral. The city had never looked at Trail's Landing. Now Maren understood why. You did not look at what you intended to bury.

She stood at the rail of Maren's borrowed ferry — her own ferry, though she could hardly think of it that way yet — and watched Mirabel Vane work the tiller through the crowded channel with the same terrible competence she'd shown on every mile of water since they'd left the gorge. The woman did not miss a current. She did not miss a buoy. She did not once look at the levee steps where, three days ago, Ilan Reyes had read the Authority's ledger aloud to a crowd while the river climbed toward their knees.

The widow lived on Cordwainer Lane, in the tangle of streets behind the customs house where the floodwater ran ankle-deep and smelled of drains and drowned cellars. Ilan had the address from his brother's letters — Aldous Wren, junior clerk, Rivergate Authority, correspondence division. Dead now, Ilan said. Fever, two winters back. His wife still kept the house.

"Her name is Sera," Ilan told them, huddled under the ferry's canvas awning while rain needled the roof. "I wrote to her twice last year. Both letters came back unopened." He turned the returned envelope over in his fingers, the wax seal unbroken, the paper gone soft with handling. "She knows what her husband left her. She's been sitting on it for nine years because she's afraid."

"Afraid of what?" Tamsin asked.

"Of exactly what happened to everyone else who held a piece of this." Ilan folded the letter away. "The magistrate who buried the docket. The witnesses who recanted. My brother, who drowned in a river he knew how to swim." He said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Mrs. Wren isn't a coward. She's a survivor. There's a difference, and Roe's money has spent nine years making sure no one confuses them."

Maren said nothing. She was thinking about fear. She was an expert on fear; she had carried it out of Trail's Landing in her chest like ballast, and it had weighed the same every day since.

* * *

Cordwainer Lane was a gutter of standing water and sagging shutters. A cat watched them from a windowsill with the flat judgment of all cats. At the far end, one house stood apart from its neighbors — not grander, but *kept*. The doorframe freshly painted. The step swept despite the rain. Shutters closed against the weather but clean behind their bars.

Someone in that house had decided, every single morning for nine years, to go on living properly. Maren recognized the discipline of it. It was the discipline of people who had lost something too large to mourn in public.

Mirabel knocked. Of course she did — Maren would have stood in the lane until dark, working up to it, but Mirabel Vane had spent nine years running from doors and had apparently decided to stop, all at once, everywhere.

No answer. Rain drummed on the awning Ilan held.

Then, from inside: "I know why you've come."

The voice was a woman's, tired and level. "There are men who came in a fine carriage two months ago. Lawyers. They sat in my parlor and offered me a pension and a house above the flood line if I burned certain papers I don't have." A pause. "And there was a man before that, nine years ago, who didn't offer anything. He just asked my husband whether his daughter liked the river."

Maren felt Mirabel go still beside her. Utterly still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"My husband said yes," the voice continued through the door. "He said our girl loved the river. And the man said, then you'll want her to grow up somewhere safe, won't you. And Aldous understood that it wasn't a kindness. It was arithmetic." The bolt slid. The door opened six inches, on a chain. One eye, gray and unblinking. "You're the ones from the levee. I heard the reading. Everyone heard the reading. The whole city heard it, and then the magistrate resigned, and the papers went quiet again, and I thought — there it is. Money wins. It always wins. So why have you come to my door?"

"Because it hasn't won yet," Maren said.

The eye moved to her. Took her in — the stilt-house hands, the river scar along her jaw, the face of a town that had drowned.

"You're from the Landing," the woman said.

"I'm from the Landing."

The chain rattled. The door opened.

* * *

Sera Wren was smaller than her voice, a thin woman in a gray dress with ink stains on her fingers that no amount of washing had ever fully lifted — a clerk's wife down to the bone. Her parlor was immaculate and almost empty, as if furniture had been sold off year by year to keep the rest. On the mantel stood a photograph of a girl of perhaps ten, gap-toothed, holding up a fish nearly as long as her arm.

"She's married now," Sera said, following their eyes. "In the hill country. She thinks her father died of fever and her mother is comfortably dull." She sat, folding her hands. "That was the price of keeping us safe. I made myself boring. I made myself nothing. Nine years of being nothing, Mr. Reyes, so that no one would ever look twice at my door."

"And your husband's copy?" Ilan asked gently. "Did you burn it?"

Sera Wren smiled, and it was the saddest thing Maren had seen since the Landing went under.

"Do you know what a correspondence clerk does? He copies. Everything that crosses the Authority's desk passes through his pen twice — once to send, once to file. When the sluice orders came through, Aldous copied them the way he copied everything, because that was his hand and his habit and he didn't even know he was doing it until afterward, when the flood came and the men in fine coats started visiting every clerk in the building." She unfolded her hands. "He hid his copy inside something no lawyer would ever search. And when he was dying, he told me where, and he told me: *not while the magistrate lives, not while Roe has friends, not while our daughter needs me alive.*"

"And now?" Mirabel asked. Her voice was rough. Maren had learned, these past weeks, to hear what lived under Mirabel Vane's roughness — the freight of it. "What's changed?"

Sera looked at her for a long moment.

"You have his hands," she said. "Edmund Vane's hands. He used to sign in front of my husband's desk sometimes, big flourishes, impatient man, kind underneath it. I filed his correspondence for six years." She rose, went to the cold hearth, and knelt. "You want to know what's changed? What's changed is that a woman from the drowned town walked into my parlor and I saw her face, and I remembered that being afraid for nine years was not the same as being loyal to the dead. Aldous wanted this found. He just couldn't survive finding it. But I'm still here."

She pressed a loose brick in the hearth's side wall. It swung out on a hidden hinge, and from the cavity she drew a tin — a tea tin, rusted at the corners, ordinary as bread. She set it on the table between them like a heart on a scale.

"I opened it once," she said. "Nine years ago, the night he died. Then I sewed it shut and I never touched it again, because touching it felt like holding a lit lamp in a powder store." She pushed it toward Maren. "You open it. Not the clerk's wife. Not the cartographer. Not the engineer's daughter. Someone from the Landing. It was taken from your town. It should be given back by your hands."

Maren's own hands were shaking. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain.

Inside the tin, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age: a ledger. Slim, cheap-bound, filled edge to edge with a neat clerk's hand — shipping marks, docket numbers, dates. And clipped inside the cover, a single sheet in a different hand, official and cramped: the sluice order, dated nine years and two days before the monsoon that drowned Trail's Landing. Signed. Countersigned. Below the signatures, in Aldous Wren's careful copyist script, a note:

*Original filed and withdrawn same day by direction of R. Cassian Roe, Commissioner. This copy retained per standard duplication practice. God forgive us all.*

Nobody spoke. The rain filled the silence, and beneath the rain, faintly, the river — high and hungry against the levee, three streets away.

"He tried to close them," Mirabel said at last, barely audible. "My father. He went up to the gates himself and they overruled him. That order—" her finger hovered over the signatures without touching— "this is why they couldn't let him live quietly afterward. This is why he drank himself into the current."

"I know what it is," Sera said softly. "I've known for nine years. Knowing and saying are different animals, Mrs.—"

"Vane."

"Yes." Something moved in the gray eyes — not quite warmth, not quite pity. "Vane. Well. The whole city will know that name soon enough, one way or the other. Are you ready for that?"

"No," Mirabel said. "But I'm done letting that decide things."

* * *

They were still at the table — Ilan copying pages by lamplight, Tamsin at the window watching the street, Maren simply sitting with the tin in her lap like something warm she refused to put down — when Tamsin said, quietly, "Lanterns."

Two of them, at the mouth of the lane. Men standing in the rain without hurrying, the way men stand when they are paid by the hour and the hour is theirs.

"They've been there since before we knocked," Sera said calmly, as if reporting the weather. "They rotate at midnight. I stopped pretending I hadn't noticed years ago. It was almost company."

"The lawyers' men?" Tamsin asked.

"Or Roe's. Or the Authority's receivers'. They all wear the same coats eventually." Sera began gathering teacups with steady hands. "Here is what matters, and listen carefully, because I am only brave once per decade and I've spent today's ration. That ledger is a copy of a copy. Roe's lawyers will say it's forged — they've already begun saying it about the levee reading. What makes it true isn't the paper. It's the testimony attached to it. Mine. Yours." She nodded at Maren. "The drowned town's faces. Paper can be bought. We cannot all be bought, and they know it, which is why they watch my door instead of burning my house."

"So we move tonight," Ilan said. "Before midnight rotation. Before their masters hear the widow's door finally opened."

"Move it where?" Tamsin asked.

Maren looked down at the ledger in her lap, at the dead clerk's careful hand, at the signature that had signed away four hundred lives for insurance money and thought itself arithmetic.

"To the courthouse," she said. "Before the flood peaks. Before the courts open tomorrow and Roe's people get there first. Tonight, in front of witnesses, with the whole city watching the water rise — we walk this through the front doors and we make them take it."

Outside, the lanterns at the mouth of the lane began, slowly, to move closer.
