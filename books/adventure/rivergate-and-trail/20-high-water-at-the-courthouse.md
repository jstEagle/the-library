## 20. High Water at the Courthouse

Ilan had spent nine years learning to read paper the way other men read water — for what moved beneath it. So when he climbed the courthouse steps that morning and saw the docket clerk's window shuttered, and a boy in a fine gray coat waiting at the bottom of the stairs with a sealed envelope in his hand, he knew the shape of the day before it began.

"They've filed an injunction," Tamsin said beside him, her voice flat as slate. She'd come down from the Landing with Maren and two of the survivors to give testimony, and she stood now with her arms crossed against the drizzle, watching the gray coat like it might draw a knife.

"Of course they have." Ilan shifted the oilcloth bundle under his coat. It held everything: the ledger from the coffin, the widow Aldessa Cray's copy of the Authority's shipping marks, her husband's marginalia in a hand so small it looked like stitching. Nine years of paper. It weighed less than his brother's boots had, and it was heavier than the whole drowned town.

The injunction named them all — Vane, Reyes, Oduya, "and persons unknown" — and demanded the immediate surrender of "fraudulently obtained documents of the Rivergate Navigation Authority" pending review by the magistrate. The same magistrate. Corvel Hask. The man whose signature sat on the original docket from nine years ago, the one that had been paid into silence while Trail's Landing went under the water with forty-one souls aboard the low quarter.

"He can't hear this case," Maren said. She had dressed for the city in a coat too good for her, borrowed from a cousin, and she wore it like armor she didn't trust. "He buried it once."

"He'll recuse himself," Ilan said. "That's the play. He steps aside, looks clean, and the case passes to whoever they've already bought. A week's delay. Maybe two. By then Roe's lawyers will have the ledger declared stolen property, impounded, sealed." He looked out past the courthouse colonnade to where the river showed between buildings, brown and swollen, climbing the levee in slow surges. "They don't need to win. They only need to hold it underwater long enough."

"Like everything else," Maren said.

* * *

Inside, the courtroom was cold and smelled of lamp oil and wet wool. Half the benches were filled with clerks and one or two of Roe's backers — Ilan recognized a silk-vested man from the Authority's old board photographs, a man named Quillan whose face had aged well on other people's money. Mirabel sat at the front with her hands folded, wearing the only clean shirt she owned, and she did not look at the papers on the bench. She watched the doors.

Magistrate Hask came in at the hour, took his seat, and read the injunction aloud in a voice like dry leaves. Ilan listened for the tell. It came exactly where he expected it — a small hesitation before the words *pending review*, a breath drawn through the nose, the pause of a man reading lines someone else had written for him.

Then Hask set the paper down and removed his spectacles.

"Before argument," he said, "I must address a matter of my own standing." The courtroom stilled. "It has been represented to me — correctly — that I presided over an earlier filing concerning the Rivergate Navigation Authority, some years past. That filing was dismissed on procedural grounds." His eyes went nowhere near the plaintiffs' table. "In light of the present matter, and to avoid any appearance of partiality, I recuse myself. The case will be reassigned."

Maren made a sound low in her throat, half laugh, half wound. Tamsin's hand closed on her arm.

Silk-vested Quillan smiled the way ice smiles.

But Ilan was watching the door, because Mirabel was watching the door, and at that moment it opened and a woman came in soaked to the knees — a printer's apprentice from the *Rivergate Ledger*, the little opposition sheet that ran off three hundred copies a night in a basement on Cordwainer Street. Her name was Sef. Ilan had met her at midnight two days ago, sliding the widow Cray's copy across a type-stained table while rain hammered the alley grates.

She caught his eye and gave him a single nod.

*Set.* It was done. Or it would be, if the hours held.

* * *

The reassignment was announced for the following morning — which meant a night in which anything could be bought, and everything would be. Ilan spent it moving.

First to the widow's house, where Aldessa Cray met him at the door with a traveling bag already packed and her dead husband's copy wrapped in waxed linen against her chest. "They came by at dusk," she said. "Two men, very polite. Offered me a pension. Then they offered me a boat ticket south, one way." Her chin lifted. "I told them I don't travel."

Second to the print shop, where Sef and two ink-blackened men were setting type by lamplight, the formes locked and waiting. They had chosen excerpts — the sluice orders, dated eleven days before the flood; the internal memo marked *insurance posture*; the shipping manifest showing the pay-barge's cargo written off twice, once as loss and once as salvage; and the signatures. Four of them. One belonged to Cassian Roe.

"How many copies?" Ilan asked.

"As many as the press gives us before someone kicks the door in." Sef wiped her hands. "Hundred and fifty, maybe. We're printing the full text besides, for whoever wants to stand and read it."

"That's the part that matters," Ilan said. "Paper can be seized. A hundred voices can't."

Third, last, to the levee, where the river had come up another four feet since noon and the sandbags went down the line like a spine being set. He found Mirabel there, sleeves rolled, passing bags with the dockhands who didn't know her name and wouldn't have cared if they had. The flood had taken her boat ten days ago and she still worked like a woman with a deck under her feet.

"Hask stepped aside," Ilan told her.

"I heard." She didn't stop working. "Your friend Sef?"

"Printing since dark."

Mirabel set down a bag, straightened, and looked at the water. It was the highest he'd ever seen it, a moving country where the channel markers used to be, carrying whole trees and parts of fences and, somewhere far upstream, the topsoil of the gorge. "When I was a girl," she said, "my father told me the river keeps every secret it's given. I thought he meant it kindly." She picked up the next bag. "Tomorrow, then?"

"Tomorrow," Ilan said. "Noon. The steps."

* * *

Noon came gray and dripping, and the flood peaked below the levee like a held breath.

Ilan arrived early and found the steps already filling — not with the crowd he'd hoped for but with something better: dockhands off shift, washerwomen with their arms folded, ferrymen, the widow Cray in her black coat, Maren Oduya standing straight as a piling with six survivors of the Landing ranged behind her, people who had lost houses and husbands and children to a flood the city had spent nine years calling an act of God.

Roe's men were there too. Three lawyers in good boots, a clerk with a writ, and two large quiet men positioned at either end of the steps with the patience of furniture. At the sight of them, Tamsin muttered, "Here we go," and planted herself.

At five minutes past noon, Magistrate Hask himself appeared at the top of the steps.

A murmur went through the crowd. Ilan felt Mirabel go rigid beside him. Hask was supposed to be gone — recused, retired behind his shutters until the money finished its work. Instead the old man descended slowly, holding a document case, and stopped on the third step from the bottom, close enough to the crowd that no writ could be served without everyone seeing it.

"I am not here in any official capacity," Hask said, and his dry-leaf voice carried strangely well over the sound of the river. "I am here as a citizen, to correct a record." He opened the case. "Nine years ago, a docket concerning the Rivergate Navigation Authority came before me. I was persuaded — I allowed myself to be persuaded — that it was without merit. I have since had cause to examine my conscience, and certain documents that reached me late." He drew out a sheaf. "I will read from it now, and let the city judge what merit means."

"You can't—" one of the lawyers started up the steps.

"I am a private man on public stairs," Hask said, "reading from papers in my possession. Arrest me if you find a law against it."

And he began to read.

He read the sluice order first — the date, the gate numbers, the authorization line. The crowd was quiet in the way deep water is quiet. Then the memo on insurance posture, and at the phrase *actuarial advantage of a total-loss event* a woman near the front said, softly, "Oh, God," and covered her mouth. Then the manifest. Then the names.

When Hask read Cassian Roe's signature, the two large quiet men shifted, and the clerk with the writ pushed forward — and that was when Sef's apprentices came around the corner of the customs house with their arms full.

A hundred and fifty broadsheets, still warm from the press, ink smelling of the basement on Cordwainer Street. They went into the crowd like seed into turned earth. Hands took them, passed them back, held them overhead against the drizzle. Within minutes there were more copies than could be seized, more readers than could be silenced, and the text itself began to move outward through the city in a hundred mouths at once — dockhands reading aloud to men who couldn't read, Maren Oduya reading the casualty figures in a clear hard voice while the flood pressed at the levee below as if it had come to hear.

Ilan stood on the edge of it all and thought of his brother, who had gone under at nineteen with the low quarter, who had never been given a name in any record except *deceased, cause: inundation*. The paper in a thousand wet hands was not justice. Justice would take months, trials, years. But it was the thing that comes before justice, the thing without which nothing else is possible: it was the truth, loose, unownable, running downstream faster than any launch Roe could hire.

The lawyers withdrew. There was nothing left to enjoin. You cannot serve a writ on a city.

* * *

Near dusk, with the crowd thinning and the flood holding at its crest a hand's width below the levee lip, Cassian Roe came to the steps.

Ilan saw him before anyone else — the pale suit, the unhurried walk, two hired men trailing at a distance that was meant to look accidental. Roe stopped at the bottom, where the water lapped the lowest step, and looked up at the crowd with an expression of genuine, almost gentle regret.

"A remarkable performance," he said, loud enough to carry. "But papers are not proof of intent. Courts decide intent. And courts," he added, smiling at Hask, at Mirabel, at the widow Cray, "are patient. More patient than rivers, even."

"The river doesn't need to be patient," Maren called down. "It already knows what you did. It's just waiting its turn."

There was laughter in the crowd then, hard and brief, and Roe absorbed it the way a hull absorbs a glancing blow. His gaze found Ilan's and held it — and there was something in it Ilan hadn't seen before, something almost like curiosity.

"Your brother," Roe said quietly, "would have wanted you safe, I think. Not famous."

"My brother," Ilan answered, "wanted the gates closed. Someone signed the order that kept them open. Tomorrow the courts start finding out who."

Roe held his look a moment longer, then inclined his head — courteous to the last, courteous the way a knife is clean — and turned away up the flooded street, his men falling in behind him. Ilan watched him go and felt no triumph at all, only the tightening certainty of a man who has read a chart and seen the shoal ahead: a cornered man with money, a rising river, and one night left before the reassigned magistrate took the bench.

Beside him, Mirabel exhaled slowly.

"He'll run," she said. "Tonight, or tomorrow. Downstream, ahead of the crest, ahead of the warrant."

"Then we'd best hope," Ilan said, "that somebody fast is already on the water."

Mirabel looked out at the brown roaring dark where Maren's ferry lay tied, riding high and light, and Ilan saw the answer arrive in her face before she spoke it.
