## 22. Rivergate and Trail

The new ferry came off the ways at Rivergate City on a morning so clear the river looked like hammered tin. Mirabel stood at the rail of the fitting-out dock with her hands in her coat pockets and watched the yard crew swing the wheelhouse into place, and she thought: *that box of glass and paint is going to be my whole life now.* The thought did not frighten her. That was the strange part. Nine years of running had taught her to feel the river as a series of departures — every mooring a held breath, every dock a place to leave before anyone asked too many questions. Now she was about to sign a contract that bound her to two docks and nothing in between, back and forth, back and forth, until her hands wore grooves into the wheel.

She signed it anyway. She signed it with a pen the harbormaster lent her, because hers had gone down with the Sparrowhawk, along with everything else she'd owned that wouldn't fit in a duffel.

"Vane." The harbormaster — a broad woman named Okonkwo with sawdust in her braids — tapped the paper. "You understand this is a fixed route. Trail's Landing to Rivergate, twice daily, weather permitting. No detours. No private charters. No vanishing up some tributary for three weeks because you heard a rumor of cargo."

"I understand," Mirabel said.

"Because I've heard about you. Half the pilots on this river have stories about Mirabel Vane. The woman who could read a sandbar through fog. The woman who never tied off."

Mirabel looked out at the water. A barge train was coming down from the gorge, riding high and empty, and the lead tug's wake spread long and silver across the tin-bright surface. "The stories are mostly true," she said. "That's why you want me. Nobody else can run this route in flood season without losing her hull."

Okonkwo grinned. "Nobody else would take it. The Landing's a ghost stop. Twelve fares a week if you're lucky."

"It won't be," Mirabel said. "Not after spring."

* * *

They let her name the boat, which surprised her; she'd assumed the Authority's receivers would want something dignified and forgettable, something like *Civic Duty* or *Progress*. But the receivers were desperate for good press, and a pilot with a famous name and a famous wreck was good press, and so in April they painted the letters on the bow while she stood on the dock and watched: **SPARROWHAWK**, in white, six feet tall.

It wasn't the same boat. It wasn't even the same kind of boat — this one was flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted, built for the shoals above the Landing, with a paddlewheel instead of a screw and room for forty passengers under an awning instead of cargo in a hold. When Mirabel first walked her decks she kept expecting the old familiar roll beneath her feet, the deep-water sway, and finding instead this lighter, quicker motion, like walking on a different animal. For the first week she caught herself reaching for handrails that weren't there, calling orders to a crew that didn't exist yet.

But there were things that were the same. The way the wheel answered. The smell of fresh caulk and hot engine oil. And when she took her out alone for the first time, upriver past the city levees into open current, Mirabel stood at the wheel with the wind coming off the water and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched so long she'd stopped noticing it.

Tamsin found her there an hour later, leaning on the rail, watching the banks go by.

"You're smiling," Tamsin said, suspicious. "I want it noted for the record that you're smiling."

"Noted."

"She's not the Sparrowhawk, you know. This one. She's slower. She turns like a barge pig."

"She turns exactly as fast as she needs to," Mirabel said. "And she draws eighteen inches. You could run her across a wet lawn."

"That's what I'm saying. She's a ferry."

"Yes," Mirabel said. "She is."

Tamsin leaned beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of conversation between people who'd shared a sinking. Then: "Odji signed on. Deckhand. He says he wants to see whether you actually know where all those sandbars are or whether you've been lying for nine years."

"He'll find out."

"And Ilan?"

"Gone south," Mirabel said. "Cartography bureau offered him a commission — mapping the whole lower basin, post-flood. He took it. Said somebody has to draw the river as it is now, not as it was." She paused. "He wrote to me. Last page, he said the best maps aren't the ones that show you where things are. They're the ones that show you how to get back."

Tamsin snorted. "That's very him."

"It is very him."

"When does he come back through?"

"Summer, maybe. He wants to see the Landing rebuilt." Mirabel turned the thought over. "We both do."

* * *

The first official run was set for the first of May, and Mirabel spent the last week of April doing something she had never once done in nine years of command: preparing to arrive.

She'd always been good at leaving. Leaving was a craft like any other — you learned the tide windows, the harbor watch rotations, the exact moment a debt collector's patience curdled into action. She could be off a dock in four minutes with lines still warm in her hands. Arriving was different. Arriving meant staying put while people looked at you. Arriving meant being somewhere when evening came, and again the next evening, your face becoming furniture, your name becoming ordinary, spoken without weight by people who expected you tomorrow.

At Trail's Landing, her face was not going to be ordinary. Not for a long time.

Maren Oduya made sure of that — though not in the way Mirabel had feared. In the months since the courthouse, since the ledger had been read aloud on the levee steps while the flood roared brown and furious below, Maren had become something the survivors hadn't had in nine years: a voice with standing. She sat on the reparations board now. She met with the receivers. She walked the mud flats where the old town had drowned and marked, with stakes and red string, where the new pilings would go. The stilt houses were coming down, one by one, replaced by proper foundations on the high ground — but slowly, because the survivors insisted on deciding the layout themselves, street by street, house by house, restoring what the flood had erased according to memory rather than surveyor's convenience.

Mirabel went up two days early to help load the ferry's inaugural stores, and Maren met her at the temporary dock with a clipboard and no greeting at all.

"Pilot Vane."

"Maren."

"The receivers sent the schedule. Two runs daily except Sundays. I told them Sunday service matters more than they think — half the town works the Saturday market in Rivergate and needs to get home." She handed over the clipboard. "Manifest's there. Passenger shelter needs repainting before summer. And the gangway at this dock is a death trap at low water, so we'll need to coordinate on tides."

"All noted."

Maren studied her a moment. The hostility of that first night on the river — the spit, the grief worn like a blade — had burned down to something harder to name. Not warmth. Not forgiveness, either. Something more useful than either: the plain regard of two women who had work to do and had decided, provisionally, to do it in sight of each other.

"My mother used to take this ferry," Maren said abruptly. "The old one. Before. Every Thursday, down to Rivergate for cloth and sugar, home by dark. She'd stand right about where you're standing and complain the whole way about the engine noise." Her jaw worked. "There are maybe sixty people left who remember that ferry. When yours comes in Thursday mornings, some of them are going to cry. I want you to be ready for that."

Mirabel looked down at the deck planks, then up. "I'll be ready."

"And Vane." Maren tucked the clipboard under her arm. "What you did at the courthouse. Saying his name out loud. Your father's. Telling them he tried to close the gates." She stopped, started again. "Half this town spent nine years hating the name Vane. Some of them still do. But the ones who were here that night — the ones who saw the gates fighting the current before they gave — they knew. They always knew someone had tried. Nobody believed us." Her voice roughened. "So. That's all. That's what I came to say."

She turned and walked back up the dock before Mirabel could answer, which was probably the point.

* * *

The first of May dawned gray and soft, with a light rain that the passengers complained about and Mirabel welcomed — rain meant she could read the water honestly, no glittering deceptions, just the plain surface telling her where the current ran. She cast off from Rivergate at seven with eleven passengers, a hold full of lumber and nails and seed potatoes, and Odji at the bow line looking absurdly solemn in a new canvas jacket.

"Course, pilot?" he called, grinning.

"You know the course. It doesn't change. That's the whole point of it."

"Never thought I'd hear you say that with a straight face."

"Log it," Mirabel said, and rang the bell, and the Sparrowhawk — the second Sparrowhawk, the ferry Sparrowhawk — swung her nose upstream and began the run to Trail's Landing.

It took three hours against the spring current, and Mirabel spent every minute of them learning her new boat the way she'd once learned the old one: by feel, by small corrections, by listening to the engine's note change when the paddles bit into slack water versus green. The route was simple — that was the point of it — but simple was not the same as easy, and the stretch below Widow's Teeth still demanded respect even with the flood long fallen. She passed the Teeth at half past eight and did not look away from them this time. The rocks stood black and wet in the rain, and the river broke white around them, and she gave them their berth and moved on.

Near the top of the run, the valley opened, and there it was.

Trail's Landing had been, for nine years, a wound in the landscape — a smear of ruined foundations and dead pilings visible from the water, the place boats passed quickly and didn't name. Now it was becoming a town again. The high ground above the old flood line bristled with new framing, whole streets rising at once, and the stilt houses were mostly gone — dismantled plank by plank, the salvaged wood going into sheds and fences, nothing wasted. Down at the waterline, a proper dock was under construction, but the temporary one stood ready, and along it, in the gray rain, people were waiting.

Not a crowd. Maybe thirty. But thirty was enough.

Mirabel brought her in slow, the way the old ferry must have come in, the way Maren's mother's ferry had come in — paddles backing, wash folded flat, the hull kissing the fenders with barely a sound. Odji made the bow line fast. The gangway went down. And the passengers — the eleven who'd boarded at Rivergate, most of them returning survivors riding their own new route for the first time — went down it into the arms and voices of the people on the dock, and the sound that rose off the water was not cheering, exactly. It was something quieter and heavier. Greetings. Names said aloud. A woman holding a man's face in both hands like she needed to confirm it was really him.

Maren stood at the head of the dock. When Mirabel came down the gangway, last off her own boat, Maren didn't offer a hand, but she stepped aside to make room, which in the grammar of the two of them amounted to the same thing.

"Eleven minutes late," Maren said.

"Current's stronger than the survey says. I'll adjust."

"Do that." A pause. Rain dripped off the awning between them. "My mother's sister came down on this run. First time she's been back since the water. She stood at the rail the whole way up the valley." Maren looked out at the boat, at the white letters on the bow, rain-beaded. "Sparrowhawk. Hm."

"It seemed right."

"It's a scavenger bird," Maren said. "Eats what the flood leaves."

"So do we all," Mirabel said. "The difference is what we build with it."

Maren almost smiled. It was a narrow thing, quickly gone, but Mirabel had been reading water for twenty years and she knew a channel opening when she saw one. "Thursday run leaves Rivergate at seven?" Maren said.

"Seven sharp."

"There'll be more passengers Thursday. Word travels." She turned to go, then stopped. "Vane. Where are you sleeping tonight?"

Mirabel blinked. "Aboard. There's a bunk in the—"

"No. The Harrow house has rooms. Rebuilt in March. You'll take one." She said it the way she said everything, as logistics, as manifest, as fact. "Pilots who sleep on their boats burn out by August, and I am not training your replacement."

For a moment Mirabel couldn't speak. Nine years of bunks that moved, of waking to check the anchor drag, of never fully sleeping anywhere because anywhere might become somewhere she had to flee. The idea of a room — a fixed room, in a fixed house, on a fixed street, in a town that knew her name and her father's name and had decided, provisionally, to keep her anyway — hit her harder than any flood had.

"All right," she managed. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Rent's due monthly." Maren walked off up the dock road, and Mirabel stood in the rain a moment longer, watching her go, watching the town rise around her, watching the river slide past carrying everything downstream toward the sea.

Then she went back aboard, checked her lines, logged the arrival — *Trail's Landing, 10:47, on time minus eleven minutes* — and began, for the first time in her adult life, to prepare for the return trip knowing full well that she would make it, and the one after that, and the one after that, all the way into whatever the river and the years had left to give her.

The rain thinned. Somewhere up the new street, a hammer started ringing. The Sparrowhawk rocked gently against the fenders, patient, going nowhere, home.
