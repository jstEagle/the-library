## 1. The Town That Keeps Its Head Down

The trick to being nobody, Ines Halloran had learned, was to be useful in small ways and remarkable in none.

Rivergate made that easy. It was a town built low and close to the water, as if it had agreed long ago not to give the river anything tall to knock down. The buildings hunched under slate roofs gone green at the seams. The streets ran crooked because they'd been cow paths first. Even the church steeple leaned a little, apologetically, like a man who'd stood up too fast in company.

Ines had been there eleven months. Long enough to know which dock boards rotted in spring. Not so long that anyone asked questions worth answering.

They took the survey job the way they took all their jobs now: through a middleman, for modest pay, with no name on the paperwork. The contract said *field technician, hydrographic assessment, seasonal*. It did not say *Ines Halloran, formerly of the Halloran & Okonkwo Expedition Partnership, formerly certified at the highest tier of mountain and river guiding on two continents, formerly a person whose signature meant something.* Paperwork was kinder that way. Paperwork let you start over without anyone having to watch you do it.

The work itself was simple. Run the survey lines up and down the lower river, log depths, mark shoals, note the silt drift after each rain. The town council wanted a chart updated before they leased out mooring rights to some outfit from downriver. Ines didn't ask which outfit. Asking questions was how you became memorable, and being memorable was how you ended up like—well. Like before.

* * *

Mornings, Ines rose before the gulls got greedy and walked down to the public dock where the survey skiff was tied. The walk took four minutes if you didn't stop, and Ines never stopped, because stopping was how conversations started.

"Morning, Halloran."

Damn it. Tam again.

Tam Vess worked the docks the way other people breathed—continuously, and with an air of doing it better than you. She was maybe twenty-five, broad-shouldered, with a permanent smear of tar on one forearm that she claimed was hereditary. Her grandmother had fished this stretch of river; Tam said so often enough that Ines felt they knew the woman personally, deceased though she was.

"Morning," Ines said, and kept walking.

"You know your portside bilge pump's making that noise again."

"It always makes that noise."

"Yeah, but today it's making it *worse*. Like it's complaining about you specifically." Tam fell into step, uninvited and undeterred, the way she did every morning. "Also, Marta at the chandlery says you bought rope last week. Good rope. The expensive kind."

"I needed rope."

"For a depth survey."

Ines kept their eyes on the skiff. "Rope is rope."

"See, that's what somebody would say who just needed rope." Tam grinned, hopping onto the dock ahead of them and crouching to check the tie-off with a familiarity she had absolutely not been granted. "Somebody else might say a person who buys forty meters of marine-grade line is planning something. Going somewhere. Doing a little more than logging mud depths for the council."

Ines stepped past her and began untying the skiff. "I'm planning to log mud depths."

"One day," Tam said, standing, brushing off her knees, "you're going to tell me what you actually did before Rivergate. And I'm going to act very surprised, and you're going to feel better, and then I'll buy you a drink."

"That sounds like a lot of talking."

"It does." Tam backed away down the dock, hands in her pockets, entirely pleased with herself. "I'm patient, though. Grandmother used to say the river keeps everything, it just makes you wait for it."

Ines watched her go and felt the familiar ache settle in behind the ribs—the one that wasn't quite loneliness, because loneliness implied you wanted company, and this was more complicated than wanting. This was the discipline of not wanting. Eleven months of practice. You could get good at almost anything with practice, Ines had found. Including shrinking.

* * *

The survey lines ran from the town moorings up toward the mouth of the gorge, where the river narrowed between walls of grey granite and the current went from lazy to opinionated. Ines worked the skiff alone, running transects with the sonar puck trailing behind, marking numbers in a waterproof notebook with a pencil worn down to a stub.

It was good work. That was the cruel part. Ines was good at it, and being good at it felt like a hand closing around an old scar—not painful exactly, but insistent. Reminding you there had been a time when competence was a whole identity, when people looked at you across a rope bridge or a flooded passage and trusted you with their lives, and you had earned that trust every single day or died trying.

Now the trust extended to: *this person will accurately record the depth of the channel.*

It was enough. Ines told themselves it was enough the way you tell yourself a room is big enough once you've moved all the furniture against the walls.

On the fourth day of the survey, the weather turned. Rain came up the valley in a grey wall, and Ines ran the last line of the afternoon in a hiss of water on water, visibility down to nothing, the gorge walls vanishing into cloud. They should have turned back early. A smarter person would have. But the rain covered sound, covered sightlines, covered *them*, and there was a shameful comfort in being out here where nobody could watch Ines Halloran be small.

That was when the dredge snagged.

Not the usual snag—driftwood, old netting, the occasional shopping cart some teenager had sent over the rail years ago. This was a hard, deep pull, the line going taut and staying taut, the skiff swinging quarter-to the current like a dog hitting the end of its leash.

Ines killed the engine and listened. Rain on the canopy. River shoving past the hull. And below, faintly, through the hull itself, a grinding note—metal on stone, but wrong somehow. Too resonant. Too *kept*, like something that had been waiting underwater and had opinions about being disturbed.

They spent twenty minutes working the line, easing it, taking strain off and putting it back on, reading the snag the way they'd once read rockfall slopes. Whatever it was, it wasn't fouled. It was *anchored*. Something heavy, set deliberately, holding position in the current.

Ines marked the coordinates. Then, because the rain was still falling and nobody was watching and the ache behind the ribs was louder than usual, they did something they hadn't done in eleven months.

They made a decision like a professional.

Tomorrow, they told themselves. Tomorrow, with daylight and slack water and someone else on shore who knew where I went. That's the protocol. That's what a responsible guide does.

But the river had already coughed once, and Ines Halloran had spent a year learning to keep their head down, and the two facts sat together in the swaying skiff like strangers forced to share a bench.

* * *

Back at the dock, soaked through, Ines coiled the lines and found Tam waiting under the fuel shed awning, eating something out of paper.

"Your bilge pump's worse," she said.

"It's fine."

"And you're smiling."

"I am not smiling."

"You're doing the thing where your face forgets it's supposed to be bored." Tam crumpled the paper, missed the bin, didn't care. "Something happened upriver."

"The river shifted some silt. Snagged my gear."

"On what?"

"Silt."

Tam studied them for a long moment, rain drumming the awning above them both, and then—mercifully, unexpectedly—she shrugged. "Fine. Keep your secrets. Everybody in this town's got one. Old Pell at the chandlery isn't really named Pell. The Widow Anser keeps a second stove going all summer for reasons nobody's ever established. My grandmother came off a boat sixty years ago from a country that doesn't exist anymore, and she never once said its name out loud, and we all just... let her." She pushed off the post she was leaning against. "Point is, Rivergate keeps its head down. It's sort of our whole thing. So whatever you snagged—" she tapped her own nose twice, "—I didn't see anything."

She walked off into the rain, and Ines stood there feeling something dangerous loosen in their chest.

A country that doesn't exist anymore. Ines filed the phrase away without knowing why, the way you pocket a strange coin.

* * *

That night, in the rented room above the chandlery, Ines laid out the survey notes and copied the coordinates onto a separate scrap of paper, then burned the scrap in the stove and watched the ash curl. Old habits. Paranoia was a skill too, and theirs had stayed sharp even as everything else went soft.

Eleven months of being nobody. Eleven months of small jobs, cash wages, no name on paperwork, no signature that meant anything. Eleven months since the inquiry, since the report, since the word *blacklisted* got attached to their name like a tag on a corpse—and since Sefa.

Ines didn't think about Sefa. That was another discipline, practiced daily, mostly successful. But disciplines were like muscles: they ached most on quiet nights, and tonight the rain was loud and the room was cold and the ash in the stove was still warm.

Somewhere out there, the thing on the riverbed sat in the dark, anchored against the current, waiting with a patience older than Ines's whole ruined career.

Tomorrow, Ines promised it. Tomorrow, with daylight and slack water.

The river, being a river, did not agree to wait.
