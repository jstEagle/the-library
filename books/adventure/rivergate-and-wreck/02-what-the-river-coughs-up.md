## 2. What the River Coughs Up

The Rivergate survey was the kind of job Ines Halloran took precisely because nobody would ever write a book about it.

Three weeks of depth soundings and silt samples along six kilometers of the Alderwash, upstream of the town's rickety commercial docks. The client was a gravel concern that wanted to know whether its barges could run heavier loads in late summer without scraping bottom. The pay was modest. The paperwork was minimal. The questions asked of Ines Halloran, formerly of the Kestrel Traverse Expedition, formerly of every respectable guide registry on two continents, were exactly zero.

That was the whole appeal.

Ines had learned, over seven years of exile, that a person could shrink their life down to something small enough to hide behind. Not disappear — rivers kept records, and so did people — but become unremarkable. A surveyor with a rented dredge barge and a laminated permit was furniture. Furniture didn't get interviewed. Furniture didn't get recognized at fuel docks by former clients who'd read the inquiry findings.

So Ines rose before dawn each morning, motored the *Mudlark* — a name the rental company had painted on her hull with what Ines suspected was deliberate irony — out past the last mooring buoy, and spent the day listening to the river talk.

Rivers talked constantly. Most people just never learned the language. The Alderwash spoke in a low gravelly dialect this time of year, post-spring-melt, all sediment and slow opinion. It told Ines where the channel had wandered since the last survey. It told Ines which banks were eating themselves and which were building new ground out of stolen dirt. And on the fourth morning of the second week, somewhere below the old ferry landing where the water deepened from four meters to nine in a single green step, it said something Ines had never heard a river say before.

It said: *there is something down here that does not belong to me.*

* * *

The snag announced itself as a shudder through the dredge cable — not the soft stutter of a root or the grudging scrape of bedrock, but a hard, bell-like *thunk* that traveled up the line and into Ines's sternum like a knock on a door.

Ines killed the winch and stood very still.

Seven years of small jobs taught you the vocabulary of snags. There were maybe a dozen common ones, and you catalogued them the way a doctor catalogues coughs. Logs rolled free. Boulders held fast but gave honest resistance. Old fence wire came up in miserable bird's nests. Sunken boats — and the Alderwash had its share, mostly rowboats and one memorable paddle steamer the town still argued about — came up in pieces, reluctantly, complaining the whole way.

This was none of those. This was solid. Compact. Heavy in a way that suggested iron bands and dense wood, sitting square on the bottom like it had been set there rather than dropped.

"Whatever you are," Ines muttered to the water, "you're somebody's problem."

The professional move — the smart move, the invisible-person move — would have been to log the obstruction, mark the coordinates, note *possible debris, recommend avoidance*, and motor on. Gravel companies did not pay for curiosities. Curiosities attracted attention, and attention was the one commodity Ines Halloran had spent seven years learning to live without.

Ines logged the coordinates anyway. Then, after a long moment of argument with no one, reversed the barge and dropped the dredge head back down.

Habit, Ines told herself. Due diligence. A good surveyor confirms an obstruction's nature before recommending anything. This was textbook procedure. This was absolutely not curiosity.

The Alderwash, for its part, said nothing, which from a river was practically a wink.

* * *

The problem with hauling something up alone became apparent around the moment the thing broke the surface.

It was a chest. Not a sea chest, not a tool chest — a strongbox, the kind built to survive being thrown overboard on purpose. Black oak gone silver-grey with age, bound in iron straps that had rusted to the color of dried blood, and sealed — actually sealed, Ines realized with a jolt — under a skin of pitch that someone had applied with care, corner to corner, like they'd expected it to spend decades underwater and wanted it to come out intact.

And stamped into a lead plate set into the lid, worn but unmistakable:

A mountain above three converging rivers. A crown floating above the peaks, detached, as if the crown belonged to nothing beneath it.

Ines knew that mark. Everyone past forty knew that mark, the way everyone past forty knew the smell of a particular decade. Vellmark. The kingdom-that-wasn't, the country that had been on maps when Ines was a child and wasn't by the time Ines could read them properly. Erased in the Border Reconciliation — a phrase Ines's mother had always delivered with enough vinegar to pickle eggs. Borders redrawn, nation dissolved, archives scattered across three successor states like a hand of cards thrown down in anger.

Forty years dead, and here was its seal, dripping river water onto the deck of a rented dredge barge called the *Mudlark*.

Ines sat down on the winch housing without entirely meaning to.

Professional detachment, Ines discovered, was a skill like any other, and it had limits. You could keep your face flat through a blacklisting hearing. You could keep your hands steady signing forms that ended your career. But you could not look at the national seal of a drowned country rising out of a river you'd been hired to measure for gravel and feel nothing. That wasn't detachment. That was brain damage.

The chest sat on the deck, streaming, patient, smug.

"I'm logging you," Ines informed it. "As an obstruction. Possibly a hazard to navigation. And then I am going to report you to the harbormaster, because that is what a careful, invisible person does."

The chest continued to exist, magnificently.

"...After lunch," Ines added, and hated how relieved that sounded.

* * *

Lunch happened at the dockside chandlery, which doubled as the only place in Rivergate that sold coffee worth drinking and opinions worth avoiding. Ines was halfway through a sandwich and a chart annotation when the dockhand appeared.

Tam Brannick was twenty-three, built like a capstan, and possessed of the specific energy of a young person who had grown up on working docks and found them insufficiently interesting. She'd helped Ines tie off the *Mudlark* twice now, chattering cheerfully both times about things Ines hadn't asked about — the migratory habits of cormorants, the correct way to insult a barge pilot, her grandmother's recipe for eel stew ("it's a threat, not a recipe, that's how she phrases it"). Ines had responded with the minimum viable number of words required by politeness, which Tam had apparently interpreted as *deep rapport*.

"Heard your winch screaming from the ferry landing," Tam said, dropping onto the bench opposite without invitation. "That's a heavy pull. What'd you snag?"

"A log," Ines said.

"On the deep shelf below the old ferry crossing? Where the channel drops to nine meters?" Tam drank half of Ines's coffee without asking. "Nobody logs there. Current scours it clean. My gran says the deep shelf eats things, though. Says during the war years stuff went in the water upriver and came back down years later."

"Your gran sounds like a reliable hydrographic source."

"She's a hundred and one and remembers everything, so yes, actually." Tam leaned forward, and Ines watched with mounting resignation as the dockhand's eyes went to the open hatch of the *Mudlark*, then narrowed, then went wide. "That's not a log."

"It's a box."

"That's a *strongbox*. That's got iron banding. That's—" Tam was already standing. "That's got a seal on it."

"Tam."

But Tam was moving, and Tam moved the way weather moved — with total commitment and no regard for local objections. By the time Ines caught up, Tam was crouched at the hatch, dripping coffee, staring at the lead plate with an expression Ines couldn't immediately classify. Reverence, maybe. Or recognition.

"My gran has this on a tin," Tam said quietly. All the chatter had gone out of her voice, and what remained was strange and careful. "Her sewing tin. She keeps her citizenship papers under it. Mountain, three rivers, floating crown." She looked up. "That's Vellmark."

"It's a stamp," Ines said. "Stamps get copied. Salvage fraud is—"

"Ines." Tam said the name once, gently, and Ines stopped talking. "I grew up on stories about the convoys. The last ships out before the borders closed. Half my street's grandmothers came down that river and never went home. If that chest came off one of those ships—" She swallowed. "You have to help me get it ashore properly. Off the water. Before anyone else sees it."

Every instinct Ines had honed over seven years said: *no. Report it. Log it. Be furniture.* Furniture didn't haul national relics off rivers with dockhands who drank strangers' coffee.

"Properly meaning what?" Ines heard herself say.

"Meaning my uncle has a hand truck and doesn't ask questions. Meaning it comes to my gran's boathouse, which has a dry floor and a lock, meaning it gets opened by lamplight instead of on a public dock." Tam was already looping a sling under the chest with the easy competence of someone who'd moved a thousand heavy things and respected every one of them. "Come on. Between the two of us we can walk it up the ramp if we're careful. It weighs less than it looks — pitch-sealed, see, it's been keeping itself dry. Somebody loved this thing."

Somebody loved this thing. Ines took the other end of the sling and found the weight settle against her shoulder with a strange, almost ceremonial gravity, like taking a turn carrying something in a procession.

The chest came ashore at half past four in the afternoon, borne up the ramp between a disgraced mountain guide and a dockhand who wouldn't stop talking, while the Alderwash ran high and quiet behind them, saying nothing at all.

* * *

Gran Brannick's boathouse smelled of tar, wool, and eel stew, and contained exactly one hundred and one years' worth of opinions, most of them currently aimed at the chest.

"So," the old woman said. She was tiny, fierce, and seated on an upturned crate with her hands folded like a magistrate. "Forty years I wait, and the river sends it to a surveyor and my granddaughter."

"You know what it is?" Ines asked.

"I know the seal." Gran tapped her sewing tin, which bore the same mountain-and-crown, rubbed nearly smooth by thumbs. "My father packed that mark on everything he owned the night we left. Said as long as one box with that stamp floated, Vellmark wasn't finished." Her eyes, pale and sharp as gull's beaks, fixed on Ines. "Well, surveyor. Are you going to open it, or are you going to stand there looking guilty?"

"I was going to suggest we document the exterior first. Condition notes. Photographs. Provenance matters if—"

"If anyone ever asks," Tam finished, grinning. "Nobody's going to ask, Ines. That's the whole point of my gran's boathouse."

Ines looked at the chest. The chest, pitch-black and patient, looked back.

Somewhere out on the darkening river, a barge horn sounded, low and long, like something clearing its throat before speaking.

Ines picked up the pry bar.
