## 3. The Manifest in the False Bottom

The chest sat on Ines's kitchen table like a guest who refused to explain itself.

They had carried it up from the dock the night before, wrapped in Tam's tarpaulin, past the shuttered fishmongers and the boarding house where the landlady pretended not to count Ines's comings and goings. Rivergate was good at pretending not to see things. It was, Ines had come to understand, the town's chief industry after fishing and before regret.

Now morning light came through the salt-hazed window and lay across the chest in a long pale stripe, and Ines sat across from it with two cups of coffee because their hands wanted something to do and one cup hadn't been enough of a job.

It was a handsome thing, for a box that had spent forty years learning the river's manners. Iron-banded, oak-planked, its corners fitted with brass that had gone the color of old honey. The Vellmark seal was stamped into a lead medallion set into the lid: a bridge over three waves, beneath a crown with no king left to wear it. Ines had seen the seal exactly twice before—once in a history primer as a child, once on a salvage tag in a museum case in another country, behind glass, labeled *RECOVERED ARTIFACT. NATION EXTINCT.*

Nations weren't supposed to be extinct. Companies were supposed to be extinct. Ines had spent the last six years being personally acquainted with how thoroughly a company could erase a person, and even they had needed paperwork and a hearing and a committee vote to do it. A whole country going under—that took either an ocean or an act of shareholders.

"Right," Ines told the chest. "You're going to be reasonable about this."

The chest said nothing, which Ines found respectful.

* * *

The lock was the first surprise. It wasn't rusted shut, which it should have been. When Ines worked a pick into it—guides learned locks the way they learned knots; both kept you alive when plans failed—the mechanism turned with a soft, oiled willingness, as if someone had serviced it recently.

Ines sat back and looked at it for a long moment.

Forty years underwater. Oiled lock. Either the river had developed hobbies, or the chest had been sealed against water so well that the interior had never flooded. Salvage chests of the old passenger lines were built that way—double-walled, waxed seams, meant to survive a sinking so the manifests could be recovered for insurance. The companies had cared about paper more than passengers. That was not a lesson history had taught anyone, apparently.

Inside, the lid opened on dry air that smelled of cedar and cold iron. No water. Not a drop. Ines felt something tighten pleasantly in their chest—the old thrill, the one from finding a route through a serac field or a cave mouth where the maps showed none—and they made themselves breathe slowly, because the last time they'd let that feeling drive, it had cost them everything.

The chest held three things.

The first was a bundle of documents wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred cord. The second was a small strongbox, palm-sized, made of dull grey metal with no visible seam and no lock at all—just a seal of the same lead-and-bridge design pressed flat into its face, as if the box itself were the seal. And the third was a folded chart, heavy stock, hand-annotated, of a stretch of river Ines knew better than their own signature.

Ines didn't touch the chart yet. They unwrapped the documents instead, because documents told you what you were looking at, and charts only told you where.

The top sheet was a manifest.

Not cargo. Passengers.

*V.S. *Corvenne*, Passenger Registry, Final Sailing.* One hundred and twelve names. Ines read them the way you read any list of the dead—slowly, though you knew none of them, because names deserved it. Families grouped together. Children listed by initial. Occupations noted in a neat clerk's hand: *weaver, hydrologist, schoolteacher, pilot.* An entire town's worth of people, it seemed, or the part of a town that had been getting out.

The final sailing. Out of Vellmark, forty years ago, when the maps were being redrawn and the borders were being closed and everyone who could afford passage was buying it from whoever still sold it.

Destination column: blank. Cause of loss: blank. Instead, where the clerk's tidy columns should have concluded, someone had written one line in a different hand—faster, harder, the pen pressed deep enough to emboss the page:

*Lost in the Gorge. All hands. All souls. Ask the river why.*

Ines set the manifest down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening.

"Ask the river why," they repeated. Their voice came out flatter than they intended. "Sure. Love a riddle I can drown in."

The second document was the shipping order. It named the *Corvenne*'s route—not the official channel, but the gorge run, the deep cut twenty miles upstream of Rivergate where the river dropped through basalt narrows and the water moved like something with opinions. There was a note appended, unsigned, in the same hard hand: *Chartered dark. No port logs. Manifest travels separately. If we don't surface, the chest does.*

And then the third document, and this was the one that made Ines put down their coffee so they wouldn't spill it.

A receipt. For one sealed testimony case, lodged with the vessel's master, to be delivered upon arrival to the Commission on Border Territories. Contents itemized in bureaucratic language that tried very hard to sound boring: *depositions, navigational records, correspondence pertaining to convoy movements, season 40 prior.*

Convoy movements. Season 40 prior. Ines did the arithmetic without wanting to. Forty years ago. The last year of Vellmark. The year the convoys stopped running and the nation stopped appearing on anything, all inside eighteen months, neat as a ledger being balanced.

Testimony. Sealed testimony, traveling on a passenger ship chartered in secret, addressed to a commission that—if it had ever existed—had certainly never published whatever it received.

Ines looked at the little grey strongbox at the bottom of the chest, sitting there smug and seamless, and felt two feelings arrive at once and refuse to queue politely.

The first was dread. It came up through the floorboards, through the soles of their boots, the way it always did: a cold recognition that nothing good had ever come from knowing too much about why powerful people erased things. Ines had a blacklist stamped through their own name for asking one question at the wrong meeting. They lived in a rented room above a chandlery in a town whose entire civic philosophy was *keep your head down*. They had a survey contract with a company that paid promptly precisely so that no one would ever have a reason to look at them closely. This box was not a discovery. This box was a lit flare in a dry forest, and Ines was standing in it.

The second feeling was worse, because it was hungrier.

Temptation. The old animal want of it: *you are the one who found it, you are the one who can read the coordinates, you are the one who knows the gorge, nobody else alive knows that stretch of water the way you do.* Six years of being careful, of being small, of taking surveys and saying thank you—and here, in their kitchen, was a thing that mattered. A real thing. A true thing, maybe, sealed and waiting, and the river had gone to the trouble of coughing it up directly into their hands, which even Ines's flattened faith had to admit looked like intent.

"You're a surveyor," Ines said aloud, to themselves, in the voice they used on clients who wanted to cut corners. "You dredge silt. You measure depths. You file reports that say *silt, depth, filed.*"

But their eyes went back to the chart.

* * *

They spread it open at last, weighting the corners with the coffee cups and a tin of matches, and there it was: the gorge, drawn by someone who had known it intimately. Not the commercial chart—the honest one. Side channels marked. Eddies named. Depth soundings in a confident hand, corrected in places, argued with in others, little marginal notes in a language Ines didn't speak but could half-read anyway, because numbers are numbers: *too shallow in August. Falls loud, rocks deaf. Here the river forgives nothing.*

And in the narrows, behind the great falls where the gorge bent north, a single mark in red ink. A circle, and beside it, in the same hard hand as the manifest:

*Corvenne. Resting. Do not disturb without cause. Bring cause.*

Ines sat back. The light had moved; the coffee had gone cold; somewhere below, the chandlery was opening its shutters, and Tam would be on the dock, and the day's survey was waiting, and none of that seemed to belong to the same world as the red circle.

Because Ines knew that bend. Of course they did. They'd guided the gorge twice, back when guiding was still a word they were allowed to use, and they'd looked at that stretch of white water below the falls and thought, professionally, *nothing rests down there, that's a meat grinder, anything that went in got chewed.* But the chart said otherwise. The chart said the falls hid a ledge—a back-eddy pocket behind the curtain of water where the current curled and gave up, where something heavy and lucky could settle and stay. Ines remembered the eddy. Remembered thinking it was the one merciful piece of water in the whole gorge.

Something had used it. Something one hundred and twelve souls large.

The dread and the temptation had stopped queuing and started dancing, and Ines stood up abruptly, needing their body to do something their mind couldn't referee.

They washed the cups. They coiled rope that didn't need coiling. They ate bread they didn't taste. Outside, gulls argued over the fish waste, and the river ran brown and patient past the docks, carrying on with its long work of keeping every secret it had ever been given.

Here was the arithmetic Ines kept arriving at, no matter how they shuffled the terms:

If the chest was salvage, it belonged to whoever held the concession—which was the survey company, which was a chain of ownership that ended somewhere Ines had sworn never to stand again. Report it, and it vanished into a warehouse, and Ines went back to being small, and whatever truth was welded inside that seamless grey box stayed welded.

If the chest was *not* reported—if Ines kept it, opened it, followed it upriver—then they were doing the exact thing that had gotten them blacklisted in the first place. Not the falsifying. The other thing. The refusing. The *I know what the report is supposed to say, and I'm telling you what the water actually says.* They had lost everything once to that instinct, and the interesting question, the one Ines had avoided for six years, was whether the instinct had ever been the problem—or whether the problem was the people who kept punishing it.

"Bring cause," Ines murmured to the chart.

The red circle gleamed, wet-looking in the window light, though the ink was forty years dry.

There was a knock at the door. Two knocks, quick, then a pause, then one more—Tam's rhythm, established over a winter of shared coffee and shared silence, the knock of someone who had learned not to startle a person with a past.

Ines looked at the chest. At the manifest with its hundred and twelve names. At the grey box that had crossed a drowned nation and a drowned ship and forty years of brown water to end up on a kitchen table in the one town full of grandchildren of the people who had escaped.

They covered the chest with the tarpaulin, which fooled no one, least of all themselves.

"Yeah," Ines called, opening the door. "Come in. You're going to want to sit down."
