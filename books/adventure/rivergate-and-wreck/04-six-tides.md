## 4. Six Tides

The Harbor Office of Rivergate occupied the ground floor of a building that had once been a customs house, then a chandlery, then briefly — according to a faded plaque nobody had ever bothered to remove — a Museum of Riverine Antiquities, which had closed after eleven days when it turned out the entire collection was one canoe. Now it smelled of wet paper, pipe smoke, and the particular staleness of bureaucracy that had been left out in the rain and dried wrong.

Ines stood at the counter while a clerk with ink-stained cuffs and the expression of a man who had personally witnessed every mistake in municipal history shuffled through a drawer of permits.

"Halloran," the clerk said, reading the name off the survey license like it might be an alias. "You're the one doing the channel soundings for the ferry company."

"That's me."

"Routine work."

"Very routine."

"Nothing interesting about it."

"Nothing at all," Ines agreed, and waited.

The clerk produced a folded document and a stamp, stamped it, blotted it, stamped it again for reasons known only to himself, and slid it across the counter. "Concession permit. Covers the lower gorge from the ferry landing up to the narrows. You'll want to read the back."

"I always read the back."

"Most people don't." The clerk looked at Ines over his spectacles with something that was almost sympathy, or possibly indigestion. "Read this one twice."

* * *

Ines read it three times, first on the office steps, then walking, then sitting on the bollard at the end of the public dock where the light came through the morning fog in long gray bars. The permit language was the usual sediment — clauses, subclauses, exceptions to exceptions — but near the bottom, in type only slightly smaller than the rest, was the sentence that mattered:

*All concession activity within Gorge Survey Zone 4 shall terminate upon commencement of the sixth tidal cycle following issuance, at which point seasonal high water shall render the zone impassable.*

Six tides.

Ines did the arithmetic without wanting to. The gorge flooded every spring when the snowmelt came down from the high country and met the sea coming in, and the two of them argued about who got the riverbed until the whole lower gorge became a churning brown throat that no sane pilot entered and no salvage crew could work. Every year the river took the gorge back for six weeks, scrubbing it clean of human ambition. It was one of the reasons Rivergate kept its head down. The town had learned, over generations, that the river did not negotiate.

Twelve days, give or take, depending on how the moon felt about it. Twelve days before the wreck site — if there was a wreck site, if the coordinates on that impossible manifest meant anything — went under forty feet of violent water until autumn.

Ines sat on the bollard and watched the fog lift off the water and felt something wake up in their chest that they had spent four years teaching to sleep.

It wasn't excitement, exactly. Excitement was a young person's word, all elbows. This was older and more suspicious — the feeling of a lock clicking open after years of rust, accompanied by the immediate thought: *and what exactly have I just unlocked?*

"You've got your thinking face on," said Tam, arriving with two cups of dock coffee, which was a substance technically legal in most jurisdictions. She handed one over. "That's either good news or you're planning a crime."

"Could be both."

"Those are my favorite kinds of news." Tam perched on the next bollard. She was maybe twenty-three, built like someone who'd been hauling rope since she could walk, with her grandmother's charts rolled in a waterproof tube that never seemed to leave her shoulder. "So. The chest."

"The chest," Ines said.

They had agreed — without ever actually saying it aloud — not to talk about the chest where anyone could hear. Which meant they talked about it at dawn on the empty dock, or in Tam's boathouse with the doors shut, or in the particular silence of two people drinking bad coffee and communicating entirely through eyebrow movements.

"Permit runs out in six tides," Ines said. "After that the gorge floods the whole lower reach. Whatever's down there stays down there till fall."

Tam blew on her coffee. "And the manifest says—"

"A passenger vessel. Lost in the gorge. Forty years ago, give or take." Ines kept their voice level, professional, the voice of a person discussing soundings. "There's no passenger vessel lost in the gorge. Not in any registry, any accident report, any fisherman's story worth the telling. A ship that size doesn't vanish. Ships that size make paperwork. Wrecks make wrecks. That gorge has given up three hulls in living memory and I've dived two of them."

"So either the manifest is lying."

"Or everything else is."

The fog was burning off. Downriver, the water lay flat and silver, innocent as a held breath. Ines looked at it and their mind — the old mind, the expedition mind, the one that used to run routes through icefields and rapids and once, memorably, through a diplomatic incident — started laying out the problem the way it used to lay out everything: in pieces, in order, in time.

Tide one: confirm the coordinates against the gorge bathymetry. Tide two: get eyes on the site, even from the surface. Tides three through five: dive it, if it was divable, if the currents allowed, if the ledges hadn't shifted in forty years. Tide six: be gone, because the river did not issue warnings and did not accept apologies.

Four years of small careful life, and here was a problem with a shape again. A real one. With tides.

"There's a complication," Ines said.

"There's always a complication. Complications are the fun part."

"The chest is salvage. Salvage has law attached. If I report it, the harbor authority impounds it, the claim gets filed, and every licensed outfit within six hundred miles gets notice." Ines turned the cup in their hands. "Including, eventually, whoever actually wants what's inside that chest badly enough to seal it in a false bottom and sink it."

Tam considered this. "And you don't think it sank by accident."

"Nobody packs a manifest in wax cloth and hides a second compartment under a false bottom by accident, Tam. Somebody wanted that chest to survive being lost. That's not cargo. That's a message in a bottle, thrown very hard."

Tam grinned, and it was such a young, uncomplicated grin that it made Ines feel simultaneously ancient and oddly lighter. "So we don't report it."

"We don't report it yet."

"That's the crime face again."

"It's the *prudence* face."

"It's definitely the crime face." Tam drained her coffee and stood, shouldering the chart tube. "My grandmother used to say the gorge gives you exactly as much time as it decides to give you, and never a minute more. Six tides." She looked downriver, and for a moment the grin was gone and something older looked out of her face — the refugee's inheritance, the sense that maps and nations and even rivers could be taken away between one season and the next. "Then we'd best not waste the first one standing around."

* * *

The first tide, Ines spent the way they'd spent ten thousand hours of their former life: preparing.

Not the glamorous kind of preparing. The kind that looked, from the outside, like a disgraced guide puttering around a rented boathouse with a hand pump and a bad attitude. Ines checked the survey skiff's seams and found them honest. Checked the dredge winch and re-spooled the line so it wouldn't foul. Checked the tide tables against the almanac against the harbor master's board, because tide tables were written by optimists and the gorge was not an optimist's environment.

Oskar Vint turned up mid-morning, ostensibly to complain about the noise.

"You're sharpening an anchor fluke," he said from the doorway. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a river pilot's license suspended somewhere in a filing cabinet and hands that still knew knots his brain had forgotten he knew. "With a whetstone. On a Sunday."

"It needed sharpening."

"Nothing needs sharpening on a Sunday. That's how you know a person's about to do something stupid. They start maintaining equipment on their day off like the equipment's going to save them." He came in anyway, because Oskar always came in anyway, and picked up the spare fluke and started sharpening it without being asked. "What's the job?"

"Channel soundings."

"In the gorge?"

"Lower reach."

"With a dive kit I can see drying behind you." Oskar didn't look up from the fluke. "I ran that gorge for nineteen years, Halloran. Before. I know what the lower reach sounds like at slack water, and I know what a dive compressor sounds like, and those are different songs." He tested the edge with his thumb, nodded grudgingly. "Whatever you're doing down there, the current at the narrows runs four knots on the ebb. Five if the wind's northerly. And there's a ledge shelf below the falls that isn't on any chart because the chartmakers gave up. Forty feet down, maybe more now. Silt moves on it every spring like the river's making its bed."

Ines kept their hands moving on the whetstone. "Sounds like you remember it well."

"I remember everything well. That's my curse." Oskar set the fluke down. "I'm not asking what you're doing. I'm telling you that whatever it is, six tides is five more than the gorge will politely allow if you go in arrogant, and one less than you'll wish you had if you go in scared. There's a number in between. Pilots call it *respect*. Divers call it *coming back up*."

"That's the plan."

"Plans are what people write down so the river knows what to laugh at." But he stayed another hour, and together they rigged the skiff's tow points and re-tarred the anchor line, and neither of them mentioned that a suspended pilot had no business helping with any of it, and both of them were better for the company.

* * *

Evening came down the valley the way it always did in Rivergate, blue and slow, lighting the water up like hammered tin. Ines sat on the boathouse roof — the flat part over the tool shed, accessible via a ladder that Tam had declared structurally fine and which Ines trusted approximately sixty percent — with the manifest unrolled across their knees and the last of the light on it.

*Passenger vessel, name withheld by consignor. Loss location: gorge reach, below falls. Coordinates follow.*

The coordinates were precise. Not approximate, not dead-reckoned — precise to the minute, the kind of numbers you only got from someone who had been there, or someone who had been told by someone who had been there and understood exactly what the numbers were worth. Forty years ago, someone had known precisely where that hull lay, and had written it down, and sealed it, and set it adrift or sunk it or hidden it in whatever chain of custody ended with a dredge snagging it in the shipping channel like the river handing over evidence.

And beneath the coordinates, in the same careful hand, a single line Ines had read perhaps forty times since the boathouse:

*Testimony enclosed. Let it surface.*

Ines looked upriver, toward the gorge, where the walls went dark first and the water went dark last. Somewhere past the narrows, past the falls, under a ledge that wasn't on any chart, there was a ship full of people that official history insisted had never existed, carrying words that somebody had died — or lived, which was sometimes harder — to preserve.

Six tides. The clock wasn't ticking; it was tidal, which was worse. It didn't tick. It pulled.

Old competence moved through Ines like warmth returning to cold hands — painful at first, then steady. Lists assembled themselves. Risks sorted into columns. The body remembered how to sleep lightly and eat efficiently and stand at a rail reading water. Four years of shrinking, of keeping the head down, of being a person shaped like a gap in a ledger — and underneath it all, apparently, the guide had been waiting, patient as ballast, for a reason to stand up.

"One tide down," Ines said to the darkening river. "Five to go."

The river said nothing, which from a river was never reassuring.

It was Tam who brought the news at full dark, running up the dock road with her coat flying, and Ines knew before she spoke — knew by the way the harbor lights beyond her were suddenly, urgently awake, by the low horn sounding twice off the point, by the shape of the future arriving early as it always did.

"Ship coming up the channel," Tam panted, grabbing the ladder rail. "Three masts, floodlit, riding high and heavy. Big one. Industrial." She caught her breath. "Flying salvage colors, Ines. Working lights and everything. And painted on her bow, big as a barn door—"

Ines was already looking downriver, where the fog had opened like a curtain, and where a vessel too large for Rivergate's modest ambitions was sliding toward the harbor with the calm of something that had never once been told *no* by water.

On her bow, in letters visible from the moon: **IRONWAKE**.
