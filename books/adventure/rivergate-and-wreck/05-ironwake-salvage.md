## 5. Ironwake Salvage

The fleet came up the river on a Tuesday, which Ines would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day Rivergate's market sold the bruised fruit nobody wanted at half price, and that was essentially the business model of Ironwake Salvage: everything discounted, nothing fresh, all of it dented by somebody else's disaster first.

Ines heard the fleet before seeing it. The sound traveled up the gorge the way weather did—a low, patient thrumming, like the river had swallowed an engine and was chewing on it. Tam felt it too. She was coiling rope on the survey barge when she stopped mid-loop, head cocked, the way she did when something was wrong with the water.

"That's not a fish boat," she said.

"No," Ines said.

"That's a *lot* of not-fish-boats."

Ines set down the depth gauge they'd been calibrating and walked to the rail. Around the bend below the old customs house came the fleet: four vessels, gray and white, riding high and clean, the kind of boats that had never known a leak or a bad season or an honest day's rust. The lead ship was long enough to make the customs house look like a child's drawing of a building. On its hull, in letters the size of doors, ran the word IRONWAKE, and beneath it, smaller, the company motto, which Ines had heard recited at industry conferences in another life: *We Recover What the Water Keeps.*

"Subtle," Tam said. "Like a fist in a glove made of money."

Ines didn't answer. Their stomach had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with the fleet and everything to do with the chest sitting in the boathouse three hundred meters away, with its manifest folded inside Ines's coat, with the coordinates they had not yet told a single living soul about. A fleet like that did not come to Rivergate by accident. A fleet like that came because somewhere, in some ledger or some leak, the river had already told its secret.

"Go find Oskar," Ines said. "Tell him to move the chest. Not to the boathouse. Somewhere with a floor that isn't mine."

"Whose floor, then?"

"Anyone's. A stranger's. A church. Somewhere the paper trail goes cold." Ines heard their own voice and was surprised by how steady it was. Old competence, reawakened, had a sound like that—flat, unhurried, the voice of a person stacking sandbags while the water rose.

Tam went. Ines stayed at the rail and watched the fleet come in, and made themselves do the thing they had spent four years practicing: nothing. Stand still. Be a surveyor with a government contract and a boring face. The river carried the fleet's wake up to the pilings, and the pilings groaned, and Ines counted the vessels again because counting was a thing hands and eyes could do while the mind ran elsewhere.

Four ships. A survey vessel, two salvage barges with cranes like gallows, and the command ship. That was not a permit operation. That was a *concession* operation, the kind that required ministerial signatures and six months of filings. Which meant Ironwake had filed those signatures and those filings before the chest ever surfaced. Which meant the chest had not told them anything they didn't already know.

Which meant Ines was not the first person to know about the wreck in the gorge.

They were, possibly, the first person to know it and not be paid to forget it.

* * *

The fleet anchored midstream in the insolent manner of things that assumed the river belonged to them, and by late afternoon a launch had detached from the command ship and come nosing toward Ines's berth at the survey dock. A man stood at the bow. He was not large, but he occupied space the way a filing cabinet occupies a room—vertically, completely, with the confidence that no one would ask him to move. He wore a gray coat that probably cost more than Ines's barge, and he smiled before he was in shouting distance, which Ines had learned, over a long career, was never a good sign. Honest people smiled after they'd assessed you. Salesmen smiled first, to save time.

"Ines Halloran." He said it like a toast. "Corven Ashe, Director of Recovery Operations, Ironwake Salvage and Reclamation. I'd say it's a pleasure, but I suspect we'd both know I was lying, so let's call it a professional interest instead."

"You have the advantage of me," Ines said, which was a lie so comfortable it practically introduced itself. Everyone in salvage knew the name Halloran. It was on the blacklist the way weather was on the horizon.

"Do I?" Ashe stepped onto the dock without asking, which was also a kind of introduction. He looked around at the survey barge with the expression of a man appraising a horse he intended to buy cheap. "River survey. Sediment mapping, flow telemetry, a bit of dredging. Honest work. Quiet work." His eyes came back to Ines. "You always were good at quiet work. That's what the Karst Expedition report said, before the other report said otherwise."

The name landed like a hook set behind the ribs. The Karst Expedition. Ines had not heard anyone say it aloud in four years. They had done their best to make it a word that did not exist, along with *Lira Voss* and *whiteout* and *the rope was cut*.

"I don't know what you're referring to," Ines said.

"No," Ashe agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. That was the point of the whole exercise." He turned, considered the river, spread his hands at it as though presenting it. "Here's my problem, Halloran. Six days from now, the seasonal permits on this stretch of gorge expire, and when they do, the spring tide takes the lower channels and nothing with a hull goes in there until autumn. My company holds a salvage concession on a wreck site in this gorge. The site is real. The wreck is real. What we recover from it is—let's say *commercially sensitive*. And my dive crews are good, but they're company men, and company men dive to a schedule, and schedules are how wrecks eat people."

He let that sit. Downstream, one of the cranes swung lazily, testing its own weight.

"I'm told," Ashe went on, "that there is a guide in this town who once ran Class Five water blind, in a whiteout, with a broken hand, and brought nine people home. I'm told that guide knows the gorge's lower channels better than my charts do, because my charts are forty years old and the river has had forty years to rearrange its furniture." He smiled again, and this time it had teeth in it somewhere, deep down. "I'm also told this guide is available. Because this guide cannot get a permit, a berth, or a job above the level of dredge operator anywhere on this coast."

"Your information is out of date," Ines said. "I have a government survey contract."

"I read it. Congratulations. It pays what, two hundred a month?" He didn't wait for an answer. "I'll pay you four thousand. For six days. Plus a completion bonus that would buy this dock and the bad coffee served on it."

The number sat between them like a dropped tool. Ines looked at it without picking it up.

"And what," Ines said, "would I be diving for?"

"Recovery of historical materials," Ashe said smoothly. "Documents. Effects of the deceased. The usual dignified language for a chest of paper."

"The usual dignified language," Ines repeated. "I've heard it before. It's what companies say when what they mean is *evidence*."

Something moved behind Ashe's face—not a flinch, nothing so honest as a flinch, but a recalculation, the brief internal shuffling of a man deciding how many cards to show. He chose, apparently, to show one more.

"You know," he said, conversationally, "I've always found it interesting. The Karst Expedition. Four people went up that mountain. Two came down. The official report says Halloran made an error in route judgment, abandoned a client, falsified the survivor account. The blacklist went out within a week. Very fast, as these things go. Almost as if the paperwork was already written." He examined his cuff. "And the interesting part, the part I admire, professionally, is that you never fought it. You never appealed. You just—went quiet. Shrunk. Took a survey job in a town where nobody asks questions." He looked up, and his eyes were pale and patient and utterly without warmth. "Most people fight. You folded. That tells me either you're guilty, or you were told very clearly what would happen to the people around you if you didn't fold. And I don't think you're guilty, Halloran. Guilty people don't go quiet. Guilty people go *loud*."

The river moved under the dock. Somewhere behind Ines, Tam's footsteps stopped, just out of sight, listening.

"Why are you telling me this?" Ines asked. Their voice came out lower than they intended.

"Because I want you to understand your position accurately before you refuse me." Ashe straightened his coat. "You have six days of relevance left, Halloran. After the tide takes the gorge, you go back to being a name on a list. I'm offering you the last six days of your old life, at four thousand a day, doing the one thing on this earth you are genuinely great at. All you have to do is dive where I point, recover what I describe, and hand it to me sealed. You don't have to read it. You don't have to know what it is. Ignorance, I'm told, is restful. You've had four years of practice."

He extended a hand. Ines looked at it. The hand was well-manicured and steady and had probably never held anything heavier than a pen, and with that pen, Ines understood suddenly and completely, men like this had written the Karst report, the blacklist, the quiet little machinery that had shrunk a life down to a survey barge and bruised fruit.

"I'll consider it," Ines said.

Ashe's smile didn't waver, but it cooled by a degree, the way a room cools when a door is left open in winter. "Consider quickly. I sail for the gorge at first tide tomorrow." He stepped back onto his launch, then paused, one hand on the rail, and delivered the last of it lightly, almost kindly, which was the worst way. "Oh—and if a certain chest happened to surface in your dredge last week, do be careful with the manifest. Paper degrades. And some paper, when it degrades in the wrong hands, takes other people down with it." He touched two fingers to his brow, a little salute. "Until tomorrow, Halloran. Or not. Your choice. It always was."

The launch pulled away. Ines stood on the dock and watched it go, and the tightness in their chest had a name now, and the name was four years old.

* * *

Tam found them ten minutes later, sitting on the bollard at the end of the dock, doing the breathing exercise a wilderness medic had taught them a decade ago for exactly this kind of moment. In through the nose. Out slow. Pretend you are a rock with a career.

"He knew about the chest," Tam said.

"He knew about the chest."

"Which means someone told him. The dredge crew? The harbormaster? The—"

"Or nobody told him," Ines said, "because he already knew. The chest just confirmed it. Tam." They looked up. The evening light was going copper on the water, and the Ironwake fleet sat in the middle of the river with its running lights coming on, one by one, like a city deciding to stay. "How much do you know about who owned this river forty years ago?"

Tam sat down on the next bollard, which was as close to a shrug as her posture ever got. "My gran said the old maps had another country on them. Up past the gorge. She said the maps got reprinted and the country got... left off. Like a typo nobody fixed on purpose." She frowned. "Vell-something. Why?"

"Because the chest we hauled up had that country's seal on it. And because Corven Ashe just offered me a fortune to dive on a wreck he already knows about, and to hand him paper I'm not allowed to read." Ines rubbed their face with both hands. "And because he told me he knows why I was blacklisted. Not the story. The *reason*. There's a difference, and I've spent four years not looking at it, and he looked straight at it in about nine seconds flat."

Tam was quiet for a moment. Then, in the gentle tone she used on drunks and drowning victims: "You're not going to take his money."

"No."

"You're not going to tell him no, either. Because telling a man like that no is just telling him where you'll be standing."

Ines huffed something that was almost a laugh. "When did you get good at this?"

"Rivergate," Tam said. "Whole town's good at this. We keep our heads down professionally." She bumped her shoulder against Ines's, an anchor-light of contact. "So what do we do?"

Ines looked at the fleet. At the gorge beyond it, going blue and shadowed, where a dead ship held its dead secret behind a curtain of falling water, and the tide was coming in six days to take it all.

"Tomorrow at first tide," Ines said slowly, "Corven Ashe sails for the gorge expecting an answer. So the answer has to already be gone." They stood, and the old competence settled over them like a coat that still fit after all—not comfortably, but it fit. "Find Oskar. I need a pilot who can run the narrows in the dark and hates corporations more than he hates me."

"That's a short list," Tam said. "He's the only one on it."

"Then we only need one."

Tam grinned and went. Ines stayed on the bollard a moment longer, watching the Ironwake lights burn on the black water, and thought about Ashe's parting words. *Some paper, when it degrades in the wrong hands, takes other people down with it.*

He thought he was threatening her.

He had no idea he was recruiting her.

Ines turned toward the town, toward the boathouse, toward the manifest with its impossible coordinates, and began, at last, to plan.
