## 6. The Partner Who Didn't Drown

The rain came in off the river around noon, the kind of gray, patient rain that Rivergate wore like an old coat. Ines was under the awning of the Salt & Cinder, mending a frayed towline with a sailmaker's palm and a bone needle, when they saw her cross the street.

They knew her before they knew her — by the walk, if nothing else. Sefa Okonkwo had always walked like the ground owed her money. Quick heel-strike, shoulders square, head moving in that constant quarter-turn sweep she'd learned doing glacier travel, reading crevasses into every sidewalk. Twelve years, and Ines's hands went still on the rope before their brain caught up.

She stopped at the far corner under a lamppost, consulting something on a slate tablet, and the rain ran off the brim of a company-issue hardshell with the Ironwake trident stitched at the shoulder. Slate blue. New. Expensive.

Ines considered, seriously and for perhaps three full seconds, simply getting up and walking into the sea.

* * *

It wasn't as though they hadn't imagined it. You don't share four years and two hundred rope-lengths with a person and then not rehearse the meeting you're dreading. Ines had run it a hundred times in the dark hours: on a trail somewhere neutral, maybe, or at some conference neither of them should have been attending. In the rehearsal, Ines was calm. Witty, even. They said things like *I hope the mountains are treating you well* and *no hard feelings*, and Sefa said whatever the script required, and then they parted ways forever, dignified, two adults who understood that some ropes are cut for good reasons.

The rehearsal had never once taken place in the rain outside a chowder house, with Ines holding a half-mended towline and fish grease on their fingers.

Sefa looked up from her tablet. Her eyes crossed the street, snagged on the awning, and stopped.

For a moment neither of them moved. The rain filled the silence with its small industrious noise. A gull screamed somewhere with the enthusiasm of a creature that has never once been blamed for anything.

"You've got to be kidding me," Sefa said.

"Hello to you too," Ines said.

* * *

They ended up inside the Salt & Cinder because Sefa said "we're not doing this in the street" and Ines, whose options were that or physical flight, agreed. The chowder house was nearly empty at this hour — old Marta behind the counter, two fishermen playing cards, a dog asleep in a puddle of its own contentment. They took the corner table, the one farthest from the door, which Ines noted was also the table where you'd sit if you didn't want to be overheard, and wondered which of them had chosen it.

Sefa ordered coffee she didn't drink. Ines kept the towline on the table between them and kept working at it, because their hands needed a job or they'd do something embarrassing, like shake.

"Ironwake," Ines said. "That's a long way from the survey corps."

"People go where the work is."

"The work being what, exactly? Because I watched your fleet come up the channel yesterday. Eleven vessels. For a river survey concession." Ines pulled a stitch tight. "That's not salvage tonnage. That's an occupation."

"It's a job, Ines."

"It's a job with a director who knows my name." They said it lightly, watching her face. "Corven Ashe. Very charming man. Offered me a contract with my full expedition history attached, which is interesting, since my history is supposed to be sealed. Blacklist files don't circulate. Somebody gave him the key."

Something flickered across Sefa's face — quick, quickly buried. Guilt had a shape; Ines knew it the way you know weather coming over a ridge. But she said only, "Maybe you made more enemies than you think."

"I made exactly one enemy that mattered." The needle paused. "And she's sitting across from me drinking coffee she hasn't touched."

"I'm not drinking it because your chowder house burns the beans."

"Marta's beans are fine."

"Ines." Sefa set the cup down with a click that was much louder than a cup should be. "What do you want from me?"

And there it was, the real question, surfacing through twelve years of silt. Ines put down the needle after all.

"What happened on the Kestrel Col descent, Sefa. That's what I want. Not the report version. The version where you tell me why."

* * *

Here is what Ines remembered, and had remembered every night for twelve years, in the rotation the mind keeps for its worst inventory:

The Kestrel Col route, late season, the ice bridge failing a week ahead of forecast. Ines had called the turn-back — called it clean, called it early, called it in front of witnesses. And then the client, a mapping consortium with deadlines and money, had leaned on the second guide. Had leaned on Sefa. And Sefa had stood at the col with the wind pulling her voice apart and told the clients the route was still viable, that the senior guide was being conservative, that anyone who wanted the summit could proceed at their own discretion.

Three of them proceeded. One came back.

The inquiry had been a slaughterhouse. Ines took the fall for inadequate route assessment — the senior guide's sin, always — but the thing that had actually ended everything, the thing that lived under the blacklist like a stone under snow, was the moment in the hearing room when they'd asked Sefa directly: *Did Halloran order the turn-back?*

And Sefa Okonkwo, who had heard the order, who had repeated it back word for word the way she repeated everything back, the most reliable echo Ines had ever tied into — Sefa had looked at the panel and said, "I don't recall."

*I don't recall.* Three words. Ines had built a cathedral of ruin on those three words.

"You know what I remember," Ines said now, quietly, because the chowder house was empty but not empty enough. "I remember you standing at the col. I remember you saying *copy, turning back* — your voice, your exact voice, the way you always confirmed orders like scripture. And then I remember the hearing. *I don't recall.*"

Sefa's jaw worked. Outside, the rain thickened against the windows, and the dog by the stove sighed like all this human business was beneath both of them.

"You think I don't know how it looked," she said.

"I don't think about how it looked. I think about Renn Voss going into the bergschrund, and I think about his daughter sending me letters for six years asking why nobody stopped him, and I think about the fact that the answer is *you* were supposed to help stop him, and you didn't, and then you couldn't even say out loud that I'd tried."

"I was twenty-six years old—"

"So was I!"

The card players looked up. Marta looked up. Ines became aware that they were both half-standing, which was absurd, two grown guides squaring off over a chowder table, and sat back down with the deliberate care of someone setting down something fragile.

"I was twenty-six," Ines said, lower now. "And I lost everything. Certification, reputation, the work — the only thing I was ever any good at. And you walked away with a reference letter and a career. So yes, Sefa. I want to know why. Was it the money? Were you scared? Did you just decide I was expendable?" Their voice cracked on the last one, traitorously, and they hated it. "Which one was it."

Sefa looked at her hands. They were good hands, steady hands, hands Ines had trusted with their life more times than either of them could count, and they were shaking, just slightly, around the coffee cup.

"It was none of them," she said. "It was worse than all of them."

* * *

She told it in pieces, the way people tell things they've never said aloud — out of order, with gaps, circling back.

The consortium hadn't just leaned on her. They'd owned a piece of her. Her expedition fees had been routed through a subsidiary — standard practice, she'd thought, everyone did it, the paperwork was dense and she was twenty-six and flattered that the big firm wanted her signed on. When the accident happened, the subsidiary's lawyers arrived within a day. Before the bodies were cold, before the rescue teams had even finished sweeping the route. They explained, very kindly, very clearly, what would happen to her contracts, her insurance, her future employability, if her testimony contradicted the firm's position. And the firm's position was that the senior guide, Halloran, had misjudged the route, and that no one else bore responsibility.

"They weren't protecting themselves," Sefa said. "That's what I finally figured out, years later. They were building something. A precedent. A file. Halloran refuses to falsify, Halloran gets destroyed, and every guide in the corps learns what happens to the ones who won't play. You weren't the target, Ines. You were the lesson."

"That doesn't explain *I don't recall.*"

"No." She breathed out. "No, that one's mine. That one I own. They told me if I confirmed your order, I'd be admitting the route was called, which meant the clients who went up went against guidance, which meant the consortium's liability opened up like a crevasse. Confirming you would have ended me. Denying you—" She spread her hands. "Denying you cost me almost nothing. That's the arithmetic they handed me. That's the arithmetic I did." Her voice went rough. "I did it in about four seconds, Ines. Four seconds, and I spent twelve years wishing I'd been slower."

The rain filled the pause. Ines found that their eyes were hot and refused, absolutely refused, to do anything about it.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because the same firm—" Sefa stopped. Started again. "Because the consortium that owned my contracts got bought, restructured, renamed. Twice. It's called Ironwake now. And three days ago I was assigned to a salvage operation in Rivergate, and I read the personnel notes on local contacts, and your name was in the file, flagged, with a note about managing the blacklist situation." She met Ines's eyes. "They sent me, Ines. Ashe asked for me specifically. He wants someone who can predict you."

The towline lay forgotten on the table. Ines looked at it, at the neat stitches, at the frayed place they'd been mending, and felt the old familiar vertigo — the sense of standing on a cornice, the snow beneath them hollowed out by something they couldn't see.

"Ashe offered me a contract," Ines said slowly. "This morning. Generous. He knew my whole file. He said — I remember the exact words — he said, *everyone has a price, and yours is cheaper than most.*"

"He'll raise it. He'll keep raising it until you take it, or until you're a problem. And Ines—" Sefa leaned forward, and twelve years fell away for a moment, and she was the partner again, the echo, the other half of the rope, saying the thing she'd been sent here not to say. "Whatever you pulled out of that river last week — whatever's in that chest — it matters to them. More than the salvage rights. More than the wreck. People who build lessons out of guides don't send eleven ships for cargo."

"How do you know what's in the chest?"

"I don't. But I've seen the manifest requests coming down from the directors' deck, and I've seen what gets redacted, and I've been redacted before." She stood, dropping bills on the table that would cover ten coffees. At the door she paused, rain-wet light behind her, and didn't quite look back.

"For what it's worth — the summit route was dead. You were right. You were right, and I knew you were right, and I have known it every day since." The bell over the door chimed her out. "Watch your six, Halloran. Ashe doesn't hire people to predict you unless he plans to use the prediction."

* * *

Ines sat for a long time after she was gone. Marta refilled the untouched coffee without being asked and retreated with the tact of her profession. The dog relocated to Ines's feet, offering the only uncomplicated affection available in Rivergate.

Twelve years of a story, revised in forty minutes. It didn't fix anything — Renn Voss was still gone, the hearing was still a slaughterhouse, and *I don't recall* still echoed in Ines's worst hours. But the shape of it had changed. The betrayal had a new architecture: less a knife in the back than a machine that had ground them both, one to powder and one to a lesson. It was somehow worse and somehow easier to carry, the way a heavy pack is easier once you know where the weight actually sits.

And underneath it, colder: *they sent her.* Corven Ashe had reached into Ines's past and pulled out the single person capable of reopening the wound, and aimed her like a tool. Which meant the chest in Tam's boathouse — the dead nation's seal, the manifest, the coordinates Ines hadn't yet admitted knowing — was worth that much to him.

Ines left coins for Marta, shouldered the towline, and stepped out into the rain. Halfway down the block they stopped, turned, and walked the other direction — away from the dock, away from Ironwake's slate-blue fleet — toward the boathouse district, and Tam.

If the company wanted the chest badly enough to dig up ghosts, Ines intended to find out what was in it first. And for that, they were going to need a map of nobody's river.
