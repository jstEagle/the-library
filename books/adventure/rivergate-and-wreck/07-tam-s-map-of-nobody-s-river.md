## 7. Tam's Map of Nobody's River

The rain came back that evening, the way it always did in Rivergate—without apology and without warning, like a neighbor who'd decided your porch was hers now. Ines sat in the back room of the Netmender with a mug of barley tea gone cold an hour ago, turning the Vellmark seal over in their mind the way you'd turn a river stone: thumb over the smooth places, pausing at every crack.

The chest sat on the table between them, open, its false bottom lifted. The manifest lay where they'd left it that morning, though Ines had read it so many times the creases were softening. *Passenger vessel* Meridian Star. *Lost with all hands, gorge reach, forty-one years past.* And beneath that, in a clerk's cramped hand: *one (1) sealed testimony case, priority of conveyance.*

Priority of conveyance. As if testimony were cargo. As if a person's last words could be stacked between crates of salted herring.

"You've been staring at that paper like it owes you money," Tam said from the doorway. She had a towel around her neck and her hair plastered flat, and she was eating something out of a paper cone—fried something, by the smell. She never seemed to eat at proper times or in proper quantities; she ate the way gulls did, opportunistically and without shame.

"It might," Ines said. "Owe me money. Everything in this chest does, one way or another."

Tam dropped onto the bench across from them, set her food down, and looked at the manifest upside down for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, in a voice Ines hadn't heard from her before:

"That's my grandmother's handwriting."

Ines looked up.

"The clerk's hand. The annotations." Tam tapped the margin, where someone had corrected a date and added a note about tide tables in faded brown ink. "She wrote like that. Slanted forward, like she was always in a hurry to get to the end of the sentence." She swallowed. "Her name was Bette Okaru. Before Rivergate, I mean. Before it was anything."

Ines set the manifest down very carefully, as if it might startle.

"Okaru," they repeated. "That's not a Rivergate name."

"No," Tam said. "It's a Vellmark one."

* * *

The rain filled the silence for a while. It drummed on the Netmender's tin roof the way it had drummed on every roof Ines had ever sheltered under, and there was a strange comfort in that—in weather being the same everywhere, indifferent and reliable.

"Tell me," Ines said finally.

Tam pushed her fried whatever-it-was aside, which Ines had never once seen her do, and folded her hands on the table like a woman about to give evidence.

"She didn't talk about it much. Grandmother. You know how it is—the ones who come through the worst things talk about them least, and the ones who watched from shore never stop talking." Tam's mouth twisted. "But she kept things. A tin box under her bed, and when I was small and couldn't sleep she'd let me look at what was in it. A spoon with a lion stamped on the handle. A bus ticket for a city that isn't on any map anymore. And charts. Hand-drawn charts of the gorge, done in pencil on the backs of flour sacks, because that's what she had."

"Flour sacks," Ines said.

"She worked in a bakery, after. First job she got off the boats." Tam shrugged. "You draw what you have paper for. Anyway—I thought they were just old lady scribbles when I was six. When I got older I asked her about them and she went quiet in that way she had, like a door closing, and I didn't ask again. Then she died, and I kept the box, because you keep things, and I never opened it again until—" She stopped. Looked at the chest. "Until you hauled it up out of the water with that seal on it, and I went home and dug the box out from under my own bed, and I sat on the floor all night looking at charts I'd been sleeping above for twenty years."

Ines's heart was doing something complicated. They took a slow breath and made it behave.

"Can I see them?"

"They're outside," Tam said. "Under my coat. Because if I'm wrong about this, I'd rather get rained on than be embarrassed indoors."

"You're not wrong," Ines said. "I saw the handwriting too. I just didn't know whose it was."

* * *

The charts, when Tam unrolled them across the table and weighted the corners with mugs and the salt cellar and Ines's cold tea, were nothing short of astonishing.

They were drawn in pencil, yes, and on flour-sack cloth, yes—but the hand was sure. Every line confident. The gorge unwound across three separate sheets, north reach to south, and threaded through it were channels Ines had never seen marked on any survey, any chart, any permit map issued by the River Authority. Side channels. Backwaters. A slough behind the falls labeled in careful capitals: *DEEP WATER, HOLDS EVEN AT LOW TIDE.* Another, near the narrows: *SHALLOWS LIE. THE RIVER PRETENDS HERE.*

"The river pretends here," Ines read aloud, and felt something loosen in their chest that had been tight for years.

"Grandmother's humor," Tam said. "Such as it was." She leaned over the sheets, and her finger traced a channel with the unconscious familiarity of someone who'd absorbed these lines before she could read print. "She drew these from memory, Ines. From *memory*. She was nineteen when Vellmark fell. Nineteen, and she knew the gorge well enough to redraw it from inside her own head, forty years later, on flour sacks."

"Why would a refugee girl know the gorge channels better than the River Authority does?"

"Because," Tam said, "the last convoy went through the gorge. The one that sank. Her family was supposed to be on it." She said it flatly, the way people say the worst facts, having sanded all the edges off long ago so the fact wouldn't cut anyone standing nearby. "They put her on a fishing boat instead—a smaller boat, a smuggler's boat, going a different route—because she was sick, and her mother thought the big ship would be gentler on her stomach. The convoy took the main channel. Everyone knows the convoy went down in a storm. That's the story. Storm, overload, bad luck."

"But your grandmother's charts show a main channel that doesn't match the official wreck site."

Tam looked up. Rain-light from the window caught her face, and for once there was no joke queued up behind her eyes.

"The official wreck site," she said slowly, "is in thirty feet of water on a gravel shelf. I've fished over it my whole life. There's no wreck there, Ines. There's never been a wreck there. Everybody in Rivergate knows it and nobody says it, because saying it means asking why the story's wrong, and asking why the story's wrong means asking who told the story, and—" she spread her hands— "nobody in this town keeps its head down harder than us. We learned it from the refugees. Keep your head down, thank the river, don't ask. My grandmother used to say the river forgets nothing but forgives everything, and the men with ledgers are the exact opposite."

Ines stared at the charts. At the channel marked behind the falls. *Deep water, holds even at low tide.*

The coordinates on the manifest matched none of the official surveys. Ines had noticed that days ago and filed it away as clerical error, or salvage-company sloppiness, or simple mystery. But against Bette Okaru's flour-sack gorge, the numbers landed squarely in the deep water behind the falls. The place the river pretended didn't exist.

"She knew," Ines said. "She always knew where the Meridian Star actually went down."

"She knew, and she drew it, and she hid the drawings under her bed for fifty years because she was afraid of exactly the right thing." Tam sat back. "And then you fish a Vellmark chest out of the river, and I'm sitting here thinking maybe the head-down thing only works until the river decides it's done waiting."

* * *

They worked through the evening. Ines fetched their own survey charts from the boat and laid them alongside, and the two sets of lines argued with each other in the lamplight—official ink versus refugee pencil—and the pencil won every argument. Where the Authority showed a shoal, Bette Okaru showed a channel. Where the Authority showed open water, she showed a ledge. Ines had run the gorge perhaps two hundred times in their working life, and every discrepancy on those flour sacks explained some confusion they'd chalked up to their own error, some current that had behaved strangely, some echo sounding that had come back wrong.

"She wasn't guessing," Ines murmured. "These aren't memories of a passenger looking out a window. These are pilot's charts. Soundings. Tide corrections." They looked up. "Tam. Your grandmother ran boats through this gorge."

"I know," Tam said simply. "After she got here. Nobody would hire a Vellmark pilot with papers, so she ran the night routes. Smuggling, mostly—at first just goods, then people, other refugees coming down out of the hills. She never called it smuggling. She called it *ferrying*. She said a ferry takes people where they need to go and the law is just another kind of weather." Tam smiled, small and crooked. "I used to think that was grandmother nonsense. Now I think she was describing your entire profession."

"My entire former profession," Ines corrected, and heard how bitter it came out, and wished it hadn't.

Tam studied them across the table. She had a talent, Ines was learning, for letting silences do work that questions would only ruin.

"You want to tell me about it?" she said at last. "The blacklisting? You don't have to. But you've been carrying it around like a stone in your pocket since the day I met you, and stones get heavy, and I've got strong arms."

Ines laughed despite themselves—a short, surprised sound. "That's the worst offer of comfort I've ever received."

"I'm a dockhand. We're not trained in comfort. We're trained in hauling."

So Ines told her. Not all of it—not yet—but the shape of it. An expedition four years ago. A client company with a safety report that needed amending before submission. A guide who read the amended draft and refused to sign it, because the amendment turned eleven documented equipment failures into three, and three fit within regulatory tolerance and eleven did not. A guide who was thanked politely, escorted off the charter, and never hired anywhere again, because the client company's word traveled faster than any reference, and their word was: *difficult, unreliable, unsafe.*

"Eleven failures," Tam repeated. "What happened on that expedition?"

"Nothing," Ines said. "That was the whole point. Nothing happened, because I pulled the team off the route on day six. If we'd continued with the gear they wanted logged as sound, something would have happened. But there's no certificate for disasters prevented. There's only paperwork, and mine said the wrong number."

"And the company?"

"A conglomerate. Marine survey, salvage, heavy transport. Half a dozen names over the years—it keeps reorganizing, buying and selling itself like a rumor." Ines rubbed their eyes. "Why does it matter?"

"Because," Tam said, and her voice had gone careful, "my grandmother had a phrase for companies like that. Companies that buy and sell themselves until nobody can say who they were last year. She called them *paper knives*. She said they cut things and leave no handle to hold." She hesitated. "The company that owned the Meridian Star's route—the one whose seal is on your chest—it got bought, piece by piece, over about ten years. Folded into bigger and bigger concerns. I looked it up once, out of schoolgirl spite. You want to know what it's called now?"

The rain hammered the roof. Somewhere down the street a dog complained about it. Ines found they already knew, the way you sometimes know a thing a half-second before it's said, the way you feel the eddy before the rock shows.

"Ironwake," they said.

"Ironwake," Tam agreed softly.

* * *

Later—much later, when the lamps had burned low and the charts were rolled and tied and sitting on the table between them like a treaty—Tam stood to go, then didn't. She stood there with her hand on the doorframe, rain-coat on, and looked back at Ines with an expression that was trying very hard to be casual and failing completely.

"My grandmother spent fifty years keeping her head down," she said, "and drawing maps anyway. Just in case. Just in case somebody someday needed to know where the river really goes." She jerked her chin at the rolled flour-sack charts. "Those are yours now. Or ours. Whichever. I'm not good at—"

She stopped. Started again.

"What I mean is, you shouldn't go into that gorge alone. Whatever's down there behind the falls, whoever wants it badly enough to blacklist a guide over it—you'll need somebody who knows which lies the river tells. So." She shrugged, aggressively. "Consider yourself ferried."

Ines looked at her—at this dockhand who'd appeared in chapter one of their Rivergate life as a pair of strong arms and a running commentary, and who had just handed over her grandmother's fifty-year secret without flinching—and felt the strange, unfamiliar ache of something that wasn't grief.

"Tam," they said. "Thank you."

"Yeah, well." She yanked the door open onto the rain. "Don't make it weird."

The door banged shut. Ines sat alone with the charts and the chest and the cold tea, and after a while they unrolled Bette Okaru's southern sheet one more time and traced the deep channel behind the falls with one finger, following it down and down to where the manifest's coordinates waited like a held breath.

Then they noticed it—a notation in the corner they'd taken for a watermark, faint pencil letters pressed so hard into the flour sack they'd survived fifty years of folding:

*BETTE OKARU, PILOT, LICENSE NO. 4411-V. ISSUED BY THE CONCESSION AUTHORITY OF [illegible].*

And beneath that, smaller, angrier, the pencil nearly tearing the cloth:

*License revoked by order of Corven Ashe, Regional Director, Ironwake Salvage & Concession Ltd.*

Ines sat very still.

The rain kept on. Outside, far down toward the docks, a fleet's running lights had appeared through the weather—white and green and gold, sliding upriver in formation, arriving early, arriving hungry.

Ironwake was in the gorge.
