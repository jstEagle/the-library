## 8. Permits and Paper Knives

The rain had been falling on Rivergate for three days straight, the kind of patient, businesslike rain that seemed to have filed paperwork for its stay. Ines Halloran sat in the back room of the harbor office with a mug of tea gone cold and a stack of documents that smelled of mildew and old lies.

Tam had gone to fetch Oskar. Ines had told her not to—told her it was a two-person errand at most, that Oskar would come if asked nicely and bring his own biscuits—but Tam had looked at Ines with that particular expression she wore when she'd already decided something, the expression of a woman who had grown up on stories about a drowned country and had no patience left for people who moved slowly.

"You'll want witnesses," she'd said, pulling her oilskin hood up. "When you find out what you find out. That's what my gran always said. Never open a door like that alone."

"What kind of door is it?"

"The kind you can't close again," Tam said cheerfully, and went out into the rain.

So now Ines waited, alone with the door.

* * *

It had started, as these things did, with boredom.

Two days after Sefa Okonkwo had stood in the rain outside the Saltline Tavern and said things that Ines still hadn't finished digesting, Ines had done what they always did when their chest got too tight: work. The survey contract was finished, its charts delivered, its modest payment collected. So Ines had volunteered to help Tam's uncle reorganize the harbor office archives—a job nobody wanted, a job of water-stained ledgers and permit filings going back forty years, a job so small and dull that no one would ever look twice at a blacklisted guide doing it.

That was the point. Ines had spent six years learning to be furniture.

But furniture, it turned out, could read.

The archive room was a lean-to attached to the harbor office, built by someone who had believed Rivergate's history deserved better than it got and then died before proving it. Boxes were stacked in towers. Rats had opinions about several of them. Ines had spent a day and a half just establishing an order to the chaos, and somewhere in the tedium, the tightness in their chest had loosened, the way it always did when their hands had a task and their mind had a puzzle.

Then, on the second afternoon, they found the file.

It was misfiled—of course it was, everything here was misfiled—which was the only reason it survived. Someone decades ago had shoved it into a box labeled *DREDGE PERMITS 1961–63* where no one dredging anything would ever look. The folder itself was thin, gray, unremarkable, stamped with the letterhead of a company called **Meridian Channel Works**.

Ines knew that name. Everyone who'd ever worked the northern rivers knew that name. Meridian had been one of the great salvage-and-dredging conglomerates of the old era, back before the consolidations, back before the mergers that had swallowed half the industry into fewer and fewer hands. Meridian had gone under—or rather, had been bought, absorbed, renamed, its logo redrawn, its debts laundered through three holding companies until it emerged gleaming and new.

Ines had seen the modern version of that logo every day for the past week, painted on the hulls of the ships sitting in the river mouth.

Ironwake Salvage.

They should have put the folder down then. Some part of them—the part that had kept them alive for six years, the part that knew how to be small—screamed to put it down, close the box, go home, drink the cold tea, be furniture.

Ines opened it instead.

* * *

The first document was routine: an incident report, dated eleven years ago, concerning an expedition on the Kestrel Range. Ines's expedition. Ines read their own name in a stranger's flat bureaucratic prose and felt the old shame rise like floodwater—*guide error, failure to file accurate terrain assessment, contributing factor in loss of equipment and serious injury to expedition member*—and then, halfway down the page, they stopped.

Because the report wasn't the report.

Ines remembered the incident report. They had written it. They had written it honestly, which had been the whole problem: the route had been sound, the weather data had been wrong because the client's own meteorological contractor had supplied fabricated forecasts to save money, and Ines had said so, in writing, with citations. Their report had named names. It had been, in retrospect, a professional suicide note written in very tidy handwriting.

But this document in the gray folder was not Ines's report. It was a *summary* of Ines's report—one page, heavily edited, every mention of the client's falsified weather data struck through or softened into passive-voice fog. And stapled behind it was a memo, internal, from Meridian Channel Works' regional office:

*RE: HALLORAN INCIDENT — RECOMMENDED DISPOSITION*

*Subject guide has declined repeated requests to amend findings. Subject's original report, if entered into the regulatory record, exposes [REDACTED] liability exposure exceeding projected insurance recovery. Recommend certification revocation proceeding be initiated citing guide error per attached summary. Note: subject has no patron firm and limited means; contest unlikely.*

*Contest unlikely.*

Ines sat very still in the mildew-smelling room while the rain ticked against the window, and read the sentence again, and again, letting it do its slow work.

*Contest unlikely.* Four words. Somebody had sat at a desk—probably a nice desk, probably near a window—and looked at Ines Halloran's life the way a surveyor looks at a bend in the river, assessing flow, assessing obstruction, and concluded that removing them would be cheap and easy and that nobody downstream would notice.

Six years. Six years of sleeping in rooms above taverns, of taking jobs under the table, of flinching whenever anyone in a company jacket said their name. Six years of believing, in the secret hours, that maybe they really had made the mistake—that maybe the shame was earned, that maybe if they'd been better, sharper, more careful, the blacklist wouldn't have happened. Ines had carried that doubt like a stone in a pack, checking its weight every morning, never once thinking to ask who had packed it there.

And now here was the answer, in triplicate, with a signature.

The signature belonged to a name Ines didn't recognize—*A. Voss, Regional Compliance*—but the letterhead beneath it, the corporate lineage printed in fine type at the bottom of the memo, traced a line as clean as any chart: Meridian Channel Works, successor entity, merger of record, and there, third from the bottom of the acquisition chain, a name that had been splashed across the news for a month when the deal closed.

Ironwake.

Not a rival. Not a competitor who'd profited from Ines's fall. The same company. The same hands, wearing different gloves, reaching down through eleven years to make sure that a guide who once refused to lie stayed ruined forever.

Why? The question arrived cold and practical, pushing the fury aside the way a pilot reads water around a rock. A decade-old incident report shouldn't matter to a corporation this size. Unless the incident mattered. Unless the *client* mattered. Ines flipped to the last page of the folder, the redacted one, and stared at the black bar covering the client's name, and thought about the shape of the redaction—too short for a person, too long for initials—and about what kind of client Meridian would burn a guide's career to protect.

And underneath that thought, quieter but growing louder, another one: *if they did this to me, what else did they do? What else is in these boxes?*

Ines pulled the next box off the shelf. Then the next. The rain kept its patient time outside, and Ines worked the way they used to work on a wall face—fast, precise, no wasted motion—pulling Meridian files, Ironwake files, cross-referencing dates against the Vellmark convoy disaster that Tam spoke of like a wound in the family. There was nothing about Vellmark in the harbor office archives. Of course there wasn't. But there were shipping manifests, channel-clearance permits, dredging contracts along the gorge—Meridian's fingerprints all over the upper river, decades deep, including a cluster of permits dated the very season the last Vellmark convoy went missing.

Ines wrote it all down. Names, dates, file numbers, in a waterproof notebook, in the shorthand they'd used for route notes, compact enough to fit in a pocket, durable enough to survive a dunking. Old habits. The hands remembered how to prepare for hard country even when the mind had forgotten why.

* * *

The door banged open at dusk, letting in rain and Tam and, behind her, Oskar Vell—no, not Vell, though sometimes he signed his name as if he wished it—Oskar Brandt, disgraced river pilot, carrying a paper bag of meat pies and looking like a man who had been dragged out of a warm tavern on false pretenses, which he had been.

"Right," Oskar said, setting the bag down. "Tam says you've found something. She also says I'm to stand witness, which sounds legal, and I'd like the record to show I objected."

"Noted," Tam said. "Objection overruled. Eat your pie."

Ines laid the folder on the table between them, and the memo on top of it, and turned it so they could read. Then Ines did something they hadn't planned: they told the whole story aloud. The Kestrel expedition. The falsified forecasts. The honest report. The hearing they hadn't been allowed to attend. The blacklist that fell like a portcullis. Eleven years compressed into ten minutes, spoken in the flat voice of a briefing, because that was the only voice that could carry it without breaking.

Oskar read the memo twice. His jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice had a strange gentleness in it, the gentleness of a man recognizing machinery he'd been inside of himself.

"Compliance dispositions," he said. "I've seen these. On the convoy contracts, before—" He stopped. Started again. "This is how they do it. This is exactly how they do it. You don't sink a ship, mostly. Mostly you sink a *person*. You write them out of the trade, and the trade forgets them, and the ships keep running, and nobody asks why the honest ones are all gone." He tapped the memo. "*Contest unlikely.* That's not a prediction, Halloran. That's a budget line. They priced you."

"They priced me," Ines agreed. And here was the strange thing, the thing they would turn over for nights afterward: the words didn't hurt the way they'd expected. Somewhere between reading the memo and saying it aloud, the shame had drained out and something else had filled the space—something with a much lower freezing point.

Cold fury doesn't shout. Ines had met it before, on mountains, in clients who lied about their experience, in the moment when you realize the danger isn't the weather but the person who sold you the weather. Cold fury makes lists. Cold fury checks its knots. Cold fury sits in a mildew-smelling room and writes in waterproof shorthand and thinks, with terrible calm: *they spent eleven years making sure I couldn't reach them. They never once considered that I might get closer anyway.*

"Say the rest," Tam said quietly. She'd gone still in the way she did when her grandmother's stories were moving under her skin. "There's a rest. I can see it."

Ines turned the notebook around. The gorge permits. The dates. The Meridian contracts overlapping the season the Vellmark convoy vanished.

"I don't know yet what it means," Ines said. "But Meridian was working the gorge the exact season the convoy went down. And the company that buried my career for telling the truth once is the same company that became Ironwake. And Ironwake is sitting in our river right now with a salvage fleet and a director who knows my name, hunting a wreck that holds testimony somebody sealed inside a hull." They paused. "I don't believe in coincidences that well-funded."

Oskar had stopped eating. He was staring at the permit dates with an expression Ines recognized from mirrors: the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"There's a cordon," he said at last. "Ashe's boats run the narrows in pairs, dawn and dusk sweeps. Permits expire after the sixth tide. We're two tides in." He looked up. "You're not actually thinking—"

"I'm thinking," Ines said, "that they took six years from me with a pen. A pen, Oskar. No boat, no rope, no weather. Just paper." They closed the folder, gently, the way you close a coffin. "Paper got me into this. Paper's going to help get me answers. But the answers themselves are at the bottom of the gorge, behind the falls, and we have four tides left to reach them."

Tam grinned. It was not entirely a comforting grin. "My gran had a saying about companies like that."

"Let me guess," Oskar said. "'Never trust a man whose name changes more often than his shirt.'"

"'Rivers remember,'" Tam said. "Companies change their names. Rivers don't. And neither do I." She stood, pulling her hood up again, already halfway to whatever she'd decided next. "Fourth tide turns at moonrise tomorrow. If we're going to slip a cordon, someone has to know which channels the sweep boats can't enter at low water."

"And you know those channels," Ines said.

"I grew up on stories about them," Tam said. "Tomorrow you grow up on charts."

Oskar sighed the sigh of a man boarding a ship he'd sworn off, and reached for the last pie. "For the record," he said, "I objected."

"Noted," said Ines, and for the first time in eleven years, it sounded like the beginning of something instead of the end.
