## 9. First Tide: Down the Gorge

The cordon began at the old ferry landing, where Ironwake had strung a chain of orange buoys across the river like a child's necklace and posted two men in matching jackets to watch it do nothing. Ines watched them from the reed bank above the landing for the better part of an hour, chewing a strip of dried fish and learning their rhythm.

They had a rhythm. That was the trouble with corporate hires — they came from cities, from harbors with schedules, and they brought their schedules with them like luggage they couldn't put down. Every forty minutes one of them walked the length of the dock, checked the buoy line, and went back to his thermos. The other never moved at all except to shift his weight from one foot to the other, which meant he was either very disciplined or very bored, and Ines was betting on bored.

"You're going to wear a groove in that hillside," Tam said, arriving at Ines's elbow with the soundless ease of someone raised on a working dock. She carried a canvas roll under one arm and a thermos of her own, which she offered without ceremony. It was chicory coffee, bitter enough to strip paint, and Ines drank it anyway.

"Forty minutes," Ines said. "One walks, one stands."

"The standing one's named Pell. He bought my grandmother's net weights off me last spring and haggled me down to nothing, so I'd personally enjoy watching him get wet." Tam settled onto her heels beside Ines, squinting at the buoys. "Oskar says we go tonight. Says the tide turns at moonrise and the current through the narrows will be running our way for about three hours before it turns mean."

"And you trust him?"

Tam considered this with the seriousness she gave all questions, which was more than most people gave their taxes. "I trust that he wants back on that water worse than he wants anything else in the world. People who want something that badly are usually honest about the parts that matter."

It was as good a philosophy as any. Better than most, honestly. Ines had spent years trusting credentials, certifications, the tidy stamps of institutions, and every one of those institutions had turned out to be paper over a hole. Oskar Venn had a revoked pilot's license, a drinking reputation he only half deserved, and hands that had steered the gorge for twenty years before anyone thought to take the steering away from him. Ines would take the hands.

* * *

They launched from the old quarry slip, half a mile downstream of the cordon, where the bank dropped away into water dark as tea. Oskar's boat was called the *Maren*, after nobody he would name, and she was ugly in the way of all truly reliable things — patched hull, engine held together with wire and stubbornness, a wheel worn smooth in the places a pilot's palms rest.

Ines stood at the bow with the chart Tam had unrolled across their knees that afternoon, the one drawn in her grandmother's careful hand, ink faded to the color of weak tea. *Nobody's River*, Tam called it. The official charts showed the gorge as a single bold channel with depth soundings taken decades ago by men in government boats who had no reason to look closely. Tam's grandmother's chart showed the truth: the side channels, the drowned timber cribs, the ledge behind the falls where the water folded over itself like a page being turned.

"There," Ines said, tapping a small annotation in a language neither of them read fluently but both understood anyway. A mark like a bird's footprint. "She wrote something there."

"She wrote everything," Tam said. "Half of it's warnings. The other half's recipes. You learn to tell them apart by how angry the handwriting gets."

Oskar took them out slow, engine idling low, until the quarry slip was a gray smudge behind them and the walls of the gorge rose up on either side like the covers of a closing book. Then he opened her up.

Ines had forgotten what it felt like — not the memory of it, which lived in the body somewhere below the ribs, but the feeling itself, arriving whole and immediate. The bow lifted. Spray came over the gunwale cold as a slap. The gorge narrowed and the river, compressed, gathered itself and ran, and the *Maren* ran with it, and Oskar stood at the wheel with his chin tucked and his shoulders loose and steered the way other men breathed.

"Timber crib, port side, three meters down!" Ines shouted over the engine, reading the water's surface the way Tam's grandmother had taught herself to — the smooth green tongue where current slid over submerged wood, the seam of white foam stitching along its edge.

"I see her!" Oskar called back, and there was something in his voice Ines recognized because it had been missing from their own for six years. Joy. Plain, undiluted joy, the kind that has no room in it for shame.

They threaded the crib. They shot the first riffle with the hull singing. And when the gorge opened into the long pool below the second bend, where the cliffs leaned apart just enough to show a stripe of evening sky, Ines laughed out loud — actually laughed, a rusty sound that startled a heron off the bank — and heard Tam laughing too, and understood that the wilderness had done what wilderness does. It had remembered them. Not their files, not their blacklisting, not the signatures on documents filed by companies that became other companies. It remembered their weight and their nerve and their hands, and it let them in.

* * *

The cordon found them anyway. Of course it did.

Ironwake's patrol boat came around the third bend with its running lights already on and a searchlight sweeping the water ahead of it, and Oskar said a word that Tam pretended not to hear and killed the engine.

"Under the willows," he murmured, and swung the dead *Maren* toward the bank with the last of her momentum, sliding her into the black lattice of branches hanging over a backwater no wider than the boat itself. Ines crouched low. Tam pulled the canvas tarp over the gear. And then they waited, three people holding their breath in a pocket of shadow while the searchlight crawled past close enough to count the midges dancing in it.

Through the gap in the willows, Ines watched the patrol boat pass. Two crew. A mounted sonar unit, new, expensive, its transducer trailing a wake of bubbles. On the stern, stenciled in Ironwake's clean corporate blue: *IW-7*.

"They're mapping," Tam whispered. "Not patrolling. Mapping. That's survey equipment."

"They're looking for the wreck," Ines said quietly. "Ashe doesn't want to wait for permits and procedure. He wants coordinates."

The patrol boat's light swept on, indifferent, and dwindled around the bend. For a long moment none of them moved. Then Oskar exhaled through his nose, restarted the engine at a whisper, and nosed the *Maren* back into the current.

"That man Ashe," he said, without turning around. "He came to my hearing, you know. Four years ago. Sat in the back row taking notes while they stripped my ticket." He adjusted the wheel a degree, gentle, the way you correct a horse. "I always wondered why a salvage director cared about a river pilot drunk on his own boat in his own slip. Now I know. He was collecting names. Everyone who knew the gorge. Everyone he might need, or need gone."

The words settled over the boat like river mist. Ines looked at Tam, whose jaw had gone tight, and then at the chart spread under the bow light, at the little bird's-footprint mark drawing closer with every minute.

"Then let's make sure he's wasted his money on us," Ines said.

* * *

The falls announced themselves long before they appeared — a change in the air first, cool and mineral, then a vibration in the hull like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. Oskar brought the *Maren* in against the eastern wall, where Tam's grandmother's chart showed a shelf of rock just under the surface, and killed the engine for good.

They went the rest of the way by paddle, silent, the only sounds the drip of the blades and the growing breath of the falls. And then the channel bent, and there it was.

Even in the failing light, even knowing what they knew, Ines felt their throat tighten. The falls didn't drop so much as pour — a broad silver curtain thirty feet wide falling over a lip of black stone into a churning pool, and behind the curtain, visible only as a darker darkness, the ledge. The shelf where forty years ago a passenger vessel had come to rest, wedged in the fold of the gorge like a letter slipped between the pages of a book.

Nobody had ever come for it. That was the whole horror of it, Ines thought. The river had kept it, and the maps had erased the country it belonged to, and the company that should have answered for it had grown fat and renamed itself twice, and the dead had simply waited. Patient as water.

"We anchor here," Oskar said softly. "Swim the ledge at slack tide, first light. I'll hold the boat." He paused. "If I'm still allowed to hold a boat."

"You're holding the boat," Tam said firmly, in the tone of a woman settling a matter permanently. "Anyone who says otherwise answers to me and my grandmother's ghost, and she was meaner than me."

They made camp in a hollow above the pool, a fire small enough to hide in cupped stone, dried fish and chicory and hard bread passed around without ceremony. Above them the stars came out in the narrow slot of sky, unfamiliar constellations this far into the gorge, and the falls sang their one note over and over, patient, enormous.

Ines lay awake after the others slept, listening to it, and let themselves feel the thing they'd been refusing to feel since the chest came up dripping out of the shallows. Fear. Not of the dive — the dive was work, and work they could do. Fear of what was waiting inside the hull. Forty years of silence sealed in a case built to outlast water. Whatever testimony Mara Vell had left behind, it had cost someone everything to record it, and it would cost something to carry it out. Everything always did.

But underneath the fear, steady as the current, was the other thing. The feeling from the afternoon. The wilderness remembering.

*First tide,* Ines thought, watching the moon clear the eastern rim of the gorge and begin its slow pull on the river. *Five to go.*

And then, from the direction of the narrows — faint, but unmistakable, threading through the voice of the falls — came the sound of an engine. More than one. Running without lights, moving fast, coming upstream.

Oskar was awake instantly, hand on Ines's shoulder in the dark. "You hear that?"

"I hear it." Ines sat up, heart hammering, and reached for the chart. "Somebody else knows the way in."
