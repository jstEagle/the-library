## 10. The Hull Beneath the Falls

The gorge did not want them there, and it made no effort to pretend otherwise.

Ines had known rivers that welcomed boats—broad, lazy brown rivers where the water seemed to say *come along, then, no hurry*. The Vell was not that kind of river. The Vell was a rumor with teeth. It ran green-black through the gorge, deep as a held breath, and every hundred meters it reminded them whose territory this was: a deadfall swept clean of bark, a boulder the size of a chapel, a seam of white water that appeared from nowhere and vanished just as fast, as if the river were practicing.

"Cheerful place," Oskar said from the stern, hands easy on the tiller of the borrowed skiff. He was a heavy, gray-bearded man who moved like a man who had once been fast and had decided, on balance, to stop. "Reminds me of my first wife."

"You were married?" Tam asked, delighted, from the bow where she crouched with Tam's grandmother's charts spread across her knees, weighted at the corners with river stones.

"Once. Briefly. She also tried to drown me."

"That was probably a misunderstanding," Tam said.

"It was entirely fair," Oskar said. "I'd like to think we've both grown."

Ines let them talk. That was part of the job now—letting people talk, keeping the air in the boat warm, because cold air made cold hands and cold hands made mistakes. It was a skill they'd learned the hard way, on expeditions where silence had curdled into something worse than silence. Sefa had been the one who taught them that, actually. The thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts about Sefa always did lately, and Ines set it down on the gunwale and let the river take it.

They were running the last quiet stretch above the falls, engine at a whisper, hugging the northern wall where the chart showed a backwater behind a shelf of collapsed rock. The gorge narrowed here until the walls leaned in like conspirators. Ferns hung from cracks in the basalt, dripping. Somewhere above, unseen, the falls made a sound like continuous distant applause—the sound of something enormous approving of nothing.

"Here," Tam said, tapping the chart. Her grandmother's handwriting looped across the paper in faded brown ink: *behind the white curtain, the old road sleeps.* Below it, almost as an afterthought: *do not wake it angry.*

"Your grandmother was a poet," Oskar said.

"Your grandmother was a refugee who mapped a river nobody official would admit existed," Tam corrected. "Poetry was just what happened when she got tired."

Ines cut the engine. The silence that followed was enormous, layered—water over stone over whatever the gorge kept at the bottom of itself. They drifted. Ines shipped the oars and let the skiff swing slowly on the backwater's lazy current, and for a moment nobody spoke, because the moment had the quality of a church about it, and even Oskar seemed to feel it.

"Sound check," Ines said finally, because reverence was a luxury you could afford for exactly four seconds on a river. "Everyone hear the falls?"

They did. Everyone always did. The falls were the whole argument of the place.

"Behind that curtain of water, if my grandmother's chart is honest, there's a hollow," Tam said. "A pocket in the rock where the current curls back on itself. She called it the still room. Fisherman's legend—said you could drift a boat in there and hear your own heartbeat."

"And behind the still room?" Ines asked.

Tam's finger traced the chart to a small, careful symbol. Not an X. X's were for treasure, and her grandmother had apparently disapproved of treasure on principle. It was a rectangle, drawn with a draftsman's precision, and beside it in that same brown ink: *the sleeper.*

Ines had spent two nights with that word, turning it over. *The sleeper.* Not the wreck. Not the dead. The sleeper—something resting, something that would wake. Grandmothers who mapped forbidden rivers did not choose words carelessly.

"Okay," Ines said. "We go in quiet. Oskar holds the skiff at the curtain's edge. Tam, you're on the line. I go first. Nobody argues with the person who's done this for a living, and yes, I know my living is currently a matter of legal debate."

"Disgraced guides dive free of charge," Oskar said solemnly. "It's in the bylaws."

* * *

Getting behind the falls was the kind of thing Ines had once been paid very well to do and was now doing for the love of the work, which was either a demotion or a promotion depending on how honest you were willing to be about your life.

The trick was the seam. Falls never fell as one clean sheet; there was always a place where the rock stepped out and split the water, a seam of thinner flow where the curtain went translucent. Ines found it by sound first—a thinner hiss, a higher note—then by feel, running a hand along wet basalt until the roar opened into a channel of cold spray and sliding stone. Behind them, the world went green and dim. Ahead, the water fell in a moving wall, and through it, light came down in broken coins.

And behind the curtain, exactly as promised: the still room.

It was a pocket maybe ten meters across, walled in smooth dark stone, floored with gravel the river had spent centuries polishing. The water here barely moved. It was cold in the way deep water is cold, and clear in the way water only gets when it has been left utterly alone—clear enough that Ines could see down, and down, and further down, into a green twilight where shapes waited.

Ines surfaced slowly, treading water, and just breathed for a moment.

"Found it," they called through the curtain, voice oddly flattened by the falls' white noise. "Tam! It's real. The still room is real."

They heard Tam whoop, a bright sound nearly swallowed by the water.

Ines dove.

The first thing the light found was the bow.

It came up out of the green like a memory surfacing—riveted steel, a stem post curved in that old-fashioned swan-neck style that had gone out of fashion before Ines was born, furred with a pale sediment that softened every edge. A river steamer. A small one, a packet boat, the kind that had once carried mail and passengers and hope up rivers that ate bigger boats for breakfast. The nameplate was crusted over. Ines wiped it with a thumb, gently, the way you'd wipe a sleeping person's brow.

*V.S. ALDERMIST.*

Ines's heart did something complicated.

*Aldermist.* The chest's manifest had named her. *Passenger vessel Aldermist, lost with all hands, gorge of the Vell, spring crossing.* Forty years the river had kept her, and here she was—not scattered, not broken open, not the torn ruin the official record described. Wedged. Tucked. Laid into a ledge of rock behind the falls like something being put away carefully by enormous hands.

Ines rose, filled their lungs, and went down again along the hull.

She lay heeled maybe thirty degrees on the ledge, her port side buried in gravel, her starboard side rising clean out of the sediment. The current that thundered past the falls never touched her. In here the water was a held breath, and the river, for once in its violent life, had been gentle. The paint—there *was* paint, under the silt, a deep bottle-green Ines could see where a flake had lifted—still clung in places. A porthole stared at Ines from two meters away, its brass rim gone the color of an old coin, its glass intact, and behind the glass: darkness, and the faint suggestion of a curtain, still hanging.

A curtain. Forty years underwater, and someone had hung a curtain.

Ines had recovered bodies before. It was the part of guiding nobody put in the brochures—the seasons when the mountain gave back what it had borrowed, and you went up with a stretcher and came down slower than you went up, and you learned to be gentle with people who could no longer feel the cold. Ines had thought they knew every shape that grief took underwater.

This was a new shape. This was not a disaster. This was a *bedroom.*

Ines hung in the green water beside the hull and felt their professional detachment—hard-won, carefully maintained, the whole architecture of their survival—go soft as wet paper.

They checked the manifest in their memory. *Aldermist, forty-one souls.* Forty-one people had boarded a river steamer on a spring crossing and simply ceased to exist, in the official sense. No wreck site surveyed. No salvage claimed. No graves. A nation erased from the maps, and its dead erased with it, and the river asked no questions and told no stories—except, apparently, to grandmothers who drew charts in brown ink.

Ines surfaced, spat spray, and called through the curtain: "Bring the light down. And Tam—bring the manifest copy. I need to see it down here."

* * *

They rigged the lamp on a rock shelf in the still room and took turns diving with the slate. The water was cold enough that twenty minutes was the honest limit, and Ines enforced it with the flat authority of someone who had once pulled a hypothermic client off a glacier by the collar.

Oskar went down second. He came up slower than he went down, and when he broke the surface he didn't say anything for a long moment, just hung there in the lamp light with water running off his beard.

"Well," he said finally. "I've seen wrecks."

"I know," Ines said.

"No. You've seen wrecks. I've *seen* wrecks—thirty years of them, most of them ugly, most of them torn open like something had been in a hurry." He wiped his face. "This one isn't torn. She's *parked.* Somebody put her to bed."

"The river did," Tam said. They were crouched on the shelf, holding the manifest copy inside a plastic sleeve, reading it by lamplight while the falls applauded eternally ten meters away. "My grandmother wrote that the Vell doesn't destroy what it respects. She wrote—" Tam stopped, and Ines watched the young woman's throat work. "She wrote, *the river keeps what it is sorry for.*"

Nobody had anything to say to that for a while.

Then they worked, because reverence without method is just standing around getting cold. Ines ran the manifest against the wreck, item by item, and the wreck answered every question correctly, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't.

The Aldermist's registered length matched the hull. The swan-neck stem matched the 30-year-class packet boats the manifest described. The porthole spacing matched. And on the starboard side, just aft of midships, where the manifest noted the vessel carried a sealed strongroom for documents—*one (1) document case, diplomatic, non-negotiable*—there was a hatch. Small, round, dogged down with a wheel of tarnished brass, unopened, undamaged, sitting in the green dark like a held secret.

"There," Ines said, and touched the wheel through the water, not gripping it, just resting two fingers on the cold brass. "That's the strongroom. That's where the case is."

"That's where *she* is," Tam said. "The testimony. Mara Vell's recording, if it survived."

"Sealed case, diplomatic grade, in a strongroom behind forty years of silt in cold still water." Oskar's voice had gone quiet and technical, the voice of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Cold, dark, no oxygen. Paper survives. Tape survives, maybe. It's possible. It's—" he exhaled through his nose, "—it's the kind of possible that costs."

"Everything costs," Ines said. "The question is the exchange rate."

They made three more dives before the cold won. Ines mapped the hull on the slate in careful strokes: the ledge, the angle, the strongroom hatch, the gravel-choked port side, and—on the final dive, in the last minutes before their hands went stupid with cold—something Ines hadn't gone looking for and couldn't unsee.

Through the second porthole. The one amidships, where the manifest said the passenger cabin was.

The lamp light found a table. A small table, bolted to the deck, tilted with the hull's lean. On it, a cup. A tin cup, upright, or nearly upright, held by forty years of fine silt that had settled over everything like snow that never melted. Beside the cup, a book, closed. And on the bench beside the table, the shape of a person—no, Ines corrected themselves, the *absence* of a person, a silhouette in the sediment where someone had sat down and the river had drawn around them and kept the shape of them, gently, the way you keep the shape of someone in a bed after they've gone.

The river had kept what it was sorry for.

Ines hung there until the cold made the decision, and rose, and broke the surface, and found Tam and Oskar looking at them with the particular expression of people who have watched someone come back from a church.

"We found her," Ines said. Their voice came out rougher than intended. "She's whole. She's—" Ines stopped, started again. "Somebody set a table in there. Forty years ago. Somebody was having tea."

Tam looked down at the manifest, at the line they all knew was coming, and read it aloud, softly, the way you read names at a memorial: *"Souls aboard: forty-one. Crew of six, passengers thirty-five. Two children."*

The falls went on applauding. They always would. That was the thing about the Vell—it never stopped being loud, and it never once said anything.

"We get them out," Ines said. "The testimony. Whatever's in that strongroom, it gets into the light. Not for us. For them." Ines looked at the green curtain of falling water, at the hull sleeping behind it. "Forty years is long enough for a nation's dead to go unnamed."

Oskar nodded slowly. Tam was writing something in the margin of her grandmother's chart—Ines caught it upside down: *the sleeper, found, still sleeping, we will be gentle.*

It was Oskar who said it, on the run back downriver in the failing light, engine at a murmur, the gorge walls sliding past like the shoulders of something turning over in its sleep:

"You know Ironwake's got sonar on every boat they own. Fleet like that doesn't come to a gorge for gravel. Sooner or later their beams are going to sweep this ledge."

Ines watched the last light die on the water and felt the clock that had been ticking since the chest came up out of the dredge tick louder.

"Then we'd better hope," Ines said, "that their sonar operator is having a very distracted week."

They had no way of knowing, then, that she was.
