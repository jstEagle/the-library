## 11. Second Tide: The Fleet's Sonar

The *Ironwake Meridian* was the kind of ship that smelled like money trying to be a boat. Sefa Okonkwo had worked on enough salvage vessels to know the difference. Real working boats smelled of diesel, rust, and the particular sourness of men who hadn't washed their socks since the last port. The *Meridian* smelled of lemon polish and fresh coffee that cost more per cup than Tam's entire dockhand wage, poured from a machine in the galley that had more buttons than the sonar console.

Sefa had counted the buttons twice out of spite.

"Okonkwo." Director Corven Ashe appeared at her elbow the way he always did, soundless, as if he'd been assembled from the ship's own quiet. He was holding two cups of that coffee. He offered one. "You've been staring at the bathymetric feed for three hours."

"I'm staring because it's my job," Sefa said, taking the coffee anyway. Free was free. "Some of us work for a living."

Ashe smiled his thin smile, the one that suggested he found her insolence charming in the way one finds weather charming when one is indoors. "Any anomalies?"

The question landed in Sefa's chest like a dropped anchor chain. Because yes. Yes, there was an anomaly. There had been an anomaly for six hours now, sitting on the edge of her screen like a secret she hadn't decided whether to keep.

* * *

It had started innocently, the way all catastrophes do — with paperwork.

The second tide had come up gray and patient, and Ironwake's survey grid had crawled another kilometer down the gorge. Sefa ran the sonar interpretation station, which meant she took the raw returns the towfish dragged up from the dark and turned them into pictures the directors could nod at. It was meticulous work. It was also, she'd realized somewhere around hour two, the perfect place to hide.

Because there it was. Grid sector fourteen. A return that didn't make sense.

A hull shape, wedged into a ledge behind the falls curtain — which shouldn't have been possible, because nothing should have survived the falls curtain, and yet there it was, a passenger vessel's lines clear as a signature: the long salon, the tiered aft decks, the distinctive canted funnel fairings that no shipwright had built in forty years.

Vellmark lines. She knew them the way you know a face from an old photograph you'd convinced yourself you'd forgotten.

Sefa had sat very still for a long time after that, listening to the sonar ping its patient rhythm — *ping, wait, answer; ping, wait, answer* — like some enormous patient heart asking the river questions and getting them back.

She should have flagged it. That was the job. Flag the anomaly, log the coordinates, push it up the chain to Ashe, who would smile his thin smile and say something about efficiency and then do whatever he'd been planning to do all along.

Her hand had actually been on the flag control. Her finger had actually hovered.

And then she'd thought: *what exactly am I flagging this for?*

* * *

Here was the thing about Sefa Okonkwo that nobody aboard the *Meridian* understood, least of all Sefa Okonkwo: she had come to Rivergate telling herself a story, and the story had holes in it big enough to pilot a tug through.

The story went like this: I took Ironwake's contract because the pay is good and the work is honest and the past is the past. The past is a country I emigrated from. I don't hold citizenship there anymore.

But stories need maintenance, and hers had been springing leaks ever since she'd stood in the rain outside the Saltcrust Tavern and watched Ines Halloran walk away from her with that look on their face — not anger, worse than anger, the flat patience of someone who has already grieved you and gotten on with it.

Ines had looked at her the way you look at a wreck you once loved diving. Beautiful, ruined, better left where it lies.

"You're doing the face," said a voice beside her.

Sefa startled so badly she nearly wore the coffee. Junior Analyst Priya Raghavan had materialized at the second console, a wiry young woman with a permanent expression of someone perpetually about to ask a question she'd been told not to ask.

"What face?"

"The face you make when the data says something inconvenient." Priya nodded at the screen. "You've got your jaw doing the thing."

"My jaw isn't doing anything."

"It's doing the thing. Sector fourteen?" Priya leaned in, and Sefa's stomach dropped through the deck plates. "I saw it too. Weird return. Too regular to be scree, too wrong to be modern construction. I ran it against the historical registry database."

"And?"

"And the registry database for that region has a gap in it." Priya pulled a face. "Forty years of gap. Everything before a certain date just — redacted. Not deleted, redacted. Somebody went through and blacked out an entire nation's shipping history."

Sefa kept her voice level with an effort that felt physical, like holding a door shut against wind. "Companies redact old records all the time. Liability."

"Sure." Priya chewed her lip. "Except the redaction order predates Ironwake's founding by nine years. Which means somebody at the predecessor company did it, and Ironwake inherited it, and nobody's ever asked why." She glanced sideways. "You're going to report the anomaly, right? Protocol says—"

"Protocol says a lot of things," Sefa said. "Go run the interference calibration. The northern grid's drifting again."

Priya hesitated — Sefa could feel the question burning off her like heat haze — but she was young, and young people on corporate ships learn quickly which questions keep you fed. She went.

Sefa sat alone with the screen and the pinging heart of the sonar and the shape of a dead ship wearing a dead country's name, and she asked herself the question she'd been avoiding for six hours:

*What's in it?*

* * *

Ships like that don't sink empty. That was the first thought, and it was a professional thought, and she clung to it like a handhold. Passenger vessel, gorge route, forty years gone. Whatever sank it, it would have been carrying cargo manifests, mail, personal effects. Salvage value, maybe. Historical value, certainly.

And then the second thought arrived, and it was not professional at all.

*A testimony case.*

She'd seen the manifest page. Everyone in Rivergate with eyes had heard about the chest the river coughed up — small towns are telegraphs with taverns attached. A sealed case, listed in a false bottom, bound for the Vellmark consulate. Sealed testimony. Those were the words everyone used, though nobody seemed to know what the testimony was *of*.

Sefa knew how to read a company, though. She'd been reading Ironwake like a chart for eight months now, and here's what she'd plotted: Ironwake didn't send a fleet this size for salvage rights to a passenger wreck. Three vessels, a dive complement, a legal attaché who never took off his sunglasses even indoors — that wasn't a salvage operation. That was a retrieval operation. You don't spend that kind of money on brass fittings and china dolls.

You spend it on paper. On recordings. On the kind of sealed case that, opened in front of the wrong audience, could unmake a company.

And if the case implicated Ironwake's founders—

The thought completed itself without her permission, cold and clean as the river water in the sonar's returns.

Then the founders had sent a fleet to bury it. And they'd hired her — her specifically, poached her from a competitor with a number that had made her laugh out loud — because she was good, and because she was discreet, and possibly, though she hated the possibility, because she was Ines Halloran's former partner and might know how Ines thought. Might predict where Ines would go. Might be, in the polite vocabulary of corporate contracts, an asset.

She set down the coffee. It had gone cold anyway, and it tasted like lemon polish and complicity.

* * *

At 1600 by the ship's clock, Director Ashe held his daily briefing in the wardroom, which was less a briefing than a liturgy. Twelve officers and analysts sat around a polished table while Ashe stood at the head and summarized progress in the tone of a man reading a grocery list written by God.

"Grid twelve through fifteen complete. No significant contacts. The seasonal window remains favorable. Questions?"

Silence, except for the hum of the ventilation and the distant, ceaseless mutter of the gorge.

Sefa raised her hand. It moved on its own; she watched it go up the way you watch yourself step off a ledge.

"Director. On the survey methodology. If we're running parallel tracklines at current spacing, we're leaving gaps in the acoustic coverage near the cliff walls. Reflections off the rock mask weak returns. A wreck tucked into a ledge or overhang could sit inside those gaps indefinitely."

Ashe regarded her. Somewhere in that regard, Sefa felt, was a man recalculating something. "Your recommendation?"

"Tighten the spacing along the walls. Slower passes. It costs us time, but—" she shrugged, aiming for bored technician and hoping she'd hit somewhere in the neighborhood, "—if we're going to do it, we may as well do it properly."

"We are paying for proper," Ashe agreed mildly. "Adjust the plan, Ms. Okonkwo. I want full wall coverage by the fourth tide."

"Aye, Director."

He dismissed them, and the room emptied, and Sefa sat there feeling like a woman who had just committed treason in both directions at once. Because here's what she'd actually done: she'd told the company how to find the wreck. Full wall coverage by the fourth tide would sweep every hiding place in the gorge, including the ledge behind the falls, including whatever Ines was doing down there right now in some stolen boat with some dockhand and a disgraced pilot.

She'd handed them the map.

Unless.

Unless she'd also bought time. Tightened spacing meant slower passes meant days, and days were what Ines needed, and Sefa told herself that was why she'd done it — sabotage dressed as diligence — and then she made herself stop lying, because lying to herself was the one skill she'd sworn off after the expedition, after the mountain, after Ines's face in the snow saying *you signed it, Sefa, you signed the report.*

She hadn't signed it. That was the truth she'd carried for years like a stone in her boot. She'd refused to sign the falsified incident report, same as Ines. But she'd also taken the settlement, quietly, and let Ines take the blacklist alone, and told herself survival was neutral. Survival wasn't neutral. Survival was a choice, and choices had weight, and hers had been pressing on her chest for eleven years.

Now the sonar had found a drowned ship carrying a drowned country's testimony, and the company that owned her contract had probably built the drowning, and Sefa Okonkwo was sitting in their wardroom drinking their expensive coffee and adjusting their survey plans.

*Ping,* said the sonar, faintly, through the bulkhead. *Wait. Answer.*

* * *

She found Priya in the analyst berth that evening, cross-checking calibration logs. Sefa closed the door, which nobody did, and Priya looked up with the expression of someone whose forbidden question was finally being answered.

"Tell me about the redaction," Sefa said. "Everything you found. And then tell me who else has seen sector fourteen."

"Just me and you. I haven't logged it." Priya swallowed. "Is that bad? Should I log it?"

"No." Sefa sat down across from her. Outside the porthole, the gorge walls slid past, black against a bruised sky, and the river ran beneath them with all its secrets zipped up tight. "Listen to me, Priya. In about two days, things on this ship are going to get strange. When they do, I want you to remember tonight, and I want you to remember that you chose not to log it. Can you do that?"

"That's cryptic and slightly terrifying," Priya said. "Yes. Obviously yes."

"Good." Sefa almost smiled. Almost. "One more thing. The galley coffee machine — does it record usage?"

"...What?"

"Never mind. Forget I asked. Go to sleep."

She went up on deck afterward, alone, because the *Meridian*'s decks at night were the only place aboard that belonged to nobody. The second tide was turning. She could feel it in the water's mood, the way the current below began to argue with itself. Six tides until the gorge flooded the site for the season. Five, now, and counting.

Somewhere downriver, in the dark, Ines Halloran was doing something reckless and principled and probably illegal, and Sefa realized — standing at the rail with the spray coming up cold and the company's lights burning behind her like a hotel that had wandered out to sea — that she was rooting for them. Had been rooting since the rain outside the Saltcrust. Maybe since the mountain. Maybe since always.

The question wasn't whether her conscience had stirred. Consciences were cheap; hers had been stirring for a decade like porridge left on a low flame.

The question was what she was willing to lose this time. Last time she'd chosen herself and called it survival, and it had cost her the only partnership that had ever felt like family. This time the ledger was bigger. A dead nation. A sealed voice. A crew of misfits in a stolen boat, and one stubborn guide who'd chosen the truth over their own name while Sefa had chosen a paycheck over a friend.

Below her, the sonar pinged its patient question into the deep, and the deep, for once, answered honestly: *here. Here. Here.*

Sefa pushed off the rail and headed below, moving fast now, mind already drafting plans the way she used to draft route cards — contingencies, fallbacks, escape margins. If she was going to betray Ironwake, she'd do it properly. Full coverage. Every contingency.

Starting with finding out exactly what the dive schedule said, and exactly where Ashe kept his charts.

She passed the legal attaché on the companionway stairs. He was indoors, at night, and he was still wearing the sunglasses.

That settled it.
