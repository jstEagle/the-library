## 12. Third Tide: The Drowned Corridor

The third tide came in gray and mean, the way Ines had known it would from the moment they'd seen the clouds stacking up over the western rim of the gorge like plates on a shelf nobody had bothered to batten down.

They made camp in the lee of a collapsed watchtower — Vellmark work, Tam said, her grandmother's people had manned these posts when the river was a border instead of a graveyard — and Oskar built a fire small enough to hide under a tin sheet, because a man who'd spent eleven years piloting contraband runs knew exactly how far light carried on water.

"Rain by midnight," he said, squinting at nothing in particular. "Hard rain. The corridor will silt up after that."

"Then we go before it," Ines said.

Tam looked up from the chart she was copying onto waterproof paper, her pencil pausing mid-stroke. "You're going *tonight*? Ines, you haven't even slept since—"

"I slept on the boat."

"You slept sitting up with your eyes open for forty minutes while muttering about line tension," Tam said. "That's not sleeping, that's haunting your own body."

Oskar snorted into his tea. Ines chose to ignore them both, which was becoming a full-time occupation.

The truth was simpler and uglier than either of them wanted to hear: they had three tides left where they'd planned for four, because Ironwake's cordon boats had cost them half a day slipping past, and because Sefa's stolen dive plans — folded now into oilcloth in Ines's pack like a letter from an old wound — showed that Ironwake intended to run their own divers into the corridor on the fifth tide. Whatever was inside the hull of the *Vespera of Vellmark*, they would have hours, not days, to reach it first.

And whatever else Ines Halloran was — disgraced, exhausted, haunted by their own body or otherwise — they were still, when it came down to it, the best free diver on this stretch of river, and everyone in the camp knew it, including Ines, which was the most annoying part.

* * *

The corridor entrance was a black seam in the cliff face below the falls, half-drowned even at low tide, breathing. That was the word Tam used, and Ines hated how accurate it was: the surge pushed in and pulled out on a slow rhythm, six feet of rise and fall, so that the seam gulped air and exhaled spray like something enormous sleeping with its mouth open.

"You'll have maybe ninety seconds of slack between breaths," Oskar said. He'd rigged the safety line himself, checking every knot twice, then a third time, which Ines had learned was just how Oskar did everything — as if penance could be paid in over-tightened bowlines. "After that it starts pulling. If you're still inside when it pulls—"

"I won't be."

"That's what the last guy said." Oskar's face didn't change. "I know because I was the last guy."

Ines paused with the mask halfway on. "You dove this?"

"Ran cargo through the gorge, back when. Not this wreck." He looked at the seam, and something moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice. "Other things. Places the company wanted emptied before anyone official came looking. You get good at holding your breath and bad at sleeping. You'd know."

Ines did know. They let that sit between them for a moment, two people who understood each other in the way that only damaged divers can — completely, and without comfort.

"Ninety seconds," Ines said finally. "Keep the line honest."

"Always."

Tam squeezed Ines's arm once, hard, which was her version of a speech. "Come back," she said. "That's the whole assignment. Everything else is optional."

* * *

The water took them the way it always did — cold first, a fist closing around the chest, then the strange quiet, the world going blue-black and soft-edged. Ines counted strokes. Down through the seam, left along the ledge where the current eddied backward, following the guideline Oskar had anchored to a rusted stanchion that must have been part of the old towpath, forty years drowned and still doing its job. There was something in that, Ines thought. Things held fast long enough outlast the reasons they were built.

The corridor opened after thirty meters into a flooded gallery, and Ines's light found the hull.

Even expecting it — even having seen the sonar ghost of it, even knowing every rivet by heart from Sefa's stolen plans — the reality of the *Vespera* stopped Ines's heart for one full beat, which underwater is a dangerous luxury. She lay on her side against the ledge behind the falls, wedged like a coin in a crack, her white paint gone to bone-gray, her name still legible along the stern in flaking gilt letters. Forty years of dark water, and the river had kept her. Had *preserved* her. Fish drifted through her broken windows like tenants who'd never signed a lease.

Ines swam the length of her slowly, touching nothing. Through a gash in the starboard side, the interior showed: a passenger corridor, luggage racks, a child's shoe standing upright on the silt as if set there yesterday. The dead were elsewhere — deeper compartments, sealed doors, the places the plans marked in red ink. Ines had no wish to see them. Some rooms you enter; some rooms you pass with your light lowered, the way you'd lower your voice at a funeral.

The testimony case, according to the manifest, was stored in the purser's strongroom, aft, behind the second bulkhead. Sefa's plans showed the route: through the gash, down the corridor, portside hatch, ten meters of flooded passage barely wide enough for shoulders.

Ines checked the line. Checked the time. Ninety seconds had become sixty-five, and the gallery was beginning to breathe — Ines felt it in their ears, that faint pressure shift, the sleeper stirring.

Through the gash. Into the ship.

Inside, the water was clearer, colder, untouched by current, and the silence had a texture to it, a felt weight, like wool pressed against the ears. Ines's light swept the corridor: brass fittings green with age, a life vest hanging off its hook as if someone meant to come back for it. Portside hatch. Open, thank God — the plans said it jammed sometimes, but forty years of rust had apparently finished the job of opening it.

Ten meters of passage. Ines went in on a full breath, arms tight, kicking easy, conserving. The passage narrowed. Turned. And there — bolted to the wall of the strongroom, its seal unbroken, a steel case the size of a bread loaf with the Vellmark crest stamped deep in its lid: the crowned bridge, the nine stars.

Mara Vell's voice, if Tam's grandmother's stories were true, locked in steel for forty years.

Ines got fingers under the case's mounting bracket. It didn't move. Of course it didn't move. Nothing on this ship had moved in forty years except the water. Ines braced boots against the bulkhead and pulled, and the bracket groaned — a sound that traveled through the hull like a shout through a house — and gave.

The case came free. And the ship, offended, answered.

It started as a vibration in Ines's teeth, then a rumble, then the particular deep *crack* of old metal failing that every diver knows and none of them ever gets used to. A bulkhead somewhere above the passage shifted. Silt exploded downward through the corridor mouth Ines had come through, a brown curtain, and the deck plates underfoot bucked like a spooked horse.

Ines swam. No more conservation, no more elegance — raw sprint, case hugged to chest, through the turn, through the narrowing —

— and the narrowing wasn't narrowing anymore. It was *closed*. The ceiling section that had hung by forty years of stubbornness had chosen this exact moment to lie down across the passage, a slab of hull plating wedged at an angle, leaving a gap maybe fourteen inches high near the floor. Maybe. In the roiling silt, Ines couldn't even be sure of that.

Air. Ines needed air. The case could wait — no. No, it couldn't, because dropping it meant losing it in the collapse, and everything — Tam's grandmother, Oskar's confession, Sefa's defection, Ines's own ruined name — was welded to this steel box. Ines hooked an elbow through the bracket strap and clawed upward along the gap, found it real, found it *just* wide enough, and went through it the way a drowning person goes through anything: gracelessly, painfully, skin scraping metal, lungs screaming their final argument.

Then the gallery. Then the seam. Then the pull — the tide exhaling, dragging outward — and Ines rode it, kicked with it, let the river do half the work for once, and broke the surface into rain and torchlight and Tam's voice cracking on the second syllable of their name.

* * *

They got the case ashore. They got Ines ashore. Ines lay on the rocks coughing up half the gorge while Tam wrapped a blanket around them and Oskar held the safety line and stared at the seam with an expression Ines was too oxygen-starved to read.

"We should open it," Tam said, hands hovering over the seal. "Shouldn't we? See if it's—"

"Not here." Ines's voice came out scraped. "Not in the rain. And not until—" A cough took the rest of the sentence.

Until what, Ines didn't say. Until we're sure. Until we're safe. Until it can't be taken from us again. All true. None of it spoken, because speaking it made it feel fragile.

Then Oskar said, quietly, "Where's my line?"

Ines turned. The safety line ran taut from Oskar's anchor point down into the seam — and it was moving. Not with the tide. With a rhythm. Pulling, resting, pulling. The rhythm of a person.

"He followed me." Ines sat up so fast the world tilted. "He clipped on and followed me in — why would he—"

"Because you went alone," Oskar said, already stripping his outer layer, already moving, "into a wreck, on a short tide, and he's Oskar." He said it like that explained everything, and the horrible thing was that it did. Eleven years Ines had known the man — eleven years of knots triple-checked and silences kept and a guilt he never named — and in all that time Oskar had never once let someone he loved walk into dark water without someone on the other end of a rope.

Loved. Ines filed the word away, shaking, to be examined later or never.

Oskar went in clean and fast, no wasted motion, a man diving toward something he'd been swimming toward his whole life. Tam fed the line. Rain hammered the rocks. The seam breathed, and breathed, and breathed.

Two minutes. Three. Tam's hands shook on the line and her face was a stone with eyes.

At three minutes and some seconds, the line went slack — then heavy — then it *jerked*, three sharp pulls, the emergency signal, and Tam hauled and Ines hauled and the river gave them back a shape: Oskar first, dragging, and behind him, caught under his arm, limp, the safety line tangled around one boot.

They got him onto the rocks. He wasn't breathing.

What happened next happened in the space where Ines's whole life had been training without admitting it: tilt, clear, compressions, the count, Tam's voice breaking on numbers, rain, the torch guttering, again, again, *again* —

Oskar convulsed and brought up the gorge, and lived.

He lay on his back in the rain, chest heaving, and laughed — a cracked, terrible laugh with water in it. "Slab came down on my leg," he managed. "Pinched me against the frame. Couldn't get loose. Heard you coming back through, figured—" Another cough. "Figured I'd rather be a problem than a mystery."

"You idiot," Tam said, pressing her forehead to his wet one. "You beautiful, impossible idiot."

"Mystery's worse," Oskar agreed solemnly. "Nobody solves those."

Ines knelt in the rain beside them both, the testimony case clutched against their chest like a child, and felt the cost of the night settle into their bones where it would live forever. They had the case. They had Oskar, alive, with a leg that Tam was already splinting with a boat oar and language she'd clearly learned from dockworkers. They had, Ines realized with a fresh drop of dread, about forty hours before the fourth tide, and a wreck that was now actively collapsing, and a duplicate testimony — per Mara Vell's own manifest notation — still hidden somewhere in the hull, behind passages that tonight had shown them exactly what those passages did to people who entered them.

And they had a new problem, arriving on schedule, because that was how problems worked in Rivergate: a light sweeping the river below the falls. Slow. Deliberate. An Ironwake survey skiff, running dark except for that single lamp, tracing the shoreline.

It paused across the water from their camp. Held there for a long, long moment.

Then it moved on — but slower than a skiff with nowhere to look should move, and Ines watched it go and understood that Ironwake now knew someone had been inside the wreck tonight.

The question that turned Ines's blood colder than the gorge ever could was: *how much* did they know — and who on their side of the river had told them?
