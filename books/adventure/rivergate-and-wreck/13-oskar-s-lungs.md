## 13. Oskar's Lungs

Tam had grown up on stories about the gorge. Her grandmother used to say the river down there breathed twice a day — in with the tide, out with the tide — and that anything caught between breaths belonged to the water until it was done with them.

Oskar Vey had been caught between breaths for eleven minutes when Tam finally saw his boot through the crack in the collapsed passage.

"Here!" she shouted, and her voice came back at her three times off the wet stone, thin and echoey, like the gorge was mocking her. "Ines! *Here!*"

The headlamp beam found what her hands already knew: a slab of ceiling rock the size of a kitchen table had come down across the corridor mouth, and behind it, wedged into a gap no wider than a coffin, Oskar's leg. His boot was pointing the wrong way. Not broken-wrong. Trapped-wrong. The kind of wrong that meant he couldn't pull himself free and couldn't stop trying.

"Ines!" Tam hammered on the rock with the flat of her palm. It didn't so much as hum. "He's in here!"

Boots splashing. Ines came around the fallen shelf of stone fast, lamp swinging, and dropped to their knees beside the gap without ceremony. Water ran over Tam's boots, cold as a promise. Somewhere above them, muffled by forty feet of rock, the third tide was still going out. They had maybe four hours before it came back and turned this whole corridor into a throat that swallowed.

"Oskar." Ines's voice did the thing it always did in bad moments — went flat and calm, like a table set for terrible news. "Talk to me."

A pause. Then, from inside the gap, a voice like gravel being sorry for itself: "I'm talking."

"You're breathing?"

"I'm doing a lot of things. Mostly regretting."

Tam let out a laugh that was half sob. That was Oskar all over. Even pinned under half a mountain, he'd find time for a joke, because the alternative was screaming and he'd decided somewhere along the line that screaming wasn't for him.

"Can you move your foot?" Ines asked.

"No."

"Your knee?"

There was a long silence, the kind that had an answer hiding in it. "The rock's on my thigh," Oskar said finally. "Not crushing. Pinning. I can feel my toes. I can wiggle them." A beat. "Don't ask me to prove it, it's not pretty."

Tam crouched at the gap's edge, angling her lamp past Ines's shoulder. She could see him now — or pieces of him. One shoulder. Half a face, gray with river silt, eyes blinking against the light. He looked smaller than he had two hours ago, when they'd gone into the corridor together and the ceiling had come apart with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

*My fault,* part of her thought. *I led us through the low section. My grandmother's charts.* But another part of her, the part raised on dock work and salvage law, knew better: the charts were good. The rock had been waiting longer than any chart. Rivers kept their own schedules.

"We're getting you out," Tam said. "So don't die yet. I owe you a drink from the night you taught me to tie a bowline, and I don't pay debts to ghosts."

"Ghosts drink free," Oskar said. "It's the one perk."

Ines was already working, hands moving along the slab's edge, reading it the way Tam read weather. "This isn't bearing load," they murmured. "It's bridging. Something underneath is holding it up." They reached into the gap, careful as a surgeon, feeling along the underside. "There's a rib of stone here. If we lever it right—"

"If you lever it wrong," Oskar said, "it comes down and I become a rumor."

"Then we lever it right."

"That's your whole plan? *Right?*"

"It's worked so far," Tam said. "You're alive, aren't you? Barely. Rudimentarily. But alive."

She loved this, she realized, even now — the three of them talking nonsense over a man's crushed leg while the river counted down outside. It was how dock people survived funerals. You joked because the water didn't care about your feelings and you refused to let it have the last word.

* * *

Getting the lever took twenty minutes.

They had a wrecking bar in the gear cache back at the skiff, which might as well have been on the moon. What they had instead was a length of driftwood, a boat hook, and Ines's belt knife, which Ines sacrificed without a word to wedge a fulcrum point — driving it into a seam in the stone until only the horn of the handle showed, like a flag planted in enemy territory.

"You liked that knife," Tam said.

"I liked Oskar's leg more."

"Debatable," said Oskar.

They worked in shifts of leverage and rest. Ines took the boat hook; Tam took the driftwood; on Ines's count they lifted together, and each time the slab rose an inch, maybe two, and each time it settled back with a grinding sigh, taking most of the gain with it. Progress measured in millimeters. Hope measured in the same currency.

On the fourth lift, Oskar screamed.

Not loudly. That was the worst part. It was a small sound, bitten off at the teeth, the scream of a man who'd practiced being quiet about pain for a very long time. Tam's arms shook and she held anyway, wood flexing, sweat stinging her eyes in the lamplight.

"Hold," Ines said. "Hold. Hold. *Now slide.*"

Oskar moved. Tam heard cloth dragging on stone, heard him gasping words she couldn't make out, and then suddenly the weight changed — the slab dropped, boomed, dust and spray everywhere — and there was a scrambling, splashing sound, and then Oskar Vey was lying in the shallow water on their side of the collapse, whole and filthy and shaking, staring up at the ceiling like it owed him money.

For a moment nobody spoke. The corridor dripped. Far away, the river muttered to itself.

"Well," Tam said, sitting down hard in the water, because her legs had opinions about standing. "That's the last time I trust a ceiling."

Oskar laughed. It came out cracked and wet and turned into coughing, and then he was just breathing, big ragged gulps of it, and Tam put her hand on his shoulder and left it there because some things needed weight to believe them.

His thigh was a mess — not broken, Ines determined after a grim examination, but deeply bruised, the muscle torn up underneath, the leg usable in the way a bent oar is usable. They wrapped it with a compression bandage from the med kit and fed him water and half a bar of pressed grain, and Oskar ate like a man remembering food existed.

They should have moved straight back to the skiff. The tide clock was ticking. Later, Tam would understand why they didn't. Later, she'd understand that Ines had seen something in Oskar's face in the lamplight — something that had come loose in him along with the rock — and had chosen to sit down in the flooded corridor and wait.

* * *

It was Oskar who broke first.

They were resting against the wall, letting his leg get enough feeling back to walk on, when he said, to the water rather than to either of them:

"The company that sank the convoy. Ironwake's grandfather company. Meridian Deepwater." He swallowed. "I worked for them."

Tam stopped chewing.

"I was nineteen," Oskar went on. His voice had gone flat, the way voices go when a man has rehearsed a thing so many times it's worn smooth. "Deckhand on a survey tug. Good pay, bad reputation, and I didn't care, because I was nineteen and my mother was sick and reputation doesn't buy medicine." He turned his head and looked at Ines. Really looked. "You know what Meridian did. Everyone in this trade knows, or half-knows, the way you know a house on your street is haunted. The Vellmark convoy. Seven ships carrying out the last of the archives, the families who wouldn't sign the annexation papers. Official record says storm. Bad weather, overloaded hulls, tragedy of the age."

"And?" Ines said quietly.

"And I stood watch the night it happened." Oskar's hands were flat on his thighs, pressing down, like he was keeping himself from floating away. "We were escort-adjacent. Two miles off. I saw the lights go out on the lead ship. Not flicker. *Out.* All at once, in a pattern. And then the second ship. And the sea stayed calm as a bathwater the whole time." His jaw worked. "I reported what I saw. My report went into a file. The file went into a drawer. The drawer went into a company archive, and I got promoted, which is how companies say *shut up* with money."

The water lapped. Somewhere overhead, through the stone, a bird cried out once and gave up.

"Why are you telling us this now?" Tam asked. It came out gentler than she meant it to. Or maybe exactly as gentle as she meant it to.

"Because I spent eleven minutes under a rock thinking about what I'd leave behind," Oskar said, "and it was a list of things I never said." He laughed, short and awful. "Eleven minutes. Do you know how long eleven minutes is when you're alone with yourself? It's a *decade*. I met every version of me I ever was down there. The nineteen-year-old who watched lights go out. The thirty-year-old who drank the memory quiet. The fifty-year-old—" he gestured at himself, at the bandaged leg, the silt, the ruined corridor, "—who signed on with a disgraced guide and a dock girl to dive a Vellmark wreck and told himself it was just work. Just a job. Same words, forty years apart."

"Oskar," Ines started.

"Let me finish. I'll lose my nerve, and I've got maybe one nerve left." He drew a breath that shuddered on the way in. "Meridian became Halvard Marine. Halvard Marine became Ironwake. Same ledgers, same drawers, same men wearing newer coats. Corven Ashe knows what's in that testimony case. I'd bet my other leg on it. Why else send a fleet? Why else buy permits, station sonar, hire—" his voice caught, just slightly, "—hire people like Sefa Okonkwo to do the finding? They're not salvaging a wreck. They're retrieving an embarrassment. And everyone who helps them is holding the drawer shut."

Tam looked at Ines. Ines was very still, the way they went still when something enormous was settling into place behind their eyes.

"The manifest," Ines said slowly. "The chest we pulled up. It listed the testimony case as sealed *for delivery to the Vellmark exile council*. Which means someone survived long enough to seal it. Someone who knew."

"Or several someones," Oskar said. "And Ironwake has spent forty years making sure the case stayed lost, and anyone who went looking stayed ruined." He met Ines's eyes again, and this time he didn't look away. "Ask me how I know blacklisting works. Ask me whose name was on the letter recommending yours."

The corridor went very quiet.

"What," Tam said.

Oskar dragged a hand down his face. "After the convoy, after my report vanished, they asked me to sign a supplementary statement. Recanting. Saying I'd been mistaken about the lights, the calm sea, all of it. I signed it. God forgive me, I signed it, and they filed it next to your file, Halloran, years later, when you wouldn't play ball on the Kestrel Shoals report. My name was on the corroborating affidavit. My signature helped bury you." He was crying now, Tam realized — silently, the way he did everything, tears cutting clean lines through the silt on his cheeks. "I've been rowing your boat for three weeks trying to work up the courage to say it, and it took a rock falling on me to do it. That's the size of me. That's what you've got for a pilot."

Tam waited for Ines to be angry. She braced for it — the flat voice going colder, the old wound opening in front of her.

Instead Ines reached over and gripped the back of Oskar's neck, the way you hold a man who might bolt, or drown, or both.

"You were nineteen and scared," Ines said. "I was thirty-four and not scared, and it cost me everything, and I have spent six years telling myself that made me better than the people who signed. It didn't. It just made me expensive." A rough exhale. "You watched the lights go out, Oskar. You're the only living person who did. That makes your testimony worth more than my whole ruined career, and if you tell it — on record, to the regulators, to the Vellmark descendants, to anyone with ears — then the drawer opens, and everything in it comes out. Including me."

Oskar made a sound that was half laugh, half something else entirely. "You're forgiving me by making me useful."

"I'm a guide," Ines said. "I get people where they need to go. Where you need to go is the truth. I don't know another route."

Tam found she was crying too, which annoyed her enormously, and she wiped her face with a sleeve already soaked in river water so it made no difference whatsoever. "Right," she said, when she trusted her voice. "New plan. We get out of here, we get Oskar's story written down word for word before the tide comes back and gives him an excuse to drown instead of testify, and then—" she pointed at the dark end of the corridor, toward the falls, toward the wreck wedged behind them like a secret the river had been keeping, "—we get that case before Ironwake does. Because if Ashe gets it first, it goes in a drawer. And I have heard quite enough about drawers."

"Amen," Oskar whispered.

Ines stood, checked the tide gauge on their wrist — Tam saw the number and felt her stomach drop — and shouldered the pack.

"Two hours forty," Ines said. "Oskar, can you walk?"

Oskar planted his good foot, hauled himself up the wall with Tam's shoulder under his arm, and stood. Gray-faced. Shaking. Standing.

"I can limp," he said. "Limping is walking with ambition."

* * *

They were halfway back through the side channel, Oskar hopping between footholds with Tam steadying him and Ines ranging ahead with the lamp, when Tam heard it — carried down the gorge on the outgoing wind, thin but unmistakable.

An engine. Not theirs.

Then a second engine, deeper, heavier, the diesel growl of a fleet vessel working the narrows.

Ines killed the lamp and held up a fist, and the three of them froze in the dripping dark, listening. Through the stone came the faint metallic clang of anchor chain, and beneath it, almost lost in the water-sound, a voice on a loudhailer — too far to make out words, close enough to recognize the cadence. Calm. Corporate. Certain of its welcome.

Tam leaned close to Ines's ear. "That's the *Vigil*," she breathed. "Ironwake's command barge. She doesn't leave harbor unless Ashe is aboard."

Ines was silent a moment. When they spoke, their voice was barely a thread, but Tam heard the edge in it, sharp as her grandmother's best filleting knife.

"Ashe isn't searching the gorge anymore," they said. "He's blockading it. Which means somebody told him exactly where we are."

Behind them, Oskar went very still against Tam's shoulder.

"Not me," he whispered. "On my mother's grave, Halloran. Not me."

But Tam was thinking of someone else entirely — someone clever and careful and loyal to a company that had never once been loyal back — and she felt the shape of the next six hours close around her like the tide coming in.

"Sefa," she said.
