## 14. Fourth Tide: Sefa Defects

The dive plans lived in a locked drawer in Corven Ashe's cabin, and the drawer's lock was a joke.

Sefa had known that within an hour of boarding the *Iron Meridian*. Ashe was a man who believed in locks the way some men believed in lucky socks — ritually, and without any evidence they worked. The real security was the assumption that nobody aboard would dare. And for three days, Sefa had been exactly that nobody. She'd eaten his excellent coffee, nodded at his reasonable-sounding plans, and let him call her "our resident expert" in front of the dive crew while something in her chest curdled like milk left on a warm sill.

On the fourth night — the fourth tide, if you counted the way Rivergate counted, which by now everyone aboard did, because Ashe had made the tides into a kind of liturgy — she opened the drawer.

The plans were beautiful. That was the worst part. Ironwake paid its draftsmen properly, and it showed: the gorge rendered in cross-section, the falls drawn with almost loving attention to their violence, the wreck of the passenger vessel sketched in ghostly dotted lines where the sonar had found her wedged behind the curtain of water. *Site V-7*, someone had penciled in the margin. Below it, in Ashe's own narrow hand: *Priority: testimony case. Secondary: silence.*

*Secondary: silence.* Two words doing the work of a confession.

Sefa stood there in the lamplight with the folder open on her knees and felt the last comfortable lie of her life go out of her like air from a punctured lung.

She had told herself a story for eleven years. It went like this: Ines Halloran had been reckless, and then unlucky, and then gone, and Sefa had done nothing wrong except survive and keep working. It was a good story. It had kept her fed through two bad winters and one very bad marriage. But here was the thing about stories — eventually you met someone who'd read the ending, and Corven Ashe had read the ending, and he'd hired her anyway, which meant he knew precisely what she was worth to him: a name that could stand in front of regulators and say *I was there, I saw nothing improper.*

She copied the plans. All of them, including the tide tables marked in red, including the note about the narrows — *ambush point if Halloran attempts approach* — including, at the very back, a single sheet that stopped her breath: an internal memo, dated forty-one years ago, referencing "the Vellmark convoy matter" and instructing that all records be "consolidated under corporate seal pending resolution."

Consolidated. Like flour. Like bodies.

Sefa put everything back exactly as she'd found it, right down to the angle of the folder's corner, because she had been a meticulous person even when she'd been a coward, and those two qualities had always gotten along fine.

Then she sat on the edge of Ashe's bunk in the dark and laughed once, quietly, at herself. Eleven years. Eleven years of keeping her head down and her hands clean, and it had taken a dead nation's paperwork to make her move.

Outside, the river muttered against the hull. Fourth tide. Two left after this one.

She packed light.

* * *

Getting off the *Iron Meridian* was easier than it should have been, mostly because Ironwake's night watch consisted of a young man named Pell who was afraid of the dark and compensated by being aggressively friendly to anyone who approached him. Sefa brought him tea. By the time she'd finished explaining — warmly, patiently — that the generator housing needed checking before the morning shift, Pell had walked her past the gangway himself, holding the lantern high, grateful for the company.

"You're going ashore late," he said.

"Paperwork," said Sefa. "It never sleeps, and apparently neither do I."

He laughed. She hoped, distantly, that he'd land somewhere better than this when it all came down. Companies like Ironwake didn't fall gently; they fell the way trees fell, and everyone standing underneath got to find out what they were made of.

Rivergate at night was a town pretending to sleep with one eye open. Lamplight leaked from the survey office above the chandlery, and beneath it, in the boathouse, a single candle burned behind fogged glass. Sefa stood across the lane in the rain for a long moment, watching that candle, feeling eleven years of unsaid things stack up in her throat like ballast.

Inside the boathouse, voices. A woman's — Tam, the dockhand, sharp and quick. A man's — Oskar, the pilot, low and careful. And a third voice, and Sefa's heart did something undignified at the sound of it, because eleven years is a long time but the ear is loyal.

Ines Halloran was saying, "...if the fourth tide comes up the way Tam's grandmother's chart says it does, we've got maybe ninety minutes of slack water behind the falls, not two hours, and I want the line rigged before—"

Sefa knocked.

The candle guttered. The voices stopped. She heard a chair scrape, heard Oskar murmur something that was probably *that'll be trouble*, and then the door opened six inches on a chain, and one gray eye looked out at her through the gap.

"You," said Ines.

"Me," said Sefa.

"You're supposed to be on the fleet."

"I'm supposed to be a lot of things." She held up the oilcloth-wrapped bundle. "I'm also supposed to give you this, and then probably get arrested, so can we do the reunion inside? It's raining sideways and I've been brave for about four hours now and I'm running low."

The chain rattled. The door opened.

* * *

They spread the plans on the workbench between a coil of rope and somebody's abandoned supper, and Sefa watched three faces change as they understood what they were looking at.

Tam got there first — she was quick, quicker than anyone gave her credit for. "That's the narrows." Her finger came down on the penciled note. "*Ambush point.* They knew you'd come through there. They were going to take the case off you at the narrows."

"They already did," Ines said quietly. "Fifth tide's tomorrow. We lost the case at the narrows yesterday." A muscle worked in their jaw. "Ashe has it."

"Then he doesn't need the plans anymore," Sefa said. "Which means he doesn't need me, which means I'm either useless or dangerous, and I'd rather find out which from your side of the river than his."

Oskar hadn't spoken since she'd come in. He sat with his big hands flat on the bench, looking at the drawings of the wreck the way a man looks at a grave he dug. Finally he said, "You worked for them eleven years."

"Yes."

"And you just — walked out tonight?"

"I copied the plans first. I'm not a complete amateur." She tried for lightness and missed; the room didn't laugh, and neither did she. "No. Not *just*. I've been walking out for three days. Tonight I finished."

Tam was studying her with open, unembarrassed curiosity, the way young people do when nobody's taught them yet that staring is rude. "You're the partner. From the expedition. The one who—"

"Tam," Ines said.

"No, it's all right." Sefa held up a hand. "The one who signed the report. Yes. That's me. Hello. I'm the reason your friend here spent a decade hauling dredge lines for pocket money instead of guiding expeditions."

"That's not—" Ines started.

"It is, though. Let's not spend our last two tides pretending otherwise." Sefa pulled out the back page of the memo and laid it flat. "But if we're going to be honest, let's be honest all the way down. Look at this."

They bent over it together — all four of them, shoulders nearly touching, and something about that simple geometry squeezed Sefa's chest. When had she last stood shoulder to shoulder with people over true paper? She couldn't remember. That was the whole disease, right there. She couldn't remember.

"The convoy," she said. "Forty-one years ago. Ironwake's founders didn't just salvage Vellmark's fleet after the country collapsed. They *made* it collapsible. This memo references a 'matter' that needed consolidating. Mara Vell's testimony — whatever's on that case Ashe took — names them. I'd bet my certification on it. Which is ironic," she added, "because I don't have one anymore either. We match."

"We don't match," Ines said.

"Ines—"

"We really don't." Ines straightened up and looked at her, and Sefa braced for the cold thing, the earned thing, the eleven-years-late accounting. What she got instead was exhaustion and something underneath it that might have been relief. "You signed a false report. I know why. I've always known why — they had your family's debts in one hand and your pen in the other, and I knew that even when I hated you, which I did, thoroughly, for about six years." A pause. Rain ticked on the boathouse roof. "Then I spent five more years being tired instead of angry, and somewhere in there I figured out that being angry at you was easier than being angry at them, because you'd answer letters and they wouldn't."

"You never answered my letters," Sefa said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated it.

"I read them. There's a difference." Ines rubbed their face with both hands. "You stole their dive plans and walked off their ship in the dark with no permit, no boat, and no guarantee I'd even open the door. That's not matching me. That's outrunning me." They exhaled. "So. Forgiveness. Here's what I've got: I forgive you for the report. I don't forgive myself for letting you carry it alone, and I'm not going to pretend tonight fixed anything, because it didn't, and we haven't got time to fix things anyway. We've got a wreck to rob and a company to bury. Can you live with unfinished?"

Sefa discovered she was crying, which was embarrassing, and that Tam had silently slid a rag across the bench toward her, which was somehow worse, and that Oskar had gotten up without a word to put the kettle on, which broke her entirely.

"I can live with unfinished," she managed. "I've been living with it for eleven years. It's nice to finally share it."

"Good." Ines turned back to the plans, businesslike again, ears slightly red. "Then look at the fifth-tide table. If Ashe thinks we're broken, he won't reinforce the narrows. And if he reinforced the narrows anyway, we're not going through the narrows."

Tam grinned and unrolled her grandmother's chart beside Ironwake's pristine drafting. "Nobody's river," she said.

* * *

They worked until the candle burned down to a thumb's width, and it was — Sefa kept noticing this, kept being ambushed by it — it was *fun*. Terrible, doomed, probably-illegal fun, but fun. Tam argued tide mathematics with Ines and won twice. Oskar, who confessed over tea that he'd once run cargo for the same company whose founders had drowned a nation, drew channel markers on the old chart with a steadiness that suggested penance. Sefa translated the Ironwake notation for them, and every secret she handed over felt like a splinter coming out.

Around three in the morning, Tam looked up from the charts and said, apropos of nothing, "You know you're staying, right? After. Whatever happens with the wreck. You're staying in Rivergate."

Sefa blinked. "I have a cabin on the *Meridian*."

"You have a bunk on a company that's about to become a very interesting legal question," Tam said. "Also the chandlery makes buns on Thursdays and Oskar hogs them, so we need another adult with negotiating skills."

"She's not wrong," Oskar said solemnly. "I'm a menace with the almond ones."

Sefa laughed — really laughed, the first time in longer than she wanted to count — and caught Ines watching her with an expression she couldn't parse and decided not to try. Unfinished. Fine. Some things you built the way you built a route down a river: one honest reading at a time, checking your depth, trusting the water to hold you if you respected it.

Dawn came up gray-gold over the gorge. Fifth tide in six hours. Somewhere behind the falls, a drowned ship waited with a duplicate case in her hull, and somewhere downstream, Corven Ashe was waking up believing he'd already won.

Sefa rolled the charts, tied them with string, and slung them over her shoulder.

"All right," she said. "Show me this nobody's river of yours. I want to see what we're stealing a country back from."

Ines shouldered their pack and pushed open the boathouse door onto the brightening water. "Stay close," they said. "And Sefa — watch your footing. The rocks down here are older than anybody's mistakes."

The river ran high and cold ahead of them, and the fifth tide was coming.
