## 15. The Testimony of Mara Vell

The case came open the way secrets do: reluctantly, and only after you'd already broken something getting there.

Ines sat on the floor of Tam's boathouse with the testimony case across their knees, a screwdriver, two flathead chisels, and a growing collection of bent lock picks that had once belonged to a version of themselves who thought picking locks was a useful skill and not just a way to lose good tools in river mud. The case itself was beautiful — Vellmark workmanship, all of it was, brass and dark lacquered wood, seams fitted so precisely you couldn't slide a hair between them. Forty years underwater and the seal around the lid had held like a promise.

"It's not locked," Oskar said from his chair by the stove, where he'd been sitting with a blanket around his shoulders and the particular stillness of a man who had recently nearly drowned and was renegotiating his relationship with water. "It's *sealed*. There's a difference. Locked means someone doesn't want you in. Sealed means someone wanted it kept whole until the right person came."

"And how do we know we're the right person?" Tam asked. She was cross-legged on the floor opposite Ines, her grandmother's charts spread out beside her like an altar, and she'd been absently folding and unfolding the corner of one for the last ten minutes.

"Because," Ines said, working the chisel into the seam, "we're the ones who bothered to come."

The seam gave with a sound like a held breath released. Inside, packed in oiled wool that had somehow kept its softness through four decades of cold black water, lay a cylinder of dense grey composite about the length of a forearm, its ends capped in brass, and beneath it a folded letter on paper so old it had gone the color of weak tea.

Ines lifted the letter out first. The handwriting was small and fierce, slanting hard to the right, as if the writer had been in a hurry or angry or both.

*To whoever finds this,* it began.

*I am Mara Vell, second engineer of the convoy vessel* Meridian's Daughter*. If you are reading this, then either the river has finally given up what it took, or you are one of Corven Ashe's people, in which case put this down, because everything I am about to tell you is worth nothing except in the hands of someone who will use it.*

Tam made a small sound. Ines looked up. She'd gone pale under her freckles.

"That name," she said. "Ashe. That's —"

"I know." Ines's voice came out flatter than they intended. "Keep reading."

There was more in Mara Vell's hand — dates, names, a list of eleven crew members of the convoy who had survived the sinking and been quietly disappeared afterward, their families paid off or frightened silent. But the heart of it was the cylinder.

"Recording core," Sefa said. She'd arrived an hour after dawn, dripping rainwater and carrying Ironwake's stolen dive plans in a waterproof tube slung across her back like a rifle, and nobody had quite known what to say to her, so nobody had said anything, and she'd simply sat down on a crate near the door and started helping. Now she turned the cylinder over in her hands, frowning at the brass caps. "Deepwater archival type. We used them for pipeline inspections. They're built to last centuries." She paused. "They're also built to be read by company equipment."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning whoever manufactured the reader controls who gets to hear it." Her jaw tightened. "Ironwake has readers. Of course they do. They've probably got the original order for this thing's destruction filed somewhere in a nice cabinet."

"So we can't play it," Tam said.

"I didn't say that." Sefa set the cylinder down on the wool with exaggerated care, the way you handle something that might go off. "I said we can't play it on *their* equipment. The encoding is standard deepwater format. Any hydrographic office has a decoder. The university at Grayharbor has one. Half the fishing fleet has sonar rigs that could be jury-rigged into one by somebody who knew what they were doing." She glanced at Oskar. "Somebody who spent twenty years running salvage gear before he decided he hated the people who owned it."

Oskar pulled the blanket tighter. For a long moment he looked at the cylinder the way you look at a dog that bit you once and might again.

"There's a rig on my old boat," he said finally. "The* Kestrel*. Assuming she hasn't rotted through at her moorings, which is a coin flip." He rubbed his face. "I can build a decoder. It'll take most of a day and it will sound like a seagull dying, but it'll play."

"Then we have a day," Ines said.

Nobody moved. The rain ticked against the boathouse roof, patient as a clock.

"Before we do this," Tam said quietly, "I want to say something." She picked up the letter, smoothed it on her knee. "My grandmother came out of Vellmark when she was nine years old. She never talked about it. Not once, my whole childhood. She taught me songs in a language I didn't know the name of and she called the river 'the long road home' and I thought that was just a thing grandmothers said." Her voice wobbled and steadied. "If that recording is what we think it is — if it's one of hers, one of my grandmother's people — then I want to hear every word of it. Even the hard parts. *Especially* the hard parts. Because nobody else was listening for forty years, and somebody should have been."

Ines found, to their surprise, that their throat had gone tight. They nodded, because words weren't going to cooperate.

"Right," said Oskar, standing with the groan of a man whose lungs had recently been full of gorge water. "Somebody make tea. This is going to take solder."

* * *

It took most of the day, exactly as promised, and the tea ran out twice.

By late afternoon the boathouse had transformed into something between a workshop and a shrine. Sefa had stripped the reader housing from the* Kestrel*'s sonar unit and rebuilt it on Tam's workbench with the focus of a surgeon and the vocabulary of a dockworker, and Oskar sat beside her feeding her tools before she asked, the two of them falling into an easy rhythm that made Ines feel like a stranger at their own campfire — which, they supposed, in a way they were. These were Sefa's people now. Or Oskar's. Or maybe they were becoming everyone's, which was a thing Ines hadn't let themselves imagine in a very long time.

"You're staring," Sefa said without looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"You're staring at my solder joints."

"They're very good solder joints."

"*Thank you.*" But there was something almost warm in it, buried deep, like a coal under ash. She finished the last connection, sat back, and blew out a breath. "That's it. That's the whole ugly miracle. Somebody give me the cylinder before I think about it too hard and find a reason not to."

Ines handed it over. Their hands were steady. They noticed that their hands were steady and wondered briefly when that had stopped being comforting.

Sefa seated the cylinder in the cradle, checked the contacts twice, and pressed the switch.

Static. A long wash of it, hissing like the river itself, and then — a click, a breath, and a voice.

A woman's voice. Younger than Ines had expected. Rough-edged, exhausted, but level, the voice of someone who had decided that if she was going to speak into the dark she was going to speak *properly*, damn it.

*"Testing. Testing. Can you hear me? Good. I don't know how much power this thing has left, so I'm going to talk fast and I'm not going to repeat myself.*

"My name is Mara Vell. Today is — well, I don't actually know today's date, because I've been in a flooded cargo hold for six days, but the convoy went down on the ninth of Thawmonth, forty-one years ago, and if anyone ever hears this, check my arithmetic.*

"We were the last convoy out of Vellmark. Eleven ships. Refugees, records, the national archive, whatever could float. The border was closed behind us and the war was over and we thought the worst part was done. We were wrong.*

"On the third night, in the narrows above what your maps call the Gorge of Saint Aldric — our maps called it the Vellnar Reach — we were boarded. Not pirates. Company men. Ironwake Consolidated Salvage, though they didn't fly any colors, because what they were doing didn't bear colors.*

"They wanted the archive. Specifically, they wanted the ledgers. Vellmark's shipping registries contained forty years of contracts — salvage concessions, wreck claims, insurance settlements — and a great many of those contracts had Ironwake's founders' signatures on them, and a great many of those signatures were attached to frauds. Sunk ships that weren't wrecks. Claims paid on cargoes that were sold twice. And worst of all, the record of the* Halvard* disaster, where an Ironwake captain scuttled his own vessel with the crew aboard rather than return empty-handed, and collected on all of them.*

"The convoy refused to hand over the ledgers. So Ironwake sank us. All eleven ships. In peacetime. In a river. With witnesses on the banks — fishermen, farmers — who were paid, threatened, or in eleven documented cases, killed. I know because I kept count. Counting was all I could do.*

"I survived because I was in the engine spaces when they opened our hull, and the engine spaces flooded slow. I got into an air pocket with two other people. One of them was the archivist, Master Denu. He had the ledgers' index sealed in a case like this one. He told me: *if you live, make them hear it.* Then the pocket collapsed and he didn't live, and I did, which is the arithmetic I can't make come out fair.*

"I got ashore. I tried to testify. I was nineteen and I had no papers and a foreign accent and a story about a massacre nobody wanted to be true. The commission that heard me was chaired by a man named Aldous Ashe. Corven Ashe's grandfather. He ruled my testimony inadmissible on grounds of insufficient documentation, and then he made sure I would never have documentation, and then he made sure I would never stop being afraid, and it worked for a while. It worked for years.*

"But I am tired now, and I am dying — the water got into my chest and it isn't going to leave — and I have decided that fear is a poor use of my remaining time. So I made this recording, and I sealed it in a salvage chest with a copy of the manifest, and I gave it to the river, because the river is the only witness left who cannot be bought.*

"To whoever is hearing this: the ledgers' index is in the forward hold of the* Meridian's Daughter*, third ship of the convoy, wedged on the ledge below the falls. There may be others. There should be others; Denu was thorough. Find them. Find the families of the eleven. Tell them their dead have names, and the names are these—"

And she read them. Eleven names, spoken slowly, each one given its full weight, each one followed by a detail — *he played the fiddle, she was pregnant, he was fourteen and scared of eels* — until the list became less a document than a graveyard you could walk through with your eyes closed.

Ines didn't realize they were crying until Tam pressed a rag into their hand. Around the boathouse, nobody spoke. Oskar had his head bowed. Sefa's hands were flat on the workbench, knuckles white, and when Ines caught her eye there was something in her face that Ines recognized from mirrors: the expression of a person watching their own life rearrange itself around a truth they'd been walking past for years.

*"One more thing,"* Mara Vell said, and her voice changed — softened, almost amused. *"If Corven Ashe is listening to this, and I suspect he eventually will, because guilty men always check: I hope it keeps you awake. My grandmother had a saying. The river forgets nothing. It only waits. Sleep badly, Director."*

A click. Static. Silence.

The rain filled it, ticking on the roof, and somewhere out on the water a bird cried once and quit.

Tam broke first. She laughed — a wet, cracked, astonished laugh — and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "*Sleep badly, Director.* Nineteen years old, drowning slowly, and she signed off with her grandmother's curse. Gods." She shook her head. "She'd have liked you, Ines."

"Why me?"

"Because you're also the kind of person who tells powerful men to sleep badly." Tam grinned through the tears. "It's practically your job description."

Ines looked down at the cylinder, at the letter, at the eleven names now written in their memory in Mara Vell's fierce slanting voice. Something had shifted in their chest — not the grief, which remained, vast and cold as the gorge itself, but its *weight*. Grief alone was a stone you carried. Grief with purpose was ballast. It kept you upright in weather.

For forty years this voice had waited in the dark, and the world had obligingly forgotten how to listen. Well. Ines had spent seven years learning how to keep their head down, how to be nobody, how to survive by being unheard. Every miserable skill of their exile suddenly had a use.

"We broadcast it," they said. "All of it. Unedited. Not to some commission — commissions get chaired by Ashes. To everyone. Every frequency, every port board, every town square speaker from here to Grayharbor. Let the river do the talking."

"With what transmitter?" Sefa asked. "Rivergate's harbor office is company-leased. The relay tower on the bluff is Ironwake's. They'd cut us off before the second name."

"Then we don't use theirs." Ines stood, and felt the old competence rise in them like the tide it was named for. "Oskar, your boat — does she have a long-range set?"

"Ancient one. Probably works. Probably works *badly*."

"Badly is fine. Badly is untraceable." Ines was pacing now, mind sprinting ahead. "But here's the problem. The moment we transmit, Ashe knows everything — the recording, the duplicate in the hull, all of it. He'll move on the wreck immediately. Which means we don't get to choose when this happens. We transmit, and then we race him for the* Meridian's Daughter* with whatever tide is left."

"Or," Sefa said slowly, "we let him come to us. He knows we have the case. He thinks the case is the prize. He'll want to take it from us personally — men like him always do — and he'll pick ground where he thinks he has the advantage."

"The narrows," Ines and Tam said together, and then looked at each other.

"He'll stage it at the narrows," Ines said. "Single channel, high walls, his boats block both ends. Classic." They smiled, and it wasn't entirely a nice smile. "Which is fine, because Tam's grandmother drew three channels through the narrows that aren't on any chart Ironwake ever bought."

Outside, the light was failing, and far downstream the river ran high and dark toward the sea, carrying, as it always had, everything that was ever entrusted to it.

Tam was already unrolling the charts.
