## 16. Fifth Tide: The Trap at the Narrows

The narrows got their name from a cartographer who had clearly never stood on either bank and shouted across them. From the water they were not narrow so much as *opinionated* — the gorge pinched down to half its width there, the current stacking itself into long green ridges that moved like something breathing under a blanket. Tam's grandmother's chart called it *Kesselmund*, the kettle-mouth, and marked it with a small inked warning in Vellish script that Tam translated as "here the river argues back."

Ines had argued with rivers before. They knew how it ended.

They came through at slack water on the fifth tide, which was the only window anyone sane would choose, and even then Oskar took them through on a line no wider than a hallway, hands light on the wheel of the *Mara's Own* — Tam had named her, over Oskar's mild objection that naming a boat after a dead woman was asking for trouble — threading between two boulders the charts insisted were one.

"Left," Ines said, though Oskar was already going left. It was less an instruction than a ritual between them now, three tides deep. Oskar steered; Ines confirmed; the boat obeyed; nobody drowned. A working partnership, built fast out of wreckage, the way all the best ones were.

Sefa sat amidships with the testimony case strapped across her lap like a sleeping child she didn't entirely trust. She'd barely let go of it since the fourth tide. She'd stolen Ironwake's dive plans, burned her bridges behind her in a way that involved an actual fire in an actual chart room — "small fire," she kept saying, "controlled fire" — and come down to the dock soaked in river spray and shame and something that might have been relief. She hadn't apologized. Ines hadn't asked her to. There would be time for apologies later, or there wouldn't, and either way the case mattered more than the conversation.

Tam stood at the bow with her grandmother's chart open against the wind, hair whipping, grinning like this was the best week of her life. For Tam, Ines suspected, it genuinely was.

"We're through Kesselmund," Tam called back. "Grandmother says the next stretch is called the Long Patience."

"What does that mean?" Sefa asked.

"It means you wait," Tam said, "and the river doesn't."

Ines felt it before they saw it — a wrongness in the echo off the canyon walls, a hollowness where there should have been the clean slap of water on stone. Sound behaved strangely in the gorge; Ines had learned its moods during the survey, back when their biggest worry was whether the dredge would snag another shopping cart. This was different. This was the sound of an engine being deliberately quiet, which is a thing engines are very bad at.

"Kill the running lights," Ines said quietly. "Oskar. Ease us toward the north bank."

Oskar didn't ask why. That was the thing about disgraced people — they'd already learned the lesson that questions cost seconds. He swung the wheel and the *Mara's Own* slid toward the shadowed wall, and that was when the floodlights came on.

Three of them, mounted on the rail of a vessel sitting square across the narrows ahead, white and merciless, turning the river to milk. The Ironwake hull loomed behind the glare — the *Corvus*, Ashe's flagship, all crane arm and winch drum and corporate gray, looking less like a ship than like a hand reaching down into the water.

Behind them, a second engine note. Ines turned. Another Ironwake tender had slipped into the channel astern, closing the door.

"Well," Oskar said. "That's that, then."

"That's not that," Ines said, mostly to hear themselves say it.

* * *

Corven Ashe came across on a gangway plank himself, which surprised Ines. They'd expected a subordinate, a clipboard, a man paid to do unpleasant things. Instead the Director of Ironwake Salvage walked down onto their deck in a dry, immaculate jacket, hands clasped behind his back, wearing the expression of someone arriving slightly early for a meeting he'd scheduled months ago.

"Ines Halloran." He said the name carefully, the way you'd handle a document with brittle edges. "I want you to know I've read everything about you. Everything. Your incident reports are genuinely excellent writing. Terse. Honest to a fault." He smiled. "That was always your problem, wasn't it."

"Ashe." Ines stepped forward, putting themselves bodily between him and the case. Behind him, four Ironwake crew fanned out along the rail with the bored competence of people who had done this before. "You're trespassing on my boat."

"Your boat." He looked around at the *Mara's Own* — the patched deck, the secondhand winch, Tam's grandmother's chart curling in its tube by the helm — with an expression of genuine, unguarded curiosity, like a man studying an artifact from a civilization he'd helped end. "Do you know what I admire about you, Halloran? Everyone else I've ever ruined stayed ruined. They take the settlement. They move inland. They learn to flinch. But you—" he tapped the deck with his shoe, twice, almost affectionately— "you keep floating up. Like salvage."

"You didn't ruin me," Ines said. "You filed some paperwork."

"I did a great deal more than paperwork, but we can discuss that another day." His eyes went past Ines's shoulder, to Sefa, to the case. "Ms. Okonkwo. There's a version of tonight where you're still employed. It involves handing that over and a very generous severance package and never seeing any of these people again. I suspect you've already priced that option and found it wanting, but I'm obliged to offer."

"I'm obliged to decline," Sefa said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles, wrapped around the case handle, were white.

"Mm." Ashe sighed, and the performance dropped away all at once, like a coat slipping off a hook. What was underneath was tired and efficient and much worse. "Then here's what happens. My crew takes the case. You keep the boat — I have no interest in boats, or in you, frankly. The seasonal flood comes through this gorge in—" he checked his watch, a small cruel gesture— "roughly five hours. The narrows will be impassable well before that. You'll be stranded on the north bank above the high-water line, which is uncomfortable but survivable, and in the morning my fleet will be gone and the wreck will be under forty feet of new river, and none of this will ever have happened. No one dies. I'd prefer that. Dead guides generate inquiries."

"And if we don't hand it over?"

Ashe looked genuinely puzzled by the question, as if Ines had asked what would happen if they declined to exist. "Halloran. Look around you."

Ines looked. Four crew along the rail. A tender astern. The *Corvus*'s crane arm swinging slowly overhead like a slow black pendulum. And beyond it all, the dark mouth of the gorge downstream, where the river was already beginning to lift and swell with the tide coming in from the coast — the fifth tide, right on schedule, indifferent as arithmetic.

There was no version of this where they fought. Ines had spent enough years around violence-adjacent men to know the difference between a standoff and a math problem. This was a math problem.

"Sefa," Ines said quietly. "Give it to him."

"No." Flat. Immediate.

"Sefa." Ines turned, met her eyes, and tried to put into a look everything they couldn't say in front of Ashe: *the duplicate is still in the hull. We dive tomorrow at first light. Don't die for a box.* "We'll make another one. Give it to him."

Something moved behind Sefa's eyes — the old expedition trust, buried but not dead, doing arithmetic of its own. Then she unstrapped the case and held it out, and one of the crew took it, and Ines watched forty years of sealed history get carried up a gangplank in the arms of a stranger.

Ashe received it with both hands, and for just a moment his composure flickered — something hungry and young crossing his face, there and gone. "Thank you," he said, and meant it, which was somehow the most obscene part. "For what it's worth, Halloran — and it isn't worth much — you were the best guide I ever blacklisted."

"You only ever blacklisted one guide."

"Yes," Ashe said. "It only ever took one."

He turned and walked back up the plank, and the floodlights cut, and the *Corvus* began to swing its great mass around in the narrows with the tenderness of a whale turning in a bathtub, and Ines stood on their own patched deck and listened to the river rise.

* * *

They grounded the *Mara's Own* in a side chute on the north bank, wedging her between two granite shoulders above the obvious flood line, because whatever else happened, they weren't losing the boat too. Then there was nothing to do but climb.

The north bank rose in terraces — old flood benches stacked like the rings of a tree, each one marking some ancient spring melt. They made camp on the third bench, high enough to be safe, low enough to see the water. Tam built a fire from driftwood with the brisk cheer of someone who refused to acknowledge circumstances, and Oskar produced a tin of coffee from somewhere in his coat with the solemnity of a man producing a relic, and for twenty minutes they managed something adjacent to warmth.

Then the tide arrived properly, and nobody managed anything.

Ines had seen floods before. They had built a career, and lost one, on knowing what moving water intended. But this was different — this was the gorge doing what it did once a year, the whole river system backing up into the canyon like breath drawn into lungs, and it didn't creep. It *climbed*. The narrows they'd threaded at slack water vanished in under an hour, the arguing green ridges swallowed whole, the water rising up the walls in visible surges, each one a little higher, patient as rent coming due.

"It's going to reach the wreck by morning," Tam said. She wasn't grinning anymore. She sat with her knees pulled up, watching the water eat the second flood bench below them. "Full submergence. Grandmother's chart says the site won't surface again until next season."

"Next season," Oskar repeated. He laughed once, without humor. "By next season Ironwake will have stripped that hull to the rivets and melted the evidence into rebar."

"The case," Sefa said. She was staring at the water with an expression Ines recognized from expeditions past — the look of a person replaying a decision on a loop, hunting for the branch point where it could have gone differently. "I shouldn't have given it up. I should have made them take it off me. I should have—"

"Sefa." Ines sat down beside her. Up close she looked older than she had four days ago, or maybe Ines was finally seeing her accurately for the first time in six years. "If you'd fought them, they'd have taken it anyway, and taken you with it, and we'd be short one diver tomorrow."

"Tomorrow." She said the word like it was a foreign currency she wasn't sure would spend. "What tomorrow? The site floods by dawn. Even if we could reach the hull, the corridors will be surge channels. You told me yourself — you don't dive flooded gorges on a rising tide. It's the first thing they teach and the last thing anyone obeys."

"I know what I taught you."

"Then what's the plan, Ines? Because from here it looks like we gave him the only copy, and in eight hours the river closes over the whole story forever, and we sit on this rock and watch it happen." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, Ines could tell — hated the crack the way she'd always hated any evidence of being a person rather than a professional.

Ines didn't answer right away. Because the truth was that Ines had been doing the same arithmetic Sefa had, and the sum kept coming out the same: the case gone, the wreck drowning, the testimony sealed inside Mara Vell's hull about to be entombed for another year — or forever, if Ironwake's lawyers found a way to own the concession permanently, which companies like Ironwake always eventually did.

But there was the duplicate. Mara Vell had been a careful woman; the chest's false bottom proved that. And careful women who hid one recording in a testimony case did not, as a rule, leave the only other copy in an obvious place. Ines had spent two dives in that hull. They remembered the shape of it the way you remember a house you grew up in — the corridor, the chapel-turned-air pocket, the row of cabins aft where the manifest said the officers slept. And they remembered the captain's cabin, and the heavy iron strongbox bolted beneath the bunk frame, too corroded to open on the first pass, logged and left for later.

Later was now, or never.

"There's a strongbox," Ines said. "Captain's cabin. Under the bunk. If Mara Vell duplicated anything, it's in there."

Sefa stared at them. "The site will be fully flooded by dawn. The corridors will be moving water. You're talking about diving a collapsing wreck in a tidal surge."

"I'm talking about it, yes."

"That's suicide."

"It's a plan with poor survival odds," Ines said. "Suicide has better weather."

Oskar snorted into his coffee. Tam said, "I want it noted that I vote yes," and Sefa closed her eyes as if praying for patience and finding the line busy.

"You can't hold your breath through those corridors on a surge," Sefa said. "And we have no tanks, no lines, no—"

"We have the *Mara's Own*," Tam said. "And we have the high chute on the south bank — grandmother's chart marks a pothole chain that surfaces inside the gorge above the wreck. At peak tide, the water level reaches the lower potholes. You wouldn't swim *to* the wreck. You'd drop in *on top of it*, from inside the gorge, during the one hour the surge pauses at full flood. Slack water at the top of the tide. Thirty minutes, maybe forty."

Everyone looked at her.

"How do you know that?" Sefa asked.

"Because my grandmother drew a map of nobody's river," Tam said, "and because I've been reading it every night since we started, and because somebody in this crew had to do the homework while the rest of you were having feelings." She unrolled the chart by the firelight, and there it was — the pothole chain, inked in faded brown, annotated in Vellish script, with a small notation beside it that Tam translated: *"At the sixth tide, the kettle fills and holds its breath. One may enter where the water enters. Do not be greedy."*

"Do not be greedy," Oskar repeated. "Sound advice. Historically ignored by everyone on this boat."

Ines looked at the chart, and at the water climbing the walls below them, black and enormous and unhurried, and felt the old thing wake in their chest — not hope exactly, something older and less comfortable. Competence with nowhere to go. The river had taken the case. Fine. The river had also, in its patient annual way, just built them a door.

"One hour of slack at peak," Ines said. "One dive. Two divers maximum — the potholes won't fit more. After that the ebb starts and the wreck goes under real water, silt water, and it's gone until next year, and probably forever."

"So it's now or never," Sefa said.

"It's now or never," Ines agreed. "Get some sleep. All of you. We dive at the top of the sixth tide."

Sefa didn't move. She was still watching the water, jaw tight, and when she spoke again her voice was low, meant only for Ines.

"When we get through this — and I'm not saying we will — there's something you need to know. About the blacklist. About why it really happened. I've known for years and I let you carry the shame of it alone, and that's—" She stopped. Started again. "You deserve to hear it from me before you hear it from anyone else. After the dive. Whatever happens."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. Six years of silence sat between them, and beneath it, faintly, like a signal under static, something that might still be salvaged.

"After the dive," Ines said.

Below them, the river climbed another foot, and held its breath, and waited.
