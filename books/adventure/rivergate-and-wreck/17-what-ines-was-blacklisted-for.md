## 17. What Ines Was Blacklisted For

The tide was coming in, and Sefa Okonkwo could not sleep.

She sat on the overturned hull of Oskar's skiff, which had been dragged up above the waterline and turned into a bench by some unspoken agreement nobody remembered making. The camp behind her — three tents, a cook fire banked to embers, Tam's charts weighted down with river stones — smelled of woodsmoke and wet rope. Somewhere out in the dark, the gorge breathed. Sefa had spent eleven years listening to rivers breathe on other continents, and she had never once gotten used to it. They sounded patient. That was the worst part. Rivers sounded like they could wait forever.

Ines Halloran came out of their tent without a lamp, moving the way they always moved at night: quiet, certain, a person who had learned to navigate darkness so thoroughly that darkness had stopped being an obstacle and become a courtesy.

"You're sitting on my boat," Ines said.

"It's Oskar's boat."

"Oskar's asleep. Possession is nine-tenths of snoring." Ines lowered themselves onto the hull beside her, leaving a careful hand's width of space between them. Old habit. Or maybe new caution. Sefa couldn't tell anymore which habits were which.

They sat in silence for a while. The fire popped. Downriver, faint as a headache, Ironwake's floodlights stained the clouds orange over the narrows.

"You should sleep," Ines said finally. "Fifth tide's tomorrow. Whatever that means for us, we'll want our hands steady."

"I know why you were blacklisted."

Sefa hadn't planned to say it. She'd been carrying the sentence around for two days like a stone in her boot, and it simply fell out of her mouth the way stones do — all at once, and where they land.

Ines went very still beside her. Not startled-still. The stillness of someone who has heard a sound they've been dreading for years and is now cataloguing every possible response to it.

"Do you," they said.

"The Kestrel Narrows job. Nine years ago." Sefa kept her eyes on the river, because looking at Ines while she said this felt like a cruelty she wasn't entitled to. "The survey vessel *Cormorant*. Fourteen crew. It went down in a storm that Ironwake's own meteorologists had flagged forty hours early, and the company sent it out anyway because the client contract had a penalty clause."

"I don't want to—"

"You filed a report saying the sinking was preventable." She pressed on, gently, relentlessly, the way she'd once pressed through whiteout conditions because stopping meant dying. "You wrote it up clean. Cause, negligence, names. And then your name got pulled off every certification registry from here to the coast, and the report itself—" she exhaled, "—the report doesn't exist. I checked. I checked for years, Ines. There's no record it was ever written."

"There isn't," Ines said quietly. "Because I never submitted it."

Sefa turned to look at them then, and the sight of Ines Halloran in the ember-light — thinner than she remembered, gray coming in early at the temples, hands folded like a penitent — made something in her chest crack along a fault line she'd thought had healed into scar tissue.

"What do you mean, you never submitted it?"

"I mean I wrote it. All of it. Every page." Ines's voice was level, almost conversational, which Sefa knew — had always known — was how they sounded when they were holding something down with both hands. "And then a man from Ironwake's legal division came to my hotel room in Vellmark City — this was before, obviously, before anyone knew what was going to happen to Vellmark — and he sat down across from me and opened his briefcase, and inside the briefcase was a revised version of my report."

"A revised version."

"Same findings, different cause. Weather anomaly. Act of God. Nobody's negligence, nobody's name." Ines picked up a pebble from the hull and turned it over between their fingers. "He told me the company valued my expertise and hoped I'd see the wisdom of the revision. He used the word 'wisdom' four times. I counted. When people are lying to you, they repeat the words they've rehearsed."

"And you refused."

"I refused."

"So you were blacklisted for refusing to falsify—"

"No." Ines set the pebble down with terrible precision. "That's the part you've got wrong, Sefa, and I need you to hear it right, because I've had nine years to get it wrong myself and I'm tired of it. I wasn't blacklisted for refusing. Refusing is private. Refusing happens inside your own head and hurts no one but you." They looked at her, and their eyes in the low light were the same eyes that had once read a glacier's face for her at three in the morning and pronounced it safe when every instrument disagreed. "I was blacklisted because I told him I'd already sent copies. Three copies. To the maritime board, to the families' coalition, and to a journalist I trusted. Which was true. And then the company bought the maritime board, settled with the families quietly enough that most of them never understood what they were settling for, and buried the journalist's story under six inches of advertising revenue. But they couldn't buy me. So instead they did the next best thing."

"They erased you."

"They erased me." A ghost of a smile crossed Ines's face, and it was such a strange, sad, self-deprecating expression that Sefa nearly laughed despite everything. "You know what the legal man said to me as he left? He shook my hand. He shook my hand, Sefa. He said, 'Mr. Halloran, you should understand that this isn't punishment. This is risk management.' Risk management. Like I was a rockfall zone. Like I was a section of river that needed rerouting."

* * *

Sefa laughed. She couldn't help it — a short, broken sound that escaped before she could decide whether it was allowed.

"Risk management," she repeated. "God. They really do talk like that. You spend long enough inside Ironwake and you start believing disasters are just line items that got misfiled."

"How long?" Ines asked. Not accusingly. Just asking, the way you'd ask about the weather.

"Six years. Contract diver, then survey lead, then—" she waved a hand at herself, at the whole improbable architecture of her career, "—then whatever I am. Ashe recruited me personally. Did you know that? He flew out to meet me. Brought a bottle of wine I couldn't afford and told me I was the best field hydrologist he'd seen in a decade." She paused. "I've thought about that wine a lot lately. Whether he'd have brought it if he'd known what I'd eventually figure out."

"And what did you figure out?"

"That the company keeps two sets of books. One for the regulators and one for the truth." Sefa pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. Out on the black water, something splashed — a fish, or a log turning over, or nothing at all. "I found the Kestrel file eight months ago. Your file. It was in an archive vault in the Corven building, mislabeled as a personnel dispute. I opened it expecting to find evidence you'd done something wrong. Some mistake. Something that would finally explain why you walked away from me on that dock in Halvard with nothing but a duffel bag and a sentence I've replayed ten thousand times."

"'It's better if you're not near me,'" Ines recited softly. "I remember the sentence."

"I hated that sentence. Do you understand? For nine years I hated that sentence. I built a whole person out of hating it." Her voice wobbled and she let it; there was no one awake to hear except Ines, and Ines had earned every unsteady note. "And then I open the file and there's your report. Forty pages. Your handwriting in the margins. And a memo from legal recommending 'reputational containment' of the reporting officer, and a signature approving it, and the signature belongs to a man whose grandson is currently running salvage operations upriver trying to bury a second set of witnesses."

Ines was silent for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had gone rough at the edges.

"You read my report."

"I read it twice."

"What did you think?"

Sefa considered the question seriously, because Ines deserved seriousness. "I thought it was the best incident analysis I've ever read. I thought whoever wrote it understood vessels and weather and human stubbornness the way some people understand music. And then I got to the last page, and there was a paragraph addressed directly to the review board — not about the sinking, about the culture that caused it — and I thought: this person was never dangerous. This person was *accurate*, and accuracy was the danger." She turned to face them fully. "Ines. You weren't disgraced. You were *quarantined*. There's a difference, and it took me nine years and a stolen archive file to see it, and I am so sorry it took me that long."

"Don't apologize." Ines's jaw worked. "If anything, I owe you the apology. 'It's better if you're not near me' — that was cowardice dressed up as protection. I told myself I was keeping you clear of the fallout. Really I just couldn't stand to watch you choose the company over me, so I chose for you."

"I would have chosen you."

"I know that *now*." Ines huffed a breath that was almost a laugh. "Back then I was thirty-one years old and freshly erased, and every instinct I had said: shrink. Get small. Get so far under the radar that even the radar forgets you exist. Rivergate was perfect for it. Nobody in this town asks questions. Asking questions is bad for business when your business is pretending the twentieth century didn't happen."

"Tam asks questions."

"Tam is a special case." Something warmed in Ines's voice — the first genuine heat Sefa had heard from them since Halvard. "Tam asked me forty questions in my first week and I answered thirty-nine of them wrong on purpose, and she saw through every single one, and instead of calling me a liar she started teaching me card games so I'd have something to do with my hands besides shake." Ines shook their head slowly. "Her grandmother came out of Vellmark with nothing but a recipe book and a hatred of official seals. You should ask her about the seals sometime. It's a whole thing."

"I will." Sefa hesitated, then reached across the space between them — the careful hand's width, the nine years of width — and put her hand over Ines's. Their fingers were cold from the night air and calloused exactly where she remembered. "For what it's worth, the shame was always misplaced. You didn't fail the industry, Ines. The industry failed you, publicly, on purpose, and then spent a decade making sure everyone believed their version."

Ines looked down at her hand. Didn't pull away. Didn't quite hold on either — not yet, not fully, but their fingers curled a degree or two toward hers, like a compass needle finding north after years of being shaken.

"You know the worst part?" Ines said. "All these years I told myself the blacklist was a verdict. That somewhere, in some ledger, there was a reason, and the reason was me. That I'd been found wanting. And I built my whole life around that verdict — small life, quiet life, a life shaped exactly like a guilty conscience." They lifted their head, and in the dark their eyes caught the last of the firelight. "And it turns out the ledger entry was written by the same people who sank a convoy full of civilians to hide a recording. Turns out my great crime was telling the truth on paper. Turns out I've been doing penance for being *right*."

"Yes," Sefa said simply.

"That's—" Ines exhaled, long and slow, and something seemed to leave them with the breath, some weight carried so long its absence must have felt like vertigo. "That's going to take some getting used to."

"Take your time. We've got—" Sefa glanced at the sky, calculating tides out of habit, and the calculation landed in her stomach like ice water. "Actually, we've got about a day and a half. Sixth tide comes tomorrow night, and when it does, the gorge floods the wreck site until spring. Everything down there — the hull, the corridors, whatever Ashe hasn't already stripped — goes under twenty meters of moving water."

"Then we'd better hope the duplicate testimony is where Mara Vell's recording says it is."

"If it's not—"

"It is." Ines stood, and Sefa watched nine years fall off their posture in a single motion — shoulders back, chin level, the guide who had once led her through a collapsing icefield singing sea shanties to keep her panic quiet. "Mara Vell hid that case from professionals for forty years. She hid it from Ironwake's divers, from the salvage crews, from every official search. She was good at hiding things from institutions." A dry smile. "So am I. It runs in the blacklisted."

They started toward the tents, then stopped, half-turned.

"Sefa. The Kestrel report. The original. Do you still have access to the archive?"

A slow grin spread across Sefa's face, and she felt it like sunrise. "Ines Halloran. Are you asking me to help you commit document fraud against a multinational corporation?"

"I'm asking whether you kept a copy."

"Of course I kept a copy. I keep copies of everything. It's why they hired me." She stood and brushed the damp off her trousers. "Why?"

"Because tomorrow night, when the river takes the wreck, the only proof left in the world will be paper." Ines's voice dropped, steady and certain, the voice of someone reading a route off a map only they could see. "And paper, my friend, can be copied, mailed, and delivered to every regulator, every descendant, and every journalist on the coast before Ironwake's lawyers finish their morning coffee. Mara Vell waited forty years for someone stubborn enough to carry her truth out of the gorge." They looked out at the black, patient water. "Tomorrow, we go get the rest of it. And this time, nobody revises my report."

Downriver, over the narrows, the first gray hint of dawn touched the clouds — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of Ironwake's winches starting up early.
