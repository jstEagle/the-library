## 18. Sixth Tide: The Wreck Gives Everything

The gorge at dawn sounded like a throat clearing before it spoke.

Ines stood at the edge of the black water with the dive plan spread across a slab of fallen rock, weighted at the corners with four stones and one very opinionated river pilot's boot. Oskar leaned over the map, chewing the same unlit pipe he'd been chewing for two days, as if the tobacco were a strategic reserve he wasn't ready to spend.

"That's the corridor," Ines said, tracing the line with a finger. "Third compartment aft. The duplicate case should be in the purser's strongroom—Mara Vell's recording said so herself. *The paper lives twice,* she said. Once in the case, once in the ship."

"And the ship," Tam said, "is currently trying to become a river."

"Currently," Ines agreed.

The water level had come up a full meter overnight. You could hear the difference in the falls—deeper, rounder, a bass note where yesterday there had been a baritone. Somewhere upstream, the seasonal melt had begun its annual argument with the gorge, and the gorge was losing on purpose, the way it always did, opening its arms to the flood and swallowing everything wedged inside it. Including, in approximately nine hours, the *Vellmark Star* and everything she still held.

"Nine hours," Oskar said, around the pipe. "Give or take."

"Give or take is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"I'm a pilot. Precision is for people with insurance."

Ines almost smiled. Almost. There wasn't much room in their chest this morning for anything except the cold arithmetic of it: dive down, through the corridor they'd mapped on the third tide, past the collapse they'd caused on the twelfth—Oskar's collapse, Oskar's crushed ribs, Oskar's week of breathing like a bellows with a grudge—into a strongroom that might already be silted shut, and back out again before the river finished its breakfast.

Sefa crouched at the waterline, checking the second regulator for the third time. She'd been checking gear all morning with the focused, slightly aggressive tenderness of someone who had run out of words and decided that equipment maintenance was a language she still spoke fluently. She'd stolen Ironwake's dive plans, burned her career to the waterline, and shown up at their camp soaked and shaking and defiant, and Ines had said *you're late* and she'd said *I know* and that had been the whole reconciliation so far. It was holding. Barely. Like a knot tied in wet rope.

"You don't have to do this part," Sefa said, not looking up. "I can go down."

"You can't. You've got forty dives on your certification and half of them were in a quarry."

"I've got *your* training."

"My training taught you to turn back." Ines knelt beside her and took the regulator from her hands, gently, the way you'd take a knife from someone considering options. "This is the part where I don't."

* * *

The first dive had been wonder. The third had been grief. This one was neither. This one was work, and work, Ines had learned over a long and complicated career, was the only reliable cure for fear. Not the absence of it—fear came along on every dive, sat in the boat, checked your gauges, kept its little clipboard. Fear was crew. You just didn't let it steer.

They went down in the grey-green light of early morning, Ines first, Sefa on the line behind despite everything, because Sefa had looked at Ines with the exact expression she'd worn on the Karst Ridge twelve years ago—the one that said *I am coming whether you like it or not, and we both know how this ends*—and Ines had run out of arguments. Tam held the surface line. Oskar, ribs strapped, refused to leave the ledge above the wreck and stationed himself at the corridor mouth with a spare tank and a pry bar, which was either heroic or stupid, and with Oskar it had always been both.

The *Vellmark Star* rose out of the murk like a cathedral that had lost an argument with God.

She lay on her port side, wedged in the ledge behind the falls, forty years of silt and patience draped over her hull. Ines had been inside her twice now, and both times had felt the same thing: the strange, unbearable courtesy of a ship that had died with her passengers and kept their secrets like a promise. The dining salon with its chairs still stacked. The children's shoes. The nameplates. You did not rush in a place like that. You moved the way you'd move through a church, even if you didn't believe in anything the church believed in.

The corridor to the purser's section was where it had all gone wrong before. The bulkhead doorway gaped now, wider than Ines remembered—their earlier rescue of Oskar had torn the frame loose, and the collapse that had trapped him had also, inconveniently, opened the way forward. The river giveth. The river also, in approximately nine hours, taketh away everything including the river's own geometry.

Ines checked the line, checked Sefa, and got the double squeeze on the wrist that meant *with me*. They went in.

The corridor was a throat of rust and shadow. Their lights carved tunnels through the suspended silt, and the silt was moving—fine, lazy spirals of it, the ship breathing out her last sediment as the water pressure above her began to change. Ines counted doorways. Two. Three. The purser's strongroom was behind the fourth, a steel box the size of a generous closet, and when their light found it, their stomach dropped.

The door was open.

Not forced. Not collapsed. Open, and hanging by one hinge, and the strongroom beyond was full of silt to the height of a seated man.

"No," Sefa said, the word coming out as a burst of bubbles, distorted and furious. She pushed past Ines into the room and began digging, hands flat, sweeping the silt in great brown clouds. Ines grabbed her shoulder, made the *slow* signal, made the *think* signal, and then—because some hypocrisy is load-bearing—joined her.

They dug. It was the least dignified work Ines had ever done in a wreck, and they had once spent six hours in a flooded mine shaft retrieving a client's dentures because the client's wife had insisted and the client's wife paid well. Silt came up in armfuls. A ledger, swollen to twice its size, its ink long gone to ghost. A cash box, empty, its lock rusted open decades ago. A boot. Ines lifted the boot out and set it aside with the care you'd give a boot, because it had belonged to somebody.

And then Sefa's hand stopped, and her whole body went rigid, and she turned and held up her light, and there it was.

The case.

It was smaller than Ines had imagined—smaller than the salvage chest that had started all of this, a slim steel rectangle with the dead seal of Vellmark pressed into its face, the twin-headed river and the mountain, the emblem of a country that existed now only on the belongings of its dead. Mara Vell's handwriting was on a tag wired to the handle. Forty years underwater and the ink had survived because the tag had been waxed, because Mara Vell had been the kind of person who waxed her tags, and Ines thought, with a grief that came out of nowhere and went through them like a spear, *she knew. She knew she'd need it to last.*

Sefa's hands were shaking. Ines took the case, because someone had to have steady hands, and clipped it inside their harness, against the chest, where it sat like a second heart.

* * *

The ship chose that moment to object.

It started as a sound—a long, groaning complaint somewhere below them, in the keel, the sound of a hull that had held its shape for forty years deciding, under new pressure, to reconsider. The silt in the water thickened. The corridor behind them shivered, and a rain of rust flakes came down through their lights like orange snow.

Ines looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at Ines. Neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the ship wasn't already saying louder.

They went.

The corridor was worse on the way out. The collapse that had trapped Oskar had settled further, and where there had been a gap you could squeeze through, there was now a gap you could argue about. Ines went first, flat against the deck plates, pushing the case ahead of them into the gap, and for one long moment they were stuck—truly stuck, hips wedged, the steel case jammed against a buckled frame, the exit six feet away and six years away and possibly forty years away—and they felt the old panic rise, the Karst Ridge panic, the dark and the crush and the sound of Sefa screaming their name—

And then Sefa's hand closed on their ankle. Steady. Present. *Here.*

Ines breathed out, all the way out, the way they'd taught a hundred clients to do when the mountain squeezed. Exhale, and the ribs give, and the ribs give and the body is smaller, and the body is smaller and you are through.

They came out into the corridor trailing rust and dignity, and Sefa came through after them with a torn sleeve and a laugh that was half sob, and they swam for the light.

* * *

The surface hit them like a slap and a blessing in the same instant. Tam hauled them over the gunwale—Tam, who had grown up on her grandmother's stories of a country that no longer existed, Tam whose hands were stronger than they had any right to be—and Oskar was there with a blanket and, inexplicably, a thermos.

"Soup," he said. "Don't ask where I got soup. There are things a man does for his crew and things a man does not explain."

Ines lay on the deck of Oskar's battered pilot boat and laughed until their ribs hurt, holding the case above the waterline like a lantern, and Tam knelt over it with her grandmother's charts spread out beside it, and for a moment nobody spoke. The falls roared. The river was rising. You could see it now, the difference from hour to hour—the ledge where the wreck lay already vanishing, the black water climbing the rock like something patient finally standing up.

"Is it—" Tam started.

Ines unclipped the case. The seal was intact. The wax tag was intact. Mara Vell's small, stubborn handwriting was intact. Whatever was inside—a recording, a testimony, the truth of what Ironwake's founders had done to a convoy full of witnesses—was still sealed in steel, still waiting, still *lasting*.

"We've got it," Ines said. "We've got it, and we've got—" They looked at the water. Looked at the sky. Did the arithmetic they'd been refusing to do. "Four hours. Maybe less. The narrows are between us and Rivergate, and Ashe knows we're here."

Oskar's pipe stopped moving. "The *Narrows*."

"The Narrows," Ines confirmed.

Sefa wrung out her sleeve and said, in the flat voice she used for delivering bad news she'd already rehearsed, "There's a boat at the narrows. I saw the fleet's tender repositioned there two days ago. I assumed it was for the salvage lift."

"Assumed," Oskar said. "There's a word that's sunk better ships than this one."

Ines sat up, soup in one hand, testimony of a drowned nation in the other, and looked at their crew—Tam with her charts, Oskar with his ribs and his pry bar, Sefa with her forty quarry dives and her treason and her steady hand on an ankle in the dark—and felt something that had no business being hope, but was.

"He wants the case," Ines said. "And we're the only ones who can carry it through water he doesn't know."

Tam was already unrolling her grandmother's chart of Nobody's River, the hidden channels, the back ways the gorge kept for people who bothered to learn its real name. Her finger found a line through the rock that no Ironwake pilot would ever find, because it wasn't on any chart but hers.

"Grandmother says there's a way," Tam said. "It's narrow. It's fast. And it goes right past the narrows where they're waiting."

"Past them," Ines said. "How past?"

Tam smiled, and it was her grandmother's smile, the smile of a country that had refused to die politely.

"Close enough," she said, "to wave."
