## 19. The River Takes Its Due

The water came for them the way debt collectors come: politely at first, then all at once.

Ines felt it before they heard it—a pressure change in the flooded corridor behind them, a soft shove against their fins like the river clearing its throat. They were forty meters into the *Vellmark Star*'s drowned promenade deck, Oskar's line in one fist and the duplicate testimony case strapped to their chest, when the current reversed.

Not reversed. That was the wrong word. The current *woke up*.

They had six hours of slack tide, Tam had said, reading her grandmother's chart by lamplight with that particular squint she inherited from a woman who'd fled a country that no longer existed. Six hours, and they'd used five of them finding the second case wedged behind the purser's bulkhead, exactly where Mara Vell's recording had promised it would be—*where the crew kept their letters home*, the dead woman's voice had said through the tinny speaker, and Ines had wept into their regulator, which is a thing you can do if you're careful about it.

Now the river wanted its house back.

Ines kicked hard for the junction where Oskar waited. The old pilot was wedged into the frame of a doorway, holding the guide line taut, his lamp beam shivering. Even through the murk Ines could read his body language: *hurry*.

They hurried.

The promenade deck had been beautiful once. Ines knew this the way you know things underwater—not through evidence but through some older sense. The arched windows along the port side were still intact, furred with forty years of silt, and the light that came through them was the color of weak tea and old grief. Chairs lay on the ceiling. A child's shoe sat upright on the carpet of sediment, patient as an appointment. Somewhere aft, in the sealed first-class compartments, the passengers of the *Vellmark Star* were still seated where the river had arranged them, dressed for a crossing that never finished.

*We're taking your story out*, Ines had told them, stupidly, aloud, before the final dive. Silt doesn't answer. But it had felt rude not to say something.

The junction arrived out of the gloom and Ines grabbed the line beside Oskar, tapped twice on his shoulder—*go, go*—and felt him launch upward into the vertical shaft that led to the breach in the hull. The current shoved again, harder, and somewhere below them a door that had held shut for four decades gave up its argument with a boom Ines felt in their teeth rather than heard.

The shaft was narrow. Ines went up it hand over hand, the case digging into their sternum, and thought—with the strange clarity that only terror and cold water provide—about how many times they had done this exact thing in reverse. Descending. For Ironwake. For contracts. For a name on a certification that had turned out to be made of paper knives.

Up was better. Up had always been better.

Daylight appeared above them like a coin at the bottom of a well, except inverted, which Ines's waterlogged brain found very funny indeed. Then Oskar's hand shot down through the silver ceiling of the surface, grabbed their wrist, and hauled, and Ines broke into open air with a sound that was half gasp and half laugh and entirely undignified.

* * *

Tam had the boat idling in the lee of the falls' outer curtain, right where the chart said nobody's river ran deep enough to hide a skiff. She hauled Ines over the gunwale with the strength of a dockhand who'd spent twenty years arguing with cargo, and then she did the same for Oskar, and then she sat back on her heels and looked at the two of them dripping on her deck like landed fish.

"Well?" she said.

Ines patted the case. It was dented, salted—or rivered, rather—and heavier than any object has a right to be. "Got it."

"Both?"

"Both."

Tam nodded once, businesslike, and then her face did something complicated, and she turned away quickly to kill the engine and pretend to be very interested in the fuel gauge. Ines let her have the moment. There were things you didn't watch people feel. That was a rule Ines had learned the hard way, mostly by violating it.

Oskar lay flat on the deck planks, chest heaving, staring at the sky. He was sixty-one years old and had no business free-diving a wreck, and he'd done it anyway, because he said he owed the river a truth and intended to pay it in person. Now he raised one trembling finger at the falls.

"It's going," he said.

It was. They could hear it—the deep, subsonic groan of the gorge filling, the seasonal flood coming down from the high country like a tide that had gotten ambitious. Within hours the ledge would go under. Within days, the whole site. By spring the *Vellmark Star* would be buried under thirty meters of churning brown water, and no permit, no fleet, no sonar array in the world would find her again.

"The wreck gives everything," Tam said quietly, "and then the river takes its due."

"That's not what I said it meant," Ines said.

"It's what it means now."

Ines looked back at the falls. From here, in the failing light, you couldn't see the breach in the hull, couldn't see anything but white water and shadow. Forty years ago, two hundred and eleven people had boarded a passenger vessel in a country called Vellmark, and a company that would eventually call itself Ironwake had opened her seacocks in open water to drown the witnesses to a massacre, and the maps had been redrawn, and the paperwork filed, and everyone had agreed—politely, professionally, with signatures—to forget.

Everyone except a grandmother in Rivergate who drew charts nobody asked for. Everyone except a survivor who hid two copies of her testimony in a dead ship's hull and trusted the river to keep them. Everyone except a disgraced guide who'd been blacklisted for the crime of telling the truth, and hadn't even known it until three tides ago.

"I want to stay," Ines heard themselves say.

Tam looked over sharply. "What?"

"A minute. I want to stay a minute."

"Ines—"

"One minute. Then we run the narrows ahead of the flood. I know the window. I wrote half the survey notes on this stretch." They paused. "The other half were falsified, but the ones I wrote are good."

Tam's mouth twitched despite everything. "Your humility remains breathtaking."

"I work at it."

She checked her watch, checked the water line crawling up the gorge wall, and then did the thing that Ines was beginning to understand defined her: she gave it to them. "One minute."

* * *

Ines stood at the bow while the skiff drifted, engine off, letting the current hold them just outside the reach of the falls' pull. Oskar sat up, wrapped in the spare blanket, and didn't say anything, which from Oskar was practically a eulogy.

The light was going gold. The flood came down the gorge in visible pulses now—you could see the water level climbing the rocks, a dark tongue licking higher with each minute. Somewhere beneath that rising brown, the arched windows were filling. The chairs on the ceiling. The child's shoe.

"You know what I keep thinking?" Oskar said. His voice was rough from the dive, from the years, from whatever it costs a man to confess to complicity in a drowning. "I keep thinking about the manifest. Two hundred eleven names. And I helped file the insurance claim. I was twenty-four. I signed a form that said *act of nature*."

"I know."

"And now there's a recording of a woman saying my company's founders opened the hull like a tin of sardines, and I'm sitting in a boat helping steal it back, and I don't feel redeemed. I feel—" He searched for it. "I feel like a man paying interest. On a debt where the principal's already drowned."

Ines crouched next to him. "That's what paying debts looks like, Oskar. Nobody sings while they do it."

He laughed—a wet, broken sound—and wiped his face with the blanket, and Ines pretended very hard to be watching the falls.

Because here was the thing Ines could not say out loud, not yet, maybe not ever: they were grieving. Not for the two hundred eleven—they had never met them, and grief for strangers is a luxury the living borrow—but for the version of themselves that had spent eight years believing the blacklist was earned. Eight years of small rooms and low-profile jobs and flinching whenever anyone said the word *expedition*. Eight years of believing that some part of them must have deserved it, because the alternative—that they'd been punished for integrity, methodically, by men in good suits—was too large a thing to carry alone.

Sefa had carried it for them, in her way. Had shown up soaked in rain and corporate loyalty and told them the truth with a face like a woman handing over a live grenade. *You were blacklisted because you wouldn't lie.* And Ines had stood there in the rain and felt eight years of shame invert itself like a chair being righted, and hadn't known whether to thank her or throw something at her, and had settled, characteristically, on neither.

The past couldn't be saved. That was the lesson of the whole rotten year. You couldn't dive down and haul up the person you'd been before the fall. The river had them. But you could bring up what they'd left behind—the true thing, the recorded thing, the voice of a woman named Mara Vell saying *my name is Mara Vell and I am the last*—and you could carry it to shore, and you could make it loud enough that no one got to file it under *act of nature* ever again.

The water climbed another handspan up the rocks.

"Time," Tam said softly.

"Yeah." Ines took one last look. "Goodbye," they said to the wreck, to the windows, to the shoe. "Thank you for keeping it safe. We'll take it from here."

Then Tam started the engine, and the skiff swung its nose downstream, and the *Vellmark Star* began the long process of becoming a rumor.

* * *

The narrows nearly killed them anyway, because rivers are not sentimental about farewells.

The flood surge hit the constriction while they were mid-channel, and the water stood up in places it had no business standing, and Tam steered like a woman possessed by her grandmother's ghost—which, Ines reflected, gripping the gunwale, might not be entirely a figure of speech. Oskar bailed with a bucket. Ines read the water ahead and called the lines, and for ninety seconds the three of them were not a disgraced guide, a dockhand, and a broken pilot, but a crew, functioning, wordless, precise.

Then they were through, spat out into the wide lower channel, and the roar of the narrows fell behind them, and Tam slumped over the tiller laughing the high unhinged laugh of someone who has recently almost died and is choosing to find it funny.

"We need to stop doing that," Oskar said.

"We absolutely do not," said Tam.

They made the landing below Rivergate as full dark came down. Sefa was waiting on the dock with a lantern, and Ines watched her face do the arithmetic—counting heads, counting cases, counting the absence of the fleet's searchlights upstream—and settle into something that wasn't quite relief.

"Ashe knows you slipped the cordon," she said without preamble. "He's furious. He's also filing an emergency injunction at dawn to seize 'all materials recovered from the concession site.' Which means everything you're carrying becomes his property legally by nine tomorrow morning."

"Then we're not carrying it at nine tomorrow morning," Ines said.

Sefa's lantern light caught the case as Ines lifted it from the boat. Her eyes went to it, then to Ines, and Ines saw her arrive at the same conclusion they'd been steering toward since the fifth tide.

"You're going to play it," Sefa said. "Publicly."

"Tomorrow. Dockside. Every ear in Rivergate, and every recorder Tam can beg, borrow, or liberate." Ines set the case down between them on the wet planks, gently, the way you set down something that belongs to the dead. "Ashe wants it locked in a warehouse until a judge he owns rules on it. Mara Vell waited forty years in a flooded hull. She's not waiting one more morning in a crate."

Silence, except the river.

"He'll destroy you," Sefa said. "Whatever's left of your certifications, your standing—"

"Sefa." Ines looked up at her, and found, to their mild surprise, that they were smiling. "I've been destroyed for eight years. Turns out it builds character. Terrible character, but character."

And Sefa laughed—short, startled, real—and Tam put her hand on the case like a blessing, and Oskar sat down heavily on a bollard and said, "Well. Someone should probably wake the printer," and downstream, unseen in the dark, the river rose and rose and rose, closing over the falls, the ledge, the hull, the windows, keeping its dead the way it always had—

—forgets nothing, forgives slowly, and tells the truth eventually, if you're brave enough to ask it out loud.
