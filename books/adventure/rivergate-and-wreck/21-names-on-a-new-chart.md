## 21. Names on a New Chart

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Ines would later decide was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day Rivergate's postmistress delivered bad news with the least ceremony. It came in a heavy cream envelope with the crest of the Continental Guides' Certification Board embossed in gold that probably cost more than Tam's boat.

Ines sat on the dock behind the boathouse and turned it over twice before opening it. The river ran high and green-gold beneath them, carrying leaves like small bright boats going somewhere better.

*Dear Ms. Halloran,* the letter began, in the Board's usual tone of a doctor delivering news about a tumor.

*Following the regulatory proceedings of last month, and the voiding of Ironwake Salvage's concessions, the Board has reviewed the circumstances surrounding your 2019 blacklisting. The review has found the original filing to have been made in bad faith by a corporate entity acting to suppress protected testimony. Your certification as Lead Mountain and Whitewater Guide is hereby reinstated, effective immediately, with all prior endorsements restored. We further wish to express—*

Ines stopped reading there. They'd read enough expressions of regret in their life to fill a dredge net.

They read the rest anyway, because they were thorough, because thoroughness was the one habit the blacklist had never managed to strip from them. The Board offered back everything: the lead certifications, the alpine endorsements, the expedition clearances that had once let Ines Halloran stand on glaciers in three countries and tell other people, with legal authority, *I know what I'm doing here.*

All of it back. All of it theirs again, stamped and sealed and notarized.

It had taken eleven years to get it back, and it had taken one recording of a dead woman's voice to do it, and Ines sat on the dock holding the letter and felt almost nothing at all.

That was the strange part. They'd spent years imagining this moment — rehearsing it, even, in the bad hours, the way you rehearse arguments you'll never get to have. In the rehearsal there was always triumph. There was always the fantasy of walking into some certification office, sliding the letter across the counter, watching some clerk's face change. *You made a mistake about me. Here is the proof.*

But the clerk who'd processed the original blacklist was probably retired. The company that filed it had been dissolved into the company that had become Ironwake, which had been gutted by regulators and was currently being picked apart by lawyers representing descendants of a nation that hadn't existed on maps for forty years. Corven Ashe was in custody awaiting trial, and according to Sefa, he'd been remarkably quiet about the whole thing, which somehow made him worse.

There was nobody left to show the letter to who would be diminished by it.

And the people Ines actually wanted to show it to — well. Ines could already hear Tam's reaction, which would involve her reading it upside down, snorting, and saying something like *"Congratulations, you're officially allowed to do the thing you've been doing all spring."*

Ines laughed out loud at the dock, alone, and the sound startled a heron off the pilings.

* * *

Tam found them an hour later, as Tam always did, materializing at the elbow with the unerring instinct of someone raised on a river that hid things. She had two mugs of dock coffee — the thick, sweetened sludge her grandmother's recipe called for, which Tam claimed was traditional Vellmark brewing and which Oskar claimed was "burnt sugar with a grudge in it."

"So," Tam said, handing over a mug and dropping onto the piling beside Ines without asking, because piling-sitting rights had been established weeks ago. "The Board wrote back."

"How do you know the Board wrote back?"

"Because you've been sitting here since the mail boat left, and you only sit still when something's eating you. Also Marta the postmistress told Oskar, who told me, because this town has never once kept its head down when it mattered."

Ines handed her the letter. Tam read it fast, lips moving slightly, then read it again slower, and then did something Ines didn't expect, which was go quiet.

"This is everything," Tam said finally. "This is the whole thing. You could take a real contract tomorrow. Alpine work. Big expeditions. You could leave."

"I could."

"You could go back to being *Ines Halloran*, certified, endorsed, the name on the plaque." Tam turned the letter over, examining the gold seal with the suspicion she reserved for anything official. "Instead of Ines Halloran, guy who lives above a boathouse and owes me rent in coffee."

"I don't owe you rent in coffee."

"You absolutely do. I keep a ledger." She tapped the letter against her knee. "So what are you going to do?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Ines looked down the river, toward the gorge, where the falls ran loud even from here, a white smudge of sound at the edge of hearing. Somewhere under that water was a wreck that no longer existed, taken by the sixth tide, its corridors collapsed, its dead finally given over to the current. Mara Vell's voice lived now in three copies — one with the regulators, one with the Vellmark diaspora council, and one that Tam kept in a waterproof case in the boathouse because she said her grandmother would have wanted it close to the water.

"It's a good offer," Ines said. "It's everything I wanted for eleven years."

"But?"

"But I got everything I wanted," Ines said slowly, working it out as they said it, "and it turns out what I wanted was a door back into a house where nobody lives anymore. The person they're certifying—" they tapped the letter, "—is a person I invented so I'd have something to want. The actual person is sitting right here drinking your terrible coffee."

"It's *traditional* terrible coffee."

"The actual person," Ines went on, "spent this spring learning that the thing I thought I lost — the work, the name, the credibility — wasn't the thing. The thing was the crew. And crews don't come with endorsements. You build them or you don't."

Tam was quiet for a moment. Then, with tremendous effort at casualness: "Hypothetically. If a person were to stay. What would that look like?"

"Hypothetically," Ines said, "it might look like a guide service. Independent. Small. No fleet, no directors, no permits bought from anybody. Just charts and honesty and people who know this river better than any corporation ever will."

"A guide service needs a name."

"I've been thinking about that."

"You've been thinking about that for three weeks," Tam said. "You write names on the chalkboard in the boathouse and erase them at night like a guilty man."

"That's called deliberation."

"That's called being bad at deciding. What have you got?"

Ines took a breath. "'Halloran Expeditions.'"

Tam made a face like she'd bitten the coffee grounds.

"'Whitlock River Tours.'"

"Worse. Who's Whitlock?"

"Nobody. Forget it. 'Rivergate Outfitters.'"

"Generic. Sounds like a place that sells boots and also bait." Tam swung her legs. Then she stopped swinging. "What about the chart?"

"What chart?"

"My grandmother's. The one I showed you. The hand-drawn one, with all the channels nobody else maps." Tam was looking out at the water now, and her voice had gone softer, the way it did when she talked about the old woman. "She drew it after they came here. After Vellmark was gone. And at the bottom, where a proper chart would say *Surveyed by the Admiralty* or whatever, she wrote something else. In Vellmark script. You know what it says?"

"I never learned to read it."

"*Nobody's river belongs to nobody.*" Tam smiled crookedly. "Grandmother's joke. Because the empire erased the country, but they couldn't erase the channels. The river didn't sign their paperwork. So she mapped it herself, and gave it to whoever needed it. That's what a guide is, isn't it? Somebody who gives people the map the officials won't."

Ines felt something settle in their chest, a knot they'd been carrying since the envelope arrived loosening one strand at a time.

"'Nobody's River,'" Ines said, testing it. "Nobody's River Guide Service."

"Sounds like a place that doesn't sell boots or bait."

"Sounds like us."

* * *

Oskar took the news standing up, which for Oskar constituted celebration. He was at the boathouse workbench re-lashing a cleat, hands moving with the economy of a man who'd spent forty years on water and trusted nothing on land to hold.

"An independent service," he said, not looking up. "No corporate backing. No insurance worth mentioning. Permits through the seasonal lottery like everyone else."

"That's the shape of it."

"And you're turning down full reinstatement to do it." He tied off the lashing, tested it, tested it again. "You know I worked for the convoy companies. Before. Before Vellmark fell."

"I know."

"I signed their manifests. I steered their boats. I told myself the cargo was just cargo." His hands went still on the bench. "A man spends his whole life trying to steer straight after a turn like that. Some days I think the river forgives. Most days I think the river just keeps records." He looked up then, and his eyes were wet and level. "You're sure you want a man with my history running boats for you?"

Ines considered the question seriously, because Oskar had earned serious consideration. "I want a man who knows exactly what happens when companies buy silence," they said. "I can't think of anyone more qualified to keep us honest."

Oskar nodded once, hard, the way men nodded when accepting things too large for words. Then he cleared his throat and became briskly practical, which was his way of surviving emotion. "Then we'll need a second boat. The *Maren* leaks above the waterline, which is the wrong half. And we'll need rates posted. And Tam will insist on painting the name on the hull in both scripts."

"She already bought the paint," Ines admitted. "Two months ago."

Oskar huffed — the closest he came to laughter — and went back to his cleat. But as Ines reached the door he said, quietly, "For what it's worth. Turning it down. That's the first certification I'd trust."

* * *

Sefa came last, because Sefa always came last; it was practically a character trait at this point. Ines found her at the narrows overlook at dusk, sitting on the flat rock where they'd once, a lifetime ago, planned an ascent that nearly killed them both. She'd heard about the letter too — of course she had; Marta the postmistress ran a intelligence network the Board itself should envy.

"They gave it all back," Sefa said, without turning around. "Everything."

"They did."

"And you're not taking it." Now she turned, and her expression was complicated — pride and disbelief wrestling, with disbelief currently winning on points. "Eleven years, Ines. Eleven years I watched what that blacklist cost you. The jobs you couldn't take. The rooms you walked past because your name wasn't on the list anymore. I helped bury you, in my way, by staying quiet, and I have hated myself for it every single day, and now they hand it all back and you're going to open a *guide shack* with a dockhand and a disgraced pilot and me."

"Your pitch needs work."

"Ines." Her voice cracked on the name. "Why?"

Ines sat down beside her on the rock. Below them the river gathered itself for the gorge, patient and enormous, keeping its records.

"Because when I had no name at all," Ines said, "you four were the ones who pulled me out of the water anyway. Not the Board. Not the certification. People. And I've learned — expensively, embarrassingly, at the bottom of a flooded corridor — that reputation is a thing other people hold over you, and a crew is a thing you hold together. I'd rather hold something together than be held over."

Sefa was silent a long time. The falls murmured in the distance. A lamp came on down in town, then another, Rivergate lighting itself for evening the way it had for a hundred years, head down, steady.

"Ironwake offered me a directorship before it all came apart," she said at last. "Did I tell you that? Regional operations. Real money. I sat in Ashe's office and let him talk, and part of me — a part I'm not proud of — listened." She shook her head. "And then I stole his dive plans instead. Best career decision I ever made, and it ended my career."

"Or started a new one. Partner."

Sefa looked at them sideways. "Partner. In a business called *Nobody's River*."

"Working title."

"It's a terrible name for a business."

"It's a perfect name for a promise."

And Sefa laughed — really laughed, the first time Ines had heard it in eleven years, rusty and too loud and wonderful — and stuck out her hand, and Ines took it, and that was the whole contract. No seals. No false bottoms. Just grip and gravity and two people who'd finally stopped keeping score.

"First job starts Thursday," Ines said. "Fishing party up the lower channels. Tam's cousin's friends. They pay in fish."

"We're going to be bankrupt by winter."

"Probably. But we'll be bankrupt *together*, with excellent charts."

* * *

They painted the name on the hull the next morning, all four of them, because Tam insisted it required witnesses. The paint was Vellmark blue — or what Tam swore was Vellmark blue, mixed from her grandmother's notes — and she painted the words twice, once in Common script and once in the old letters, curling and proud, a dead nation's handwriting alive again on a living boat.

*Nobody's River.*

Oskar inspected the lettering, pronounced it seaworthy, and went to check the bilge. Sefa brought coffee that was somehow worse than Tam's, which everyone agreed was an achievement. And Ines stood on the dock in the early light, watching the name dry, and felt the letter from the Board in their jacket pocket like a stone they'd decided to keep — not as a weight, but as ballast. Proof of what they'd been offered. Proof of what they'd chosen.

Tam came to stand beside them, paint on her knuckles, looking at the boat with her grandmother's crooked smile.

"You know what she'd say?" Tam said. "About all this?"

"What?"

"She'd say the river keeps nothing and forgets nothing, and the trick is knowing which days are which." Tam bumped Ines's shoulder with hers. "Today's a keeping day."

Downriver, at the mouth of the gorge, the morning ferry rounded the bend — and it was carrying passengers Ines didn't recognize, a whole deck of them, standing at the rail and staring up at the falls with faces Ines knew from photographs in the diaspora council's files. Faces out of old Vellmark portraits. A woman at the bow held a folded cloth of the same impossible blue as the paint on the hull.

"They're early," Ines said quietly. "The memorial group. They weren't due till next month."

Tam shaded her eyes, following the boat's wake. "Marta said something about them moving the date. Said they wanted to come while the river's high." A pause. "They asked for guides, you know. By name. *Our* names. On the request form. Officially."

Ines watched the ferry come on, blue cloth snapping at the bow, and understood that the next chapter of their life had already cast off from the far shore and was heading toward them whether they were ready or not.

"Well then," Ines said, and reached for the mooring line. "Best look like a guide service."
