## 22. The River Forgets Nothing

The morning of the memorial, Ines Halloran woke before dawn out of habit rather than necessity, lay in the dark of the loft above the boathouse for a full minute listening to the river, and then got up anyway, because some habits were load-bearing.

The gorge had been loud all spring. Snowmelt off the high country came down in a brown-green rush that made the pilings groan and the dock boards hum underfoot like a struck string. But this morning it had settled into its summer voice — deep, steady, conversational. A river that had said what it needed to say and was now just keeping company.

Ines stood at the window with a mug of Tam's grandmother's tea — the recipe written on a card in Vellmarki script that Ines could now read well enough to know it said *steep it properly, don't be an animal* — and watched the light come up over the water. One year ago this view had been something Ines looked at through a crack in their life, a place to hide. Now it was just home. The difference, Ines had learned, was not the view. It was who knew where you slept.

Downstairs, someone had already started the coffee, which meant Oskar was up, which meant the *Mara's Wake* would be fueled, checked, and re-checked twice before anyone else arrived. Oskar had turned out to be the kind of man who expressed love through pre-flight inspections, and after everything his lungs had been through in the drowned corridor, nobody was going to take that from him.

Ines found him on the dock, crouched at the stern with a flashlight in his teeth, reading the fuel line like scripture.

"She's fine," Ines said.

"I'll decide when she's fine." He spat the flashlight into his hand and squinted up. "You're wearing the good coat."

"It's a memorial."

"You're wearing the coat you bought specifically so people would stop asking about the blacklist."

"That's also called dressing for an occasion."

Oskar grinned — a crooked, gap-toothed thing that had taken most of the year to come all the way back — and went back to his fuel line. Ines left him to it. Some prayers were mechanical.

* * *

Tam arrived at seven with her grandmother's charts rolled under one arm and her little sister, Pell, riding on the cart beside her, legs swinging, wearing a life jacket two sizes too big because Tam did not negotiate on safety equipment, ever, not even for family, not even for a trip that wouldn't leave sight of the dock.

"River's running clean," Tam reported, hopping down. "Clear to the second shelf. You could count pebbles at ten feet."

"After the flood last year?"

"After the flood," Tam agreed. "Grandmother used to say the river washes its face before company comes."

Pell, who was nine and took everything literally, leaned over the edge of the dock and peered down. "It doesn't have a face."

"It does if you're polite enough to look," Tam said, and Pell considered this with the gravity of a harbor master.

Ines watched them argue their way up the ramp toward the office and felt the old ache — the one that lived where the shame used to live — shift and settle into something else. Not gone. Ines suspected it never fully would be. But a year ago that ache had been a stone Ines carried everywhere; now it was more like ballast. It kept the boat steady.

The descendants were coming on the noon packet from downriver. Forty-one of them, by Sefa's final count, though Sefa had counted three times and gotten three different numbers because half the families had kept adding relatives right up until the permits were signed. They were coming from four countries and one floating settlement that technically belonged to none of them, and they were coming to stand above the falls and say names into the water.

Names that hadn't been spoken publicly in forty years. Names that Ironwake's founders had tried to drown along with the convoy, along with the testimony, along with Mara Vell's voice sealed in a case in a flooded hull behind a waterfall.

The case sat in Ines's office safe now, or rather, a certified copy of its contents did; the original had gone to the international tribunal, where it had done its slow, grinding work. Corven Ashe had resigned in March. The word "resigned" was doing heroic labor in that sentence, but he had walked out of the hearing room upright, and Ines supposed that was more than the hundred and six souls of the *Vellmark Star* had been given.

Ironwake's permits were voided. The salvage fleet had gone downriver in April, one barge short, sold to pay fines. And Rivergate — the town that kept its head down — had spent a week arguing about whether to put up a plaque, and then another week arguing about what it should say, and had finally settled on something so brief it satisfied everyone: *They were here. We remember.* Ines thought it was the best piece of public writing the town had ever produced.

* * *

Sefa arrived at nine, which was late by Sefa standards, which meant something had happened at the dock office, which meant Ines went looking.

Found them standing at the counter with a telegram in one hand and an expression Ines had learned to read across twenty years and one catastrophic estrangement: jaw set, eyes bright, somewhere between fury and laughter.

"What is it?"

"The Vellmark Historical Society," Sefa said, "has voted to make me an honorary member of the Order of the River."

"There's an order of the river?"

"There is now. Apparently there wasn't before, so they invented it, and I'm the first member, and the medal is being cast as we speak, and—" Sefa put the telegram down flat, smoothing it with both hands like it might bolt. "And they've asked me to read the roll of names today. All hundred and six."

Ines was quiet a moment. "Can you?"

"They asked me, Ines. Me. Two years ago I was carrying Ironwake's dive plans in a waterproof tube and telling myself it was just a job." Sefa laughed, short and unsteady. "I keep waiting for somebody to check my references."

"Somebody did. It was the river."

"That's not a reference, that's a witness."

"Same thing, sometimes."

Sefa looked at Ines then, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because there was still a seam between them — there always would be, a scar tissue of things said in the rain outside the Saltcellar Inn — but a year of shared work, shared tides, and shared terrible coffee had made the seam strong. Forgiveness, Ines had come to understand, wasn't a door you walked through once. It was a boat you both had to keep bailing.

"Read the names," Ines said. "All hundred and six. Take your time. The river's not going anywhere."

"No," Sefa said softly, looking out the window at the bright water. "It really isn't."

* * *

The packet came in at noon, riding high and heavy with passengers, and Rivergate's entire working waterfront turned out to watch it dock, which was to say: eleven people, a dog, and Pell, who had appointed herself official welcomer and was handing out wildflowers she had absolutely picked from someone's garden.

The descendants came ashore blinking in the sunlight — old men in Vellmarki wool despite the heat, young women with the same dark hair Tam's grandmother had, children clutching paper boats. They had grown up scattered across the world, citizens of a country that existed only in kitchen conversations and hand-embroidered table linens, and now they stood on a dock in a border town most maps still got wrong, and one of the old women looked at the gorge rising blue and green above the rooftops and began, quietly, to weep.

Tam's grandmother would have been among them. She had died in February, in her sleep, in the house above the chandlery, with her charts spread out on the bed around her like wings. Tam had inherited the charts, the tea card, and a letter Ines had only read once, because once was enough:

*To the guide who pulled my chest from the river: you gave my granddaughter a country. Take care of her river.*

Her river. As if it had ever been anything else.

* * *

They ran two boats up the gorge. The *Mara's Wake* carried the elders; Oskar piloted the second with the younger families, narrating the whole way in a voice pitched for tourists even though these weren't tourists — pointing out the heron rookery, the seam of white quartz, the ledge where the current folded over itself "like it's thinking." Pell rode shotgun and corrected his terminology with the confidence of a nine-year-old who had memorized Tam's charts.

At the narrows, where Corven Ashe had once stranded them with the tide rising and the case in his fist, Ines slowed the boat without being asked. Nobody spoke. The rock walls rose sheer and dripping, and the water ran through them clean and quick, carrying nothing but light.

Then the falls.

They'd changed since the wreck was lost. When the seasonal flood had finally claimed the *Vellmark Star* — taking the hull, the corridors, the drowned ledges, everything, folding it all into the gorge's deep gravel — it had also rearranged the falls themselves, splitting the main curtain into two braids around a pillar of black stone. The locals called it the Widow. Ines privately thought of it as a headstone, which was, in the end, what it was.

They moored in the pool below, engines off, and for a long moment there was nothing but the sound of falling water and forty-one people breathing.

The elders came to the bow one by one. They had brought things: a scrap of embroidered cloth, a brass button, a photograph gone soft at the corners, a child's shoe that had never been worn. One by one they spoke names into the spray. Not shouted. Spoken — the way you speak to someone sleeping, or to someone standing just on the other side of a wall.

Sefa read the roll. All hundred and six, unhurried, giving each name its whole weight, and the river took each one and carried it down through the pool, past the Widow, out toward the sea, and Ines stood at the helm with the good coat buttoned to the throat and let the water do what water does.

When it was done, the old woman who had wept at the dock turned to Ines.

"They say you found her voice," she said. "Mara Vell's voice."

"We found the case. She did the rest."

"And the wreck? They say the river has it now."

"The river has it," Ines said. "And the river forgets nothing. That's not a comfort, exactly. But it's not nothing, either."

The old woman smiled through the last of her tears. "No," she said. "It's not nothing. My mother used to say the dead aren't gone, they're just upstream." She touched the rail, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a shoulder. "Thank you for rowing us here."

* * *

They came back down in the gold part of the evening, and Tam had the grill going on the dock, and somebody had produced a fiddle, and the memorial became — as memorials among river people always do — a party, because grief and gratitude are the same current viewed from different banks.

Oskar danced with three grandmothers and lost to all of them. Pell fell asleep in a coil of rope with a paper boat still clutched in her fist. Sefa sat on the edge of the dock with the honorary membership telegram folded in their breast pocket, feet dangling over the water, and Ines went and sat next to them, and for a while they just watched the river run.

"You know what I keep thinking?" Sefa said eventually. "A year ago I was on a corporate vessel with sonar and a budget, and I couldn't find a damn thing worth finding. Now I've got a rowboat, a partner with a criminal-adjacent reputation, a pilot who inspects fuel lines like they're rosary beads, and a teenager who owns the only accurate maps of this gorge." They shook their head. "Best crew I ever served with."

"Careful. Oskar will hear you and get emotional, and then he'll inspect something."

"He already re-inspected the grill twice."

"See?"

Sefa laughed, and the sound went out over the water, and Ines thought: *home made, not found.* That was the whole of it. You didn't find a place like this. You built it out of the people who showed up — the dockhand with her grandmother's charts, the broken pilot learning to breathe, the partner who chose the truth over the paycheck, the kid asleep in the rope. You built it plank by plank, tide by tide, and then you kept bailing.

The lanterns came on along the waterfront, one by one, and the party went on without them, and the river ran high and clean beneath it all, carrying the light downstream.

"Same time next year?" Sefa asked.

Ines looked out at the dark water, at the falls hidden somewhere upstream behind the black shoulders of the gorge, holding whatever it held, keeping every name.

"Same time next year," Ines said. "The river's expecting us."
