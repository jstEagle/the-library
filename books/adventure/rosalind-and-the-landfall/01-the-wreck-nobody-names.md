## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The tide chart said nothing was there. That should have been Mirabel's first warning.

She'd been diving the coast of Isla Verde since she was nine years old, when her grandmother first strapped a tank onto her small back in the shallows off Punta Salada and told her, *the sea doesn't lie, mija. People lie. The sea just shows you what it kept.* Twenty-two years later, Mirabel Quintana made her living on that principle — salvage diving, mostly: lost anchors, dropped cargo, the occasional outboard engine some fisherman wept over. Honest work on honest water.

But the reef six kilometers east of Cabo Hondo wasn't honest water. It was a blank spot on every chart she owned — not marked as dangerous, not marked at all, which was stranger. Good cartographers marked hazards. They marked reefs and wrecks and shallow bars with the diligence of priests recording sins. A blank space meant someone had decided there was nothing worth knowing, and the sea around Isla Verde was never nothing.

The morning she went out, the water lay flat as poured glass, the kind of calm that came before the trade winds woke up mean. Her boat, the *Alba*, sat low in the swells with her usual cargo of tanks and line and the rusted patience of a woman who worked alone because crews asked questions.

"Nobody names this reef," she said to the water, an old habit of talking to the sea like a colleague. "So I'll name it myself."

She geared up on the rocking deck, checking her gauges twice, spat in her mask and rinsed it the way her grandmother had taught her, and rolled backward into a blue so clear it felt like trespassing.

* * *

Below ten meters, the reef fell away in terraces, coral shelves stacked like the ruins of some drowned amphitheater. Parrotfish ground their teeth against the stone. A hawksbill turtle regarded her with ancient indifference and paddled off. It was beautiful, and it was wrong.

Mirabel had dived enough of Isla Verde's waters to read them the way farmers read clouds, and everything here read wrong. The coral grew in patterns interrupted by old scars — long, straight gouges too regular for anchors, too deep for storm drag. She followed one down the second terrace, fingers trailing above it without touching, and noticed the fish avoided this whole section. Schools parted around a zone of emptiness the way crowds part around a man shouting in the street.

At eighteen meters she saw the shadow.

It didn't resolve all at once. First it was just a darkness in the blue ahead, a suggestion of geometry where geometry shouldn't be. Then the light shifted as she kicked closer, and the shape came forward out of the murk like something surfacing from sleep: steel. Riveted plates furred with twenty years of barnacle and coral, but steel, unmistakably, running in clean naval lines longer than her boat, longer than three of her boats laid end to end.

A ship. A big one. Sitting upright on the sand at the base of the reef, half-buried at the stern, its bow angled up toward the light like a swimmer frozen mid-stroke.

Mirabel hung in the water above it and felt the cold come through her wetsuit that had nothing to do with temperature.

There were wrecks all around Isla Verde — fishing boats, a sugar barge, a mail steamer gone down in '54 with everyone on Isla Verde able to recite the names. Wrecks got found. Wrecks got named, mourned, salvaged, stripped, turned into dive sites with hand-painted signs. This hull had no growth pattern of a wreck found young. It had been down long enough for the coral to make peace with it, and no one — not her grandmother, not the fishermen, not the harbor office with its coffee tin of rumors — had ever breathed a word.

Her grandmother had dived these exact coordinates. Mirabel knew it the way you know a scar is yours; the old woman's logs, the ones Mirabel kept in a locked chest she hadn't opened since the funeral, were full of soundings from this stretch of empty sea. Entries dated across decades. Her grandmother had come back here again and again, and never once written why.

Mirabel pushed the thought down where she kept all thoughts about the chest, and swam toward the bow.

Up close, the hull was a city of detail. Coral had colonized the rail stanchions; a school of sweepers poured out of an open porthole like smoke. She ran her glove along the plating, feeling beneath the marine crust for the thing every diver looks for on an unknown wreck: the name. Ships carried their identities on their bows and sterns, welded or painted or bolted, and even the sea usually left a ghost of them.

No name. The bow was blank under the fouling — not worn blank, but *finished* blank, the letters ground away deliberately, the weld scars still visible beneath the coral like healed-over wounds. Someone had erased this ship before the sea could claim the job.

Her breath hitched, bubbles streaming silver past her face. She made herself slow down. Made herself be a professional instead of a woman whose heart was climbing into her throat.

Near the stern, on a plate sheltered by an overhang of coral head, the fouling grew thinner. Mirabel scraped at it carefully with her dive knife, working in short strokes, watching the gray-green crust flake away in little avalanches. Underneath, stamped into the steel itself — not painted, *stamped*, deep and official and meant to outlast paint — was a string of characters:

**NV-1147-K**

And below it, smaller, almost shy: **VERDE STATION**.

She stared at it until the numbers seemed to float. NV. Naval vessel. She knew the registry — every islander with a boat knew the registry the way they knew the saints' days. Her cousin worked the harbor records; Mirabel had helped him alphabetize the microfiche during a slow hurricane season once, out of boredom and family obligation. She had held every naval serial number registered to these waters in her own two hands.

NV-1147-K did not exist.

Not decommissioned. Not lost-with-all-hands, which would have been worse and would have been remembered. It simply wasn't anywhere. No launch record, no assignment, no fate. A ship the size of a chapel, sitting upright in sixty feet of water off a reef that appeared on no chart, with its name chiseled off and a serial number that matched no registry, stamped by hands that wanted it findable only by people who already knew what it was.

*Verde Station.* Not a port she'd ever heard of. Not a name on any map of the island. A station. Like a lighthouse station. Like a listening post.

Mirabel hovered there while her air gauge whispered down, and the unease settled into her bones and made itself comfortable. This was the feeling she'd been taught to trust above all others — not fear exactly, but the diver's animal sense that the story and the water had stopped matching. Her grandmother's voice, thirty years dead in life but alive as gulls in her memory, murmured through her: *when the story and the water disagree, believe the water.*

The water was saying: somebody put this ship here on purpose, and somebody else decided you would never look.

She took out her waterproof slate and copied the serial number in blocky letters, pressing hard enough to dent the plastic. Then, because she was who she was — because curiosity in the Quintana family was less a trait than an organ — she swam the length of the hull one more time, slower, reading it like a page.

Toward the midships section, where the coral grew thickest, she found the anomaly. A section of plating near the waterline was newer than the rest — not new, but newer, replaced sometime in the last few decades, its welds cleaner. And beneath that plate, if her eyes weren't lying to her in the shifting light, the seabed fell away. Not gradually, the way a reef shelf should. There was a dark seam in the rock under the hull, a vertical slot where the sand moved lazily in and out with the surge, breathing.

The ship hadn't run aground on the reef. The ship was parked over something. Anchored, deliberately, above a hole in the world.

Her slate pen trembled slightly as she added a rough sketch: the hull, the seam, the depth readings. Around her the sweepers regrouped and scattered, regrouped and scattered, and somewhere far above her the surface bent and shimmered like a ceiling between two lives.

Her reserve alarm would chirp soon. She gave the wreck one last look — the blind bow, the erased name, the breathing seam — and promised it, silently, the way you promise anything to something that has waited this long: *I'll come back. And next time I'll bring questions you can't drown.*

She ascended slowly, watching the blue lighten, doing her safety stop at five meters while the wreck watched her go with the patience of all buried things.

* * *

The *Alba* rocked gently as she broke the surface beside it. Mirabel climbed the ladder, stripped her mask, and stood dripping on her own deck with the slate clutched in one hand, looking east toward Cabo Hondo where the navy maintained its modest coaling-era station and its modest, well-documented history.

The wind had begun to rise, combing whitecaps out of the channel. On the horizon, right where the charts said there was nothing, a gray patrol boat sat motionless against the brightening sky. Too far to read a name. Close enough that it hadn't been there by accident.

Mirabel looked down at the slate in her hand. **NV-1147-K**, in her own handwriting, already drying at the edges.

Her grandmother had dived this water for forty years and told her everything about the sea — how to read current, how to respect swell, how to hold your breath through panic — and had never once mentioned a ship without a name sitting over a hole in the ocean floor. Forty years of logs in a locked chest, and silence where this reef should have been.

The patrol boat began, very slowly, to move. Toward her, or along the invisible line between her and the reef — impossible to say.

Mirabel started the engine and turned the *Alba* for home, running easy, unremarkable, a salvage diver coming back from empty water with nothing in her hold. But her eyes stayed on the horizon, and the number burned on the slate in her hand like a coal she couldn't put down, and for the first time in her life the sea had shown her something that felt less like a discovery than a summons.

Someone had paid to keep this quiet. She intended to find out how much the truth cost, and who had been paying all along.
