## 3. The Crew of Strays

The harbor at Puerto Sombra woke the way it always did: gulls first, then diesel, then the church bell that nobody had ever gotten around to silencing. Mirabel Quintana stood on the deck of *La Sirena*, her late grandmother's salvage boat, and watched the light come up over the breakwater. The hull below her feet still smelled faintly of engine oil and salt, and beneath both, if she let herself be honest, of the sea cave — of that black water she'd seen through the crack in the naval wreck, the water that had looked back at her.

She'd slept four hours. She'd spent most of them staring at the ceiling of her grandmother's house, listening to the tide, trying to decide whether Commander Reyes' money was a blessing or a muzzle.

It was both. That was the trouble with this island. Everything worth having came wrapped in something you had to swallow.

She needed a crew. Not a big one — big crews talked, and talking was the one thing Reyes had paid her not to do. She needed three people, maybe four, who could keep their mouths shut and their hands steady, and there was exactly one way to find people like that on Isla Verde: you found the ones with debts.

* * *

Tomas Reyes — no relation to the commander, a joke he'd made so many times it had stopped being funny somewhere around his fortieth birthday — lived above his machine shop on the north edge of town, in an apartment that smelled of solder and instant coffee. When Mirabel knocked, he opened the door with a wrench in one hand and a look on his face like a man expecting bad news.

"Quintana." He wiped his hands on a rag that was cleaner than his hands. "If this is about the compressor, I told you I'd have it back by Friday."

"It's not about the compressor."

He studied her face the way he studied engines: patiently, looking for the fault line. Then he stepped back and let her in.

His shop was a cathedral of salvaged things — outboard motors hung from hooks like cured fish, a lathe older than either of them, shelves of parts sorted into coffee tins labeled in his cramped engineer's hand. He cleared a stool for her and put the kettle on without asking, because Tomas had been feeding people since before either of them could remember, back when his father ran the shop and Tomas was small enough to sleep under the workbench while the grown-ups argued about carburetors.

"You dove the reef," he said. It wasn't a question. News traveled fast in a town with one bar and two churches.

"I dove the reef."

"And?"

"And I need someone who can rig lights and pumps and keep a boat's systems honest in bad water. Someone who won't ask questions in front of other people."

Tomas poured the coffee. His hands were scarred and precise, a machinist's hands, and they were not quite steady as he set the cups down. "How much?"

"Double rate. Cash."

"That's a lot of cash for a job nobody's supposed to know about."

"Yes."

He sat down across from her. Up close she could see what the last two years had done to him: the shop mortgaged twice to pay for his mother's treatments, the medical bills stacked in a drawer he pretended didn't exist, the way he flinched when the phone rang because it was usually the bank. He was fifty-one years old and he owned everything and nothing.

"My mother's care home raised its rates again," he said quietly. "Third time this year."

Mirabel said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be cruel.

"If I say yes," Tomas went on, "you tell me straight what we're diving on. Not today. But before I'm underwater with it. I don't need details for other people. I need them for me."

"You'll have them."

He turned his cup slowly on its saucer, once, twice, a man winding himself up. "Your grandmother would've hated this," he said. "Secret jobs. Navy money."

"My grandmother did secret jobs."

"Not like this one." He looked up, and his eyes were kind and tired. "But then, maybe I don't know what she did. Maybe none of us did." He stuck out his hand. "Friday. And I want my compressor back regardless."

* * *

Yolanda Ferrer worked out of a room behind the island archive that was technically a storage closet and practically a kingdom. She was thirty-four, thin as a mast, with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair she never bothered to dye, and when Mirabel walked in she was elbow-deep in a box of shipping manifests from 1911, arguing with a dead customs officer about his handwriting.

"I need someone who reads old paper," Mirabel said, by way of hello.

Yolanda didn't look up. "Everyone needs someone who reads old paper. Nobody pays for it."

"The navy pays."

That got her attention. Yolanda straightened, pushed her glasses down onto her nose, and looked at Mirabel over the rims with the flat appraisal of a woman who had spent fifteen years watching officials destroy or disappear documents that inconvenienced them.

"The navy doesn't pay archivists," she said. "The navy burns archives. I've seen it. 1998, the hurricane records, gone overnight. So either you're lying to me, or the navy wants something read that it hopes nobody else can read." She took off her glasses entirely. "Which is it?"

Mirabel had planned to be careful. She'd planned to reveal things in stages, the way you fed line to a diver. But standing in that closet, surrounded by the island's whole memory in boxes, she heard herself say: "A ship that isn't in any registry. A serial number that matches nothing. And a cave underneath it that isn't on any chart, any survey, or any map going back to the Spanish."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a moped buzzed past and faded.

"A colonial settlement," Yolanda said slowly. "Underwater. Preserved."

"Maybe. I saw a door. Stone stairs. Air."

Yolanda sat down on a crate of parish records, and Mirabel watched something happen in her face — not greed, not fear, but hunger, the pure academic hunger of a woman who had spent her whole career cataloguing a history everyone else had agreed to forget. Isla Verde's archives were full of gaps. Whole decades missing. Ledgers with pages razored out. Yolanda had written three letters to the national historical institute about it and received no answer to any of them.

"Do you understand what you're describing?" Yolanda said. "Do you understand what that would mean? Not for the navy. For *us*. For the island. Our founding records might be down there. Actual paper. Actual names."

"That's why I need you."

"And if the navy wants it kept quiet?"

"Then we document it anyway. Quietly. You copy everything. Twice."

Yolanda was quiet for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, dry sound with no humor in it. "I have eleven thousand dollars in student debt from a degree I finished by correspondence because I couldn't afford to leave the island. I catalogue other people's donations for a salary the church subsidizes." She stood, brushed dust off her skirt, and began pulling notebooks off her shelf with sudden decision. "I'll need my own camera. Film, not digital — digital can be altered. And acid-free sleeves. And you're paying for the sleeves."

"Done."

"And Mirabel—" Yolanda paused at the door, and for a moment the archivist fell away and there was just a woman looking at another woman with something close to warning. "Whatever your grandmother sealed in those ledgers of hers, whatever silence she bought — you should know that silence has a shelf life. Paper outlives promises. It always does."

* * *

Kip Osei was seventeen and already the best free diver Mirabel had ever taught, which was saying something, because Mirabel had been taught by Odalys Quintana, and Odalys Quintana had been a legend. Kip hung around the dock every afternoon doing odd jobs for anyone who'd pay, sleeping in the sail loft some nights, sending money home to a grandmother in Barranquilla with a lung condition that got worse every winter. Kip's dream was simple and enormous: a dive certification, a boat of their own someday, a name on a hull instead of a nickname on a dock.

Mirabel found them scrubbing barnacles off a tourist catamaran, working shirtless in the afternoon heat, moving through the water beside the hull like they'd been born in it.

"I've got a job," Mirabel said.

Kip surfaced, pushing wet hair out of their eyes, grinning. "Legal?"

"Mostly."

"Paid?"

"Very."

Kip climbed the ladder, and Mirabel watched them weigh it — the same weighing she'd seen in Tomas, in Yolanda, the arithmetic of need that ran underneath everything on this island like a current. Kip's grandmother's medicine cost more each month. Kip's certification course cost money Kip didn't have. Kip had been saving for six years in a coffee tin buried under the sail loft floor, and Mirabel knew, because Odalys had known, and Odalys had told her before she died.

"What's the 'mostly'?" Kip asked.

"Navy contract. Quiet work. Deep work."

Kip's grin faded into something more serious, and for a moment they looked startlingly like Odalys — that same stillness divers got when the water went from friendly to otherwise. "Deep like the reef off Punta Hueso?"

Mirabel felt the words land in her chest. "You've heard about the reef."

"Everybody's heard. Divers go out there, boats come back empty, navy says the charts are wrong." Kip shrugged. "My gran says the sea keeps a mouth out there and it takes payment."

"Your gran sounds like mine."

"She says yours knew." Kip said it simply, without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "She says Odalys Quintana knew what was out there and dove it anyway, and came back, and never told anybody why."

The gulls cried overhead. The catamaran's owner shouted something from the beach that neither of them acknowledged.

"I can't promise safe," Mirabel said finally. "I can promise trained, equipped, and never alone. And I can promise that whatever we find, you'll be in the room when it gets decided what happens to it. Nobody uses you and sends you home."

Kip looked at the water for a long moment. Then they stuck out a hand calloused from rope and coral, and Mirabel shook it, and that was that.

* * *

They met aboard *La Sirena* that evening, the four of them crammed into the cabin with the hatch open to the last of the light. Tomas brought food, because Tomas always brought food — fried plantains and saltfish wrapped in paper, enough for six people, because Tomas had never learned to cook for fewer. Yolanda spread her notebooks across the chart table. Kip sat cross-legged on the engine cover, eating with the focused seriousness of someone who had learned never to take a meal for granted.

"So," Mirabel said, and laid it all out. The wreck. The serial number. Reyes' double payment and his prohibition. The crack in the reef, the dark water beneath, the stone she'd glimpsed. Everything except the name in her grandmother's logs — that she kept to herself, one small coal held back from the fire, and she hated herself a little for it even as she did it.

Nobody spoke when she finished. The harbor slapped gently against the hull. Out past the breakwater, the sea had gone the color of hammered pewter, calm in the way that made old divers nervous.

"Tide tables," Yolanda said at last, tapping her notebook. "If there's a cave system under that reef, access will be tidal. We need spring low water, slack window, maybe forty minutes, maybe less. I'll pull the almanacs tonight."

"Pumps and lights I can do," Tomas said. "I'll want to re-rig the compressor intake and I'm putting a second regulator on every line. Redundancy or nothing." He glanced at Mirabel. "And I want that story you promised. Before we dive."

"You'll have it."

Kip said nothing for a while. Then: "Slack tide at Punta Hueso comes Thursday week. Spring low. My gran tracks it — she's got a bone ache that predicts it better than the almanac." A pause. "We're really doing this. Against the navy."

"We're really doing this."

Kip smiled then, wide and bright, and it was impossible not to smile back, and for a moment the cabin felt warm in a way that had nothing to do with the fried plantains — four strays around a chart table, bound together by debts and dreams and a secret, which was, Mirabel thought, how most families on this island had been built since the Spanish came.

Yolanda raised her cup of coffee. "To documentation."

"To redundancy," said Tomas.

"To Thursday week," said Kip.

Mirabel lifted her own cup, and the toast landed soft against the cabin walls, and outside the first stars were coming out over a sea that kept its secrets the way the island kept its silences — patiently, and not forever.

It was only later, walking home along the seawall with the tide sighing beneath her, that Mirabel remembered the last line of her grandmother's final log entry, the one she hadn't shown anyone:

*Some doors are closed for the sake of the people behind them.*

Thursday week, she thought. And prayed the door would forgive them for opening it.
