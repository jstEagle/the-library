## 6. First Descent

Kip had never been good at waiting, and slack tide was a kind of waiting that happened underwater, in the dark, with the whole ocean holding its breath.

They anchored *La Sirena* two hundred meters off the reef at 4:40 in the morning, while the sky was still the color of wet slate. Nobody spoke much on the way out. Tomas checked his gauges three times without being asked to. Yolanda sat in the stern with her waterproof case hugged to her chest like a child, and Mirabel stood at the wheel with her jaw set in that way Kip had learned meant she'd already made up her mind and was only waiting for the sea to agree.

The plan — if you could call it a plan — was simple. Slack tide would last maybe forty minutes, the window when the water stopped pulling and pushing and simply hung there, confused between the moon's arguments. That was when the current through the cave mouth would die down enough to swim it. Forty minutes in, forty minutes back, with air pockets as their margin for error.

"Nobody goes past the stairs," Mirabel said, killing the engine. "We see the chamber, we document, we come home. If anyone's gut says turn around, we all turn around. Agreed?"

Four heads nodded. Kip's nodded hardest.

Kip was twenty-three, the youngest aboard by fifteen years, and everyone on this boat knew it. They'd hired them — Mirabel had hired them — off a dock in Puerto Sombra where Kip had been patching hulls for boat owners who paid late and tipped never. Two years of apprenticeship later, Kip still sometimes couldn't believe they were allowed on the water at all, let alone trusted with a dive like this. The debt of it sat in Kip's chest heavier than any tank.

The water took them at 5:15.

Descent was easy. The reef dropped away beneath them in terraces of coral and shadow, and Kip followed Mirabel's fins down through water so clear it felt dishonest, like the sea was showing off before it changed its mind. At eighteen meters the light went from gold to green to a deep bruised blue, and then there it was: the opening.

Kip had seen it from above during the quake-tide two days ago, a black seam beneath the wreck's broken spine, but seeing it from below was different. From below, it wasn't a seam. It was a mouth. Four meters across, ringed in pale stone worn smooth by centuries of current, breathing cold water outward in slow pulses — even now, even at slack, the cave exhaled against every rule of physics Tomas had tried to explain.

Mirabel paused at the threshold and shone her light inside. The beam died about six meters in, swallowed by something thicker than darkness.

She looked back at them. Even through the mask, Kip could read her face: *last chance to be sensible.*

Then she turned and swam in, and Kip followed, because following Mirabel Quintana into impossible places was apparently what Kip did now.

* * *

Inside, the world narrowed to a tunnel of stone and moving light.

Kip kept one hand near the guide line they'd clipped at the entrance — sixty meters of braided rope, their umbilical back to the sane world. The tunnel sloped downward, then leveled, its walls carved not by water alone but by hands; Kip's light caught chisel marks in long parallel rows, deliberate as plowing. Someone had shaped this passage. Someone had wanted it navigable.

At thirty meters in, Yolanda tapped twice on her tank — the signal — and pointed up.

Air.

A pocket of it, trapped in a hollow of the ceiling, silver-bright and trembling. Kip rose into it with the others and pushed back their regulator, and the sound of four people breathing filled the little dome like a congregation. The air tasted old. Not rotten — old, mineral, like the inside of a church that hadn't been opened in a century.

"It's been cycling," Tomas said, voice echoing strangely off the stone. "There has to be another opening somewhere, higher, or the air would've gone bad generations ago."

"Another way in," Mirabel said. "Or another way out." She didn't say which possibility she preferred. She didn't have to.

They descended again, and the tunnel opened.

Kip felt it before they saw it — a change in pressure, in echo, the way sound suddenly had room to stretch. Then their light swept across a wall that shouldn't exist, a dressed stone facade with an arched doorway, and beyond it, rising out of the flooded floor, the tops of buildings.

Kip forgot, for a moment, how to breathe on purpose.

* * *

The chamber was the size of a cathedral and lit — impossibly, mercifully — by shafts of filtered daylight falling through fissures in the rock far overhead, thin as piano wire, laying stripes of green-gold across the water. Where the water ended, the city began.

They surfaced in the middle of it, in a lagoon of glass-still water inside the mountain, and pulled themselves onto a stone quay that ran along the chamber's edge like the dock of some drowned harbor town. And it was a town. Streets. Walls. Doorways. A small plaza with a fountain at its center, dry these three hundred years, its basin furred white with mineral bloom. Roofs of clay tile mostly collapsed but some intact, sagging under stalactites that had grown down through the broken rafters like the mountain slowly swallowing the houses whole.

"Madre de Dios," Yolanda whispered. She had her camera out already, though her hands were shaking too badly to shoot. "It's whole. It's *whole.*"

Tomas walked the quay like a man in a dream, running his glove along a bollard worn smooth by ropes that had rotted away before his grandmother's grandmother was born. "This is Spanish colonial. Look at the masonry — Andalusian bond, see the alternating courses? Sixteen-hundreds, maybe earlier. This should be in pieces. This should be rubble and silt. How is it—"

"The cave sealed fast," Mirabel said quietly. She was standing at the plaza's edge, looking up at the light shafts. "Fast enough that the water came in gently. Slow flood. No storm surge ever reached in here to tear it apart." She turned. "It didn't drown in a day. It drowned in years."

Kip drifted toward the nearest building, drawn the way you're drawn to a lit window in a strange town. The doorway stood open. Inside: a table, overturned but present. Shelves carved into the wall. A clay lamp on the floor, its spout still pointing toward the door as if someone had set it down mid-evening and meant to come back.

Kip's chest did something complicated. Awe, yes — real awe, the kind that made the skin prickle — but underneath it something colder was already uncoiling. Because rooms like this weren't ruins. Ruins were dead things. This was a room that looked *interrupted.*

"How many people lived here?" Kip asked. Their own voice sounded wrong in the stone silence, too loud, too alive.

Yolanda had found the church. It stood at the far end of the plaza, smaller than any church deserved to be, its bell tower truncated by the cavern roof. The doors were gone but the arch remained, and inside, Kip could see the pale shapes of pews still ranked in rows, facing an altar draped in centuries of calcite lace.

"Records said San Telmo del Mar was abandoned after the '68 hurricane," Yolanda murmured, shooting frame after frame. "Struck from the colonial rolls in 1771. 'Loss of settlement, cause unspecified.' Unspecified." She laughed, a short brittle sound. "They wrote it down and then they wrote it *out.*"

"Guys." Tomas was standing at the far side of the plaza, by a stairway cut into the living rock — wide steps ascending into shadow, toward a second level of the cavern none of them had mapped. "There's more up there. There's a lot more up here."

* * *

That was when Kip noticed the sound.

Or rather, noticed that a sound had stopped. Since they'd entered, there had been a low hush in the chamber, water moving somewhere in the dark periphery — the cave breathing, in and out, tide and counter-tide. Kip had tuned it out the way you tune out surf. Now the hush had changed pitch. Deeper. Closer. And under it, faint but unmistakable, a new note: water running over stone, moving *toward* them.

Kip checked their dive computer. 6:02. They'd been down fifty minutes.

"Tide's turning," Kip said.

Mirabel was already at the edge of the quay, light aimed down the flooded tunnel they'd come through. The water there had been clear and still. Now it moved — visibly, steadily, a river sliding inward through the cave mouth, and the light shafts overhead seemed dimmer than they had ten minutes ago, though Kip knew that was impossible. Nothing had changed. Everything had changed.

"We have time," Mirabel said, in the voice she used when she was convincing herself first. "Slack runs long in spring tides. We go now, we're fine. Yolanda — five more minutes on the ledgers, nothing more. Tomas, mark that stairway on the slate. Kip—"

But Kip had stopped listening, because Kip was looking at the water in the street.

It was rising. Not dramatically — a centimeter, maybe two, creeping up the base of the fountain like a slow gray tongue. But streets don't fill evenly. Streets fill according to their lowest point, and the lowest point Kip could see was the very lane they were standing beside, the one that sloped gently down from the plaza toward the flooded quarter, toward the tunnel mouth, toward home.

The water was coming up that street like it knew the way.

"Mirabel." Kip's voice cracked on the second syllable. "The exit. Look at the exit."

The tunnel they'd swum in through was no longer a tunnel. It was a throat, and the throat was filling. The surface of the inflow had climbed to within a meter of the tunnel's ceiling arch, and it was still climbing, and through the arch Kip could see the churn beginning — the slack dying, the current waking, the whole ocean remembering which direction it flowed.

Tomas swore in three languages. Yolanda lowered her camera.

"Everyone breathe," Mirabel said. "Breathe, and think. We planned for this. Air pockets on the route — we marked three. We swim it on the line, single file, me first. It'll be faster going out with the current behind us. Kip, you're second. Tomas third, Yolanda on my count. Nobody panics, nobody bolts the line. We do this calm and we do it together."

Her voice was steady. Her hands, checking her gauge, were not quite.

Kip clipped onto the guide line and looked back once at the little town — at the lamp lying in the doorway, at the pews waiting in the church, at the water sliding patient and inevitable up the street toward all of it. Somewhere above, the light shafts were fading as the sun climbed and the angle changed, and the chamber that had felt like a miracle now felt like a held breath, like a mouth slowly closing around them.

The water touched Kip's boots.

"Go," Mirabel said, and slipped under, and the cave swallowed the light behind her.
