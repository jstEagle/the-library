## 8. Reyes' Deadline

The summons came at dawn, folded into a coffee cup at the Cantina del Muelle like it was contraband. A navy courier — young, sunburned, trying very hard not to look at anyone — set it down beside Mirabel's elbow and said, "Commander Reyes requests your presence. Zero-nine-hundred. Pier office."

Mirabel read the note twice. It was written in the kind of handwriting that had been drilled out of a person at an academy: block letters, no flourish, no warmth. *Regarding the recovery operation. Time-sensitive.*

Tomas, hunched over his second espresso across the table, watched her fold it. "That's not a request," he said. "That's a court date."

"It's a conversation."

"Nobody in a uniform says *time-sensitive* because they want to chat." He drained the cup and stood, rolling his shoulders like a man preparing to lift something heavy. "You want me to come?"

Mirabel thought about it. Reyes had been careful so far — careful in the way of a man walking across ice, testing each step. Bringing Tomas would tell Reyes the crew was a unit. Coming alone would tell him Mirabel was a lone operator, someone who could still be bought, isolated, managed.

"Stay with the boat," she said. "Keep Kip off the water today."

"Kip's already on the water."

"Then keep Yolanda off the water. One of them. Please."

She left him frowning into his empty cup and walked the length of the pier through a morning that smelled of diesel and fried plantains and the green rot of the mangroves at low tide. The navy had taken over the harbormaster's old office — a squat concrete building with a salt-eaten roof — and hung a flag out front that snapped in the trade wind like it was impatient.

Reyes was waiting inside, standing, not sitting. That was deliberate too, Mirabel guessed. He was in working khakis rather than dress whites, sleeves rolled, as if to say *I'm one of you, a practical man.* He was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the deep tan of someone who'd spent decades standing on decks telling other people to dive.

"Quintana." He gestured at a chair. She didn't take it. He nodded as if she'd passed a test. "You dive the reef again Tuesday night."

It wasn't a question, so she didn't answer it.

"I'll be direct with you," he said. "I've been patient. I've been generous. And you've treated both as invitations." He picked up a folder from the desk — thin, which surprised her; she'd expected a brick of surveillance photos — and held it without opening it. "The vessel you found is navy property. Salvage rights were never yours. The survey contract you signed gave you recovery of designated materials and nothing else. You've exceeded scope."

"Designated materials," Mirabel said. "You never told me what they were."

"You didn't need to know."

"That's not how contracts work on this island, Commander. That's how they work in books."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing an argument he'd already had with someone else, years ago, and lost. "Your grandmother," he said, "was easier to work with."

The words landed like a hook under her ribs. Mirabel kept her face still, the way her grandmother had taught her — *the sea reads your face, mija, don't give it weather it can use.* But inside, something turned over. *Easier to work with.* As if it were a thing they'd both known. As if Reyes had sat across from Elena Quintana in this same room, or one like it, and made the same speech.

"You knew her," Mirabel said.

"I knew of her." He set the folder down. "That's not why you're here."

"No," she agreed. "I'm here because you're going to threaten me."

"I'm going to inform you." Reyes opened the folder at last and turned it around. Inside was a single sheet: a demolition order, stamped and signed, with a date. "Seventy-two hours. At the end of which, the site is closed. Permanently. Structural charges, placed by engineers, detonated on a falling tide. The reef shelf above the cave will come down, the cave will be sealed under forty tons of rock, and the wreck will be buried with it. There will be no further dives. By anyone. Ever."

Mirabel looked at the date and did the arithmetic without wanting to. Three days. Seventy-two hours to document a colonial settlement that had survived four hundred years underwater, that her own grandmother had helped bury once already.

"On what authority?" she asked. "A wreck that isn't in any registry. A cave that isn't on any chart. You can't demolish what officially doesn't exist."

Reyes almost smiled. "That's exactly why I can."

* * *

She walked out of the office with the sun too bright and her thoughts moving too fast, and she made herself do the thing her grandmother had drilled into her: when the water closes over your head, stop kicking. Sink. Look around. Find the current.

The current, here, was this: Reyes wasn't threatening her because she'd found the cave. He'd known the cave was there. The demolition order was typed, signed, stamped — it hadn't been drafted in the last two weeks since her first dive. It had been sitting in a drawer for years, maybe decades, waiting for someone to trip the wire. Her grandmother had tripped it once. Mirabel had tripped it now.

Which meant the seventy-two hours wasn't a deadline. It was a countdown they'd started the moment she surfaced with a serial number in her camera.

She was halfway down the pier when the voice found her.

"Quintana! Mirabel Quintana, as I live and breathe."

Dario Cruz was leaning against a piling like he'd grown there, a heavyset man in a shirt open to the sternum and a gold chain that caught the light the way he intended it to. He was fifty-something and built like a barrel, with a laugh you could hear from the far breakwater and eyes that never laughed at all. His boat, the *Perla Negra*, sat three slips down — a converted trawler with a crane on her back and too much new electronics for a man who claimed to be broke.

"Dario."

"You look troubled." He pushed off the piling and fell into step beside her, uninvited, the way he did everything. "Bad news from the man in the nice uniform?"

"How did you know where I was coming from?"

"Mira." He spread his hands. "The navy takes over the harbormaster's office, puts a flag up, and then the only diver on this island who's been diving the empty reef gets summoned? I can count. I own a calculator." He grinned. "Seventy-two hours, is it?"

Mirabel stopped walking. A fisherman hauling ice looked over, looked away, suddenly very busy with his buckets. "You have ears in that building."

"I have ears everywhere. It's cheaper than sonar." Cruz dropped his voice, and the grin faded into something more careful. "I'm going to make you an offer, and I want you to hear all of it before you spit. Because I know you're going to spit."

"Then save your breath."

"Here it is anyway." He held up one thick finger. "You've got something down there. I don't know what — a cargo, a vault, papers, I don't care. The navy wants it gone, which means it's worth keeping. That's the oldest rule in salvage, Mirabel: whatever they bury, they're afraid of." A second finger. "In seventy-two hours it's all rock. Unless somebody gets to it first. You've got the location, the dive plan, the tide windows, and — if the rumors are right — a way in that doesn't go through the front door." A third finger. "I'm offering you two hundred thousand, cash, today, for everything in your head and your grandmother's sea chest. Coordinates, logs, charts. You walk away clean, license intact, debts paid — and I know about the debts, Quintana, the *Perla Negra* has a better credit report than you do — and I take the risk."

"And if I say no?"

"Then in seventy-two hours your grandmother's cave is gravel, and you've got nothing but a story nobody will believe." He shrugged, vast and easy. "And I'll have spent two hundred thousand on nothing, which is why I'm hoping you're smarter than you look."

Mirabel studied him. Under the cologne and the chain there was something else — a tightness around the eyes, a man performing ease the way Reyes performed informality. Cruz wasn't offering two hundred thousand out of confidence. He was offering it out of hunger.

"Who told you about the debts?" she asked.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters a lot, Dario. Because it means somebody's feeding you, and I don't think it's the navy. I think the navy's feeding *you* to me." She stepped closer, and for the first time since she'd known him — since the days when he'd crewed for her grandmother and been thrown off the boat for reasons nobody would say out loud — Cruz's grin flickered. "You're not the shark here. You're the chum. Somebody wants to know what I'll do under pressure, and you're how they're measuring it."

For a long moment the only sound was the flag snapping on its pole and the ice man's shovel scraping the hold.

"You always were Elena's girl," Cruz said finally, and there was something in it that wasn't mockery — something that sounded almost like grief. "She said the same thing to me once. Almost the same words." He stepped back, hands rising in surrender. "The offer stands. Seventy-two hours. You know where to find me."

He walked away down the pier, heavy and unhurried, and Mirabel stood in the sun and felt the walls of the hour glass pressing in.

* * *

The *Salomé* sat at her mooring in the late afternoon light, and the crew was waiting for her on deck, which told Mirabel that Tomas had already told them everything, which told her that Tomas had read her face at breakfast and decided the time for secrets was over. Good. She was tired of deciding.

Yolanda sat cross-legged on the hatch cover with her documentation folders spread around her like a card game she was losing. Kip perched on the rail, wet to the waist, smelling of the harbor, having clearly disobeyed the instruction about the water. Tomas leaned against the wheelhouse with his arms folded.

"Seventy-two hours," Mirabel said, without preamble. "Then charges. The cave gets sealed under the reef. Permanently."

Nobody gasped. She'd expected gasping. Instead Yolanda's pen stopped moving, and Kip's face went through something complicated — fear and fury fighting for the same ground — and Tomas just nodded slowly, like a man confirming a diagnosis.

"They've had that order typed up for years," Mirabel went on. "It wasn't a response to us. It was a contingency. My grandmother tripped the same wire in — I don't know, the sixties, the seventies. Reyes knew her. He said—" she heard her own voice roughen and pushed through it, "—he said she was easier to work with."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere across the harbor a church bell rang the hour, wrong, as the island's bells always were.

"So that's it," Kip said at last. "They win? They blow up four hundred years of history because it's inconvenient, and we what — go home?"

"We have seventy-two hours," Yolanda said quietly. She'd been turning a photograph over in her fingers — the chamber, the stairway, the roofline of the sunken town rendered in Kip's camera. "The full documentation would take a month. A proper archaeological survey, six. But we don't need the full survey. We need enough." She looked up at Mirabel. "Enough to make the destruction a crime instead of a rumor. Enough that when the charges go off, there's a record that can't be drowned. Ledgers, letters, photographs with scale bars, the manifest if we can reach it. Enough that some archivist in twenty years can prove what was lost and who lost it."

"That's a lot of dives," Tomas said. "The tide windows alone—"

"Then we dive every window," Kip said. "All three days. Slack tide, night, whatever the sea gives us."

"And Cruz?" Tomas asked. "He'll be watching. He's always watching."

Mirabel looked out across the harbor. The *Perla Negra* rode at her slip, her deck lights just coming on against the blue dusk, and Mirabel found herself thinking about Cruz's face when she'd said *you're the chum* — the flicker, the grief, *she said the same thing to me once.* Her grandmother had thrown him off her boat. Her grandmother had worked with Reyes. Her grandmother had helped seal the cave. And now Dario Cruz was circling the same waters, hungry and frightened, taking orders from somebody Mirabel couldn't yet see.

Every road out of this led back through Elena Quintana's choices.

"Here's what we do," Mirabel said. "We say yes to nothing and no to nothing. Not to Reyes, not to Cruz. We dive the windows, we document everything Yolanda can carry, and we get into that second chamber before the seventy-two hours run out. The manifest — if there's a manifest, if there's a reason the navy has spent sixty years afraid of a drowned town — I want it in our hands, not theirs, not the sea's. And Kip—"

Kip straightened on the rail.

"—from now on, everything gets copied. Every photo, every note. Two sets. One stays with Yolanda. One goes somewhere no navy officer and no salvager will ever think to look."

Kip grinned — that quick, crooked grin that had made Mirabel take her on in the first place. "Copy that, boss."

Tomas pushed off the wheelhouse and came to stand beside her, and for a moment the four of them stood in the last of the light, a stray crew on a borrowed boat, with the sea going dark and patient around them.

"One more thing," Mirabel said. "Tonight, I'm opening my grandmother's sea chest."

Yolanda looked up sharply. "The locked one?"

"The lock's been broken for years. I just never—" Mirabel stopped. Started again. "I never wanted to know what was in it. Seventy-two hours is long enough to be a coward about that."

The chest sat in the fo'c'sle where it had sat through three owners and two hurricanes, a green steel box with Elena Quintana's initials stenciled on the lid and a padlock that had rusted open sometime before Mirabel inherited it. She knelt in front of it in the lamplight while the crew pretended, badly, to be busy elsewhere.

Inside: dive gloves gone stiff with age. A coil of line. A brass compass with a cracked face. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle as autumn leaves — a logbook. Navy-issue cover. The spine broken from use.

Mirabel opened it to the first page and the handwriting hit her like cold water: the sharp, slanted script she'd seen on a hundred grocery lists and birthday cards, here turned to the business of the deep.

*12 June 1968. Dived the reef at first slack. Found the mouth. It was exactly where the old man said it would be.*

The old man. Mirabel turned pages with hands that were not quite steady. Tide tables. Sketches — good ones, her grandmother had always drawn well — of the reef, the wreck, the cave mouth, the stairs. And then, three pages in, a passage that made the lamplight seem suddenly very far away:

*Reyes' people were on the dock when I came up. They already knew. They showed me the order — the same order they'll show whoever finds it next. I told myself it was for the island's own good. I told myself some doors are better shut. I signed.*

*I don't know if that was wisdom or fear. I have decided never to find out.*

Mirabel sat back on her heels. Outside, the tide was turning, and somewhere beneath the dark water the cave mouth waited with its four hundred years, and the countdown had already begun.

"Boss," Kip said softly from the doorway. "You need to see this. There's a boat running dark off the point. No lights. Heading for the reef."

Mirabel closed the logbook and held it against her chest, and felt the seventy-two hours begin to bleed away faster than any clock could count.

"That'll be Cruz," she said. "Or it'll be whoever's holding Cruz's leash. Either way — nobody sleeps tonight."
