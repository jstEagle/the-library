## 9. Grandmother's Logbook

The sea chest had been sitting in the back of Abuela's workshop for as long as Mirabel could remember, and in all those years she had never once seen it open.

It wasn't hidden. That was the strange part. It sat under the window where the afternoon light came through salt-hazed glass, plain as a church pew, iron-banded, its hasp fitted with a padlock gone green at the seams. As children, she and her cousins had used it as a seat, a table for dominoes, a stage for toy boats. Abuela would watch them climb on it with that particular stillness of hers — not anger, not quite, but a kind of held breath — and eventually she'd say, *Not so hard on the old wood, mijita,* and the game would move elsewhere.

Mirabel had assumed it held rope. Old charts, maybe. Divers accumulate things the way barnacles accumulate on pilings: slowly, uselessly, until the weight becomes part of the structure.

Now she sat on the workshop floor at two in the morning with a cold cup of coffee going slick with oil on the boards beside her, and the chest open in front of her, and it was not rope.

She'd broken the lock. Not out of drama — there was no crowbar, no theatrics, just a stubborn screwdriver and twenty minutes of swearing in the dark after Tomas went home. The hasp screws were soft brass, older than she was, and they gave up their dignity one by one until the lid lifted on a breath of air that smelled like every dive shop on the island: neoprene, kerosene, mildew, and underneath it all something mineral and cold, like the inside of a stone.

Dive gear first, folded with military neatness. A mask whose rubber had stiffened to leather. Weight belt, lead shot sewn into canvas, the stitching done by hand. A slate with writing ghosted into it, illegible now except for one word near the edge: *no*.

And beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed string, a logbook.

Mirabel knew the shape of it before she unwrapped it. Hardbound, navy cloth, the kind sold at the chandlery in Puerto Sombra for forty years without changing design. Her own shelf held eleven of them, filled front to back in her own hand. This one was older. The cover had faded to the color of shallow water over sand, and on the spine someone had written in white paint pen, in a slanted, confident script she would have known anywhere:

**R.Q. — 1961–1964**

Rosa Quintana. Her grandmother. Three years of dives, locked away in a box under a window, while Mirabel grew up reading the other logs like scripture — the ones Abuela kept on open shelves, the ones full of reef names and fish counts and tide tables, the ones that made Mirabel want this life before she could swim.

Those logs had never mentioned this.

* * *

She read the first entries standing up, then sitting, then lying on her stomach on the floorboards with her chin in her hands like a girl with a library book, because that was what it reduced her to. The handwriting was beautiful. That was the thing that undid her first, before any of the content — the sheer confidence of it, letters leaning forward like they were in a hurry to be read.

*March 1961. North reef, the cut past the old cannon site. Visibility poor but the fish don't mind. Took the boys out for their first blue-water run; Emilio panics, Tomás (the younger) is a natural. Charged them nothing. Should have charged them double for the grief.*

It was ordinary. That was the second undoing. Page after page of ordinary — weather, bottoms, catches, the small economies of a woman making a living in a man's trade and doing it better than most of the men. Mirabel had heard the stories her whole life: Rosa Quintana, who dove deeper than the navy instructors, who once brought up an anchor from sixty feet by herself because no one else on the boat could be bothered. The legend. And here was the legend's own hand, complaining about a leaking regulator and a client who got seasick in flat calm.

Then, on the fourth page of June 1962, the ordinariness stopped.

*June 14. Went out past the north reef where the chart says nothing is. Something is.*

Mirabel's mouth went dry. She looked at the window, at the black glass, at her own reflection ghosted over the night outside, and then made herself keep reading.

*The navy survey boat was out again Tuesday. They told the harbor master they're mapping currents. Nobody maps currents for three weeks. I followed their buoy line down on the ebb and found their marker chain running into a crack in the reef that isn't on any chart I've ever seen or drawn. Went in thirty feet. Air pocket. Stone work. Cut steps.*

*I know this coast like my own teeth. There are no cut steps on this coast.*

*Going back with lights.*

There was a gap then — a torn page, edges ragged, deliberately ripped. Mirabel ran her thumb over the stubs of it and felt her heart do something complicated. Someone had removed days. Days between finding carved stairs underwater and whatever came next.

The next surviving entry was dated nine days later, and the handwriting had changed. Not worse. Tighter. Smaller. Like the writer was trying to take up less room on the page.

*June 23. I have seen it now, all of it, or enough. A town. Under the rock, behind the wreck they won't name. Streets. A chapel with its bell still hanging. Houses with their doors shut like the people just stepped out to check the nets. Everything dry where it's dry, preserved where the water took it, like the sea decided to keep rather than destroy.*

*The officers found me inside. I thought they'd arrest me. Instead they bought me lunch and asked me questions for four hours, and the questions were not about the town. They were about who else knows. Who I've told. Whether my crew saw the entrance. Whether I can keep my mouth shut.*

*They offered me money. I laughed at them. Then they said the words "for the island's own good" and explained about the cargo, and I stopped laughing.*

Mirabel turned the page. The paper felt warm under her fingers, which was impossible, and she knew it was impossible, and she felt it anyway.

*What's down there isn't treasure in the way people mean it. It's a manifest. A record. Things certain families put away in 1740-something and paid to have forgotten, and the navy has been looking for it ever since because what's written in those ledgers makes some of the big names on this island into thieves and worse. If it comes up, half the old money goes on trial from the grave, and the other half buys judges to make sure it doesn't matter. Either way, the island tears itself apart, and the men holding the knives will be from off-island.*

*So they say seal it. For the island's own good.*

*I said yes.*

The words sat there. *I said yes.* Two words, small and neat, and Mirabel read them perhaps ten times, and each time they landed somewhere new — first as betrayal, then as confusion, then, horribly, as recognition.

Because she had stood in Commander Reyes' office six days ago and listened to him offer her double rate for silence, and she had walked out furious and clean, and she had thought: *I am not the kind of person who takes that bargain.* She had thought it in her grandmother's workshop, surrounded by her grandmother's tools, wearing her grandmother's trade like a second skin.

And here was the proof that the bargain was inherited furniture. That it had been offered before, in this same room maybe, to this same surname, and accepted.

* * *

Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, and the palm behind the workshop scraped its fronds against the tin roof in long slow strokes, like something trying to get in. Mirabel lit the lamp because the bulb overhead had begun to flicker, and the yellow light made the pages look older, more honest somehow, and she kept reading.

*July 1962. They've posted the reef as a hazard. Blasting scheduled for the autumn. I asked what happens to the town and the officer — Vargas, his name is Vargas, he has soft hands and doesn't dive — said "the sea keeps what the sea keeps." I asked if that was meant to comfort me. He said it was meant to close the conversation.*

*I went down again anyway. Alone, at slack, through the crack. I wanted to see it once with my own eyes before they close it, so that when I'm old I'll know whether I helped bury a crime or prevent a war.*

*It's beautiful. God forgive me, it's beautiful. There's a courtyard with a fig tree grown through the floor of a house, and the tree is dead and white as bone, and the light comes down through the crack in shafts like the church windows at Easter. I stood in a street that hasn't felt rain in two hundred years and I thought: this belongs to us. To the island. Not to the navy, not to the families in the ledger, to US. And then I thought: and that is exactly why they can't let it out. Because everyone would come. And everyone coming would be the end of it.*

*I sealed the inner passage myself. Showed them where the siphon runs, how the tide works, where to set the charges so the entrance closes natural-looking. My hands did it. These hands.*

Mirabel looked at her hands. In the lamplight they were her grandmother's hands — the same broad knuckles, the same scar across the left palm from a barnacle cut that had gone bad in '09. She flexed them and watched them obey, and hated them a little, and couldn't.

*August 3. The blasting is done. The crack is closed. I dove the site after to confirm and there's nothing to find now but rubble and a hull the charts will call empty. The fish are already back. Fish forgive faster than I will.*

*Emilio asked me today why the north reef is off-limits. I told him currents. He believed me. Everyone believes me. That's the payment they gave me instead of money — the trust of everyone I love, spent like it was mine to spend.*

*For the island's own good. I write it here so that someday, if anyone reads this, they'll know those words were spoken to me by a man with soft hands, and that I carried them down into the dark and used them as mortar.*

*If you're reading this, and you're a Quintana, and you've found the place again — I'm sorry. I truly don't know if I was wrong. Check the ledgers. Read what's in the second chamber. Then decide better than I did.*

Mirabel closed the logbook.

The workshop was very quiet. The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the hill, the sea worked at the seawall with its patient, indifferent sound, the sound she had slept to her entire life, the sound that had always meant safety and now meant something with a mouth.

She sat for a long time with the book in her lap, feeling the shape of the betrayal settle into her bones and take its place beside the grief that had lived there since the funeral. It wasn't a clean feeling. She wanted it to be clean — wanted to be angry at a coward, at a sellout, at a woman who traded a town for quiet. But the entries wouldn't let her. Every page showed a woman who understood exactly what she was doing, who weighed it, who chose with open eyes and then spent thirty years paying for it in a currency nobody else could see. The locked chest under the window. The held breath when children climbed on it. The shelves of ordinary logs displayed like an alibi.

Abuela hadn't hidden the truth. She'd curated it. Left it where a granddaughter would someday need it, locked just firmly enough that opening it would be a decision.

*Check the ledgers. Read what's in the second chamber. Then decide better than I did.*

Yolanda had photographed documents in the first chamber — manifests, letters, the recurring name. But the second chamber, the vault Reyes' deadline was racing toward, remained unopened. And now Mirabel understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the ears, that the vault wasn't just cargo. It was testimony. It was the reason a woman with soft hands had wanted it buried, and the reason her grandmother had helped him, and the reason the navy was willing to dynamite a national coastline rather than let it surface.

Her radio crackled on the bench. She reached for it out of habit, and Kip's voice came through, low and fast, the way it got when the kid was excited and trying not to show it.

"Boss? Sorry — late, I know. Yolanda finished cross-referencing the chamber documents tonight. She says the ledger entries stop in 1742 mid-sentence, like whoever was writing got pulled away. And she found a reference to a *second* room — 'the strong room beneath the chapel' — with its own seal. Boss, she thinks the chapel floor might be a lid."

Mirabel looked at the logbook. At the entry dated July 1962. *I sealed the inner passage myself.*

Her grandmother had known where the strong room was. Had stood in it, maybe. Had written *check the ledgers* like a woman leaving a map for someone she loved and couldn't warn.

"Kip," Mirabel said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "How soon can we be ready to go back down?"

A pause on the line, the sound of the kid doing math against a tide table.

"Slack's at oh-six-forty. We could be ready by then."

"Be ready by then."

She set the radio down, wrapped the logbook back in its oilcloth — then stopped, unwrapped it, and tucked it instead into the waterproof bag she always packed for dives. Some inheritances weren't meant to stay in chests.

Through the window, far to the northeast, the horizon over the north reef was beginning to gray. Hours yet until slack, hours yet until the crack in the world opened again — and somewhere below it, beneath a chapel floor her grandmother had once stood upon, the truth waited with its seal unbroken.

Mirabel packed her kit in the dark, working by touch, the way she'd been taught by a woman who had taught her everything except this.
