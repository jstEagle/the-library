## 10. Dario's Price

Tomas heard the engine before he saw the boat.

It was past two in the morning, and he was supposed to be asleep in the wheelhouse bunk of the *Mariposa*, but the compressor had been running hot all week and he'd stayed up to nurse it, hands black with grease, a radio playing low. The sound came across the water the way engines do at night — no shape to it, just a pressure, a wrongness in the dark south of them where there should have been nothing but open sea and the long swell rolling in from the deep water.

He killed the radio and listened.

The *Mariposa* lay at anchor off Punta Hueso, three hundred meters from the reef line, riding a gentle chop. Mirabel had wanted to run one more survey pass at first light — mapping the siphon channels they'd found on the last dive, the ones that had nearly swallowed Kip and Yolanda — and Reyes' deadline hung over all of them like weather. Sixty-two hours now. Tomas had done the arithmetic so many times it had worn a groove in him: sixty-two hours to document what might take six months, and then charges, and then nothing but a scar on the seafloor and a story nobody would be allowed to tell.

The engine sound grew. Then stopped.

That was worse. A boat that approaches you at night and then goes quiet is making a decision about you.

Tomas climbed out of the wheelhouse onto the deck, barefoot, still holding the wrench he'd been using. The moon was down to a fingernail, half-swallowed by cloud coming in from the east — storm weather, maybe days out yet, the kind of sky that made the sea go flat and oily and expectant. He could just make out a shape against the darker water: a low hull, no running lights, sitting dead in the water perhaps eighty meters off their port beam.

He knew that silhouette. Everyone on Isla Verde knew it. *La Sirena Negra*, Dario Cruz's boat — fast, mean, patched with fiberglass over old wounds, the way Cruz himself was patched together out of debts and grudges.

"Tomas." Kip's voice, low, from the aft deck. The kid hadn't been sleeping either; they never did well at anchor, said the water talked too much. They were standing by the dive locker in a sweatshirt, hair wild, eyes already wide awake and fixed on the dark shape. "Is that—"

"Yeah."

"Should I wake Mirabel?"

Tomas weighed it. Mirabel had finally gone below around midnight, after twenty hours of chart work, and she needed the sleep the way an engine needs oil. But there was a colder calculation underneath that, and he was honest enough with himself to see it: he didn't want her to know how afraid he was, and he knew his face would show it.

"Not yet," he said. "Maybe they're just anchored. Maybe they're fishing."

"At two in the morning. No lights. Right on top of us."

"Fishing," Tomas said again, without conviction, and Kip gave him a look that said they'd known him too long for that.

They watched the dark hull together. Ten minutes. Fifteen. It didn't move. It just sat there like something patient, and Tomas found himself thinking about the way Cruz had looked at Mirabel in the cantina three nights ago, when he'd made his offer — buy the coordinates, buy the manifest notes, name your price, *the navy's going to bury it anyway, querida, why should you be the one buried with it?* — and the way Mirabel had said no. Not angrily. Just no, final as a depth reading, and the way Cruz had smiled afterward, like a man who has already decided the answer doesn't matter.

"They're moving," Kip whispered.

A tender had come off the dark hull's far side — Tomas hadn't even seen it launch, which meant it had been ready, which meant this wasn't improvisation. It came across the water with its engine barely idling, a gray smear against the gray sea, and there were shapes in it. Three. Maybe four. One of them stood up as it closed the distance, and even in the bad light Tomas recognized the build, the roll of the shoulders, the man who ran Cruz's deck crew — Emilio Vargas, who'd once broken a man's arm over a disputed mooring fee and paid the fine in cash, counting the bills out slow, smiling the whole time.

"Tomas." Kip's hand found his sleeve. "Tomas, we have to wake her now."

"Yes," Tomas said. "Go. Wake her. Wake Yolanda too. Don't turn on any lights."

Kip went. Tomas stayed at the rail, because someone had to stand at the rail, because if you turned your back on a thing like this it only got bolder. He raised the wrench slightly, not as a threat, just so it existed in the world where the tender could see it.

"You're anchored in a working channel," Emilio called up, conversational, as the tender bumped against the *Mariposa*'s hull. His voice carried flat across the water. "We're asking you to move."

"There's no channel here," Tomas said. "There hasn't been a channel here since the charts were drawn."

"Charts change." The tender's fender squeaked against the hull. Hands came up — not grabbing, not yet, just resting on the gunwale of the *Mariposa*, casual as men leaning on a fence. "Cruz wants to talk to your captain. Man to man. Woman to woman. Whatever."

"She's asleep."

"Then wake her up, hermano. We've got all night." Emilio smiled up at him, and in the thin light his teeth looked like something washed ashore. "Or we can come up and look around ourselves. You've got a lot of nice equipment on this deck. Compressors. Sonar unit. That camera housing alone is worth more than my truck."

And there it was. Not a negotiation. An inventory.

Tomas felt fear arrive in his body the way cold water arrives in a wetsuit — all at once, everywhere, unavoidable — and underneath the fear, something harder. He thought of the chamber under the reef, the stone stairs, the roofs that had waited four hundred years under the sea for someone to come back. He thought of Yolanda's careful gloved hands turning the pages of ledgers older than every nation that claimed these waters. He thought of Kip, nineteen years old, who had signed on because Tomas had promised them — stupidly, wonderfully — that this would be the work of a lifetime.

This was the work of a lifetime. All of it. Even tonight.

"I'm going to ask you once," Tomas said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out, "to take your hands off my boat."

* * *

Mirabel came up the companionway like weather.

She didn't shout. That was the thing about her that Tomas had learned in eight years of diving together — the louder other people got, the quieter she became, until her voice dropped into a register that made the air itself feel thinner. She crossed the deck barefoot in a t-shirt and shorts, took in the tender, the hands on her gunwale, the dark hull hanging off the beam, and said, "Emilio. Tell Dario I'm not selling."

"He figured you'd say that." Emilio didn't remove his hands. "He said to tell you the offer doesn't expire. It appreciates. And he said—" the smile widened, "—he said to remind you that boats are insured, but reputations aren't. Navy liaison finds out you've been running night ops inside a restricted zone, that salvage license of yours isn't worth the paper. He wouldn't have to do anything. Just make a call."

"Then he should make the call," Mirabel said. "Because if he does, everything I've documented goes to the archive office in the same envelope, with a cover letter naming names. Tell him that. Tell him exactly that."

For a moment — just a moment — something flickered behind Emilio's eyes that wasn't confidence. Cruz hadn't counted on that. Cruz thought silence was a currency everyone spent the same way, and he'd built his whole life on the assumption that everyone had a price, and he had never once considered that some people would rather burn the money than spend it.

But then the moment passed, because Emilio was not paid to think, and the cloud cover chose that instant to swallow the fingernail moon entirely, and the world went black.

What happened next happened in pieces, and Tomas would spend the rest of his life reassembling them.

A hand closed on his wrist — the wrench arm — and twisted, and the wrench went over the side with a splash that sounded enormous. Someone came over the transom, heavy, boots on deck, and the deck lights came on — *their* deck lights, someone had hit the switch in the wheelhouse, Kip or Yolanda trying to help and only giving the intruders light to work by — and in the sudden glare Tomas saw four of them, five, big men in dark shirts moving with the unhurried coordination of people doing a job they'd rehearsed.

"No!" Mirabel's voice cracked across the deck. "Nobody touches anything—"

Emilio hit her.

It wasn't much, as such things go — a short right hand, almost lazy, the kind of blow men like him had delivered a thousand times — but Mirabel went down against the rail and the sound she made was small and human and it broke something loose in Tomas's chest. Later he would understand that he didn't decide anything in that moment. Eight years of friendship decided for him.

He hit Emilio from the side, shoulder-first, the way you drive through a surge, and they went down together onto the deck plating. Emilio was stronger — heavier, younger, fresh — and Tomas felt the difference immediately, felt his own grip torn away, felt a fist arrive against his ear and the world ring like a struck tank. But Tomas had spent thirty years fighting the sea, and the sea teaches you one lesson above all others: you don't win, you endure, and endurance is its own kind of violence. He got his arms around Emilio's legs and held on. That was all. He held on while boots moved around them, while glass broke somewhere aft — the sonar display, he knew the sound of it, knew it the way you know a bone breaking — while Kip screamed and Yolanda's voice rose sharp and furious from the companionway, while the world dissolved into noise and pain and the taste of blood in his mouth.

Someone kicked him in the ribs. Once, twice, professional, not angry. Through the red haze he saw them working: the camera housing, the spare regulator case, the hard drives from the survey laptop — Yolanda's copies, the documentation, weeks of work lifted into a duffel bag with the brisk efficiency of dockworkers loading cargo. Not vandals. Thieves with a shopping list.

"The drives," he heard Mirabel gasp, from somewhere near the rail. She was up again, blood at her lip, holding herself against the mast. "Tomas, the drives—"

One of the men — the big one, the one who'd come over the transom — paused with the duffel half-full and looked at her. Looked at her the way you look at a chart, calculating.

"Cruz says happy salvaging," the man said. "He says the sea gives and the sea takes."

Then the tender engine roared, and they were gone, over the side and away into the dark, and the *Mariposa* rocked in their wake like something violated, deck lights blazing uselessly over broken glass and scattered tools and the empty bracket where the sonar display had been.

* * *

Yolanda stitched the cut above Tomas's eye at the galley table while Mirabel stood at the rail and stared at the place where *La Sirena Negra* had been, though the dark hull was long gone, swallowed back into the sea it had come from.

"Hold still," Yolanda said. Her hands were steady. Her voice was not quite. "You're lucky. Half a centimeter more and you'd have a souvenir in your eye forever."

"Lucky," Tomas repeated. The word tasted like salt water.

"You want me to lie to you?"

"No." He breathed carefully around the ribs — two of them cracked, he was almost sure, the pain arriving in bright spikes when he drew deep. "No. Tell me the truth."

"The truth is they took the survey drives. Everything from the last three dives. The siphon maps, the photogrammetry, the site grid." Yolanda tied off the stitch and sat back, and for the first time Tomas saw how tired she looked, how frightened, this woman who had faced down archive clerks and naval lawyers without blinking. "The truth is they left the originals in the chamber, because they don't know about them, or they couldn't reach them. So we haven't lost the work. We've lost the shortcut to the work."

"And the truth about the rest of it?" Tomas nodded toward the rail, toward Mirabel's straight back. "Are we still doing this?"

Yolanda looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the eastern horizon had begun to organize itself into a single unbroken line of cloud, stacked and bruise-colored, the leading edge of something with a name already assigned to it somewhere in a weather office.

"Ask your ribs," she said.

At the rail, Mirabel turned. In the galley's spill of light her face was half shadow, the blood at her lip already drying black, and her eyes had gone to that place Tomas recognized from bad dives — the place she went when the situation was worst, where the fear got compressed into something dense and useful, like coal into diamond.

"They didn't take the logbook," she said quietly. "They didn't know to look for it. And they didn't take what's in the second chamber." She touched the rail, lightly, the way you touch a wound to learn its shape. "Dario thinks he bought himself a head start. He thinks we'll spend the next sixty hours rebuilding what he stole." A pause. The wind came off the water and it smelled different now — greener, electric, the smell of a storm deciding. "He's right about the hours. He's wrong about everything else."

Tomas looked at the eastern sky, at the bruised and stacking clouds, and felt his cracked ribs ache with every heartbeat, and understood that whatever window they'd had was closing faster than any of them had planned for.

"We need to dive tomorrow," he said. "Before the weather. Before Cruz. Before Reyes."

Mirabel nodded once.

"Before all of it," she said. "First light. Everyone."
