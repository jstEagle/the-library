## 13. Resurface

The first thing Kip felt was the cold.

Not the sea's cold — they'd been marinating in that for hours, a cold that had stopped registering somewhere past the second siphon, when Mirabel's light had found them huddled in the air pocket with the water climbing their chests and Yolanda reciting tide tables like prayers. This was a different cold. It came from above, through the hatch, down the ladder, into the flooded hold where the three of them had spent forty minutes breathing recycled terror while Tomas bled the compressor line dry and the swell outside hammered the hull like a fist on a door.

It was dawn-cold. The kind that meant the world had kept turning without them.

"Kip." Mirabel's voice, hoarse from salt. "You're up."

Kip hadn't realized they were frozen halfway up the ladder, one hand on the rung, staring at the gray oval of sky above the hatch. They climbed. Their arms shook — not from exertion but from the strange lightness of being alive, of weight returning to a body that had spent the night negotiating with the ocean for every breath.

The deck of *La Sirena* was wreckage. Lines fouled, the port rail cracked where the swell had shoved them against the reef wall, dive gear scattered like the aftermath of an argument. The sky was the color of old pewter, bruised along the eastern horizon, and the wind had a new note in it — a low, gathering hum that Kip had learned to hear growing up on Isla Verde, the sound the island made when it was counting the days until a storm.

Tomas surfaced from the hatch behind them, gray-faced, his forearm wrapped in a bandage that had soaked through twice during the night. He didn't say anything. None of them did. There was nothing to say yet that wasn't either a prayer or a confession.

Yolanda came last, clutching the waterproof case against her chest the way she'd clutched it in the cave, in the dark, in the water — the manifest inside it, the second chamber's secrets, sixty years of somebody's very expensive secret pressed to her sternum like a second heart.

Mirabel stood at the wheel, hair plastered to her skull, eyes red-rimmed and steady, and brought the boat around toward home.

*Home.* Kip almost laughed. The word had never felt so much like a destination with a tollbooth.

* * *

They came into the harbor at half past six, under a sky that couldn't decide between rain and worse. The fleet was still on its moorings; the fish market wouldn't open for another hour; gulls wheeled over the ice house screaming their usual grievances. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly as it should be, which was somehow the most frightening part — as if the night they'd just survived had happened in another country, another century, another set of lives.

Kip was coiling the stern line when they saw the truck.

It was parked at the head of the dock, navy gray, government plates, wipers ticking. Two figures stood beside it in the flat morning light, and even at fifty meters Kip knew one of them by posture alone: Commander Reyes, hands clasped behind his back, feet planted, a man who had never once in his life waited for anyone and was now waiting for them.

The other figure took longer to resolve. Leaner, moving, restless — pacing a short arc around Reyes like a dog circling a fence line. When the light shifted, Kip saw the face and felt their stomach drop through the deck plating.

Dario Cruz.

Cruz, whose crew had shadowed them for two nights. Cruz, whose men had put Tomas's arm in a sling and stolen their spare regulator kit and half their survey equipment off the moored skiff. Cruz, who was now standing shoulder to shoulder with the Commander of Naval Operations for the whole eastern sector, leaning in close, mouth moving, and — Kip watched this happen in slow, sickening detail — Reyes nodding.

"He sold us," Tomas said quietly. He'd come up beside Kip without a sound, which for a man his size was its own kind of miracle. His voice was flat, but Kip could hear the fracture running underneath it. "Or he's selling something. Either way."

"Maybe he's turning himself in," Kip said, without conviction.

"Maybe the sea's made of rum." Tomas spat over the rail. "Get Yolanda below. Whatever's in that case, it doesn't go up this dock unless Mirabel says."

But Mirabel was already at the gunwale, and she'd seen them too. Kip watched her face do something complicated — not fear exactly, though there was plenty of that to go around after last night. Something more like recognition. Like she'd known, all along, that the bill would come due at her own dock, and had only been guessing at the amount.

She tied off at the leeward cleat herself. Her hands were steady. Kip made a private note of that, filed it away the way they filed everything about Mirabel Quintana — proof, again, that composure was a skill you could learn, and that Kip intended to learn it if it killed them.

Reyes stepped forward as the gangplank went down. He was in dress uniform, of all things — pressed, beribboned, absurd at seven in the morning on a working dock — and Kip understood immediately that it was theater. Costumes meant audiences. Audiences meant records. Whatever happened here was meant to be witnessed.

"Captain Quintana." Reyes's voice carried across the water, crisp as a snapped flag. "You are aware that the waters you were operating in last night fall under a restricted naval designation."

"We're aware," Mirabel said. She came down the plank slowly, giving each word room. "We were also aware that three of my crew were trapped inside a flooding cave, Commander. Forgive me for prioritizing."

Something flickered across Reyes's face — Kip caught it, catalogued it: not anger. Calculation. He hadn't known about the trap. Or he had, and he was deciding how much knowing cost him.

"Three crew," Reyes repeated. "I see four of you. Where is the rest of your expedition?"

"Gone," Mirabel said. "Scattered. You know how it is with strays."

Behind him, Cruz laughed — a short, ugly bark — then smothered it when Reyes turned his head. Up close, in the gray light, Cruz looked wrong. Not triumphant-wrong, which Kip had braced for. *Worn*-wrong. Shadows under his eyes deep enough to swim in, a tremor in his hands he kept trying to hide by clasping them. He looked like a man who had spent the night losing arguments with himself.

"Mirabel Quintana," Reyes said, and now the formality dropped away and something colder came through, "by authority delegated under the Coastal Security Act and emergency provisions I don't have time to read you, I am placing your vessel and your operation under naval seizure pending investigation. Your salvage license is suspended effective now. You, your crew, and your equipment will not leave this dock."

"You can't—" Kip started, and Mirabel's hand came up, small and absolute, and Kip shut their mouth.

"The cave," Reyes said. "Tell me what you found."

And there it was. The whole morning balanced on that sentence. Kip watched Mirabel stand at the foot of her own gangplank with the law standing on her dock and the truth sitting in a waterproof case ten feet behind her, and understood with total clarity that whatever Mirabel said next would split their lives into before and after.

"We found what you already know is there," Mirabel said. "A settlement. Colonial. Intact. And records saying it didn't drown by accident."

Reyes went very still. Beside him, Cruz stopped pretending to be bored and stared at Mirabel with an expression Kip couldn't read at all — hunger, maybe, or horror, or the particular exhaustion of a man watching someone else walk straight into the trap he'd been pushed toward first.

"That's a serious accusation," Reyes said finally.

"It's a serious cave."

For a long moment nobody moved. A gull screamed. Somewhere across the harbor, the ice house compressor coughed awake, and the smell of diesel and salt and fish hung over everything, ordinary as bread.

Then Reyes did something Kip did not expect. He looked at Cruz.

"You told me they'd been inside," Reyes said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation wearing a question's clothes, and Cruz flinched like it had teeth.

"I told you what I told you," Cruz said. His voice was rough. "I also told you what happens if you blow that cave with people in it, and you didn't want to hear that part either."

"And I'm telling you now—"

"You're telling me now you've got arrest authority and a demolition schedule and a storm coming in thirty-six hours," Cruz said, louder, and Kip realized with a jolt that Dario Cruz was arguing *for* them. That the man who'd robbed them, shadowed them, bloodied Tomas's arm, was standing on their dock burning his own bridges in real time. "Thirty-six hours, Commander. You want to seal it? Fine. Seal it. But don't stand there talking about investigations you're going to bury along with everything else, because I've seen how you bury things, and I've got debts that say I don't get to pretend anymore."

"Cruz." Reyes's voice dropped to something quiet and terrible. "Remember who holds your paper."

The silence that followed had weight. Kip watched Cruz's jaw work, watched the fight drain out of him by degrees, watched him become again the lean, restless, cornered animal he'd been when they'd pulled into the harbor — and Kip thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water: *he's not here because he sold us out. He's here because he has nowhere else to stand.*

"Take them to the office," Reyes said to the two sailors who'd appeared from behind the truck. "Statements. All four. Separately."

"We'll want counsel," Yolanda said, coming down the plank with the case still hugged to her chest, her chin up, her voice doing its archivist's trick of sounding calm because it was precise. "And we'll want that seizure order in writing, with the statutory citation. I keep copies of everything, Commander. I'd hate for your paperwork to be less thorough than mine."

Something moved behind Reyes's eyes — the flicker of a man discovering, late and inconveniently, that he was not the most dangerous person on this dock.

"Ms. Archivo," he said. "Of course. In writing."

* * *

The harbormaster's office smelled of coffee gone bitter and decades of cigarette smoke soaked into the walls. Kip sat alone at a scarred table across from a sailor whose name tag said VARGAS and who asked questions in a flat, tired voice and wrote answers in a flat, tired hand.

Kip gave him the truth, or a version of it trimmed at the edges: the quake, the low tide, the opening beneath the wreck, the descent, the air pocket, the flood, the escape. They left out the chamber. They left out the stairs, the ledgers, the name that recurred through sixty-year-old documents like a signature on a debt. They left out the manifest, which was currently zipped inside Yolanda's case, which was currently — Kip prayed — being photographed page by page in the head, or wherever Yolanda had decided to conduct her own private investigation while the navy conducted theirs.

Vargas wrote it all down without expression. Then, at the end, he looked up.

"For what it's worth," he said, very quietly, not writing, "my grandfather fished these waters. He used to say there was a town under the point. We all thought it was an old man's story." He capped his pen. "Sign there."

Kip signed. Walking out, they passed Mirabel in the corridor, headed in for her turn, and the two of them stopped, a meter apart, guards hovering.

"Yolanda?" Kip murmured.

"Safe," Mirabel murmured back. "Keep your statement boring. Boring statements don't get remembered."

"Boring," Kip promised. "I've been practicing my whole life."

Almost — *almost* — Mirabel smiled. Then the guard cleared his throat and she went in, shoulders square, and Kip stood alone in the corridor listening to the clock tick and thinking about the word *seizure*, and how a whole life could fit inside a single syllable of someone else's paperwork.

* * *

They regrouped at dusk on the *Sirena*, which the navy had technically seized but practically ignored, posted with a single bored sentry whom Tomas had already befriended with coffee and cigarettes and the universal sailor's language of complaining about officers. The storm had announced itself properly now — a long green-black line on the southern horizon, and the radio cycling advisories every twenty minutes, each one a little worse than the last.

Kip sat in the cabin with the others around the chart table, and Yolanda laid it all out: photographs, developed in the harbor photo shop by a cousin who asked no questions, spread face-down until Yolanda gave the nod. The chamber. The stairs. The ledgers. The letters. And the manifest, page by page, in a careful colonial hand — cargo, quantities, dates, and a final entry that named the reason for the sealing in language so plain it read like a threat: *to be closed and kept closed, by order, for the good of the island.*

"They knew," Yolanda said. "Whoever sealed it knew exactly what they were hiding and why. And someone has been keeping it hidden ever since." She tapped the recurring name in the margins — *Quintana, Quintana, Q.* — and looked at Mirabel, and didn't say anything else, because she didn't have to.

"My grandmother," Mirabel said. Just that. Her voice was level, but Kip saw her hands, flat on the chart table, pressing down as if the table might otherwise float away.

"So what's the play?" Tomas asked. His arm was propped, his face drawn, but his eyes were sharp. "We're seized, suspended, and scheduled for demolition. Storm's coming. Talk fast."

"The charter," Yolanda said. "There's a settlement charter in the deeper vault — I saw the reference in the manifest index. If it exists, it's a land grant. To the islanders. Not the crown, not the navy. If we can document it, the site becomes heritage property. Demolition becomes illegal. Publicly, provably illegal." She paused. "We need one more dive."

"Into a cave," Kip said, "that tried to eat us. In worsening weather. While under naval seizure."

"Yes," Yolanda said.

Nobody laughed, which Kip felt was the correct response.

It was Mirabel who broke the silence, and she didn't answer Tomas at all. She was looking at the porthole, at the harbor, at the gray truck still parked at the head of the dock — and at the lean figure detached from it, walking down the planks toward their boat in the failing light, alone, hands visible, moving like a man coming to surrender or to defect, and no way to tell which from this distance.

"Dario," Mirabel said.

Kip looked. Cruz stopped at the base of the gangplank, rain beginning to spot the boards around him, and called up in a voice pitched low but carrying:

"Quintana. Before the storm takes the choice away from both of us — I need to show you what Reyes's superiors are really afraid is down there. And you need to decide, right now, whether you trust the man who robbed you."

He held up one hand. In it, glinting wet in the cabin light, was a brass key stamped with a naval inventory number — the same number, Kip realized with a chill, that they'd seen stenciled on the crates in the second chamber's manifest.

The rain thickened. Nobody aboard *La Sirena* moved.
