## 16. Storm Front

Tomas felt the storm before he heard about it. It came up through the soles of his boots, a tremor in the dock planks that had nothing to do with engines or tide — the sea clearing its throat three hundred miles out and letting Isla Verde know it was coming.

He was under *La Sirena's* deck plates at dawn, replacing a fuel line Kip had flagged two days ago, when Yolanda's voice cut through the hull from the wheelhouse. Not shouting. Worse than shouting. Yolanda never shouted; she simply went flat and precise, like a woman reading her own diagnosis.

"Tomas. Come see this."

He slid out on the creeper, wiping grease from his hands with a rag that was already more grease than rag, and climbed into the grey morning light. The sky over the harbor had gone the color of pewter, low and bruised, and the fishing boats were coming in early — not one or two stragglers but all of them, a whole fleet hauling out of the water like animals sensing fire.

Yolanda stood over the radio, headphones around her neck, a printout in her hand. The weather service band crackled behind her.

"Track shifted overnight," she said. "Hurricane Odalys. She was supposed to bend north past the island. She didn't." She held out the printout. "She's coming here."

Tomas read the numbers twice because the first time they refused to mean anything. Category two, strengthening. Landfall projected inside forty-eight hours. Direct hit.

"Reyes," he said.

"That's the other thing." Yolanda set down the headphones. "Navy channel's been busy since four this morning. Barges moving. If I'm reading the traffic right, they're accelerating the demolition schedule. They want the charges set before the surge hits — blow the cave shut while everyone on this island is boarding windows."

Tomas leaned against the doorframe and looked out at the harbor, at the masts swaying against the grey, and felt the old familiar arithmetic start turning in his chest. He'd spent twenty years doing this math: what a thing costs, what a thing buys, what you can afford to lose. Engines mostly. Boats. Once, briefly, a marriage. He was good at the math and tired of it.

"They can't detonate during a hurricane," he said.

"They can detonate *before* one. Twenty-four hours, maybe less. And we still don't have the charter."

The charter. The original settlement document, sealed in the vault below the second chamber, the paper that said Isla Verde belonged to the people who had lived and drowned and farmed and fished here for four centuries — not to a navy that had erased it from every chart for sixty years. Yolanda had built their whole case on it. Without it, they had photographs and copies, and copies could be called forgeries by any man with a uniform and a deadline.

"We need another dive window," Tomas said. "Slack tide tomorrow morning gives us maybe ninety minutes before conditions turn. After that—"

"After that there is no after that." Yolanda folded the printout with careful, angry precision. "Get the boat ready. I'll find Mirabel."

* * *

They found Mirabel at the sea chest.

Tomas came up the stairs of the little house above the bay and stopped in the doorway, because there was something in the room you didn't interrupt. Mirabel sat on the floor with their grandmother's logbook open across their knees, and the light through the shutters fell across the pages in bars, and Mirabel was not reading so much as staring — the way you stare at a scar you've finally decided to look at directly.

Kip sat nearby, uncharacteristically quiet, a coil of dive line in their lap they weren't actually coiling.

"The storm," Tomas said. "Odalys turned. Forty-eight hours, direct track. And Reyes is moving his schedule up to beat it."

Mirabel closed the logbook. When they looked up, Tomas saw something he hadn't seen in them before — not the grief of the last weeks, not the anger. Something settled. A weight that had been carried crooked for a long time finally finding its balance point.

"Then we go tomorrow," Mirabel said. "First light. Slack tide. We get the charter, we get out, and whatever Reyes does after that, he does it to an empty cave full of evidence we've already published."

"You're sure?" Yolanda asked from the stairs. "The swell will already be building. The entrance siphon—"

"I know the siphon better than anyone alive," Mirabel said. "My grandmother drew it. In 1963. From the inside." They tapped the logbook. "She left us the map, Yolanda. She just never thought anyone would have the nerve to use it."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Outside, the wind had begun to talk — not howling yet, just muttering, testing the shutters, rehearsing.

"And Cruz?" Kip asked.

"What about him?"

"He's been asking around the docks. About our fuel, our schedule, whether we've got a compressor running. Marisol at the chandlery says he bought three tanks filled yesterday and paid cash." Kip's jaw worked. "He knows about the vault, Mirabel. He's going to dive it himself before we can."

Tomas watched Mirabel absorb that. Watched them look down at the logbook again, at the faded ink of a dead woman's handwriting, and he understood — before Mirabel said it — what they were thinking. That Dario Cruz was a man drowning in debts to men worse than him, sent down into a hole in the ocean to fetch something worth killing for, alone, with no map, no partner, and no grandmother's hand guiding him out.

"Then we'd better hope the sea is kinder to him than his creditors," Mirabel said quietly. "But we don't wait for him. Tomorrow. First light."

* * *

The rest of the day was preparation, and Tomas lost himself in it gratefully, because work was the only prayer he'd ever learned. He serviced both regulators himself, mouthpiece by mouthpiece, though Kip protested they were fine. He doubled the anchor rode. He lashed everything on deck that could become a projectile and stowed everything that couldn't, and by late afternoon *La Sirena* sat low and heavy and ready, riding the chop that was already coming into the harbor ahead of schedule, the water gone a strange glassy green that old fishermen called *ojo del diablo* — the devil's eye. Calm water before a storm wasn't calm at all. It was holding its breath.

It was near dusk when Kip came running down the dock, and Tomas knew from the way they ran — wrong rhythm, arms pumping, no attempt at the loose seaman's amble — that it was bad.

"Tomas. The *Mariposa*. Cruz's boat. It just cleared the breakwater heading north. Alone. Nobody at the helm but him."

Tomas straightened up slowly and looked north, where the horizon had begun to organize itself into a single dark line, like a wall being built by patient hands. The light was failing. The swell outside the reef would be six feet and building. And somewhere past that reef, in a sea cave that had already taken payment from better-prepared divers, Dario Cruz was going down alone with three tanks and a debt.

"He's diving tonight," Tomas said. "In rising swell, on a falling barometer, solo."

"He'll die down there."

"He might." Tomas looked back at *La Sirena*, at the gear stacked and lashed, at the boat that was meant to sail at first light. Then he looked at the northern sky, where the first true wind of Odalys arrived — a long low breath that moved the harbor water in slow oily wrinkles and made the halyards begin, softly, to toll.

Every instinct he had, twenty years of engine rooms and emergency drills and one terrible night dragging a man's body out of surf, told him the same thing: you do not put to sea ahead of a hurricane for a man who has spent two weeks trying to rob you.

But instinct was the small voice. There was a larger one underneath, older, and it sounded a lot like Mirabel's grandmother writing *for the island's own good* in a logbook sixty years ago, and Tomas had learned enough in the last month to know what happened to people who did terrible things for good reasons and then called the silence mercy.

He pulled out the radio and keyed it. "Mirabel. You seeing the *Mariposa*?"

Static. Then: "Seeing her."

"He's solo. Night dive. Swell's already at five feet outside the pass."

A long pause, long enough for the halyards to toll three times.

"Rig lights," Mirabel said at last. "We're going tonight after all."

* * *

They cleared the breakwater into a world already half-erased. The swells came out of the northeast in long grey ranks, and *La Sirena* took them at an angle that made the deck corkscrew, and Tomas stood at the helm while Mirabel ran the bow, both of them silent, because there was nothing to say that the sea wasn't already saying louder. Kip stayed below with Yolanda, feeding the compass course through the growing murk toward the coordinates that were burned into all of them now — the reef with no name, the wreck with no registry, the mouth that takes payment.

They found the *Mariposa* anchored wrong.

That was the first thing Tomas saw, and it made his stomach drop. Cruz had dropped a single hook off the stern, no trip line, no backup, and the boat was swinging wide across the swell with nobody aboard, its running lights burning lonely and absurd in the gathering dark. A dive float bobbed fifty yards downwind, its flag snapping.

"He went down the outer ledge," Mirabel said, binoculars up. "Direct descent. No guide line visible." They lowered the glasses. "Solo, night, no line, in this. That's not a dive plan, that's a suicide note with a tank attached."

Tomas began rigging the floodlights without being asked. He'd done it a hundred times for salvage work; now the beams swept down through the green-black water and lit nothing but rain-stippled surface and the pale ghost of the reef shelf dropping away into darkness. The wind was a steady voice now, no longer muttering. Somewhere south, invisible, the whole sky was organizing itself around a name.

Then Kip shouted from the bow.

"There — bubbles! Off the starboard beam!"

Tomas saw it: a thin silver scatter of bubbles breaking the surface in the light-beam, irregular, stuttering. Not the easy exhalations of a working diver. The panicked, fragmented breathing of someone who had found something down there that did not want to let go.

And then, beneath the bubbles, deeper in the beam, a shape. A flash of tank metal. A gloved hand, clawing at rock.

"He's pinned," Mirabel said, already reaching for fins. "Caught in the entrance constriction — the surge must have shoved him into the throat of the cave." They were moving as they talked, suit half-zipped, mask in hand, and Tomas grabbed their arm.

"Mirabel. The swell interval is shortening. One more set like the last and this anchorage is untenable. We cannot hold station and run a rescue."

"Then we hold station badly and run it anyway." Mirabel shook off his grip, and in the floodlight their face was very calm, which Tomas had learned was the most frightening expression they owned. "Cruz came down here to steal from a grave. He's also the only man alive who knows what Reyes' superiors are truly afraid of, and if he drowns tonight, that knowledge drowns with him — and we lose the one witness who can burn them from the inside." They pulled their fins on. "Besides. He's somebody's son, Tomas. Down there in the dark. That has to count even now. *Especially* now."

Tomas looked at the water, at the wall of storm-light building on the horizon, at the bubbles thinning in the beam.

"Kip!" he roared down the hatch. "Topside tender, tether station, now! Yolanda — get on the radio and raise the harbor patrol, coast guard, anybody with a hull, tell them we have a diver down at the unnamed reef and a hurricane inbound! Move!"

The deck became motion. Kip on the tender line, Yolanda screaming into the static, Tomas at the throttle holding *La Sirena* against the building sea with both hands and twenty years of stubbornness. And Mirabel at the gunwale, mask on, regulator between their teeth, a knife in their belt and their grandmother's map folded in a waterproof sleeve against their chest.

Below them, in the beam, the bubbles had almost stopped.

"Hold station," Mirabel said, muffled through the regulator. "I'm going down."

The last thing Tomas saw before the water closed over them was the horizon to the northeast, where the clouds had torn open just enough to show the storm's eye-wall — a vast, slow-turning column of black, lit from within by lightning, coming on like the end of the world keeping a scheduled appointment.

Then Mirabel was gone, and the sea began to take the boat sideways, and Tomas did the only thing left to do.

He held the station.
