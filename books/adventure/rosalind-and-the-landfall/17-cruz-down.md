## 17. Cruz Down

The storm had a name now — Odalys — and the radio said it like a sentence being passed. Category two, maybe three by landfall, curving north-northwest toward Isla Verde with the lazy certainty of something that had already made up its mind.

Mirabel stood at the wheel of *La Sirena* and watched the horizon eat itself. The swell had come up overnight, long and glassy and wrong, the kind of water that moved like it was remembering something old. Behind her, Tomas worked a wrench on the compressor with one hand, his other arm still strapped tight from the night Cruz's men had boarded them — four days ago, though the bruise on his ribs had only just gone from purple to yellow.

"He'll go today," Tomas said, not looking up. "Cruz. Today or never."

Mirabel didn't answer. She'd been watching *Mariposa Negra* since dawn, anchored off the point in the lee of the wreck reef, its crane swinging its gear aboard with the nervous energy of a man packing for a trip he might not return from. Dario Cruz dove alone. Everyone knew that about him — that he trusted no one in the water with him, that he'd once spent forty minutes on the bottom off Punta Hueso while his own deckhand screamed at him through the surface hose. He came up with a bag of silver coins and a story nobody believed.

Today he'd go down into the cave, into the second chamber, after the vault Yolanda had documented and photographed and copied. After the manifest that named what the navy had been hunting for sixty years.

After the thing Reyes wanted buried and Cruz wanted sold.

"Let him," Mirabel said finally.

Tomas looked up. "That's not mercy, jefa. That's arithmetic."

"It's both." She turned the wheel a few degrees, holding their drift. "He goes down, he finds out how bad the siphon runs are in this swell, and he comes back to us with something we can use. Or he doesn't come back, and I have to live with that too."

"And which one are you praying for?"

Mirabel watched the black shape of the reef break white under the building swell. The mouth of the cave lay beneath it, invisible from here, breathing with the tide. She thought of her grandmother's logbook open on the chart table below, the handwriting going careful and small on the page where she'd written *for the island's own good*, as if writing small could make the decision smaller.

"I'm praying for the version where nobody else pays for what we started."

* * *

It went wrong at 14:40, and Mirabel knew it before anyone told her.

The swell had built all afternoon, sets coming through at eight seconds, then seven, each one lifting *La Sirena* and dropping her like a hand testing fruit. Kip was topside on the wreck reef running a line survey, and Mirabel heard them shout before she saw anything — a flat, cracked sound, the voice of someone who has seen the sea do something it shouldn't.

"Boat! Boat's dragging!"

She came up the companionway fast enough to tear her sleeve. *Mariposa Negra* had swung broadside to the swell, its anchor dragging across the reef shelf in long white scars, and Cruz's deckhand — the young one, Rafael, who'd pointed a gun at Tomas four nights ago and now looked like he wanted to be anywhere else in the world — was hauling at the windlass with no rhythm at all, panic in every motion.

And there was no dive flag up. No bubbles at the stern. No tender.

"He went down twenty minutes ago," Kip said, sliding down onto the deck beside her, hair plastered to their forehead. "I saw him kit up. Alone. Mirabel — the surge is pushing straight into the entrance. The whole reef is flushing like a lung."

Mirabel did the math without wanting to. Twenty minutes down. The swim from the entrance chamber to the second chamber took eleven minutes at an easy pace, longer hauling gear. The tide was still two hours from low slack, but the storm swell didn't care about slack — it pumped the cave like a bellows regardless, and every set pushed a wall of green water through the throat of the entrance and dragged it back out again, and somewhere inside that throat, a man who trusted no one was swimming alone in the dark.

"Get the rescue bottle on the rail," she said. "Tomas — spare reg, cutting tool, my fins are by the tank rack. Kip, you're boat cover. You do *not* follow me in."

"The hell I don't—"

"You're the one who copied the manifest." She grabbed their shoulder, made them look at her. "If I don't come back, the evidence doesn't die with me. That's not a speech, Kip, that's a job assignment. Say yes."

Kip's jaw worked. The swell hit the hull and the whole boat groaned. "Yes," they said, hating it.

Mirabel was in the water ninety seconds later, and the ocean tried to kill her before she'd cleared the boat.

The surface between *La Sirena* and the reef was chaos — chop stacked on swell, spray tearing sideways, the kind of water where a swimmer and a piece of driftwood were the same thing. She swam head-down and hard, sighting on the white water boiling over the reef shelf, and when the first big set came through she felt it take her — a hand under her body, lifting, hurling her forward ten meters in a rush of foam — and understood, with a cold clarity that had nothing to do with fear, exactly what that same force was doing inside the cave.

The entrance appeared beneath her on the third dive down: a black slot in the living rock, exhaling a plume of silt that rolled out like smoke. Water moving *out*. A gap in the bellows-breath.

She went in on the exhale.

Inside, the world narrowed to a gray tunnel and the hammer of her own regulator. The carved stairs her team had documented were invisible now, scoured by surge; the air pocket where Kip had first surfaced was slapping against the ceiling like a live thing. She pulled herself along the guide line they'd installed — thank God, thank Yolanda's stubbornness, for the line — hand over hand into the dark, counting kicks, counting seconds, feeling each new set arrive as a pressure wave that shoved her forward and then sucked at her heels like something hungry.

At the junction where the tunnel forked toward the second chamber, she found the first sign of him: a scuffed fin, wedged in a crack, torn free at the strap. New. Expensive. Cruz dove with gear better than hers, gear bought with other people's money.

Then, faintly, through the water, a light — strobing, erratic, not the steady sweep of a working diver but the panicked flicker of someone signaling *here, here, HERE* — and under it, almost lost in the roar, a metallic banging.

Mirabel kicked toward it.

* * *

The second chamber's anteroom was half-flooded and roaring. When she surfaced into the air space, the sound hit her like weather — the surge funneling through some new channel, the ceiling dripping in ropes, and in the beam of her light, a hill of rubble where there hadn't been one.

Cruz had brought a pry bar and a small charge — she'd learn that later, the idiot's shortcut, a quarter-stick to crack the vault seal instead of the hours of careful work Yolanda had planned. The charge had done its job on the seal and done its job on everything else too. The old colonial masonry above the vault alcove, sixty years waterlogged and load-bearing in ways no one had mapped, had let go. Half the alcove was down. And Dario Cruz was pinned under it to his chest, in maybe eighteen inches of surging water, his mask pushed up on his forehead, his face gray as the stone.

His light was clamped in the rubble, pointing at the ceiling. His voice, when he saw hers sweep over him, came out cracked.

"Quintana." A laugh with nothing in it. "Of course. Of course it's you."

"Don't talk." She was already in the water beside him, hands on the slab pinning him, judging it. Limestone block, maybe eighty kilos, resting on a slope of smaller collapse. Not immovable. Not movable fast. "How long?"

"I don't— twenty minutes? Thirty." His breath hitched, and she saw the blood then, a thin ribbon coming from under the slab, diluted pink in the surge. Something inside was bleeding. "The water's rising. You can feel it. Every set it comes up another—"

"I know how the cave breathes." She got her shoulder under the edge of the slab, found a purchase for her hands in the wet stone, and lifted until her vision starred. It shifted a centimeter. Maybe two. "I need you to help me. On three, you push up with whatever you've got left, and you slide. Not pull — slide, toward me, flat. Can you do that?"

"Why?" His eyes found hers in the dark, and there was something in them worse than pain — bewilderment, naked and total. "Why would you come back for me? I put your engineer in a sling. I'd have taken everything you had."

"Because leaving you is the kind of thing my grandmother would've done," Mirabel said, "and I'm done doing things she'd do. Three."

She lifted. He pushed — a broken, grunting effort, arms shaking — and the slab ground up two inches, three, and he slid, screaming through his teeth, and then he was clear and she dropped the stone back into the water with a boom that shook the chamber.

Getting him out took forty minutes and cost her nearly everything.

He could kick, barely, one leg working, so she clipped him to her harness and towed him through the junction, through the throat, timing each run of the tunnel to the cave's exhale the way her grandmother's logbook had described it — *the mouth opens twice an hour even in storm; go with the out-breath or the island keeps you* — and twice the inhale caught them anyway and slammed them against the line and held them there, thrashing, while the cave drank, and both times Mirabel pressed her forehead to the guide line and waited for the sea to change its mind.

They broke the surface outside the reef into daylight so violent it seemed like a different element. Kip and Tomas hauled Cruz over *La Sirena*'s gunwale, and Rafael, sobbing with relief on the dragging *Mariposa*, threw them a line without being asked, and somehow both boats ended up rafted together in the lee while the storm ate the horizon.

Cruz lay on the deck with Tomas strapping his ribs, conscious, ash-faced, watching Mirabel with an expression she couldn't read.

"You could've taken the vault contents," he rasped. "While I was under the rock. The manifest copy, the photos — you could've let the sea settle the account and walked away clean."

"I don't settle accounts that way."

"No." He laughed, winced, kept laughing until Tomas told him to stop. "No, I don't suppose you do. That's your problem, Quintana. That's always been your family's problem."

She crouched beside him. Up close, in daylight, he looked older than his file photo by a decade — the tan sitting wrong on him, the hands of a man who'd been gripping things too tightly for too long.

"Talk," she said. "You dove a collapsing cave alone, in storm swell, for cargo you can't legally sell to anyone on this island. Nobody's that greedy. So either you're stupid, or somebody owns you. Which is it?"

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer. The swell rocked the rafted boats. Somewhere inland, faint under the wind, the church bell at Puerto Claro began ringing the storm warning.

"Reyes doesn't work for the navy," Cruz said finally. "Not really. He works for Admiral Ferrán's office — the man whose signature is on the original demolition order, back in '63. Your grandmother knew him. Everybody who was anybody in those waters knew him." He closed his eyes. "Ferrán retired rich. Very rich. And thirty years later, when I found the first piece of the trail — a plate off the hull, in a private collection in Cartagena — it wasn't the navy that came asking about it. It was Ferrán's people. They bought my silence with money, and when the money ran out, they owned my debts instead. Salvage loans. Favorable terms. Terms that turned unfavorable the moment I asked questions." He opened his eyes. "I'm not diving that vault because I want to sell history, Quintana. I'm diving it because if I come back empty, the people I owe will take my boat, my house, and my daughter's school fees, and they'll do it smiling, and Commander Reyes will stand on the dock and watch."

The wind gusted. Rain arrived all at once, fat and warm, drumming on the cabin roof.

"They sent you to bury it," Mirabel said slowly, "and you decided to sell it first."

"I decided to survive first. Selling was a detail." He tried to sit up, failed, settled for propping himself on an elbow. "But here's what your mercy buys you, since you insist on spending it. I know why Ferrán's office wants that vault sealed so badly it's worth dynamite and a hurricane. It isn't the cargo. Cargo's insured, cargo's written off decades ago. It's the manifest. Because the manifest doesn't just list what was hidden." His mouth twisted. "It lists who paid for it to be hidden. Names. Signatures. The founding document of a very comfortable retirement — and underneath it, the settlement charter, the one that says the land under that cave belongs to the islanders. They've been sitting on a land title for sixty years, Quintana. Yours. Mine. Everyone on this rock."

Mirabel stood very still, rain running off her chin, and felt the whole shape of the last month click into place — the double payments, the forbidden dives, the demolition deadline, her grandmother's careful small handwriting. It had never been about a wreck. It had been about a deed.

"Tomas," she said quietly. "Get the storm sails on. We're going to need to move tonight."

Cruz caught her wrist. His grip was weak and his eyes were not.

"One condition," he said. "When you go back down for that charter — and you will, I can see it in your face — I'm on your boat. Not theirs. My knowledge of the vault seals, my gear, my name on the salvage claim if it comes to court. I'm done being owned." He swallowed. "Consider it an alliance. Uneasy. But real."

Mirabel looked at his ruined ribs, his torn fin lying in a puddle on her deck, the daughter he'd mentioned and hadn't explained. Then she looked east, where Odalys had begun to show herself — a bruised violet wall on the horizon, coming on like a verdict.

"Slack tide is at 03:40," she said. "Storm hits around noon. That gives us one window." She gripped his forearm, once, hard. "Don't make me regret this, Dario."

He managed something that was almost a smile. "Too late," he said. "We're both already regretting it. That's what makes it an alliance."

Out past the reef, the sea had gone the color of hammered tin, and the mouth of the cave breathed on in the dark, waiting for the tide to turn.
