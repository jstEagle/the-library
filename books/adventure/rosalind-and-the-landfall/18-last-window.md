## 18. Last Window

The barometer had been falling all night, and by dawn the harbor looked wrong — water the color of pewter, boats riding too high on their moorings as if the sea itself were holding its breath. Yolanda stood at the window of the expedition house with a cup of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the palm trees along the malecón begin to lean.

"Six hours," Tomas said behind her. He was taping a spare regulator to his chest harness with surgical precision, the way he did everything now that his ribs were strapped. The bruises from Cruz's night raid had gone yellow at the edges. "Maybe five. After that, Reyes' divers start setting charges whether we're back or not."

"They won't set them in this weather."

"They'll set them *before* it. That's the point." He nodded at the radio on the table, where Mirabel's voice had come through an hour ago from the navy dock, clipped and furious: demolition moved up to fourteen hundred hours. Storm front or no storm front. The order had come down from above Reyes — from whoever owned Reyes — and the commander had stopped pretending he had any choice in it.

Yolanda set down her coffee and picked up the folder she'd rebuilt three times since midnight: photocopies of the manifest, her transcriptions of the flooded ledgers, the chain of custody forms she'd drafted in case any of this ever saw a courtroom. It was thin. Photocopies could be called forgeries. Transcriptions could be called inventions. What they needed was the original — the settlement charter itself, the document the vault manifest listed first among its contents, sealed in lead and wax against exactly such days as this one.

*Acta de Fundación y Repartimiento de Tierras, 1541.* The founding act and land grant. If it said what she believed it said — if the crown had granted these waters and this coastline to the settlers' descendants rather than reserving them — then every salvage claim the navy had filed in sixty years rested on sand. Not sentiment. Sand. Law.

"You've checked the route four times," Kip said from the doorway, hair still wet from a test swim off the dock. At twenty-three, Kip had the particular calm of someone who had already been terrified once this week and found it survivable. "The siphon passage, the air bell, the drop into the second chamber. You could recite it asleep."

"I have recited it asleep. Ask Tomas."

"I heard you," Tomas said. "You were arguing with a dead archivist about foliation."

"He deserved it. He numbered pages out of order." Yolanda closed the folder. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. All week her hands had shaken — when the ledgers came up dripping, when Quintana surfaced in the ink like a name called across centuries, when Tomas bled on the deck of the boat. But not now. Now there was only one thing to do, and doing one thing was the easiest state she knew.

Mirabel came down the stairs last, dressed, weighted, carrying her grandmother's logbook in a waterproof bag slung tight against her chest. She'd barely slept. Yolanda could see it in the geography of her face — the new hollows, the eyes that had gone from grief to something harder and cleaner. Last night Mirabel had read them the final entries aloud, the ones where her grandmother described standing on this same shore while men drilled holes into the cliff, and wrote: *I told myself it was for the island's own good. I no longer know whose good I served.*

"We go now," Mirabel said. "We're back by eleven hundred. We photograph nothing we don't already have. We take the charter and we surface." She looked at each of them in turn. "Nobody is a hero today. Heroes drown."

"And cowards?" Kip asked.

"Cowards live to file paperwork," Yolanda said. "Which today is the braver act."

* * *

The *Rosalind* — Tomas had named the boat without asking anyone, and somehow it had stuck — pushed out through a harbor gone strange with the pre-storm swell. Long rolling sets came in from the southeast, ten seconds apart, each one lifting the hull and setting it down with a patience that felt deliberate. The sky to the east was stacked with cloud, bruise-colored at its base, and the wind had begun to carry salt spray even this far up the channel.

Yolanda sat amidships with the dive plan in a waterproof sleeve on her lap and made herself read it again anyway. Forty-one meters to the cave mouth. Ninety meters of penetration through the siphon gallery. Air bell at the sixty-meter mark. Descent shaft to the second chamber, where the vault stood behind its collapsed lintel — cleared now, after two days of careful work with hand tools, because explosives were the one thing even Dario Cruz hadn't dared bring near it.

Cruz himself was not aboard. He was, according to Mirabel, in the navy infirmary having his arm set, and according to Cruz, "resting." Yolanda didn't trust either version, but she trusted what he'd given them before he vanished: a hand-drawn sketch of the vault's inner seal, annotated in a cramped engineer's hand, with a single note across the bottom — *the hinge side lifts, not the latch side. She built it to be opened by someone who read.* *She*. Yolanda had thought about that word for two days. Someone had built that vault knowing a reader would someday come for it. Perhaps that was the whole of history: messages passed hand to hand through drowning time, most of them lost, one of them arriving.

Kip ran the bow toward the buoy they'd moored over the wreck site, and the reef rose beneath them through the gray-green water, the naval hull lying across it like a fallen sentinel, its unregistered serial number furred now with a week's new growth. Below it, hidden from every chart for four hundred years, the dark line of the cave mouth waited.

Yolanda checked her gauges. Checked Kip's. Checked the line reels, three of them, because one line was hope and two were arithmetic.

"Slack starts in twenty minutes," Mirabel said, mask up, voice muffled. "We will not get another slack before the storm. This is the window. Last one." She put her hand flat on the gunwale, over the water. "For the island."

"For the island," they said, and went over the side.

* * *

The descent was familiar now, and familiarity frightened Yolanda more than novelty would have. Divers died of familiarity. She counted her breathing, matched Kip's fins ahead of her, kept one gloved hand on the guide line as the light went from silver to green to the brown-black of the interior. The wreck's shadow swallowed them. Then the cave took them, and the world became the width of their lights.

The siphon gallery was running clear — the quake-tide had left it stable, the water glassy and still inside the stone throat of the island. Yolanda swam with her eyes moving in the trained rhythm: line, gauge, buddy, rock. Line, gauge, buddy, rock. The walls here were cut, not carved by water — chisel marks still sharp under a skin of calcium, colonial masons' work, four centuries old and precise as scripture. At the sixty-meter mark the air bell opened around them like a held breath, and they broke surface in a pocket of darkness that smelled of stone and old rain.

They hung there a moment, three lights swinging, three regulators hissing.

"Folks used to hide in here," Kip said quietly. The sound bounced strangely off the low ceiling. "During the pirate raids. My grandmother used to say the whole valley emptied into the ground."

"Your grandmother and mine probably shared a lantern," Mirabel said, and Yolanda heard something in her voice she hadn't heard before — not grief, exactly. Inheritance, being accepted rather than refused.

They dropped back under. The descent shaft opened below the air bell, a vertical slot of cut stairs spiraling down into the second chamber, and they swam it slowly, lights sweeping across doorways, a chapel arch, the roofless grid of streets that had been a town before the sea was ordered to keep its secret. Yolanda had documented all of it in previous dives; today she swam past it like a woman passing her own front gate. There was no time to look at anything except the destination.

The vault chamber received them in silence. Their lights found the lead-sealed cabinet against the far wall, its hinge side — as Cruz had promised — facing outward, its surface crusted but intact. The manifest had listed the contents in the archivist's careful script: *charter, parish register, treasury ledger, letters patent, correspondence of the governor.* A government in a box. A people's title deed, drowned and preserved like a seed.

Tomas anchored the lift bag. Kip held the light steady. And Yolanda swam forward alone, because some tasks are not delegable, and put her hands on the hinge.

The seal resisted, then gave with a groan of metal that echoed down four hundred years. Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas and pitch, lay the documents — and the topmost packet bore a lead tag stamped with the words she had reconstructed from fragments across three weeks of archival labor:

*ACTA DE FUNDACIÓN — AÑO 1541.*

Her hands, so steady all morning, shook now. She worked the packet free, checked the wax, checked the wrapping, and slid it into the rigid waterproof tube lashed to her chest. Then, because she was who she was, she photographed the empty vault interior, the remaining packets left undisturbed for the heritage review she intended to force, and the inner lid — where, scratched into the lead in a later hand, someone had added a single line beneath the original inscription:

*Que lo sepan los que vienen después.*
Let those who come after know.

She pressed her glove against it for one second. One second was all the schedule allowed.

On the ascent back through the shaft, her gauge read a number she liked less than the one on the way down, and the water in the gallery had begun — very slightly — to move. Not slack anymore. The tide turning early, dragged forward by the storm's distant pull, the whole ocean leaning toward the island like a listener.

"Move," Mirabel said into the water, and they moved.

* * *

They broke the surface beside the boat into wind that hadn't existed an hour ago. The eastern sky had lowered until it seemed to press on the water, and the first real gusts were walking whitecaps down the channel toward them. Kip hauled Yolanda over the gunwale; Tomas fought the lift bag aboard; and Mirabel, last up, turned once to look back at the reef sinking under the rising chop, the wreck and the cave mouth beneath it going dark together.

Yolanda knelt on the deck, tore the tube open with numb fingers, and drew out the packet. Waxed canvas, pitch-black, heavier than paper had any right to be. She peeled the wrapping back just enough to see the first page — vellum, stained amber with age, dense with secretary hand and a great wax pendant seal cracked but whole, the arms of the crown pressed into it like a fossil.

She read the opening lines. She read them twice, because the sea was shouting and her heart was louder.

*...y otorgamos a los pobladores y a sus descendientes para siempre...*

To the settlers and their descendants, forever. The waters. The anchorage. The coast from the point to the river mouth. Forever.

"It's real," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "It's real, it's executed, it names the grant — Mirabel, it names *this bay* by name."

Mirabel took the tube and held it against her chest the way she'd carried her grandmother's logbook, both inheritances pressed together over her heart. For a moment nobody spoke. The wind filled the silence for them.

Then Tomas, at the tiller, said quietly: "Eleven hundred. We made it." And then, looking past them toward the harbor: "Except—"

Yolanda followed his gaze. At the mouth of the channel, cutting a straight military line through the building sea, came a navy launch. On its deck, small with distance but unmistakable, men were handling long shapes — cases, cables, the blunt geometry of demolition gear. They weren't waiting for fourteen hundred hours. They were going out to the reef *now*, in the teeth of the coming storm, to finish it before anyone could stop them.

And standing in the launch's bow, gripping the rail against the swell, was Commander Reyes — and beside him, arm in a sling, face turned toward their boat like a compass finding north, Dario Cruz.

Yolanda closed her hands around the charter and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the edge of loss, that the last window was closing faster than any tide.
