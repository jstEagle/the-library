## 1. The Weight of Moving

The col had no name on any map Lena trusted, which was precisely why she used it. On the consortium surveys it was marked as a saddle, elevation 2,340 meters, unremarkable, a wrinkle in the green shoulder of the Verren Range where the forest thinned into scree and the border between two countries ran like a dropped thread through the grass. She had crossed it forty-one times. She knew it the way she knew the scar on her left palm — intimately, without affection, without ever being able to forget it.

She brought the family up in the dark before dawn, because the dark was a country too, and she was its citizen.

"Small steps," she said, not turning around. Her voice carried low and flat, pitched to reach six people without reaching any farther. "Feel with your feet before you trust them. If I stop, you stop. If I say down, you get down and you stay down until my hand touches your shoulder."

Behind her came the sounds she had learned to read like weather: the wet rasp of a child's breathing, the creak of a pack frame carried by shoulders too narrow for it, the soft litany of a woman counting her children under her breath — *one, two, three* — the way other people counted money or sins. The father walked last, because fathers always insisted on walking last, standing guard over a family's retreat from a country that had already taken everything worth guarding.

The trail was a goat path in summer and a rumor in every other season. Tonight it was slick with the first cold of autumn, frost forming on the stones like breath on glass. Lena moved through it with her lamp hooded, letting the mountain speak to her boots instead of her eyes. Left at the split birch. Up through the granite teeth. Along the shelf where the drop fell away into blackness so complete it felt less like height than like depth, like the underside of the world.

The child — the middle one, a boy of perhaps seven with his sister's coat buttoned wrong — began to cry somewhere past the halfway mark. Not loudly. That was the worst part. He cried the way children cry when they have already learned that crying is a luxury, small and swallowed, a sound folded up small enough to fit inside a jacket pocket.

Lena stopped. She waited for the line to bunch behind her, then crouched until she found his face in the dark, round and shining with cold and effort.

"What's your name?"

"Sef." A whisper.

"Sef. Do you know what's above us?"

He shook his head.

"The sky," she said. "Nothing else. We're walking toward the sky, and in about an hour we're going to be standing inside it, and everything down there—" she tipped her chin back toward the valley they'd left, where the checkpoints glowed like infected wounds along the river road— "will be smaller than your shoe. Can you walk an hour for me?"

He nodded. He wiped his face with a sleeve already stiff with old snot and older fear, and he walked an hour, and he did not cry again, and when they crested the col and the eastern sky had gone the color of a bruise healing, he stood at the top with his mouth open, looking at the light coming up over the far ranges like something being born.

Lena let them have thirty seconds of it. Thirty seconds of standing inside the sky. Then she touched the mother's shoulder — *down, stay down until my hand finds you* — and took them off the north side, where the pines closed over their heads and the new country received them without comment.

* * *

The handoff point was a logging cut two valleys north, a raw scar of stumps and slash where a truck would come at midmorning driven by a man named Ferro who asked no questions because questions were the one currency he refused to trade in. Lena got the family there by nine. The mother pressed an envelope into her hands with both of hers, the way you'd return something borrowed, and could not meet her eyes.

"It's all we—" the father started.

"It's what we agreed." Lena tucked the envelope inside her jacket without counting it. Counting it in front of them would have been a kind of cruelty. "There's water in the truck. There's bread in the canvas bag. Eat slowly even if you're starving — your stomachs have been climbing all night too."

The woman caught her sleeve as she turned. Her fingers were cracked at the knuckles, work-hardened, a hand that had done things with soil or cloth or other people's laundry for years. "They said you don't talk," she said. "At the crossing houses. They said the guide doesn't talk, doesn't ask names, doesn't ask anything."

"That's true."

"But you talked to my son."

Lena looked at her. In the flat morning light the woman's face was a map of its own, lines running every direction, exhaustion worn down to the grain. "Your son needed talking to," Lena said. "That's different."

The woman held her eyes a moment longer, and something passed between them that had no word in either of their languages — recognition, maybe, of the particular loneliness of women who carry other people across thresholds. Then Ferro's truck ground up the logging road, and the family became cargo again, and Lena stood alone among the stumps watching them go until the dust settled and the mountains took the sound away.

She counted the money afterward, alone, sitting on a stump with her back against the sky. Four hundred and sixty. It would cover the winter boots she needed. It would cover the bribes at the two checkpoints on her southern routes, both of which had gone up in price since spring, corruption inflating like everything else. It would cover food for maybe five weeks if she ate the way she ate, which was badly and rarely. It would not cover what she actually owed, which was not denominated in any currency she could hold.

She put the money away and started walking south, because stopping was a thing that happened to other people now.

* * *

The trailhead town was called Havel's Rest, which was a joke someone had made a century ago and everyone had forgotten to laugh at. It consisted of a supply depot, a chapel with a leaning steeple, a post office that doubled as the magistrate's office, and a tavern whose sign showed a mule drinking from a mug. Lena came down into it at dusk with three days of beard-stubble on her jaw and her hood up, and she went first to the depot, because gear was life and gossip was only death delayed.

Old Marta ran the depot the way lighthouses run coastlines: fixed, indifferent, illuminating everything whether it wanted to be seen or not. She was seventy if she was a day, with forearms like dock rope and eyes that had priced every item in the Verren Range and most of the people in it. She looked up when Lena came in, and her face did the thing it always did — a flicker of something that might have been pity, quickly buried under commerce.

"You look like a landslide," Marta said.

"I feel like one."

"Rope's gone up. Don't ask me why; ask the consortium, they own the rope now too, near enough." She hauled coils down from the wall, thumped them onto the counter, and while her hands worked, her eyes did the other thing, the thing Lena had come half-expecting and fully dreading. They flicked toward the noticeboard by the door. Flicked back. Flicked again.

Lena set down the carabiners she'd been testing. "Marta."

"Mm."

"What's on the board."

"Nothing that concerns you."

"Marta."

The old woman sighed through her nose, a long sound like wind through a cave mouth, and jerked her chin at the board. "Third town this season," she said. "You're getting popular."

Lena crossed the floor the way you cross ice you don't trust. The noticeboard was a chaos of tacked paper — wool prices, a lost dog, a sermon schedule, a warning about wolves on the east slopes — and pinned square in the center, crisp and official and new, was a printed sheet bearing the seal of the Border Magistracy of Veldt Province.

**NOTICE OF PERSON OF INTEREST.** The words sat at the top in letters that had been chosen by someone paid to make them look neutral. Below them, a description: *Female. Approximately thirty-five years. Dark hair, worn short. Scar, left palm. Known to operate as an unauthorized guide in the Verren high passes. Answers to the name Kavanagh.* And below that, the reward figure, which was new, and which made the blood move strangely in Lena's ears.

Two hundred. Two hundred was more than she'd made all season. Two hundred was a month of wages for a laborer, a winter's coal, a child's medicine. Two hundred was a price calculated by someone who understood exactly what poverty did to neighbors.

"They posted it yesterday," Marta said behind her. "Came up with the mail cart. Man who brought it asked questions in the tavern after. Bought rounds. Friendly type." She paused. "The kind of friendly that costs."

Lena read the sheet twice more, committing it to the place where she kept route details and crevasse locations and the faces of clients who hadn't listened. *Scar, left palm.* Someone had talked. Someone who'd seen her hands close-up, which meant a client, which meant one of hers, which meant the circle was drawing in from the inside now and not just the outside.

"When did the man leave?"

"This morning. Heading south, toward Corven way."

South. Toward Corven. She filed that too, though she couldn't have said why the town name snagged in her like a hook.

"He describe me to the room?"

"He described you to the room," Marta said dryly, "as a dangerous criminal known to frequent these parts, and then he drank four brandies and told everyone about his daughter's wedding. Your legend is safe. Your face isn't." She came around the counter, took the notice off the board with two fingers, and held it out. "Take it. You'll want to know the wording. And take this." From under the counter she produced a tin of salve, the kind she sold to quarrymen for cracked hands. "Don't argue. You've got the hands of a woman who's been chewing rocks."

Lena took both. The notice she folded into quarters and put beside the money. The salve she'd use tonight, in whatever shed or overhang qualified as home.

"Marta. Who else has seen this?"

"Everyone who buys lamp oil. Which is everyone." The old woman's voice gentled by a degree, which from Marta was practically a lullaby. "Nobody here will sell you. Half of them have cousins who've crossed with you, and the other half are too mean to bother. But Havel's Rest is a throat, girl. Everything going south passes through it. Including word."

"I know how throats work."

"I expect you do." Marta rang up the rope and pins and the stove fuel without being asked, and knocked a third off the total without saying so, and pushed the parcel across. "Where next?"

"North."

"North is the consortium side."

"North is where nobody's posted me yet." Lena shouldered the parcel. At the door she paused, her hand on the frame, and looked back at the old woman in the lamplight, at the walls of rope and iron and canvas, at the whole small inventory of a life spent equipping people to go places they shouldn't. "If anyone asks — anyone official — you sold rope to a quarryman."

"I've never sold rope to a quarryman in my life. Their hands are too soft." Marta waved her out. "Go on. And eat something, for God's sake. Landslides need ballast."

* * *

She didn't eat in the tavern. She bought bread and hard cheese from the chapel's charity box, leaving coins that more than covered it, and ate walking, north along the river track with the last light dying behind the western ridges and the first stars coming out over the pass she'd have to cross tomorrow if she wanted to be nowhere in particular by morning.

This was the arithmetic she did while she walked, because walking was the only time the numbers stayed still.

Two hundred on her head, posted in three towns, which meant the magistrates were coordinating now, which meant someone above the provincial level had decided she was worth the ink. A season's earnings in her jacket, minus the rope, minus the fuel, minus the tolls. Winter coming early — the frost on the col had been a month ahead of itself, and the high snows would close the passes before solstice, which compressed everything, which meant fewer crossings, which meant less money, which meant the whole fragile machine of her survival grinding toward its season of silence.

And underneath all of it, the number that never changed, the debt with no denomination: a name on a mountain, a body she'd carried down herself, a widow in a coastal town who believed — because Lena had made sure the record said so — that the guide had failed.

She stopped where the track bent away from the river, where a cairn some forgotten shepherd had raised marked nothing in particular, and she looked back once at Havel's Rest, a scatter of yellow windows in the blue dusk, smoke rising straight in the windless cold. Somewhere in one of those windows a man was sleeping who had memorized her face for two hundred units of currency. Somewhere south, in a town called Corven that kept snagging in her mind like a burr, the notice was traveling by mail cart toward whatever came next.

Her name in a third town. Her scar on official paper. Her price rising while her routes shrank.

The old Lena — the licensed one, the one with a good reputation and a partner she trusted and a future you could plan in seasons — would have looked at that ledger and concluded, reasonably, that it was time to stop. Time to come down off the range, find work on the legal side, guide tourists to viewpoints and call it a living. Time to let the mountains be mountains instead of a corridor.

But the old Lena had died on the Kessel Face along with her client, and the woman who'd climbed down out of those clouds had learned the one lesson the range taught better than any other: *stopping is how the mountain keeps you.*

Keep moving and the storm can't settle on you. Keep moving and the crevasse behind you stays behind you. Keep moving and the men with warrants arrive at the inn to find a cold hearth and a paid bill and a landlady who remembers nothing. Movement wasn't freedom. She'd stopped believing in freedom somewhere around the second blacklist. But movement was *distance*, and distance was the only currency the mountains honored, and she was rich in it, richer than the consortium, richer than the magistracy, richer than any man with a warrant and a brandy budget.

She turned north and walked.

The stars thickened overhead until the Milky Way lay across the Verren Range like a second, higher range, white peaks on black, and Lena Okafor moved beneath it with her hood up and her scarred hand loose at her side, a woman carrying nothing but rope and bread and a name that was becoming worth more dead than alive.

Behind her, in Havel's Rest, the mail cart driver slept off his brandies, dreaming of his daughter's wedding, unaware that the description he'd carried south — *scar, left palm, answers to Kavanagh* — had already begun its own journey, from tavern to tavern, mouth to mouth, toward a valley town called Corven, where a man named Aldous Vane was coughing blood into a handkerchief at the last inn before the high passes, and asking the landlady, in a voice like gravel sliding, whether she'd heard tell of a guide. A woman guide. The best there was. The kind who could get a dying man over the Wall before the snows.

The landlady said she might have heard something like that.

The landlady was lying, but not well, and Aldous Vane had spent fifty years reading mountains, and mountains and lies are read the same way: by what they fail to hide.
