## 2. A Stranger at the Last Inn

The inn at Halden's Rest had once been a place where guides drank on their winnings and clients toasted summits they'd paid to reach. Now it was a barn with a stove, a bar of blackened pine, and a landlady who charged by the hour for the privilege of sitting near the fire. Lena counted her coins twice before pushing through the door — eleven crowns and a half, the wages of three days of lying to border clerks about who she was and why she crossed mountains alone.

She'd earned worse money doing honest work.

Inside, the smell was tallow smoke and wet wool and something older, the sour ghost of spilled beer soaked so deep into the floorboards that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift it. Six tables. Two occupied. A pair of wool traders asleep over their cups in the corner, and — she noted him without seeming to note him, the way you note weather or loose rock — an old man sitting alone near the window, hunched over a bowl of stew like a man guarding it.

Lena took the table farthest from both, back to the wall, sightline to the door. Old habits. The habits were all she had left of the woman she'd been before Kessel Face, before the inquest, before her name went up on boards in two countries like a warning nailed to a church door.

*LENA OKAFOR — GUIDE LICENSE REVOKED — DO NOT CONTRACT.*

She could still see the exact weight of the letters. She'd stopped reading them years ago, but her eyes found them anyway, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

"Stew and bread," she told the landlady, laying down two crowns. "And whatever's in that jug that isn't poison."

"Everything in the jug is poison," the landlady said. "That's why it's cheap."

It was cheap. It tasted like kerosene strained through a sock, and Lena drank it slowly anyway, letting it burn a small warm hole through the cold that had lived in her chest since the col. Three days above the tree line with the Okonkwo family — a mother, two children, a grandmother who had not complained once, which Lena knew from long experience meant the grandmother was the toughest of them. She'd put them over the low col at dusk, walked them down as far as the shepherd's hut, and taken her pay in cash folded into a boot. The mother had cried when she shook Lena's hand. Not from gratitude. From the particular exhaustion of a person who has just learned that safety is a country she will never live in.

Lena understood that. She was its most experienced guide.

The stew arrived. Mutton, mostly gristle, swimming in grease the color of old candle wax. She ate every bite. You ate everything on this side of the range; waste was a sin the mountain punished eventually, always with interest.

She was halfway through the bread when the old man moved.

She heard him first — the wet rattle in his chest, a sound like water being poured through gravel, the sound she'd learned to hear in her father before the mine took his lungs too. Then the scrape of his chair, and then he was standing beside her table with his bowl in one hand and an expression on his face that she recognized because she'd worn it herself for years: the look of a man who has found something he wasn't looking for and is trying to decide whether to trust it.

"You're Okafor," he said.

Not a question. Lena set down her spoon and looked up at him properly for the first time. Seventy, maybe older, but built like the kind of old that comes from work rather than age — shoulders still square under a coat patched at both elbows, hands enormous and scarred, knuckles swollen like the roots of an old pine. His face was weathered down to leather, except around the eyes, which were quick and dark and entirely alive. He held himself carefully upright, the way men hold themselves when breathing has become a negotiation.

"That depends on who's asking," Lena said.

"Aldous Vane." He didn't offer a hand. "Prospector. Retired, if there's such a thing."

"There isn't."

"No," he agreed, and coughed — a deep, folding cough that bent him at the waist and went on long enough that Lena found herself counting. When he straightened, there was blood-bright color high on his cheekbones and his eyes had gone glassy. "There isn't."

"The name Okafor should be worth avoiding," she said. "If you've read the boards, you know what I am."

"I know what the boards say." He lowered himself into the chair across from her without asking, which she allowed because refusing would have required energy she wanted to save. "I also know what the boards don't say. I was on the Verren side in the year of the Kessel Face. I read the inquest summaries. All of them. They publish those things with pages missing, did you know that? Whole paragraphs cut out where the testimony got inconvenient." He tapped the table with one thick finger. "Your summary was missing four pages."

Lena kept her face still. Inside, something turned over — slow and cold, like ice shifting in a crevasse.

"Four pages," Vane repeated. "I made inquiries. Nobody would tell me what was in them. But I've spent forty years learning what it means when powerful men remove paper from a record. It means the paper was true."

The landlady came by and he ordered — another jug, a second bowl of stew, a plate of the hard mountain cheese that cost triple what it was worth. He paid with coins that were old minting, heavy silver, the kind prospectors hoarded because banks asked questions. Lena watched the money and felt the first real pull of the evening, the hook going in soft and deep.

"What do you want, Vane?"

"Dinner company." He shrugged. "I'm dying. Dying men hate eating alone."

"You're dying," Lena repeated flatly.

"Cancer of the lung. The doctor at Ferrenholt gave me until the thaw, generously." He said it the way other men reported the weather. "I mention it because it makes me honest. A man with nothing to lose has no reason to lie to you, and no reason to let you lie to yourself either."

The second jug arrived. He poured for both of them, and Lena surprised herself by drinking. Outside, the wind had come up around the inn's eaves, moaning in the gap under the roof tiles, and the light through the window had gone the blue-grey of iron. Snow coming, maybe. Or just the mountain clearing its throat.

"You recognize my face from somewhere," Lena said. "Not the boards. Somewhere else."

Vane smiled. It transformed him — suddenly she could see the young man he'd been, the one who'd walked into these hills with a hammer and a mule and ambition. "Twenty-two years ago. The Serrat traverse. You'd have been — what, nineteen? Twenty? You carried a client's pack up the north shoulder after he quit, on your own back, in weather that killed two sheep and a mule on the same pass. I was camped below the shoulder. I watched you come down out of the cloud with that pack and I said to myself: *that one will die on a mountain someday, or she'll be the best there's ever been.*" He drank. "I've wondered since which it was."

"Neither," Lena said. "I became illegal instead."

"Yes." And here his voice changed, dropped into something quieter and more careful. "Which brings me to my questions."

"There it is."

"You're angry."

"I'm *priced*," Lena said. "Dinner costs more than conversation. Conversation about the high passes costs considerably more than dinner. And questions about the high passes from a stranger who knows my name and my history cost more than I think you can afford, Mr. Vane, even with your heavy old coins."

He laughed — a bark that dissolved immediately into coughing, and this time Lena pushed the jug toward him without thinking about it, and he waved it off and rode the cough down and came back up grey-faced and grinning. "God, yes. That's her. That's exactly the woman from the Serrat shoulder." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "All right. Name your price."

"For what?"

"For listening to the rest of it. Whatever number you say, I'll pay it, and then tomorrow I'll pay it again, and we'll see how long your principles survive contact with my silver."

*Silver.* He said it lightly, tossed off like a joke, and Lena caught it anyway, the way you catch movement in scree at the edge of your vision. She sat back. The fire cracked. In the corner, one of the wool traders snored and shifted.

"Tell me the questions first," she said. "Then I'll price the answers."

Vane leaned forward, and the lamplight moved across his face, and for a moment the grin was gone and what was underneath was hungry and sharp and frightened.

"The Verren Range," he said. "The high passes. Not the cols everyone knows — the ones above them. The routes that don't appear on any survey. I want to know if they're passable in six weeks' time. I want to know what the snowpack does on the east faces after a dry autumn. I want to know whether a party of two could cross from the western watershed to Corven valley without touching a checkpoint, a toll station, or a consortium road." He held up one finger. "And I want to know if *you* are the only guide alive who could lead them."

The silence stretched. Lena turned her cup slowly on the table, watching the wine-dark ring it left.

Because here was the thing — the thing she'd known since he stood up from his stew, the thing she'd been circling like a hawk over a dead thing: every one of those questions was a smuggling question. A fugitive's question. She'd answered versions of it a hundred times for families with forged papers and debts and fear in their luggage. She knew the answer to all of it already. Yes, the high routes were passable in six weeks, barely. Yes, the east faces would be rotten after a dry autumn, wind-loaded and treacherous. Yes, a careful party of two could make the crossing unseen — if the party of two included her.

And yes. She was probably the only guide alive who could lead it. Because every other guide with her skills still had a license, and every licensed guide had a ledger, and every ledger had a consortium stamp on the back page.

"You're asking me to take someone across illegally," she said. "Someone important enough that you're spending prospector silver on it."

"I'm asking nothing yet." Vane settled back, and the cough rattled in him like stones in a sack. "Tomorrow, perhaps. Tonight I'm only an old man buying dinner for the best guide these mountains have produced in fifty years, and asking her opinion on the weather."

"My opinion on the weather is that it's turning early."

"So I've heard." Something flickered behind his eyes — she couldn't name it, and it bothered her that she couldn't. "So I've heard."

He rose, unsteady, joints cracking like green wood, and dropped a small stack of silver on the table — far more than the meal had cost, far more than she'd have named as a price, deliberately so, and they both knew it. A down payment. A hook with weight on the line.

"For the dinner and the opinion," he said. "Sleep on the rest. I'll be here tomorrow, if the mountain lets me."

He made it three steps toward the stairs before the cough took him again, doubled him over against the newel post, shaking. Lena stayed in her chair with her hands flat on the table and made herself watch, made herself do the arithmetic she hated: the rattle depth, the color of his lips, the way his fingers gripped the wood. She'd sat with her father through two winters of that same sound. She knew exactly what she was looking at, and what she was looking at had weeks in it, not months.

Six weeks, he'd said. Passable in six weeks.

He reached the bottom of the stairs, paused, and looked back at her — and for a moment the grin was gone and the hunger was gone and what was left was naked and pleading, a man standing at the edge of the last climb of his life.

"They'll tell you I'm mad, if you ask in Ferrenholt," he said. "Ask anyway. Ask what Aldous Vane was doing in the consortium offices twenty years ago, and watch their faces change." He tapped the side of his nose. "Paper, Miss Okafor. It always comes back to paper. Who wrote it, who signed it, and who paid to have it disappear."

Then he climbed the stairs, slowly, one groaning step at a time, and Lena sat alone with the silver and the dregs of the poison-wine and the wind rising around the eaves.

She should leave in the morning. That was the smart play. Take the family route south, pick up autumn contract work, stay away from dying men with heavy coins and missing pages and eyes full of something she couldn't name. Trouble was written on him the way weather was written on clouds, and she had survived this long precisely by reading that sky and walking the other way.

But the silver sat on the table, and it was the first real money in months, and beneath the money — she was honest enough with herself to admit it, alone, in the blue dark of the window — was the other thing. The four missing pages. The consortium offices. *Watch their faces change.*

Nobody had ever offered to look at her record before. Everyone else had only ever looked at the board.

Lena swept the silver into her pocket, carried her cup to the bar, and asked the landlady for a room facing the stairs.

* * *

She woke once in the night to the sound of coughing through the floorboards — long, tearing, endless — and lay in the dark listening to it count itself down like a clock running out, and thought: *six weeks.*

Below the coughing, faint under the wind, she heard the front door open and close. Boots on frozen mud. More than one pair.

Lena slid out of bed without a sound and stood at the shuttered window, watching through the slats. Two men in the yard, moving with the unhurried purpose of men being paid by the day, checking the stable, checking the woodpile, checking the road. One of them wore a consortium badge on his collar, dull grey in the starlight.

They weren't looking for her. She was sure of it — almost sure. They were riding the road, shaking the inns, asking their questions. But their questions would be the same questions an old prospector had asked her over stew, and by morning, if the wrong ears overlapped at the wrong inn, the two sets of questions would find each other like water finding a crack.

In the room above hers, the coughing finally stopped.

Lena stood at the window a long time, one hand closed around the silver in her pocket, watching the men ride out eastward toward Ferrenholt, toward the consortium roads, toward whatever paper it was that Aldous Vane had spent twenty years trying to dig out of the ground.

Then she went back to bed and lay awake until the grey light came, listening to the mountain breathe, and made the only decision that had ever really been available to her.

She would hear him out.
