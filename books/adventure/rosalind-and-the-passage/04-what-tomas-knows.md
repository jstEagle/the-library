## 4. What Tomas Knows

The depot at Halden's Rest smelled of rope wax and kerosene, and Tomas Merel had been breathing that smell for six years the way other men breathed sea air—something that told him where he was, and who he had become.

He was checking manifest seals when the boy came in from the corral, cheeks bright with cold, and said, "There's a woman asking after east-face conditions. Won't give a name. Says she'll pay cash for the weather window and no questions."

Tomas didn't look up. "Tell her the window's closed."

"She said you'd say that." The boy shifted his weight. "She said to tell you the Kessel Face still owes you an answer."

The manifest sheet went still in his hands. Outside, the wind moved through the spruce with that low, grinding note it made before snow, and somewhere down the valley a cart creaked under a load too heavy for its axles, and Tomas stood in his warm, licensed, lawful depot and felt six years fall away like a cornice shearing off into nothing.

He knew the voice before he heard it. He knew it the way you know a route you've sworn never to climb again—every hold, every rotten placement, every place where the rock would take your weight and every place it would lie about it.

He walked out into the yard.

She stood by the rail fence with her pack still on, as if she'd never intended to take it off, as if she were a woman perpetually mid-stride. Lena Okafor. Thinner than he remembered, or maybe the same and he'd simply spent six years thickening into respectability—softened at the edges, padded in the middle, a man who wore a laminated guide's license on a lanyard like a talisman. Her hair was shorter. There was a new scar over her left eyebrow, a thin white seam that hadn't been there on the Kessel.

"You're not supposed to be on this side of the range," he said.

"I'm not on your side of the range." She nodded east, toward the wall of grey peaks where the light was already failing, though it was barely mid-afternoon. "I'm passing through. Your depot was the last one before the col."

"Passing through." He tasted the words and found them rotten. "That's what you called it last time. Passing through. Going for a look at the Kessel with a client who'd paid extra for the summit photo."

"Tomas."

"Don't." He held up a hand and was surprised to find it steady, when everything behind his sternum was shaking like a loose piton. "Don't say my name like we're still friends. Like we're still anything."

Her eyes—dark, level, that unnerving stillness she'd always had, the stillness that clients mistook for calm and that he had learned, too late, was something harder and more terrible than calm—didn't waver. "You sent for me."

"I sent for the truth," he said. "And what walked in was you."

* * *

He made her come inside, not out of kindness but because the yard had ears, and what he had to say was not for the boy or the teamster or the two licensed guides warming their hands at the depot stove, men who knew her face from the posted notices even if they'd never say so in front of him. The depot's back room was a cage of shelving and crate-stencils, and he shut the door on the light and stood with his back against it, arms crossed, a man guarding his own door against his own past.

"Say it," she said. She'd set her pack down at last, and without it she looked smaller, almost breakable, which he knew was a lie the way the Kessel's lower slabs were a lie—easy ground that killed you two pitches up. "Say what you've been saying to yourself for six years. Get it out. I'm not going anywhere until I've heard it."

"You don't get to decide that." His voice came out lower than he intended. "You don't get to walk in here and schedule my grief like a briefing."

"Your grief." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a dry, exhausted scrape, like a boot sole on gritstone. "You think I don't carry him too? You think there's a night I don't—"

"Don't you dare." He crossed the room in three strides and stopped close enough to see the chapped ruin of her lips, the exhaustion printed under her eyes in bruise-grey. "Halloran died on *your* rope. Your call. Your route. You looked at that face after the storm front and you said *we go up*, and I stood right there and I said nothing, because you were the lead and I was the second and that's how it works, that's how it's *supposed* to work, and then he fell and you—" His throat closed. He pushed past it the way you push past the burn at the top of a carry. "And then you stood in that magistrate's office and you lied."

"I didn't lie."

"You told them he ignored the turnaround time." The words came out of him now like rockfall, unstoppable, six years of them. "You told them you ordered him down and he kept climbing for the photograph. I was *there*, Lena. There was no photograph. There was no order. There was weather coming in and a man who trusted you because you'd never lost anyone, and you fed him to that face because he'd paid for the summit and you needed the fee."

The slap of it, when she struck him, was nothing—she was too tired, too hollowed out, and it landed like a bird hitting a window. But the sound of it rang in the little room, and they both stood breathing in the aftermath, and Tomas saw that her eyes were wet and furious and, worst of all, unrepentant.

"You were there," she said quietly. "So tell me what you saw. Tell me the part you've spent six years not saying."

"You know what I saw."

"I know what you *testified*." Her voice didn't rise. That was the thing about Lena that had always frightened him a little, even back when he'd loved her—trusted her, he corrected himself, trusted her like a brother—the thing that never rose, never broke, just went quieter and harder as the ground got worse. "You testified that you were checking the anchor when it happened. That you didn't see the exchange between us. That's what's in the record, Tomas. That's what's in *your* record, clean as new rope. So either the record's true, or you lied too. Which is it?"

The room seemed to tilt. Outside, the wind had found its winter voice, and somewhere a crate strap creaked under tension, and Tomas stood in his lawful life and felt it all go thin as ice over a crevasse.

"I was checking the anchor," he said.

"Then we're done." She turned for her pack.

"You don't get to do that." He grabbed her shoulder and she let him, which was worse than fighting. "You don't get to come in here, dig all of it up, and walk out with the last word. You've done that for six years. One letter a year, first snow, no return address. *I'm well. Don't look for me.* One letter, Lena. Like I'm a creditor."

"You were safer not knowing."

"I was *buried* not knowing." He released her, stepped back, pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until the dark bloomed red. When he could speak again it came out flat and tired, all the anger spent down to the grief that had been under it the whole time, the way the storm had been under the fair weather on the Kessel, invisible until it was on top of you. "Do you know what I do now? I guide bankers up scree slopes. I teach rope rescue to men who've never been cold in their lives. I have a license, Lena. A laminated license. And every time I laminate something, every time I file a route report in triplicate, I think: Halloran is dead because we were real once, and this is what fake costs."

She was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—there was something under it now, something urgent and afraid, and it was that, more than anything, that made him turn and really look at her.

"I need to cross the range," she said. "The high routes. East to west, above the snowline, in the next six weeks. And I need to not be seen doing it."

"Six weeks." He laughed despite himself. "The passes close in four. The consortium's got weather stations on every legal col, and Renn's magistrates are pulling every undocumented crosser between here and Corven. You'd need routes that aren't on any map. You'd need—"

He stopped. Because they both knew what she'd need. She'd need the only guide alive who'd climbed the Verren's high traverse with her, who knew the hidden notch below the Serra col, who knew where the glacier had retreated and left a new ramp of ice that no licensed survey had ever recorded. She'd need the man he'd been, and the man he'd been was buried under six years of laminated paper on the Kessel Face.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard what it's for."

"I don't care what it's for. Whatever it is, it's a lie, or a debt, or a dead man's errand, and all three of those things have already taken everything from me once." He unlocked the door and stood holding it open, and the light from the depot room fell across her in a hard yellow bar. "Go, Lena. Go back to your side of the range. Go be a ghost."

She picked up her pack. At the door she stopped, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and cold stone on her, and she looked up at him with those still dark eyes, and she said, "The man who died on the Kessel Face—his name was Aldous Vane, and he was dying anyway, and he gave me a map, and I promised. That's all you get. That's all I can give you."

"That's not what I—"

"Goodbye, Tomas."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in the depot's back room with a single lamp and the manifest sheets he was supposed to be auditing, and he thought about the Kessel Face, and about the turnaround time, and about the exact words Lena had said at the base of the last pitch, words he had testified he never heard, words he had spent six years pretending he hadn't heard.

*We go up. He wants the photograph. We can beat the weather.*

And underneath it, quieter, in the voice she used only when the ground was bad: *Unless you say no, Tomas. Say no and we turn around and I'll take the blame for the lost fee.*

And he had said nothing. He had checked his anchor. He had been a second, which is what seconds were for, which is what he'd told himself every night for six years, and every night the story had held a little less weight, the way old rope holds less and less until the day it doesn't hold at all.

Because here was the thing he had never said, not in the magistrate's office, not in the inquiry, not in the letters he'd never sent: Halloran *had* ignored the turnaround. Halloran *had* kept climbing when Lena called him down—she'd called it twice, and Tomas had heard it, and then the wind had come up the face like a freight train and the world had gone white and when it cleared there was no Halloran, just the rope going slack with that terrible wrong lightness, and Lena kneeling at the belay with her hands over her face saying *I ordered him down, I ordered him down, I ordered him down*—and later, in the magistrate's office, when the consortium's lawyers had circled like gulls, when it became clear that a dead client who disobeyed orders would leave his widow nothing but the consortium's counterclaim and ruin, Lena had looked at Tomas across the table and said, in that low voice, *Let them blame me. She'll need the payout. Say you didn't hear.*

And he had said he didn't hear. He had taken the clean record and the license and the lawful life, and he had let her carry the fall.

He put his head in his hands. The lamp guttered. Outside, the first flakes of the early storm came ticking against the window like fingernails, and Tomas Merel, licensed guide, man of triplicate and lanyard, sat in the dark of his own making and understood, with the sick clarity of a man reading a route he should have read six years ago, that he had not been her second on the Kessel Face at all.

He had been her alibi.

He was still sitting there when the boy knocked, an hour past midnight, snow in his hair, breath ragged.

"Sir—the woman. The one who was asking about the east face. She's gone. Took the high trail out of the yard before the storm closed it, on foot, alone. And sir—" the boy swallowed, "—there's a man at the inn asking after her too. Big fellow. Consortium papers. He's offering silver for her route, and magistrate's writ to go with it. Says she's carrying stolen property."

Tomas stood. His legs were steadier than they had any right to be.

"Saddle the grey," he said. "And get my storm kit down from the loft."

The boy stared. "Sir, that's the closed side. In a storm. That's—"

"I know what side it is." He was already pulling his old rope from the bottom of the equipment locker, the one he'd never re-sold, the one with the Kessel's wear still in its sheath, coiled all these years like a held breath. "I'm her second," he said, and heard, for the first time in six years, that it was true. "It's how it works."
