## 5. Vane Dies at Dawn

Lena woke to the sound of coughing through the wall — the wet, tearing kind that had kept her half-alert all night, the kind she'd heard before in men who had breathed too much rock dust for too many years. The inn's timbers ticked as the cold deepened toward morning. Somewhere below, the fire in the common room had burned down to a red memory, and the smell of last night's stew still hung in the stairwell like a guest who wouldn't leave.

She lay in the dark and did the arithmetic she always did before dawn: how much money, how many days, how far. The family from the low col had paid her well enough to eat for three weeks if she was careful. The name posted in the border town at Torvask meant she couldn't go back down that way, which meant the eastern routes, which meant longer, colder, thinner margins. And above all of it, folded into the oilcloth wallet against her ribs, sat Aldous Vane's map — six weeks of promise, a daughter named Mireille in Corven, and a lode of silver sitting under land she hadn't yet seen.

The coughing stopped.

She noticed the stopping before she noticed anything else. A man like Vane didn't stop coughing because he felt better. He stopped because he was too tired to continue, or because something in his chest had finally given up the argument.

Lena dressed in the dark, boots in hand, and crossed the hall on bare feet. The floorboards announced her anyway; old buildings always did. She knocked once at his door, softly, the way you knock when you already know.

"Aldous."

Nothing. Then, faintly, a sound like wind moving through a crack — breath drawn thin and let go again.

She opened the door.

He was propped against the headboard where he'd been the night before, but he had arranged himself with a deliberateness that told her he'd known this was coming. His coat was on. His hands were folded over the journal in his lap, the leather-bound one with the cracked spine she'd seen him writing in by lamplight, hunched like a man guarding bread. His eyes were open and fixed on the frost-ferned window, where the first grey of dawn was seeping into the glass.

"Lena Okafor," he said. His voice was a husk dragged over gravel. "You're up early."

"So are you."

A laugh turned into a cough turned into a long, shuddering silence. When it passed, there was blood on his lip, a dark thread of it, and he wiped it away with his thumb as casually as a man brushing off crumbs. "I wanted to see the light come up on the Verren one more time. From the east side. You get a different mountain depending on where the sun finds it first." He looked at her then, and his eyes were clear — terribly, lucidly clear, the clarity of a man whose body had stopped wasting effort on anything but the essential. "Sit down. We're not walking out of here together, you and I. I've known that since Torvask. I think you've known it since the stairs."

Lena pulled the room's single chair around and sat, facing him, the way you sit across from a patient or a client about to sign something binding. Outside, the sky was going from ash to pearl. The peaks east of the inn caught the first light and held it, pink and merciless.

"I need you to listen," Vane said. "I don't have the strength for speeches, so I'll say it once and you'll remember it, because you have that kind of memory. I can see it in you. Guides have it. You remember every route, every lie, every debt." He drew a breath that whistled. "The map is real. The lode is real. I walked it twenty-two years ago with my own eyes and a hammer, and I sampled it, and I hid the samples where no consortium surveyor would ever think to look. The permits they hold on that concession are built on fraud — falsified surveys, bought magistrates, a valley they poisoned and then paid to forget. My claim predates theirs. It was never registered because registering it meant putting it in front of the people who owned the registry." Another breath, thinner. "Six weeks. That's what's left on their permit window. If the claim isn't contested — if someone stands up in Corven with proof before the window closes — it reverts to them forever, and everything I did was for nothing, and Mireille gets nothing but a dead father's silence."

"You could write all this down," Lena said quietly. "You *have* written it down. Give me the papers and rest."

"The papers are only half of it." His hand moved on the journal, restless. "Papers can be stolen. Papers can be dismissed. What can't be dismissed is a person standing in a hearing room saying *I was there, I saw the water run grey, I watched children cough the way I'm coughing now.*" He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gallows-thin. "That's your part. Not just carrying the map. Carrying the witness. You're the last living person besides me who's stood on that ground, Lena. I made sure of that when I hired you. I read about you for two years before I approached you. Your reputation for getting people over ground nobody else will cross — and your reputation for keeping your word even when it costs you. Especially then."

The light was strengthening. It came through the window and lay across the bed in a long pale bar, touching his folded hands, the journal, the hollows of his face. He looked, suddenly, like a man carved out of the same material as the range outside — weathered, fissured, holding its shape out of pure stubbornness.

"There's an envelope in the journal's back cover," he said. "Money. Not enough to insult you, enough to keep you fed to Corven. There's a letter to Mireille. Don't read it. Some things belong to their addressee alone." His breath caught, held, released. "And there's a page torn from my survey book, 22 years old, with the assay figures. Those figures are the fraud's death warrant. Lose the journal and live, lose the money and live — lose that page and we're finished, all of us, everyone in that valley, everyone downstream of their tailings ponds. Do you understand what I'm handing you?"

Lena understood. She understood it the way you understand a load shift on a steep pitch — instantly, viscerally, in the body before the mind. He wasn't giving her a map. He was giving her a target with a human face, evidence worth killing for, and a debt that would follow her past her own grave if she let it.

"I understand," she said.

"Say it back to me."

"Corven. Before the permits expire. Mireille Vane. The page stays with me or dies with me."

"And the rest?"

She hesitated. He watched her hesitate, and something in his expression softened — the first softness she'd seen in him, a father's ghost surfacing through the prospector's iron.

"And the rest," she said slowly, "the valley, the fraud, the poisoning — I'll do what I can. I won't promise justice. I've been on the wrong end of other people's versions of justice too long to promise it to anyone."

"That," Vane said, "is the most honest thing anyone has said to me in twenty years." He lifted the journal — it cost him visibly, arms trembling with the effort of a weight that should have been nothing — and held it out. "Take it now. While I'm still me. While it's a gift and not a theft from a corpse."

Lena took it. The leather was warm from his hands, and heavier than paper had any right to be.

He sank back against the headboard, spent, and for a while neither of them spoke. The sun cleared the eastern ridge and flooded the window, and the room went gold and ordinary, and Aldous Vane looked out at the Verren Range with an expression she recognized from mirrors — the look of a person measuring a distance they would never cross.

"I left her when she was nine," he said, to the mountains rather than to Lena. "Told myself I was doing it *for* her. Keeping the poison away from my family by walking away from it. Twenty-two years of telling myself that story until I believed it." A pause. "She won't forgive me. She shouldn't. Just — make sure she knows I died facing the right direction. That's all. Facing toward her, at the end. Make sure she knows that."

"I'll tell her," Lena said. "In my own words. The true ones."

"Good." His eyes were closing. "Good girl. Wake me when—"

He didn't finish it. His breathing changed — not stopped, not yet, but changed, dropping into a rhythm that was less breathing than leaking, each breath a small negotiation failing to close. Lena stayed in the chair. She had sat vigil before, for clients and strangers and once, horribly, for Tomas in a snow cave on the Kessel Face, listening to breath and waiting to learn whether it would continue. She knew how to do nothing well. She did nothing for two hours while the sun climbed and the gold light went white and the inn woke below her — pans clattering, the innkeeper's voice, a cart in the lane.

At half past eight, between one breath and the next, Aldous Vane stopped negotiating.

Lena sat with him a while longer. Then she closed his eyes with two fingers, the way her grandmother had taught her — gently, as if the eyelids were something breakable — and took the journal, and the envelope, and the small canvas pack he'd traveled with, and went downstairs to tell the innkeeper that the man in room three would not be needing breakfast, and to ask, in the same breath, whether anyone else had asked after him.

The innkeeper, a broad woman with flour to her elbows, frowned. "Funny you mention it. Fellow came in late last night. Big man, quiet, paid cash for a room and hasn't shown his face. Asked at the desk this morning which room the old prospector was in." She wiped her hands. "Said he was a business associate."

"What name?"

"He gave none. But he had a way of looking at you like he was weighing you for shipping." She glanced toward the stairs, then back, lowering her voice. "He's in the common room now. Eating. Waiting, I'd say."

Cold moved up Lena's spine, slow and precise, like water finding a crack in stone.

* * *

Brakke was eating eggs when she came down, methodically, the way a man eats who has never once worried about where the next meal is coming from. He was big through the shoulders without being heavy, close-cropped hair going grey at the temples, hands flat and clean on either side of his plate. His gear — and she noted it automatically, the way she noted rockfall zones — was expedition-grade, consortium-issue, maintained with a care that said discipline rather than vanity. He sat with his back to the wall and his face to the door, and when she entered, he looked up without hurry, as if he'd been expecting her for some time and was pleased she'd finally arrived.

"Rosalind Kavanagh," he said. Not Lena — Rosalind, the name from the blacklists, the name from the Kessel Face inquiry, spoken softly, correctly, like a man laying a card face-up on a table. "I hoped it was you. The description from Torvask was good, but descriptions usually aren't."

Lena kept walking, poured herself coffee from the pot on the hearth, and sat down at the table across from him without being invited. Two could play at composure. Her heart was a fist striking at the inside of her ribs, but her hands, when she wrapped them around the cup, were steady. Twenty years of guiding had taught her that fear was information, not instruction.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said.

"Brakke." He set down his fork. "I work in compliance for the Meridian Consortium. Concession integrity. It's duller than it sounds." His eyes were pale grey, almost colorless, and utterly patient. "Aldous Vane checked into this inn four days ago with a chest complaint and a reputation. This morning he's dead, and you're carrying his pack down to the cart. I'd call that a remarkable coincidence, except I stopped believing in those around my thirtieth year."

"He was dying when I met him. Anyone with ears could hear it."

"I don't doubt it. Consumption's honest that way." Brakke folded his hands. "What I'm curious about is what a dying man with no assets and a grudge twenty years old thought he could accomplish in the Verren Range in winter. And why he needed a guide with your particular — history — to accomplish it."

"He wanted to see a mountain," Lena said. "Dying men do that."

"They do." He smiled, and it was a courteous smile, and it reached nowhere near his eyes. "They also write letters, settle debts, and give things away. I've sat with several. They become very generous at the end, and very careless about who's standing nearby when the generosity happens." He leaned forward slightly. "Ms. Kavanagh — Rosalind — I'm going to be direct, because I suspect directness is the only currency you trade in. Whatever Aldous Vane gave you, it's worthless. Worse than worthless. Any claim, any document, any assertion arising from his time in that range is legally void, preempted by valid concession, and actionable. If you carry it forward, you aren't honoring a dead man's wishes. You're committing interference with lawful commercial operations, and you'll be doing it in territory where my authority is — let's say — efficiently exercised." He paused. "Or you can hand it to me now, walk away clean, and I'll note your cooperation. I can even make the Torvask posting inconvenient to enforce. Consider it a professional courtesy between people who both move through difficult country."

There it was. Lena drank her coffee and let the silence stretch, because silence was a tool too, and she'd learned from the best how to let it cut. Through the window behind Brakke she could see the eastern peaks, white and enormous, the high routes glowing in full sun, and she thought: *he doesn't know the routes. He has authority and money and patience, but he doesn't know where the mountain keeps its doors.*

"Mr. Brakke," she said at last, "I'm a guide. A man hired me to take him somewhere. He died before we started. I'm taking his belongings to his next of kin, because that's what a decent person does. If the Consortium has a problem with a widow or a daughter receiving a dead man's letters, you're welcome to explain that policy to her yourself."

Something flickered in his face — not anger; calculation, quick and cold, revising a figure. "His next of kin," Brakke repeated. "And that would be?"

Lena stood, put coins on the table for the coffee, and picked up Vane's pack. "Ask the registry," she said. "Oh — wait. Your people own that too, don't they."

She felt his gaze on her back all the way across the room, level and unblinking, a man doing arithmetic. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and turned half around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "he wasn't afraid of you. He talked about the Consortium the way you'd talk about weather. Something that ruins crops. Something you build around."

Brakke considered this. "Dead men talk easily," he said. "It's the living I'm interested in."

Lena stepped out into the morning. The cold hit her like a hand pressed flat against her chest — clean, enormous, indifferent. Above the inn the Verren Range stood in full light, and somewhere beyond those walls of ice and stone lay a valley with grey water, a daughter who didn't yet know her father was dead, and six weeks ticking down like water through a crack.

She tightened the straps of the pack, settled the journal against her ribs beside the map, and started walking — not toward the road, not toward the checkpoint, but toward the tree line, toward the routes that existed on no registry, in the one country where she had never yet been a criminal.

Behind her, faintly, she heard the inn door open, and boots come down into the snow, unhurried, following.
