## 6. Corven

The water came into Corven grey.

Not always — not on the clean days after hard frost, when the high snows locked themselves down and nothing melted, and the river ran the brief, honest green of stone and glacier. But most mornings, when Mireille Vane walked down from her rooms above the clinic and crossed the footbridge to work, the water below her carried a faint milkiness, a suspended shimmer of tailings fine as flour, and she had long since stopped seeing it the way a person stops seeing a stain on their own ceiling. The children of the lower quarter had never stopped seeing it. They pointed at the river and called it the ghost, because that was what it looked like — something dead, still walking.

She tied her hair back in the clinic's anteroom, where the smell was carbolic and wet wool and, beneath it, something faintly metallic that clung to everything now, to the linens, to the plaster, to the inside of her own nose when she blew it at the end of a long day. The stove had been lit before dawn by old Sefa, who did everything in this building before dawn and asked credit for none of it. Mireille warmed her hands over it anyway, a small theft of comfort she permitted herself the way other people permitted themselves a cigarette.

"Two from Harrow Bend came in overnight," Sefa said, not looking up from the ledger she kept in a hand like bird tracks. "The Ferro children. Both vomiting since yesterday. And a man from the tannery quarter with the shakes so bad he couldn't sign his own name."

"Which Ferro children?"

"The small ones."

They were all small ones now. That was the thing no one in the concession offices upriver would say aloud, the thing that lived in the pause when Mireille read the quarterly water reports and found the arsenic figures rounded down, always down, always to just beneath the line where a fine could be levied. The adults sickened slowly and were called unlucky. The children sickened quickly, and were called a tragedy, and the tragedy was budgeted for the way one budgets for weather.

She went in to the Ferro children first.

* * *

They lay in the same bed because there was only the one bed, a boy of six and a girl of four, both grey-lipped, both with the particular listlessness she had learned to dread more than fever — fever at least fought. Their mother sat between them like a woman guarding a border crossing of her own, and when Mireille lifted the girl's wrist the mother said, "It's the water. Don't tell me it isn't the water."

"I won't tell you it isn't the water," Mireille said.

"Because the man from the company came round in the autumn. He had a coat on that cost more than our roof. He said the levels were within tolerance." The mother's voice had the flatness of someone repeating a lesson learned by heart. "He said tolerance."

"Tolerance," Mireille repeated, and wrote it in the chart, because she wrote everything down, because she had learned that in Corven the only weapon the poor were issued was paper. She flushed the girl's stomach, set the boy on a slow drip of the electrolyte solution she mixed herself from salts and boiled water and a stubbornness that Sefa said reminded her of a mule, and gave the mother instructions she knew would be followed to the letter, because the mothers here followed everything to the letter. It was the companies that rounded down.

By midmorning the waiting room was full, and the day became what her days became: a procession of bellies and tremors and the dull skin lesions she could treat and the deeper things she could not, the slow accumulations in bone and blood that she could only record, and record, and record. She was good at this. She knew she was good at it, and the knowing gave her no pleasure, because she had come to Corven seven years ago out of spite and the spite had long since burned down to its coals, and what was left was competence, which is what spite leaves behind when it dies.

At noon she ate standing at the window of the dispensary, bread and hard cheese, looking down the valley toward the works.

The Verren Consolidated smelter stood eleven kilometres downstream, close enough that on still evenings its smoke laid a brown thumbprint across the sunset, far enough that its lawyers could stand in any courtroom in the province and say the word *adjacent* in a tone that made the word mean *unrelated*. The tailings ponds caught the light and held it, beautiful in the way of oil on water, all the colours of ruin. She had watched those ponds for seven years. She had petitioned about them, testified about them, stood in the rain outside the magistrate's office in Corven with a placard about them until the constables, who were not unkind men, suggested with real embarrassment that she might catch her death.

And every time she looked at them, underneath the anger, in the cellar of herself where she kept the things she did not take out, lived her father.

* * *

He had taught her the names of ores.

That was the cruelty of it, that her first memories of Aldous Vane were not of absence but of abundance — his hands, cracked and silvered with dust that never washed fully out, turning a stone in the firelight while he named what lived inside it. Galena, he'd say. See the cubic cleavage, mouse. See how it breaks square. Everything honest breaks along its true lines, if you know how to look. She had been six. She had believed, with the whole of her six-year-old heart, that her father could see through mountains.

Then the consortium had come to the valley where they lived then, the first valley, before Corven, and there had been the permit, and the sale, and the leaving — and she had learned later, in fragments, the shape of it: that he had signed away mineral rights to land other families still lived on, that the money had lasted eleven months, that he had gone up into the range one spring with a pack and a promise to come back rich and had simply not come back. Not dead. Worse. *Elsewhere.* A letter twice a year, then once a year, then a card, then nothing, while she grew up in her aunt's house in Corven learning to answer the question *what does your father do* with a shrug that hardened over the years into armor, and then into a wall, and then into a face she wore so long it stopped being a face she wore.

*What does your father do.* He looks through mountains, she had said once, at nine, and been laughed at, and never said anything again.

Now she was thirty-one, and she looked through mountains herself in the only way available to her — through the quarterly water reports, through the assay data she had taught herself to read at the clinic's back table with the same lamp her father would have used, and everything honest broke along its true lines, and the true lines of the Verren Consolidated's permits ran straight through the bodies of the Ferro children, and no one in the province with the power to do anything would follow them there.

"Dr. Vane." Sefa stood in the dispensary doorway with a letter in her hand, and something in the old woman's face made Mireille set down her bread. "Post rider brought it up from the low town. It's — it's addressed to you, but I think it's been looking for you a long time. It's been forwarded. Three, four times. Look at the dates."

The envelope was soft with handling, its corners rounded, the address on it rewritten in different hands as it had been forwarded from a claim office to a supply depot to a hostel to, at last, the clinic in Corven, as though the letter itself had been walking the range for years looking for her the way her father never had. The final hand was shaky, the letters tall and thin, written by someone conserving strength.

She turned it over. No return address. The postmark was a border town she'd never heard of, on the far side of the range, and the date was six weeks old.

"You don't have to open it," Sefa said.

"I know I don't have to open it."

She opened it anyway, because she was her father's daughter, and everything honest breaks along its true lines, and she had never once in her life been able to leave a stone unturned to see what lived inside it.

* * *

The letter was short. She read it standing at the window with the smelter smoke rising in the distance, and she read it twice, and the second time her hands were not steady, which angered her, because her hands were the one part of her that never shook, not over patients, not over anything.

*Mouse* — the word hit her first, like a hand out of the past, like being six again — *I don't ask you to forgive. I'm past asking for things for myself. But I've found it. What I went up there the first time looking for, and every time since. A lode the consortium's surveys missed, on land they had no right to file against, and I have the proof of it in my hands and not long left in my chest. I've sent it with a woman who I believe will bring it to you. Her name is Lena Okafor. Trust her as far as you'd trust me, which I know is not far, but farther than you think. The permits expire in six weeks. When they do, the claim dies with them unless someone stands up in the room and says the truth out loud. I'm asking you to be the one who says it. Not for me. For the water. For the children. I know what's in the water, mouse. I've known what they did to get those permits for eleven years, and I've spent eleven years failing to prove it, and now the proof and the ore are in the same place and I can't carry them down the mountain myself. You always could carry more than me. — A.V.*

She read it a third time. *Not long left in my chest.* The phrase sat in her like a swallowed stone.

"He's dying," she said aloud, and was surprised to find that her voice came out flat, factual, a chart notation. *Patient reports chest pain of eleven years' duration. Prognosis: long overdue.*

Sefa said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

Mireille looked at the letter, and then out the window at the tailings ponds holding their beautiful ruinous light, and she made herself do the arithmetic she did not want to do. If the postmark was six weeks old, and the letter had spent those weeks walking the range looking for her, then the six weeks of the permits had already begun. The clock her father had started was already running, and she was standing at the wrong end of it, eleven kilometres and a lifetime of silence away from the room where the truth would need to be said aloud.

And underneath the arithmetic, in the cellar, something moved that she refused to name — not grief, she told herself, not yet, not for that man. It was only the old question, the one from when she was nine, turning over in the dark like a stone in firelight: *what does your father do.*

He dies, she thought. Apparently that's what he does. He goes up into the mountains and he dies, and he sends a stranger down to tell me, because even now he can't face the door himself.

She put the letter in her chart bag, under the water reports, and went back in to the Ferro children, because the children were real and the letter might be one more of her father's promises, and she had a four-year-old on a drip who needed the solution changed.

But she did not sleep that night. She sat at the back table with the lamp, and she read the quarterly reports again, and this time she read them the way her father had taught her to read stone — looking for the true lines, the places where everything honest breaks — and at two in the morning she found one, a discrepancy in the filing dates of the original concession permits, small as a seam of ore, and she sat very still in the cold room with her heart going hard, and outside the window the river ran grey past the sleeping town, carrying the ghost of the mountains down toward the sea.

Three days later, the post rider came again, and this time he did not have a letter. He had word from the high trail — a name, and a question. A woman asking after the clinic. A guide, the rider said, from the far side of the range, with a partner, and they were coming down out of the high country ahead of the snows, and the woman's name was Okafor.

Mireille stood in the anteroom with the carbolic smell in her nose and her father's letter a soft weight against her hip, and she heard herself say, in a voice she did not recognize as her own:

"When she gets here — bring her to me first. Before anyone else. Bring her to me first."
