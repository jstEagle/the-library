## 8. Over the Wall by Night

The cell at the checkpoint station was not a cell. That was the first thing Lena noticed, and the last comfort she allowed herself. It was a room with a bolted door and a cot with a grey blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's sweat, and Magistrate Ilse Renn had called it *guest quarters* twice in the same sentence, as if repetition could make it true.

They had taken her rope, her axe, her pack. They had left her boots, which told her something — Renn wanted her mobile, wanted her to walk out of here on her own feet when the bargain was signed, not carried out in irons. Boots were an invitation. Lena sat on the cot's edge and looked at them for a long time, the way she'd once looked at weather coming over a ridge, reading what the mountain intended before the mountain knew itself.

Outside the single barred window, dusk was going violet over the lower shoulder of the Verren Range. She could see the wall of it from here — the great rampart of grey rock and hanging ice that separated the administered lowlands from everything north, everything unregistered, unpatrolled, unnamed on half the maps. Somewhere beyond it, six weeks ticking down like water dripping into a bucket, Mireille Vane was living a life that didn't yet know her father was dead.

Lena pressed her thumb against the map's absence. She had hidden it well — sewn flat against the inside of her jacket's storm flap, cut and re-stitched so cleanly that even Renn's thorough hands had found nothing but a woman's coat. The journal too, wrapped in oilcloth under the false sole of her left boot, which was why they had left her the boots: they hadn't thought to look, because no one hides anything in the thing they let you keep.

*You're already a criminal,* she reminded herself. *Has been true for years. The only question is which crime you commit next.*

But that wasn't quite honest either, and honesty was the one discipline she'd never been able to abandon, even when it cost her everything. The truth was that until now, every law she'd broken had felt like paperwork. Undocumented crossings, forged manifests, cash paid into her hand by people who could not exist on ledgers — these were crimes the way breathing was a crime in a house where the windows had been painted shut. She had never once felt like a criminal doing them. She had felt like a door.

What she was about to do would feel different. Escaping custody. Striking a magistrate's offered hand away. That wasn't breathing through painted windows. That was choosing the dark.

She heard Renn's voice through the wall, low and level, speaking to the two constables who had brought her in. Renn had a voice like a file drawn slowly across iron — patient, certain of eventually getting through. *In the morning,* the voice said. *She'll sign in the morning. Give her a night to understand how few doors are open to her.*

Lena almost smiled. Doors.

She waited until the lamp in the corridor guttered low and the constables' card game dissolved into snores and the smell of cheap tobacco stopped rising under the door. Then she worked the window bar loose — it had been rusted at the base for years; she'd tested it within ten minutes of being locked in, the way she tested every anchor point in every room she'd ever be locked inside — and slid out into air so cold it tasted of stone.

* * *

The station sat on a shelf above the river valley, one building, one corral, a flagpole with a line snapping in the wind. Two horses dozed hipshot in the corral. A dog lifted its head as she crossed the yard, considered her, and decided — wisely, Lena thought — that whatever this was, it was above its pay grade. It put its head back down.

She went past the horses without touching them. A stolen horse was a trail, and a trail was a debt, and she owed enough debts. Instead she took what she needed from the drying shed: a coil of half-rotted but serviceable hemp, a handful of pitons from a crate stamped with the consortium's mark — irony noted, filed, used — and a block of tallow the cook used on his pans, which would serve for chapped hands and runner wax both.

Then she stood at the edge of the yard and looked up at the wall.

By day the Verren Range was beautiful the way a blade is beautiful. By night, under a moon three days off full, it was something else entirely — a country of silver and black, the glaciers glowing faintly like buried lamps, the rock faces holding shadows so deep they seemed structural. The wind came down off it carrying the smell of ice and old snow and, beneath that, the mineral breath of stone warming and cooling, the mountain's own slow respiration.

Her whole body turned toward it the way a compass needle turns. Not toward freedom — freedom was a word politicians used. Toward *work*. Toward the only cathedral she'd ever believed in.

There were three ways over the wall from this side. The Kessel Pass was the legal route, patrolled, gated, closed at night — Renn would have wire out already, or would by dawn. The second was the Marn Col, a long scree traverse that was legal-ish, tolerated, watched. And the third was the route that existed nowhere, that lived only in her legs and her memory and a series of cairns she herself had built in years when building cairns on that side of the line was itself an offense: the chimney system above the Grey Larches, the hanging valley behind the Fang, the ice ramp she called the Staircase because it went up and up and then, near the top, simply stopped pretending to be anything but vertical.

No guide used the Staircase. No guide *could* use the Staircase. It required knowing exactly which of the forty frozen gullies feeding the upper face was solid and which was a death trap dressed in the same beautiful rime, and that knowledge was hers alone, paid for across eleven seasons, paid for in frostbite and fear and one terrible afternoon she did not let herself think about.

The mountain, she thought, beginning to climb. The mountain is the one place I've never been a criminal.

It wasn't strictly true. She had broken regulations on it, skirted borders across it, moved human beings over it like contraband when they were only families with children and paper hearts full of hope. But the mountain itself had never judged her. The mountain didn't care about her name on a posting in some border town, didn't care about blacklists or magistrates or enforcers named Brakke. The mountain cared about one thing only: whether you read it correctly. Every other sin was forgiven or punished on its own terms, instantly, impartially. It was the fairest court she'd ever stood in.

She climbed through the Grey Larches as the moon rose fully clear, and the world went sharp as etching. Frost furred the dead grass. Her breath plumed and hung. Below her, tiny as a toy village, the checkpoint station showed one lit window — Renn's, probably, the magistrate working late over files, over Lena's file, over the bargain Lena had refused. *Sign in the morning.* Well. The morning would find an empty room, a loose bar, and a magistrate learning what it cost to offer a mountain guide a cage with a view of her own church.

The exhilaration hit her halfway up the larch slope, sudden as altitude. It always did. There was a moment in every serious climb — usually around the point where retreat became more expensive than commitment — when fear flipped over into something bright and singing, and her body remembered that this, *this*, was what it was for. Not inn rooms. Not bargains. Not signatures. This: cold air, burning thighs, the whole dark world reduced to the next hold and the next and the next, until the self became nothing but attention, and attention became a kind of grace.

She moved fast and light, counting her steps, testing each one. The tallow went on her hands at the treeline. Above that, the chimneys began.

* * *

The Staircase at midnight was a lesson in humility delivered at speed.

The gully she'd chosen — the third of the forty, the one with the chockstone shaped like a sleeping animal two-thirds up — had changed since last season. A summer's meltwater had undercut its base, and the ice that should have welded the rubble together was instead a thin glaze over void. She discovered this by putting her weight on it and hearing the mountain clear its throat: a sound like a giant cracking knuckles somewhere below the rock.

Lena froze. Breathed. Backed down three moves, traversed left onto the rib between gullies, and found the old piton she'd placed four years ago, still there, still sound, its hanger furred with frost. She clipped the scavenged rope to it with fingers gone clumsy and slow, and stood a moment on the tiny stance with her heart slamming and the drop opening beneath her like a held breath.

*Terror now,* she thought. *Good. Terror means you're paying attention.*

That was the secret no one who hadn't climbed ever understood: exhilaration and terror weren't opposites. They were the same current, run through different wiring. Up here, at night, alone, on a route with no name and no witnesses and no rescue, she felt more alive than she had in months of inn rooms and cash payments and careful, exhausted survival. The mountain stripped everything down to the essential question — *can you read me?* — and she could, tonight, she still could, and the joy of that was indistinguishable from the fear of it.

She climbed for hours. The moon crossed the sky and the temperature fell through layers of cold into a deeper cold that made the rope stiffen and the rock bite. Twice she stopped in stances barely wide enough for her boots and ate snow, letting it melt against her palate, watching the lights of the lowland towns glitter far below like spilled coins — Renn's jurisdiction, all of it, every light a law. Above her there were no lights at all. Just the Fang's black fin against the stars, and beyond it the high range, and beyond that, Corven, and a daughter who didn't know.

At the hanging valley behind the Fang, she allowed herself the luxury of shelter: a shallow cave behind a flake of granite, floored with old snow, walled from the wind. She did not dare a fire. She sat with her back to stone, pulled her knees in, and let the shivering have her for a while, because fighting it cost more than spending it.

In the dark, the thoughts she'd been outrunning caught up with her.

Renn would know by dawn. Renn would wire ahead — not to stop her, Lena suspected, but to watch her. The magistrate was not a stupid woman; she was worse than stupid, she was *patient*, and she had understood something in their interview that Lena was only now admitting to herself: that the map was worth nothing dead in a drawer and everything moving. Renn hadn't offered to buy the map. She'd offered to buy *Lena* — her silence, her testimony, her name cleaned — because a bought guide was a tool, and tools could be pointed at things. At valleys. At communities downstream of poisoned water. At whatever the consortium needed disappeared next.

And Brakke. Brakke would hear she was loose, and Brakke did not file reports; Brakke solved problems. Somewhere behind her, downhill, downhill, downhill, a man who genuinely believed order was worth any price was learning that his problem had grown legs.

Lena pulled the boot sole loose and checked the journal by moonlight, just to see it, just to touch the fact of it. Aldous Vane's cramped handwriting marched across the pages like ants determined to reach somewhere before winter. *For Mireille, if she'll have it,* he'd written on the flyleaf, and beneath that, in fresher ink, shakier: *Tell her I kept trying. Tell her the trying was the point.*

She wrapped it back in oilcloth. She resewed the sole. Her hands obeyed her, steady now, the terror burned down to embers, and she understood that this was the trade she had made tonight, the real one, the one no magistrate had brokered: she had traded safety for purpose, and purpose, God help her, fit her better.

Before first light she was moving again, up the final ramps of the Staircase, where the route stopped pretending. The ice was blue and ancient and held her front points like a promise. She topped out onto the col as the sun broke the eastern wall and set the whole high range burning — glacier after glacier, ridge after ridge, a wilderness of white and gold with no border posts, no checkpoints, no names.

Lena stood on the line between jurisdictions and breathed.

Below her, on the northern side, the snows lay deep and unbroken. Somewhere out there was Tomas — or wasn't; she couldn't know, wouldn't let herself wonder. Somewhere out there was the route to Corven, six weeks shortening to five. And somewhere behind her, faint on the wind, she could almost believe she heard the far-off sound of the checkpoint station waking to an empty room.

She spat tallow-bitterness off her lips, settled her collar, and started down into the white.

Behind her, far below at the mouth of the Grey Larches, something small and dark detached itself from the treeline and began, unhurried, to climb.
