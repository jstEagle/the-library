## 9. Tomas Follows

The client was a banker from the lowlands named Herrick, and he paid double the standard rate because he wanted to summit the Wrenspire before the season turned. Tomas had taken the booking in March, back when the calendar still looked like a promise, and he had spent four days teaching the man to place his feet on scree and trust the rope. On the fifth morning, standing in the cold blue light below the Wrenspire's shoulder, Tomas looked east across the range and saw the storm.

Not a weather storm. Not yet. A storm of a different kind, the kind he had spent eleven years learning to read: a smudge of cloud sitting wrong over the eastern passes, too low, too dark, too early in the year. And beneath that cloud, somewhere in the high country where no licensed guide had any business being, was Lena Okafor.

He knew it the way he knew an approaching avalanche — not by seeing it, but by the silence underneath everything else.

"You're not listening to me, Herrick." Tomas crouched by the stove in the hut, warming his hands. The banker sat across from him, well-fed and well-equipped, everything about him purchased rather than earned. "The window's closing. We go down tomorrow or we don't go down at all."

"We have six days left on the permit."

"The mountain doesn't read permits."

Herrick laughed, the easy laugh of a man who had never been contradicted by anything that couldn't be bought. "You're the best in the valley, they said. The best doesn't spook at clouds."

Tomas looked at him for a long moment. He thought about the ledger in his pack, the season's bookings, the deposit already spent on new rope and a repaired stove and the small dignities of a licensed life — a life built, brick by careful brick, on the far side of the range from the person he had once trusted with it.

He thought about the supply depot in Halden, and the moment he'd seen her there, three weeks ago now, gaunt and hard-eyed and alive, and how he had offered to help and how she had refused, and how he had walked away feeling like a man who had left a door open behind him and heard it slam anyway.

He thought about the Kessel Face. He thought about it every day, but he thought about it differently now, the way a man thinks differently about an old wound when he learns the scar tissue has been lying to him.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing he could not put down. In Halden, Lena had said something — one sentence, thrown like a stone over her shoulder as she walked out — and the sentence had been rattling around inside him for three weeks, cracking him open a little more each day.

*You never asked what I was carrying, Tomas. Not once in eleven years.*

She was right. He hadn't. He had taken the verdict — the inquiry, the blacklisting, the client's widow weeping in the hearing room while Lena stood silent as a cut stone — and he had built his grief on top of it like a cairn, marking the place where his partner had died even though she was still walking around breathing. He had never once asked her why she said nothing in her own defense. He had told himself it was because the answer wouldn't change anything.

That was a lie. The truth was he'd been afraid of the answer. Afraid that if she explained it, he would have to admit he had abandoned her for a verdict instead of a truth, and that eleven years of careful, licensed, respectable distance would collapse into what it actually was: a cowardice with a good view.

"I'm sorry, Herrick." Tomas stood. "I'm returning your fee. All of it."

The banker's mouth opened and closed. "You're joking."

"I'll walk you down to the trailhead myself. There's a guide in Steig who owes me a favor — he'll take you up the south route when the weather holds. You'll get your summit."

"You're abandoning a contracted expedition. I'll have your license."

Tomas shouldered his pack. Outside, the eastern sky had gone the color of a bruise, and the first flakes were coming down fine and dry, the kind that meant the real snow was still hours behind them, gathering itself in the high country like something patient and enormous.

"Probably," Tomas said. "But not before I get where I'm going."

* * *

He cut the descent in half by taking the Couloir des Chèvres, which he would never have taken with a client, and made the trailhead by dark. By the time he reached Halden it was past midnight, and the supply depot was closed, and he sat on the loading dock in the cold eating dried sausage and bread and making his plan with the methodical calm that was the only inheritance his father had ever left him.

Lena would not have stayed in Halden. Halden was a checkpoint town, a place where names got read aloud. She would have gone up and over — the eastern passes, the ones that weren't passes at all so much as rumors with elevation. And she would have moved fast, because Lena Okafor moved fast when she was frightened, and she had been frightened in that depot, whatever her face had shown.

He knew her routes. That was the thing she had perhaps not considered, or perhaps had considered and dismissed, because why would the licensed guide with the good life throw it over for a ghost? But he knew her routes the way he knew his own handwriting. They had learned this range together, before the verdict, before the widow, before the silence — two kids out of the valley towns with borrowed gear and a hunger for the high white country, mapping the unmapped because nobody had told them not to. He knew she favored the northern aspect in the early season, where the wind scoured the snow to bare ice and she could move without leaving tracks. He knew she'd take the Sertig Col rather than the obvious notch, because the obvious notch had a patrol hut. He knew that on the third night she would want water, and water on the north side meant the springs below the Grau Horn, and the springs meant the old mining bivouac under the overhang, the one they'd found together when they were twenty-four and invincible, with its dry stone floor and its view of the whole eastern watershed.

He also knew she would be angry when she saw him. He counted on it, actually. Anger was a fuel he understood; he'd been running on it for eleven years.

He bought supplies in Halden at first light — deliberately overbuying, deliberately visible, because if Brakke or Renn's people were watching the depot he wanted them watching him, walking west with a client's gear, while Lena's trail went cold to the east. Then he cut east through the alder and up into the wind-scoured country, and by the second morning he was reading her sign like a letter from an old friend.

A cairn rebuilt, stones restacked in her particular way — the top stone offset, a habit she'd never been able to break. A boot print in the mud below the Sertig Col, the heel worn on the outside edge, the same asymmetric wear she'd had since the boots he'd laced for her himself on the Kessel approach, a lifetime ago. A thread of blue wool on a branch at chest height, placed where a tracker would see it and a casual eye would not — a marker she used to leave for him, years ago, on training runs. *This way. Keep up.*

He stood looking at that thread for a long time, the wind pulling at his hood, something in his chest aching in a way he had no clinical name for.

*Keep up,* it said. And he went.

* * *

He found the bivouac at dusk on the third day, and he did not announce himself, because he had learned long ago never to startle a person who slept with a knife.

The overhang leaned out of the Grau Horn's flank like the brow of a thoughtful giant, and beneath it the dry stone floor held the shape of old fires, old sleepers. She was there — a small figure wrapped in a grey blanket against the back wall, a cold fire pit, a tin cup set neatly beside it. Even asleep, even from thirty meters, he knew the set of her shoulders. He had watched those shoulders take the weight of a falling world once, on a face of ice and rock, and he had never stopped watching them since, from across a valley, across a decade, across the whole careful distance of his licensed life.

He came down the last stretch loudly, boots ringing on stone, and she was awake before his third step, knife out, body coiled — and then she saw him, and he watched the knife stay exactly where it was while every other part of her went still.

"You," she said.

"Me."

"I left a thread on the Sertig trail."

"I know. I found it."

"That thread was for anyone *but* you."

"It said *keep up*," Tomas said. "I've always been literal."

She sat up slowly, the blanket falling from her shoulders, and looked at him the way you look at a wall that has begun, very quietly, to move. "Your client season. The Wrenspire. You had a full book — Marchel told me in the spring, he was practically bragging on your behalf."

"I gave Herrick his money back."

"You gave—" She stopped. He watched her do the arithmetic, the same arithmetic he'd been doing for three days: the fee, the deposit, the bookings behind it, the whole season's scaffolding of a rebuilt life. "You're insane."

"Probably."

"Tomas." His name in her mouth sounded like a word from a language they had both stopped speaking. She stood, and in the last grey light he could see how thin she'd gotten, how the mountain was spending her like loose coin. "Go home. Whatever you think you're doing — whatever you think you owe me, or the dead man, or the eleven years — go home. This isn't a route you want to be on. There's a man named Brakke on it, and a magistrate who buys silence, and a piece of paper in my pack that has gotten people hurt."

"I know about the paper."

Her eyes narrowed. "You don't know anything."

"I know Vane died at the inn at Halden's edge with an enforcer asking questions downstairs. I know the consortium's permits come up for renewal in five weeks, and I know the valley clinics are full of children whose water runs grey. I read, Lena. The valley reads. We're not all deaf on this side of the range."

For a moment she said nothing, and the wind moved through the alder below the overhang with a sound like a long exhalation, and somewhere high above them the Grau Horn shed a rime of ice into the dark with a crack like a knuckle.

"You came all this way," she said finally, slowly, "to tell me you read the news."

"I came all this way," Tomas said, "because you never asked what I was carrying either."

That landed. He saw it land, saw it move through her like a tremor through ice, and he pressed on before either of them could retreat into the old fortifications.

"Eleven years ago I stood in that hearing room and watched you take the blame for the Kessel Face, and I said nothing, because the verdict was clean and the widow was grieving and I told myself the truth wouldn't change the outcome. And I have built a life on top of that silence the way you build a hut on a snow bridge — it holds, it holds, it holds, and then one day you cross it and hear the hollow sound underneath. You said I never asked what you were carrying. You're right. I'm asking now. Not for the map — keep the map, burn the map, I don't care. I'm asking what happened on the face, and why you let them bury you for it, and why I let you."

The silence went on so long he could hear his own blood. Lena turned away from him, toward the eastern dark, and when she spoke her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used for route briefings and other dangerous terrain.

"You don't want that answer tonight."

"Then I'll want it tomorrow. And the night after. I'm not leaving, Lena. Storm's coming in early — you've seen it, same as I have, that cloud over the eastern watershed is two weeks ahead of itself. You can't run the high passes ahead of it alone, not with the routes you'll have to take. And I'm the only person in these mountains who knows your routes better than you do."

She laughed then, a short, sharp, unamused sound with something underneath it that was almost, almost warm. "Arrogant to the last."

"Accurate to the last."

She looked at him for a long time. He watched her weigh it — he could see the scales working behind her eyes, the cost of solitude against the cost of company, the old wound against the coming storm. He watched her arrive, reluctantly, furiously, at the only answer the mountain would allow.

"Rules," she said. "You carry your own rope. You don't touch my pack. You don't ask me about the map in front of other people. And when I say move, you move, and when I say stop, you stop, and you don't argue about it until we're somewhere with walls."

"Accepted."

"And Tomas —" her voice dropped, and for a second the flat briefing tone cracked and he heard the girl from the valley towns underneath, the one who used to sing off-key on the glacier approaches — "if this goes wrong, if they take you, if Brakke or Renn or anyone asks you what you know — you tell them everything. You tell them you followed a blacklisted woman on a whim, that you know nothing, that you regret everything. You don't go down with me. Not again. That's the price of the alliance."

Tomas looked at her — at the woman the verdict had made, who had learned to price her own company at less than nothing — and felt something in him set, hard and final, like a cam in good rock.

"Denied," he said. "Counteroffer: nobody goes down alone. Not this time."

She stared at him. Above them the cloud had swallowed the last of the light, and the first flakes of the early storm came down through the beam of his headlamp, fine and dry and patient, the vanguard of something enormous.

"You always were a terrible negotiator," Lena said.

"I learned from the best."

She almost smiled. It was a ruin of a smile, frostbitten and wary, but it was real, and it was the first one he'd seen from her in eleven years, and he thought: *there it is. There's the door.*

She kicked snow over the dead fire and began spreading her blanket. "Sleep," she said. "We move at first light, before it gets deep. The east face of the watershed, then the high shelf — three days to the next valley if the storm lets us. And Tomas?"

He looked up.

"If you slow me down, I will leave you. I mean it."

"I know," Tomas said, and lay back against the stone, and found, to his own surprise, that he believed her — and that he slept better in that cold overhang beside his exiled partner, with the storm gathering over the eastern dark, than he had slept in eleven years of warm rooms on the right side of the range.

He woke once, deep in the night, to the sound of snow hissing down the mountain face, thick and relentless, weeks too soon. Lena was already awake, sitting at the mouth of the overhang, watching the white darkness where the route would be.

"It's closing the passes," she said quietly, without turning. "All of them. Even the ones only I know."

Tomas sat up beside her, and together they watched the storm come down like a verdict — and far below, at the edge of the firelight's memory, a single point of light moved along the valley floor, steady and deliberate, climbing toward them through the snow.
