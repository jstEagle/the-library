## 10. The Poisoned Valley

The storm that had sealed them into the high country broke on the third morning, and with it went the last of the flour.

Lena stood at the mouth of the bivouac cave and watched the clouds tear open above the eastern ridgeline, letting down shafts of light so clean and cold they looked solid enough to climb. Below them, two thousand meters down where the rock gave way to scree and then to the first stubborn green of the lower valleys, a thread of smoke rose from somewhere out of sight. A chimney. A hearth. People.

"We're done pretending we can stretch it," she said. Tomas crouched over their packs, doing the arithmetic he'd already done twice. Half a bag of oats. A heel of cheese gone hard as tallow. The tea was gone entirely; they'd been drinking snowmelt that tasted of stone.

"There's the emergency ration," he said.

"The emergency ration is for emergencies."

He looked up at her, and there was something almost like a smile in the exhaustion of his face. "Lena. We've been eating pine bark tea and ambition for two days."

She didn't laugh, but it was close. She turned back to the valley, studying the descent line she'd been memorizing since dawn — a rib of broken ground on the north flank, steep but continuous, dropping toward the smoke. In her mind's eye she laid the map over the terrain, Vane's careful ink, the lode marked in that confident old prospector's hand just below the eastern glacier's snout. *Silver*, he'd written, underlined twice. Enough to buy back a life. Enough to buy a name back, if names could be bought.

They couldn't. She was learning that.

"Village down there," she said. "Maybe two hours' hard descent. We go light, we buy what we can carry, we're back above the treeline by dark." She paused. "We don't talk about why we're here. We don't mention the range above them or anything east of the watershed. Small questions, small answers."

Tomas shouldered his pack. "You always this charming in villages?"

"Only the ones downstream of mines."

* * *

The descent took three hours because the mountain did not care about their hunger. The rib of rock funneled them through gullies still running with meltwater, and twice Lena chose longer lines around slabs glazed with overnight ice, and Tomas followed without argument, which was its own kind of miracle. Somewhere on the fourth pitch he said, quietly, "You're favoring your left knee," and she said, "I'm aware," and that was the whole conversation, and somehow it felt like more than most conversations they'd had in ten years.

The trees came up to meet them — first the krummholz, twisted and salt-blasted by wind, then honest pines, then birch going gold at the edges, autumn arriving early in the shadow of the range. And then the smell changed.

Lena stopped so abruptly that Tomas nearly walked into her.

"What?"

"Smell it."

He breathed. Frowned. "Sulfur? There's no fumaroles on this flank—"

"Not sulfur." She kept walking, faster now, and the smell strengthened as they dropped: a mineral tang, metallic and wrong, like coins left too long in a pocket, like blood in water. It rode beneath the pine resin and the woodsmoke and the good brown smell of leaf-rot, and once you noticed it you could not stop noticing it.

The village appeared around a bend in the river road without warning: thirty houses, maybe forty, clustered where a side-stream met the main river, with terraced gardens climbing the slopes behind them and a small stone mill sagging at the water's edge. It should have been beautiful. It had probably been beautiful once. But the stream that turned the mill ran grey.

Not murky, not silted — *grey*, a flat, opaque, chemical grey, the color of dishwater and dead things, and where it braided over the stones there was no moss, no algae, none of the brown velvet of healthy water. The stones themselves were stained orange and white where minerals precipitated out. The main river beyond the confluence ran clearer, but Lena could see the plume of grey water folding into it, spreading, diluting but not disappearing, carried on toward whatever lay downstream.

Tomas stood at the edge of the road and said nothing for a long time.

"I've seen this before," he finally said. "Kessel valley, after the flood. When the tailings pond let go."

"I know. So have I." Lena's voice came out flat. "That was an accident. This—" She gestured at the stream, at the stained stones, at the utter absence of life in the water. "This is Tuesday."

A woman was watching them from a garden terrace, a basket of late beans on her hip. Not hostile, exactly. Wary the way mountain people are wary of strangers who come down from the high routes instead of up from the low roads. Lena lifted a hand, made her voice easy, made herself the harmless guide, the hungry traveler, nothing more.

"Good day. We've come off the range — our supplies ran short. Is there a store? We'll pay fair."

The woman's eyes went from Lena's rope coil to Tomas's frost-nipped face to the grey water behind them, as if checking whether they'd drunk any. "Store's at Marta's. Blue door." She shifted the basket. "Don't drink from the Burnt Stream. Well water only. You know that already, though, coming from up there." Something flickered across her face — not quite bitterness. Something older and tireder than bitterness. "Everyone who comes off that mountain knows more about our water than we do."

* * *

Marta's store was a front room smelling of lamp oil, dried fish, and floor wax, and Marta herself was seventy if she was a day, sharp-eyed behind the counter with hands like tree roots. She sold them flour and salt pork and a block of salt and, when Lena asked, candles, and she took their coin without comment until Tomas, being Tomas, asked the small question.

"Long season up top?" he said. "We came down the north rib. Didn't expect the stream to look like that."

Marta's hands went still on the counter.

"Which stream did you cross."

"The one at the mill."

"Mm." She looked at them both for a long moment, weighing something, and Lena saw the decision happen behind her eyes the way she'd seen it happen in a hundred mountain faces: the moment someone decides a stranger might be worth the truth. "My granddaughter is eight years old," Marta said. "Her teeth came in grey. Did you know teeth could come in grey? I didn't. My mother didn't. Nobody knew, because nobody had ever seen it, until six years ago."

"Six years ago," Lena repeated quietly.

"When the consortium bought the upper concessions." Marta began stacking their goods with sudden, precise violence, each item placed like a nail driven home. "Six years of grey water. Six years of the goats aborting. Six years of the doctor coming up from Corven every month to look at children whose chests sound like wet gravel. Six years of letters to the magistrate's office in the provincial capital, and six years of letters back saying the discharge permits are *in order*." She set the last package down and put both root-hard hands flat on the counter. "In order. That's what they say. The permits are in order."

Lena's pack suddenly weighed nothing and everything. Against her spine, wrapped in oilcloth, Vane's map pressed against her shoulder blades like a hand.

"Where does the stream come from?" she asked. Her voice sounded very far away to herself. "The source."

Marta squinted at her. "Why?"

"Because I've been up there. Because I may have walked past the answer without knowing it."

The old woman studied her, and whatever she found in Lena's face made her come around the counter and unroll a cloth map of her own on the counter — a surveyor's reprint, cheap paper, the kind sold in provincial offices. Her finger traced the Burnt Stream up from the mill, up past the treeline, up into a hanging valley below the eastern glaciers.

"Adit's up there," she said. "Old workings, pre-consortium, abandoned fifty years. The consortium reopened them four years back as a test site. Cheap extraction, no proper tailings pond — or a proper one, built cheap, which amounts to the same thing. Everything they wash runs straight down into our stream." Her finger tapped the hanging valley, and Lena looked at the place her finger touched and felt the floor of the world tilt.

Because she knew that hanging valley. She had crossed its outlet two days ago. And she knew, without taking the map out, without unfolding Vane's careful ink in front of this woman and this room full of listening walls, exactly what else lay in that hanging valley besides the consortium's adit.

The lode. Vane's silver. His unregistered claim, his dying gift, his daughter's inheritance — sitting directly above the poisoned water, directly above the grey-teethed children, directly above everything the consortium had spent six years ruining and calling legal.

"Lady," Marta said, and her voice dropped, and her eyes were suddenly fierce and wet at once, "you've been up there. Tell me what you saw."

Lena opened her mouth to give the small answer, the safe answer, the answer that kept a dying man's secret and a living woman's promise intact.

What came out instead was: "How many children are sick?"

* * *

There were eleven. Marta walked them through the village to see, because she was the kind of woman who believed grief unshown was grief unfought, and Lena went because refusing would have been a kind of lie she was no longer willing to tell.

The clinic was a converted farmhouse run by a Corven-trained nurse named Odile, a brisk woman in her forties with ink-stained fingers from writing letters nobody answered. Eleven children, she told them, with elevated metals in their blood. Two with lungs that would never fully clear. One — a boy of five, solemn-eyed, sitting on a cot sorting pebbles by color with terrible concentration — whose kidneys were failing by increments the monthly bloodwork charted like a slow tide going out.

"He's from the farm by the confluence," Odile said. "Best water table in the valley, everyone always said. Was." She caught Lena looking at the boy and lowered her voice. "His mother works the consortium's sorting station downriver. They pay well. They pay better than anything else here. That's how it holds together — half the valley feeds them and half the valley fights them and everybody's family with everybody, so nobody says anything loud enough to matter."

"And the permits," Lena said. "Marta said—"

"The permits are in order." Odile smiled without a single gram of warmth. "I've read them. Or the summaries. Discharge limits calculated against flow rates that only exist during spring flood. Sampling stations placed upstream of the actual outfall. An environmental assessment signed by a firm that dissolved two years after filing and was, I'm fairly sure, owned by the consortium's own director of operations." She shrugged, a small bitter motion. "Fraud, all of it, dressed in stamps and seals. But try proving that from a farmhouse clinic with no lawyer and no money and a magistrate's office that sends back form letters."

Tomas had gone very quiet beside Lena, the particular silence she remembered from the Kessel Face years ago, the silence of a man holding something heavy and deciding whether to lift it. Now he said, carefully, "If someone had proof. Real proof. Documents, say. Original documents showing what the land actually was before the consortium's filings — claims, surveys, prior knowledge of contamination." He glanced at Lena, and she met his eyes and shook her head once, a tiny motion, *not here, not now*. He held her gaze anyway. "Would that be worth something? To people like you?"

Odile looked between them for a long moment.

"It would be worth everything," she said. "It would be the first thing anyone has ever handed us that wasn't a bill."

* * *

They climbed out of the village at dusk, taking the forest track back toward the north rib, and neither of them spoke until the grey stream was out of sight and the pines closed dark around them. Then Tomas stopped in the middle of the trail and turned to face her, breath steaming, and said the thing they had both been carrying since Marta's counter.

"You knew. Back there. The moment she pointed at the map — you knew the lode sits right over that valley."

"Yes."

"And you were going to walk past it. Deliver it to the daughter, keep the promise, collect whatever comes after, and never once tell the people drinking that water what lies above their heads."

Lena looked at him. The anger in his voice wasn't really anger; she knew its texture too well. It was fear wearing anger's coat, the same coat he'd worn on the Kessel Face, the same coat he'd worn in the supply depot. Fear for her, fear of what she'd become, fear of the arithmetic a person has to do when carrying something valuable through a world full of hungry hands.

"A promise is a promise," she said. "I gave my word to a dying man."

"To a dying man who wanted his silver delivered before the consortium swallowed it. That's the promise. Not to help the consortium keep its poison quiet while you play courier." He stepped closer. "Lena. The claim isn't just silver. It's *standing*. Whoever holds a valid prior claim on that ground can drag the whole permit structure into a hearing. Can force discovery. Can make Renn's office answer questions in public instead of by form letter. Vane wasn't leaving his daughter a fortune. He was leaving her a weapon, and he was too proud or too blind to know it, and now you're the one carrying it and you're treating it like luggage."

The words landed in her chest and stayed there, glowing like a coal. Because he was right, and she had known he was right since Marta's finger touched the map, since the grey water, since the boy sorting pebbles on the cot, and she had been trying not to know it because knowing it meant the promise had changed shape in her hands, meant the choice ahead was no longer simple delivery but something with weight on both pans of the scale.

"The daughter first," Lena said finally. "Whatever this becomes, Mireille hears it from me, with the map in her hands, before anyone else decides what her father's claim means. That much I owe him. That much I owe *her*." She pulled the pack straps tight. "But you're right. It's not luggage anymore."

Tomas exhaled, some of the fight going out of him, replaced by something wearier and more settled. "Then we move fast. Permits expire in what — four weeks now?"

"Twenty-six days."

"Then twenty-six days to get down to Corven, get ahead of Brakke, and figure out how a blacklisted guide and a burned-out climber hand a dead man's map to the one person alive who can use it." He started up the trail, then paused. "And Lena — the boy. The one with the kidneys. If this goes wrong, if Brakke takes it from us, that's who loses. Keep that in front of you. Not the silver. The boy."

Above them the eastern ridge cut its black line against the last violet of the sky, and somewhere beyond it, in the hanging valley, the adit discharged its grey burden into the dark, patient as debt. Lena climbed, and the map rode against her spine, and it did not feel like luggage, and it did not feel like a fortune.

It felt like a fuse.

Behind them, far below, a lantern moved along the river road — a single point of light traveling fast, uphill, in their direction, where no one from the village had any reason to go at night.

Tomas saw it a heartbeat after she did.

"That's not a villager," he said.

"No," Lena said, and lengthened her stride into the dark. "It isn't."
