## 11. Brakke on the Trail

The fire took the cache in under four minutes.

Brakke stood upwind of it, hands folded behind his back, and watched the kerosene do its work. The supply hut — one of the consortium's old survey stations, squat and grey-weathered under its sheet-metal roof — went up with a sound like a long exhale. First the tar paper blistered and curled. Then the shelves inside caught: flour, salt pork, lamp oil, the waxed boxes of hardtack stamped with the consortium's crest. Heat rolled out through the broken door in a soft wall, and the snow on the roofline slid off in slabs, hissing into steam.

He had counted the stores himself two days ago. Enough to keep two people alive on this flank of the range for perhaps three weeks. Now it was light and smoke and a black stain that would be gone by spring.

"You didn't have to burn it," said Voss, his younger man, from the treeline. "We could've just taken it."

"And carried it where?" Brakke did not turn around. "Down to Corven? Through a checkpoint? Renn's writ covers confiscation, not cartage." He watched a shelf collapse inward in a bloom of orange. "Besides. Taken, it's theft they could testify to. Burned, it's weather. Mountain folk lose caches all the time. Lightning, bears, careless smokers." He paused. "Kerosene is not lightning, but no one will be here to say otherwise."

Voss shifted his weight in the snow. He was twenty-six and still believed that most problems were solved by doing more rather than less. Brakke remembered being twenty-six. It seemed a very long time ago, back before he had learned that the world did not reward effort; it rewarded order, and order was mostly a matter of subtraction.

"Two days ahead," Voss said. "Maybe three, if the storm slows them."

"It won't slow them. That's the point of her." Brakke finally turned. Below them the valley fell away in white terraces, the pines dark as stitches, and beyond it the high country rose in blue-grey walls where Okafor and her partner were somewhere on the ice, climbing toward Corven with a dead man's map sewn into their gear. "She doesn't stop for weather. She stops for nothing. That's what made her useful once, and it's what makes her dangerous now."

"You knew her? Before?"

"I read files." Which was true, though not all of it. Brakke had been at the consortium's regional office in Halden the year the Kessel Face report came through — a client dead, a guide disgraced, an expedition dissolved in recrimination. He had read the file twice, because something in it had bothered him the way a loose thread bothers a tailor. The testimony never quite fit the grief. Grief was loud; the widow's statement had been quiet, rehearsed-quiet, the quiet of someone reading words she'd been given. He had flagged it. His superior had told him the matter was settled, that settled matters were the load-bearing walls of civilization, and that men like Brakke were hired to keep the walls standing, not to knock on them.

So he had stopped knocking. But he had remembered.

* * *

They walked down off the ridge as the light failed, boots crunching, breath hanging in the still air. Brakke liked this hour best. The mountain at dusk told you exactly what it was: indifferent, arithmetic, honest. Men lied about mountains constantly — romanticized them, feared them, wrote poems — but the mountain itself only ever said the same thing. *I am here. I do not care. Come prepared or don't come.*

Order, too, was like that. People called him cruel when he confiscated a family's goods at a checkpoint, when he burned a hut, when he turned a woman and her children back into the cold because their papers were a forgery so poor it insulted them both. They called him cruel, but cruelty implied appetite, and Brakke had none. What he had was a conviction, held the way other men held faith: that the difference between a valley where children starved in ditches and a valley where they did not was not kindness. It was rules, enforced without exception, by people willing to be hated.

Every soft-hearted magistrate who looked away from one forged permit created ten more forgeries. Every guide who moved undocumented families over a col for cash money taught forty others that borders were negotiable, and negotiable borders meant the strong ate the weak. He had seen the strong eat the weak. He had grown up in a mining town where the consortium's predecessor had been the only law for eleven years, and he had watched what happened when there was no one willing to be hated. His mother had watched too, from a place she never spoke of, and his father had died in a tunnel collapse that the company had known was coming and done nothing about, because doing something cost money and nobody forced them.

That was the lesson Brakke had carried out of the ground like a stone in his boot: *unregulated power devours*. And so he had spent thirty years making himself the regulation. If the consortium was corrupt — and he was not a fool; he had read the ledgers Renn now pretended not to have seen — then the corruption was a disease in the body, and you did not cure a body by letting it convulse. You contained it. You kept the roads open, the permits flowing, the mines paying wages, the towns fed. You did the ugly arithmetic quietly, and you slept, if not well, then at least soundly.

Okafor threatened the arithmetic. Not because she was evil — he did not think she was evil; evil was rare and usually stupid — but because she was a loose thread. A map. A dying prospector's claim. A daughter in Corven. Six weeks until the permits expired, and somewhere in that tangle was enough fraud to unravel a concession worth more than the whole valley's annual output. Unraveling it would not free anyone. It would freeze the payroll, panic the investors, bring the receivers in, and while the lawyers circled each other for two years the valley would eat itself. He had seen that too. Order delayed was order denied.

So: subtraction. Remove the thread.

* * *

At the waystation below the tree line they found the magistrate's courier waiting with fresh papers, stamp-warm. Renn's seal. Brakke read the warrant by lamplight while Voss fed the stove: authority to detain and search, authority to seize documents pertaining to the Vane concession, authority to compel assistance from any licensed guide or stationkeeper on the range. Renn's hand was precise as always. She was a careful woman. He respected her and did not trust her, which was, in his experience, the correct posture toward every magistrate he had ever served under.

"She's giving us everything we asked for," Voss said, wonderingly.

"She's giving us everything she can deny later." Brakke set the warrant on the table and weighted it with his tin cup. "Notice what's missing. No authorization to use force beyond detention. No provision for the high routes above the ice line. She wants Okafor stopped, but she wants to be able to say she wanted her stopped *gently*."

"Does it matter? We have the seal."

"The seal matters exactly as much as the distance between us and her desk." He looked at the fire through the stove grate, at the small orange geometry of it. "When we're three days out, the seal is whatever I say it is. When we're back in her office, it's whatever she says it is. So we finish this before we're back in her office."

Voss nodded, eager, and Brakke felt the familiar small sadness he always felt around eager young men. In twenty years Voss would either be him or be broken, and both outcomes would look identical from the outside.

The stationkeeper, an old woman named Grell who had sold tea and bunks to guides for forty years, brought them barley soup and stood at the edge of the lamplight with her arms crossed.

"You're consortium," she said. Not a question.

"We're operating under magistrate's warrant," Brakke said.

"I know what I smell." She looked at the warrant upside down across the table, unbothered. "You burned the survey hut on the north flank. I saw the smoke at noon."

"Lightning," Brakke said, "in winter, is unusual."

Grell's mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely without warmth. "There are people on this mountain who owe me favors. There are stations you'll need. There are crossings only passable with rope that lives in my loft." She set down the soup bowls with a click. "I'm telling you this so you understand your position. You can buy what you need from me, dear, or you can go without. But you don't frighten me. I've buried better men than you in worse weather."

After she left, Voss started to rise, and Brakke put one finger on his wrist without looking up from the warrant.

"No," he said quietly. "Never punish honesty. Punishing honesty teaches lying, and liars are expensive." He finished his soup. It was good, salty, over-simmered the way mountain food should be. "We pay her double. We ask her nothing. And tomorrow we move."

* * *

They moved.

For six days they climbed into the teeth of the range, following a trail that was barely a rumor — cairns half-swallowed by rime, a traverse above a frozen waterfall where the rock wore ice like armor. Brakke had hired two local men in the last town, brothers named Anselm and Piet, who moved over ground like goats and asked no questions, which was the most expensive quality a hire could have. They found Okafor's sign on the third day: boot prints in a wind-scoured couloir, two sets, spaced with a professional's economy. Beside them, half-buried, a discarded ration wrapper. Piet held it up without comment. Brakke noted the brand — Corven bakery, cheap flour, the kind sold in bulk to clinics — and filed it away. They were provisioning from the valley communities. Good. That meant dependence, and dependence meant leverage.

On the fifth day they found the bivouac cave the pair had used, still warm-smelling of bodies and burnt twig-smoke, and Brakke knelt in it for a long time, reading the floor the way other men read letters. Two sleepers. One of them restless — the prints turned and turned again, a sleeper fighting dreams. He touched the ash. Cold. A day, maybe less.

"They know we're behind them?" Voss asked.

"They know *someone* is. She'll have seen our fires." Brakke stood, brushing frost from his knees. "It doesn't matter. Look at the map." He spread it on his knee: the high routes, the glacier, the single viable descent to Corven before the snows closed the east face. "There is one road to where they're going. One. Everything else is suicide in this season. We don't have to catch them. We only have to arrive."

That was the sentence he built the whole operation on, and he repeated it to himself each morning like a prayer: *we only have to arrive.* Pursuit was a young man's game, all sprint and sweat. Interdiction was patience. You did not chase the wolf; you killed the elk it was following.

Which was why, on the sixth day, at the last supply cache on the glacier approach — a stone cairn the size of a chapel, stocked by the valley cooperatives against just such seasons — Brakke did not hesitate.

It was a bigger fire than the survey hut. The co-op had stored grain, dried fish, rope, medical stores, a small iron stove, and crates of the grey flour that went to the clinic in Corven. Anselm struck the match because Brakke's hands, he had noticed lately, were not as steady as they used to be, and he preferred not to advertise it. The flames went up through the cairn's smoke-hole in a column visible, he estimated, for twenty miles.

Piet watched it with his jaw working. "Those are valley stores," he said. "People's winter margin. That's not consortium property."

"No," Brakke agreed.

"Then it's theft. Real theft. Of real people."

Brakke turned to face him fully, and Piet, to his credit, did not step back. "Everything is someone's property," Brakke said. "The question is whose law protects it. Right now, up here, the answer is mine. In a month, if that map reaches Corven and the permits fall, there is no law at all for two years — no payroll, no magistrate with funding, no clinic with supplies. Do you know what a valley eats first when the money stops? Its winter margin." He let that sit. "I am burning one cache so that forty of them stay full. That is the entire job. That is all I have ever done."

"You believe that," Piet said. Not accusing. Almost admiring, the way one admires a large animal.

"With everything I am," Brakke said. "It's the only thing I've ever been sure of. Doubt is a luxury of people whose mistakes don't kill anyone."

Anselm laughed once, short and cold, and spat into the snow.

* * *

That night, camped in the lee of the moraine, Brakke sat awake while the others slept, and took out the file.

Not the official file — the copy he had made in Halden nine years ago and carried ever since, against some day he could not name. Kessel Face. Four climbers, one dead. Guide of record: L. Okafor. Partner: T. Reyes-Marchetti. Client: Daniel Sorel, deceased. And the widow's statement, quiet as a locked room.

He read it again by the blue light of the storm lantern, wind worrying the tent fabric like fingers at a sleeve. Somewhere above him, in the dark and the rising wind, Okafor was climbing toward the same truth he had glimpsed at twenty-six and been told to leave alone. If she reached Corven, the truth would come out — all of it, the fraud, the poison, the widow's rehearsed silence, maybe even Kessel Face itself — and the world would convulse, and people would suffer in ways no ledger could balance.

Unless he arrived first.

He folded the file away and lay back, listening to the wind climb. Above the moraine, far up the glacier, he thought he could see a spark of light — a stove flame in a bivouac tent, tiny and stubborn, a star that refused to be a star. He watched it for a long time.

*We only have to arrive,* he told himself, and almost believed the wind had not answered.
