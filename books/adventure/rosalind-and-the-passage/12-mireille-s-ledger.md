## 12. Mireille's Ledger

The clinic smelled of carbolic and wet wool, and by the fourth hour of the morning shift Mireille had stopped noticing either. She taped a dressing over a child's elbow — Toma Reiner, seven years old, scraped falling from the mill fence again — and sent him out with a stern word about fences that she did not mean, because the fence was the least of what was wrong with this valley and they both knew it.

Outside the window, the river ran grey.

Not cloudy. Not muddy after rain. Grey, a flat mineral grey like dishwater left standing, and it had run that way for three years now, ever since the consortium's stamping works went in above the valley at Kesselholt. The children called it the ghost river. They swam in it anyway, because children do, and then they came to her clinic with sores on their arms and a tiredness in their bones that no tonic touched. Old man Brecht's lungs had gone hard as slate. There were days when Mireille counted the coughs in her waiting room the way other people counted money.

She wrote *Toma Reiner, laceration, right elbow, tetanus current* in the ledger with a nib that scratched, and thought, not for the first time, that if she could have put the whole consortium on trial with nothing but this book — every entry, every grey-water rash, every name of every child who wouldn't grow properly — she would have done it years ago. But ledgers were not evidence. Ledgers were just grief with numbers attached.

"You're wanted," said Sister Anselm from the doorway. "There's a courier."

"I'm not buying anything."

"It's not a sale." The old nun hesitated, and something in her hesitation made Mireille look up. "It's marked private. It came up from Corven post with the morning sledge. It's got a lawyer's seal on it."

Mireille wiped her hands. A lawyer's seal. In six years, the only correspondence her father had ever sent her was a single card when she passed her examinations, four lines long, unsigned in any way that mattered, smelling faintly of pipe smoke and distance. She had kept it. She hated that she had kept it.

The parcel was small, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with twine. Her own name on it in a stranger's careful hand: *Mireille Vane, Clinic of St. Aldhelm, Corven Valley.* No return address. Inside the oilcloth, a letter on heavy paper from a firm she didn't know — Hargrave & Dunn, Concession Agents — informing her, in language designed to be read quickly and understood slowly, that one Aldous Vane, holder of certain unregistered claims in the upper Verren Range, had named her beneficiary of record, and that certain documents pertaining to those claims would be conveyed to her by an independent carrier, and that the concession permits governing the district would lapse in six weeks' time, whereupon all unregistered claims would revert to the consortium under the terms of the Verren Accord.

She read it twice. Then she sat down on the examination stool, because her knees had opinions.

*Beneficiary of record.* Her father had been dead to her for eleven years — not dead dead, worse, alive and choosing elsewhere — and now some paper firm in the lowlands informed her she was his beneficiary of record, as though a record existed somewhere, as though he had written her name down in some ledger of his own while writing her out of everything else.

"Bad news?" Sister Anselm asked.

"I don't know yet." She looked at the letter. "It's from my father. Or about him. I can't tell which is worse."

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She lit the lamp in her rooms above the clinic and laid the lawyer's letter flat and stared at it until the words wore grooves in themselves. Six weeks. Independent carrier. Unregistered claims. It sounded like him, even across eleven years of silence — the same appetite for the long gamble, the same belief that somewhere in those mountains there was a seam of silver with his name already on it. He had chased that seam her entire childhood. She remembered being small and sitting on the tailgate of a prospecting wagon doing sums with ore samples while her mother packed and repacked and said nothing in the particular way she had of saying nothing that meant everything.

Her mother had died waiting for him to come home from a range season. Not waiting-waiting; she'd stopped waiting years before the fever took her, but there had been no one else to wait, so the shape of it stayed. And Mireille had stood at the graveside aged nineteen with the frost coming early and made herself a promise with the clean ferocity only the young can manage: *never.* Never chase him, never write him, never give him the satisfaction of needing anything he might choose not to send.

Eleven years. She had kept the promise perfectly. It was the easiest thing she had ever done, because he had never once given her a reason to break it.

Until now.

She should burn the letter. That was the correct move, the self-respecting move. Instead she found herself pulling down the box from the top of the wardrobe — the box she told herself she kept out of archival habit, a clinician's respect for documents — the box of everything he had ever sent, which was to say almost nothing, and everything he had left behind, which was rather more.

Her mother had saved his letters. That was the thing Mireille had never forgiven her mother for, or perhaps never forgiven herself for keeping them after. Three decades of letters, bundled in string, in a handwriting she knew better than her own heartbeat because she had learned her letters copying it. She untied the first bundle with hands that were entirely steady, thank you, and began to read.

* * *

The early letters were what she expected. A young man's letters, full of assay values and seam widths and the particular poetry of rock — *the quartz here runs blue-grey like a vein through a wrist, Mirrie, you'd love it, you always liked the bones of things* — and she read them with her jaw set, cataloguing his absences, his excuses, the way his affection always arrived wrapped around a scheme. She read them the way you probe a wound: to confirm it still hurts.

Then, in the middle bundle, dated the year she turned sixteen, the tone changed.

*My dear girl,* — and she flinched at *my dear girl*, at the formality of guilt — *I have news I cannot yet put in a letter but will tell you when I see you at midwinter. Something has happened at Kesselholt. Something wrong.*

She turned pages faster.

*I have seen the water reports. The company's own water reports, Mirrie. They knew before the first stamp fell. The aquifer under the east bench is contaminated — theirs is the word, contaminated, as if the river caught a cold — and they filed the permits anyway, with the assays altered. I have seen both versions. I held both versions in my hands.*

*They offered me a position. Survey chief. Good salary, a house in the lowlands, your schooling paid. All I had to do was initial the survey maps as drawn. I want you to understand that I thought about it. I thought about it for two days, and the reason I did not take it was not nobility, it was arithmetic — because if I signed, then in twenty years when the valley children started dying, my initials would be on the instrument of it, and you would carry my name.*

*I refused. I am telling you this because I suspect no one else will ever believe it, and I need one person in the world to know it happened.*

Mireille set the letter down. The lamp hissed. Downstairs, late, someone was coughing — Brecht, probably, or the Ferro girl, or any of forty souls drinking ghost water without knowing why their bodies were failing them by inches.

He had refused. She had built eleven years on the story that he had taken the company's coin and gone chasing silver while his family rotted politely in the valley below. And here was his own hand saying otherwise — saying he had been offered everything and chosen instead to become the kind of man who writes letters no one believes.

But then the next letter, and the next, and the story curdled into something worse than betrayal. Because he hadn't walked away either. He had stayed. For years. Buying samples quietly, copying filings, corresponding with a magistrate's clerk under initials, assembling — page by page, winter by winter — a case against the consortium that he never once brought anywhere that could act on it.

*I went to Renn's office again,* ran one letter, dated four years back. *The magistrate herself was courteous. Courteous the way ice is courteous. She listened to everything and then explained to me, kindly, that unregistered claim-holders have no standing, and that a man who lives outside the permit system cannot testify inside it. Do you see the elegance of it, Mirrie? They wrote the rules so that the only men who know the truth are men whose truth cannot be heard. I spent thirty years making myself exactly the witness they designed the law to exclude.*

*I am tired. Your mother used to say I loved the mountains more than the people in them. She was wrong, but I let her believe it because the truth was more shameful: I loved being right more than I loved being home. I thought if I proved the fraud, that would be enough. It was not enough. It was never going to be enough. Proof is just grief with paperwork attached.*

Mireille laughed — one short, awful sound in the empty room — because she had thought that exact phrase this very morning, sitting at her own ledger. *Grief with numbers attached.* Where had she gotten it, if not from him, from the letters she'd sworn she never read, from the blood-deep inheritance of a mind she'd spent her adult life disowning?

She read until the candle drowned in its own wax. Letter after letter, the case growing in the margins — assay dates that didn't match filing dates, a surveyor's initials appearing on permits for terrain the surveyor had never visited, a pattern of altered water reports stretching back fifteen years, all of it annotated in his cramped hand with citations and cross-references like a man building a cathedral out of spite and evidence. And threaded through it, thinner every year, the other thread: *I will come to Corven at midwinter. I will explain everything to you at midwinter. When the case is ready, I will come.*

No midwinter ever came. She checked. She went through the bundles twice, date by date, and there was not one letter that arrived, not one visit, not once in eleven years. He had written *midwinter* nine times and appeared zero times, and she sat back in the chair with the terrible new architecture of her grief settling onto her: he had not abandoned her out of indifference. He had abandoned her out of shame, and shame, unlike indifference, keeps writing letters. Shame sits alone in a hut above the treeline for a decade, annotating water reports, drafting apologies it never sends, telling itself next year, next season, when the case is ready — while the daughter grows up in the poisoned valley learning to hate a man who was, all along, merely a coward with a conscience.

Which was worse. Which was so much worse. Hatred had been simple. Hatred had kept her warm.

* * *

Near dawn she found the last letter, undated, unsent — folded but never sealed, tucked into the back of the final bundle like a stone at the bottom of a bag.

*Mirrie,*

*I am dying. The cough the doctor in Lowmark named last month has begun to keep its appointments. So I must finally do the thing I should have done eleven years ago, and I find I cannot do it in person, because I have earned the right to neither your door nor your anger in person. Anger requires showing up.*

*So: papers. I have arranged for everything to reach you — the duplicate filings, the original water reports, the assay comparisons, fifteen years of proof that the consortium's permits were obtained by fraud and that they have known, from before the first day of operation, what their stamps would do to the valley water. If the claims revert to them, the fraud dies with the permits and the valley drinks that river forever. If the claims pass to you, you have standing. You, who lived in the valley, who treated the children, who kept the ledger. You are the witness they could never exclude, because they never thought to design the law against a woman with a clinic and a grudge.*

*I do not ask forgiveness. I ask six weeks. Deliver the proof before the permits lapse, and then hate me freely for the rest of your life, with my blessing and my complete agreement.*

*Your father,*

*Aldous*

She read it four times. Outside, the sky over the eastern ridge had gone the colour of pewter, and the first sledge of the morning rattled past on the frozen road, and somewhere below a child coughed in a way that she would now hear differently for the rest of her life.

Grief arrived first. It arrived the way weather arrives in the Verren — all at once, from behind, flattening everything — and she let it, sitting in the grey light with an unsent letter in her lap, crying for a man she had rehearsed hating for eleven years, crying mostly, she realized, for the midwinters that never came and could now never come, for every argument they might have had and never would, for the father-shaped absence that had turned out, all along, to be a presence facing the wrong direction.

Forgiveness would come later, if it came at all. Grief had jumped the queue. That felt right. That felt like him.

When the light strengthened she washed her face, bound the letters back in their string, and set the unsent letter apart from the rest — apart, and on top, where her hand could find it. Then she went downstairs and unlocked the clinic and began the morning's list, and between the first patient and the second she heard Sister Anselm's voice at the door, pitched low and strange:

"Mireille. There's a woman at the gate. Says she's carried something up from the high passes for you. Says she's been on the mountain for weeks." A pause. "Says her name is Okafor, and that your father died keeping a promise."

Mireille closed her ledger.

She stood for a moment with her hand flat on the cover, on all those names and numbers, all that grief with figures attached, and then she went to the door.
