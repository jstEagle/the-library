## 13. The Glacier Crossing

The snow began at midnight, soft as an apology, and by dawn it had become something else entirely.

Lena stood at the lip of the moraine and watched it fall — not the fat, friendly flakes of the low valleys but the fine, dry grain of the high range, snow that didn't land so much as accumulate, sifting down through a sky the color of old pewter. It hissed against her hood. It filled the tracks they'd made yesterday in twenty minutes flat. Somewhere above them, hidden behind two thousand feet of white murk, lay the Serrat Glacier, four kilometers of blue ice draped over the spine of the range, and it was the only way east to Corven that Brakke wouldn't think to watch.

"He'll expect us to go around," Tomas said behind her. He was checking the rope with the mechanical patience of a man who had done this ten thousand times and knew that the ten-thousand-and-first was the one that mattered. "South side, through the col. It's what any sane person would do."

"We're not sane people."

"No." He cinched a knot, tested it. "We're you."

She let that sit. There had been a time — three years ago, another lifetime measured in avalanches — when he'd have said it as a joke, and she'd have laughed, and the laugh would have been the sound of two people who trusted each other with their lives without ever having to say so. Now it landed like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it falling for a long time before it hit bottom.

"The glacier's still frozen," she said. "Bridges will hold. By afternoon the sun will soften the surface and we'll posthole, but if we're across by then—"

"If we're across by then." He shouldered his pack. The storm flap snapped in the wind. "You always did love a conditional sentence."

Lena looked up at where the glacier ought to be and saw nothing but white on white, the world erased from the middle upward. Six weeks, Vane had said. Six weeks until the permits expired and everything he'd bled for reverted to men who had never bled for anything. They were eleven days in. The mountain had already spent three of them being difficult.

"Rope's good," Tomas said.

They climbed.

* * *

The Serrat Glacier in a whiteout was less a place than a condition. Lena moved by memory and inclination, reading the ice through her boots the way a blind woman reads a page: here the surface hummed solid underfoot, there it went hollow, a drum-skin note that meant water running underneath, meant a bridge, meant trust me or don't cross. She called each one aloud. *Hollow. Wide step left. Solid for ten paces.* And behind her, thirty feet back on the rope, Tomas repeated them in his flat guide's voice, and between the two of them they wove a small architecture of certainty across a landscape designed to kill the certain.

It was almost like before.

That was the treachery of it, she thought. Not the crevasses, not the storm. The treachery was how easily the body remembered. His weight coming taut on the line when she stopped; her hand signing *hold* against her thigh and knowing he'd see it even through the white. Three years since the Kessel Face, and their bodies were still having the conversation their mouths refused to finish.

At midmorning the light changed — not brightened, nothing so kind, but shifted, the flat grey taking on a faint pearlescence that meant the cloud deck had thinned somewhere above them. For perhaps twenty minutes the glacier revealed itself in glimpses: seracs leaning like drunkards, a field of pressure ridges frozen mid-shudder, and ahead, cutting across their route, a band of broken ice where the glacier poured over a buried rib of rock and shattered into a chaos of bridges and voids.

"The Ladder," Lena said. Vane's journal had named it, forty years ago, in ink gone brown. *Cross early or not at all.*

They crossed early.

The first bridge took her weight with a sound like a struck bell. The second groaned but held. The third was wider than she liked, its far lip sagging into shadow, and she probed it twice with her axe before committing, feeling the snow compact and rebound, compact and rebound, alive under her, breathing.

Halfway across, it exhaled.

There was no dramatic crack, no cinematic warning. The surface simply softened beneath her right boot the way a held breath softens, and she threw herself forward onto her belly with the instinct of an animal, arms spread, distributing herself across the snow like a woman lying down on thin faith. Below her boots, through the collapsing web of the bridge, she saw it: blue dark going down and down, walls of ancient ice polished smooth as throat glass, and at the bottom of her vision a suggestion of depth that the eye refused to finish measuring.

"Lena." Tomas's voice, very level. "Don't move."

"I'm not moving."

"You're moving."

She realized she was — a slow millimetric slide, her boot sole shearing the last filaments of the bridge, her whole body beginning the long patient negotiation with gravity. She got her axes out sideways, planted both picks, and froze again, cheek against snow that smelled of stone and deep cold. Her breath came out in ragged white flags.

"Talk to me," she said.

"On your left, two meters, there's a ridge of old windslab. Looks consolidated. Crawl to it, don't walk, don't stand, crawl." A pause. "I've got you. Rope's tight. I've got you."

And God help her, some animal part of her believed it instantly, completely, the way she hadn't believed anything in three years. *I've got you.* When had anyone last said that to her and meant it? When had she last let them?

She crawled. The windslab rang solid under her palms. She rolled off the broken bridge onto it, lay gasping, and looked back at Tomas standing anchored at the bridge's edge with the rope wrapped twice around his axe, holding her life in a loop of kernmantle and habit.

Their eyes met across the gap.

Neither of them said anything about the Kessel Face. Neither of them had to. It hung in the white air between them like a third climber neither would acknowledge.

"Your turn," she said, when she could speak.

* * *

He came across the same bridges, and she anchored him the way he'd anchored her, and it was on the fourth crossing — the easy one, the one that should have been nothing — that the mountain collected its toll.

Later, Lena would replay it frame by frame and never find the moment it went wrong. One instant Tomas was stepping off the last bridge onto solid ice, casual, almost bored, shaking snow off his cuff. The next, the ice beneath him opened like a mouth.

No warning. No hollow note. The glacier had simply decided, somewhere in its million-year patience, that this particular table of snow was finished pretending to be ground, and it withdrew its support all at once, and Tomas dropped.

The rope came tight around her waist and slammed her to her knees.

"TOMAS!"

Nothing. Just the rope humming with his weight, running down into a slot barely wider than his shoulders, and the awful knowledge — the Kessel Face knowledge, the knowledge she had carried like a stone in her chest for three years — that a hanging climber has minutes, not hours. That the harness cuts. That the cold takes the hands first. That the person on top either knows exactly what to do or spends the rest of their life learning what they should have done.

She knew exactly what to do.

Her body did it before her mind caught up: axe in, self-arrest, boot in the pick, anchor, *breathe*. Then she was moving toward the lip, feeding the rope through her brake hand centimeter by centimeter, fighting his weight and winning because she had to win, because the alternative was a word she refused to think.

"Tomas. Talk to me."

A cough from below. Then, faintly, shockingly, almost amused: "Still... here."

"What's your status? Talk."

"Hanging. Wall's—" a breath, ragged. "Wall's vertical. Ice. My axe is—" Another breath. "My axe is gone, Lena. I couldn't hold it. I can't get a swing."

"Stop swinging. Conserve. I'm building an anchor." Her hands were already doing it, two screws into the ice beside the lip, sling, carabiner, transfer the load, her fingers working with a speed that felt borrowed from someone else, someone better. "Three minutes. Hold on."

"Don't—" A wheeze that might have been a laugh. "Don't take too long. It's drafty down here."

She transferred his weight to the anchors, rigged the haul system with hands that shook only after the work was done, and leaned over the lip.

He hung fifteen feet down, turning slowly on the rope, a dark figure in a blue cathedral. The light down there was extraordinary — glacial blue filtering through the ice walls, illuminating him like something preserved, like something the mountain had decided to keep. His face was bloodless. His eyes found hers and held on.

"Kessel Face," he said quietly. "This is the Kessel Face all over again, isn't it. You on top. Me below."

"It's nothing like the Kessel Face."

"It's exactly like—"

"It's nothing like it," she said, "because this time nobody's going to die. Haul line's fixed. On three, you climb with me. Feet on the wall. *Now*, Tomas."

He climbed. Of course he climbed — he was Tomas, he had ice in his blood and stubbornness in his bones, and together, her hauling and his boots walking up the vertical blue, they brought him over the lip and onto the surface, where he collapsed on his back and lay breathing in great shuddering gulps, frost in his eyelashes, alive, absurdly, gloriously alive.

Lena knelt beside him and discovered she was crying.

Not sobbing — something worse, something silent and involuntary, tears freezing on her cheeks while her face stayed perfectly composed, as if her body had decided to grieve without consulting her. She wiped them with the back of her glove and they froze there too, little salt crystals, evidence.

Tomas watched her do it. He didn't say anything. He just reached over and put his hand on her forearm, gloved, clumsy, and squeezed once, and kept it there.

Above them, the clouds were thickening again, and the temperature was falling in the way she'd learned to read like a sentence with a bad ending.

* * *

They made the far moraine as the storm closed its fist.

Lena had hoped for the emergency hut marked on Vane's map — a stone shelter, forty years old, probably roofless. What they found instead, half-buried in the drifts behind a rib of rock, was a prospector's cache: a low dry-stone bothy, doorless but wind-tight, with a rusted stove, half a cord of split wood, and a shelf holding a tin of tea older than Mireille Vane.

Tomas got a fire lit while Lena checked him over — ribs bruised where the harness had caught, hands cut, one wrist he favored and swore was fine and wasn't. She taped it without asking. He let her. Outside, the storm arrived in earnest, a freight-train roar in the rocks, snow driving horizontal past the doorway in sheets that erased the world.

They sat against opposite walls with the stove between them, close enough to share its heat, far enough to pretend otherwise. The firelight moved on the stone. Steam rose off their gear in the corner like something escaping.

"That was clean work," Tomas said finally. "The rescue."

"You'd have done the same."

"Yes." He turned his mug in his hands. "That's the thing, isn't it. We'd both have done the same. We always could. That was never the problem."

Lena looked at him across the stove. The fire cracked. Snow hissed against the stones above the doorway like sand thrown at a window.

"Then what was the problem, Tomas?"

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer — long enough that she recognized the silence itself as an answer, the same silence he'd carried down off the Kessel Face three years ago, the same one he'd worn like armor through every argument, every accusation, every year of estrangement.

But tonight, with the storm sealing them in and the rope still coiled between them smelling of both their sweat, something in his face gave way. He set down the mug. He looked at the fire instead of at her, which was how she knew it was finally going to be true.

"You want to know what happened on the Kessel Face," he said. "The real version. Not the report. Not the widow's version. Mine."

The wind screamed past the bothy. Lena found she was afraid — more afraid than she'd been on the rope, more afraid than she'd been over the crevasse lip — and she understood, distantly, that some truths are heavier than any client, any fall, any rope.

"Tell me," she said.

Outside, the storm built toward nightfall, and Tomas drew a breath, and began.
