## 15. Renn's Second Offer

The storm had been chasing them for two days, and at last it caught them at the waystation below the Serpent Col — a low stone shelter built by shepherds a century ago and abandoned by everyone since except the wind. Lena had chosen it because it was the kind of place no one looked for anyone: a squat gray hump in a fold of the mountain, half-swallowed by scree, its door hanging on one hinge like a loose tooth. Tomas had argued for pushing higher, putting another ridge between them and whatever followed them out of the burned cache country. She had argued for stopping. In the end the storm decided, as storms do, arriving an hour after dark with a sound like the whole range being dragged across gravel.

They'd gotten a fire going in the old hearth with what remained of their fuel — a sad handful of larch scrub Tomas had pulled from a gully before the snow buried everything. The fire was small and mean and they huddled over it anyway, because a small fire is still a fire, and because neither of them wanted to say aloud what both of them were thinking: that Brakke had burned their supplies not to stop them but to slow them, and that slowing them was all he needed. The snows were closing early this year. Every hour they spent sheltering was an hour the east face grew teeth.

Tomas was asleep first, or pretending to be, curled against the far wall with his boots on and his ice axe within reach of his hand even in sleep — an old habit from the Kessel days that Lena had never mentioned and he had never explained. She sat up keeping the watch, which was also an old habit, and fed the fire splinters of wood one at a time, rationing warmth the way she rationed everything now.

That was when she heard the footsteps.

Not the storm — she knew the storm's voice by then, had been listening to it for two days, could pick its moods apart the way you pick apart a stranger's breathing in the next room. This was different. This was deliberate. Footsteps in fresh snow at eleven thousand feet, at night, in a whiteout, coming up the slope toward the door with the unhurried cadence of someone who had never once in her life been lost.

Lena put her hand over the fire's glow and killed the light. Her other hand found her axe.

The steps stopped outside. Snow fell into the doorway, a pale curtain stirring. And then Magistrate Ilse Renn's voice came through the gap, dry and precise as a ledger line:

"You can put the axe down, Okafor. I came alone."

* * *

She looked terrible. That was Lena's first thought, and it shamed her a little, how much satisfaction there was in it. Renn had always seemed carved rather than born — the magistrate who ran the checkpoint at Grell Pass like a woman defusing ordnance, immaculate even at altitude, her authority worn like a uniform pressed that morning. Now she stood in the doorway shaking snow off a travel cloak that was soaked through and badly made, and beneath the hood her face was gray with exhaustion, and there was a cut above her eyebrow that had been closed with field tape instead of stitches, and her hands, when she pulled off her gloves, were shaking. Not from cold. Lena had seen cold-shaking enough times to know the difference. This was the shaking that comes from inside, from something a person has seen or done or agreed to and cannot un-see or un-do or un-agree to.

"Tomas," Lena said quietly, and behind her Tomas stirred, and she heard the soft scrape of steel leaving a sheath.

"Both of you," Renn said. "I need to talk to both of you. Ten minutes. Then I'll walk back down and you'll never see me again if that's what you want."

"Or I put you out now," Lena said, "and save us both ten minutes."

Renn looked at her. In the firelight her eyes were red-rimmed and very tired and very clear, and what was in them was not the flat administrative certainty Lena remembered from the checkpoint at Grell Pass, where Renn had offered her freedom in exchange for a map and a lie. What was in them now was something closer to fear.

"You should hear it," Renn said. "Because the man who sent me is worse than anything you think I am. And he's closer than you think I am."

Lena weighed it. The storm outside. The hours until dawn. The fact that Renn had found them here, in a shepherd's ruin in the middle of nowhere, which meant either that Renn was very good or that someone had told her exactly where to look — and only three people alive knew where they were heading, and one of them was dead.

"Ten minutes," Lena said. "And you sit where I can see your hands."

* * *

Renn sat on the floor across the fire with her hands open on her knees, palms up, the posture of a woman surrendering a weapon she didn't have. Snowmelt dripped off her cloak and hissed in the coals. For a while she said nothing, just held her hands out to the heat, and Lena watched the shaking slowly ease, and understood that whatever Renn had come to say, part of her had come simply to be warm and not alone for ten minutes, and that was somehow the most frightening thing about her.

"Brakke burned your cache at the Grey Stair," Renn said finally. It wasn't a question.

"You know that."

"I signed the requisition." She said it flatly, without apology, the way you read out a sentence you've already served. "He needed my seal to draw consignment stores for a 'recovery operation.' Recovery of stolen property. That's what the paperwork calls your map, Okafor. Stolen property. Vane's claim reverted to the consortium six weeks ago on paper — the permits expired, the concession lapsed, everything legal and clean. So the map isn't a bequest anymore. It's evidence of theft. Which means retrieving it isn't theft. It's law enforcement." She almost smiled, and it was the ugliest thing Lena had ever seen her do. "I wrote that language myself. Two years ago. Standard clause. I thought I was closing loopholes."

"And now?"

"Now I've spent nine days watching him use it." Renn turned her hands over, studied the palms as though checking them for damage. "You know what he did at the Grey Stair? He didn't just burn the cache. There was a boy tending it. Sixteen, maybe. Local kid, hired by the outfitter in Lowmark to watch stores through the season. Brakke tied him to a post and left him there overnight in the wind. No charges. No record. The boy's family won't file a complaint because the consortium owns the outfitter and the outfitter holds their debt. So officially, nothing happened at the Grey Stair. Officially, there was no boy."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls the wind found a gap and played it like a flute.

"I'm a magistrate," Renn said. "I've spent nineteen years believing that order is the thing that stands between people and the dark. That procedure is the wall. You build it rule by rule, clause by clause, and it holds, and behind it people get to live ordinary lives. And I watched a man use my wall as a ladder. Clause by clause. All of it legal. All of it mine." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and burning. "He doesn't break laws, Okafor. He reads them. That's what makes him dangerous. A criminal you can catch. A man with clean paperwork and no conscience — you can't catch him, because there's nothing to catch. He's just weather moving through the system."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I'm compromised." Renn said it the way other people say *I'm dying* — plainly, having made peace with it somewhere on the climb up. "Every warrant I issued for him, every seal, every requisition — if this goes wrong, if anyone ever looks, it's my name on all of it. He's built it so that I can't testify against him without testifying against myself. That was the design. I understand that now. I was never his instrument. I was his insurance policy." She paused. "So yes. I'm compromised. And a compromised magistrate has exactly one thing left worth trading."

Lena felt it coming before Renn said it, the way you feel a slab shift underfoot before it moves.

"The offer," Renn said. "The real one, this time. Not the one at Grell Pass. Listen to all of it before you refuse it." She drew a breath. "Give me the map. Voluntarily, publicly, at the hearing in Corven — you walk in and surrender it as recovered property, no struggle, no story. In exchange: your record expunged. Fully. The Kessel Face finding vacated, the blacklist lifted in all three jurisdictions, your guide's license reinstated. Not bought silence this time — I'll swear the affidavit myself, on the record, that the original finding was coerced testimony from a client's widow under pressure from the consortium's insurers. I have the correspondence. I can prove it. You walk out of Corven a free woman with a clean name."

Tomas shifted behind her. Lena didn't turn around.

"And Tomas?" she said.

"Full immunity. Everything he did this season — abandoning clients, crossing illegal, aiding you. Sealed. It never happened." Renn's voice didn't waver. "And more than that. His license. His standing. His future. He goes back to the legal side of the range with nothing hanging over him, and no one ever comes asking questions about a dead season. Both of you, whole. That's the price of one piece of paper."

The fire had burned down to a red eye in the hearth. Outside, the storm went on working the mountain like a man kneading dough.

Lena found she wasn't looking at Renn anymore. She was looking at the map — or where the map was, sewn flat against her ribs inside her shirt, where it had lived for so many weeks that she'd stopped feeling it as a separate object and started feeling it as an organ. Vane's careful ink. The contour lines drawn from a lifetime of walking. *Deliver it to Mireille.* A promise made to a dying man in a half-empty inn over bad stew, the easiest promise she'd ever made and the hardest one she'd ever kept.

"It's not mine to give," she said.

"It was never yours," Renn said. "That's the point. That's why this works. You're not selling anything. You're returning stolen property to its lawful owner and being compensated for your trouble. Clean. Legal. Everyone walks away."

"Everyone," Lena said. "Except the valley."

Renn went still.

"The water runs grey in the villages downstream of that lode," Lena said. "We walked through it. Children with lesions on their arms. A clinic in Corven that buries more patients than it cures. That land is poisoned, and the poison came down from consortium workings that were never permitted, never inspected, never on any map the public is allowed to see. Vane knew. He spent years trying to prove it alone, and they ruined him for it, and now you want me to hand his proof back to them so they can bury it with everything else." She shook her head slowly. "You're offering me my name, Renn. My name isn't worth a valley."

"Your name is your livelihood—"

"My name is mine," Lena said. "It's the one thing I've carried through two blacklists and three borders and every col and scree slope between here and Corven, and I know exactly what it weighs, and I know it's not heavier than what's sewn against my ribs. You want the map? Take it from me. But I won't hand it to you, and I won't hand it to him, and I will stand in that hearing in Corven and say everything I know about the grey water and the forged permits and the boy at the Grey Stair, and I'll say it with your seal on my arrest warrant if that's how it has to be." She leaned forward. "Here are my terms, Magistrate, and they're the only ones: stand with us. Testify. Put your name on the right side of the ledger for once. That's the deal. There is no other deal."

For a long moment Renn said nothing. The wind filled the silence, and the fire ticked, and Lena heard Tomas breathing behind her, slow and controlled, the breathing of a man listening hard.

Then Renn laughed — short, bitter, exhausted, a laugh with no humor in it anywhere.

"Do you know what I came up that mountain prepared to do?" she said. "If you refused? I was authorized to detain you both. To hold you at this station until Brakke's party arrived. That was the arrangement. My seal, his men. I told myself it was lawful custody. I told myself a great many things on the climb up." She looked into the coals. "And then I got here, and I watched you feed a fire splinter by splinter like a woman rationing the last of something, and I thought: she knows exactly what things cost. She's the only person in this whole affair who does."

She rose. Her knees cracked; she was older than her face let on, Lena realized, older than the uniform had ever allowed her to seem.

"I can't promise you the hearing goes your way," Renn said. "Brakke has men in Corven. He has men everywhere. And if it goes badly, my affidavit dies with me and you hang for everything, including things you didn't do." She pulled her hood up. "But I'll tell you this for free, Okafor, since you gave me tea I didn't drink and warmth I didn't earn: he's not following your trail anymore. He stopped needing it two days ago. He knows where you're going. Everyone does. The daughter. The hearing. He's not chasing you across the mountain." Her eyes met Lena's, and in them was the thing she had climbed through a whiteout to deliver, the thing she had risked her compromised neck to say. "He's waiting for you at the bottom. And he's arranged the ground so that every road into Corven passes through his hands."

She stepped to the doorway, into the flying snow, and paused there with her back to them.

"The east face," she said. "That's the only road he hasn't bought. Because no one's fool enough to take it." A beat. "No one but you."

Then she was gone, down into the white, her footsteps filling in behind her almost before she'd made them, until there was nothing but the storm and the small red eye of the fire and the weight against Lena's ribs, warm from her body, patient as a debt.

Behind her, Tomas spoke for the first time, his voice rough with sleep and something else.

"She's right," he said. "About the east face. About all of it." A pause, and then, quieter: "Lena. You know what's on the east face."

Lena looked at the fire. Below them, miles down through the storm, the glacier waited — and beyond the glacier, the descent she had sworn, after the Kessel Face, never to make again.

"I know," she said. "Wake up properly, Tomas. We leave at first light."
