## 16. The Descent to Corven

The snow came at dawn like a decision.

Lena stood at the mouth of the couloir and watched it fall — not the flirtatious flurries of the past week, not the dry powder that hissed off the ridgelines and vanished, but a real snowfall, dense and silent and committed, the kind that closed passes for a season and buried the careless. It fell straight down, windless, which was worse in its way. Wind you could read. This was just the sky emptying itself over them with the patience of something that had all winter to wait.

"Six hours," Tomas said beside her. He'd been studying the east face through the scratched lens of his goggles since first light, and now he lowered them and rubbed the raw skin beneath his eyes. "Maybe five if we move well. After that the gullies fill and we're climbing a waterfall of slush."

"Then we don't stop."

He looked at her — that long look he'd developed somewhere on the glacier, after the crevasse, after the storm-bound night when he'd finally told her what happened on the Kessel Face and she'd learned that the exile she'd carried for six years had been built on a kindness she never knew existed. She still didn't know how to stand inside that knowledge. It fit her like borrowed boots.

"I wasn't going to suggest stopping," he said.

"No," Lena said. "You weren't."

Below them the east face dropped away in a chaos of ribs and gullies, grey rock streaked white, the whole descent stitched together by ledges barely wide enough for a boot. It was the route she'd promised herself she'd never use again — too exposed, too loose, the kind of ground where one man's mistake became everyone's funeral. But Brakke had burned their caches, taken the northern line, and left them this: the dangerous way down, the way no sane party would follow.

Which meant Brakke would follow it anyway. He'd already proven he was willing to spend men like currency. The question was whether he understood the exchange rate on this particular face.

Lena checked the map one last time — Vane's map, soft as cloth now from handling, its ink lines worn faint at the folds — then folded it into the inner pocket of her jacket, against her chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat. The journal went beside it. Everything that mattered about the last two months weighed less than a kilogram and could be lost in a single stumble.

They roped up. They descended into the falling snow.

* * *

The face talked to them all morning.

It spoke in small voices — the tick of expanding rock warming under the snow's blanket, the whisper of spindrift pouring off the seracs above, the groan of the couloir settling. Lena had spent enough years on stone to know that mountains were never silent; they were always negotiating with gravity, and climbers were simply eavesdroppers hoping to cross the room before the argument turned physical.

She moved first, testing each hold before trusting it, knocking loose stones clear with deliberate taps so they wouldn't surprise the rope behind her. Tomas followed thirty feet back, then paid out line for the anchor transitions. Neither of them spoke much. There was nothing to say that the mountain wasn't already saying better.

At the third ledge system they stopped to re-rack, and Lena risked a look back up the route.

For a moment she saw nothing — just the white blur of the falling snow, the grey smear of the couloir. Then the snow thinned for a breath, the way it did sometimes, a curtain parting, and she saw them: four figures descending the upper gullies, roped in pairs, moving with the grim efficiency of men who climbed for wages rather than love. Brakke's party. And at the front of the lower rope, unmistakable even at distance, the wide set of Brakke's own shoulders, hunched into the weather like a man leaning into his own conviction.

"They took the bait," Tomas murmured, following her gaze. "The east face."

"They didn't take it. They chose it." Lena turned back to the rock. "There's a difference. He knows exactly what this ground costs. He's decided to pay it."

"And us?"

"We're trying not to pay at all." She clipped the rope back onto her harness. "Move. Every hour we're ahead is an hour of snow between us and him."

They descended. The ledge traversed left across a wall of banded schist, slick now with wet snow, and below it the gully plunged four hundred feet to a fan of debris where the mountain had been dropping its garbage for ten thousand years. Lena kept three points of contact and counted her breaths and did not look down except to place her feet, because looking down on the east face was a luxury for people with time.

An hour past midday, the mountain raised its voice.

It started as a sound she felt before she heard it — a deep percussion through the soles of her boots, transmitted through the rock itself, the way you feel a door slam in another room. Then the actual noise arrived, rolling down from above: a crack like a rifle shot, then another, then a staccato drumroll of impacts.

"Rockfall!" Tomas shouted. "Get under — *move!*"

Lena threw herself against the wall beneath the one overhang the ledge offered, a slab of protruding schist no bigger than a table, and pressed herself flat as the sky came down. Stones rattled past her — first pebbles hissing like sleet, then fist-sized rocks shrieking off the walls, then something huge that hit the gully below with a boom she felt in her teeth. Snow and dust exploded outward. The world went white, then brown, then white again.

She counted. Ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty. The drumroll faded downslope, punctuated by diminishing crashes, until only the snow remained, falling as if nothing had happened.

"Tomas." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Tomas, report."

"Here." A cough, then movement, then his face emerging from the dust cloud, goggles plastered white. "Whole. You?"

"Whole." She peered upslope through the thinning haze. The couloir above had been scoured clean, fresh scars of dark rock showing through the snow. Somewhere up there, the source had let go — a frozen seep thawing, a block losing its grip. The east face collecting its toll.

Then she saw the rest.

One of the figures on the upper rope was gone. Not fallen — she could see the rope still running taut down the gully, anchored to the remaining three — but down, crumpled at the base of the rockfall scar, motionless in a way that living things were not motionless. The other three had gathered around him, small and black against the white, and even at that distance she could read the geometry of their bodies: the crouch, the hands pressed to a head or chest, the terrible stillness of men discovering the price of the ground they'd chosen.

Lena lowered her goggles and did not look away, because she thought someone should witness it.

"He's dead," Tomas said quietly. He'd seen it too. "Or close enough."

"Yes."

"That could have been us. We were on that line an hour ago."

"It could have been us any hour for six weeks." Lena unclipped from the anchor and began re-racking, her fingers working automatically while her mind worked elsewhere. "That's the arithmetic of this face. We've all been spending from the same account since the pass. He just made a withdrawal."

Tomas was quiet a moment. Below them the fan of debris smoked with dust. "Do we go down to them?"

The question hung in the falling snow. Lena looked at the three figures above — one dead or dying, three alive — and then down the route, toward Corven, toward Mireille, toward a hearing that would happen with or without her, permits expiring on a date that did not care about grief.

And she found, examining herself honestly, that she wanted to go down to them. That some part of her — the part that had carried a client's death for six years, the part that had saved Brakke's employer's enemy without hesitation on the glacier — was already reaching for the rope.

But she also knew what Brakke would do with that mercy. He'd take it, and then he'd take her map, and then he'd take her freedom, and he'd call all three of it order. She had seen the cache burn. She had seen what he believed.

"No," she said, and hated the word in her mouth. "If we go up, we lose the face. If we lose the face, we lose Corven. And if we lose Corven, that man died for nothing — his boss wins everything he climbed here to win." She met Tomas's eyes through the snow. "I'm not spending the valley to mourn him. I'm sorry. That's the arithmetic today."

Tomas held her gaze for a long moment. Six years ago he'd have argued. On the Kessel Face he'd have argued. Now he just nodded once, slow, like a man signing something.

"Then we climb down fast," he said, "and we make it mean something."

* * *

They made the lower wall by late afternoon, and that was where the stalemate found them.

The route funneled through a slot — a chimney between two buttresses, twenty feet of awkward climbing, the last real difficulty before the slopes eased toward the valley forests. Lena led it, jammed and stemmed and mantled over the chockstone at the top, and came out onto a shoulder of snow-covered grass with the whole eastern watershed spread below her: the grey-green fall of the forest, the thread of the river, and far down, half-hidden in the weather, the smoke of Corven rising from its chimneys like a handful of grey ribbons.

Home stretch. Twelve hours, maybe less, if the snow held off from turning serious.

She fixed the rope for Tomas and was hauling the packs up behind him when she heard the shout — not from above, but from the flank, from the ridge to the north where the east face met the arête.

Brakke. Three of them now, the fourth's body left where the mountain had put it, or perhaps carried no further than decency required. They stood on the ridge shoulder two hundred meters north, and the snow had thinned again, and there was nothing between the two parties but open slope.

For a long moment nobody moved. It was almost formal, the way they regarded each other across the gap — two expeditions arriving at the same ledger from opposite pages.

Then Brakke raised one arm, not in threat but in something like address, and shouted across the wind. His voice arrived shredded, but the words survived:

"You can't outrun the permit date! Neither can I! We both end up in Corven!"

Lena stood on the shoulder with the rope in her hands and let the wind take his meaning apart and put it back together. He was right, and he knew she knew it. The race was nearly run. Whatever happened next would happen in streets and hearing rooms, among lawyers and ledgers, on ground where neither of them had any advantage — where a magistrate's conscience and a daughter's nerve were worth more than any enforcer's hirelings.

So she did the only thing left that the mountain allowed. She cupped her hands and shouted back, and her voice cracked on the cold air but carried:

"Then bring paper instead of ropes! Because in Corven, the truth climbs better than you do!"

She couldn't tell, at that distance, whether his face changed. She liked to think it did.

They descended through the treeline as the light failed, moving fast now on gentler ground, the snow thickening around them until the forest became a corridor of white silence. Behind and above, the lamps of Brakke's party bobbed down the ridge like slow, patient stars. Nobody fired anything. Nobody shouted again. Two parties walking toward the same valley, spending nothing more tonight, saving everything for what waited below.

Near midnight they reached the river trail, and Lena stopped, finally, under a spruce heavy with new snow, and looked back up at the range one last time before the trees closed over it.

Somewhere up there, under the accumulating white, lay the east face and its new grave. Somewhere up there lay the lode, and the poisoned valley, and every promise she was carrying against her chest. Six weeks ago she'd crossed a col with a family of migrants and understood that stopping was no longer an option. Tonight, with Corven's lights glowing through the snowfall below her, she understood something else: the running was almost over. What came next would require a different kind of courage — the kind that stood still in a room full of powerful people and refused to sit down.

"Tomas," she said. "Tomorrow we walk in the front way."

He pulled down his goggles and looked at her, snow crusted in his beard, exhaustion and something brighter sharing his face.

"And if they arrest you at the gate?"

"Then Renn will have to decide," Lena said, "which side of the ledger she's standing on. Because tomorrow, everyone's accounts come due."

She shouldered her pack, and together they started down through the falling snow, toward the lights of Corven.
