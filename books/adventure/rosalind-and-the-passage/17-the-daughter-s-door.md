## 17. The Daughter's Door

Corven announced itself the way dying things do: by smell.

Lena caught it from the ridge above the valley, two hours before the town resolved out of the morning haze — a sour, mineral tang riding up on the thermals, like water left too long in iron. She had smelled it before, in the poisoned valley below the lode, and her stomach tightened with a recognition she didn't want. The wind off the mine workings carried the same signature here, diluted but unmistakable, threaded through the woodsmoke and wet stone of a town waking up.

Below her, Tomas paused on the switchback, breathing hard, his left hand still wrapped where the crevasse ice had chewed it. He looked back the way they'd come — at the east face, white now to its shoulders with the first real snowfall, at the dark thread of scree where Brakke's hired man had fallen three days ago and not gotten up.

"They'll be a day behind us," he said. "Maybe less."

"Then we don't stop." Lena shifted the weight of her pack. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, the map rode like a second heartbeat. Vane's journal was tucked beside it, its leather cover soft as old skin. "The clinic first. Then the hearing."

"And if the daughter won't listen?"

Lena looked down at Corven — the slate roofs, the crooked bell tower, the grey ribbon of the river cutting through it all, running the color of dishwater even from this height. "Then I put it in her hands anyway," she said. "And she can throw it in the river."

Tomas made a sound that might have been a laugh. "You'd let her?"

"It's hers to throw."

They came down into the valley through terraced fields gone fallow, fences leaning like tired men, and crossed the river on a footbridge whose planks were furred with something pale and wrong. Lena touched the rail as she passed and wiped her fingers on her coat without thinking about it. The water beneath was opaque, moving without sparkle, a living thing that had been taught not to shine.

* * *

The clinic sat at the low end of town, a converted granary with a new sign over the door and old damp climbing the walls inside. Lena smelled carbolic and wet plaster and, underneath both, that same mineral sourness, as if the building itself had been drinking the river.

Mireille Vane was easy to find because she was impossible to miss. She moved through the ward like weather — quick, certain, trailing orders behind her. A tall woman with her father's heavy jaw and none of his stoop, hair scraped back, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hands red from washing. She was setting a splint on a child's forearm when Lena stopped in the doorway, and she didn't look up for a full minute, and Lena used that minute to study her face and find Aldous Vane in it, line by line, the way you find a route on a wall you've only seen drawn.

When Mireille finally raised her eyes, they were the color of slate and just as warm.

"We're full," she said. "If it's the fever, there's a list. If it's the cough, sit down and wait your turn like everyone else."

"It's neither." Lena stepped inside and let the door swing shut behind her. "My name is Lena Okafor. I'm a guide. Your father hired me six weeks ago."

Something happened in the woman's face — a flicker, quickly banked, like a match struck and shaken out. "I don't have a father," she said. "I have a name he happens to share. What do you want?"

"To give you something. It will take ten minutes of your time and I know you don't have them, so I'll spend them carefully." Lena held the woman's gaze. "Your father is dead, Mireille. He died at dawn on the fourth day after I met him, in a room at the Last Inn at Halden Saddle, coughing his lungs out into a basin while I sat with him because there was no one else. He died asking me to bring you this."

She unslung the pack. Her hands were steady; she noticed that with a kind of distant professional pride, the way she'd once noted her own pulse on the Kessel Face, calm hands on bad ground. She drew out the journal first, then the map in its oilcloth, and laid them on the table by the door, between a bowl of splint-wraps and a chipped enamel lamp.

Mireille didn't look at them. She looked at Lena, and her voice when it came was flat as the valley floor. "Get out."

"No."

"You come down off my mountain carrying his name and his— whatever that is, and you think—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, visibly, jaw tightening around the break like a fist around glass. "He sent strangers. That's what he did. He sent letters I never answered and money I never spent and now he sends a guide with a blacklisted name I've heard whispered in this valley for years, and you expect me to stand here and grieve on schedule? On your schedule?" She pointed at the door with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "Out. Both of you. This is a clinic, not a confessional."

Behind Lena, Tomas shifted his weight. She felt him deciding whether to speak and was grateful when he didn't.

"I'm not leaving until you take them," Lena said. "Not because I'm stubborn — though I am — but because I promised a dying man, and I keep my promises past death. It's the only currency I've got left that anyone trusts." She tapped the journal once, lightly. "Read it or burn it. But it goes home with you. That was the whole of the bargain."

"He made no bargains with me." Now Mireille did look at the journal, and Lena watched the hatred in her face meet something older underneath it, something that predated the hatred and would outlast it. "Twenty-two years. Do you know how long twenty-two years is to a girl waiting at a window? He went up for one season. One. And every year after, the letters came instead of him, and every letter had an excuse wearing another excuse's coat, and then the letters stopped coming and I learned he'd sold his gear to pay a debt and kept climbing anyway, for *them*, for the consortium, checking their stakes like a dog running ahead of a cart." Her breath shuddered. "He chose the mountain. Let the mountain keep him."

"He did choose the mountain," Lena said quietly. "But not the way you think. Read the journal. That's all I ask. One night. If you still want me gone tomorrow, I'll go, and I won't come back."

For a long moment the ward breathed around them — a child coughing two beds down, the drip of the pump, the wind testing the shutters. Then Mireille snatched the journal off the table, not gently, holding it the way you hold a snake you've decided not to kill yet.

"One night," she said. "And the map stays with you. I don't want his treasure. I never wanted anything of his."

"The map isn't treasure," Lena said. "It's evidence. And tomorrow you'll understand why that's worse."

* * *

They took a room at a lodging house near the bridge because it was the only one with a view of the clinic door, and Lena told herself this was tactics and not guilt. Tomas cleaned his hand by lamplight, wincing, while she sat at the window watching the clinic's windows glow and then, one by one, go dark.

"She hates him," Tomas said.

"Yes."

"Do you hate yours?"

Lena was quiet long enough that the question answered itself. "Mine died before I could decide," she said finally. "There's a kind of mercy in that."

Below, a figure crossed the square toward the clinic — a big man walking with the deliberate ease of someone who wanted to be seen walking. Even at this distance, even in the bad light, Lena knew the set of those shoulders. Brakke had made the valley a day faster than she'd guessed, and he wasn't going to the magistrate's office.

He was going to the daughter.

Lena was down the stairs before Tomas finished saying her name.

* * *

She reached the clinic door as Brakke reached it from the other side of the square, and for a moment they stood facing each other across ten feet of wet cobbles, the clinic's lamp burning yellow between them like a small stubborn fire.

"Guide Okafor." His voice was mild, almost courteous, which was the worst thing about it. "You've cost me two men and a season. I'd call that impressive if it weren't so expensive."

"You can count better than that," Lena said. "You've cost a valley its water and a good man his last year. We're not finished settling accounts."

Brakke smiled faintly and turned to the door, raising his hand to knock, and Lena moved — not fast, but placed, stepping into the space between his knuckles and the wood the way she'd step onto a hold she trusted.

"He's dead," she said. "Vane. Has been for weeks. Whatever you're here to take from his daughter, there's nothing left to trade for it. The permits expire in four days. Go home."

Something moved behind Brakke's eyes — not anger; arithmetic. "Four days," he repeated softly. "Yes. And in four days, a frightened woman with a famous grudge walks into a hearing with a piece of paper she doesn't understand, and my employers' lawyers fold her flat by lunch." He looked down at Lena, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "You should have taken Renn's offer on the glacier, guide. Justice is heavy cargo. Most people die trying to carry it alone."

"Not alone," Lena said. Behind her, the clinic door opened, and Mireille stood in the frame with the journal clutched against her chest and her father's jaw set hard, and Lena heard herself say, "She reads fast."

Brakke looked at Mireille for a long moment — at the journal, at the red-rimmed eyes above it — and for the first time since Halden Saddle, the enforcer hesitated on open ground.

Inside the clinic, on the table behind Mireille, lay the map in its opened oilcloth, and beside it a fresh page of notes in Mireille's slanted hand, copied through the night: dates, permit numbers, a surveyor's signature that matched no licensed surveyor in the registry — and, circled twice, the words *filed under false assay, 9th of the Reaping Month,* in Aldous Vane's failing script.

Mireille lifted her chin. "The hearing is in four days," she said. "We'll see who folds."

Brakke's smile thinned to a blade's width. "So we will."

He turned and walked back across the square into the dark, and Lena stood in the doorway beside the daughter of the man she'd promised to save, watching him go, feeling the countdown begin in her chest like a second pulse — four days, four days — while somewhere above the valley the snows kept falling on the lode, and the river ran grey, and the truth sat warm between two women who had loved the same difficult man in the ways he'd allowed.
