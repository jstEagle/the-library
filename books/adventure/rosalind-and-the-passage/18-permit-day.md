## 18. Permit Day

The morning of the hearing, Mireille woke before the bell at the clinic and lay in the dark listening to the sound of the valley breathing.

It was not a peaceful sound. It was the sound of a place that had been sick for a long time and was only now learning to name its illness. Down the hill, the Greywater — the river that had given the lower village its name and, in the last six years, its coughing children — ran the colour of tarnished coin. She had tested it herself a hundred times, filling her little glass vials at dawn, holding them up to the light like a woman reading omens. Silver in the water. That was the joke the valley told itself now, when it told jokes at all. The consortium had promised them prosperity and delivered it after all: prosperity in the runoff, silver in the wells, payable to no one but the doctors.

She dressed in the dark. The good dress — the one she'd bought three years ago for a funeral that turned out to be someone else's — still hung in the narrow wardrobe where it had been waiting, patient as a widow. Grey wool, high collar, a single line of covered buttons down the front. It had cost her two weeks' wages and she had worn it four times, each time to stand in a room full of men deciding something about her life.

Today it would be five.

On the table by the window, the journal sat where she had left it at two in the morning, weighted under the brass lamp to keep the wind from turning its pages. Beside it lay the map, rolled tight in its oilcloth, and beside that, in a folder she had made from a clinic ledger's discarded cover, the testimony — eleven statements, signed and thumb-printed, from the households along the Greywater. She had taken them over four days, walking door to door in the grey drizzle, and what she remembered most was not the anger. It was the care. How each family had read the statement twice, corrected her spelling, asked her to write down the exact words their children used to describe the taste of the water. *Like licking a spoon.* *Like old coins.* *Like the snow when it's dirty.* They wanted the record to be exact. They had waited six years to be exact.

She had not slept. That was fine. She had not slept before the other four times either, and she had learned that the body could carry you on less than nothing if the mind gave it a reason.

Her father's reason was in the journal.

She had read it three times now, cover to cover, and parts of it a dozen times. The early entries were almost unbearable — a younger Aldous Vane, full of the same certainty she had inherited without knowing its source, writing about the lode in the high country the way other men wrote about a lover. *She sings when you tap her. You can hear it through the rock.* And then, slowly, year by year, the certainty souring into something else. The permits. The survey reports that didn't match the ground. The names crossed out — the men who had signed the fraudulent assessments and then, one by one, been paid off, promoted, or buried. The last third of the book was not a prospector's journal at all. It was a case file. Her father had spent fifteen years building a prosecution and had never found a courtroom to carry it into.

And at the back, folded into a pocket sewn from waxed canvas, the letter he had never sent. *Mireille — I know what you will think of me, and you will be right to think it. But I am leaving you the truth, which is the only thing I ever had that was worth anything, and I am sorry I did not know how to give you anything else.*

She had cried over that letter exactly once, on the night it arrived with a stranger's story attached — the guide, Kavanagh, sitting in her clinic's back room with snowmelt still on her boots, telling her in a flat, exhausted voice how Aldous Vane had died. Not bravely, not dramatically. Coughing, in a cheap room, at dawn, with his boots off. It had been the boots-off detail that broke her open. Her father had always slept in his boots. Whoever he had been at the end, he had expected to get up and walk.

She had not forgiven him. She wanted that recorded somewhere too, in the exactness of her own heart. But grief had arrived ahead of forgiveness, the way weather arrives ahead of the season, and she had learned in the clinic that you treated the presenting illness first and went looking for its cause afterward.

The bell rang. She took up the journal, the map, the folder, and went down into the grey morning.

* * *

The concession office in Corven occupied what had once been the customs house, back when the valley's trade had been wool and cheese instead of ore and grievance. It was a handsome building, granite-faced, with a portico of four columns and a floor of black-and-white marble worn to a soft shine by a century of boots. The consortium had kept the building and let the grandeur go to seed — the columns were stained, the windows needed washing, and the great brass scales in the entry hall, relics of the customs days, were green with neglect. Someone had hung a banner over them for the occasion: **CONCESSION REVIEW — PERMITS 114 THROUGH 119 — PUBLIC SESSION**.

*Public* was doing generous work in that sentence. The hearing room held perhaps sixty people, and by eight in the morning every seat was taken and the standing room along the walls was three deep. Mireille recognized faces from the valley: the Harsanyi brothers, whose orchard had died tree by tree; old Petra Voss, who ran the mill and had buried a grandson; the schoolteacher, Anselm, with his ink-stained cuffs and his notebook already open. They had come up in carts before dawn. They stood in their good coats with their caps in their hands, and they had the particular stillness of people who have learned not to hope loudly.

At the front of the room, on a raised dais, sat the review panel: three concession officers from the regional capital, grey men in grey coats, with the consortium's counsel at a table to their left — a sleek advocate named Corvus Dain, whose firm's name was on more buildings in the capital than the regional governor's. And to the right, alone at a smaller table, Magistrate Ilse Renn.

Mireille had never seen Renn in person. She had expected someone harder. What she saw was a woman of perhaps fifty, plainly dressed, with the look of someone who had not slept either — and who, unlike Mireille, had not chosen it. Her hands were folded on the table in front of her, and they were very still, and Mireille thought: *those are the hands of someone holding something down.*

At the back of the room, near the doors, stood a man Mireille knew only by reputation and by the description Lena Kavanagh had given her in the clinic's back room, three nights ago, in a voice like a report from a war: broad, silver-haired, with a face like a closed ledger. Brakke. He did not sit. He stood with his back to the wall and his arms folded, and he watched the room the way a man watches water for signs of ice.

Mireille found a place in the standing room, second row, where she could see the panel. The journal was in her satchel, against her ribs. The map was in her coat, inside pocket, left side, over the heart — she had not planned that, but when she noticed it she did not move it.

The senior concession officer, a man named Feld with the voice of a man reading train times, called the session to order at half past eight. He read the schedule. He read the standing rules — no interruptions, testimony to be given under attestation, the panel's findings to be published within thirty days. Then he read the consortium's position, which Corvus Dain delivered standing, in a voice like warmed honey poured over a blade.

It was, Mireille had to admit, a beautiful performance. Dain walked the panel through fifteen years of the consortium's stewardship of the Verren concessions as though reading from a book of psalms: the employment figures, the road improvements, the schoolhouse built at Kessel Ford, the charitable clinic — *which, I note with respect, operates in the very valley we are discussing* — and Mireille felt the room shift slightly at that, felt the small cold weight of it, because it was true, the clinic took consortium money, her own salary had consortium fingerprints on it, and Dain knew it, and had put it in the record on purpose. *The permits in question have been held in continuous good standing. The renewal is a formality. We trust the panel will not mistake the grievances of a vocal minority for a material deficiency.*

*A vocal minority.* Mireille looked at the sixty people in the room, and the ones who couldn't get in, standing out on the steps in the cold. She thought of the eleven thumbprints in her folder. She thought: *you have just made a mistake, advocate. You have told the record what we are. Now the record will hear what we are.*

The panel asked its questions. Feld asked about water quality. Dain referred to the consortium's own monitoring reports — *independently verified* — which showed all parameters within tolerance. The word *tolerance* went through the room like a draft. Petra Voss, standing three feet from Mireille, made a sound low in her throat, and Mireille understood it completely, because she had made the same sound the first time she'd read the consortium's monitoring reports in the journal's appendix — the reports Aldous Vane had obtained, compared against his own samples, and annotated in a small furious hand: *tolerance of what?*

The morning went on. The panel heard the consortium's operations director. The panel heard a road engineer. At eleven, Feld announced a recess until one o'clock, and the room exhaled like a held breath released.

It was during the recess, in the marble hall under the green brass scales, that everything changed — though Mireille would not understand how much until the afternoon.

She had gone to the washroom to press cold water on her face, and when she came back she saw Renn standing alone by the windows, and she saw Brakke cross the hall to her, unhurried, and lean in, and speak. She was too far away to hear the words. But she saw Renn's face. She saw something pass over it — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman confirming a thing she had already suspected and had hoped, until that moment, to be wrong about.

And she saw Renn step back — a small, deliberate step, half a pace, the kind of step a person takes to make a boundary visible — and say something in return. Brakke's face did not change. But his hands came unfolded, and that, Mireille thought, was the loudest thing in the room.

* * *

The afternoon session opened with the public testimony, and Feld read out the order of speakers, and Mireille's name was fourth.

She heard her own name spoken in that flat administrative voice and felt the strange doubling of it — *Mireille Vane* — her father's name in a room full of strangers, attached to her like a coat she hadn't chosen. She walked to the witness table. She set down her folder. She unrolled the map and weighted its corners with the inkwell, the water glass, and the small brass scale of justice that Anselm the schoolteacher pressed into her hand from his pocket without a word.

Feld asked her to state her name and her standing.

"My name is Mireille Vane. I am the daughter of Aldous Vane, prospector, deceased eleven days. I run the free clinic at Greywater Ford. And I am here on behalf of the households along the Greywater, whose statements I hold, and on behalf of my father, who spent the last fifteen years of his life assembling evidence that the permits under review were obtained by fraud."

The room did not erupt. That was the thing no one had told her about courage — how quiet it was, how procedural, how it sounded like reading names aloud in a room that has gone very still. She opened the folder and began.

She read the statements. Eleven of them, in the families' own words, with their thumbprints. *Like licking a spoon.* She read the clinic's own records — the incidence of the grey-lung cases by year, mapped against the dates the upstream works came online. She read the consortium's monitoring figures and, beside them, her father's samples, taken from the same points on the same dates, the discrepancies laid out in a table he had drawn himself, in pencil, in a hand that shook toward the end. She read the survey reports. She read the names of the men who had signed them, and the dates of their promotions, and — in two cases — the dates of their funerals.

Then she opened the journal, and read her father's last entry, dated the day before he died:

*If I am gone before this is read, the map shows where the lode lies, and the lode lies above the Greywater, and the water tells the truth about what sits above it. Let them have the silver. Let the valley have the water. I was wrong about which one was the treasure, and it only took me sixty years to find out.*

When she finished, the room was silent in a way Mireille had never heard a room be silent. Not empty silence. Weighed silence.

Corvus Dain rose to cross-examine, and it was here that the hearing turned — though not in the direction he intended.

He was good. He attacked the chain of custody of the journal — *a document of unknown provenance, produced by an interested party, the daughter of a man with a documented grievance* — and he attacked the samples — *collected by an unaccredited rural clinic* — and he attacked the statements — *gathered by the same interested party, under conditions no court would accept.* He was smooth, and precise, and he was winning, Mireille could feel it, the panel's grey faces closing like shutters — and then he made his error. He turned to the panel and said, in his warmed-honey voice:

"Members of the panel, I would draw your attention to the fact that every document before you originates from two sources: a dead man's papers and the testimony of interested valley residents. There is no independent corroboration of any allegation before this panel. The consortium welcomes any genuine investigation—"

"Then you will welcome mine."

The voice came from the dais's right, from the small table where Magistrate Renn sat alone. She had not stood. She was still folding her hands, still in that terrible calm, and the whole room turned toward her the way iron filings turn.

Feld's voice came out careful. "Magistrate Renn. This panel has not called upon—"

"No," Renn said. "It hasn't. That is an omission I am here to correct." She rose now, and took a document from her case, and Mireille saw that her hands, for the first time all day, were not still. They were shaking, very slightly, and she let them. "Three weeks ago I was approached by an agent of the consortium — a Mr. Brakke, who is standing at the back of this room — and asked to secure the surrender of a certain map, and to obtain testimony regarding its sale, in exchange for which I was authorized to offer the bearer — one Lena Kavanagh, currently under blacklist — a full expungement of record." She laid the document on the dais. "This is my own sworn deposition regarding that approach, with dates, with the authorization letter I was shown, and with the name of the consortium officer who countersigned it. I have spent three weeks deciding what to do with it. I watched a man this morning call the people of this valley a vocal minority, and I watched this panel write it down. So: I am the independent corroboration, Advocate Dain. Here I am. Sworn, signed, and dated."

The sound that went through the room was not a gasp. It was lower than that, older — the sound sixty people make when a thing they have known in their bones for years is said aloud by someone with standing to say it.

Brakke moved. Mireille saw it from the corner of her eye — a shift of weight, a turn toward the doors — and saw that the doors were already flanked by two of the regional constables, and that they had been standing there since the recess, and that Renn had arranged that too, three weeks of deciding, and none of it wasted.

Brakke looked at the constables. He looked at Renn. And then he did the thing that Mireille would think about for a long time afterward: he straightened his coat, folded his hands behind his back, and stood still. As if order, even now, even turned against him, were the only thing he knew how to honor.

Feld's gavel came down twice, more for the panel's own benefit than the room's. "This panel," he said, and his train-times voice had cracked open somewhere in the middle, "will adjourn to consider the evidence presented. Findings will be published—"

"Tomorrow," Renn said, and it was not a request, and Feld, after a moment, wrote it down.

* * *

The panel's findings were published at nine the next morning, nailed to the customs house door in the old way and posted in the gazette in the new one. Mireille stood in the small crowd and read them over Petra Voss's shoulder, and her eye went straight to the operative lines:

*Permits 114 through 119 are declared void ab initio, having been procured through fraudulent survey and material misrepresentation. All works thereunder are ordered suspended. Full remediation of the Greywater watershed is ordered, costs to the consortium. The claims and lodes thereunder, including the deposit identified in the document known as the Vane map, revert to the public trust, to be administered for the benefit of the affected communities of the Greywater valley.*

*For the benefit of the affected communities.* Her father's silver, given at last to the water.

Petra Voss read it twice. Then the old woman took off her cap, held it against her chest, and said, to no one and everyone, "He should have seen it." And Mireille, who had spent eleven days learning how to hold a grief that had no clean shape, found that this was the shape: not her own loss, but his — the years he should have had, the morning he should have stood in this crowd for.

She walked back up to the clinic through the grey bright morning. On her desk, where she had left it, lay a note delivered at dawn, in a hand she was learning to know — blunt, slanted, written by someone used to writing in wind:

*It's done, then. The valley has what it needs. I have one more thing to finish — a man I owe a debt to, and a name I mean to clear. If I don't come back down, tell them the map was never mine to keep. — L.K.*

Mireille read it twice, and then she looked up the valley, toward the high passes, where the first true snow of the season was already whitening the shoulders of the range — and where, she understood, the last of the story was still climbing.
