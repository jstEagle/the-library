## 19. Brakke's Last Climb

The trailhead above Corven was called the Shepherd's Stair, though no shepherd had used it in a generation. It began where the valley road gave out — a shelf of packed earth and old stone walls, then a ramp of scree that climbed four hundred meters to the first true col, where the high country opened like a hand. Lena had gone up it eleven times in her life. She knew every stone that moved and every stone that held, which on scree was the only kind of knowledge worth carrying.

She came up from the valley before dawn, alone, because the last thing had to be done alone. That was not sentiment; it was arithmetic. The original map — Vane's map, ink on oilcloth, the one thing Brakke could not afford to exist once the hearing convened — could not be left in Corven, where the consortium still had men and money and a magistrate's ear, however cracked Renn's loyalty had become. It could not be carried into the hearing by Mireille, whose hands needed to hold only paper that would save her, not paper that would damn her if it were taken. So Lena would carry it up the Shepherd's Stair to the col, and over the col to the waystation at Halden Saddle, where a courier under Tomas's arrangement would take it down the far side to the provincial archive, beyond anyone's reach, its existence a matter of record before the permits expired.

A simple plan. She had learned long ago that the simple plans were the ones you survived, because they gave you nothing to manage but yourself.

The morning was the color of unpolished silver. Frost furred the stone walls. Her breath went up ahead of her like something she was following. She climbed steadily, not fast — fast was for people who hadn't yet learned what the mountain did to the hurried — and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge and laid a blade of light along the scree, she was two-thirds of the way to the col, and she let herself think, for one careless minute, that she might actually be early.

That was when she saw the figure below her.

He was small against the grey slope, moving well for a man built like a door, and she knew him before she could make out anything but the shape of him: the deliberate, unhurried gait of someone who had never once in his life believed the mountain could refuse him. Brakke. Of course. He had missed her at the clinic, missed her at Mireille's door, and there was only one road left that the map could take, and he had come to stand on it.

She did not run. Running on scree was how people died, and besides, some cold clear part of her understood that this had been coming since the inn, since the first night a coughing stranger bought her dinner and asked about the high passes. Every story she had ever been inside of ended on a slope somewhere, with someone asking her for something she would not give.

She turned uphill and kept climbing.

* * *

The col was a saddle of shattered rock between two shoulders of the ridge, wind-scoured, bare of snow even now, though the peaks above it wore the season's first white like a warning. She reached it with her lungs burning and stood a moment with her hands on her knees, and then she took stock the way she always took stock: water, half a liter. Rope, thirty meters. The map, dry inside her jacket, against her skin. And below, closing, one man.

There was a descent off the north side of the col — a steep couloir of loose rock that dropped toward the Halden drainage. She could go down it. She had gone down worse. But Brakke would see her the whole way, a small dark figure crossing open stone, and a man with a rifle didn't need to be a good climber to end a crossing. She had seen enough of what his kind did to know he'd have brought one. Order was worth any price; he'd told her that himself, or close enough, back at the checkpoint, in the flat voice of a man reciting scripture he'd written for himself.

So she did the thing the mountain offered instead, which was the harder thing, which was usually the right thing: she waited.

He came up the last of the scree an hour after midmorning, breathing hard, frost melting on his collar, and stopped ten meters from her with the easy spacing of a man who had done violence often enough to know that distance was a weapon too. He looked older than he had at the checkpoint. There was a rawness around his eyes, as if he'd slept badly for weeks, and she thought — with a strange, unwelcome flicker of sympathy — that a man who believed order was worth any price must be having a very hard autumn indeed, watching the order he served come apart in a courtroom over paperwork.

"Kavanagh." He said her name like a file number.

"Brakke."

"You're carrying it up." He nodded at the north couloir, the far valleys, the whole apparatus of her escape. "Courier at Halden. I know. I've known for two days."

"Then you know the hearing is tomorrow."

"I know the hearing is theater." He stepped closer, boots grinding, and the sound was enormous in the empty air. "Permits get voided, fines get levied, a director resigns into a pension. In five years they reorganize under a new name and start again. You don't kill a thing like the consortium with testimony. You kill it by controlling what it needs." He put out his hand, palm up, almost gentle. "The map. The original. Give it to me and walk down, and none of what happens next happens. You've already won everything else. Let me keep the one thing that keeps my employers rational."

"And if they're not rational?"

His jaw tightened. For a moment the mask slipped and she saw underneath it, and what was underneath was not cruelty. It was fear. A lifetime spent being the instrument of other people's order, and now the order was failing, and he was the man standing closest to the machine when it came apart. She recognized it, God help her. She had worn that same face on the Kessel Face, years ago, holding a rope that was holding nothing.

"They will be," he said. "If I bring them the map."

"And if I say no?"

He sighed, and she watched the gentleness leave him the way weather leaves a valley, all at once, and she was already moving before the sigh finished, because she had grown up reading mountains and men read much the same, and both of them telegraphed their avalanches.

* * *

It should not have lasted more than a minute. He was heavier, stronger, trained; she was faster and knew the ground, and the ground was the whole argument. They went together across the col in a grinding rush of loose stone, his hand closing on her jacket, her elbow finding the hinge of his ribs, the map's oilcloth corner biting her sternum where it rode against her chest. He got her arm twisted behind her once and she dropped her weight and spun out of it the way Tomas had taught her twenty years ago on a glacier neither of them had respected enough, and the memory of that — Tomas, young, laughing, certain — went through her like a hot wire and made her clumsy.

Her heel found the edge of the stable ground without her noticing. That was the truth of scree: it did not announce itself. One step held, and the next was air and sliding stone, and the world tilted, and she went down onto her hip in a hiss of gravel, sliding, grabbing, stopping herself with fingers torn on rock, and Brakke lunged to pin her —

— and the mountain reached up and took him instead.

She heard it before she understood it: a deep, structural sound beneath them, the sound of a slope deciding. The section of scree he'd driven his weight onto let go. Not dramatically. Scree never does. It simply stopped being ground, a slab of it the size of a barn door, and Brakke went with it, arms wheeling, and slid — forty meters, sixty — until the moving stone ran out and left him sprawled against a rib of bedrock with the slope falling away below him in a long grey fan of nothing.

Lena lay where she'd stopped, cheek against cold stone, breath sawing, and listened to the mountain settle. Dust hung in the sunlight like something burning. Far below, Brakke moved. Groaned. Tried to rise.

And screamed.

Not the scream of a man who'd been beaten. The scream of a man who'd discovered, all at once, the exact terms of his situation: a slab of tilted bedrock, a boot wedged in a crack, one hand gripping a nub of rock the size of a fist, and below him three hundred meters of scree funneling down into a ravine with a stream at the bottom of it that no one survived hitting. His rifle had gone with the slide; she could see it glinting fifty meters downslope, absurdly intact, useless.

"Kavanagh!" His voice cracked across the col. "Kavanagh, I'm — the rock's moving. Under my foot. It's moving."

She sat up. Her hands were bleeding. Her hip had a knot of pain in it the size of a fist, and she catalogued it and set it aside, the way you do, the way the mountain teaches you, and she looked at him.

Here is what she felt, and she would remember it honestly for the rest of her life, because honesty was the only currency she had left: she felt nothing. No triumph. No pity either, not at first. Just the flat arithmetic of the slope, the same arithmetic she'd run a hundred times — can he be reached, can she reach him, what will it cost, what is the margin. A man who had burned her supply caches. Who had hunted her across a range with a magistrate's seal in his pocket. Who had, an hour ago, tried to take the map off her body by force and would have taken whatever else stood in the way.

Below him, the scree ticked and settled. His boot shifted. He made a sound she had heard once before, years ago, from a client on the Kessel Face in the last minutes — the sound of a man negotiating with gravity, and losing.

She could walk away. That was the fact of it, plain as the sun. Walk north down the couloir, deliver the map, let the mountain do what the mountain had been trying to do to both of them all week. No one would ever call it murder. The mountain killed enforcers all the time; it was practically administrative. By tomorrow evening she'd be free — truly free, name cleared, blacklist overturned, the whole ledger balanced — and Brakke would be a line in someone's incident report, and the world would be lighter by exactly one man who believed order was worth any price.

Her body was already turning downhill before her mind finished the argument. That was the thing she understood then, kneeling on the broken stone with blood on her hands: the choice had never really been available. Not because of virtue, or not only. Because of what walking away would have made her. You could spend your whole life fighting men who treated people as arithmetic, and if you let one of them die on a slope because the sum came out convenient, you hadn't beaten them. You'd just changed whose arithmetic was running.

Tomas would have said it better. Tomas wasn't there. So she said it to herself, out loud, in the wind, the way you talk to yourself on bad ground:

"Not like this. Not today."

* * *

The rope was thirty meters. The problem was anchors. The col offered her one sound block the size of a table, and she built the anchor off it with a sling and a loop of the old stone wall, testing each piece with her full weight twice, three times, while Brakke shouted at her — first threats, then bargains, then, terribly, instructions, because he was a professional too and professionals cannot stop managing the problem even when they are the problem. She let him talk. It kept him from panicking, and panic was the one thing that would shake the slab loose under him.

"Hold still," she called down. "Both hands. Don't move your foot."

"It's slipping —"

"Then press it flatter. Weight on your palms, not the boot. Look at me, not down."

He looked at her. That was the moment she kept afterward, folded away like the map itself: Brakke's face, dust-grey, stripped of everything — the authority, the certainty, the scripture — leaving only a man on a mountain who wanted very badly to live. She had hated him correctly for weeks. Hatred was easy at a distance. At ten meters, hanging on a rope above him, hatred turned out to be a luxury the work couldn't afford.

She descended to him in three long pitches of the rope, self-belayed off friction, moving the way you move on rotten ground — slow, centered, apologizing to every stone. Twenty meters above him the scree under her own boots began to run, a soft hissing failure, and she froze with her heart slamming and redistributed her weight across six points of contact and breathed until the slope accepted her again. Below, Brakke had gone silent, watching her come with an expression she refused to name.

When she reached him she clipped his harness — he still wore a guide's harness, absurdly, the consortium's equipment budget showing through — and rigged a short loop to his chest so his weight would come onto the rope and off the treacherous boot. "On three, push up with your hands and swing your leg free. If the slab goes, the rope takes you. Trust the rope."

"Do I have a choice?"

"No," she said. "That's what makes it trust."

The slab went on two. She felt it through the rope before she heard it — a shudder, a soft exhalation of stone — and then Brakke swung free over three hundred meters of nothing, weightless for half a second, and the anchor above held, and the old wall held, and the block held, and Lena leaned back into the harness with her boots braced and her whole life running down a nylon line through her hands, and held.

He weighed nothing and everything. She lowered him meter by meter to the rib of bedrock where the slide had ended, where the angle eased and the ground turned trustworthy, and when his boots finally stood on solid stone he staggered and put a hand against the rock and hung his head, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. The wind moved over the col. Somewhere above, a raven made its one remark.

"Why," he said finally. It wasn't gratitude. It was closer to accusation, a man demanding to see the flaw in his own ledger.

Lena coiled the rope with fingers that shook now that the shaking was allowed. "Because you'd have saved me," she said, and knew it was a lie, and let it stand anyway, because some lies are load-bearing. Then, because she owed him one true thing: "Because I've stood where you were standing. And because the difference between us was never going to be the mountain."

He looked at her for a long time. Something moved behind his eyes — not conversion; she was too tired to believe in conversions — but reckoning. A man recalculating.

"The hearing's tomorrow," he said.

"Yes."

"I'll be there." He said it quietly, looking not at her but down the long grey fan of the slope, at the rifle lying in the stones where the mountain had discarded it. "I'll say what I saw. What I was sent to do. All of it." He laughed once, short and without any humor in it at all. "Order's worth any price, Kavanagh. Turns out I finally found a price I can pay."

She left him there with water from her bottle and the route down marked for him in words, and she climbed back to the col with the map dry against her chest and her hands stinging, and stood a moment at the top looking north to Halden Saddle, where the courier waited, and south to Corven, where tomorrow the permits would live or die.

The first snowflakes came while she watched — fat, slow, unhurried, the season arriving at last like a verdict. She pulled her collar up and started down the north side, moving fast now, because the last of the story was waiting below, and for the first time in longer than she could remember, she wanted to be there when it landed.
