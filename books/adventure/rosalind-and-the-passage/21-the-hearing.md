## 21. The Hearing

The courthouse in Corven had once been a granary, and it still smelled faintly of old grain and stone dust, as if the building itself remembered being useful in a humbler way. Lena sat on a bench outside the hearing room with her hands flat on her knees, watching the dust motes turn in the light that fell through the high clerestory windows. The light was the color of weak tea. Outside, the first hard frost of the season had silvered the rooftops, and somewhere down the street a cart driver was arguing with his mule in the patient, repetitive way of a man who has lost this argument every morning for years.

She had dressed in her one clean shirt. She had combed her hair with water from the clinic tap. She had done everything she could think of to look like a woman who belonged indoors, in a room with rules, and she suspected she looked exactly like what she was: someone who had spent six years sleeping on scree and learning to read weather the way priests read scripture.

The door at the end of the corridor opened, and Mireille came out.

Lena had seen her angry, seen her grieving, seen her standing in the concession hearing two days ago with her father's journal held against her chest like a shield, reading Aldous Vane's careful, furious handwriting into the record while the consortium's lawyers tried to talk over her. She had not yet seen her like this — quiet, wrung out, with the particular stillness of a person who has set down something very heavy and does not yet know how to stand without it.

"It's tomorrow," Mireille said. "Yours. They moved it up. Renn's testimony changed the schedule — they want to clear the docket before the permits are formally voided, so nothing can be appealed on procedural grounds." She sat down beside Lena, not quite touching shoulders. "That's good, I think. Renn says it's good."

"Renn says a lot of things."

"She does." Mireille almost smiled. "But she's been straight with me since the hearing. Terrifyingly straight. Like a woman who has decided to spend the rest of her career being honest and wants to get it over with quickly."

Lena huffed something that was nearly a laugh. Below them, through the window, she could see the clinic's grey slate roof, and beyond it the valley, where the remediation crews had already staked their first survey lines above the poisoned water. The lode itself sat under new snow now, unclaimed, its value spoken for by the people who had drunk the cost of it for twenty years. Vane's map hung in the courthouse archive under glass, an exhibit. It looked small there. Maps always did.

"You could still take it," Mireille said quietly. "Some of it. The communities would give you a share. They voted on it. I told them they were fools."

"They're not fools. They're generous, which is worse." Lena shook her head. "I didn't carry that map for money. I carried it because a dying man asked me to. That debt's paid. I'm not going to pick it back up and wear it."

Mireille studied her for a moment. "He'd have liked you," she said. "Which is annoying, because I spent thirty years deciding I didn't."

"He'd have liked you too. He talked about you like a man talks about a north face he never climbed. All respect. All regret."

Mireille's jaw tightened, and Lena watched her decide, deliberately, not to cry in a courthouse corridor. "Go home," Mireille said instead. "Sleep. Eat something that isn't trail food. Tomorrow you'll need to sit still for hours, and I've seen you try to sit still. It's like watching a caged animal negotiate with the bars."

* * *

The hearing room was smaller than Lena expected, and warmer, with a stove ticking in the corner and eleven faces arranged behind a long bench — three magistrates from the regional court, two surveyors, a representative of the mining registry, and a scattering of officials whose functions she never learned. Magistrate Ilse Renn sat apart from them, at a witness table, in her formal grey coat, looking like a woman who had slept badly and decided to make it everyone else's problem.

Lena gave her name. Her full name, spoken aloud into a official record for the first time in six years without it being an accusation: *Lena Okafor, guide.*

"State your history on the Kessel Face expedition," said the senior magistrate, a dry-voiced woman named Corvath who had the air of someone who had read every document twice before entering the room.

And so Lena told it. All of it, in order, for the first time since it happened.

She told them about the client — Herran Doss, wealthy, impatient, a man who had bought his way onto a technical ascent he had no business attempting. She told them about the storm window closing early, about her order to turn back at the shoulder, about Doss unclipping from the fixed line to shortcut a traverse because he wanted the summit photograph before his competitors. She told them, in flat detail, about the fall, the rope, the place where the anchor she had placed held and the rock beneath it did not. She told them how she had reached him. How there had been nothing to reach.

"And afterward," Corvath said. "The inquiry."

"Afterward, the inquiry was conducted by the consortium's contracted safety board, because Doss's expedition was registered under their commercial permit. Their findings required a guide at fault. The widow was offered a settlement contingent on that finding. If the finding changed, the settlement would be contested for years — she had two children and no other income." Lena kept her voice level. "I signed the statement accepting fault. Tomas Reiner, my partner, filed a dissenting report that was never entered into the record. The consortium buried it. I was blacklisted in three jurisdictions within the year."

Renn took the stand after her, and Lena learned things she had only guessed at. Renn testified that she had reviewed the original dissent during her own investigation into the consortium's permit fraud — that Brakke's methods had led her backward through the consortium's paper trail until she found the Kessel Face inquiry sitting at the bottom of it like a stone in a streambed. She testified that the dissenting report existed, that it matched Lena's account in every particular, and that she had verified it against Doss's own recovered journal, which the consortium had also suppressed.

"The blacklist against Lena Okafor," Renn said, in her clipped, unhurried way, "was not a judgment of fact. It was a purchase. The consortium paid for her silence because her silence protected their settlement structure and their insurance exposure. I am telling this court that I helped enforce that silence, and that I was wrong to do it."

The room was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls, wind found a gap and made a thin sound, like the mountain trying to get a word in.

The deliberation took four hours. Lena spent them walking the frozen riverside path below the town, watching ice form at the edges of the current, teaching herself again how to wait. When the summons came, she walked back up through the blue dusk with her heart doing something slow and enormous in her chest.

Corvath read the finding aloud. The words were bureaucratic, bloodless, beautiful: *the finding of fault is vacated; the record is amended; the guiding license suspended under duress is restored; the blacklisting in this jurisdiction is struck and letters shall issue to the others.*

It took her a moment to understand that the silence in the room was for her, and another moment to understand that the woman at the clerk's desk was holding out a document with her own name on it, and that the paper was real, and that her hand, when she took it, was steady.

Six years. Six years of cash payments and false names and crossings made in the dark, and it ended with a clerk handing her a piece of paper and asking her to sign next to an X.

Outside, snow had begun to fall — fine, dry, unhurried snow, the kind that meant cold nights ahead but fair days between. Mireille was waiting on the steps, and she didn't say anything at all, just put her hand briefly on Lena's arm, which from Mireille was a speech.

Tomas was waiting further down, at the bottom of the steps, hat in his hands like a man at a funeral or a wedding.

* * *

They walked together along the river path, because neither of them wanted to have the conversation indoors, where it would have walls around it. The snow thickened and thinned. The ice along the banks clicked softly as the current shifted beneath it.

"They offered me the partnership today," Tomas said finally. "The low valleys. The school, actually — training guides. Licensed routes, legal crossings, the whole respectable life." He laughed, short and self-conscious. "Me. Teaching beginners how to tie in properly. There will be forms."

"There will be so many forms."

"I said yes." He said it carefully, watching her. "I keep waiting to feel like I've betrayed something. The range, maybe. Us. But all I feel is tired in a good way, if that makes sense. Like finishing a route."

"It makes sense." Lena looked out at the water. "You know I can't do that. The low valleys. Walls and forms and—" She gestured vaguely at the town, the lamplighters working their way up the far street. "I'm going back to the high passes. Legal ones now. Real clients, real papers. But high."

"I know." He nodded slowly. "I knew before you did, probably. I used to watch you plan routes and think, she's not planning escapes, she's planning a life, and the life just happens to be vertical." He stopped walking. "Lena. On the glacier. When the crevasse took me. You didn't hesitate."

"You hesitated enough for both of us."

"I mean it. Six years I carried that day on the Kessel Face like a stone, and then you pulled me out of the ice and it just — dissolved. Not forgiven. Just gone, like it had never needed carrying." His voice caught and steadied. "I loved you. You know that. For years, in the way you love someone you climb with, which is the most complete way there is. And I think part of why I couldn't be near you was that I couldn't tell anymore where the love ended and the guilt began."

"Tomas—"

"No, let me finish, I've been rehearsing this for two hundred kilometers." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from before, and it cost him something to produce it. "I'm not asking for anything. I'm saying it plainly so it can be true on the record, since we seem to be a court of record these days. I loved you. I love you. And I'm choosing the valleys, and you're choosing the passes, and those choices are both correct, and that's the whole tragedy and the whole mercy of it."

Lena stood in the falling snow and let it land on her face, cold as truth, soft as forgiveness. She thought about reaching for him. She thought about how easy it would be, and how wrong, like clipping into a rope that wasn't anchored.

Instead she stepped forward and embraced him — brief and fierce, forehead to forehead, the way climbers say goodbye at a col when the weather is turning and each must descend a different face.

"Duty honored," she said against his ear. "Love released. Someone smart should write that down."

"Someone did. In a dissenting report nobody read for six years." He pulled back, wiped his eyes with the heel of his glove, pretending it was the snow. "Write to me. From the high country. Tell me about routes I'll never lead."

"Every month. Whether you answer or not."

"I'll answer. I'm a man of forms now." He shouldered his pack — he had carried it all evening, packed, ready, the way he did everything, thoroughly and sadly and well. "There's a coach south at dawn. And Lena — the first crossing you lead, legal, papers and all. Send me the date. I want to stand at the trailhead and watch you walk out the front gate of your own life."

* * *

She saw him onto the coach at first light, and then she walked up through the waking town to the registry office, where a bored official stamped her new license without ceremony, which felt exactly right. The mountains stood east of the town in the early sun, white to the summits, utterly indifferent to paperwork, and more beautiful for it.

At the trailhead noticeboard, among the legal postings — route conditions, avalanche advisories, a lost dog — she pinned a single card in her own name, her own handwriting:

*Guide available. High passes. Licensed. Spring openings.*

Below it, someone had already chalked a small mark — the old sign, the one the undocumented used, meaning *this one is safe.* She looked at it a long time. Then she left it there, because some clients she would always recognize, even now.

She turned east toward the range, and the snow squeaked under her boots, and the morning was cold and clean and hers.
