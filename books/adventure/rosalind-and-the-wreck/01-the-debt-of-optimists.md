## 1. The Debt of Optimists

The storm came off the Gulf like something with a grudge, and Quentin Eriksen drove straight into the middle of it because that was where the money was.

Not much money. Enough. A weather service contractor in Anchorage paid four hundred dollars per verified mesonet deployment, and a supercell tracking northeast across the Kenai Peninsula at thirty-eight knots meant six stations, six deployments, twenty-four hundred dollars before the light died. Against the eleven thousand he owed the Vane brothers — eleven thousand plus the "administrative interest" that grew like kudzu, compounding weekly in a ledger Maren Vane kept in a leather notebook she never let him see — it was a rounding error. But it was a rounding error he could control, and control was the only currency Quentin had never gone broke spending.

He had the storm on radar and in his teeth. The windshield wipers had given up an hour ago, surrendering in a squeal of burnt rubber, so he drove with his head out the cracked window when he needed to see, cold rain needling his cheek, the world beyond the glass a gray-green smear of spruce and drowned gravel road. The truck — a 2004 Dodge with a rebuilt transmission, a cracked block heater, and a paint job the color of old bruises — handled like a barge with opinions. He loved it the way you love a thing that has never once loved you back.

"Come on, girl," he said, downshifting through a washout. "Six more miles."

The Dodge did not answer, but it did not die, either, which in Quentin's experience was as close to affection as machinery came.

The supercell had organized over Cook Inlet around noon, feeding on air so warm and wet for October that the old-timers on the peninsula were calling it unnatural. By two it had a wall cloud you could hang laundry on. By three, spotters north of Homer were reporting rotation so clean it looked drawn with a compass. Quentin had watched the velocity couplets tighten on his laptop's battered radar feed and felt the old lift in his chest, the one thing in his life that had never asked him for collateral: that clean, cold certainty that he could read the sky better than the sky could read him.

He was, everyone agreed, an optimist. Maren Vane said it like a diagnosis.

The road turned to washboard where the gravel gave out, and the Dodge's left front tire dropped into a pothole deep enough to have its own tide chart. The whole chassis slammed, the laptop slid off the passenger seat, and Quentin caught it one-handed against the dashboard without slowing down, because slowing down cost light, and light was the thing he was running out of. October on the Kenai gave you maybe five usable hours, and the storm was eating them.

Station four was a white fiberglass enclosure on a galvanized mast, bolted to a bench of rock above the tree line with a view of the whole gray throat of the fjord country to the east. Quentin had installed it himself two springs ago, back when he still told himself the weather service work would dig him out. He backed the truck up the service track, wheels spinning in the greasy mud, and got out into wind that hit him like a door thrown open in a house fire.

The air tasted of ozone and glacier. Above him, the cloud base had lowered until it seemed to drag along the ridgelines, and the whole sky was turning the greenish yellow that meant hail, and the hail meant the updraft was strengthening, and the strengthening updraft meant —

It meant everything was exactly on schedule, which was the last time that day anything would be.

He was halfway up the mast with the calibration kit when he saw the funnel.

Not a tornado. Not yet. But a lowering, a ragged gray tongue reaching down out of the wall cloud two miles to his northwest, rotating with a laziness that lied. Quentin had chased for eleven years across four states and two coastlines, and he knew the difference between a scud cloud and a promise. This was a promise. It was also two miles off, and station four needed eight minutes of work, and the math was simple: eight minutes was nothing. Eight minutes was a rounding error.

He stayed on the mast.

Later — in the weeks that followed, in the long nights when he lay awake doing the arithmetic of his ruin — he would come back to this moment again and again. Not the crash. The crash was physics. This was the decision. The eight minutes he stayed on the mast because the deployment was worth four hundred dollars and because he believed, with the whole stubborn engine of his heart, that he was faster than the weather, smarter than the road, better than the odds that had been grinding him down since the day he'd signed the first paper Maren Vane slid across a bar table with a ballpoint pen and a smile.

The funnel touched down at 4:41 in the afternoon, and Quentin was still on the mast when the pressure dropped so fast his ears popped like champagne.

He got down. He got to the truck. He even got it turned around, which was the last competent thing the day asked of him. The tornado was crossing the valley to the northwest, chewing a line through the spruce — he could see the debris flash, a brown halo of shredded timber lit by the green light under the cloud — and it was moving away, and if he simply sat still for ten minutes he would be fine.

But the road home ran northwest.

He told himself he'd wait it out at the station. He told himself a lot of things. What he actually did was drive, because the light was going and because the contractor's bonus structure paid double for same-day verification of all six stations, and because optimism is a debt that always comes due at the worst possible interest rate.

The tornado crossed the road a mile and a half ahead of him. He saw it happen — saw the funnel lift, thin to a rope, and then, in the way of storms that have not finished with you, reorganize behind a curtain of rain and drop again without warning, without the courtesy of a visible funnel, into the cut of the road he was already committed to.

The wind hit the Dodge broadside at what he'd later estimate was a hundred and ten miles an hour.

The truck went light. For one strange, floating second Quentin was not driving at all but flying, the wheel loose in his hands, the gravel falling away beneath him — and then the world came back all at once. The Dodge left the road, took the ditch at forty, went airborne over the culvert, and came down nose-first in a flooded borrow pit with a sound like the end of an argument.

Quentin came to with rain in his face and the driver's door standing open and the windshield gone entirely, and the storm walking away to the northeast, satisfied, taking its green light with it. Water was rising in the footwell. The engine ticked. Somewhere under the hood, something that had been whole for eleven years and two hundred thousand miles was finished.

He sat in the flooded cab until the cold reached his bones, and then he climbed out and stood in the mud and looked at the last thing the Vanes didn't own.

* * *

The Dodge was a total loss by any measure that mattered. He knew it before the tow truck came, before the state trooper took his statement with the flat sympathy of a man who had written this exact report nine times that autumn. The storm had dropped four tornadoes on the peninsula that afternoon, injured three people, and flattened a church camp, and Quentin's truck was a footnote. He sat in the trooper's warm Suburban while the wrecker winched the Dodge out of the pit, and he watched it swing on the cable, streaming water, one wheel turning slowly like a dying insect, and he understood that the number in Maren Vane's notebook had just stopped being survivable.

Eleven thousand four hundred in principal. Administrative interest, compounding. The truck, which the Vanes held paper on, worth perhaps six. Which meant the difference — the part of him they didn't yet own — was now a number with no asset behind it at all.

The Vanes did not send letters. The Vanes sent Maren.

She came to Seldovia on the evening ferry two days later, and Quentin was in the harbor bar when she walked in, because he had known she would come and because he had decided that if a man is going to be repossessed, he should at least be sitting down for it. She was forty-something and built like the boats her brothers ran, all working steel and no ornament, with gray coming in at her temples that she never bothered to color because it did the intimidating for her. She took the stool beside him without being asked and ordered what he was having.

"Quentin."

"Maren."

"Bad weather last week."

"Good chasing."

She smiled at her glass. "The truck's at the yard in Homer. My brothers had a look at it. Sixty-two hundred, retail, if it were running." She let that sit. "It isn't running."

"No."

"So." She turned on her stool to face him, and up close her eyes were the pale gray of winter water, and they did not blink enough. "You owe us eleven-four. The truck covers sixty-two. That leaves five-two, and nothing to cover it with, and you know how Aldous feels about unsecured debt."

Aldous Vane ran the books and the fleet and, in the way of men who never raise their voices, ran most of the western gulf. Errol Vane ran the things Aldous didn't want to touch. Quentin had met Aldous exactly once, in an office that smelled of diesel and old paper, and had signed things.

"I'll have work in the spring," Quentin said. "The contractor —"

"The contractor called us in September, Quentin. Before you did." Maren's voice was gentle, which was worse. "You've been borrowing against spring for two years. Spring's a nice place. You should visit it sometime."

The bar noise went on around them — the ferry crowd, the fishermen, a television muttering about the unusual October warmth — and Quentin sat in the middle of it feeling the particular cold that comes not from fear but from the end of a story you'd been telling yourself. He had always believed he was smarter than the sea. Smarter than the market, smarter than the interest rate, smarter than the storm. It was the family disease. It had killed his mother at thirty-one, according to his father, who'd died of it too, slower, in a chair in front of a television in a house the bank took in '09.

"What happens to unsecured debt," Quentin said. "With Aldous."

Maren finished her drink and set the glass down with the care of a woman who never broke anything she might need later.

"Aldous has a proposition," she said. "Actually, it's Errol's. But Aldous likes it."

"I'm listening."

"Are you? Because last time you were listening, you heard what you wanted to hear, and now here we are." She signaled the bartender for two more, and when they came she pushed one in front of him and didn't touch her own. "You know the Halvard."

It wasn't a question. Everyone on the peninsula knew the Halvard, the way everyone knew the name of a dead relative. Supply ship, 1911, out of Seldovia into the fjord country with the season's payroll for the mining camps — a payroll in gold and bearer notes, by every account, meant for men who'd been on strike since August and hadn't been paid since June. The Halvard never made the camps. She went up the fjord ahead of a storm and never came down, and when the ice went out the next spring, no wreck, no boat, no bodies, no payroll. The company stopped asking. The miners stayed on strike through the winter on nothing, and half of them left for the Interior in the spring, and the whole thing curdled into the kind of story towns keep in a jar: the fjord that swallows the greedy, the cave that opens at the lowest tides and takes what it's owed.

"Nobody's found her in a hundred years," Quentin said.

"Nobody's found her," Maren agreed, "because nobody's looked with sonar and a budget. The route up the fjord — you know it's opening."

"I know it's ice."

"It's ice until the third week of October. Then the meltwater channel runs clean for a few weeks before the calving season seals it. My brothers have three boats that could be up there inside a week. What they don't have is a man who knows the water, knows the weather, and knows the fjord, and isn't on anyone's crew list." She finally picked up her glass. "You find the Halvard. You give my brothers the location and first salvage rights, documented, notarized, clean. They do the rest. And the five-two goes away. The whole eleven-four goes away. You walk out of this bar owing nothing to anybody."

Quentin looked at his drink. The harbor lights moved on the surface of it, orange and unsteady, and he thought about the Dodge going down into the borrow pit, that one floating second where he'd been neither driving nor flying, and how he'd sworn, hanging there, that he would never again let a machine or a man or a number own him.

"And if I don't find her?"

"Then the debt's still there, and there's no truck to hold it." Maren said it the way she'd say the tide was coming in. "Aldous doesn't like unsecured debt, Quentin. But he's a reasonable man. He'd accept other security."

"Other security meaning what?"

"Meaning you don't want to find out." She stood, took a folded paper from inside her coat, and laid it on the bar between them, next to his glass. "It's a simple agreement. Location of the wreck, exclusive to Vane Marine Salvage, in exchange for satisfaction of all outstanding obligations. Sixty days to perform." She put a pen on top of it. "You're the smartest man on this peninsula, they tell me. Smarter than the sea, even. Here's a sea that's been sitting still for a hundred and thirteen years, waiting for somebody smart."

She left the paper and the pen and walked out into the rain without hurrying, and Quentin sat looking at the agreement the way a drowning man looks at a rope that someone else is holding.

The bartender drifted down. "You want another?"

"I want a chart of the eastern fjords."

"We're a bar, not a chandlery."

"Then another's fine."

He drank it slow. Around him the ferry crowd thinned, the television moved on to a story about glacier melt in the eastern range — record low ice, the anchor said, unprecedented exposure along the fjord walls — and Quentin read the agreement twice, front and back, looking for the hook. There was always a hook. The hook was in the sixty days, probably, or in the phrase *exclusive to Vane Marine Salvage*, or simply in the fact that Maren Vane had smiled at him twice in one evening, which in his experience preceded every disaster he'd ever survived.

But here was the thing, the thing he turned over and over as the bar emptied and the rain kept on and the harbor lights swung: the fjord was real. The legend was real enough that three generations of salvagers had gone broke looking, which meant the payroll was real, which meant the wreck was real, because gold doesn't evaporate and ships don't vanish, they just get lost by people who aren't smart enough to find them.

And he was smart enough. That was the whole of it, the ugly, load-bearing truth of Quentin Eriksen at thirty-four: he believed it. Standing in the wreckage of his optimism with the last of his assets on a hook in Homer, he believed that he could read the water the way he read the sky, that the fjord would open to him the way storms opened — grudgingly, dangerously, but open — and that he would walk out of this debt the way he'd walked out of a hundred storms, wet and grinning and ahead.

He signed.

The pen scratched. The paper took his name the way the mud had taken the Dodge, easily, completely, without any intention of giving it back.

* * *

He was still at the bar at closing, doing the only thing he knew how to do with a bad decision, which was plan around it — tide tables, the meltwater channel, the six-week window the ice would allow — when the bartender nodded at the door and said, "Friend of yours?"

A woman had come in out of the rain, shaking it off her shoulders like a bird. She was somewhere in her early thirties, dark-haired, with a canvas bag slung across her body and the look of someone who had been traveling a long time and had gotten very good at not showing it. She scanned the bar — the bartender, the empty stools, the last three fishermen arguing about herring — and then she found Quentin, and she stopped.

She looked at him the way you look at a photograph you've studied so long that meeting the real thing is a kind of shock.

"You're Quentin Eriksen," she said. Not a question either. That seemed to be the kind of night it was.

"Depends who's asking."

"Rosalind." She came toward him, and up close there was something in her face that made the back of his neck prickle, some architecture of cheek and brow that he'd seen every morning of his life in the mirror. "Rosalind Eriksen. I'm — " She stopped, and he watched her decide, in real time, how much truth the moment could hold. "I think you're my brother."

The rain ran off her coat onto the floorboards. Behind the bar, the television was still muttering about the glaciers, about what the melt was uncovering, all along the coast, that hadn't seen daylight in a century. Quentin sat very still with a signed contract in his jacket pocket and a stranger's face wearing his mother's jaw, and he heard himself say:

"Okay. Start from the beginning."

She set her canvas bag on the bar. "It starts with a map," she said, "in our mother's hand."
