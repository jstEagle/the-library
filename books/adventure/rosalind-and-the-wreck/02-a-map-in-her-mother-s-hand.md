## 2. A Map in Her Mother's Hand

The ferry into Seldovia ran late because the weather was turning, and Rosalind Eriksen spent the crossing pretending she hadn't noticed.

She stood at the rail with her back to the wind, watching Kachemak Bay go from pewter to slate to something darker, a color that had no name in English and probably never would. The water-stained chart was inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat. She had carried it that way for eleven days — through Seattle, through Anchorage, through the long white drive down the peninsula where the mountains came down to meet the road and seemed to lean over her, reading her intentions.

*You'll know him when you see him,* her mother had said, near the end, when the morphine made her honest. *He looks like our father did at thirty. All jaw and bad decisions.*

Rosalind had asked why she'd never told him. Her mother had looked out the hospital window at a parking lot and said, *Because I was a coward with good handwriting,* and then she'd slept, and three weeks later she was gone, and all she'd left behind was a name — Quentin Eriksen, Seldovia, Alaska — and a chart of a fjord that didn't appear on any modern map.

Now the ferry swung wide around the spit, and Seldovia assembled itself out of the murk: a crescent of gray shingle beach, boardwalks on pilings, houses stacked up the hillside like driftwood caught in the trees. Fishing boats rode at their moorings with the patient hunch of animals waiting out a storm. Somewhere beyond the headland, hidden by rain, there was a fjord, and in that fjord, according to a woman who had lied to everyone she loved for fifty years, there was a ship.

Rosalind shouldered her duffel and went looking for her brother.

She found him, as it happened, without looking — which felt appropriate, given everything else about this arrangement. He was at the harbor bar, the one built on stilts over the water with a wood stove going and a smell of diesel and fryer grease that she suspected was the closest thing the town had to perfume. He sat alone at the end of the counter with his back to the door, which told her one of two things: either he was a man with no enemies, or he was a man who had stopped checking.

Given what she knew about the Vanes, she doubted it was the first.

He was talking to the bartender in a low, easy voice, laughing at something, and even before he turned she could see it — the jaw, the set of the shoulders, the way he held his glass like an argument he intended to win. Their grandfather's face. Their mother's stubbornness, worn openly instead of buried. It was like looking at a photograph of a stranger who had been standing behind her all her life.

Rosalind took a stool at the far end, ordered a beer she didn't want, and watched him in the mirror behind the bottles.

*Brother or obstacle.* That was the question she'd been carrying since Anchorage, turning it over like a stone, looking for what lived underneath. Because here was the truth she admitted to no one: she hadn't come for sentiment. Sentiment was a luxury for people whose debts were paid. She had come because the chart was worth money — real money, the kind that could buy back the boat the bank had taken, the kind that could make the last two years of her life un-happen — and Quentin Eriksen, whoever he was, was either a partner or a problem, and she had exactly enough time to find out which.

And then Maren Vane's voice came back to her, cool as bilge water: *Find out what he knows. Report anything that matters. Do this, and your paper goes away.*

Rosalind drank her beer and hated herself in the small, efficient way she'd gotten good at.

* * *

The storm hit at dusk, and with it, Quentin's luck.

She saw it happen in pieces, through the bar's salt-fogged windows. First his truck — a battered white Ford with a lightning-science logo stenciled on the door — pulling out of the lot at speed, chasing the black wall of cloud coming off the bay like a man chasing a debt collector. Then the radio chatter, half the bar leaning in as someone reported a supercell rotating over the peninsula, the kind of thing that wasn't supposed to happen this far north, the kind of thing men like Quentin drove toward on purpose.

Then, forty minutes later, the phone call that made the bartender wince, and Quentin walking back in soaked to the sternum, carrying nothing but himself and a look on his face that Rosalind recognized from mirrors: the expression of a man doing arithmetic he already knows comes out wrong.

"Truck's totaled," he said to the bartender, not quite steadily. "Went off the gravel past Foxstone Creek. Hydroplaned." A laugh with no floor under it. "Rolled it four times. Walked away."

"That's the third vehicle," the bartender said.

"I'm aware."

"You know what they'll say."

"I said I'm aware." He dropped onto his stool and put his face in his hands, and for a moment — just a moment — the swagger drained out of him and left something younger, something that looked, unbearably, like a boy Rosalind had invented once, in a childhood spent inventing him. A brother who would have been there. Who would have known her. Who might have made the years less narrow.

Then he lifted his head, bought a round for the room like a man paying tribute, and the swagger came back on like floodlights, and Rosalind understood that she was looking at armor, not a man. The armor was the point. Underneath, presumably, was whatever the Vanes were about to strip bare.

She finished her beer. She told herself the tightness in her chest was indigestion.

* * *

They came for him at ten o'clock.

Rosalind saw the truck first — a black Suburban with commercial plates, rolling down the boardwalk road slow as a shark through chum. Two people got out. The driver stayed put, engine running, headlights washing the harbor gold. But the passenger — a tall woman in a rain shell the color of wet slate, hair cropped short, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who has never once needed to run — walked straight into the bar as if she owned the tide itself.

Maren Vane. Rosalind knew her only from photographs and one brief, chilling meeting in a Seattle office that smelled of cedar and old money. In person she was worse. In person she smiled.

"Eriksen." Maren settled onto the stool beside Quentin like a cat claiming a warm hood. "Heard you lost another truck."

"Maren." He didn't look at her. "Come to offer condolences?"

"Came to talk numbers." She signaled the bartender for water, which somehow made it worse. "Your outstanding balance is two hundred and eleven thousand. Your assets are—" she paused, savoring it, "—a duffel bag and a reputation. So we're at a decision point."

"It's a salvage claim. The *Kestrel* went down in legal waters—"

"The *Kestrel* went down in *our* waters, because you signed papers saying so when you needed the equipment." Maren's voice never rose. That was the thing about her; Rosalind had noted it even in Seattle. She spoke the way winter does — quietly, and everywhere at once. "But let's skip ahead. There's a ship called the *Halvard*. Supply vessel, lost in 1911, up a fjord past the glacier. Carrying payroll for the mining company. You've heard the stories. Everyone on this coast has heard the stories."

Quentin had gone very still. "That's a ghost story."

"Ghost stories are just history nobody's monetized yet." Maren turned her water glass a quarter-turn, precise as a lock. "There's a tidal event coming. Unprecedented low series, glaciologist's dream, six cycles before the calving seals the route. We have a fleet. What we don't have is a location — because the fjord's got more channels than a liar, and every survey since 1911 has come home empty." She finally looked at him, and her smile was almost kind, which was the cruelest thing about it. "Bring me the wreck, Quentin. Or bring me everything you own, which as of tonight is a duffel bag."

"And if I say no?"

"Then tomorrow morning we start with the boat you're living on, and work our way down to your boots." She stood, smoothed her shell, and paused. "Oh — and Quentin. Ask around about your family while you're deciding. You might be surprised who turns up."

Her eyes swept the bar once — casual, cataloguing, a lighthouse beam in miniature — and passed over Rosalind without stopping. Then she was gone, and the Suburban swallowed her, and the bar exhaled.

Rosalind stared into her glass and felt the chart against her chest like a brand.

*Ask around about your family.* Either Maren didn't know Rosalind was already here — or Maren knew exactly, and wanted Quentin primed to trust the sister who was about to walk in out of the rain. Both possibilities were terrible. Both were useful.

She was starting to learn how the Vanes worked. Everything was both.

* * *

She waited until closing. Waited until the bartender chased the last gillnetters out and Quentin sat alone at the end of the counter, staring at a whiskey he hadn't touched, wearing the specific stillness of a man calculating whether drowning now or later makes any difference.

Rosalind picked up her beer, crossed the room, and set it down beside his glass.

"Buy you a drink," she said. "Since you're having such a good week."

He looked up. And there it was — the double take, the narrowing, the slow, unwilling recognition of a face he'd never seen but somehow knew, the way you know your own hands. She watched him arrive at it in stages: confusion, suspicion, and underneath both, sudden and naked, hope. Hope was the part that hurt.

"Do I—" He stopped. Started again. "You look like—"

"Our grandfather. I know." She slid onto the next stool. "I'm told I also argue like him, but I'll need witnesses for that."

"Who are you?"

"Rosalind." She let it sit there between them, the whole name, the whole weight of it. "Rosalind Eriksen. My mother was Lillian Marsh. Born Seldovia, 1954. Left in '71 and never came back." She watched his face do the math. "Your mother too, Quentin. Same woman. Which makes us—"

"No." Quietly. Not denial so much as vertigo. "My mother died when I was six. Car accident. My dad never talked about her. There was no—"

"There was another life before yours. Another state. Another child." Rosalind kept her voice level, kept it gentle, the way you keep a hand flat near a strange dog. "Me. I got her for forty-one years, and she never once mentioned you until the end, and then she couldn't stop. So believe me when I say I understand that this is a lot."

He was staring at her like a man staring at a hole in the world where a wall used to be. "Why now? Why not a letter? Why not—"

"Because letters can be ignored." She reached inside her coat, slowly, and drew out the oilcloth bundle. Set it on the bar between them. "And because I brought you this."

He unwrapped it with the care of a man defusing something. Inside, the chart lay yellowed and soft as old skin, its linen backing cracked, its ink faded to the brown of dried blood. Water stains bloomed across it in pale continents. Someone had drawn the fjord by hand — the headland, the glacier's snout, the deep notch of water running inland — and marked along its eastern shore, in faded pencil, a symbol like a fishhook. Beneath it, in her mother's upright, careful hand:

*Where the sea gives back what it takes. Only at the lowest of lows. Bring nothing you cannot lose.*

Quentin read it twice. His fingers hovered over the pencil mark without touching it, the way you hover over a sleeping animal.

"This is the Halvard fjord," he said. His voice had gone rough. "This is — how did she have this? How did she *have* this?"

"I don't know." The lie came out smooth as ballast. Rosalind had rehearsed it for eleven days, and it still tasted like copper. "There are things in her papers I haven't sorted through yet. Letters. Photographs. Whatever she was, she wasn't just somebody's wife in Tacoma."

"She knew." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and shining. "She knew where it was. This whole time. And she never—"

"She had her reasons." *I was a coward with good handwriting.* "People always do. They're just usually worse reasons than they think."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The stove ticked. Out in the harbor, rigging clinked against masts like a room full of restless clocks. Rosalind watched her brother — her brother, the word still foreign in her mouth, still sharp-edged — watch the chart, and she saw the exact moment the arithmetic changed for him. Saw the debt and the deadline and the drowned ship rearrange themselves into a single shape. A way out. Maybe the only one.

She also saw what it cost him to look up at her afterward and say, "The Vanes will kill for this. You understand that. If they think you have it—"

"They won't hear it from me."

"Everyone says that." He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, exhausted and grateful at once. "Everyone always says that right up until they say otherwise."

"Well." Rosalind raised her glass. "Here's to saying otherwise, then. May we both live long enough to regret it."

He laughed — a short, surprised bark, the first true thing she'd heard out of him — and clinked his untouched whiskey against her bottle, and for one unguarded second they were just two strangers at a bar who happened to share a face, and it was almost enough to make her forget the black Suburban, and the cool voice, and the number saved in her phone under a false name that she was supposed to call tonight.

Almost.

* * *

She walked out into the rain after midnight and stood on the boardwalk until his light went out across the harbor, on the old boat he apparently lived aboard, a lamp burning in a porthole like a single held breath.

Then she took out her phone. Stood over the dark water. Typed the message with her thumbs, quick and businesslike, before the guilt could get a word in edgewise:

*Confirmed contact. He has the chart. Location matches the fjord. Will report.*

Send.

The phone buzzed back in under a minute, the reply arriving cold and prompt as tide tables:

*Good. Stay close. The fleet sails in five days.*

Rosalind pocketed the phone and looked out at the harbor — at her brother's lamp, at the black water, at the hills where somewhere behind the rain a glacier sat waiting with its hundred-year secret.

*Brother or obstacle.* She'd thought it was a choice. Standing there in the rain, she understood it was actually a test, and she had no idea yet whether she was going to pass it or fail it, or which answer the Vanes would consider passing.

Five days. Six tides. One map in her mother's hand, and one lie sitting in her chest like swallowed ballast.

She pulled up her collar and went to find a room, and behind her the harbor kept ticking and clinking in the dark, counting down.
