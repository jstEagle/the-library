## 3. Six Tides

The glaciologist's office smelled like burnt coffee and old instruments, the way it had smelled when we were both twenty-three and broke and certain the world would reward curiosity. Dr. Ingrid Halvorsen — no relation to the ship, though she'd checked twice, hoping — had traded storm chasing for ice cores and a corner office at the university annex in Anchorage with a window that looked out on a parking lot.

She hadn't looked up when I came in. She'd just said, "You look like a man who totaled his truck."

"News travels."

"Maren Vane called me an hour ago asking about fjord bathymetry in Prince William Sound." She finally raised her eyes. "When Maren Vane calls me for science instead of money, somebody's about to have a very bad month. I assumed it was you."

I dropped into the chair across from her desk. My ribs still ached from the rollover, three days healed and lying about it. Outside, October was doing its gray arithmetic on the city — rain that couldn't decide whether to be snow, light that gave up by mid-afternoon.

"I need to know about the fjord," I said. "The one where the *Halvard* went down. Kachemak side, past the glacier head. Whether there's a way in before winter."

Ingrid leaned back, and something in her face shifted from colleague to friend to something more careful than either. "Quentin. What did you sign?"

"A contract."

"Vane paper?"

I didn't answer, which was an answer. She exhaled through her nose, stood, and crossed to the wall of charts behind her desk — real paper ones, because Ingrid trusted ink more than satellites. She pulled down a survey map of the southern Kenai fjords, yellowed at the folds, annotated in her own cramped hand across a decade of field seasons.

"All right," she said. "Lesson first. Then you'll understand why my answer is going to ruin your week."

* * *

She spread the chart flat and weighted its corners with coffee mugs and a fossil ammonite she kept for exactly this purpose.

"The fjord is fed by the Sermilik Glacier — call it that; the locals have their own name and they're right not to share it. The glacier has been retreating for eighty years, but it calves on a cycle nobody's ever fully modeled because the meltwater channel at its face is the only deep-water approach to the upper basin. Everything else is shallows and moraine. A supply ship in 1911 could make that run. A fishing skiff today can, barely, in the right conditions. Anything bigger than thirty feet, no."

"And the conditions?"

"That's the lesson." Her finger traced the channel — a blue thread no wider than a river, threading between the glacier's face and a shoal bar marked in warning red. "The glacier calves when the tide drops. Not rises — drops. The ice is grounded on a sill at the channel mouth. At high water, the buoyancy supports the front face and it holds. When the tide falls six, seven feet, the support goes away, stress builds along the fracture lines, and she lets go. Big calvings happen on the ebb, almost always within two hours of low slack."

"So you run the channel at flood."

"You run the channel at flood or you don't run it at all, and even then you're gambling, because every calving event dumps ice into the channel and the current carries it back toward the bar. Some of those bergs are the size of houses and they don't melt, they ground out and sit there like mines." She tapped the shoal. "But here's what matters. Every autumn — late September, early October — the glacier does something different. The melt season ends, the ice stiffens, and the whole face re-advances. It builds a new front out over the sill until the channel is choked. Sealed. No boat gets through until the next melt season opens it again in June. Seven months, minimum."

"When does it seal this year?"

Ingrid looked at me for a long moment. Then she turned to her laptop, woke it, and pulled up a data feed I didn't pretend to understand — strain gauges, satellite radar interferometry, a graph of millimeters that meant nothing to me and everything to her.

"I've got four monitoring stations on that glacier. Two are mine, two belong to a Norwegian consortium that pays better than the state does." She scrolled. "The re-advance started nine days ago. The face has moved eleven meters into the channel since then. Based on the acceleration curve and the forecast cold snap coming down off the range—" she stopped scrolling, and her jaw tightened— "the channel closes in six tidal cycles. Six ebbs, Quentin. After the sixth low water, the ice gate shuts and stays shut until June."

I did the math without wanting to. "Six tides is three days."

"Three days, if you count them the way the sea counts them. And the Vanes' fleet — how many days out?"

"Five. They're staging out of Whittier. Maren told me five days, maybe less if the weather holds."

"Then the fleet arrives two days after the door closes." Ingrid said it flatly, the way she'd say *the core sample is contaminated*. "Whoever reaches that basin does it inside seventy-two hours, alone, in a boat small enough to thread the channel between calving events. Or nobody reaches it at all until summer, and whatever the Vanes think is down there waits under forty feet of new ice for eight months while lawyers pick apart their claim."

I sat with that. The office hummed around us — the radiator, the laptop fan, the rain giving up at last against the window and turning to sleet.

"There's another way in," I said. "Isn't there? There's always another way in."

Ingrid's expression changed. Not softer — warier. She glanced at the door of her office as though the walls had ears, which, given who'd already called her once today, wasn't paranoia so much as prudence.

"Sit down properly," she said. "And Quentin — after I tell you this, you were never here."

* * *

She cleared the survey chart away and unrolled something older from the bottom drawer of her filing cabinet: a hand-drawn hydrographic sketch, browned with age, the ink faded to the color of weak tea. No title block. No surveyor's stamp. Just depth soundings in a spidery nineteenth-century hand and a coastline drawn with the loving precision of someone who'd measured it by rowboat.

"This came out of the university archives in 2009, when they digitized the old Alaska Commercial Company papers. It's attributed to a Tlingit guide working for a fur trader out of Seldovia, sometime in the 1880s. Look at the upper basin."

I looked. The fjord narrowed to its head, the glacier shown as a hatched mass — but the glacier on this drawing reached only halfway to where the modern charts showed it. Behind it, upstream, the sketch showed a second water body, a long drowned valley running back into the mountains, connected to the main fjord by a narrow passage. And cutting through the basalt shoulder of the north cliff, drawn in cross-section with almost reverent care, was a feature labeled in the guide's hand — transliterated, Ingrid had penciled beneath it:

*THE SEA'S THROAT.*

"A cave," I said.

"A sea cave, cut through the basalt ridge that separates the outer fjord from the inner basin. The guide drew it because his people used it — at extreme low tides, the cave floor drains and you can walk partway through. At high water, the tide floods it completely. The legend attached to it is older than the fur trade." Ingrid's voice had gone quiet, the register she used for things she respected. "The story goes that the cave swallows the greedy. That men who went in for what the sea held took what they carried and never came out, and the ones who went in empty-handed walked through to the other side. The village elders used it as a boundary marker — beyond the Sea's Throat, you don't take, you only look."

"Is it real? Geologically?"

"Oh, it's real. Basalt coastal caves in that formation run sixty, eighty feet deep easy, and the ridge there is narrow — the sketch shows the passage spanning it. But whether it's passable, whether it connects water to water, whether it isn't collapsed after a century of freeze-thaw — nobody's surveyed it. It's on no chart. The 1911 pilot books don't mention it. Which means whoever wrote the *Halvard*'s route plan never knew it existed." She met my eyes. "Or the man who planned that route heard the same legend I just told you and chose the glacier channel anyway, because he didn't believe the sea keeps accounts."

I stared at the faded line of the cave, threading through solid rock like a secret kept for a hundred and forty years.

"If the cave connects," I said slowly, "a man could take a skiff up the outer fjord at high water, anchor below the cliff, wait for the low, and walk or wade through to the inner basin. Skip the glacier channel entirely. Skip the ice gate."

"If the cave connects. If it isn't silted. If the inner exit isn't ten feet underwater at all tides. If the tide tables cooperate — and Quentin, the lowest tide of the season, the big minus tide, is day after tomorrow. After that, the lows get shallower every cycle until the equinoctial series is done." She began rolling the sketch back up, and her hands were steady but her voice wasn't quite. "Six tides until the glacier seals the channel. One true low tide left worth the name. You do that arithmetic."

I did it. It came out ugly.

Day after tomorrow: the season's lowest tide, the one window when the Sea's Throat might stand open. Three days after that: the last of the six cycles, the final ebb before the ice gate closed forever. And somewhere behind all of it, closing fast, the Vane fleet with five days of water under its keel and Maren Vane's signature waiting at the end of the run like a hook.

Time wasn't just short. Time was the antagonist now — patient, indifferent, counting down in a language older than debt. The glacier didn't care about contracts. The tide didn't care about the Vanes. The sea had been keeping its own ledger for ten thousand years, and it balanced every account eventually, and it did not accept payment in promises.

"Ingrid," I said. "Why are you helping me?"

She set the sketch back in the drawer and closed it gently, the way you close a coffin.

"Because in 1911," she said, "my great-grandfather was one of the strikers at the cannery camps. He never got paid. The company said the payroll ship sank, and everyone starved quietly, and the company's owners got rich, and my family ate dandelion greens for two winters." She picked up her coffee, found it cold, drank it anyway. "If the *Halvard*'s down there, I've wanted someone to find her for thirty years. I just never thought it would be a man working for the Vanes."

The words landed like a sounding lead hitting bottom.

"I haven't decided what I'm working for," I said.

"Decide fast." She walked me to the door, and in the glass of the corridor window I caught our reflection — her, rumpled and resolute; me, bruised and borrowed-out, wearing a coat I'd soon owe to somebody. "Six tides, Quentin. And the first one's tomorrow night."

* * *

I walked out into the sleet and stood on the annex steps with the sketch's image burned behind my eyes. The Sea's Throat. A passage through stone that legend said swallowed the greedy and passed the empty-handed. A payroll that had never been paid. A window of water narrowing by the hour.

My phone buzzed. Maren Vane.

*"Fleet moved up,"* she said, no greeting, wind noise behind her voice like the sea clearing its throat. *"We sail in four days now. Weather's cooperating. Have something worth our time when we arrive, Eriksen, or the conversation changes."*

Four days. Not five. The arithmetic shifted under me like scree.

I watched the sleet turn to snow over the parking lot and thought about a cave that swallowed the greedy, and a sister I'd learned about two days ago sitting somewhere in Seldovia with half our mother's map, and a glacier counting down in the dark where no one could hear it.

Six tides. Maybe fewer.

I turned south, toward the water, and started walking.
