## 4. What the Tide Gave Back

The tide chart on the wall of the Seldovia harbormaster's office had a red line drawn through the fourth of June, and beneath it, in a hand that belonged to someone who did not believe in luck, a single word: *minus 5.8*.

Rosalind stood before it with coffee going cold in her hand and understood, finally, why Maren Vane had smiled at her across the dock two nights ago. Not the smile of a woman making a threat. The smile of a woman who had done her arithmetic and found it pleasing.

"Five point eight below mean low water," the harbormaster said behind her. He was seventy if he was a day, with hands like braided rope and the patient air of a man who had watched a thousand strangers misread his charts. "Happens maybe once a season now. Used to be more common, my father said. The water's been coming up for years." He tapped the red line with one yellowed nail. "You want to see something nobody's seen since their grandfathers were young men, that's your morning. Six-forty-one. And you'll want to be off the flats by nine, or the sea will take back what it lent you and charge interest."

"What's out there?" Rosalind asked. "Under the cliffs."

The old man looked at her for a long moment, the way people in small towns look at outsiders when they're deciding how much of the truth the outsider can carry without spilling.

"Basalt," he said. "And whatever the basalt has been keeping."

* * *

They left the harbor at five, Quentin running the borrowed skiff with the particular economy of a man who had nothing left to break. He hadn't spoken to her beyond what navigation required since the dock — since Maren Vane had put her hand on Rosalind's shoulder in front of God and the fishing fleet and said, *You'll go down together. Family should keep family honest.* As if she'd known Quentin longer than eleven days. As if blood were a leash instead of a thread.

Rosalind sat in the bow with their mother's chart open across her knees, weighted at the corners with two bolts and a coffee mug, and let the water speak first.

The fjord narrowed as they ran north, walls rising sheer and black on either side, streaked white where meltwater had spent centuries writing its signature down the stone. The air came off the glacier at the far end cold enough to taste — mineral and old, like licking an iron fence. Somewhere ahead, ice groaned. Not loudly. The way a house groans at night when it settles. A sound that meant the thing was awake and thinking.

"There." Quentin's voice, flat. He cut the throttle and the skiff glided into a silence so complete Rosalind could hear her own pulse.

Ahead, the basalt cliffs stepped down in terraces to a shelf of exposed seabed that glistened like something newly born — kelp lying in ropes, anemones closed tight as fists, tide pools holding the last of the night sky. And at the base of the lowest terrace, where no opening should have been, a mouth.

Black. Arched. Perhaps four meters high, dripping, its rim furred with barnacles that must have spent decades underwater and were now, this morning only, gasping in the air. Water still ran from it in threads, draining back toward the sea as though the cave itself were exhaling after a long held breath.

Rosalind had grown up on stories of the place. Every child within a hundred miles had. *The sea keeps its own books,* her grandmother used to say, and then would not say anything else about it, which was its own kind of lesson. They called it the Swallower's Door in some tellings, the Greedy Man's Window in others, and in all the tellings the moral was identical: what went in owing stayed in owing.

She had thought it was myth. Standing there, watching the tide retreat from a doorway carved by no human hand, she felt the myth stand up inside her chest and look around.

"It matches," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange against the rock. She smoothed the chart flat. Their mother had drawn this coastline in ink gone brown with saltwater — the terraces, the shelf, and here, at the base, a small arch marked with a symbol Rosalind had spent thirty years assuming was decorative. An arch with a line through it. A door, barred from the outside. Or a door, opened once.

Quentin leaned over the chart without touching it. Close enough that she could see the sleeplessness ground into him, the debt sitting on his shoulders like wet canvas. "How did she know?"

"I don't know." It was the truest sentence she'd said to him yet. "I don't know anything about her. That's the whole problem, isn't it? You didn't know she existed. I knew her and never knew *that*." She tapped the arch. "So either she stood on this shore, or someone told her who did."

Quentin said nothing. He turned the skiff toward the shelf and let the keel kiss kelp.

* * *

Walking the exposed seabed was like walking on the skin of something living.

The ground sucked at their boots — mud threaded with eelgrass, scattered with the debris of a century: a boot sole, green with age; a bottle whose glass had gone purple in the sun; the rib of some animal, or possibly of a boat; a horseshoe crab dead so long it had become architecture. Above them the basalt rose in columns, and the cave mouth grew as they approached until it stopped being a feature of the cliff and became the cliff's whole meaning.

Quentin went first. Of course he did. Rosalind noted it without warmth — the older brother reflex of a man who'd learned he had a sister three weeks ago but had apparently decided the protective part came standard.

Inside, the light died fast. They ran lines — Quentin's idea, a coil of polypropylene off the skiff, tied to a basalt horn just inside the entrance. Then headlamps on, and the dark organized itself around their beams.

The floor sloped gently upward, dry past the drip line, floored in gravel and silt that held footprints — not theirs. Old ones. Faint ones, pressed into hardened mud, boots with heel irons, the long stride of men carrying weight. Rosalind crouched over them and felt the hair rise on her arms. Footprints do not survive a hundred years of tides unless the tides had never reached them. Which meant the water had never come this far in. Which meant —

"Someone walked out," Quentin said quietly. "Or someone walked in and never walked out, and the prints are all we get to know about it."

The passage widened. And then the beams found the rigging.

It hung from a natural shelf of rock like the skeleton of a spider that had fed too well: blocks and tackles, tarred line gone grey and brittle, a deadeye the size of a dinner plate, all of it arranged — *arranged*, that was the word that made her stomach turn — coiled and hung with the care of men who expected to come back for it. Salvage gear. Ship's gear, brought ashore and stowed. On the wall beside it, driven into a seam of softer stone, iron spikes still bright under their rust.

"1911," Rosalind breathed. She touched a coil of line and it shed flakes like burnt paper. "This is ship's stores. This is — Quentin, this is a *work site*. Somebody ran the Halvard aground on purpose and unloaded her in here."

"Or somebody salvaged her after." But his voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Look at the placement. That's not panic. Panic doesn't coil line."

Beyond the rigging the passage bent, and the bend held a draft — cold, moving, smelling of ice and deep stone. The cave went on. The cave went *back*, toward the glacier, under the mountain, and their mother's chart, Rosalind realized suddenly, showed the coastline only. Whatever lay beyond the arch, their mother had drawn the door and never the room.

On the floor at the bend, half sunk in gravel, a disc of metal caught her beam.

She knelt. Brushed it clean with her thumb. It was a brass tag, the kind pinned to coats and slung around necks in the age when men were numbered so companies wouldn't have to learn their names. Stamped letters, worn but legible, and Rosalind read them twice because the first time her eyes refused:

**HALVARD CREW — No. 9**

The brass was cold. Colder than the cave had any right to make it. She held it in her palm and thought: *a man wore this. A man ate breakfast wearing this, argued about wages wearing this, and then walked into a hole in a cliff wearing this, and the hole kept him.*

"Legend says the cave swallows the greedy," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "That's the story. That's the story I got anyway. But greedy men don't leave their rigging coiled."

"No," Quentin said. He was looking down the passage, into the dark that breathed cold air, and Rosalind watched something move across his face that she recognized from mirrors — the moment awe curdles into dread. "Greedy men don't leave anything. These men *stayed*. There's a difference, and I think it's the difference that matters."

From somewhere deep in the mountain — deep enough that it might have been imagination, might have been water finding its level, might have been nothing at all — came a sound. A slow, grinding exhalation, like the glacier shifting its weight, like a great door testing its hinges.

Then, fainter, almost lost under the drip of the tide beginning its return: voices? No. Wind. Just wind, moving through passages no chart had ever drawn.

Rosalind closed her hand around the tag, No. 9 warm now from her skin, and understood with total clarity that they had not found a legend this morning. Legends were stories you could put down. This was inventory. This was evidence. This was a room where men had made choices, and the choices were still in here, waiting, patient as brass, for someone to come and read them.

Behind them, far off across the flats, the sea had begun to come home.

"We've got maybe two hours," Quentin said, and clicked his lamp toward the cold breathing dark. "We go back far enough to know what she drew us here for. Then we run."

Rosalind followed him into the mountain, the miner's tag clenched in her fist, and did not tell him what she already knew and he did not: that somewhere in her coat pocket, her phone held coordinates she had sent weeks ago to a number that paid well — and that whatever waited in this cave, she had already sold the address.
