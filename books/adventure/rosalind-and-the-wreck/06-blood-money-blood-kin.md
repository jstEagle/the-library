## 6. Blood Money, Blood Kin

Rosalind waited until Quentin's headlamp swung away down the passage before she took out the phone.

The cave gave her the cover she needed. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with a patience older than either of them, and the tide pools at her feet held a light that wasn't quite light — the bioluminescence the locals called *dead-man's milk*, glowing faint blue-green in the hollows of the rock. Beautiful, if you didn't think about what fed it. She'd been thinking about what fed things her whole life.

The ledger lay open on the flat stone where they'd found it, pages fanned to dry under Quentin's insistence. He'd wanted to photograph everything himself, catalog it like evidence for some court that existed only in his imagination — as if a debt contract could be argued with, as if men like the Vanes read confessions instead of burning them. So he was deeper in now, mapping the inner chamber by memory and headlamp, calling out distances to himself in a voice gone soft with wonder. He hadn't sounded like that since he was nine years old and showing her a tornado on the horizon through a truck window, both of them pressed to the glass while their father gripped the wheel and said nothing at all.

She lifted the ledger's last page and set her phone above it.

The camera clicked — a small sound, swallowed whole by the cave. She shot each page twice, adjusting the exposure against the damp, angling the screen so its glow fell away from the passage mouth. The paymaster's handwriting crawled across the images: dates, sums, the word *withheld* repeated until it stopped looking like accounting and started looking like theft in progress. Forty-one names. Forty-one men who'd walked off the job in February 1911 and were told their wages would be waiting when they came back to reason. Wages that had been loaded onto the *Halvard* instead, sealed in a strongbox, and sent into a fjord that kept them.

Rosalind photographed all of it. Then she photographed the tag — HALVARD CREW, stamped brass, green with a century of salt — because Anders had asked for proof, and Anders always asked for proof.

Her thumb hovered over the satellite messenger app.

Here was the arithmetic she'd been doing since Seldovia, since before Seldovia, since a rainy Tuesday in Anchorage eight months ago when she'd sat across from a man with a Vane cousin's jaw and a banker's patience and heard him say the number that owned her: two hundred and six thousand dollars, plus interest, plus the cost of the mistake she'd made signing her name to a fishing permit she couldn't afford. Her boat was gone. Her credit was gone. What remained was a chart in her mother's hand and a half-brother she'd never met, and Elias Vane's cousin had looked at the chart's photograph on her phone and said, *You're not selling us anything. You're buying your way out.*

That was the story she told herself, anyway. She'd gotten good at telling it. It went like this: she wasn't betraying Quentin, because you couldn't betray someone you hadn't met yet. She was protecting them both. The Vanes were going to find the wreck regardless — fleets don't need maps, they need time, and time was the one thing the fjord was running out of. If she handed over the coordinates quietly, cleanly, before anyone got hurt, then her debt died and Quentin's debt died and nobody stood in front of Maren Vane holding something Maren wanted. That was protection. That was practically mercy.

The other version of the story — the one where she sold her brother's discovery within forty-eight hours of meeting him, the one where her mother's map became blood money the way everything else in her life eventually did — that version stayed in the drawer where she kept it, under the same lock where she kept the memory of her mother saying *don't tell him, Rosalind, he has enough burdens* and meaning, though she'd never once said it plainly, *he has enough family.*

She typed the coordinates. 59-point-something north; she'd taken the fix from the cliff top that morning with the GPS she'd told Quentin was for safety. Latitude, longitude, tidal window, the phrase *strongbox located, contents documented*. Her thumbs moved without her, muscle memory of a transaction she'd performed four times already this year — harbor positions, fuel schedules, the Vanes' patrol patterns, small treasons priced by the kilobyte.

Guilt rose in her the way the tide did: not suddenly, but total.

She thought of Quentin an hour ago, kneeling in the cold water with the ledger open across his knees, reading the paymaster's confession aloud in a voice that cracked on the third paragraph. He'd looked up at her with an expression she recognized from photographs of herself at that age — the look of someone who has just learned the world is worse than advertised and is deciding, right there, whether to be worse too or to be the thing that stands against it. He'd said, "They have to see this. The families. Someone has to give them back their own history." And he'd meant it. God help her, he'd meant it, this stranger with her mother's stubborn chin who owed a salvage syndicate more than he'd ever earn and still thought truth was a currency anyone accepted.

If she sent this message, the fleet wouldn't come for the ledger. They'd come for the ledger *and* everyone who'd touched it. She knew how Maren Vane operated; she'd spent months learning it from the inside, trading scraps for extensions, watching what happened to a fisherman in Kodiak who'd tried to hold back a coordinate. The man hadn't been hurt. He'd simply been ruined so thoroughly and politely that his daughter now worked Maren's books in Homer. That was the Vane method: they didn't break people, they *absorbed* them.

So yes. Protection. If the coordinates left her hands tonight, then tomorrow the Vanes would owe her, not hunt her. Quentin would never know how close he'd sailed to the reef. She'd carry the guilt alone, which was fitting, because she'd been carrying alone since she was old enough to understand why her mother cried over water-stained charts and never explained them.

She pressed send.

The messenger pulsed once — *delivered* — and the little blue checkmark glowed in the cave dark like dead-man's milk, like something beautiful feeding on something drowned.

Rosalind put the phone away and stood very still, listening to the water drip its patient count. Somewhere ahead, Quentin's lamp bobbed, and his voice drifted back to her, muffled and bright: "Ros! There's more chambers back here — come see this —" He'd started calling her Ros on the second day, casually, as if they'd been doing it for thirty years, and every time it landed in her chest like a coal.

*He'll forgive me,* she told herself. *When it's done and we're both free, I'll tell him, and he'll understand that I did it for us.* She was aware, even as she said it inwardly, that she was building the sentence backward — constructing the forgiveness first and the confession to fit it — and that this was what liars did, and that she had therefore become, by any honest measure, a liar.

But she was a liar with a paid-off debt and a brother alive and unharmed, and in the ledger of her life that was the first black ink she'd ever seen.

She turned toward his voice, stepping carefully between the tide pools, and the glow of the dead-man's milk followed her footfalls like a witness that couldn't testify.

Behind her, in the dark she'd already given away, the strongbox sat open and empty, and the sea breathed in and out against the stones, keeping its own count of what she owed.

* * *

They surfaced into evening.

The tide was coming back for the cave mouth, silvering the basalt shelf inch by inch, and the fjord had gone the color of hammered pewter under a sky the exact gray of her mother's ashes. Quentin climbed out onto the rocks first and turned to offer her a hand, and she took it, and the warmth of his grip was another entry in the column of things she'd have to answer for.

"You were quiet in there," he said. Not accusing. Worse — concerned. He squinted at her against the low sun breaking through the cloud deck, and she watched him decide not to press, watched him file it away the way their mother must have filed away decades of unspoken things. "Cold get to you?"

"Something like that."

He nodded toward the water. "Forty-one men," he said. "You think any of them made it out?"

"The legend says the cave swallows the greedy." She heard her own voice go flat and was glad of it. "Maybe the crew just weren't greedy enough to leave."

Quentin laughed — a short, surprised sound — and shook his head. "You're exactly like her, you know. Mom. I only met her twice, but—" He stopped, and she saw him catch up to what he'd said, see the question surface behind his eyes: *how did you meet her twice when I never met her at all?*

It would be so easy to tell him. The cabin outside Anchor Point, the summers, the charts spread on the kitchen table while her mother traced the fjord with one finger and said *someday, Rosalind, someday the water will give it back.* Easy, and catastrophic, because every truth she told him narrowed the corridor between her and the message already traveling south through satellites toward a man named Anders, and she needed that corridor wide for one more day. Just one more day. After the fleet committed to the route, after her debt converted to credit, she'd tell him everything, and he'd—

"He'll hate you," she murmured, too low for Quentin to hear over the water.

"What?"

"Nothing. Cold getting to me." She smiled, and it cost her the way withdrawals cost. "Come on. We should get the ledger somewhere dry before the damp gets into those pages."

They hiked up the goat trail toward the skiff, Quentin talking the whole way — plans, contingencies, the glaciologist friend who'd calculated six tides, the arithmetic of ice and money — and Rosalind answered in the right places and let the rest wash past her. At the top of the trail she paused, once, and looked back down at the fjord.

Far out, at the mouth of the strait, a boat was running dark. No lights, no wake worth mentioning, hull low in the water. Too far to make out a name. Too close to be nobody.

Her phone sat heavy in her jacket pocket, one delivered checkmark heavier than it had been that morning.

Quentin followed her gaze, and she felt him go still beside her, felt the change in him — the storm chaser reading a sky, except the sky was water now. "Probably a tender," he said. "Or fishermen running home late."

"Probably," Rosalind agreed.

But she had sent the coordinates twelve hours ago, and fleets needed five days, and whatever was standing silent out there in the pewter light had arrived like an answer to a question she'd asked in secret — and as Quentin turned to lead the way to the truck, she understood, with the cold certainty of the incoming tide, that she had not bought protection at all.

She had summoned something. And it was already here.
