## 7. Maren's Patience Runs Thin

The fog came in off Kachemak Bay like something with intent, and Quentin Eriksen stood at the end of the Seldovia dock watching it eat the far shore, and he thought: *this is what my life looks like from the inside now.*

He'd been at the harbor bar until midnight, not drinking so much as sitting with a glass of rye the way a man sits with a decision he hasn't made yet. The ledger was wrapped in oilcloth and hidden under the floorboards of his rented room above the chandlery, and every hour he left it there felt like leaving a lit match in a hay barn. He knew what it was. He knew what it meant. He also knew — with the cold clarity that had come to him somewhere around the second glass — that knowing and deciding were two different animals entirely.

Now it was six in the morning, the tide draining out of the bay, and the fishing boats were going out one by one into the gray, their running lights smearing long reflections across water the color of gunmetal. He liked this hour. He'd always liked this hour. It was the only part of the day that hadn't been repossessed.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic," said a voice behind him. "I hate arithmetic done by other people."

He didn't turn around right away. He let himself have the three seconds. Then he turned, and Maren Vane was standing at the head of the dock with her hands in the pockets of a canvas coat that had seen more weather than most boats, and behind her, at a respectful distance, two of her crew were pretending to study a crab pot like it was the most fascinating object ever built by human hands.

"Maren."

"Quentin." She came down the dock toward him with the unhurried gait of someone who owned the dock, or would soon, or had already bought the paper saying she did. She stopped an arm's length away — close enough to be familiar, far enough to remind him she wasn't. "You didn't call in your position last night."

"I don't have a position. I'm on land."

"You know what I mean." Her eyes went past him, out to the bay, to the fog, to the invisible route through the strait where a glacier sat calving ice into the water like a slow-motion explosion. "Six tides. We're down to four. My fleet sails tomorrow morning from Homer, and when it gets here, I intend to know exactly where my salvage is."

"It's not your salvage."

"Not yet." She smiled, and it was the smile that made grown men sign things they shouldn't. "That's the whole point of contracts, Quentin. They turn *not yet* into *mine*."

He looked at her. Fifty-one years old, hair going silver at the temples in a way that looked earned rather than aged, forearms like dock line. She'd pulled her brother Halvard Vane's syndicate out of receivership twice, and the story everyone told was that she'd done it by being smarter than the sea, which was a lie, because nobody was smarter than the sea. She'd done it by being willing to stand closer to the edge than anyone else and smile while she did it.

"The fleet doesn't need me," he said. "You've got the chart. You've got the coordinates Rosalind transmitted. You've got divers who work for you because they can't say no."

"I've got all of that." She nodded slowly, agreeably, the way she did before she took something away. "What I don't have is the cave mouth timed. What I don't have is a man who's stood inside that basalt and knows which channel floods first. And what I don't have—" she stepped closer, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse than anything else she could have done— "is the ledger."

The word landed between them like a dropped anchor.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Quentin said.

"Quentin." She said his name the way a doctor says a diagnosis. "I've been in this business thirty years. I've watched men lie about gold, about guns, about bodies. You're lying about paper. And you're bad at it, because you've got ink on your fingers — archival ink, the kind that comes off old ledgers — and you've got the look of a man who read something that rearranged his furniture." She glanced down at his hands, then back up. "Wash better next time."

His stomach dropped through the dock planks and into the black water below. He kept his face still. He'd gotten good at that over the years — the stillness, the flat calm, the storm-chaser's trick of looking bored while the sky tried to kill him. But inside, everything was recalculating.

She couldn't know for certain. If she knew for certain, she wouldn't be talking. She'd have taken it already. Which meant she was bluffing — no. Not bluffing. *Fishing.* Casting a wide net and waiting to see what thrashed.

"The fleet sails tomorrow," she repeated. "Here's how the rest of your week goes. Option one: you meet my lead boat at the strait entrance on the third tide, you take my diver down to the strongbox, and whatever's in it comes up with my signature on the manifest. Debt cleared. Clean slate. Maybe even a finder's fee, if you don't insult me."

"And option two?"

"Option two," Maren said, "is that the ledger surfaces without my signature on it. Anywhere. A museum, a newspaper, a lawyer's office, a bonfire. And if that happens—" she reached into her coat and produced a folded document, holding it out between two fingers like something dead— "your debt transfers."

He didn't take it. She shook it once, patiently, and he took it, because not taking it would have meant something, and he wasn't ready for things to mean things yet.

He unfolded it. His own signature stared back at him from the bottom of the original contract — the one he'd signed in Chapter One of this whole disaster, the one he'd told himself was just paper, just leverage, just a formality. But above his signature, in fresh type, was language he hadn't agreed to. *In the event of unauthorized disclosure of salvaged materials, the undersigned debtor assumes personal liability for all outstanding sums, secured against future earnings, person, and estate.*

"They'll enforce it," Maren said. "Not with lawyers. With boats."

"This isn't legal."

"Neither was 1911, when the mining company stole a payroll from men who were dying in the dark." For a moment — half a moment — something moved behind her eyes that wasn't business. Something older. "The difference is they had the decency to lose the evidence. Don't make me be less decent than a dead company, Quentin."

He folded the paper along its original creases. Very carefully. Like it might go off.

"You mentioned a sister," he said.

Maren tilted her head. "Did I?"

"You're about to."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, quickly filed away, quickly replaced by the smile. But Quentin caught it. He caught it the way he'd learned to catch the green tint in a supercell's base, the way he'd learned to read a wall cloud before the radio confirmed it. Maren Vane had expected him to beg. She had not expected him to ask questions.

"Rosalind," he said. "She showed up three days ago with a chart in our mother's handwriting. Our mother. A woman whose name I learned from a death certificate and a box of photographs I didn't know existed until she walked into the harbor bar with them." He stepped closer, and now he was the one closing distance, and he watched Maren register that. "So here's my question, Maren. How did she find me? Because I've spent thirty years not being found. I've been careful. I've been good at it. And then she appears, out of nowhere, carrying exactly the map we need, at exactly the moment you need it, and tells me a story about inheritance that I wanted so badly to believe I didn't check the seams."

The fog pressed in around them. Somewhere out in the bay, a boat horn sounded, low and mournful, like the sea clearing its throat.

"Careful, Quentin," Maren said quietly.

"I'm done being careful. Careful is what got me here." He held up the amended contract. "You want to talk about leverage? Fine. Here's mine: I found the cave. I found the strongbox. I know what's in it, and you don't, and Rosalind doesn't, and your divers don't. The only thing standing between your fleet and that wreck is whether I decide to draw you a map or draw you a picture."

"That's not leverage. That's a hostage situation with yourself as the hostage."

"Maybe." He shrugged. "But hostage-takers get demands met."

For a long moment, neither of them spoke. The tide kept going out beneath them, exposing the barnacled pilings inch by inch, the way it exposed everything eventually, given enough time. That was the thing about tides that Quentin had come to understand these past few days: they didn't hide things. They just waited.

Maren studied him, and he let her, and he kept his breathing even and his pulse down where she couldn't see it, and inside he was screaming, because everything he'd just said was built on sand and both of them knew it. She had the fleet. She had the guns. She had the contract with his name on it and the amendment with his name on it and probably a third document somewhere with his name on it that he hadn't signed yet but would.

Then she laughed. Short, genuine, almost warm.

"There he is," she said. "There's the man who chased storms into counties that had already evacuated. I wondered where he'd gone. The debtor was boring. The debtor I could buy." She buttoned her coat against the wind coming off the water. "Fine, Quentin. You want to play? Play. Meet my lead boat at the strait on the third tide. Bring the sister. If she's yours, bring her. If she's ours—" she paused, and the pause was a knife laid flat on a table— "bring her anyway. We'll find out together."

She turned and walked back up the dock, and the two crewmen fell in behind her, and Quentin stood alone at the end of the planks with a fraudulent contract in his pocket and the taste of copper in his mouth.

*If she's ours.*

The words followed him up the dock, through the fog, into the chandlery, up the stairs to his rented room, where he knelt on the floorboards and pried up the loose plank and took out the oilcloth bundle and unwrapped it, and there it was: the union ledger, the paymaster's confession, the names of forty-seven men who had struck in 1911 and been robbed for it, and the proof that the Halvard hadn't sunk carrying treasure at all. It had sunk carrying truth. And truths, unlike gold, got heavier the longer you carried them.

He sat on the floor with his back against the wall and the ledger open on his knees, and he thought about Rosalind. About the way she'd watched him across the bar that first night, cataloguing him. About how she never talked about their mother without checking his face first, the way you check a mirror before you lie. About the transmission he'd half-seen her send from the cliff path two nights ago, hunched over her phone, thumb moving fast, face lit blue in the dark.

He'd told himself it was a weather report. He'd told himself a lot of things.

*If she's ours.*

He closed the ledger. And somewhere in the closing of it, in the small decisive sound of the cover meeting the pages, something in Quentin finished turning over. The humiliation was still there — it would always be there, the memory of standing on this dock while a woman told him the price of his own body — but humiliation, he'd learned chasing storms, was just fear that hadn't found its job yet. Give it a job. Point it at something.

He got up. He found a pencil and the back of a tide table, and he started making lists. Who in town owed him favors. Who owed the Vanes favors and hated it. Where the village boats moored, and which skiffs could run the strait light and fast. When the third tide peaked, and what the wind would do at slack water, and how long the meltwater channel would stay navigable after the glacier shed its morning ice.

He wasn't negotiating anymore. Negotiating was what you did when you accepted the terms.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the bay in ragged sheets, and the first real sunlight in three days hit the water and turned it briefly, mercifully, into something that looked like the world used to look. Down at the harbor, Rosalind's rented skiff bobbed at its mooring, empty, her oilskin gone from its hook.

And pinned to his door — he found it when he went to leave, square and white and deliberate — was a note in handwriting he was beginning to know too well, the same hand that had written the chart, the same hand that had signed the photographs:

*Quentin — Low water at the cave, tonight's tide. Come alone. There are things about Mother you need to hear before the Vanes arrive. — R.*

He stood in the doorway holding it, the sun warm on his neck, and thought: *alone, like the cave, like the ledger, like every important thing in my life.*

Then he folded the note, put it in his pocket next to the fraudulent contract — one lie, one truth, side by side over his heart — and went to find out which one his sister was made of.
