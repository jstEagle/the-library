## 8. Confrontation at Low Water

Rosalind had learned long ago that the best lies were built on true foundations, and so she told Quentin the truth about almost everything. She'd told him about Denver. About the collections agency that had bought her debt for pennies and pursued it with religious fervor. About the two jobs she worked and the third she'd lost when the restaurant closed in February. All of it true. All of it a wall she could hide behind.

The only lie was the one that mattered: that she'd come to Seldovia for him.

She waited until he fell asleep in the cabin's single chair, his boots still on, the miner's tag from the cave sitting on the table between them like a small tin accusation. Outside, the tide had turned and begun its slow retreat toward tomorrow's low water, and the harbor lights smeared across the black water like oil paint someone had dragged sideways.

Her handler's transmitter was a battered satellite unit no bigger than a paperback book, and it lived inside the false bottom of her mother's old chart case — the one piece of luggage she'd carried onto the ferry from Homer, the one thing she'd refused to let out of her sight. She'd told Quentin it was because the map was irreplaceable. That part was even true.

She keyed the unit on. The screen's glow turned her hands blue-white in the dark cabin.

*Position confirmed. Cave mouth at 59-point-1 north. Inner chamber accessible at minus tides only. Strongbox located. Contents as discussed.*

She hesitated over the send button. This was the moment, she understood dimly, where a person either became what they'd already decided to be or found out they were something else entirely. She thought about Quentin asleep three feet away, his face slack and younger than it had any right to be, a man who signed contracts because he believed he was smarter than the sea. She thought about the $40,000 that would vanish from her ledger the moment this message landed. She thought about her mother, dead eleven years now, who had never once mentioned having another child, who had kept this chart hidden in a cedar chest like something shameful.

She pressed send.

The confirmation tone was soft. A single note, almost gentle. In the chair, Quentin didn't stir.

Rosalind exhaled and began packing the unit away, and that was when she heard the floorboard.

He was standing in the doorway to the sleeping loft, and he wasn't slack-faced anymore. He was holding the tag from the cave in one hand, and his eyes went from the transmitter to the chart case to her face, and she watched the arithmetic complete itself behind them — watched him arrive at the sum she'd been dreading since Homer.

"How long," he said.

"Quentin—"

"*How long.*"

She set the transmitter down on the table, deliberately, the way you set down a weapon when running is no longer an option. "Since March."

"March." He laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "I signed Maren Vane's contract in April."

"I know."

"You knew when you got off the ferry." He came down the steps slowly, and Rosalind realized with a strange, floating clarity that she had never seen him angry before. Not once, in all their weeks of careful sibling diplomacy. She had seen him tired, scared, determined, wry. Never this. "You knew exactly what I was, and you sat in that bar in the harbor and bought me a beer and asked about Mom like you cared."

"I did care."

"Then what did you just send?"

She could have lied. God help her, some cold efficient part of her brain was already drafting versions — weather data, a message to her landlord, anything. But she looked at his face and understood that lying now would cost her the last thing she had left, which was the truth of her own motives, and she was too tired, too far from shore, to keep swimming.

"The coordinates," she said. "And the contents of the strongbox."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere outside, water lapped against the pilings, patient and indifferent, and Rosalind thought absurdly that the tide didn't care who betrayed whom; it would come back tomorrow at the same hour regardless.

"The Vanes," Quentin said finally. "You've been reporting to the Vanes since before you ever met me."

"To *a* Vane. There's a difference." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Cousin. The quiet one. Anders. He approached me in Denver eight months ago. He knew about the chart. He knew about you. He knew things about our mother that I—" She stopped. Started again. "They bought my debt, Quentin. Forty thousand dollars. They didn't buy it to collect. They bought it to hold. Every month I don't transmit, they add interest that doesn't exist anywhere but in their heads. I've been paying rent on my own life since March."

"So you sold me."

"I sold the *wreck*." Even as she said it she heard how little distance there was between those two things, and hated herself for the distinction. "You were going to give it to them anyway. That was the whole deal. I just made sure I got paid on both ends."

"That was the whole deal." He repeated it flatly, tasting it. "You know what the funny thing is? I spent my whole life thinking I was alone because my father never talked about her. Your mother. My mother. Whatever she was. And then you show up with her handwriting on a chart, and I think — okay. Okay. Maybe the story's bigger than I was told. Maybe there's a reason. Maybe there's a family somewhere in all this after all." He picked up the miner's tag and turned it over in his fingers. HALVARD CREW. "And the whole time, you're on their payroll."

"It wasn't payroll. It was survival."

"Don't you dare use that word to me." His voice cracked upward and he wrestled it back down. "Don't you *dare*. You know what survival costs in this family? It cost me my truck. It's about to cost me everything else. I know exactly what survival costs, Rosalind. What I'm trying to figure out is what it was worth to you."

And there it was — the question under all the other questions, the one she'd been avoiding since the ferry, since Denver, since the day Anders Vane slid a photograph of her mother's chart across a diner table and said *we can make your problems disappear*. She sat down slowly at the table because her legs had opinions of their own.

"She left you," Rosalind said. "Did you know that? Not died. Left. I was nine. One morning she was making breakfast and telling me about tide tables, and by dinner she was gone, and my father wouldn't say where, and for two years I checked the mail every day like a dog." Her hands were shaking. She put them flat on the table to still them. "She came back once. When I was fourteen. Just for a night. She smelled like woodsmoke and salt and she cried in the kitchen and told me she'd made a mistake she couldn't undo, and then she left again, and I never saw her alive again. So when Anders showed me that chart — her chart, her *handwriting* — and told me there was a brother, a half-brother, living up here chasing storms and drowning in debt — I didn't come for the money, Quentin. The money was the excuse I gave myself. I came because if she left something behind, I wanted to be the one who found it. Not them. Me."

"And the transmitter?"

"The transmitter was already in my bag when I got on the boat." She met his eyes and refused to flinch. "That's the truth. All of it. I told myself I'd feed them nothing useful. I told myself I'd protect you. I told myself a hundred things, and every single month, when Anders called, I told him something useful instead, because the alternative was watching my life get repossessed room by room. That's what I am. That's what she made, maybe. Somebody who survives first and grieves later."

Quentin stood very still for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quiet in a way that was worse than shouting.

"Do you know what I did when you told me about her? At the bar, that first night?" He shook his head. "I went home and I dug out every photo my father ever owned. Three pictures of her. Three. And I sat on the floor looking at them thinking — I have a sister. Blood. An actual person on this earth who shares whatever our mother was. And I thought, whatever happens with the Vanes, whatever happens with the wreck, at least I'll have that. At least I'll have *you*."

"Quentin—"

"And tonight I find out you've been selling the only thing I have left to the people who are taking it." He set the tag down on the table between them, gently, like laying a flower on a grave. "Blood's supposed to be thicker than water. Everybody says it. Nobody who says it has ever been tested."

"It was tested," Rosalind said, and her voice broke clean through. "It was tested in 1911, in that cave, and you know what the crew of the Halvard chose? They chose strangers. Miners they'd never met. They stayed with the proof instead of saving themselves, and the sea took them for it, and nobody remembers their names. So don't stand there and tell me blood means something. I've read the ledger, Quentin. I know exactly what family loyalty is worth to the people who write the checks."

The words hung there, and she knew immediately she'd gone too far — that she'd taken the wreck's terrible sacrament and used it as ammunition — but there was no taking it back. There never was. That was the family disease, maybe: say the unforgivable thing, survive first, grieve later.

Quentin looked at her for a long moment. Then he took his jacket off the hook by the door.

"Where are you going?"

"Low water's at four a.m." He didn't look back. "Maren wants an answer about the ledger by then. I'm going to go give her one. Don't wait up."

The door closed behind him with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Rosalind sat alone in the cabin with the transmitter glowing faintly in its false bottom, and outside the tide kept falling, falling, falling, exposing rock and weed and the black ribs of old wrecks, laying everything bare the way it always did, the way it always would, whether anyone aboard was ready to be seen or not.

Her handset buzzed once against the table.

A text. Unknown number. Anders.

*Fleet moved up. Two days out. Keep him away from the cave tomorrow or the deal changes — for both of you.*

She stared at it until the screen dimmed, and in the reflection of the dark glass she caught her own face, and for the first time in eight months she didn't recognize the woman looking back.

* * *

She found him at the dock an hour before dawn, because of course she did — because surviving first apparently included knowing exactly where he'd be. He was sitting on the edge of the planks with his legs hanging over black water, and Maren Vane's skiff was just a red taillight shrinking up the inlet.

Rosalind stood at the head of the dock for a long moment, hugging her arms against the cold, working up the sentence she'd rehearsed the whole walk down. Then Quentin spoke without turning around.

"She offered to erase everything. Full forgiveness. All I have to do is hand over the ledger and swear you were never involved." A pause. "She knew about you before I did, Rosalind. Did you know that? She said your name like she was doing me a favor."

The tide was at its lowest, and beneath the dock the exposed mudflats breathed and glistened, and somewhere out in the channel, far past the point, the water was pulling back from the mouth of a cave that had swallowed men whole a century ago.

"What did you tell her?" Rosalind asked.

Quentin finally turned, and his face in the dock light was unreadable, and that frightened her more than anger ever had.

"I told her I'd have an answer after the next low tide." He stood, and shouldered past her toward the cabin, and paused just long enough to say the thing that would keep her awake until the water came back:

"Come with me or don't. But decide fast, sister. Because in six hours, one of us is going to be standing in that cave holding the truth — and I need to know whose side of the glass you'll be on when we find out what it costs."
