## 11. Sabotage in the Fog

Quentin woke to the smell of diesel and wrongness.

It was a small difference — the harbor at four in the morning should have smelled of diesel, of brine and bilge and the sweet rot of kelp on the tide line — but this was diesel *under* something else, a thinner note, like solvent. Like someone had been working with rags and a funnel in the dark.

He lay in the bunk of Ida Lund's guest room for thirty seconds, listening. The fog had come in off the strait around midnight, the kind of coastal fog that didn't drift so much as settle, pressing against the windows until the streetlight outside became a smudge of amber floating in milk. In that fog, Seldovia made sounds it didn't make in daylight: halyards ticking against masts, a dog somewhere up the hill deciding against barking, the harbor's generator humming its one flat note.

And beneath all of it, very faintly, the sound of an outboard running at low throttle, moving away from town toward the point.

Quentin was out of bed before he'd finished deciding to be.

* * *

The fuel dock told the story before anyone spoke a word.

Ida Lund's skiff sat low in the water, listing just enough to be noticeable if you knew her lines, which Quentin by now did. Her fuel line — the rubber hose running from the tank to the engine — hung severed at the tank fitting, cut clean, and below it the bilge pump had been doing its dumb faithful work all night, pumping diesel overboard in a thin rainbow slick that spread across the harbor water like oil paint on a wet canvas.

Two other boats showed the same wound. Old Peter Amos's gillnetter. The Kachemak family's aluminum runner. Three boats out of the six the village had committed to the run up the fjord.

Quentin crouched on the fuel dock planking with a flashlight cupped in his hand and looked at the cuts. They bothered him more than the act itself did. These weren't hacks. A kid with a knife, an angry drunk, a rival fisherman settling a grudge — any of them would have sawed at the hose, left fibers, left fury in the work. These cuts were single passes, made with something sharp and patient, angled to look like chafe failure if you glanced and didn't look. Whoever did this had known exactly how much damage would disable a boat without announcing itself until morning.

Whoever did this had done it before.

"You're up early." Ida's voice behind him, gravel and coffee. She came down the ramp in her rubber boots with her coat over her nightgown, and when she saw her own skiff she stopped walking for a moment — just a hitch, half a step, quickly corrected, but Quentin caught it. He was learning to read these people the way he read cloud structure: not by what they said but by what they almost said.

"Somebody cut your fuel line," he said. "Peter's too. And the Kachemaks'."

Ida stood over the severed hose for a long time. Fog beaded on her gray hair. "Chafe," she said finally, without conviction.

"It's not chafe."

"I know it's not chafe." She turned and looked down the harbor, counting boats the way a shepherd counts sheep after a bad night. "I know exactly what it is. I grew up hearing about 1911, Quentin. My grandmother was eleven years old when the company men came through cutting nets. Same work. Same hands, probably, if you go back far enough." She spat over the rail, an old woman's punctuation. "Some things don't change. They just change employers."

"The fleet isn't due for three days," Quentin said. "The Vanes are still two days south."

"They are." Ida's eyes came around to him, and there was something in them he hadn't seen before — not suspicion exactly, but its cousin. "Which is a funny thing, isn't it? Because whoever did this knew we were going. Knew which boats mattered. That's not a stranger's knowledge, boy. That's kitchen-table knowledge."

The fog pressed in. Somewhere across the harbor a gull complained once and gave up.

Quentin thought about Rosalind asleep in the next room, twenty feet of wall away, and about the photograph he'd found tucked in her chart case three nights ago — a photograph of the ledger pages he'd assumed she'd taken for herself, for the same hunger that drove him. He thought about the unknown number he'd seen glowing on her phone screen at midnight, two nights ago, when he'd gotten up for water and she'd thought he was asleep. He hadn't asked. He'd told himself not asking was trust.

Now three boats sat dead in the water and every hour burned a third of their remaining margin, and trust felt like a word from another language.

"We need fuel," he said. "And we need it before the tide turns at nine."

"Harbor store opens at seven. Gunnar keeps the keys." Ida said the name flatly, and moved on, but Quentin filed it away the way you file a pressure reading that's a little too high.

* * *

Gunnar Halvorsen ran the harbor store, the fuel dock concession, and, as far as Quentin could tell, half the informal economy of Seldovia. He was sixty-ish, broad as a door, with a beard gone to salt-and-pepper wire and a laugh that could be heard across the bay. He'd been in town nineteen years. He'd fixed Quentin's winch solenoid on the second day with a part he happened to have in a drawer, and refused payment, and told a forty-minute story about a moose while he did it. Everyone loved Gunnar. Gunnar was the first person in town who'd shaken Quentin's hand instead of measuring him.

That was exactly why the thought arrived the way it did — unwelcome, cold, sliding in under the door of Quentin's mind while Gunnar stood at his counter ringing up jerry cans.

"Three boats hit," Gunnar said, shaking his head, ringing up diesel additive nobody needed. "Damn shame. Damn shame. You want me to run a pressure test on the others while I'm at it? Take me an hour, no charge."

"That'd be good," Quentin said. Then, casually, watching Gunnar's hands rather than his face: "You hear anything last night? I thought I heard an outboard around four."

"Four?" Gunnar laughed. "At four I was dead asleep, my friend. Though—" and here he paused, and the pause was nothing, it was a man thinking, it was entirely innocent, "—Erik said he heard something too. Down by the point. Probably seals. Harbor's full of them this time of year."

"Erik heard it too."

"Sure. Seals." Gunnar waved a big hand. "You know how fog carries sound. Could've been anything."

Could have been, Quentin agreed silently. Could have been seals. Could have been a saboteur idling out past the breakwater after cutting three fuel lines with surgical patience. What bothered him wasn't the answer. It was that Gunnar had answered a question Quentin had barely asked, supplying Erik's corroboration unprompted, sealing the explanation shut like a lid.

He carried the jerry cans out to the dock and didn't let himself breathe properly until he was clear of the store.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day being paranoid in a methodical way.

This was a skill storm chasing had taught him, though he'd never framed it that way until now. When you're sitting in a truck under a rotating wall cloud deciding whether the tornado is coming at you or away from you, you learn to separate what you *know* from what you *fear*, and to build only on the first column. So he built.

Known: three boats disabled between roughly two and five a.m. Known: the cuts were professional. Known: the target list was precise — Ida, Peter, the Kachemaks were three of the four fastest boats in the village fleet; whoever chose them understood that speed decided who reached the cave first. Known: the fourth fast boat was the one Quentin and Rosalind had been promised, and it had been left untouched, which meant either the saboteur wanted them mobile or wanted them to be the only ones mobile, and both possibilities were ugly.

Unknown: everything else. Including, in the dark hours of the afternoon, whether the sister sleeping ten feet from him had been the eyes that gathered the target list.

He went looking for Erik instead.

Erik Vasse was seventeen, worked the fuel dock summers, and was the closest thing Seldovia had to a night owl. Quentin found him at the boatyard, sanding paint off a skiff hull, earbuds in. He took one out when he saw Quentin coming.

"Heard anything weird lately?" Quentin asked. "Last night, early morning?"

Erik shrugged the shrug of the young, then reconsidered, because Quentin had helped pull his father's net off a reef two weeks ago and some debts got paid in honesty. "Outboard around four, maybe. Low throttle. Heading out past the point, toward the old cannery." He hesitated. "I figured it was Gunnar checking his crab pots. He goes out at weird hours sometimes."

"Does he."

"Yeah. More lately, actually." Erik went back to his sanding, then stopped. "Hey — is it true about the Halvard? My great-great-grandpa was on the strike committee. My mom says the payroll was never—"

"It's true," Quentin said. "We'll talk about it later. Right now I need you to do something for me and not tell anybody, including your mom."

Erik's eyes lit with the pure uncomplicated joy of a teenager handed a secret mission. "Okay."

"Gunnar's skiff. While he's at the store. Look at the fuel line. Don't touch anything, just look. Tell me if the hose has been replaced recently — new clamps, bright rubber, anything that doesn't match the age of the rest of the rigging."

"Why?"

"Because if somebody cut those lines with a spare hose in their pocket," Quentin said, "they'd have had to carry the evidence home. And people are lazy. People reuse things."

* * *

Erik found him at dusk on the seawall, walking fast, trying to be casual and failing the way teenagers fail at everything — completely and transparently.

"It's been replaced," Erik said. "The whole line. New clamps, shiny. Rest of his rigging's ancient. And there was a coil of the old hose stuffed under his stern seat, cut into pieces, like—" Erik made a scissors motion. "Like somebody cut it up so it wouldn't match."

The fog was thickening again, rolling in off the strait in long gray arms, and Quentin stood on the seawall and felt the particular vertigo of a world reorganizing itself around a fact. Nineteen years. Nineteen years Gunnar had been in Seldovia. Nineteen years of fixed winches and free advice and moose stories, and underneath it, all along, whatever the Vanes paid a man to be — a cousin, Maren had called the network once, in a tone Quentin had taken for affection. *Cousins everywhere, Eriksen. That's what a fleet really is.*

Not a fleet of boats. A fleet of faces.

He thought about the village hall meeting, three nights ago, where the great-granddaughter of the strike leader had read the paymaster's confession aloud and old men had wept openly and the whole town had voted, voices raised, to commit its boats. Gunnar had been there. Gunnar had stood in the back with his arms crossed, and when the vote came he'd raised his hand high, higher than anyone, and shouted *yes* loud enough to make people laugh.

The perfect cover wasn't silence. The perfect cover was the loudest yes in the room.

"Quentin?" Erik was watching him. "You okay?"

"No," Quentin said honestly. "But thank you. Go home. Don't say anything to anyone — not yet. If Gunnar asks, you were sanding all evening."

* * *

He walked back through the fog with his collar up and his mind running the problem like a storm cell, rotating.

If he confronted Gunnar, he burned the only thread he had. If he told the village, half of them wouldn't believe it — nineteen years of favors buys a lot of disbelief — and the other half would tear the man apart on the dock, and whatever else Gunnar was, Quentin wasn't ready to hand a mob a traitor before he understood the shape of the thing. And if he told Rosalind—

There it was. The rock in the channel his whole course kept snagging on.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape: the saboteur had known which boats to cut. Kitchen-table knowledge, Ida said. And Rosalind had a photograph of the ledger in her possession and an unknown number in her phone and a history with money trouble that she'd admitted to in fragments, each fragment arriving late, each one worse than the last. She'd sold something once already — she'd told him that much, in the worst hour of their fight, her face wet and furious: *I sold the location months ago, Quentin, I was drowning, you don't get to judge what drowning people grab for.* Maybe she'd sold more since. Maybe the handler she'd confessed to was feeding her orders now, and maybe those orders included a list of boat names.

Or maybe — and this was the possibility that shamed him even as he held it — maybe she was exactly what she appeared to be: a woman who'd made one terrible trade and was spending every day since trying to buy it back, lying awake in the next room deciding, hour by hour, whose side she was on.

He didn't know. That was the whole of it. He didn't know, and the fog was full of shapes, and every ally wore two faces, and the tide table on the harbor board said the water would start rising at 2:14 in the morning and the ice gate at the glacier wouldn't wait for anyone's certainty.

The lights of the cabin were on when he came up the hill. Through the window he could see Rosalind at the table, bent over their mother's chart, tracing the fjord route with one finger, her lips moving slightly — memorizing it, he realized. Committing the route to memory the way he'd taught himself to read radar. Preparing.

For the run together. Or for something else.

Quentin stood in the fog outside his own front door for a long moment, soaked through, watching his sister study the map their mother had died holding, and understood that the next twenty-four hours would decide not just who reached the wreck first, but who he was willing to believe — and that whichever answer he chose, he'd have to live inside it.

Then the window light flickered, and he saw Rosalind's head come up sharply, and he saw her reach for her phone where it lay on the chart, screen glowing, an incoming message lighting her face from below.

She read it twice. She looked at the door — at him, though the fog and glass must have hidden him — and her expression did something complicated and afraid.

Then she blew out the lamp, and the cabin went dark, and Quentin stepped back into the fog to decide, before morning, whether to knock on his own door or watch it until dawn.
