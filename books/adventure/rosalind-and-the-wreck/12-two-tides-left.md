## 12. Two Tides Left

The fuel came in by night, the way contraband always does.

Rosalind heard the skiff before she saw it — a muffled four-stroke coughing through fog off the point, running without lights, and she was already moving down the beach path with her hood up when the hull ground against the shingle below Old Halvorsen's boathouse. Three figures unloaded jerry cans in silence, passing them hand to hand like pallbearers. One of them was Quentin.

She stood in the shadow of a drying rack, cod split and hanging like pale flags around her, and watched her brother work. He'd lost weight since Seldovia. Or maybe he'd never had any to lose and the storm season had simply stripped him down to sinew and stubbornness. He moved with the economy of a man who had stopped expecting help from anyone, and something about that — the self-sufficiency of it, the loneliness dressed up as competence — made her chest ache in a way she refused to name.

The third figure was Ingrid Halvorsen, the strike leader's great-granddaughter, sixty years old with forearms like dock line. She pressed a canvas satchel into Quentin's hands and said something too low for Rosalind to catch over the water. Quentin nodded once. Then Ingrid spat on her palm and held it out, and after a moment's hesitation — visible even at forty yards, a whole argument happening in the set of his shoulders — Quentin spat in his own and shook it.

An oath. The old kind. The kind you couldn't burn or shred or lawyer your way out of.

Rosalind turned back toward the cabin before they could see her, because there were things a person had to decide alone, in the dark, with the smell of fish and salt in her nose, and this was one of them: whether to be on that skiff when it left, or whether to let him go without her and spend the rest of her life listening for an engine that never came back.

* * *

He found her at dawn, which meant he'd known where she was all along, which meant he'd been watching her the way she'd been watching him, and neither of them had said a word about it for two days.

She was sitting on the cabin's rotting porch steps with her mother's chart spread across her knees, going over the fjord route for the hundredth time, tracing the channel the glacier had cut and abandoned and cut again over a century of bad temper. The paper was soft as cloth now from handling. Her mother's pencil annotations had faded to ghosts — soundings, tide notes, a small X near the cave mouth that might have been nothing or might have been everything.

Quentin stood at the bottom of the steps holding two tin cups. Steam rose off both of them. He didn't offer one so much as extend it, arm out, eyes elsewhere, the way you'd feed a dog you weren't sure wouldn't bite you.

"Black," he said. "I remembered."

"You couldn't have. You were nine."

"I remember the kitchen." He sat down on the step below hers, not beside — below, deliberately, keeping his head lower than hers, and she understood that too. An offering. "The percolator on the wood stove. You used to stand on a chair and watch it."

"I used to stand on a chair and steal coffee when she wasn't looking."

"That too." He drank. The fog was lifting off the water in rags, and beyond it the fjord waited, gray and patient, its ice gate glinting six miles north like a locked door. "Two tides."

"I can count."

"Didn't say you couldn't." He turned the cup in his hands. "Ingrid's people got us eleven gallons. Enough for the run in, maybe enough out if I don't push the engine. It's the skiff with the patched transom, the one tied behind the cannery. Nobody'll miss her until we're gone, and by then—"

"By then either it won't matter or it will." She folded the chart along its old creases. "You rehearsing that speech long?"

"Since midnight."

"It needs work."

A sound escaped him that might have been a laugh in a better life. He looked out at the water instead of at her, and she watched him gather himself, and she knew what was coming because she'd been dreading it since the night on the rocks — since he'd stood over her with the transmitter in his fist and his face gone white as the glacier, asking her how long, how much, *how could you*, three questions and only time to answer one.

"Roz." He said it carefully, testing the word. He hadn't called her that since they were children, since before they knew they were children of the same mother under different circumstances, different fathers, different cities, the same silence. "Before we go. I need to know what I'm carrying in that boat. Not just the ledger. You."

The fog closed a little. Somewhere down the beach a gull screamed once and gave up.

"You want the whole accounting," she said.

"I want enough that I'm not surprised twice."

So she gave it to him. Not the version she'd rehearsed in her head for two days — the clean version, the sympathetic version — but the ugly one, start to finish, because he'd asked for honesty about dishonesty and she was tired of carrying the pretty lie around like luggage.

* * *

"My debt started in Anchorage," she said, "but it was born in Ketchikan, and it was born the same way yours was — somebody selling me a future I couldn't afford."

She told him about the fishing permit. Hers, legitimately hers, bought with seven years of deckhand wages on boats that treated her like ballast with opinions. A limited-entry permit, the kind that made a woman independent, that let her run her own season and answer to nobody. She'd financed it through a lender whose paperwork was clean and whose ownership, she learned too late, ran uphill through two shell companies to a name she hadn't recognized yet: Vane.

"The first year was fine. Second year the price collapsed and I missed a payment. Third year they bought my processor contract out from under me, so I had no buyer, which meant no revenue, which meant more missed payments. By year four the interest had eaten the permit whole and I was working my own boat as crew to pay them for the privilege." She laughed, short and bitter. "They didn't take anything from me, Quentin. That's the elegant part. They just arranged things so I took it from myself."

"And the handler."

"Hollis." The name came out flat. "Vane cousin. Runs the chandlery here, has for nine years. Everybody loves Hollis. Hollis remembers birthdays." She stared at the water. "He approached me eight months ago. Before Mother died. Before the map. He said the family had a historical interest in the Halvard — salvage interest, he called it — and that a woman with my surname and my mother's papers would be well positioned to help. And in exchange—"

"They'd erase the permit debt."

"They'd erase the permit debt." She said it again because saying it twice made it real, and real was what she owed him. "Not forgive. Erase. There's a difference and I've had eight months to learn it. Forgiveness is a gift. Erasure means the paper never existed, which means I never existed to them, which is exactly what I wanted." Her voice caught and she hated it and pushed through. "I said yes before he finished the sentence. That's the part you should know. There wasn't a struggle. There wasn't a night where I paced and wrestled. He offered and I said yes, and then I spent eight months telling myself it was survival, and it was, but it was also—" She stopped.

"Also what?"

"Also the first time anyone had ever offered me a way out. Anybody. Ever." She finally turned and looked at him straight on. "You had Maren Vane corner you on a dock and offer you a deal, and you signed because you believed you were smarter than the sea. I had Hollis Vane buy me a beer and offer me a deal, and I signed because I believed nobody was ever going to save me, so I may as well save myself dirty. Same signature. Different desperation. Don't tell me they're not the same, because I've compared them every night for two days and they're identical."

Quentin was quiet for a long time. The tide was turning; she could hear it change beneath the fog, that vast slow inhale the bay took twice a day.

"When you transmitted," he said finally. "From the rocks. What did you send?"

"Coordinates. Photographs of the tag. A confirmation that the cave was accessible at low water." She swallowed. "And that you'd found the strongbox chamber."

"Not the ledger itself."

"No. I photographed it for them, but I never sent the pages. I told Hollis the box was corroded shut and I needed another dive window." She saw him register that — the one thing she'd withheld, the one thread she'd kept in her own fist. "I don't know why. At the time I told myself it was leverage. Insurance. But sitting here, I think—" She exhaled. "I think some part of me already knew what was in it. Before I opened it. Some part of me grew up on our mother's stories and knew the payroll story was too neat, and knew that whatever was really in that box was going to belong to somebody, and I couldn't quite make myself hand it over blind."

"And now?"

"Now I know it belongs to Ingrid's people. Now I know the company stole their wages and drowned the proof and called it weather." She set the chart aside. "Now I know exactly what I sold, and to whom, and I'd like to unsell it, and I don't expect that to count for much, but you asked for the accounting and that's the balance. Debt on one side. One withheld transmission on the other. You do the math. You're better at it than me."

* * *

He didn't do the math out loud. Instead he was silent so long she thought he'd gotten up and left, and then he spoke, and his voice was rougher than she'd ever heard it.

"Maren offered me the same arithmetic. On the dock. She said if the ledger surfaced without her signature on it, my debt transfers to my person." He turned his cup over, dumped the cold dregs onto the boards. "Which is a polite way of saying they'd own me instead of my truck. Body instead of property. I've been chewing on that for two days, and here's what I keep coming back to." He looked at her. "We're both collateral. That's the whole design. They don't want gold, Roz, they want *people*. People who owe them are worth more than wrecks. A wreck pays once. A debtor pays forever."

"So what do we do about it?"

"We stop paying." He said it simply, the way you'd state the weather. "Two tides. We run the route, we get the ledger to the village fleet, we make it public before the Vanes' lead boat clears the strait. Copies already went out by radio photograph — Ingrid sent them to the union hall in Juneau, the newspaper in Anchorage, three other places I'm not supposed to know about. The originals matter less every hour. But Maren doesn't know that yet, and by the time she does, it'll be done."

"And my debt? My erasure? The moment I turn on Hollis, that's gone. He'll bury me in paper by Friday."

"Yeah." Quentin rubbed his jaw. "It will. Mine too. We'll both come out of this owning nothing but each other and a patched skiff." He almost smiled. "I've been poorer than that."

"Have you?"

"Once. It didn't stick." He stood, and offered her his hand — actually offered it this time, palm up, looking right at her, no dog-feeding caution in it. "I'm not forgiving you. I want that clear. Forgiveness is for later, maybe, if we live, and it's earned in installments, and you've got a lot of installments ahead. But I trust you. Today. On this run. Because you told me the truth when lying was easier, and because you kept the ledger pages back when handing them over was safer, and because—" He stopped, jaw working. "Because you're the only family I've got and I found out I had you three weeks ago and I'm damned if I'm going to let the Vanes have the only thing our mother left either of us."

Rosalind looked at his hand. Rough knuckles, a healing rope burn across the palm, the hand of a man who'd been holding on to things that pulled back.

She took it.

"Wary alliance," she said. "Necessity. Not forgiveness."

"Not forgiveness."

"But Quentin—" She stood, and they were close enough that she could see the exhaustion banked behind his eyes like coals. "If it comes down to it, in that channel, with their boat behind us — you run the ice, I'll handle the radio. And if one of us doesn't come back, the other one finishes it. Swear it. Spit and shake, the old way. I saw Ingrid give it to you and I want the same terms."

He spat in his palm. She spat in hers. The slap of their grip sounded loud in the fog, and somewhere down the beach a gull took wing as if released.

* * *

They loaded the skiff through the morning, working without speaking most of the time, and the silence was different now — not the armed silence of two days ago but the working silence of people who'd been crew together in some other life and were finding the rhythm again. Fuel cans lashed amidships. The ledger wrapped in oilcloth and sealed in a dry bag, strapped under the thwart where Quentin could reach it and she could see it. Her mother's chart in a ziplock inside her jacket, against her ribs, where it had lived since Seldovia.

At noon the fog burned off and the fjord showed itself: six miles of gray-green water running north between walls of black basalt, narrowing toward the glacier's blue-white wall, the meltwater channel already visible as a seam of milky jade threading the ice field. The ice gate. Somewhere beyond it, the cave. Somewhere behind them, three days early, the Vane fleet was running dark through the strait.

Rosalind cast off the lines while Quentin started the engine. It caught on the second pull, coughed, steadied. The patched transom groaned once and held.

"One more thing," she said, stepping aboard, taking her place at the bow. "Hollis promised me more than the erasure. Last week, after the sabotage — after I told him the village boats were fueled and ready — he said if the Vanes got the ledger back clean, he'd see me paid. Actual money. Enough to start over anywhere." She met Quentin's eyes across the skiff's small span. "I'm telling you because you said no surprises. That was the offer on the table yesterday morning. This morning I'm in your boat instead."

Quentin put his hand on the tiller and looked north, at the ice, at the door in the world's coldest wall.

"Then let's go make sure nobody gets paid," he said, and opened the throttle, and the skiff swung her bow toward the fjord as the first of the last two tides began to lift them in.

Behind them, at the mouth of the harbor, a boat that had been sitting dark at anchor for two days quietly raised its own engine and followed.
