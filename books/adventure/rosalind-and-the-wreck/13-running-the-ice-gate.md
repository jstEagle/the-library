## 13. Running the Ice Gate

The meltwater channel opened before them like a wound in the glacier's face, a corridor of gray-green water no wider than a county road, and Quentin cut the throttle to half and let the skiff drift into it nose-first.

"Talk to me," Rosalind said from the bow. She had the binoculars up, her hood cinched tight against the spray, and she hadn't looked back at him once since they'd left the village dock in the dark. That was fine. He didn't want to look at her either. Not yet. Maybe not ever. But the water ahead of them was alive with ice, and he needed her eyes more than he needed his anger.

"Lead boat's still behind us," she said. "Maybe two miles. Running without lights."

"Of course they are." Quentin eased the skiff forward, feeling for the current through the wheel the way you'd feel for a pulse. The channel was fed by the glacier's undermelt, and it ran cold and fast and treacherous, full of brash ice that looked soft as slush and hit like masonry. "They'll have Maren aboard. She won't stop at the gate. She'll come all the way in if she has to."

"She doesn't have to," Rosalind said. "She just has to keep us from getting there first."

The ledger was wrapped in oilcloth inside his jacket, against his chest, and he could feel it there like a second heartbeat. Two tides left. The calving window would close tomorrow night, and after that the fjord route would be sealed behind a hundred feet of new ice until autumn — sealed with everything inside it, the strongbox, the confession, the names of men who'd been dead for a hundred years and still hadn't been paid.

He throttled up.

* * *

The channel narrowed a mile in.

Quentin had run water like this before — not this water, nothing was this water, but the Kenai rivers in flood season, the tidal cuts around the barrier islands where the current moved faster than a man could swim. He knew the grammar of it. Read the surface. The smooth glassy stretches were the deep ones; the water that boiled and feathered was telling you about rocks underneath. Ice floated where the current pushed it, and the current pushed it away from the deepest line, so you steered toward the ice, not away from it, which was the opposite of every instinct in your body and exactly why most boats died here.

"Hold on," he said, and took them through the first field of brash at eleven knots.

It was like driving through a demolition. The chunks came off the bow in white sheets, some of them the size of refrigerators, and one caught the skiff's port quarter and spun them fifteen degrees before Quentin corrected, hands moving without consulting him, throttle down, rudder hard, throttle up again, the whole conversation conducted in fractions of seconds. Rosalind braced herself against the bow rail and said nothing, which he chose to interpret as professional respect rather than terror.

"There," she said suddenly, pointing left. "Smoke."

He saw it a moment later — a smudge of diesel haze hanging over the water behind them, catching the flat gray light. The Vane lead boat. The *Iron Meridian*, sixty feet of salvage tug with a reinforced bow built for breaking harbor ice, carrying Maren Vane and whatever men she'd decided she needed. It couldn't fit everywhere the skiff could fit. But it didn't need to. It just needed to be close enough.

A sound rolled across the water then, flat and enormous, like a door slamming in a house the size of a state. Both of them flinched. Up ahead, maybe two miles distant, a shelf of the glacier's face let go and slid into the fjord, and even from here they could see the white plume rise, could feel the wave coming through the hull a long moment later — a slow lift and drop, gentle as breathing, that would not be gentle at all if it happened closer.

"That's the schedule keeping itself," Rosalind said quietly.

"Yeah." Quentin's mouth was dry. "Glacier doesn't negotiate."

* * *

The shooting started at the narrows.

The channel bent hard to starboard around a headland of black rock, and as they came around the bend Quentin saw the narrows ahead — a gap between two grounded bergs, maybe forty feet wide, water pouring through it fast and green and deep. And beyond it, framed in the gap like a picture in a frame, the glacier itself: three hundred feet of blue-white face, streaked with moraine, groaning under its own weight, shedding a constant rain of meltwater into the channel below.

The first bullet hit the water ten feet off their stern.

It made a sound like a whip crack, and then a geyser, and then Rosalind was down flat in the bow and shouting something Quentin didn't hear because the second and third rounds were already coming in, walking across the water toward them in a line, someone on the *Meridian*'s bow deck firing by eye at a target too small and too fast and too far.

"They're shooting at us!" Rosalind's voice cracked somewhere between outrage and disbelief.

"They're shooting *near* us," Quentin said. "Maren wants the ledger, not corpses. Corpses make paperwork." Even as he said it he knew it was only half true, and the half that wasn't true was the part that scared him. Salvage men got paid by results. Somewhere on that tug was a man with a rifle who'd been told results mattered more than method.

He put the narrows dead ahead and did the arithmetic. Forty feet of gap. Current running through it at four, five knots, funneling between the grounded bergs. Wind against current on the far side, which meant chop standing up in sharp little waves right where he'd come out. And behind him, closing, a boat that could take hits he couldn't.

"You trust me?" he called forward.

Rosalind turned her head and looked back at him for the first time since the dock. Her face was wet, her eyes were furious and afraid, and what she said was: "You're my brother. I don't have anyone else to trust."

It wasn't forgiveness. He knew that. It was arithmetic too — the same cold arithmetic he'd been doing since chapter one of this whole disaster, except hers had a different answer. He felt it land in his chest anyway, warm and unwelcome, and he shoved it down where it wouldn't distract his hands.

"Hold on to something that's bolted down," he said, and drove at the gap.

* * *

Later, he would try to remember it in order and fail. Memory wouldn't hold a sequence; it held flashes.

The walls of ice rising on either side, close enough to touch, dripping, ancient, blue in their cores like something lit from within. The roar of the current under the hull, felt more than heard, the skiff wanting to yaw and him holding her straight with both arms shaking. A rifle round sparking off the portside berg six feet above his head, spalling ice into the cockpit, and Rosalind screaming his name. The gap opening ahead of him like an eye.

And then the other thing. The thing that came after.

He was halfway through when the glacier spoke.

Not the door-slam of a distant calving — this was closer, deeper, a sound like the earth clearing its throat, and Quentin's eyes went up past the gap's exit, past the open water beyond, to the glacier face two miles on, where a section the size of an apartment building leaned out over the water at an angle that was suddenly, obviously, catastrophically wrong.

"Berg!" he heard himself shout, though the word meant nothing, communicated nothing, because there was nowhere to go. The section dropped. It fell the way mountains fall, slow at first and then all at once, and the crash when it hit the water was a physical blow, a wall of sound that slapped across the channel, and then the sea itself stood up.

The displacement wave came at them as a single gray ramp, eight feet high, maybe ten, moving faster than any wave had a right to move, and behind it came the berg itself — hundreds of tons of calved ice surging out from the face, rolling, the size of a building, carving a wake of its own.

Every instinct Quentin had screamed to turn and run for the gap behind them. He strangled the instinct. Turning broadside to that wave was death; the skiff would roll and fill in nine seconds. Running back meant meeting the wave beam-on in the narrows, in the worst water in the channel, with the *Meridian* coming the other way.

There was one direction, and it was insane, and it was the only one.

He gunned it — full throttle, straight at the wave, straight at the berg behind it.

"QUENTIN—"

"Trust me!" he roared, and the word *brother* was in it whether he wanted it there or not, and then the wave had them.

The bow climbed. Climbed. Climbed until the sky was all there was and the propeller sang free in the air, and Quentin stood into his knees and kept the nose square to the crest because square was survival and anything else was a roll. For one suspended instant the skiff hung at the top of the gray water with the whole drowned world visible below — the channel, the bergs, the tiny dark shape of the *Iron Meridian* turning frantically in the distance — and then the bow came down and down and down and slammed into the trough with a impact that folded him over the wheel and knocked the breath out of him in a white flash.

But the hull held. The hull held, and they were through the wave, and now the berg was coming, rolling toward them with the ponderous inevitability of geology, and Quentin did the last piece of arithmetic he had left.

A rolling berg pushes water ahead of it. That push radiates outward in a ring. Which means there is a line — a moving line, a few yards wide — where the berg's own wake cancels the wave behind it, where the water goes briefly, impossibly, flat.

He'd seen it once, years ago, from a hillside above Columbia Glacier, watching a charter captain thread exactly that seam while tourists screamed and filmed. The man had made it look like a trick. It wasn't a trick. It was reading, the same as reading the surface for depth, except the book was closing in real time and the penalty for a misread page was everything.

Quentin steered for the flat water.

It was there — he saw it, a lane of eerie calm sliding along the berg's flank, marked by a seam of foam — and he put the skiff into it and held her there, matching speed with a mountain, close enough that the ice threw cold onto the deck, close enough to see the old dirt lines frozen in it, the air bubbles like trapped constellations, a hundred thousand years of weather pressed clear. The berg rolled beside them, vast and indifferent, and the chaos of its wake broke white on either side of their little lane of calm, and for perhaps twenty seconds Quentin Eriksen steered a course that existed for less than a minute in the history of the ocean and would never exist again.

Then the berg settled, the lane dissolved, and they were through — out into the open water beyond, rocking in the berg's dying swell, engine smoking, hull scraped but whole.

Rosalind was on her knees in the bow. She was laughing, or crying, or both; from where Quentin sat it sounded the same.

"You absolute—" She gave up on the sentence. "How. How did you know that would work?"

"I didn't," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him, because his hands were playing the wheel like a piano, every finger vibrating. "I knew it *could*. That's not the same thing."

"It is today," she said.

* * *

Behind them, the *Iron Meridian* had stopped.

Quentin watched her in the mirror of the rearview he'd rigged from a truck part, watched the tug hang back at the edge of the ice field, her bow swung away from the channel, and understood what he was seeing. The wave and the berg had redrawn the map. The narrows they'd shot through were gone — one of the grounding bergs had shifted, and the gap was now choked with fresh brash, a white rattle of debris turning slowly in the current. The *Meridian* was a sixty-foot tug with a crew Maren couldn't afford to bury. She would wait for the channel to reopen, or she would go around, and going around cost hours they didn't have to give.

"We've got her beat to the cave," Rosalind said, climbing back over the console, wet to the skin, alive, her eyes bright with something that wasn't fear anymore. "By an hour, maybe two."

"An hour," Quentin agreed. "And then we're all in the same room with one exit, and the tide comes in at noon."

Ahead of them the fjord opened up, wide and silent, the glacier filling the horizon at its head like a wall at the end of the world. The water was strewn with ice that glowed faintly blue even in the gray light, and the air coming off it was cold enough to taste, clean and mineral and older than any debt. Somewhere under that water was the Halvard. Somewhere under it, the men who'd stayed with her.

The wilderness had tested him, and he'd passed, and passing hadn't felt like triumph. It had felt like being accepted — provisionally, the way ice accepts a load, until the next test.

Rosalind touched his arm. Pointed.

At the base of the basalt cliffs, half-hidden behind a curtain of falling meltwater, a dark seam showed in the rock. The cave mouth. Exposed, waiting, exactly where the legend said it would be.

And tied off to a boulder below it, bobbing gently in the swell, was a skiff that wasn't theirs.

Someone had beaten them to the gate after all.
