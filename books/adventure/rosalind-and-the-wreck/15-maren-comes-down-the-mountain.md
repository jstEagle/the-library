## 15. Maren Comes Down the Mountain

The light in the inner chamber came from two headlamps and one dying chemical stick Rosalind had cracked open, and it turned the scratched messages on the rock wall into something between scripture and a wound. Quentin stood close enough to read them twice, three times, his breath fogging in the cold air that came off the flooded passage behind them.

*We stay. The proof goes to the men who earned it or it goes to the bottom with us. — E. Halvard, 14 March 1911.*

*Tell my wife the wages were never lost. They were taken.*

*Tell them all.*

Below, in the green-lit water, the Halvard's mast still stood at a drunk angle, its topmast fouled with a century of anemones, and the strongbox sat on the ledge where Rosalind had wedged it — empty of coin, heavy with paper, the paymaster's confession wrapped in oilcloth like a thing worth more than the gold it had replaced. Which, Quentin thought, it was. Gold could be counted. This could convict.

"Quentin." Rosalind's voice came low, careful, the way it had been since the reconciliation that wasn't one — two people agreeing to stand in the same boat without pretending the water between them had drained away. "Do you hear that?"

He did. A sound under the drip and sigh of the cave. Boots on wet stone. More than one pair, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the ground they walked on, or believed they did.

He killed his headlamp first, then reached over and killed hers, and they stood in the dark with only the chemical stick's dying green, listening.

The footsteps came down the sloped passage from the cave mouth — the same passage they'd entered by, the one that was already swallowing itself as the tide turned. Quentin did the arithmetic without wanting to. Four hours to high water. The lower gallery would flood first. The passage they'd come through would be chest-deep in two, impassable in three. Whoever was coming down had timed it the same way he would have.

"Maren," he said quietly.

"You don't know that."

"I know how she walks. She walks like the floor owes her money."

The green light caught the edge of Rosalind's mouth — almost a smile, gone before it landed. Then the first headlamp beam swung into the chamber, and the dark broke apart.

Maren Vane came down the mountain the way she did everything: as if the world had been arranged in advance for her convenience. She was in a dry suit, no helmet, her gray-streaked braid still dry above the collar, and behind her came four men Quentin recognized from the dock at Seldovia — the Vanes' salvagers, men who moved salvage the way other men moved furniture. Two of them carried short-barreled shotguns with the casualness of tools. The third carried a crowbar. The fourth carried nothing at all, which was somehow worse.

They fanned out along the ledge above the flooded gallery, boots ringing on stone, and Maren stopped three meters from Quentin with her hands loose at her sides and her eyes going — deliberately, he noted — to the strongbox, to the ledger's oilcloth corner, to the scratched messages on the wall. She read them. He watched her read them. Something moved behind her face and was filed away, unspent.

"Quentin." She said his name like an invoice. "You've been busy."

"Traffic was light."

Behind her, one of the shotguns shifted. Maren didn't look at it. She never had to look at anything; it was her gift, the way she made other people's weapons do her looking.

"Rosalind." Her gaze moved past him, and Quentin felt his sister go very still beside him. "Your handler's been worried. You stopped transmitting."

"I've been in a cave," Rosalind said. Her voice was level. Quentin heard the effort in it anyway.

"Mm." Maren turned back to him, and now the transactional warmth came up, the version of her that had sat across from him in the harbor bar with a contract and a pen. "Here's where we are, Quentin, and I'll say it once because my patience and the tide are on the same schedule. The fleet's in the strait. My brother's on the lead boat. There are eleven men topside who have been told there's a payroll in this cave, and they've been told it because payroll is a word that keeps men rowing. What's actually down here —" she tipped her chin at the strongbox — "is paper. Paper that says the company stole from every family in that village, paper that says the Vanes' founding money came out of the same till, paper that a hundred years of lawyers have kept drowned. My grandfather's grandfather signed the order that put this ship in this fjord. I'd prefer it stayed drowned."

"And if I prefer it didn't."

"Then we're having a different conversation." She took one step closer. The chemical stick guttered, and for a moment her face was all planes and shadow. "Here's the offer, and it's the only one. The ledger comes to me. You walk out of here clean. Not paid — clean. The debt, the interest, the truck, the contract with my brother's signature on it — gone. Burned. You owe the Vanes nothing, and no one in this family ever says your name again except to say he squared himself."

Quentin said nothing. The offer sat in the water between them like something surfacing.

"And Rosalind," Maren went on, softer, which was how you knew the knife was coming, "keeps her arrangement. Her debts — and I've seen them, Rosalind, I've seen all of them, they're uglier than your brother's — those go the same way. She walks out a free woman with her handler's number deleted and her history unwritten. She was never here. Neither of you was ever here. The cave swallows the greedy, the legend holds, and everyone goes home."

"And if I say no?"

Maren sighed — genuinely, he thought, with something almost like regret — and nodded at the man with the crowbar. "Then the tide does the arithmetic for us. This gallery floods in four hours. The passage out floods in two. My men and I will be at the top of it drinking coffee while you two learn to swim in the dark with your boots on. And when the water's done, there'll be no ledger, no witnesses, and a wreck that finally, officially, killed the last two people fool enough to go looking for it. The insurance alone will make my brother weep with joy."

The shotgun men hadn't moved. They didn't need to. The cave was doing their work for them — the water rising in the gallery below with a sound like slow breathing, the cold coming up off it, the light getting smaller.

* * *

Quentin looked at the strongbox.

That was the thing he couldn't stop doing — looking at it, the way you look at a door you know is locked. He thought about the truck he'd totaled on the Kenai road, about the number Maren's brother had recited to him in a dockside office while a fan turned and the smell of diesel came through the window. He thought about what clean would feel like. Not rich. Not safe, even. Just — unowned. Waking up and having the day belong to him. He had not had a day that belonged to him in six years, and here it was, being offered, held out in Maren Vane's open hand like a coin.

All it cost was a lie.

That was the arithmetic that kept snagging. Not the danger — he'd made his peace with the danger somewhere around the third tide, when the glacier had groaned across the fjord like a door in a haunted house. Not even Rosalind; she'd made her own bed with the Vanes and they both knew it, and if she chose the clean exit, that was hers to choose. No. What snagged was the wall behind him. *Tell them the wages were never lost. They were taken.* Fourteen men had stayed in this chamber with water climbing their chests so that a piece of paper could outlive them. They had chosen the paper over their own lungs. And Quentin could stand here and trade it for the price of a truck.

The lie would cost him nothing, Maren said. She was wrong. It would cost him his name, and he'd finally figured out — standing in a cave, of all places, in green light, with his sister breathing beside him — that his name was the only asset he had left that the Vanes hadn't lien against.

"Quentin." Rosalind's voice, barely a breath.

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned her head, and the chemical light caught her eyes, and what was in them wasn't fear. It was something he'd never seen her aim at him before. It looked like a question she was afraid to ask out loud: *who are you going to be, right now, in front of me?*

He thought about their mother — the mother he hadn't known he had until a water-stained chart arrived with a stranger's handwriting on the back of it. He thought about the map, and the route she'd drawn, and the fact that she had drawn it at all, had sat somewhere with a pen and cared enough to leave him a way in. He'd spent his whole life believing he was alone because people left. Standing here, he understood for the first time that some people stayed and said nothing, and that was its own kind of staying.

He looked at Maren Vane.

"Here's my counter," he said.

Maren's eyebrows rose a millimeter. She was a woman who enjoyed counters; they gave her something to demolish.

"The ledger goes public," Quentin said. "Not to you. Not to the bottom. To the village. The confession, the ledger, the paymaster's signature — all of it, read aloud in the hall where the strikers' grandchildren drink their coffee. And you're going to let it happen, because the alternative is that the Vanes' name ends up on it too, in front of everyone, and I will make sure it does. I'll read your grandfather's grandfather's order myself if I have to."

The chamber went very quiet. Below them, the water kept its slow breathing. One of the shotgun men shifted his weight, and the stone rang under his boot.

"You're not in a position to make offers," Maren said. Her voice had cooled by ten degrees. "You're in a position to drown."

"I've been in worse positions. I was in debt to your family."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not a smile, but the ghost of respect that sometimes visits people right before they decide to destroy you. She took another step, close enough now that he could smell the salt and the faint chemical sweetness of her dry suit, and her voice dropped to the register she probably used on sinking men.

"Quentin. Listen to me as one professional to another. That paper is a hundred and fourteen years old. The men who wrote it are bones. The company is a memory. The village doesn't want the truth — the village wants the *money*, and there is no money, and when they find that out they'll hate you for being the one who told them. I'm offering you the only version of this story where anybody walks away whole. You'd be a fool —" and here she glanced, deliberately, at Rosalind, and the knife turned — "you'd both be fools, to trade your lives for paper nobody living cares about."

"Somebody living cares," Quentin said. "I do."

"Then you'll die polite."

"Probably."

Maren held his eyes for a long moment. Then she straightened, and the transactional warmth came back up like a shopkeeper's shutter opening, and Quentin understood that she had decided — that the decision had been made before she ever came down the mountain, that the offer had been a formality, a courtesy extended to a man she'd once liked, and that the four men on the ledge were not here to negotiate.

"Last chance," she said. "The box, Quentin."

"No."

She exhaled through her nose. "Then we do it the old way." And she turned to her men and said, in the flat tone of a woman ordering a drink, "Take the box. Put them in the low gallery. Leave the lamps — I'm not a monster."

* * *

They moved fast, the way professionals move when the conversation's over. The man with the crowbar got to the strongbox first, wrenched it off the ledge with a screech of stone, and handed it up to the second man, who slung it against his back with a webbing strap that had clearly been brought for the purpose. The whole thing took eleven seconds. Quentin counted. Eleven seconds for a hundred and fourteen years to change hands.

"Rosalind." Maren paused beside her on the way to the passage, and for a moment the two women stood face to face in the green light, and Quentin saw his sister's jaw set against whatever was in Maren's eyes. "You still have a choice. You always did. That's more than he's got."

"I know," Rosalind said. "That's why I'm staying."

Maren looked at her for a beat longer than she meant to — Quentin saw it, the flicker of genuine surprise, the first honest thing she'd shown since the lamps came in — and then she turned and led her men up the passage, boots ringing, the strongbox swaying between the salvager's shoulders, the light shrinking and shrinking until the chamber was theirs again: two headlamps, one dying stick, the breathing water, and the wall that said *tell them all.*

They stood in the dark and listened to the footsteps fade.

"Two hours," Rosalind said finally. "Maybe less. The passage floods at the top first — there's that dip where we came down. Once it's over the lip, the air in here starts going."

"I know."

"And the box is gone. The originals are gone. Everything those men wrote is walking up that passage on a Vane strap."

"I know." Quentin turned in a slow circle, playing his lamp across the chamber — the ledge, the mast tip below, the scratched wall, the black water rising in the gallery with the patience of something that had been waiting a century and could wait two hours more. And then his lamp stopped on the far wall, the one they hadn't examined, where the rock shelved down toward the flooded gallery, and he saw it: a seam. A crack in the basalt, hand's width, running down and back into the dark, and out of it came a thread of moving air — cold, clean, tasting of glacier and open water.

Air meant a way through. Air meant the cave breathed somewhere else.

"Rosalind," he said. "How do you feel about tight spaces?"

She followed his lamp to the seam, and he watched her do the arithmetic — the same cold arithmetic he'd learned at his friend's kitchen table with the tide tables spread out, the same arithmetic that had run his life since the day Maren Vane put a contract in front of him. But this time the numbers came out different. This time the numbers said: *two hours, one crack in the rock, and a ledger's worth of truth already photographed, already copied, already halfway to the village by radio if the kid at the hall had done his job.*

The originals were walking up the passage on a Vane strap. But Maren Vane had just made one mistake she didn't know she'd made. She'd put the truth in writing, in front of witnesses, in a chamber that was about to flood — and Quentin intended to be alive, on the other side of that seam, when the water came for her version of events.

The seam breathed its cold clean air. Somewhere above, muffled through a hundred feet of basalt, the glacier groaned like a thing turning over in its sleep.

"Quentin," Rosalind said quietly, "the tide's turning."

"I know," he said, and put his shoulder to the rock.
