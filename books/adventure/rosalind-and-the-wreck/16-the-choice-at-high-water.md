## 16. The Choice at High Water

The water came in like a rumor that had finally decided to be true.

Quentin heard it before he saw it — a low, muscular hiss from the tunnel behind them, the sound of the fjord reclaiming what it had lent. The chamber's floor was black stone worn smooth by a century of tides, and across it now ran thin silver sheets of seawater, spreading, finding old channels, filling them. Somewhere below their feet, through fissures too narrow to see, the ocean was rising into the room the way blood rises into a bruise.

Maren Vane stood ten feet away with a lantern in one hand and a pistol in the other, and she was not pointing it at him. That was the thing about Maren — she never had to point it. She held it the way other people held a pen, casually, an instrument for signing things.

"Last chance to be sensible," she said.

Behind her, two of her salvagers blocked the tunnel mouth, their headlamps cutting white cones through the cave's wet dark. Behind Quentin, Rosalind stood close enough that he could feel her breath on his neck, quick and shallow. The ledger lay in its oilcloth wrapping on a shelf of rock between them all, exactly where he'd set it down when the lights had come up the tunnel behind them like a verdict.

The water reached Quentin's boots. Cold soaked through the leather in seconds. He didn't move.

"You said forgiveness," he said. "Full debt. Both of us walk out of here clean."

"I said that." Maren's voice was even, almost gentle, which was how you knew she meant business. "I'm offering you the only trade anyone's ever offered you, Quentin. A piece of paper for your whole life back. Your boat, your truck money, your name off every list my brothers keep. And her silence." A small tilt of her head toward Rosalind. "She walks out of this cave owing nothing to anybody. Including me."

"And all it costs is everything those men died carrying."

"It costs nothing," Maren said. "That's what you keep failing to understand. It costs *nothing*. Nobody alive remembers 1911. Nobody alive cares. The company that stole those wages dissolved before the Depression. The men who signed that confession are dust, Quentin. Their grandchildren are fishing boats and gas stations and gravestones. You're standing in a flooded hole at the edge of the world, holding the only copy of a crime that has no victims left to complain." She raised the lantern slightly, and the light caught the water sheeting past her boots, turning it briefly gold. "Hand it over, and in twenty minutes this chamber floods to the ceiling and there's no proof any of you ever saw anything. That's not a threat. That's the tide doing your laundry."

The water was at his shins now. He could feel the current strengthening, the fjord pushing in through the throat of the cave with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more.

He thought about what forgiveness would feel like. He made himself actually think it, because he owed himself that much honesty. Waking up without the number hanging over him. Answering his phone without his stomach dropping. Walking into the harbor bar in Seldovia and ordering a beer like a man who belonged to himself. Twenty years of debt, gone, for the price of one oilcloth bundle and a closed mouth.

It would cost nothing. She was right about that.

Except it would cost his name, and he had finally, after forty-one years, figured out that his name was the only asset he'd ever owned outright.

"Rosalind," Maren said, softer now. "You're the smart one. Talk to him."

"He's not listening to me," Rosalind said. "He hasn't since I got here."

"That's not true," Quentin said.

"No?" Her voice cracked on it. "You've been waiting the whole time for me to be the thing you already decided I was. Well, I was. I did the worst thing I've ever done in this cave, and I'll answer for it. But right now, in this minute, I'm telling you the same thing I told myself last night: don't hand it to her. Don't let them win by being reasonable. They're always reasonable, Quentin. That's how they eat people."

The salvagers shifted. One of them glanced at Maren, and Maren gave a fractional shake of her head — *not yet* — and Quentin understood the shape of the next few minutes better than he wanted to. If he refused long enough, the pistol would stop being a pen and start being a gun. He had maybe one exchange left before the arithmetic changed.

So he stopped doing arithmetic.

He picked up the ledger.

* * *

The oilcloth was slick with spray, heavier than paper should be, as if the weight of it had been accumulating since 1911. Under his hands he could feel the box-within-the-box, the tin strongbox lining they'd pried open an hour ago by lantern light while the glacier groaned somewhere above them like a sleeping animal. He thought of the tag they'd found near the entrance — HALVARD CREW, stamped in brass, hung on a nail where a man had left it a hundred years ago. He thought of the scratches deeper in, the ones Rosalind had found with her lamp: *STAYED WITH HER. THE BOOK GOES ASHORE IF GOD IS WILLING.* Fourteen men who had chosen a flooded ship over a lifeboat because the paper in the strongbox mattered more than their lungs.

They had stayed with it so that someone, someday, would carry it out.

And here was the joke, the beautiful terrible joke: the sea had carried it out. Every spring tide for a century, the fjord had breathed into this cave, and the cave had kept its secret the way the legend said it swallowed the greedy — but it hadn't swallowed the honest. It had waited for them. Waited for a bankrupt storm chaser and a sister who'd sold him out, because apparently the sea has a sense of humor and no standards at all.

"Quentin." Maren's voice had lost its gentleness. "Put it down."

"I'm going to tell you what's in it," Quentin said. "Not because you asked. Because you should have to stand here and hear it."

"Quentin—"

"The Halvard wasn't carrying payroll." His voice echoed off the wet rock, doubled, tripled, the cave repeating him like a jury. "There was never any gold in this box. There's a union ledger — every name of every man who struck in 1911, every shift they walked off, every dollar the cannery consortium docked them for striking. And underneath it, a confession, signed and witnessed, from the paymaster himself. Marcus Thorne. He wrote out how the company took the miners' wages, called them losses, and split them three ways between the owners. He names names. He dates it. He apologizes to the men he robbed and says he hopes God is more forgiving than the company was."

Maren's face didn't change. But the salvager on the left looked at the one on the right, and the one on the right looked at the water, and Quentin saw the seed go in.

"Nobody living cares," Maren said. But it came out a half-beat late.

"Their great-grandchildren care." Quentin lifted the ledger above the water line. "I know because I've met them. Because three days ago I sat in a village hall while Alma Kvasnikoff read that confession out loud to forty people, and I watched an eighty-year-old woman find her grandfather's name in a thief's handwriting and weep like it happened last Tuesday. It did happen last Tuesday, Maren. That's what evidence does. It makes a hundred years ago into now."

"There are no originals that matter," Maren said. "One book. In a cave. With me."

"Wrong." And here Quentin smiled, and it felt strange on his face, like wearing someone else's coat. "Copies went out yesterday. Radio photograph, page by page, transmitted from the village hall to the state historical society, to the university archive, to the descendants' association in Kodiak, to a journalist in Anchorage who's been sitting on the strike story for twenty years waiting for exactly this. Forty-one pages. They're everywhere now. You can't buy them all, and you can't drown them, and neither can I."

He watched her understand it. Watched it land behind her eyes the way cold water lands — first the shock, then the spreading chill. The ledger in his hands was already a ghost. The real treasure had left the cave by radio wave while the Vanes' fleet was still running dark through the strait.

"So why," Maren said slowly, "are you still holding it?"

"Because the original belongs to them." He turned toward the tunnel, toward the two headlamps blocking it. "Because fourteen men drowned keeping this dry, and it doesn't go to a syndicate, and it doesn't stay in a cave, and it sure as hell doesn't get traded for my debts. It goes ashore. To the people whose names are written in it. Even if all they get is the shell."

"Then give it to me nicely," Maren said, "or we take it and the cave keeps you."

The water was at his knees. The current tugged at him now, insistent, the fjord's breath going in and out faster. Above them, faint through a hundred feet of ice and stone, came a sound like a rifle shot — the glacier settling, or announcing itself. Six tides, the glaciologist had said. This was tide five, flooding. Tide six would bring the calving, and the calving would bring the collapse, and everyone in this chamber knew it, including Maren, which was why her men were edging back toward the tunnel even as they tried to hold it.

Quentin looked at his sister.

She was soaked to the waist, hair plastered to her face, and she was looking at him with an expression he'd never seen on her — not calculation, not guilt, not the guarded longing she'd carried into Seldovia like contraband. Something open. Something terrified and certain at once.

"Do it," she said.

He waded forward, one step, two, the water dragging at his thighs, until he was close enough to Maren Vane to see the salt drying white on her collar. He held out the ledger.

She didn't take it. Her pistol came up — actually up, this time, actually pointed — and her voice dropped into the register her brothers probably used in boardrooms. "Set it on the rock and step back."

"No," Quentin said. "Take it. From my hands. I want you to carry it out of here yourself. I want it to be your hands that bring it to the surface, and your name on the delivery, and your face when Alma Kvasnikoff opens it in front of the whole town and reads her grandfather's name out of a Vane courier's package. You wanted a signature on this, Maren. Congratulations. You're about to sign it."

"I will shoot you."

"You won't." His heart was slamming against his ribs hard enough to hurt, and his voice shook, and he let it shake, because the truth doesn't need to be steady. "Shoot me and the ledger goes in the water, and you fish a ruined book out of a flooding cave while your own crew watches you murder a man over paper that's already worthless to you. Everything in this book is public, Maren. Killing me buys you nothing except being the woman who killed somebody for a ghost. You didn't sail a fleet through the strait to be that. You sailed it to make this problem disappear quietly. Well." He pushed the ledger closer to her chest. "It disappeared. Loudly. Yesterday."

For a long moment the only sounds were the water and the glacier and Rosalind's breathing.

Then Maren Vane took the ledger.

She took it the way you take something hot — fast, fingers barely touching the oilcloth — and she stared at it, and Quentin watched thirty years of syndicate arithmetic fail to produce an answer. The debt contract in his pocket was ash by morning; he'd already decided that, had decided it somewhere around the moment he'd understood that the treasure was evidence. But she couldn't know that yet. All she knew was that the thing she'd crossed a fjord to bury was now the most famous document on the Kenai Peninsula, and her family's name was welded to its delivery, and the tide was rising around her expensive boots.

"This isn't mercy," she said.

"No," Quentin agreed. "It's a receipt."

Above them the glacier cracked again, longer this time, a sound like the world clearing its throat. One of the salvagers said something urgent and low, and Maren's jaw tightened. She backed toward the tunnel, the ledger clutched against her rain jacket, her men falling in around her, and at the mouth of the passage she stopped and turned half around, lantern light carving her face out of the dark.

"For what it's worth," she said, "my brothers would have shot you."

"Yeah," Quentin said. "That's why I'm glad they sent you."

She went. The headlamps shrank down the tunnel, bounced on the wet walls, and vanished, and the sound of their footsteps merged with the hiss of the incoming sea until Quentin couldn't tell them apart.

* * *

Then it was just the two of them, and the water, and the light.

Rosalind's lamp was guttering; she'd cracked it on the rocks during the scuffle at the entrance, and the beam pulsed like something dying. She held it anyway, aimed at the shelf where the strongbox had lain, at the brass tag on its nail, at the century of dark that was about to close over all of it.

"They're going to make it out," she said. "Barely."

"Maren's good in tight spots. It's the open ones that kill her."

The water was at his waist. He could feel the chamber changing around them, the air compressing under the low ceiling, the exit passage already drowning — they had minutes, maybe less, and the skiff was tied outside the cave mouth in a swell that was getting uglier by the second. He should have been moving. He made himself stand one more moment in the flood, in the place where fourteen men had chosen to stay, and he put his palm flat against the black rock one last time, the way you'd touch a coffin lid.

"Thank you," he said to the dark. He didn't know who he was saying it to. It didn't matter. The cave took it the way it took everything.

"Quentin." Rosalind's hand found his arm. "We have to go. Now."

They went — swimming the last stretch of the tunnel where the air pocket pinched shut, bursting up into the night on the seaward side of the basalt cliffs, gasping, hauling themselves along the guide rope to where the skiff pitched and snapped at its painter. The fjord was black glass streaked with white, and the glacier above them was a pale wall under the stars, and it was groaning now continuously, a deep tectonic complaint, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep.

Rosalind got the engine on the third pull. Quentin cast off and swung the bow toward the channel, toward the village lights five miles south, and then he stopped, one hand on the tiller, and looked back.

The cave mouth was a low arch of darker darkness at the base of the cliffs, and the sea was pouring into it in a long silver tongue. By morning it would be underwater. By tomorrow night, if the glaciologist's math held, it would be under a hundred thousand tons of ice.

"Look," Rosalind said.

He followed her finger up. High on the glacier's face, a seam had opened — a hairline of blue-white visible even in the dark, weeping meltwater, ticking wider. The ice groaned again, and this time the sound didn't stop. It built. It rolled down the fjord and came back off the far shore and built again, and every dog in the village five miles away was going to start barking, and Quentin Eriksen, who had chased storms across two continents and never once been afraid of the sky, felt the fear hit him like a wave and felt, underneath it, something he had no better word for than *right*.

The mountain was coming down to settle the account.

"Hold on," he said, and opened the throttle, and the skiff leapt south with the roar of the calving chasing them across the black water like the applause of something vast and finally satisfied.
