## 17. Rosalind's Crossroads

The water in the inner chamber had climbed past the first scratched messages on the wall, and the lantern light made the rising surface look like hammered pewter, trembling with every drip from the ceiling. Rosalind stood ankle-deep in a century of cold, and the gun in her hand weighed more than any object she had ever held.

It wasn't heavy because of the steel. It was heavy because of what it was for.

"Pick it up properly," her handler said. He stood on the rock shelf near the flooded entrance passage, where Maren Vane's salvagers had come down into the cave like men descending into a church they intended to rob. His name was Anders — Anders Vane, cousin to the brothers, the quiet man who ran the fuel dock in Seldovia and had shaken her father's hand at funerals. Eleven years he'd been embedded in that town. Eleven years of buying coffee and bait and small talk while he waited for someone to find the Halvard.

She had been his instrument for eight months of those years. Maybe longer, if she was honest about when it started — honest the way she was learning to be honest now, too late and all at once.

"Rosalind." Anders's voice was gentle, which was worse than if it hadn't been. "You know how this works. You know what happens to people who freeze."

Quentin stood three meters away, chest-deep where the chamber floor dropped off, one arm hooked through the strap of the satchel holding the ledger. The original ledger. The copies were already gone — radioed as photographs to the village before dawn, printed and read aloud in a hall full of fishing families who had waited a hundred and thirteen years for someone to say their great-grandfathers' names without shame. The paper in Quentin's hands was worth nothing now except as an act.

That was the thing Anders hadn't understood. That was the thing she herself hadn't understood until this exact moment, standing in rising water with a pistol her handler had pressed into her palm two minutes ago.

"You don't need to do this," Quentin said. Not pleading. Just stating it, the way he stated weather. "Nobody needs anything anymore. The story's out. Shoot me and you're shooting a man who already gave it away."

"Then it's not about the ledger," Anders said. "It's about the next one. There's always a next one. Maren wants the town to understand what loyalty costs." He turned to Rosalind, and in the lantern light his face was almost kind, which was the cruelest trick he'd ever played on her. "One shot. Through the shoulder if you can't do the head. Prove it, and your debt dies tonight — every page of it. You walk out of here clean. We never speak again. Isn't that what you wanted? Wasn't that the whole reason you called me from that motel in Homer?"

The whole reason. She could still feel the motel room: the rain on the window, the phone warm against her ear, her own voice saying *I'll give you the fjord coordinates* in exchange for numbers being erased from a column somewhere in Anchorage. She had told herself it was protection. She had told herself it was survival, and survival was neutral, and everyone in her family had always confused survival with living.

Her mother had died confusing them too. Rosalind knew that now — knew it from the chart in its oilcloth sleeve, from the marginal notes in a hand she'd spent thirty years trying to read without loving. Her mother had carried the map and the silence both, and had passed one to each of her children, and neither gift had been a kindness.

"Rosalind." Anders again, softer. "The water won't wait and neither will I."

The water was at their knees now. Behind Quentin, deeper in the chamber, the scratched messages caught the light — the crew's last words cut into basalt by men who had chosen to stay with the ship rather than let the company bury what the miners were owed. She had read them an hour ago by lantern flame and felt something crack open in her chest that had been sealed since she was nine years old. *We keep the books true.* That was what the last man had written. *We keep the books true, whoever drowns.*

She looked at the gun.

She thought about all the versions of herself that had led here. The girl who learned to check exits before she checked faces. The woman who photographed a dead man's ledger by phone-light and told herself photographs weren't theft. The informant who had sold her brother's location before she'd ever met him, and then sat in a harbor bar watching him drink, testing whether blood was a currency or a coincidence.

Every version of her had survived. None of them had lived.

And here was the arithmetic she'd been running her whole life, finally done correctly: there was no column anywhere in Anders Vane's books where *Rosalind, clean* appeared. There never had been. The debt would die tonight or it wouldn't, but it would not die because she pulled a trigger. Debts like theirs didn't work that way. They compounded. She had watched them compound in her own body for forty years — every favor a lien, every silence an interest payment.

"Rosalind," Quentin said quietly. "Hey. Look at me."

She looked at him. Her brother. A stranger six days ago, a stranger she'd betrayed before she ever met him, and somehow — somehow — the only person in the world whose face she could read without translation. He was shivering in the black water, holding a dead man's book above the tide line, and he was looking at her the way nobody had ever looked at her: like she was allowed to choose.

"It's going to calve soon," he said. "You can hear it. Whatever you decide, decide fast — but know that I decided about you a long time ago. Before the cave. Before the map." A ghost of the crooked grin she was beginning to know. "Took me thirty years to find out I had a sister. You think I'm giving her up because her boss has a gun?"

*Her boss.* Yes. That was the word. That was the whole rotten architecture of her life in two syllables.

The glacier spoke then — a deep report through the rock, muffled and vast, like a door closing in another wing of the world. Dust sifted from the ceiling. The salvagers on the shelf shifted, nervous, and Maren Vane's voice came down the passage, tight: "Anders. Now. Or we leave them."

Anders raised his own sidearm and leveled it at Quentin's chest, casual as a man checking a gauge. "Then we simplify. Rosalind — step aside, or share his account."

"No," Rosalind said.

The word came out of her with no effort at all, which surprised her, after a lifetime of words that cost everything.

Anders's eyes flicked to her. "No?"

"No." She turned the pistol in her hand — not toward Quentin, not toward Anders, but examining it, feeling its balance, remembering the safety course she'd taken in Juneau under a name that wasn't hers. "You want proof of loyalty, Anders. Here's mine." And she drove the pistol down against the rock shelf beside her, once, twice, three times, until the frame cracked and the mechanism ground and the barrel bent against the basalt like a wishbone.

The sound was very loud in the chamber. Then the water sounds came back, patient, indifferent.

"That was my collateral," Anders said. His voice had lost its gentleness entirely, and underneath it she could finally see the thing itself: not a handler, not a cousin, just a creditor. "That was your way out."

"It was your leash," she said. "I'm done wearing it." She waded forward, past Quentin, until she stood between him and the muzzle, close enough to see the salt drying on Anders's collar. "And here's what you can carry back up the mountain to Maren. The ledger's public. The confession's public. Every boat in that village has a copy and half of them have laminated them. You can drown us, drown the originals, drown the whole legend — and Monday morning the union hall opens with our great-grandparents' names in it anyway. You didn't lose a treasure, Anders. You lost the argument. A hundred years ago."

Behind her, Quentin moved — she heard the water part — and then his voice, over her shoulder, steady as a barometer: "And the contract's ash by tomorrow. Both of ours. Write it down in whatever book you people keep."

Anders looked at the two of them — the siblings, standing together in the flood, backlit by a dying lantern — and something in his face performed a long, slow calculation and arrived at nothing. Because there was nothing left to calculate. That was the truth of it. The Vanes' entire power had always been arithmetic: debts, tides, windows, odds. And arithmetic had no answer for two people who had stopped counting.

He lowered the gun — not in surrender; in dismissal, which from a Vane was nearly the same thing. "The calving will seal this chamber in twenty minutes," he said. "Maybe less. The passage floods at the sill. If you're still in here when it goes, you're part of the wreck." He backed up the shelf, boots splashing, and paused at the mouth of the passage. For one moment the old gentleness flickered across his face like a fish under ice. "Your mother," he said, "would have done exactly what you just did. That's why we kept her file open for forty years."

Then he was gone, boots receding up the wet stone, and the salvagers' lights with him, and Rosalind stood alone in the dark with her brother and the rising sea.

* * *

They had perhaps fifteen minutes, and they spent the first one not moving at all.

Quentin reached out and took her hand — took the hand that had held the gun, turned it over, looked at the split skin across her knuckles where the pistol had broken against the rock. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. Thirty years of separate lives, and it turned out they shared a language after all: the language of people who had learned that touch was a debt, and were learning, right now, in knee-deep seawater, to spend it freely anyway.

"We have to go," she finally said. "He wasn't lying about the sill."

"I know." But he didn't move yet. "Rosalind. What you did—"

"Don't." She shook her head. Water dripped from her hair, from her chin. "Don't make it noble. I did it because I was tired. Eight months of reporting to a man who calls me *kiddo*, four decades of owing people for existing. I'm not brave, Quentin. I'm bankrupt. It just turns out bankruptcy looks like freedom from the inside."

He laughed — a short, real laugh, swallowed quickly by the cave. "Then we're both insolvent. Come on."

They went. The passage back toward the outer chamber was already thigh-deep and pushing hard, the tide pouring in through channels that had been dry air six hours ago, and the glacier was talking overhead in long rolling reports, each one closer than the last. Quentin went first, reading the water the way he read storms — feeling for the pull of it, the direction, the lie underneath. "Left wall," he called back. "There's an eddy behind the pillar. Stay in the slack."

She stayed in the slack. She followed her brother through a drowning cave, and found that she trusted him completely, instantly, without audit — and understood that this was the thing she had actually crossed the country to find. Not the Halvard. Not the payroll. Not even the mother. This: a person whose wake she could follow without checking the exits.

In the outer chamber the water was waist-high and rising in visible pulses now, each surge from the calving glacier arriving as a slow breath through the cave. The tag stamped HALVARD CREW still hung on its nail above the old rigging, turning gently in the current like a compass finding north. Rosalind reached out as they passed and closed her fingers around it, and didn't examine why, and didn't need to.

Some things you take out of a wreck so they don't drown twice.

"The skiff?" she shouted over the water-noise.

"Beach side, if it's still there." Quentin hauled himself onto the sloping ledge below the exposed cave mouth, then turned and pulled her up beside him. Outside, the fjord had gone strange — the low gray light, the glassy water, the mountain of ice at the fjord's head shedding white dust with every groan. And out on the water, small and stubborn and absurdly brave, the village fleet: a dozen hulls holding position beyond the ice line, waiting. Waiting for them.

"They stayed," she breathed.

"Of course they stayed." Quentin squinted at the glacier, doing his arithmetic — but she saw, for the first time, that it wasn't cold arithmetic anymore. It was the arithmetic of a man with something to lose. "Big face is hanging over the channel. When it goes, the wave will run right down the throat of the fjord. We need to be in open water when it does, and we need—" He stopped.

"What?"

He was looking past her, back into the cave. Back at the inner chamber, where the water had swallowed the scratched messages, the strongbox's empty cradle, the whole drowned testimony of nineteen-eleven. Where Maren Vane and her salvagers had entered — and where, unless they'd run fast, they might still be.

The fjord answered before either of them could speak. A crack like the sky splitting ran down the valley, and high on the glacier's face, a slab the size of a church began, slowly, majestically, to lean.

"Run," Quentin said.
