## 22. Next Season's Tide

The first hard frost came to Seldovia on a Tuesday, and by Friday the harbor smelled like woodsmoke and diesel and the particular green rot of kelp dying back for winter. Quentin Eriksen stood on the dock at six in the morning with a thermos of coffee he hadn't poured yet, watching the light come up gray over the strait, and thought: *this is what it feels like when nothing is chasing you.*

He'd forgotten. That was the strange part. He'd spent so many years running — from creditors, from storms he was chasing only because they were faster than the men who wanted his truck, his boat, his name — that he'd assumed the running was just what a body did. Like breathing. Like the tide.

Now the tide came in and went out and asked nothing of him at all.

Behind him, boots on the planks. A rhythm he knew the way you know your own heartbeat once it's been gone long enough to miss. Rosalind came down the ramp carrying two paper bags from the bakery and a rolled chart under her arm, her hair pinned back against the wind coming off the water, and she didn't say good morning because neither of them had said good morning to each other in four months, not since the day they'd started working together. It wasn't coldness. It was something better than coldness. It was the silence of people who no longer needed to fill the air to prove they were still there.

"Kringler," she said, holding up one bag. "And the cinnamon thing you pretend you don't like."

"I don't pretend."

"You bought two last week."

"Rationing."

She almost smiled. With Rosalind, an almost-smile was a full sentence. She handed him the bag and unrolled the chart across the hood of the weather service skiff — *his* skiff now, technically the agency's, but the paperwork had his name on the operator line and nobody had ever put his name on anything official before without also putting a lien on it.

"Front's coming down off the peninsula," she said, tapping the chart. "Low pressure sitting over the gulf like it owns the place. Service wants eyes on the fjord before it closes for the season."

Quentin looked north, where the mountains had gone white above the treeline overnight. The fjord. The word sat differently in his mouth now than it had in June, when it had been a route, a shortcut, a gamble with six tides on the clock. Now it was just geography. Just ice and water and rock doing what they'd done for ten thousand years and would do for ten thousand more, indifferent to payroll ledgers and debt contracts and the Vanes of the world.

"Glacier's been quiet since August," he said.

"Quiet isn't calm." Rosalind folded the chart with the crisp economy of someone who'd grown up reading water the way other children learned to read street signs. "Calving face is unstable. Service wants a survey run before the freeze-up so we have baseline imagery for next spring. We go in, we shoot the channel, we come out. One tide."

One tide. Quentin felt the old arithmetic stir somewhere under his ribs — the counting of hours against water, the cold ledger of time — and then it settled again, because this time nobody was holding the note. This time the only deadline was the season itself, and the season didn't send enforcers.

"All right," he said. "Let's go look at some ice."

* * *

They ran the strait with the swell quartering astern, the skiff rising and falling in that long patient rhythm that Quentin had come to think of as the sea's resting heartbeat. The Kenai coast fell away behind them in bands of color — black spruce, rust alder, the first snow dusting the high shoulders — and ahead the fjord opened its throat between basalt walls that still, after everything, made his hands tighten on the wheel.

Rosalind sat beside him with the radar and the radio log, calling depths off the sounder in a flat, unhurried voice. She'd turned out to be a natural navigator, which shouldn't have surprised him and did anyway. Everything about her surprised him. That was the cost, he supposed, of thirty years of not knowing she existed: every small thing she did arrived with the weight of a revelation. The way she marked a chart. The way she drank her coffee in three exact sips and set the cup down as if closing a meeting. The way she'd started, sometime in September, humming when she thought he couldn't hear — an old tune he'd finally placed as something their mother used to sing, though neither of them said so.

"How's the shoulder?" she asked, not looking up from the radar.

"Fine."

"You flinched on the wheel."

"I adjusted my grip."

"Mm." She let it go, which was its own kind of progress. In July she'd have pushed. In August she'd have sulked. Now she just logged it somewhere behind her eyes, the way she logged everything, and he understood that this too was inheritance — their mother's habit, probably, of watching weather and people both for the change that comes before the change.

The truth about the shoulder was that it ached when the barometer dropped, which Maren Vane had given him with a rifle butt in the cave, and which he'd decided to keep as a private instrument. Weather in the bone. Old fishermen swore by it. He figured if he had to carry a scar, he might as well make it useful.

The glacier appeared around the headland at 0940, and as always it took his breath and gave nothing back.

It looked smaller than it had in summer. That was the trick of it — the calving face had retreated another forty meters since the survey flight in September, and where the ice had been there was now a raw brown rampart of moraine, wet and gleaming, like the mountain showing bone. The face itself was dirty blue, crevassed, groaning faintly in the cold. Above it the icefield climbed into cloud until you couldn't tell where the glacier ended and the sky began.

"There," Rosalind said quietly, and pointed left.

The cave.

Even sealed, even drowned, you could see where it had been — a darker seam in the basalt below the old high-tide line, half-hidden now behind a fan of fallen rock. In June it had stood open like a mouth, the season's lowest tide pulling back the curtain on sixty years of legend: rigging, strongboxes, the bones of a ship that had chosen loyalty over cargo. Now the entrance was buried under tons of fresh collapse, and above it the cliff showed the scar of the calving wave that had brought the whole face down — with the Vane salvage crew scrambling for their boats, alive and beaten, their claim drowned under stone and seawater along with the evidence they'd crossed an ocean to bury.

Quentin cut the throttle and let the skiff drift.

Neither of them spoke for a while. There wasn't much to say that the rock wasn't already saying. He watched the seam in the cliff and thought about the messages scratched into the inner chamber wall — *WE STAY WITH THE SHIP*, and the names beneath, eleven of them, men who had drowned in 1911 rather than hand the strikers' proof to the company's thugs. He'd read those names aloud once, in the village hall, his voice cracking on the fourth one. He'd never been able to remember all eleven since. He suspected that was right, somehow. Some things were meant to be carried by a whole town, not one man's memory.

"The service wants imagery of the face," Rosalind said at last. "Not the cave."

"I know what they want."

"We could get closer."

"We're not getting closer."

She looked at him then, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod and mark the position on her chart without argument. That was the other thing that had changed between them: they'd learned which silences were truces and which were agreements, and this one was an agreement, clean as a signature.

He eased the throttle forward and ran the survey line at a respectful distance, close enough for the camera to do its work, far enough to leave the legend alone. The glacier calved twice while they worked — small pieces, house-sized, the reports rolling across the water like distant artillery — and each time Rosalind called the splash point and Quentin adjusted course without being asked, and the two of them moved around each other in the narrow cockpit with the ease of a crew that had been together for years instead of months.

On the third pass, she said, "Maren sent a letter."

The word landed in the cockpit like a fish dropped on deck. "A letter."

"Postmarked Anchorage. Forwarded through the service office." Rosalind pulled a plain envelope from inside her jacket and held it out. "I haven't opened it. It's addressed to both of us."

Both of us. Quentin took it and turned it over. Heavy paper, expensive, the handwriting precise and unhurried — Maren Vane wrote the way she negotiated, giving away nothing in the style. His thumb found the flap and stopped.

"She concede?" he asked.

"It's Maren. She doesn't concede. She *repositions*."

He opened it. One page, typed except for the signature.

*Mr. Eriksen. Ms. Eriksen.*

*The syndicate has written off the Halvard claim. The loss is recorded as environmental casualty; the insurance settled in October; no further action will be taken regarding either of you or the matter of the ledger. I am instructed to inform you that the debt file is closed. You will find the original contract enclosed with the courtesy copy retained in our records.*

*I will add one observation, unofficially. In twenty-two years I have watched men bargain with the sea, and I have never seen one win. You did not win either. You simply understood sooner than we did that the sea was not the party across the table. Consider that a professional compliment. It will not be repeated.*

*M.V.*

Folded behind the page was the contract itself — the document Quentin had signed in June with a cornered man's pride and a fool's confidence, the paper that had owned his truck, his gear, his future earnings, his name. The Vane brothers' seal stamped across it. And across the whole face of it, in red ink, in a clerk's indifferent hand: *VOID — CLAIM ABANDONED.*

Rosalind read it over his arm. For a moment neither of them moved.

Then Quentin did the thing he'd been imagining since the night he'd burned the copy in the flooded chamber, when the real thing had been locked in a Vane filing cabinet two hundred miles away. He reached into the skiff's storage locker, took out the small steel lighter he carried for flare emergencies, and held the contract over the transom.

"Wait," Rosalind said.

He waited. He'd gotten better at that, too.

She dug the cinnamon pastry out of the bakery bag, tore it in half, and handed him his share. "If we're burning things," she said, "we do it properly."

So they ate standing up in the cold wind off the glacier, and when they finished, Quentin touched the lighter to the corner of the contract and held it until the fire reached his fingers, and let the ashes go over the side. The wind took them north, toward the ice. The water closed over the last fleck of red ink without a ripple.

"Well," Rosalind said. "That's that."

"That's that."

She picked up the radio handset and reported the survey complete, conditions nominal, returning to base. Her voice was steady and official and entirely her own, and Quentin listened to it and thought about the word *belonging*, which he'd spent a lifetime misunderstanding. He'd thought it was something you earned, like wages. Something you could be paid or cheated out of. But watching his sister log the position of a glacier, he understood it was simpler and harder than that. Belonging was just staying. Showing up tomorrow, and the day after, until the staying became the shape of your life and the people around you stopped being able to imagine the picture without you in it.

He was going to stay. That was the whole plan now, and it was the best plan he'd ever had.

* * *

They cleared the headland at noon with the front building behind them — a great bruised wall of cloud sliding off the peninsula, the pressure falling so fast his shoulder sang. By the time they reached open water the first flakes were coming down sideways, and Quentin found himself grinning into the wind like an idiot, because it was a *good* blow, an honest one, a storm with no men hidden inside it.

The radio crackled. The duty forecaster in Homer, voice thin with static: *"Skiff One-Niner, be advised, we've got a mesolow developing on your position — rapid cyclogenesis, possible bombogenesis inside six hours. Recommend you come south now. Over."*

Quentin keyed the mic. "One-Niner copies. Requesting permission to hold station and observe. We've got instruments aboard and a navigator who reads pressure fields better than your models do. Over."

Static. Then, dry: *"The director says you're a menace, Eriksen. The rest of us say bring back data. Over."*

Rosalind was already unclipping the barograph case, already clearing space on the bench, already — he noticed, and pretended not to — humming that old tune under her breath. Outside, the snow thickened and the sea darkened and the wind began to speak in the long vowels of a storm being born.

"One more thing," she said, looking up from the instrument. "Before it gets rough. I've been thinking about next season."

"Yeah?"

"When the ice goes out in spring. The service will want the fjord surveyed again. Baseline comparison." She paused, and he watched her choose her words the way she chose a course through a reef — carefully, with full knowledge of what lay under the surface. "We'll pass the cave again. Or where it was. And I want to know, before we're out here in the fog with the decision in front of us — are we leaving the legend alone? Both of us? Because I'll follow your lead on it, Quentin. But I need to hear you say it in calm water, not in a storm, when it's easy to talk yourself into shortcuts."

Quentin looked north one last time. Through the snow and the failing light he could just make out the glacier's white shoulder, and below it the dark seam of rock where eleven men had kept faith with a promise, and where the sea had kept faith with them, sealing the door against everyone who came after with gold in their eyes.

Some wrecks were best kept by the sea. He'd learned that the way you learn most true things — too late to save himself any trouble, and exactly in time to save what mattered.

"Leaving it alone," he said. "All seasons. All tides. That's the heading."

Rosalind nodded once, wrote it on the corner of her chart in her small precise hand — *FJORD CLOSED TO US, OPEN TO THE SEA* — and turned back to her instruments as the first real gust hit the skiff and the storm came down to meet them.

Quentin turned the bow south, into the weather, home.
