## 1. The Mood of the Water

The river was low, and low water told the truth about everything.

Ulyana Nystrom stood at the wheel of the Sable Queen with her boots planted wide and her weight forward on the balls of her feet, the way she'd stood for eleven years, and read the water like a ledger that couldn't lie. Where it ran smooth and green over the shallows ahead, there was sandbar. Where it wrinkled in long diagonal seams, current pushing against current, there was a bar buried just under the surface, waiting for a hull stupid enough to trust the channel markers. The markers themselves — three of them this season, leaning drunk where the spring ice had knocked them — were worth exactly what the River Authority had paid for them, which was nothing.

"Green water left of the willow point," she said.

Tamir repeated it back from the bow deck, voice carrying flat across the morning calm. He had his sounding pole out, tapping the bottom every few seconds, and each tap came back shallow — four feet, then three and a half, then a hollow *thock* that meant gravel bar and no mistake.

"Three and a quarter," Tamir called.

"Hold her center." Ulyana eased the wheel a hand's width to starboard, feeling the Sable Queen answer through the spokes the way a good horse answers the reins — not instantly, not eagerly, but honestly. Ninety tons of freight boat does not dance. She leans into your hands and tells you what she thinks, and if you've been listening long enough you can hear her thinking before she says it.

The bar slid past off the port side close enough that Ulyana could have spat on it. Behind the stern, the wake curled out lazy and brown, folding back on itself in the still air. Marta appeared at the galley door with two mugs of coffee, set one on the ledge by the wheel without a word, and went back inside. That was the whole conversation. Eleven years of mornings compressed into a mug set down at the right height.

Ulyana drank. The coffee was terrible. It was the best coffee on the river.

*Low water tells the truth.* Her father used to say that, back when he'd been alive and the Nystroms had run their own boat instead of somebody else's collateral. High water flatters you. High water floats you over bars you have no business crossing, lets you cut corners, makes you believe you're better than you are. Low water strips it all down. Low water says: here is what you actually know, here is what your boat actually draws, here is who you actually are when there's nothing between you and the bottom but judgment.

She was forty-one years old and she knew this river the way other women knew their own kitchens. She knew that the bend below Miller's Landing ate its outside bank a foot every spring flood and gave nothing back. She knew the snag lodged under the surface at Coyle's Eddy that the Authority had promised for six years to remove. She knew which landings would have grain waiting and which would have nothing but mud and a dog, and she knew the dog's name.

What she did not know — what she made a discipline of not knowing, the way a man with a bad tooth makes a discipline of not touching it — was the exact figure she owed Corvin Hale.

She knew the shape of it. She knew it had started at nine thousand crowns eleven years ago when she'd signed for the Sable Queen's refit after the fire took everything else, and she knew it had never once gone down, because the interest was calculated to make sure it never could. She knew Hale's clerks sent the statement quarterly, printed in that neat copperplate hand, and she knew the number grew by roughly the price of a good horse every ninety days whether she ran freight or sat frozen in at winter dock. She knew that last quarter's figure, glimpsed upside down across the clerk's desk, had begun with a two.

Two and something thousand. Tens of thousands. She hadn't let herself read the rest.

"You're doing the thing," Marta said, materializing beside her with the second mug now wrapped around her own knuckles. Marta Okafor was sixty-two years old, built like a root cellar, and had cooked on riverboats since before Ulyana was born. She did not look at the wheel. She looked at Ulyana's jaw.

"I'm steering."

"You're steering and doing the thing. The arithmetic face."

"The arithmetic face keeps us fed."

"The arithmetic face," Marta said, "is going to give you lines. You want to be a handsome old woman or a rich one? Can't afford both."

Ulyana almost smiled. Almost. Out on the bow, Tamir was working the pole through a long slow reach where the channel wandered between two gravel spits like a drunk walking home, and she watched him instead. Twenty-three years old, all elbows and ambition, with a way of standing at the rail that said he was measuring the distance to somewhere else. He'd been with her three years, signed on as deckhand when nobody else would take a boy with no references and a split lip, and he was good — quick, strong, learning the water faster than she'd learned it at his age. Which was the problem. Good men on the river either settled or left, and Tamir had leaving written on him the way the Sable Queen had her draft marks painted on the bow: plainly, and for everyone to read.

He'd find out soon enough that leaving cost money too. Everything did. That was the one lesson the river taught slower than any other.

* * *

They made Miller's Landing by midday, riding the last of the morning's current, and tied up against pilings furred with dried mud a foot above the waterline. Low water. The landing master, a stooped man named Grieves who had been old when Ulyana was young, came down the ramp with his ledger under his arm and his face already apologizing.

"Half your grain's not come down from the valley," he said, before she'd even got the gangplank dropped. "Cart broke an axle up at Hollis farm. Other half's here. Freight rate's the same, I'm told."

"It's the same," Ulyana said.

"That's robbery."

"That's the contract." She signed his book without reading it, because she'd read it once, years ago, and once had been enough. Then she stood at the rail while Tamir and the one hired roustabout Miller's Landing could produce swung the grain sacks aboard, and she did the arithmetic she'd sworn off doing, because half a load at full rate meant the Sable Queen would run this leg at a loss, and losses went onto the ledger, and the ledger went to Corvin Hale, and Corvin Hale —

Corvin Hale owned the boat.

That was the sentence she kept in a locked drawer at the back of her mind, and once a year or so it got out, and walked around, and she had to put it back. Eleven years ago the Sable Queen had been hers outright — her father's hull, her mother's money in the refit, her name on the papers. Then the fire. Then the debts behind the fire, the ones nobody talked about, the ones that had eaten the family savings and the insurance and finally the boat itself, sold not to Hale directly but through three layers of paper so clean and so legal that by the time Ulyana understood what had happened, she was crewing on her own vessel for wages, and the wages were calculated to keep her exactly where she was: running, always running, never gaining.

Hale had offered, once, early on, to sell her back equity. A share for every year of faithful service, he'd said, smiling with all the warmth of a man discussing weather. She'd thought about it for one night and understood it for what it was — a longer rope, tied more comfortably. Faithful service. As if the debt were a kindness. As if the leash were a gift.

"Captain?" Tamir, at the foot of the gangplank, pointing. "Man coming down. Says he needs passage."

Ulyana followed his gaze up the ramp. The man coming down was old the way driftwood is old — bleached, cracked, shaped by weather into something that had stopped being a tree. He walked with a stick he wasn't really using, more leaning on than walking with, and his coat hung on him like it belonged to a bigger man who'd died some time ago. His eyes were wrong. Bright and wet and fixed on the middle distance, seeing something that wasn't the landing.

"Fever," Marta said quietly, appearing at Ulyana's shoulder with the unerring instinct of cooks and grandmothers. "Look at him sweating in this cold. That's fever walking."

"Passage costs money," Ulyana called down, because it did, because the ledger existed, because sentiment was a luxury item and she'd been priced out of it a decade ago.

The old man stopped at the bottom of the plank. He looked up at her, and for a moment the brightness in his eyes focused, sharpened, became almost ordinary — a tired man looking at a boat.

"I got money," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "I got — I got more than money. I got a delivery needs making." He patted his coat, over the chest, where something flat and rectangular rode inside it. "Kestrel Gap. You going up?"

Nobody was going up. Kestrel Gap was three weeks' hard travel in the wrong direction, over passes that would be closing with the snows, and every soul on this stretch of river knew it. Ulyana opened her mouth to say so.

And then she looked at him properly. At the sweat freezing on his temples. At the way his hand trembled against his coat, not from cold. At the particular quality of his standing there — the absolute stillness of a man who has spent his last strength getting to this one spot, and has nothing left for anywhere else.

She had seen that stillness before. In a mirror, fifteen years ago, the night after the fire, standing in the road with ash in her hair and nowhere in the world to stand.

"Two crowns to the next landing," she heard herself say. "Deck passage. You sleep in the cargo hold where it's warm off the boiler."

Marta made a small sound beside her that in a younger woman would have been a laugh.

The old man fumbled at his coat and produced coins — actual coin, silver, more than two crowns' worth — and pressed them into Tamir's hand with a grip that didn't match the trembling. "Voss," he said. "Ede Voss. Much obliged, Captain. Much obliged." And he came up the plank slowly, one step at a time, like a man climbing out of a hole he'd been in a long while.

Ulyana turned back to the wheel. Below, Grieves was already shouting the departure call, and Tamir was casting off, and the current took the Sable Queen's bow and swung her downstream the way it always did, patient and enormous and indifferent to ledgers.

She loved the river. That was the truth underneath everything else, the one clean fact in a dirty stack of them. Men had taken her parents, her home, her name on the papers, her future — piece by piece, year by year, legally, with signatures and witnesses. But the river belonged to nobody. The river could not be held as collateral. Every morning it rose under her hull and carried her whether Hale liked it or not, and every night it asked nothing of her except attention, and attention was the one payment she could always make.

It was the only thing they hadn't figured out how to take from her.

She told herself that, standing at the wheel with the terrible coffee cooling at her elbow, and almost believed it.

* * *

They ran the Narrows at midafternoon, because the light was right and the water was dropping and tomorrow it would drop further. The Narrows in low water was a mile of channel barely wider than the boat herself, threading between bars that shifted with every freshet, and most captains waited for rain or paid a pilot. Ulyana had run it forty times. She ran it now with the wheel moving in small constant corrections, a conversation rather than a command, and the Sable Queen slipped through with six inches under her keel where another boat would have grounded, and the hired roustabout crossed himself, and Tamir watched her hands with an expression she recognized because she'd worn it once, at his age, watching an old pilot named Brandt do the same thing.

That expression was the closest thing to an heir she would ever have, and it lived on a boy who was going to leave her.

Downstream of the Narrows the river widened, relaxed, went golden with the late sun. Ulyana set Tamir at the wheel — "small corrections, feel her, don't fight her" — and walked the deck to clear her head, past the stacked grain, past the hold where the old man Voss lay rolled in a tarp near the boiler with his coat still buttoned over whatever flat thing he carried, breathing in the fast shallow rhythm of a man burning up from inside.

Marta met her at the rail with supper bread and no coffee. "He asked me for paper," she said. "And ink. Said he had a letter to finish before—" She shrugged. "Before. You know."

"Did he finish it?"

"He's finishing it. Writing like the words cost him blood." Marta tore her bread in half and handed half over, an old ritual, neither of them acknowledging it anymore. "You know who he is, Captain?"

"Should I?"

"Ede Voss. Prospector. There was trouble up in the Kestrel country years back — him and two partners, the Aldous brothers. Big strike, bigger falling-out. He sold his rights cheap or had them taken, depending who tells it, and the Aldouses went on to be mine owners and he went on to be that." She nodded at the hold. "Twenty years of that."

Ulyana chewed and said nothing. On the river you learned quickly that everyone's history arrived eventually, and the wise thing was to let it arrive rather than chase it.

"And now he wants to go to Kestrel Gap," she said at last. "Where the daughter is. Or the partners are."

"Both, I'd guess." Marta brushed crumbs from her apron. "Captain. I've cooked on boats thirty years and I'll tell you what I know, which is only what I see. That man is dying, and he knows it, and he isn't afraid of it, and that frightens me worse than fear would. Men who aren't afraid of dying are men who've got something left to do that matters more." She looked out at the water, gold going to copper. "Whatever's in that coat, it isn't a letter."

The sun went down behind the western bluffs and the river turned the color of iron, and Ulyana stood her evening watch with the wheel speaking softly in her hands and the lights of no town anywhere ahead of them, just water and dark and the far-off hills where the passes waited with their early snows.

Near midnight, Tamir came aft to relieve her and found her still at the wheel, though the channel was straight and easy and a child could have held it.

"Captain," he said. "There's riders. On the south bank. Been keeping pace with us since the Narrows — I saw their lantern twice."

Ulyana looked. The bank was a black seam against blacker sky. For a long moment there was nothing.

Then, briefly, a lantern flickered among the trees — two lights, side by side, unhurried, keeping pace.

Riders don't follow a freight boat for miles in the dark out of curiosity. She turned the wheel over to Tamir and told herself it was probably nothing, the way you tell yourself a wrinkle in the water is probably wind.

Probably wind. Probably nothing.

But she slept that night in the pilothouse chair, boots on, with the river talking to itself below the hull, and the old man's fever-breath rising faint from the hold, and somewhere upstream in the dark, a debt she didn't yet know she owed began quietly coming downriver to collect.
