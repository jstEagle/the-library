## 2. A Stranger at the Landing

The landing at Coyle's Bend was less a landing than a wound in the riverbank — a place where the mud had given up pretending to be solid and simply surrendered to the water. Ulyana brought the *Sable Queen* in against the current the way she always did, bow first, engine reversed to a mutter, and let the hull kiss the pilings soft enough that Tamir could step off with a line without jarring his teeth.

"Watch the starboard fender," she called down. "It's riding loose."

"It's been riding loose since June," Tamir said, but he looped the line anyway.

She didn't answer. There wasn't time in her days for answers to things she already knew. The *Sable Queen* had forty minutes at Coyle's Bend — long enough to take on cordwood, short enough that Corvin Hale's schedule stayed intact, because Hale's schedule was the one thing on this boat that wasn't hers, and she guarded it with the same ferocity she'd have used guarding her own name, if her name were still worth anything.

Coyle's Bend in late September smelled like every landing on this stretch of river: wet clay, split pine, woodsmoke from the shanties, and under all of it the sweet rot of things left too long in the water. A dozen men waited on the bank with crates of pelts and sacks of ore samples, and Ulyana stood at the rail doing what she did at every landing — counting them, weighing them, deciding which ones would pay and which ones would plead.

It was the pleading ones she watched for. Not because she liked them. Because she'd been one.

* * *

He came out of the tree line an hour before they were due to cast off, which should have been her first warning. Men who arrive early at landings are either desperate or dishonest, and the two are not so different as respectable people like to believe.

Ulyana saw him first from the wheelhouse — a figure moving down the slope through the alders with the particular gait of a man whose legs had stopped consulting him about where they were going. He fell twice before he reached the mud flat. The second time he stayed down long enough that she set down her coffee and marked his position against the pilings, calculating whether he'd drift into the channel if the river took him.

He got up. He kept coming.

By the time she reached the main deck he was at the gangplank, and she had him whole: sixty years old if he was fifty-five, beard gone gray at the chin and yellow at the mustache from tobacco, clothes that had been good gear once — oiled canvas, brass buttons, the kind of coat a man buys when he means to live outdoors for years and does. The coat was ruined now. Everything under it looked ruined too. His eyes had the shine she knew too well, fever polished to a brightness that looked almost like health from across a room and was nothing like health up close.

"Passage," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through water. "Downriver. I'll pay."

"We're not going downriver," Ulyana said. "We're going up."

Something crossed his face then — not disappointment exactly. Recalculation. She watched him try to work out how many days he had left in his body and whether any direction would serve, and she recognized the arithmetic because she'd done it herself once, eleven years ago, standing on a dock in Vellmark with four coins and a debt that grew faster than the interest tables said it should.

"I'll pay," he said again, as though repetition were currency. He dug in his coat and produced a pouch, worked the drawstring with fingers that wouldn't quite obey, and spilled a little fan of dust gold and flakes onto his palm. Enough to matter. Not enough to matter much.

Ulyana looked at the gold, then at his face. Up close the fever had a smell — sour sweat and something underneath it, something going wrong in the blood. She'd cooked for a crew once, her first season, under a captain who never turned anyone away, and she'd learned to read sickness the way other people read weather.

"When did you last eat?" she asked.

He blinked. The question had come from somewhere outside the transaction and he needed a moment to find its shelf. "I don't— there was a trout. Day before yesterday. Maybe three."

"And you're asking me to carry you upriver in that condition."

"I'm asking you to carry me anywhere." He closed his hand over the gold. It seemed to cost him something, closing it — as if letting go of even the gesture of payment might undo whatever dignity the offer had bought him. "Name's Voss. Ede Voss. I'm told you're Nystrom. The captain." He swayed, caught himself on the gangplank rail. "They say you don't turn people off your boat."

"They say wrong," Ulyana said. "I've turned off plenty."

"Not people who paid."

"People who paid I carry. That's not charity, that's freight."

He smiled at that, and the smile was worse than the fever-shine, because behind it was a man trying to be charming while his body quietly closed its accounts. "Then I'm paying," he said. "Whatever you're charging. Whatever direction. I just need—" He stopped. Looked past her, at the boat, at the smoke rising from Marta's galley pipe, at the stacked cordwood and the coiled lines and the plain honest clutter of a working vessel. Something in his face went young for a moment, or maybe just unguarded. "I need to be moving," he finished. "You understand that. A man can't stop. Not out here. You understand."

She understood. That was the trouble.

* * *

Marta found her ten minutes later at the rail, arms crossed, watching Ede Voss sit on a crate by the pilings because sitting was apparently all he could manage until the boat decided something.

"You're going to take him," Marta said. It wasn't a question. Marta's questions were rhetorical; her statements were the real interrogations.

"He's paying."

"He's dying."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

"No," Marta agreed. She was a broad woman built like a stove, with forearms that had hauled more pots than most men hauled traps, and she had a way of standing beside Ulyana that made conversations feel like inspections. "And when he dies aboard, whose problem is he?"

"The river's."

"That's cold, even for you."

Ulyana didn't answer right away. Below, Voss had begun coughing — deep, wet, structural. Tamir glanced over from the cordwood, then away, the way young men do when mortality gets between them and their work.

"Eleven years ago," Ulyana said, "I stood on a dock in Vellmark with nothing. You know that story."

"I know the version you tell."

"There isn't another version." She said it evenly, and Marta had the grace not to look at her. "A man named Ostergaard took me on. No references, no coin, sick half out of my mind from the crossing. Everyone told him I'd rob him blind inside a month. He said — I remember the exact words — he said, 'The river sorts them. My job is just to keep them afloat till it does.'" She watched Voss wipe his mouth with the back of his hand. "Ostergaard drowned three years later. But I keep thinking about the sorting. About how much of it happens before the river ever gets its chance."

"So it's habit," Marta said.

"It's habit."

"Not kindness."

"Kindness is expensive." Ulyana pushed off the rail. "Habit's free. Tell him he can have the bunk aft of the galley. And feed him something with fat in it — he needs the calories more than the flavor. We cast off in twenty minutes."

Marta studied her a moment longer, the way she did when she suspected there was more on the table than had been served. Then she went below, and her voice carried up the companionway, dry as kindling: "Well, come on then, old man. Let's see if you can eat without drowning."

* * *

He ate like a man who'd forgotten food was possible and remembered mid-bite. Marta put salt pork and barley in front of him and he demolished it with a focus that excluded everything else — the boat, the river, the two of them watching — and when the bowl was empty he sat back and looked around the galley as if seeing it for the first time, and Ulyana saw the moment the warmth hit him, the stove-heat and lamp-oil and the small human orderliness of a vessel's heart, and his eyes filled and he turned his face to the bulkhead and pretended it hadn't happened.

She pretended along with him. That was courtesy, out here.

"Voss," she said, to give him something to hold. "That's a north-country name."

"Far enough north the snow doesn't bother leaving." He wiped his eyes with his sleeve, briskly, businesslike. "I had a place up in the Kestrels. Twenty years, off and on. Prospecting." He said the word the way some men say a wife's name — with history in it, and grievance, and something tender underneath both.

"Any luck?"

"Luck." He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and he rode it out with his fist against his chest. "There's a question. Ask me if I had luck and I'll tell you about the winter of — no. Never mind. Doesn't matter now." He looked at her, and the fever-brightness steadied into something else, something deliberate, and Ulyana felt the deck shift under the conversation the way it sometimes shifted under the hull when the current changed without warning. "Captain Nystrom. Why'd you take me on? Truly."

"Habit."

"Habit." He weighed the word. "Men your age don't have habits yet. Habits take decades. What I see in you is a woman who's already spent her decades, just fast." He held up a hand before she could decide whether to be insulted. "No offense meant. I read people. It's how I've stayed alive this long, reading what's under what's offered. Under your 'habit' there's something else. Recognition, maybe."

"You're feverish," Ulyana said. "Everything looks familiar when you're feverish."

He grinned — and it was a good grin, crooked, self-aware, the grin of a man who'd talked his way out of trouble in a hundred camps and intended to talk his way out of this one too, even if the trouble was death itself. "Maybe so. Maybe so." He settled back against the bulkhead. "Either way, I'm obliged to you. And I pay my obligations, Captain. Every one. That's the only ledger I've ever kept clean."

Somewhere above them, Tamir's boots crossed the deck, quick and light. The engine ticked as it cooled. Through the galley's little porthole Ulyana could see the last light going amber on the water, and she thought about casting off, about the schedule, about Hale's ledger and the numbers in it that never got smaller no matter how hard she ran the boat — and she thought, briefly, unaccountably, about Ostergaard on his dock, saying *my job is just to keep them afloat.*

"Ede," she said — using his first name without deciding to, the way you take a stranger's hand in bad footing. "Where were you trying to go? Before you washed up at my plank. Where was 'anywhere'?"

He was quiet a long moment. The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the companionway and stood back up.

"Kestrel Gap," he said finally. "Up in the Kestrel Range. Before the passes close." And then, softer, almost to himself, with a look on his face she couldn't read and wouldn't try to: "There's someone there I owe more than gold."

* * *

That night, moored at the bend below the rapids, Ulyana stood her usual watch alone in the wheelhouse. The river ran black and silver under a half moon. In the bunk aft of the galley, Ede Voss coughed himself toward sleep in fits and starts, and twice she heard Marta's low voice through the deck plating, saying something she couldn't make out, and once she heard him answer — one word, repeated, like a man practicing a name he hadn't spoken aloud in twenty years.

*Ingrid,* he said. *Ingrid.*

Ulyana turned her collar up against the draft and looked north, upriver, toward the dark line of the foothills where the Kestrels would be, weeks away and in entirely the wrong direction, and she told herself what she always told herself at landings: *not your cargo, not your route, not your promise.* The river sorted people. Her job was just to keep them afloat until it did.

Below her feet, the old man slept, and dreamed, and muttered, and the compass of the night — the stars, the current, the pull of things owed — began quietly turning toward a bearing she had no intention of following.
