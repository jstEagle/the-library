## 3. What Ede Carried

Tamir found the bundle on the second night, and he would spend the rest of his life pretending he hadn't gone looking for it.

That was the lie he settled on first: that it had simply been there, in the old man's gear, when Tamir went below to fetch the spare blankets because Ede's fever had him shivering through the canvas of his bunk like a man caught in a current. A reasonable errand. A kind one, even. Nobody could fault a man for fetching blankets.

But the truth was that Tamir had been curious since the landing. Curious was a kind word for it too.

* * *

The Sable Queen ran dark that night, tied off at a willow bank twenty miles below the landing, because Captain Nystrom didn't trust the channel after sundown in low water and Tamir had learned not to argue with her about water. She'd been born to this river or the river had been born to her; either way, the argument always ended the same, with Marta laughing into her stew and Tamir feeling about nine years old.

Ede Voss lay in the forward bunk they'd cleared for him, under two blankets and Marta's third, sweating and muttering. The fever had come up fast since the afternoon, and now it had him talking — not to anyone in particular, but to the low ceiling of the cabin, to the river, to somebody who wasn't there.

"Silver," he said. "You don't understand. Not a seam. A *lode*. Wide as a wagon road and it runs clean as a sermon."

Tamir sat on the bench across from him, mending a lamp wick he didn't need to mend, listening the way you listen to a drunk or a dying man — half pity, half hunger.

"They'll say I'm mad." Ede's eyes were open now, fixed on nothing. "Aldous said mad. Both of them, standing there with my paper in their hands, telling me the assays were wrong, telling me the vein pinched out. Pinched out." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him double, and Tamir leaned over and held the tin cup to his lips until the coughing passed. The old man's hand was hot as a stove plate. "Watered-down ore samples. I watched them do it, boy. Watched them salt the poor rock and bury the good. And then they bought my share for a mule and a sack of flour, and I signed, because a starving man signs."

"You should have fought it," Tamir said. It was the wrong thing to say and he knew it before it left his mouth.

"Fought it with what?" Ede looked at him then, and his eyes were clear for a moment, terribly clear, the way fever sometimes burns everything else away. "With what, boy? With what?"

Tamir had no answer. He went back to the wick.

The old man drifted again, and the talking changed course, the way river talk does, sliding from one channel into another. "Ingrid," he said. "Ingrid Voss. Kestrel Gap. She'd be — how old now — twenty-six. Twenty-seven." His voice cracked on the number. "She has her mother's chin. Marta's chin — no, her mother's, her mother *was* —" He lost the thread, found it again. "I left her a hatbox of letters once. Never sent them. Couldn't spell half what I wanted to say."

Tamir set down the wick.

He thought of his own father, who had left in a way that was quieter than Ede's but no less complete — walked out of a house in Corvane with a carpetbag and a promise to send for them, and sent nothing for eleven years except one letter with money folded inside and no return address. Tamir knew exactly what kind of arithmetic a daughter did with an absent father. He knew how the sum never came out even, no matter how many times you ran it.

"She hates me," Ede said, almost peacefully. "Good. Hate means she remembers."

* * *

Marta came down near midnight with willow bark tea and took over the watch without being asked, which was how things worked on the Sable Queen — nobody assigned anything, things just got done, and if you were the one who noticed them undone, they were yours. Tamir climbed the ladder to the deck to give the sickroom air and stood at the rail watching the river run black and silver under a half moon.

He was twenty-three years old and he had been on this boat four years, and he could tell you to the penny what he'd saved: forty-one dollars and sixty cents. He kept the figure the way other men kept scripture. Forty-one sixty against a berth on a steamer east, against a room of his own, against a life where nobody told him when to eat or where to stand or that the deck needed scrubbing before the captain woke. Four years of hauling line and coiling rope and eating Marta's beans, and he was still closer to the beginning than the end.

It wasn't that Ulyana Nystrom was cruel. She wasn't. She paid fair, fed better than fair, and had pulled him out of the Corvane gutter when every other door had shown him its back. But a boat is a small world, and he could see the whole of his future from where he stood: ten more years of this deck, twenty, and then maybe a boat of his own if the river gods smiled, which they mostly didn't, and in the meantime the banks slid past like pages of a book someone else was reading.

Forty-one sixty. He turned the number over the way you turn a stone looking for what's underneath.

Below him, faintly, Ede was still talking.

* * *

He told himself he went down for the blankets. That was the story, and by the third retelling — to himself, in the dark of the forehold, where the passengers' gear was stowed — it would be nearly true.

Ede's gear was one canvas duffel, soft with age, and a leather satchel that the old man had carried against his chest at the landing like a man carrying a child through a crowd. The satchel sat now at the foot of the spare bunk where Marta had stowed it, and Tamir stood over it for a long moment with the blanket rolled under his arm, listening to the fever-talk from the main cabin and the river slapping the hull, and hating himself in advance for what he was about to do, and doing it anyway.

The buckle was brass and stiff. It gave with a click that sounded enormous.

Inside: a shirt, rolled. A tin of tobacco, nearly empty. A Bible with a broken spine. And beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, a flat bundle the size of a hymnal, heavy in a way that paper shouldn't be heavy.

Tamir untied the cord. His fingers were steady, which surprised him. Inside the oilcloth were two things.

The first was a map. Not a surveyor's map — something older, drawn in ink gone brown, on paper backed with linen, creased so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth. Even in the dim lantern light spilling from the main cabin he could see the confidence of the lines: a river he didn't know, a range of mountains rendered in quick sure strokes, and marked in red — a mark like a wound, pressed hard enough to score the paper — a place where the lines converged. Words in a cramped hand ran along the margin. He couldn't read them all. He read three: *the lode runs*.

The second thing was a compass.

He'd seen a hundred compasses. This wasn't shaped like any of them. It was brass, palm-sized, but octagonal rather than round, its faces worn bright at the edges as if handled daily for decades, and on the reverse someone had engraved a single word in letters so small he had to hold it to the lantern: *BEARING*. When he tilted it, the needle swung — lazily, then with strange insistence — and settled pointing somewhere between north and northeast, quivering at the edge of the card like a dog scenting something on the wind.

Northeast. Toward the mountains. Toward the red mark on the map, if he was reading the geography right, and he was fairly sure he was, because Tamir had spent four years learning water and the country that made it, and he knew where the high country began.

His heart was going hard. He wrapped both items back in the oilcloth with hands that finally, finally shook, retied the cord, buckled the satchel, and stood there in the dark holding the blanket he'd come for, doing arithmetic.

A lode wide as a wagon road. Silver. The Aldous brothers had cheated a dying man out of it once already, which meant it was real enough to cheat for. Real enough to kill for, maybe — though Tamir pushed that thought to the edge of the table and refused to look at it directly. Forty-one dollars and sixty cents against a mountain of silver. Against a map that could buy a steamer, a fleet, a life with no one's name on the door but his own.

And against a promise, whispered across a fever, that the map belonged to a girl in Kestrel Gap who hated her father and had every right to.

He was still standing there when Ede's voice rose sharply from the main cabin — not words, just a cry, the sound a man makes falling — and Tamir stuffed the blanket impulse aside and went to him, and helped Marta hold the old man down through the worst of it, and felt the satchel's weight in his memory the whole time, sitting ten feet away, patient as a debt.

* * *

Near dawn the fever broke, or seemed to. Ede lay gray and quiet, breathing in shallow pulls, and Marta dozed upright in the chair, and Tamir went up on deck because he couldn't stand the smell of camphor and sweat another minute.

Ulyana was at the wheel even though the boat was tied, standing with her hands resting on the spokes the way she did when she wasn't steering but couldn't stop steering. She didn't look at him when he came alongside. She rarely did, right away. You had to earn her eyes.

"How is he?" she asked the river.

"Worse. Then better. Then worse again." Tamir put his hands in his pockets so she wouldn't see them fidget. "Captain — can I ask you something?"

"You just did."

"What would you do with a thing that wasn't yours, but everybody who owned it was dead or gone?"

Now she looked at him. Her eyes were pale river-gray and missed nothing, and Tamir felt the satchel's weight shift in his conscience like cargo settling in a hold. "Depends," she said. "Was it given to me, or did I take it?"

"Given," he said, too fast. "Hypothetically given."

"Hypothetically." She turned back to the water. "Then I'd carry it where it was supposed to go, and sleep fine. Things get heavy when you set them down in the wrong place, Tamir. Doesn't matter how light they looked when you picked them up."

He nodded as if this were wisdom instead of a warning, and went forward to check the lines, and told himself the conversation meant nothing, and knew it meant everything, because she had looked at him a half second too long at the word *given*, and Ulyana Nystrom did not miss things.

* * *

The fever came back with the sun, worse than before, burning through the old man like grass fire. By midmorning he was lucid in flashes — terrible lucidity, the kind that spends itself fast — and between the flashes he talked, and this time he talked to Ulyana, who sat by his bunk with the patience of stone while the Sable Queen rocked at her moorings and the day got away from all of them.

But it was late afternoon, with Marta below spooning broth into him and Ulyana up on deck arguing with the sky about weather, that Ede's hand shot out and caught Tamir's wrist with a strength that had no business living in it.

"The satchel," the old man rasped. "You found it."

Tamir's mouth went dry. "I —"

"Don't." Ede's grip tightened. "Don't insult us both. Fever talks, boy, but fever also *hears*. I heard you down here last night. Heard the buckle." His eyes were bright and awful and amused, all at once. "Good. Better you than a stranger. Now listen, because I'm only going to have the wind for this once."

"I didn't take anything," Tamir said, which was true, and also the smallest possible truth, a truth shrunk down to fit through a keyhole.

"No. You didn't. That's the interesting part." The old man smiled with cracked lips. "Most men would've. Most men *did*, once — men I trusted with my life and my ledger both. You looked, and you put it back, and you came and held the cup to my mouth after. I've been wrong about men my whole life, Tamir-who-hears-buckles. Maybe I'm owed one right guess at the end."

"I'm not —"

"Kestrel Gap." The grip tightened again, urgent now, the lucidity guttering. "Ingrid Voss. Before the snow closes the passes, or it dies with me twice. Swear it. Swear it on whatever you've got, boy, because I haven't got long and the captain's got a boat to think of, and boats are honest things but captains are people, and people hesitate."

From above came the creak of the wheel and Ulyana's voice, low, giving some order to the river that the river ignored.

"And if I won't swear?" Tamir asked quietly. It was the bravest thing he'd ever said, and it cost him everything he had.

Ede Voss studied him for a long moment, and then he laughed — softly, so Marta wouldn't hear — and let go of his wrist, and sank back into the pillow looking suddenly, enormously tired.

"Then you'll do it anyway," he said. "I saw your face when you read the margin. Nobody looks at a fortune like that and walks away clean. You're already carrying it, boy. Question is whether you carry it honest."

He closed his eyes. Within minutes he was asleep, or past sleep, breathing in that thin torn way that Tamir had heard once before, from his grandfather, three days before the end.

Tamir went up on deck. The evening was coming down gold and cold off the water, and Ulyana stood at the wheel looking north — or what passed for north on this crooked stretch — toward the distant blue wall of the mountains, toward Kestrel Gap, toward everything the Sable Queen was not built for and every mile that led away from the only world Tamir had.

He thought about the compass in its oilcloth, the needle swinging off true, insisting on northeast.

He thought about forty-one dollars and sixty cents.

He thought: *she'll say no.*

And then, watching the captain's face as she stared at the high country — watching something move behind those gray eyes that he'd never seen there before, something that looked almost like fear — he thought:

*Or maybe she won't.*
