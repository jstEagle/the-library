## 4. The Dying Trade

The fever broke at midnight and came back with the dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern bluffs Ede Voss was talking to people who weren't in the room.

Ulyana heard him through the bulkhead of the small aft cabin — the one she'd given up without deciding to, the way you give up things when someone is dying and there's no argument worth having about it. He was arguing with his daughter. Or a woman he called Ingrid, which might have been his daughter or might have been the fever wearing her name like a borrowed coat.

"You don't get to be angry," he said. "You don't — I carried it. Twenty years, girl. You think I didn't carry it?"

Marta was already in the galley when Ulyana came down, heating water over the little iron stove, and Marta looked up with the expression she used instead of saying *he's worse*.

"How much laudanum left?" Ulyana asked.

"Enough for two bad nights. Three if we're liars about the doses."

"Be liars."

"He'll hate that."

"He's past hating anything but the fever." Ulyana took the tin cup Marta handed her — willow bark, bitter as regret — and stood a moment in the galley's warmth, listening to the river slap the hull. The Sable Queen lay tied at a private mooring above the Narrows, water low enough that she'd grounded twice yesterday and Ulyana had walked her off both bars by feel alone, standing at the wheel with her eyes half shut, reading the current through her boots like a blind woman reads a face. She was good at this. It was the one thing she had never had to pretend to be good at.

She climbed back up and went into the cabin.

Ede Voss looked smaller than he had three days ago, as if the fever were rendering him down, boiling away everything that wasn't essential. What remained was mostly bone and beard and a pair of eyes too bright for the face around them. His gear — what Tamir hadn't gone through, and Ulyana still hadn't decided how she felt about that — sat in the corner under an oilskin, and Ulyana found herself not looking at it, deliberately, the way you don't look at a card game you've been invited into by men who cheat.

"There she is." Ede's voice had gravel in it. "Captain Nystrom."

"Captain of nothing much," Ulyana said. "Drink this."

He drank. Some of it. Then he pushed the cup away with a hand that shook and fixed her with those bright eyes, and Ulyana understood, with the particular dread of a woman who has spent years avoiding conversations, that the conversation had arrived.

"I know what you're doing," he said.

"Getting you well."

"No." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "You're being kind at me. There's a difference. Kindness is a thing you do. Being kind *at* someone is a wall you build so they can't ask you for anything real." He coughed, wet and long, and Marta appeared in the doorway with a cloth and Ulyana waved her off because Ede gripped her wrist with surprising strength and said, "Sit down, Captain. I haven't got the strength to ask twice."

She sat.

* * *

Outside, the river ran low and gold in the morning light. Inside, the cabin smelled of camphor and sickness, and Ede Voss talked for a long time in a voice that kept fraying and being mended.

He told her about the claim. Not the silver itself — he was careful about that, careful the way a man is careful who has learned that everyone wants what he has — but the shape of it. A lode found twenty-two years ago in the high country above Kestrel Gap, staked legal and true, worked for four seasons with two partners whose names he spat like seeds. The Aldouses. He told her how they'd sold their shares out from under him to a syndicate that existed on paper only, how the paperwork had been doctored, how he'd spent six years and every coin he had fighting it in courts that sat a thousand miles away and spoke a language made entirely of money. How he'd lost. How he'd gone back anyway, alone, and found the vein again where it dipped under land nobody thought worth claiming, and mapped it. Every shaft, every assay, every bearing — drawn in his own hand on paper that would outlive him.

"And the Aldouses know," he said. "They always knew I had it. That's why they sent men after me in Darrow's Landing. That's why I came to your boat looking like something the river coughed up. Captain — I didn't choose you. I chose the only deck in reach."

"That's honest," Ulyana said. "Most people dress it up."

"I'm done dressing things up. It's the dressing-up that killed me." He turned his head on the pillow and looked at her, and there was something in the look that made her want to stand up and leave, and she stayed seated because leaving would have confirmed whatever he saw. "I'm going to die on this boat. Probably tomorrow. Maybe tonight if the fever has opinions. I've made my peace with the dying. It's the other thing I can't make peace with."

"The map."

"The map." He closed his eyes. "Ingrid. My daughter. Kestrel Gap. She'll tell you herself she hasn't got a father — she's right, she hasn't, I spent the inheritance of her childhood on a hole in the ground. But she's the only one left who carries my name, and the map is the only thing left that carries mine, and I want them put back together before the snow does what the Aldouses couldn't." His eyes opened again. "Carry it to her. Before the passes close. That's all. That's the whole of what I'm asking."

Ulyana looked at the porthole. The river slid by, brown and patient, going south.

"Kestrel Gap is eleven days' hard travel from the nearest landing," she said. "Overland. Through country I don't know, in a season that's coming early — ask any trapper, they'll tell you the snows are weeks ahead of themselves. And it's the wrong direction. Everything I owe is south. My route, my freight contracts, my—" She stopped. She had been about to say *my life*, and it was too true to say out loud to a dying man.

"Your debt," Ede said. "Hale."

Her head came around. "How do you know that name?"

"Because Hale's name gets attached to everything between here and the sea, the way barnacles attach to a hull. Because the Aldouses drink with him. Because a man dying in your cabin hears the crew talk." He smiled without warmth. "And because you flinched just now like I'd pressed a bruise."

Ulyana stood up. It was reflex more than decision — the body leaving before the mind agreed — and she found herself at the porthole with her back to him, watching the water, hating the view because it gave her nothing to say.

"Captain Nystrom." His voice was quieter now. The fever was spending him. "I'm not asking you to save me. Nobody can save me. I'm asking you to carry a thing. It weighs less than a pound. It's the heaviest thing I own."

"Why me?" She said it to the glass. "You could pay anyone. Any courier, any—"

"Because couriers can be bought and I have nothing left to buy them with. Because you refused my money at the landing, which means either you're honest or you're proud, and I've watched you three days, and it's both, and both will do." A pause, filled with labored breathing. "And because when Tamir found the bundle — yes, I know, I was awake more than you thought — you didn't open it. You put it back and you didn't ask me a single question about it, and a woman who doesn't open other people's bundles is a woman who keeps what she's given."

"I keep promises badly," Ulyana said. It came out flat and fast, the truth arriving ahead of her discretion. "That's the thing you should know about me. I don't lie well and I don't keep promises well. Ask anyone who's lent me money."

Silence behind her. When Ede spoke again his voice had changed — thinner, but steadier, as if the request had given him something to brace against.

"Do you know what I did for twenty years?" he said. "Every night, in every camp, I told myself: next season. Next season I'll go north, I'll find her, I'll put the map in her hands and take whatever she says to me, even if it's spit. And every season the vein needed working, or the money ran short, or the passes were closing, or — it was always something, Captain. The mountain always gives you a reason. And now I'm here, and the reason finally ran out, and I'm asking a stranger to do the one thing I couldn't do because I was busy staying alive." He laughed, and it cost him. "Staying alive. As if that were the assignment."

Ulyana turned around.

He had propped himself up against the bulkhead, and in the grey light through the porthole he looked carved rather than sick, all edges, a man reduced to his outline. On the shelf beside the bunk lay the bundle under its oilskin, and beside it the brass compass Tamir had described — though Ulyana had not yet held it, had not let herself hold it.

"It's not treasure I'm giving her," Ede said. "Or not only. It's proof. Of what was taken. Of what I was, before I was nothing." His hand moved toward the bundle and stopped, trembling, an inch short. "But I won't put it in your hands unless you say the words. I'm old enough to know that a thing given without a promise is a thing that gets set down somewhere and forgotten. Say you'll carry it, or say no, and I'll respect the no. But don't say maybe. Maybe is what killed me."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere forward, Tamir was coiling line, the rope hissing through his hands, the ordinary sound of an ordinary morning on a boat that owed Corvin Hale nine hundred marks and eleven days of grace, and Ulyana stood in the small hot cabin and felt the whole shape of her life press in around her like water against a seam.

Here was the arithmetic, and she ran it the way she ran a bar channel, honestly: she could not afford this. Eleven days overland meant missed contracts, meant penalties, meant Hale's men waiting at the winter landings with their little books and their little smiles. It might mean the Sable Queen herself. And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, was the thing she never said even to herself: that she did not trust her own word. That a promise from Ulyana Nystrom was a note drawn on a bank that had burned down once already. That some part of her believed she was the kind of person who would carry this man's map exactly as far as it was convenient, and then set it down somewhere and call it fate.

And here was the other arithmetic, the one she hated: a man was dying ten feet away, and he had looked at every soul on this river, and he had chosen the one who flinched.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "I say it whole. Not best efforts. Not weather permitting. If the passes close, we're on the wrong side of them, and I don't come home, and neither does my crew, and I'll have lost my boat for a dead man's errand. You're asking me to bet everything I have on a promise I've already told you I keep badly."

"Yes," Ede said simply.

"And if I fail? If the snow takes us, or the Aldouses—"

"Then you'll have failed carrying it toward her," he said. "Which is a different thing than failing to start." He reached out again, and this time his hand closed on the oilskin, and he drew the bundle onto the blanket between them, and Ulyana saw the corner of heavy paper beneath the wrapping and the dull brass gleam of the compass, and felt something in her chest lean toward it the way a compass needle leans, treacherously, toward anything metal.

"Say the words, Captain."

She looked at him for a long moment. Fever-bright eyes. Bone and beard. A man who had spent twenty years finding reasons and had finally run out, and was handing the last of himself to a stranger because strangers were all he had left.

"I'll carry it to Ingrid Voss in Kestrel Gap," Ulyana said, and heard her own voice go quiet and formal, the voice she used for vows she meant, of which this was, she realized, the first in fifteen years. "Before the passes close. Whole, or not at all. On my name."

Something went out of Ede then — not the fever, unfortunately, but the tension, the twenty-year clench of it — and he sagged back against the bulkhead like a man setting down a load at the top of a stair.

"On your name," he repeated. "Good name. Hard name. Names like that hold a promise better than soft ones." He pushed the bundle across the blanket with two fingers, an effort that visibly emptied him. "Take it. And the compass goes with it — that's not sentiment, that's instruction. Paper lies when the wind's wrong; the needle doesn't. It was my father's. It's pointed me home twice when I had no business getting home." His mouth twisted. "Third time's yours."

Ulyana picked up the bundle. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. She tucked it inside her coat, against her ribs, where she'd feel it with every breath, and only then noticed that the compass had been wrapped separately, and that Ede was holding it out to her now on his open palm, the brass worn bright along one edge by decades of a thumb worrying it.

"This too?"

"That too. A promise needs an instrument." His eyes were losing focus, the fever reclaiming him, but his hand stayed steady. "Check it against the sky tonight. Tell me what you see."

She took it. It was warm from his skin, absurdly warm, and heavier than it looked, and she slipped the loop of it over her head and felt it settle against her sternum like a small cold heart.

"Ede," she said. "Rest now. I've got it."

He was already drifting. "Kestrel Gap," he murmured. "Tell her the lode runs deeper than the shame. Tell her—" But the sentence dissolved, and his breathing lengthened, and Marta appeared in the doorway with the laudanum and the liar's dose, and Ulyana stepped out into the morning.

On deck, the light was hard and clean off the water. South, the river ran toward the landings, the contracts, the debt — toward everything with her name written on it in other people's books. North, past the bluffs, the country rose into foothills already whitening at the shoulders, going up and up into ranges she'd only ever seen from the safety of the water, ranges that would be closed by snow within the month.

Tamir straightened from his coiling and looked at her, and then at the coat over her ribs, and said nothing, which was its own kind of question.

"Change of heading," Ulyana said. "We'll need provisions for twelve days overland, and we'll need them quietly."

Tamir's eyebrows went up. "Overland? Captain, the passes—"

"I know where the passes are, Tamir." She touched the compass through her coat, feeling its small weight swing against her breastbone, and looked north at the white shoulders of the world. "Ask me again tomorrow whether I know what I've done."

Downriver, far off at the tree line, something glinted — sun off glass, or sun off a spyglass, held very still by someone watching the Sable Queen lie at her mooring.

Ulyana watched it back for a long count of ten. Then she turned and went to make her plans, and the compass hung against her heart, pointing — she would swear it later, though she checked it a dozen times — not north at all, but steadily, patiently, toward the mountains.
