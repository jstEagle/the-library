## 5. Men on the Bank

Tamir saw them first, because Tamir was always looking at the shore.

It wasn't a habit anyone had taught him. It had grown in him the way moss grows on the north side of pilings — quietly, and because conditions favored it. He'd spent four years on the Sable Queen learning to read banks: where the driftwood piled told you about the spring floods, where the cattle stood told you about the mud, where the lanterns hung told you whether a landing was worth stopping at. He read banks the way Marta read weather and the captain read water. It was just what his eyes did when they weren't needed elsewhere.

So he was up on the cargo deck coiling line in the late gold light of an October afternoon when the two riders came out of the cottonwoods on the eastern bank, and he saw them before they meant to be seen.

They didn't do anything dramatic. That was the first wrong thing. Men who wanted something from a riverboat waved. Men selling something rode down to the bank and shouted prices. Men in trouble ran for the boat with their arms out. These two did none of that. They simply stopped at the tree line, side by side, and sat their horses and watched.

One was big through the shoulders, wearing a canvas duster gone gray with trail dust. The other was smaller, younger maybe, in a black coat too good for the country. Neither raised a hand. Neither turned away. They just watched the boat slide past at her easy four knots against the current, and Tamir felt the look of them go through him like a fishhook finding skin.

He kept coiling the line. His hands knew the work; the figure-eights laid themselves down without him. But some cold animal part of his brain had already logged everything — the horses were lathered, ridden hard from somewhere inland, not along the bank. The big one had a rifle scabbarded but his hand resting near it, not on it, which was its own kind of message. And the young one in the black coat never moved at all except his head, tracking the boat, tracking it, tracking it, until the river's bend took the cottonwoods and both riders away like a curtain drawn.

"Captain," Tamir said, and then didn't know what came after *captain*.

Ulyana Nystrom was at the wheel below him, one hand on the spokes, watching the channel the way she always did — as if the river might try to cheat her and she intended to catch it at it. She didn't look up. "What."

"Two men on the east bank. Just past the willow bend. They watched us the whole way by."

"How long?"

"From the trees. Maybe three minutes."

Now she looked up. Her eyes were gray as the water in November, and they went past him to the receding bank as if she could pull the cottonwoods back into view. "Armed?"

"One rifle I saw. The other one's coat was too nice for a rifle."

Something moved across her face — not fear, Tamir thought. Recognition. Like a woman hearing a knock and knowing before she opens the door who's standing there. "Did Ede come out on deck this afternoon?"

"He's below. Sleeping, or close to it." Everyone on the boat had started saying *sleeping* about Ede Voss instead of *dying*, the way you say *passed over* instead of *dead* before the man has even stopped breathing. It didn't fool anybody. It just made the hours quieter.

"Keep him below," Ulyana said. "And Tamir — if those men are still in sight at dusk, you tell me before you tell anyone else. Not Marta. Not the passenger list. Me."

"They're not passengers' kin? Somebody waiting on a son, maybe—"

"Nobody waits on a sandbar," Ulyana said. "Men who mean well come to landings. Men who don't come to banks." She put her eyes back on the channel. "Coil your line."

He coiled his line.

* * *

That evening the Sable Queen tied up at Cobb's Landing, which wasn't much of a landing at all anymore — a rotted stage platform, a fuel cache under a lean-to, and a mile of cordwood stacked by nobody in particular. There was no town. There was no reason for anyone to be there. Which was why Ulyana chose it, Tamir figured, though she didn't say so. She just took the boat in against the last light, backed her onto the mooring dolphins with that economy of motion that made piloting look like arithmetic, and ordered no lamps lit on the shore side.

Marta fried perch and onions, and they ate in the galley with the shutters half-closed, and nobody talked much. Ede Voss slept in the small stateroom they'd given him, breathing in that wet, rattling way that made Tamir think of a pump drawing the last of something out of a well. The bundle — map and compass and whatever else the old man guarded — lay under his coat where his arm could find it without waking.

The captain ate fast, wiped her mouth, and said, "I'll take first watch. Tamir, midnight to three. Marta, after."

"Watch for what?" Marta asked. She asked it flat, the way she asked whether he wanted more potatoes.

"Anything that isn't a raccoon."

At midnight Tamir came up into a night so clear the stars seemed strung too low, like lanterns hung by someone drunk. Frost already silvered the rail. The river ran dark and quiet, talking to itself around the dolphin piles, and beyond the far bank the country went black to the hills. He walked the deck slow, boots soft, hands in his pockets, and let himself do the thing he'd been refusing to do all evening, which was think.

Because here was the truth he'd been carrying around since the willow bend, warm as a stolen coin in his pocket: he *wanted* the men on the bank to be nothing. He wanted them to be surveyors, trappers, brothers riding home. Because if they were nothing, then the bundle under Ede Voss's coat was still just a dying man's treasure, and treasure was a story, and stories didn't have teeth.

But Tamir had held the bundle. Two nights ago, going through the old man's gear for dry socks, he'd found it wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed string, and he'd undone the string with fingers that shook, and he'd looked. A map, drawn in a prospector's careful hand, with markings in a code of numbers and crosses he couldn't read but understood anyway — every mark a promise of silver enough to buy a life back from whoever owned it. And the compass, brass-cased, heavy, its needle settling with a strange deliberate slowness, like a thing deciding.

He'd retied the string exactly as it had been and put the bundle back and told no one, and ever since he'd been walking around with a door open inside himself that he couldn't shut. On that map was enough money to leave the river. To leave decks and diesel and Captain Nystrom's gray eyes weighing him and finding him lighter than he ought to be. Four years he'd served, and four years he'd dreamed, and the dream had always been shapeless — *away*, *out*, *somewhere with streets* — and now it had a shape drawn in a dead man's ink, and two strangers on a bank were looking at the boat with faces that said the shape had a price.

His watch crawled. At half past one the frost thickened. At two, the river fog began to come off the water in low rags, and Tamir almost welcomed it — fog was cover, fog was kind — and that was exactly when he heard the oars.

Not the sound of oars, exactly. The absence of sound where sound should be. A boat moving quiet on black water makes a certain hush against the current, a parting, and once you've lived on a river you hear it the way you hear a note held past its length. Tamir froze at the rail on the shore side and stared down into the fog and saw nothing, and then saw the nothing move — a long low shape sliding toward the hull, riding the eddy behind the middle dolphin where the current would carry it silent.

He should have run for the captain. Every second he stood there was a second borrowed against something. But his legs had gone strange on him, and his voice stranger, and by the time he made his body obey, the shape had reached the hull and a grapnel rose out of the fog with terrible patience and hooked the rail not six feet from his hands.

Tamir did the only thing his body would agree to: he stepped back into the shadow of the deckhouse and stopped existing.

Two men came over the rail. The big one first, duster and all, moving with a surprising softness for his size, and then the young one in the black coat, who came up the line like he'd boarded boats before. They crouched on the deck a moment, listening. The whole world was fog and river-sound and Tamir's own blood, which had gotten loud as surf.

"Staterooms aft," the young one breathed. "You take the old man's. I'll take the wheelhouse and the crew bunks."

"And if the old man wakes?"

The young one was quiet a moment. "Then he dies dreaming," he said. "Aldous wants it clean. No marks the law can read."

They split. Tamir pressed himself against the deckhouse wall, in the gap between a coil of spare hose and the wood, and watched the big man's shape drift aft along the deck, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the adventure had just stopped being hypothetical. This was the moment in every story he'd ever told himself about leaving — the moment where the story showed its teeth. And he was hiding behind a hose.

He thought about the captain asleep in the wheelhouse bunk. He thought about Marta in the galley, who slept with a boning knife under her pillow and would use it and hang for it. He thought about Ede Voss, breathing his wet rattle twenty feet from a man sent to make sure the breathing stopped.

Move, he told himself. Move, you coward, move —

The wheelhouse door opened ahead of him. Not the young one — the captain. She came out barefoot, in her coat over her nightshirt, and she didn't hurry, and she didn't call out. She simply stood in the doorway, a silhouette against the lamplit compass, and said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the aft deck and no farther:

"The old man's effects are sealed and witnessed at the Kestrel Gap probate. You're welcome to row back and explain to the court why you're boarding a licensed vessel at two in the morning."

Silence. Then the big man's shape detached itself from the shadows aft and came forward, unhurried, and Tamir saw the rifle was in his hands now, held loose and low. "Evening, Captain. Name's Aldous. My brother and I got business with a passenger of yours."

"Your brother's on my deck right now, poking at my crew bunks. Get him off my boat."

"We'll go when we've seen what the old man carried."

"You'll go now, or you'll go in irons at Fort Brannan, and I'll swear out the papers myself."

Another silence, longer. In the fog Tamir could barely see them — two shapes facing each other across ten feet of frosted deck, and between them the whole weight of the question, the one Tamir had been dodging since the willow bend: did the captain actually have anything to trade, or was she spending nerve she didn't have?

It was the young one who ended it. He came around the deckhouse — passing within arm's reach of Tamir, so close Tamir could smell wet wool and tobacco — and said softly, "Brother. Leave it. Old man's dying anyway. Dead men don't testify."

"They leave paper," the big one said.

"Then we'll find the paper." A pause, and Tamir heard the smile in it, and hated himself for hearing it. "Besides. Boat's headed upriver, ain't she? Everything heads somewhere. We'll be there first."

There was a scrape, a shift of weight — and then the captain moved. Tamir never fully saw what she did; later he'd piece it together from sounds alone. Something struck the deck hard. The rifle clattered. A body hit the rail, and the rail groaned, and for one suspended second the whole night balanced on the question of whether the big Aldous was going over the side into black October water in a duster full of river.

He didn't go over. He came off the rail swearing, and the two shapes retreated down the mooring line, quick now, all their patience spent, and the grapnel splashed free, and the long low shape slid back into the fog with the brothers in it, oars biting fast, heading for the east bank.

The captain stood at the rail a long time, listening them out. Then she turned, and her eyes found the gap between the hose coil and the wall, and found Tamir in it, the way a searchlight finds a skiff.

"How long were you standing there?" she asked.

"Since they hooked on."

"Since they hooked on." She repeated it without inflection, which was worse than anger. "You watched them board my boat."

"I couldn't—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "My legs wouldn't—"

"Your legs work fine. You used them to hide." She came closer, and up close her face in the dark was all planes and iron. "Listen to me, Tamir. Fear's honest. Fear's fine. I've been afraid every day since I signed Hale's paper, and I'm still standing. What's not fine is freezing alone and letting everyone else sleep through the knife coming. Next time — and there will be a next time — you wake me. You wake me *first*. Before you decide you're worthless. Understood?"

"Yes, Captain."

"Go get Marta. Wake the boat. Nobody sleeps the rest of this night." She looked out at the fog, at the invisible east bank, and Tamir saw her hand close around something at her throat — the old man's compass, which she'd taken to wearing since morning, though she hadn't explained it and he hadn't asked. "They said we're headed upriver," she said, mostly to herself. "They think we're taking him home the easy way."

"Aren't we?"

She was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence, talking against the piles.

"No," Ulyana Nystrom said. "Kestrel Gap is overland, and we leave the boat tomorrow."

Tamir stared at her. Below them, faint through the deck, Ede Voss's breathing rattled on — slower now, each draw of air costing more than the last — and the captain turned toward the stateroom door as if the sound had called her, and Tamir understood that whatever window the old man had left, it was closing faster than the fog could burn off.

Tomorrow they would abandon the river. Tonight, the map still breathed under a dying man's coat, and somewhere in the dark across the water, two brothers were rowing hard to be somewhere first.
