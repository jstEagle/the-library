## 6. Ede's Last Morning

Ulyana woke before the light, the way she always did, and knew before she opened her eyes that Ede Voss was dying.

She couldn't have said how she knew. The river told her things like that — a shift in the current's voice, a wrongness in the way the pilothouse glass fogged. This morning the whole boat felt it. The Sable Queen sat at her mooring off Miller's Landing with a stillness that wasn't rest, and somewhere below, in the narrow bunk Marta had made up for him, the old man's breathing had changed from labor to something thinner. A thread being drawn out.

She dressed in the dark. Boots, coat, the small ritual of it. On her desk the ledger lay open to the page she hated — the Hale page, the numbers that never moved in her favor no matter how many seasons she pushed against them. She closed it without looking. There would be time enough for that arithmetic later. There always was.

The cabin below was warm from Marta's stove, and it smelled of coffee and of sickness — that sweetish, used-up smell that gathers around a body when it's finishing its work. Tamir sat on the bench by the door, elbows on his knees, and he stood when she came down, too quickly, the way a man stands when he's been sitting with something he shouldn't have been sitting with.

"Captain," he said.

"Has he woke?"

"Once. Around three." Tamir hesitated. His eyes went to Ede's gear, stacked in the corner where they'd moved it after the men on the bank came asking questions, and then away again, fast. "He was asking for his daughter. Or he was asking for water. Hard to say which."

Ulyana nodded. She crossed to the bunk and knelt, and Ede Voss opened his eyes.

They were clear this morning. That was the cruelty of it — the fever had burned through him in the night and left the last of him polished and bright, like river glass after a flood. He looked at her and he knew her. He had maybe half-known her for two days, calling her by names that weren't hers, by the daughter's name, by the name of a woman named Solveig who Ulyana guessed had been dead a long time. But now he knew her.

"Captain Nystrom," he said. His voice was a dry channel. "You're still here."

"Where would I go?" She wet a cloth and pressed it to his lips, and he drank from it like a man remembering how.

"The passes," he said. "The snows. You have to beat the snows." His hand came out from under the blanket and caught her wrist. His grip was astonishing — a prospector's grip, a grip that had spent fifty years pulling silver out of stone that didn't want to give it up. "Ingrid Voss. Kestrel Gap. You promised."

"I promised."

"Say it again."

She could have refused. She could have said *the dead don't get to make demands of the living*, which was true, and she could have said *I have a boat and a debt and a crew and no business walking into mountains*, which was truer. Instead she said, "I'll carry it to Ingrid Voss in Kestrel Gap before the passes close. I keep my promises, Mr. Voss. Badly, sometimes. But absolutely."

Something moved through his face that might have been a smile. "Badly," he repeated. "Yes. That's the only way to keep them. The men who keep them well —" a breath, rattling, "— they keep the wrong ones."

His hand slid from her wrist and went to his own throat, to the cord that hung there under his shirt, and he drew it out with what was left of his strength. The compass swung free. It was a heavy thing, brass gone green at the seams, older than anything else he'd carried. The lid was dented on one side as if it had taken a fall from a height and been forgiven.

"Take it," he said.

"I can't —"

"Take it." The word came out with the last of his force, and she took it, because there are moments when refusing a dying man is the one cruelty you can't come back from. The compass sat in her palm, cold and heavier than it looked. He watched her open the lid. The needle swung, wobbled, settled — pointing not north, she noticed, not quite, a degree or two off true, though the light was bad and her hands were shaking and she told herself it was the light.

"True bearing," Ede said. His eyes had gone somewhere past the cabin wall, past the river, past all of it. "A man spends his whole life finding north. Forty years I chased a needle. And the whole time —" his breath caught, held, released, "— the whole time the bearing that mattered was the one I ran from. You understand, Captain?"

"No," she said honestly.

"Good." The smile came again, fainter. "You will. That's the trick of a compass like this one. It doesn't point where you want. It points where you owe."

Then his eyes came back to her, and they were clear to the last, and he said, "Ingrid. Tell her —" and stopped. He tried again. "Tell her I —" and stopped again, and Ulyana watched a man discover, at the very edge of his life, that there were sentences too heavy to lift.

"Tell her yourself," Ulyana said. "I'm just carrying the map."

He looked at her for a long moment. "Yes," he said. "Yes, that's better. That's exactly better." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "She'll want to know if I thought of her. Tell her the needle always came back. Every time. No matter where I dragged it."

The light was coming up gray through the one small window. Somewhere above them Tamir moved on deck, boots on planking. Ede Voss's breathing slowed, and slowed, and Ulyana knelt there with a dead man's compass in her hand and did not move until Marta came and stood beside her and put a hand, brief and hard, on her shoulder.

* * *

They buried him at Miller's Landing because there was nowhere else. The ground was soft from the low water, which was the one mercy the season offered, and Marta knew the woman who kept the landing's burying plot — a widow named Osk who took payment in coffee and news and asked no questions, which was why Marta knew her.

It was a short service. Tamir read the words he half remembered from his grandmother, in a language Ulyana didn't know, and the sound of it was right even untranslated. Marta wept the way she did everything, efficiently and with visible annoyance at herself. Ulyana stood with the compass in her coat pocket and her hand closed around it like a fist around a coal.

She was still standing there when the riders came down from the tree line.

She heard them before she saw them — horses on soft ground, two of them, moving without hurry. She turned, and there they were: the same two men who had watched the Sable Queen from the bank two nights ago, the same two who had come aboard in the dark with a lantern and a warrant that was no warrant at all, just paper with a signature and a threat behind it. Brothers, she'd decided. The younger one had a face like an open hand — everything showed. The older one had a face like a closed door.

They reined up at the edge of the burying ground and did not dismount, and Ulyana understood that too. It was a message. Men who wouldn't stand on the ground where a man was being put into it.

"Captain Nystrom," the older one said. Aldous — she'd gotten the name off the paper. His voice was pleasant the way ice is pleasant, all shine and no give. "We're sorry to intrude on your morning."

"You're not intruding," Ulyana said. "You're watching. There's a difference, and it's the difference between you and a guest."

The younger brother shifted in his saddle. The older one smiled without any of his face joining in. "Is he dead, then?"

"He is."

"And his effects? A dead man's effects belong to his partners, Captain. That's the law in this territory. You'll find it's the law in every territory. His gear, his papers, his —" a pause, delicate, "— instruments. We'll take them off your hands and spare you the bother."

Ulyana looked at him. And here was the thing she would carry with her down every mile of the trail ahead, the thing that would sit in her chest like ballast: the lie came easily. It rose in her the way water rises in a hold, smooth and level and without any effort at all, and she heard herself speak it in the same flat, competent voice she used for sandbars and freight rates and all the other hazards she'd learned to talk her way past.

"He had nothing," she said. "A man who dies of fever on charity passage doesn't die rich, Mr. Aldous. His gear is in the plot with him — his blanket, his coat, the little he had. What papers there were, there weren't any. A man who's been out of the hills twenty years doesn't carry papers. He carries coughing and regret." She let that sit. "You're welcome to dig him up if you'd like to check. I'll lend you the spade."

The younger brother looked away. The older one studied her, and for a moment the door of his face cracked and she saw the calculation behind it, quick and cold: *is she lying, and can I prove it, and what does it cost.* Then the door closed.

"The partners," he said, "are reasonable men, Captain. They'll pay fair for anything of his the river gave you. They'll also remember, going forward, who helped them and who inconvenienced them. Those are the only two categories, out here. I'd think about which one you're in."

"I think about a lot of things," Ulyana said. "Mostly sandbars."

He held her eyes a moment longer. Then he turned his horse, and the brothers rode back up into the trees, and the burying ground was quiet again except for Osk's widow's wind chime, clicking somewhere behind the fence, and the sound of Marta breathing beside her.

"You lied well," Marta said. There was no judgment in it. That was the worst part.

"I know," Ulyana said.

"It came easy."

"I know."

Marta looked at her — Marta, who had cooked on the Sable Queen for nine years and buried a husband and never once asked Ulyana a question she couldn't answer herself. "Does that worry you?"

Ulyana turned back to the fresh-turned earth, to the plain plank that would have to serve until Osk's son could cut a proper marker. Somewhere under that dirt was a man who had pressed a compass into her hand and said *you will* — *you will understand* — with the confidence of a man placing a bet he'd already seen settled. Somewhere under that dirt was a daughter who didn't know yet that she was the answer to a question her father had spent twenty years failing to ask.

"He trusted me with it," Ulyana said. "A man who hadn't earned anyone's trust in twenty years looked at me — a stranger, a boat captain in hock to Corvin Hale up to her wheelhouse — and decided I was the one who'd keep his promise." She worked the compass out of her pocket and looked at it. The needle lay steady in its glass, pointing its degree or two off true, patient as a creditor. "And the first thing I do with his trust is lie to two men's faces and enjoy how well it goes."

"You lied to protect him," Marta said.

"I lied because lying is what I'm good at." She closed the lid. The click of it was small and final. "There's a difference, Marta, and I've spent fifteen years not knowing which side of it I stand on."

Marta said nothing to that, which was its own kind of answer, and after a while she went back up to the boat, and Tamir with her, and Ulyana stood alone at the grave with the compass in her hand until the cold came up through her boots.

* * *

They cast off at noon.

The river was low and mean, the color of old tin, and Ulyana took her up the channel with the particular attention the season demanded — every sandbar shifted, every mark moved, the whole river rewritten by the dry summer and waiting to punish anyone navigating from memory. She worked the wheel and the leads and the soundings and did not think about the compass in her pocket, and thought about nothing else.

Tamir stood beside her in the pilothouse. He'd been standing there more and more, she'd noticed, since Miller's Landing. Watching her hands. Watching the water. Watching her.

"Captain," he said, finally. "Those men. They'll come back."

"They'll come back."

"And Hale — if the Aldouses are talking to Hale, and Hale is talking to anyone, then by Friday every landing from here to the fork will know what we're carrying."

"Nothing," Ulyana said. "We're carrying nothing. That's what I told them."

Tamir was quiet a moment. "Do you believe that?"

She looked at him. He was twenty-four and he had the kind of face that had never once successfully concealed a thought, and right now the thoughts were queuing up behind his eyes like boats at a lock. She thought about the bundle in her cabin, wrapped in oilcloth, hidden in the one place on the Sable Queen she'd never shown another living soul. She thought about the compass on the cord, which she had not put down since the burial and which hung now against her sternum like a second heartbeat, a degree or two off true.

"No," she said. "I believe I made a promise to a dead man, and the dead are the only creditors who never renegotiate."

"And if Hale comes aboard?"

Ulyana brought the Sable Queen around the point below Split Rock, where the channel doglegged hard to starboard, and felt the boat answer the wheel the way she always did, the way nothing else in Ulyana Nystrom's life had ever answered anything.

"Then Hale," she said, "is going to learn what I learned this morning about how easily I lie."

But that night, anchored in the willows above the fork, she took the compass out and opened it under the lamp, and the needle swung, wobbled, settled — and she could no longer tell herself it was the light. It was off true. It had been off true since the old man's hand left it. And it was not pointing north, and it was not pointing south, and it was not pointing at Kestrel Gap.

It was pointing, steady as a debt, back downriver.

Behind them. Toward the country she had left fifteen years ago and had not once, in fifteen years, gone back to look at.

Ulyana closed the lid and did not sleep, and in the morning the first frost of the year lay white on the deck, early and wrong, the snows coming down out of the mountains ahead of schedule — and with them, at the landing below, the masts of two boats flying Corvin Hale's house flag, moored square across the channel like a hand laid flat on a table.
