## 7. Hale's Ledger

Ulyana saw the Hale barge before she heard it — a black wedge of smoke standing too straight above the treeline downstream, the kind of smoke that came from a boiler run hard and run rich. Nobody on this stretch of the Vell ran their boiler like that except Corvin Hale.

She cut the wheel two points and called down to Tamir in the engine room. "Ease her back. We're going to have company."

The Sable Queen came off the bar current slow and sullen, the way she did everything since Ede Voss died aboard her six days ago. They had buried him at Miller's Bend under a cottonwood, Marta reading words over the grave that weren't from any book Ulyana knew, just old ones, older than books maybe. The compass hung now inside Ulyana's shirt, against her skin, and she had caught herself touching it three times that morning without deciding to.

Ede's effects were hidden in the flour bin in the galley, wrapped in oilcloth, under forty pounds of the least interesting thing on the boat. It wasn't much of a hiding place. But then, it didn't need to be, if nobody came looking.

Somebody was coming looking.

* * *

The Hale barge was called the *Ledger*, because Corvin Hale believed in naming things what they were. She drew up alongside with the practiced grace of a vessel that had done this maneuver a hundred times — fenders out, lines across, no scraping, no hurry. Two deckhands Ulyana recognized from years of freight contracts made fast, and then Corvin Hale himself stepped onto the Sable Queen's deck as if he owned it.

In every way that mattered, he did.

He was a big man gone soft at the edges but not at the eyes, dressed for the river in clothes that had never once been splashed. He took off his hat — a courtesy he extended to boats, if not always to people — and looked around the deck with the mild interest of a man inspecting property he already held the paper on.

"Captain Nystrom." His voice carried easily; it was built for auction houses and courtrooms. "You're running light."

"Between contracts." Ulyana kept her hands on the wheel. "Didn't expect you this far upriver."

"I go where my interests are." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like the rest of him. "And my interests have developed a certain... specificity, of late."

Behind him, one of the deckhands drifted toward the galley door. Marta appeared in it, arms crossed, a ladle in one hand that she held the way other women held knives. The deckhand reconsidered his drift.

"Hale." Ulyana came down off the pilothouse stairs slowly. Every step was a decision. "Say what you came to say."

"Direct. I've always liked that about you." He settled against the rail, unhurried, a man who had never once been late for anything because everything waited for him. "How is your debt these days, Captain? Last I looked at the ledger — and I look often — you stood at four hundred and twelve dollars, plus carrying charges. That was in spring. With the season you've had..." He made a small gesture, a man brushing crumbs from a table. "I'd say you're north of five hundred now. Am I close?"

"You know exactly how close."

"I do. It's my ledger." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Cruelty would have been easier to hate. "Five hundred and thirty-one dollars as of Tuesday. A boat worth perhaps three hundred in her current condition, though I'd never insult her by saying so aloud. Which means, Captain Nystrom, that you are not sailing a boat. You are sailing my patience, and it has a shorter draft than she does."

The compass pressed cold against Ulyana's sternum. She thought about Ede's hand around hers, the weight of it, the terrible trust of a dying man who had nothing left to give but a promise he needed someone else to keep.

"What do you want, Corvin?"

"Ah." He straightened. "There it is. You always did get there faster than most people. Most people need me to build the road first. You just look at the destination." He reached into his coat and produced a folded paper, held it between two fingers without offering it. "Two nights ago, I received visitors. Brothers. Aldous, by name. Rough men, but they pay promptly, which in my experience correlates strongly with how badly they want something."

The river moved beneath them, indifferent. Somewhere upstream a loon called, once, and got no answer.

"They told me," Hale continued, "that an old man boarded your vessel at Cooper's Landing. Feverish. Carrying certain effects. They were very interested in those effects. Interested enough to make it worth my while." He let the paper drop onto the deck box between them, face up. It was a bill of sale — Ulyana's debt, written out in full, stamped PAID in red ink that looked wet even in the flat gray light. "Sign nothing. Hand over whatever the old man gave you. And walk away owing me nothing for the first time in nine years."

* * *

Nine years.

Ulyana had been twenty-three when she signed for the Sable Queen, and she could still recite the terms the way some people recited prayers. Sixty percent of gross freight, all repairs at her cost, the hull itself as collateral, and a clause about default that Hale's lawyers had worded so gently it took two readings to understand it meant he could take the boat out from under her for missing a single payment.

Her father had warned her. Her mother had said something softer but meant the same thing. Both of them dead eleven years now, along with her brother, in a fire whose cause the county record listed as *undetermined* and whose cause Ulyana had never once said aloud, even to herself, in the dark, where lies go to be honest.

She had signed anyway. Because the river was the only thing that hadn't been taken from her, and the Sable Queen was how she stayed on it.

PAID. In red ink. For the price of a dead man's bundle in a flour bin.

"That's generous," she said.

"It's business." Hale picked up the paper again, smoothed it against his knee. "Though I'll confess the brothers' money is only half the transaction. The other half is my own curiosity. Ede Voss." He said the name carefully, tasting it. "I knew him, you understand. Twenty-five years ago, up in the Kestrel country. He was a fool about silver, but he was an honest fool, which is rarer and less profitable. I signed as witness on his original claim registration. Did he mention that? No, I suppose he wouldn't have. Dying men edit."

Something turned over in Ulyana's chest. *Witness.* The word went into her like a fishhook and sat there.

"So you knew him," she said. "And now his former partners want what he carried, and they've paid you to fetch it. And you're fetching it."

"I'm *offering*," Hale corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm giving you a choice, which is more consideration than the Aldouses would show you. Those men burned a claim shack over a dispute about survey stakes, Captain. They don't offer choices."

*Burned a claim shack.* The words landed somewhere near the hook already sitting in her chest, and both of them twisted.

"And if I refuse?"

Hale sighed, genuinely, the sigh of a man disappointed in a student he'd liked. He put the bill of sale back in his coat, folded, unhurried. Then he walked the length of the deck, looking at things — the patched rail, the new paint over the old name, Tamir watching through the engine room hatch with his jaw set, Marta still holding her ladle in the galley doorway like a sentry.

"She's a good boat," he said. "Better than her paper. I'll give you until the snows. First snow, wherever you are, whatever river or trail you've taken — that's when the ledger closes. After that, I sell the debt."

"Sell it." Ulyana laughed, and hated the sound. "To whom? Who buys a debt on a half-dead boat?"

"To whoever pays." He turned back to her, and for just a moment the auction-house polish dropped and she saw the thing underneath, the thing that had bought three steamboats and a warehouse district and, quietly, half the sheriffs in two counties. "Debts are instruments, Captain. They can be held, traded, or *collected*. And collectors don't care about your feelings or your promises to dead men. They care about assets." He put his hat back on. "The old man's effects. Wherever you've hidden them — and do spare us both the pretense that you haven't — they are worth less than nothing to you. They're worth a hanging, if the Aldouses find them first. Whereas my offer is worth five hundred and thirty-one dollars and your future."

"The snows," Ulyana said.

"First snow." He nodded once, almost respectfully, and stepped back across to the *Ledger*. The lines came off. The fenders came in. The black-smoked barge pulled away downstream with its owner standing at the rail, watching her the way a man watches weather he intends to profit from either way.

* * *

They didn't talk about it for an hour. The Sable Queen ran the middle channel, past willow banks and a heron standing in the shallows like a monk at prayer, and nobody spoke. Tamir stayed in the engine room. Marta eventually brought coffee up to the pilothouse, poured two cups, and stood at the window with hers, saying nothing, which was Marta's way of saying a great deal.

"He knew him," Ulyana said finally. "Hale knew Ede. Signed his claim papers."

"Mm." Marta blew on her coffee. "Small mountains."

"Twenty-five years ago. Before the brothers sold his rights out from under him. Before—" She stopped. Before whatever had happened that turned Ede Voss into a feverish ghost drifting down the Vell with a map sewn into his coat and a daughter he couldn't face.

"You're doing the arithmetic," Marta said.

"I'm always doing arithmetic. It's the only language that man speaks." Ulyana set her cup down. "Marta. If I give it to him — the bundle — we walk free. Nine years of hauling freight for half of nothing, over. Done. We could take contracts again. Real ones. Tamir could—"

"Tamir could what?" Marta's voice was dry as August. "Leave? He talks about it every month. He'd be gone inside a year whether you owed Hale or not. Boys like that don't stay anywhere." She drank. "That's not the question and you know it. The question is what the old man bought with his last breath, and whether it's yours to sell."

"He didn't buy anything. He asked. There's a difference."

"There certainly is." Marta set her cup on the sill. "One of them you can put a price on."

She went below, and Ulyana stood alone at the wheel with the compass against her skin and the river pulling north and the debt pulling south and herself stretched between them like a line in a hard current.

* * *

She found Tamir at dusk, coiling line on the foredeck that was already coiled, which was what Tamir did with his hands when his head was loud.

"You heard all of it," she said.

"Boats carry sound." He didn't stop coiling. "First snow. That's what, three weeks? Four?"

"Three, if the mountains mean it."

"And after that, he sells the debt to men who burn claim shacks." He finally looked up. He was twenty-two and had been on the boat since he was seventeen and had never once lied to her, which she suspected was about to change someday, though she couldn't have said why. "Cap. I'm not saying give it up. I'm asking what we do in three weeks."

Ulyana leaned on the rail beside him. Downstream, the last light was going copper on the water, and the current ran steady and sure toward a place none of them could follow it.

"We don't owe Hale the truth," she said. "We owe Ede Voss a delivery. Those aren't the same thing, and only one of them has a due date I can live with."

Tamir frowned. "Meaning what, Cap?"

Meaning, she thought, that she had spent nine years paying a debt in money and fifteen years paying a different one in silence, and she was done confusing the two. Meaning that Corvin Hale had offered her a clean ledger and she had felt, for one whole second, the pull of it — and then she had felt the compass against her chest, pointing at nothing yet, pointing at everything, and understood that some debts you settle by paying and others you settle by keeping your word.

"Meaning," she said aloud, "we're going to need a friend with a dock."

Tamir stared at her. Then, slowly, he began to grin — the first grin she'd seen on him since the riders on the bank, since the night search, since Ede died. "You're taking her off the water."

"I'm taking *us* off the water." Ulyana pushed off the rail and headed for the pilothouse, and behind her the Sable Queen ran on through the failing light, light and unburdened for the first time in nine years, which was exactly how a boat feels in the hour before it's abandoned. "Wake Marta. Tell her to start packing flour."
