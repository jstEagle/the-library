## 8. Abandoning the River

Tamir had tied a thousand lines to a thousand cleats in his three years aboard the *Sable Queen*, and he had never once felt what he felt now: that the knot was the wrong thing. That the rope should stay with him and the boat should go free.

"Pull it taut," Ulyana said behind him. "You're leaving slack like you expect her to come back on her own."

"She might," Tamir said.

"She won't." Ulyana came down the dock with her pack already slung, her river coat traded for a wool one that made her look like a stranger — some upland woman with no business near water at all. "That's the point of leaving."

The dock belonged to Sera Quill, who ran coal and cordwood out of a bend called Halfway Light, and who had owed Ulyana Nystrom a favor since the flood year, when Ulyana had run her stranded barge off a bar in the dark with the ice coming down. Sera stood at the head of the dock now with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the particular way of people watching someone do something they'd argued against and lost.

"You're sure about the shed," she said. "I can put her inside. Dry berth. It's not so much—"

"Sera." Ulyana touched the older woman's arm once, briefly, the way you'd touch a rail to steady yourself. "If Hale comes looking for the boat, I want her exactly where I said she'd be. Moored, honest, plain as Sunday. If I hide her, it tells him she matters more than the debt. She doesn't. She *is* the debt."

"That's a grim way to talk about your own deck."

"It's an honest way. That's the fashion this season."

Tamir gave the line its last turn and set the hitch, and his hands did it perfectly, automatically, while the rest of him stood there feeling like a man burying something alive. The *Sable Queen* sat low in the gray water, her paint gone chalky at the waterline, her wheelhouse windows fogged with morning cold. He knew every board of her. He'd caulked half of them himself. Three years ago he'd signed on as a deckhand because it was supposed to be for one season, and then another season, because the river got into you the way silt gets into a current — invisibly, and then everywhere.

He hadn't planned on wanting to leave. But he had wanted it, quietly, for a year now. And now that leaving was actually happening, in boots and packs and a road instead of a gangway, he found the want had changed shape entirely.

Marta came down the dock last, which was unlike Marta, who was always first to anything involving departure or food. She carried two bags and a wrapped loaf and her face arranged into something carefully neutral, which on Marta meant furious.

"I've left a week of meals labeled in the galley," she said. "In case anyone eats aboard. Which no one will, because no one will be aboard, because we are all insane."

"Noted," Ulyana said.

"There's a jar of pickled onions under the chart table. If Sera finds them before they go soft, tell her they're hers."

"I'll tell her."

"And if the stove shifts in the frost, shim the left leg, not the right, everyone shims the right and it throws the flue—"

"Marta." Ulyana turned fully around. For a moment Tamir saw something pass between the two women that he wasn't invited into — some old understanding, freighted and wordless. "She'll be here in spring. Or she won't, and she'll have kept us alive by being here instead of wherever we'd otherwise be. Either way she's done her job. Say goodbye properly or don't, but don't stand on my dock instructing the frost."

Marta pressed her lips together. Then she walked back up the dock alone, laid her palm flat against the *Sable Queen*'s hull just above the waterline, and left it there a long moment, the way you'd leave it on the shoulder of a sleeping animal. When she came back her face was ordinary again, and she said, "Well. Road's that way," and started walking, and that was that.

Sera shook Ulyana's hand hard. "Halfway Light, spring thaw. If she's still floating."

"If she's still floating," Ulyana agreed, "and if I'm still breathing, I'll buy you a barrel of the good stuff from Kestrel Gap, and if I'm not, drink it anyway and say something rude about me."

They watched the boat shrink behind them for the first mile. None of them said anything about it. The trail climbed away from the river through birch scrub gone gold and bare, and every time the path bent, Tamir looked back, and every time there was less river and less boat, until there was only the valley smoking with morning mist and the sound of water going on without them.

It should have felt like grief. It did feel like grief. But underneath the grief, shamefully, unmistakably, ran something else — a bright thin wire of exhilaration, the feeling of a knot coming undone in his chest. He was twenty-four years old and he had never been farther from the river than a day's walk. His whole map of the world was drawn in channels and bars and landings. Beyond the valley walls was territory marked, in his own private atlas, with nothing at all.

He was terrified of it. He couldn't wait.

* * *

They made twelve miles the first day, following an old freight track that dwindled to a hunter's path and then to a suggestion. Marta navigated by a method she refused to explain, which appeared to involve asking directions at every cabin and crossroads and disbelieving roughly half of what she heard. Ulyana navigated by the compass.

That was the strange part. That was the part Tamir kept circling back to as they walked.

Ede Voss's compass hung on a lanyard around Ulyana's neck, brass-cased, heavy-looking, its face scratched nearly blind. She'd taken to checking it at every halt the way other captains checked their watches. And twice today — once at midmorning, once when they stopped to eat — Tamir had seen her stop, frown, and turn the instrument over in her hands as though it had spoken nonsense.

At the noon halt he finally asked. "It's broken?"

Ulyana didn't answer right away. She held the compass level, waited for the needle to settle, and read it. Then she sighted along her arm toward the ridgeline ahead, where the trail bent east around a shoulder of rock.

"No," she said slowly. "It's not broken. It's just not pointing north."

"What's it pointing at, then?"

She lowered the compass. Something crossed her face — quick, controlled, gone — like a fish turning under the surface. "North enough," she said. "Needle's probably dragging. Old housing. Ede carried it thirty years; things wear."

Tamir let it go, because you learned early on the *Queen* which questions Ulyana answered and which ones she banked like coals for later. But he watched her check it again an hour later, and again at dusk, and each time she took longer to put it away.

They camped in the lee of a fallen spruce, and Marta produced a supper of flatbread and beans that tasted, impossibly, almost like the galley, and the cold came down off the peaks in earnest, and Tamir lay in his blanket listening to the wind work the high country and thinking about the wire of exhilaration in his chest and whether it would still be there in a week, or whether the mountains would burn it out of him the way the river had burned out everything softer.

Ulyana sat up past her turn at watch, the compass open in her cupped hands, turning it slowly, slowly, like a man trying to read a letter in failing light.

* * *

On the third day the trail crossed a saddle and dropped into a long green valley with a creek running down its spine, and there, on a fencepost at the mouth of a cattle track, someone had nailed a strip of paper.

Marta reached it first. She read it, and Tamir watched her whole body change — the way a boat changes when its load shifts, everything the same and everything different.

"What is it?" Ulyana asked.

Marta pulled the paper off the nail and handed it over without a word. Tamir crowded in and read it upside down against Ulyana's shoulder.

**SEEKING INFORMATION — THREE TRAVELERS OFF THE RIVER ROAD, ONE WOMAN TALL GRAY COAT, ONE OLDER WOMAN, ONE YOUNG MAN. GENEROUS REWARD FOR WORD OF THEIR ROUTE. INQUIRE AT THE CROSSING HOUSE, MILLBROOK.**

No name signed. No name needed. The paper was good stock, the printing professional, and the reward amount — generous, it said, and Tamir believed it — told him everything about who could afford it.

"The Aldouses," he said. His voice came out thinner than he wanted.

"Hale's money, Aldous riders, same purse." Ulyana read the notice twice, unhurried, then folded it into quarters and tucked it inside her coat. "Millbrook's the market town two days south of here. They're casting wide. Good."

"*Good?*" Marta said.

"They're spending time and coin on paper instead of trackers. Paper is slow. Every farmer who reads that notice has to decide whether we're worth betraying, and most of them will decide we aren't, because farmers don't love freight magnates." She was already walking. "But it means they know we've left the river, and it means they know our general heading, which means we stop being clever and start being fast. We make the high trail tomorrow and we don't come down until Kestrel Gap."

Tamir walked on in silence for a while, doing arithmetic he didn't like. Two days to Millbrook. Two days, maybe three, to the high trail. If the Aldouses had riders working the valleys — and men paid generous rewards did not ride alone —

"We could split up," he said, before he'd decided to say it. "Cover more ground. Leave word at—"

"No," Ulyana and Marta said together, with such identical flatness that Tamir shut his mouth and felt, absurdly, warmed by it.

* * *

That night the compass stopped pretending.

They'd camped high, in the last stunted trees below the scree, and Tamir had taken first watch because sleep wouldn't come anyway. The moon was up, thin and sharp, laying silver edges on the ridgelines. Below them the world fell away into black valley. Above them the stars did their ancient indifferent work.

Ulyana wasn't asleep either. She sat against her pack with the compass in both hands, and in the moonlight Tamir could see the needle clearly — could see it swing as she rotated the case, and settle, always, always, to the same bearing. Not north. North was behind them and slightly left. The needle insisted on a point somewhere out ahead and to the west, deep into the range, where Tamir knew from the freight maps there was nothing but stone and old burns and the far headwaters.

"Captain," he said quietly.

She didn't startle. She'd known he was awake; she probably knew his breathing patterns better than his mother had. "Go to sleep, Tamir."

"It keeps pointing the same way. Doesn't it. Wherever you turn it."

A long pause. Wind moved through the trees above them, and somewhere downslope a rock clattered — a marmot, probably, probably — and Tamir's heart did something complicated.

"Ede told me once," Ulyana said at last, "that a compass doesn't point north. Everyone says it does. It doesn't. It points along the field — it aligns itself with whatever force is strongest where it's standing. North is just usually the biggest force in the room." She turned the case over in her fingers. "This one seems to disagree about what's biggest."

"What do you think it's pointing at?"

"I think," Ulyana said, and stopped. In the moonlight her face was unreadable, but her thumb was moving over the scratched brass cover in small circles, the way Marta's hand moved over worry beads, the way Tamir's own hand moved over a cleat when he was thinking about leaving. "I think it's pointing at something I know. And I think I'd rather it were broken."

She closed the compass, threaded it back under her collar, and lay down with her back to him, and that was the end of the conversation. But Tamir stayed on watch a while longer, looking west, at the black mass of the range where the needle wanted to go, and he thought about how three days ago the strangest thing in his life had been leaving the river, and how quickly a person's supply of strangeness could be used up, and how much more of it these mountains seemed to be holding in reserve.

Down in the dark, far behind them, the *Sable Queen* rode at her moorings on the gray water, and Tamir found he could see her perfectly — every chalky board, every fogged window — and understood suddenly that he would carry her the whole way to Kestrel Gap and past it, the way you carry a harbor.

He just hoped he arrived carrying nothing else quite so heavy.

Above the tree line, the first clouds of the season were stacking up against the peaks, white and enormous and patient, building themselves into weather.
