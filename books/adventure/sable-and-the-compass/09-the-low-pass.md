## 9. The Low Pass

Marta had cooked for rivermen for eleven years, and she had learned one thing above all others: a man's true character showed in how he ate when he was afraid. Tamir ate everything and asked for more. Ulyana ate nothing at all until the food was gone, then picked at the scraps like a gull. Marta herself ate steadily, because fear was no reason to waste good bread.

They were three days out from the Sable Queen, climbing through foothills the color of old copper, and Marta was doing the arithmetic she always did — portions against days, boots against miles, luck against sense. The arithmetic kept coming up short.

"Pass is called Low," Tamir said, huffing beside her on the switchback. "Why's it called Low if it's so high?"

"Because the other ones are worse." Marta shifted her pack. She carried the heaviest load of the three of them, not because anyone made her but because she was fifty-one years old and had been carrying heavier loads than everyone else since before either of them was born. It saved argument. "Everything out here is named by what it isn't."

Ahead of them Ulyana walked with her head down and her stride long, the way she'd walked since they'd left the river. Marta had watched that woman pilot the Sable Queen through water thick as porridge without losing a plank, and she'd never seen her look like this. On the boat, Ulyana was certain. Out here, in country where there was nothing to steer by but a brass compass that behaved like a drunk, she looked like a woman reading a letter she didn't want to finish.

The compass bothered Marta too. She'd seen it twice now, needle swinging lazy off north, settling somewhere it had no business settling, and each time Ulyana's jaw went tight as a cleat line. Marta didn't ask. In her experience, people told you things when the telling got cheaper than the silence.

* * *

On the fourth day they came down into a valley where somebody had tried to make a town and mostly succeeded. It was called Brindle Ford, or maybe just Brindle — the sign had lost an argument with weather years ago. One street, two saloons, a feed store, a smithy, and a market square where farmers sold potatoes to miners who pretended they'd have gotten better prices downriver.

"We buy food, we keep moving," Ulyana said, stopped at the edge of the square. "Twenty minutes. Nobody talks about where we're going. Nobody talks about where we've been."

"And if someone asks?" Tamir said.

"Then we're freight handlers walking to a winter contract at Copper Bend." Ulyana's eyes moved across the square the way they moved across water, reading every current. "Which is even true, if you squint."

Marta bought flour, salt pork, dried apples, and a twist of tea, and she did it the way she did everything: slowly enough to see everything, quickly enough that nobody wondered why. It was while she was counting coins at the grocer's counter that she saw the horses.

Two of them, tied outside the far saloon, and both of them wrong. Not wrong like stolen — wrong like expensive. River-country men rode mules and plow horses. These were mountain-bred animals with the deep chests and hard feet of beasts that earned their keep, and their tack was oiled and matched, and nobody who could afford matched tack in a potato town was here for potatoes.

She finished her transaction. She thanked the grocer. She walked out into the square at a cook's unhurried pace and found Ulyana near the well, and said, quietly, "Two riders at the saloon. Matched tack. They're watching the street, not drinking."

Ulyana didn't turn her head. Marta watched her take one slow breath, the kind she took before running a rapids. "Describe them."

"Big one, gray beard, coat too fine for here. Younger one, lean, keeps his hand near his hip like it lives there."

"The Aldouses." Ulyana said it flat, without surprise, which frightened Marta more than shouting would have. "Or their men. They were always going to hire local trackers once we left the water. Word travels slower on foot, but it travels."

"So we go around."

"There's one road out of this valley going our direction, and it goes through this square." Ulyana's gaze swept the rooftops, the alleys, the church with its leaning steeple. Then it stopped. "There," she said. "The livery. Back exit?"

"Every livery has a back exit," Marta said. "Horses are cowardly animals. So are the men who build their houses."

They collected Tamir from the feed store, where he'd been chatting up the owner's daughter with the shameless ease of a twenty-two-year-old who had never yet met a consequence, and Marta watched Ulyana pull him toward the livery by the sleeve while he was still mid-sentence.

"What—"

"Don't talk. Walk."

They went through the livery's dim interior — hay dust hanging in the light, horses rolling their eyes at strangers — and out the back gate into a lane that ran along a creek behind the town. And that should have been the end of it, clean and dull, except that as they passed the second fence a man stepped out of a doorway ahead of them.

He wasn't one of the horsemen. He was local, rawboned, in a sheepskin vest, and he had the particular stillness of a man who has been paid to stand somewhere. His eyes went over the three of them — and stayed on Tamir.

"You'd be the river folk," he said. "Come up from the Queen."

Marta felt the temperature of the morning change.

"Freight handlers," Ulyana said pleasantly. "Walking to Copper Bend."

"Mm." The man smiled without warmth. "Man described you different. Said one of you was a woman captain, tall, carries herself like she owns whatever she's standing on. Said one of you was young, dark-haired, friendly as a dog." His eyes flicked to Tamir again. "Said one of you was an older woman, cooks, keeps to herself. Said they'd pay fifty dollars gold for word of where you crossed the pass, and a hundred for the bundle the old man gave you."

"Fifty dollars is insulting," Marta said. "For three people? I'd want two hundred minimum, and I'd want it in advance, because trackers who work on promises die owing money."

The man blinked. For a moment the professional stillness cracked and underneath it was just a hired man being contradicted by somebody's grandmother, and Marta used that moment the way she used everything — efficiently.

"Walk," she said, and turned, and to their credit neither Ulyana nor Tamir hesitated. Behind them the tracker swore and started after them, and then Ulyana did something Marta would think about for weeks afterward: she stopped, turned fully around, and looked at the man the way she looked at water that meant to kill her.

"You can follow us if you want," Ulyana said. "But understand what you're following. Those brothers you're working for sold a dying man's claim out from under him and burned his name off the books. When they're done needing you, you'll be a loose end, and loose ends are cheaper than witnesses. Fifty dollars won't cover your funeral. Go home."

Then she turned and walked, and Marta followed, and the tracker did not.

* * *

They didn't stop until the light went long and red, six miles up a creek drainage with the town hidden behind two ridges. They made cold camp — no fire, Marta's call, because smoke in a valley was a telegram — and sat among the rocks eating dried apples and hard bread, and it was Tamir who finally broke.

"He knew us." His voice had a crack in it Marta hadn't heard before. "Not 'might be.' Knew. Described us. Which means somebody talked, which means—" He stopped. Marta watched him arrive at it, watched the color come up his neck. "The landing where we took Ede aboard. Somebody at the landing sold us."

"Somebody at the landing sells everybody," Marta said. "That's what landings are for."

"But the bundle—" Tamir was looking at his hands. "The map. If they know Ede gave it to us, then they don't just want to scare us off. They need it. And they know exactly three people have it."

Marta looked at him for a long moment. There was something in his face she couldn't name yet — something shifting under the fear, like a fish under ice — and she filed it away the way she filed everything, in the cold pantry of her attention, to be dealt with later.

"Then we stop being three people they can find," Ulyana said. She was sitting with the compass in her palm, turning it over, not looking at the needle. "Tomorrow we leave the trail. Cut straight up the shoulder instead of following the creek, cross the pass at night, come down the far side where there's no road and no reason for anyone to be."

"No trail means no water sometimes," Marta said. "No trail means I carry more and we all eat less."

"Yes."

"Fine." Marta repacked the apples. "I've cooked on less than nothing before. Once, on the Maribelle, I made stew for forty men out of one elk and a rumor of onions. A week without a trail won't kill me. Might improve my temper."

Tamir laughed — a short, surprised sound, like he hadn't expected to have it in him. Ulyana almost smiled. And Marta thought, not for the first time, that this was the whole trick of surviving: not courage, exactly, but the refusal to let the fear choose the menu. You fed people anyway. You walked anyway. You made the joke about the onions, and the joke held the roof up one more night.

* * *

They crossed the pass on the third night after Brindle, in wind that came off the snowfields like something with intent. Marta climbed with her head down and her hands bare because gloves made her clumsy, and she counted her steps the way she counted anything — a hundred at a time, over and over — because counting kept the mind in the body and the body on the mountain.

Near the top, in the lee of a granite spine, Ulyana called a halt, and the three of them pressed against the rock while the wind tried to peel them off the world. Below, far below on the southern approach, Marta saw it: a thread of firelight, small as a struck match, where no honest traveler would camp.

"They're on the trail," Tamir breathed.

"They're a day behind and a route wrong," Ulyana said. "Let them warm themselves. We'll be over and gone by morning."

And they were. They came down the north side into a country of gray stone and white pine, silent as the inside of a pocket, and for two days Marta let herself believe the worst was behind them.

It was on the third morning, in a fold of the hills where a stream cut through a meadow stiff with frost, that they found the cairn.

Somebody had built it recently — the stones still showed dirt on their undersides — and on top of it, weighted with a flat rock, was a strip of birchbark with charcoal writing. Tamir reached for it and Marta caught his wrist.

"Could be tripped," she said. "Could be marked. Could be nothing." She took it herself, because she was the oldest and had decided long ago that if anything on this journey was going to kill one of them, it could start with her.

The bark read, in a careful hand:

**THREE CAME UP THE LOW PASS. TWO WENT OVER. WHERE IS THE THIRD?**

Below it, a crude drawing: a circle, a line through it. The mark the Aldous men had chalked on the Sable Queen's pilothouse door the night they boarded, back on the river. Marta had scrubbed it off herself.

They stood in the frost and looked at it, and the meadow that had seemed empty suddenly felt crowded.

"It's a message," Tamir said. "For us."

"It's a wedge," Marta said. "It's for whoever reads it and wonders about the other two. It's for us to distrust each other." She turned the bark over, found nothing, dropped it, and set her boot on it. "We came up three and we go over three. That's the whole answer. Anybody wants to count differently can climb up here and argue with the stones."

Ulyana was quiet a moment. Then she said, "You know, most people would've panicked."

"I've been panicking internally since 1849," Marta said. "It's very efficient now. Nobody can tell."

Tamir laughed again, and this time Ulyana laughed too, short and rough, and the three of them stood in the freezing meadow laughing at nothing, and Marta understood that this — this, exactly this — was why she'd left a warm galley and a moored boat to walk into mountains full of hired wolves. Not for the map. Not for the promise, which had never been hers to keep.

Because the Sable Queen had been a family the way a ship is a family, which is to say a family that could sink. And these two, standing in the frost with their breath smoking, were the part of it that floated.

"Come on," she said, shouldering her pack. "Copper Bend by dark. I'm making real food tonight, and if either of you complains about the onions, you can cook tomorrow."

They started down. And it was Tamir, twenty paces later, who stopped as if he'd walked into glass, and pointed at the far tree line, and said in a voice Marta had never heard him use:

"That's a survey party. Coming up the valley. And Ulyana — the woman leading it — she has Ede's eyes."
