## 10. The Compass Turns

The wind came off the shoulder of the range like something with an opinion.

Ulyana stood at the lip of the shelf they'd chosen for camp — a flat of granite wedged between two walls of scree, high enough that the pines below had given up trying to grow straight and instead leaned all one direction, combed flat by years of weather. The Sable Queen was three days behind her and a thousand feet down, moored at Dessa Vole's dock on the Graywater, and Ulyana had not let herself think about the boat since the morning she'd handed Dessa the line. Thinking about it was like pressing a bruise to check if it still hurt. It did. It always would.

"Wind's turning," Marta said behind her. She was crouched over the little iron stove they'd hauled up from the boat, coaxing heat out of damp scrub pine. "By morning it'll be in our faces. If we're crossing the saddle tomorrow, we cross early."

Tamir sat against his pack with his knees drawn up, working a whetstone along a skinning knife he'd owned for six years and never once used for its purpose. He'd been quiet since the market town. Quiet in a way Ulyana recognized and didn't like — the quiet of a man rehearsing something. She'd meant to ask him about it. She kept meaning to ask him about it, and kept finding reasons to wait, because the trail was thin enough already without putting weight on it.

"Early, then," she said. "We make the saddle before the light's fully up and we're over before anyone who's watching can pick our line."

"Anyone *is* watching," Tamir said, not looking up from the blade. "You know that."

"I know that."

The Aldouses hadn't shown themselves since the town — since the moment in the fish-smoke alley when Marta had grabbed both their sleeves and said, in her flat way, *split*, and they'd gone three directions through the drying racks and met again at dusk by a fallen bridge pier, breathing hard, nobody saying what all three of them knew: that the brothers had trackers now, local men, men who read ground the way Ulyana read water. Three days of doubling back, of walking creek beds to kill their sign, of camping cold because a fire was a signature. Tonight was the first night she'd allowed flame, and only because the wind would shred the smoke into nothing a tracker could trust.

She turned back to the camp and that was when she saw the compass.

It hung around her neck on its leather cord, Ede Voss's brass compass, the one he'd pressed into her hand with his last morning breath still warm on it. *True bearing,* he'd said. *A man can steer by anything, girl, but there's only one true bearing.* She'd taken it as the rambling of a dying man. Then Hale had boarded her boat and offered to erase her debt for whatever Ede carried, and she'd refused, and the words had started to feel less like rambling and more like freight.

She took it in her hand now, absently, the way you touch a rail in rough water. The case was warm from lying against her chest. She flipped the lid open to check the needle — habit, pure habit, the same motion she made every night, confirming north the way other people said their prayers.

The needle wasn't pointing north.

Ulyana went very still.

* * *

She'd noticed drift before. On the second day out from the river, the needle had wobbled east of true, and she'd blamed the ore in the hills — this whole range was shot through with iron and worse, and any prospector could tell you a compass was a liar in country like this. On the fourth day it had swung west and she'd blamed the stove, the iron pot, Tamir's knife. Instruments lied near metal. That was physics, that was seamanship, that was a thing you corrected for and moved on.

But tonight the needle wasn't drifting. It wasn't trembling or wandering or confused. It was *fixed*. Steady as a rivet, steady as a thing nailed down, pointing south-southeast and holding there while she turned the compass slowly in her palm — left, right, upside down and back — the needle swinging with the case and then settling, always, on the same bearing. South-southeast. Down the long fall of the mountain, past the dark comb of the pines, into the country they'd climbed away from.

"Marta," Ulyana said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Come look at this."

Mara wiped her hands on her apron and came. She looked at the compass for a long moment. She didn't touch it.

"That's not north," she said.

"No."

"You've been letting it lie to you for days."

"I've been correcting for it." Ulyana heard how that sounded even as she said it. Correcting for it. Carrying an instrument that pointed somewhere else entirely and telling herself the fault was in the ground. "There's iron everywhere in these hills. Any compass—"

"Any compass would wander." Marta's voice was dry as seed husks. She'd cooked on riverboats for thirty years and buried a husband and a son, and she had a way of speaking that made evasion sound like a child's excuse reported faithfully to a parent. "This one isn't wandering. This one has decided."

Tamir had stopped whetting the knife. He was watching them both, and there was something in his face Ulyana couldn't read, and didn't have time to.

She held the compass out at arm's length and sighted along the needle the way Ede must have done ten thousand times, the way she'd done a thousand times herself on the river, except on the river the needle always agreed with her and here it was insisting on something else. South-southeast. Down through the notch in the ridge where the land fell away in long timbered folds. Down toward the low country. Toward the valley.

She knew the valley.

That was the thing. That was the cold thing sitting down inside her chest like swallowed river water. She knew exactly what lay on that bearing, because she had spent fifteen years making sure she never once calculated it, and now the calculation had been done for her, by a dead man's brass instrument, with a steadiness that permitted no argument.

Twelve miles, maybe fourteen, on that line. Over the shoulder, down the timber, across the shallow gorge of the Alder Fork. And then the valley opened up — a wide green bowl the maps called Hatcher's Bend and the locals called nothing at all, because there was nothing left there to name. A mill town once. A mill town until the autumn Ulyana was nineteen years old, when it burned in a single night so hot and so complete that the fire crews from two counties arrived to find the fire already finished with its work, standing in the road watching the embers go up like slow black snow.

Her family had lived at the north end of the bowl. Her father ran the tally office. Her mother kept bees. Her brother Anders was eleven and wanted to be a pilot on the big river boats, and Ulyana, nineteen and already crewing for Corvin Hale, had come home that October with a consignment manifest and a hired dray and forty drums of lantern oil, and delivered them to the warehouses at the south end of the bend, and signed the paper, and driven back to the river, and told herself the warehouses were empty, because the man who paid her had said they were empty, and she had been nineteen and the money had been good and asking questions was not in her nature yet.

Three nights later the whole valley burned. Forty-one dead. The tally office. The bees. Anders, who never got to be a pilot.

And Ulyana Nystrom had stood on the deck of a Hale barge two hundred miles downstream and watched the smoke smear the northern horizon like a bruise on the sky, and understood — not slowly, not over years, but in one long terrible minute — exactly what she had hauled, and for whom, and what the word *empty* had been worth.

She had never spoken of it. Not to Marta, who had cooked beside her for nine years. Not to Tamir. Not to the river priests at the confluence landings, not in confession, not drunk, not once in the small hours when the truth climbs a person like floodwater. She had built her whole self on top of it the way you build a house on a filled-in cellar — carefully, load by load, never digging, never testing, knowing without admitting you knew that the ground under everything was hollow.

And now a dead man's compass was pointing at the cellar door.

* * *

"Ulyana." Marta's voice, closer now. "You've gone white as the granite. Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine."

"You're not, and I've known you too long for you to waste both our time pretending." Marta steered her to a rock by the fire with the gentle ruthlessness of a woman who had hauled bigger creatures than captains out of tighter places. "Give it here."

Ulyana gave her the compass. Watched Marta hold it, turn it, frown at it. Watched the needle ignore the fire iron, ignore the knife in Tamir's belt, ignore everything, and settle again on its bearing like a dog returning to a scent.

"Ede carried this twenty years," Marta said slowly. "Maybe longer. He was a surveyor before he was a prospector — he told me that the second night, when the fever was on him and he thought I was someone else. He trusted instruments more than people, he said. Said an instrument can't be bought." She looked up. "What's it pointing at, Captain?"

"A valley."

"What valley?"

Ulyana looked at the fire. The scrub pine popped and spat. High above them the first stars were coming out, hard and white, and somewhere down the black face of the mountain the wind moved through the timber with a sound like a crowd murmuring before bad news.

"One I used to know," she said.

Marta waited. Marta was very good at waiting; it was half her profession. Tamir had set the knife down entirely now, and he was looking at Ulyana with an expression she couldn't afford to examine closely — something hungry and frightened at once, the look of a man watching a strongbox being opened and wondering whether there'd be enough inside for him, or whether what came out would burn everyone in the room.

"There was a fire," Ulyana said. "Fifteen years ago. Hatcher's Bend. You might have heard of it — it was in the papers for a month. Mill town, burned clean, forty-one dead." She heard her own voice doing what it always did with that story: flattening it, filing the numbers smooth, handling it like cargo. "I grew up there."

Marta's face didn't change, but something behind it did. "You never said."

"There was never a reason to say."

"And the compass is pointing at it."

"The compass is pointing at it."

The wind gusted across the shelf, bending the flames sideways, and for a moment the three of them were lit and shadowed and lit again, like figures on a stage uncertain of their cue. Ulyana took the compass back from Marta. The brass was warm again immediately, or seemed to be — body heat, she told herself, nothing else, though some animal part of her had begun to insist otherwise, had begun whispering that Ede Voss had known exactly what he was handing her, that *true bearing* had never been about navigation at all, that the old man had spent his last feverish hours doing with a gift what she had spent fifteen years refusing to do with words.

He'd known what it was to carry a secret that ate its keeper. He'd abandoned a daughter rather than face his own ledger. And at the end, dying on her boat, he'd handed her the one instrument he owned that couldn't be bought — and maybe he'd known, somehow, the way dying men know things, that the woman carrying him home was carrying something heavier too.

*A man can steer by anything, girl. But there's only one true bearing.*

"Captain," Tamir said quietly. "If we follow that needle, we're going the wrong way. Kestrel Gap is northwest. That—" he gestured at the compass, at the dark southern sky beyond it, "—that's back down. Toward the river. Toward Hale's country. Toward the Aldouses' easiest ground."

"I know where it points, Tamir."

"It's broken. It has to be broken. Or the old man rigged it, or—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "We're three days ahead of the snow and four days behind the trackers, and you're talking about following a dead man's toy into a valley that isn't on our route."

"I'm not talking about anything," Ulyana said. "I'm holding a compass."

But even as she said it she felt the ground shift under the sentence. Because she *was* talking about it. Some part of her had been talking about it since the second night on the trail, since the first small drift of the needle, since the moment she'd caught herself checking the bearing not to confirm north but to see where else it might want to go. Fifteen years of disciplined silence, and a two-inch disc of brass had undone it in a week. Memory, she thought, was like water behind a dam — it didn't push. It just waited, and rose, and rose, patient as geology, until it found the crack.

And the crack had a shape. The crack had always had a shape. It was the shape of a dray loaded with oil drums, and a man named Corvin Hale signing her pay chit with the same hand that had later signed the insurance papers, and a warehouse foreman who'd looked at her — nineteen, tired, eager for the bonus — and said *empty buildings, girl, don't fret*, and she hadn't asked. God help her, she hadn't asked. Not one question. Not when the drums were heavier than lantern oil should be. Not when the delivery was scheduled for midnight. Not when Hale's clerk had torn her copy of the manifest out of her hands and told her the less she remembered the route the better for everyone.

Forty-one dead, and the only living soul outside Hale's circle who knew what had gone into those warehouses the week before the fire was standing on a granite shelf twelve miles from the ashes, holding a compass that pointed at them like an accusation.

* * *

"We sleep," Marta said at last, into the silence. "All three of us. And in the morning we decide with daylight heads, not wind-blown ones." She began banking the fire, moving with the economy of long practice, and her voice softened by a degree it almost never did. "Whatever that needle knows, Captain, it'll still know it at dawn."

Ulyana nodded. She coiled the compass cord back around her neck and tucked the instrument against her chest, and felt it there like a second heartbeat, stubborn and off-beat, pulling always south-southeast.

She lay on her bedroll and did not sleep. Above her the stars wheeled the way they do at altitude, fast and pitiless, and below her, down the black folds of the mountain, the valley lay in the dark with its dead. Her father's tally books. Her mother's hives. Anders, eleven years old forever, who had once asked her what the big river was like and been told *someday I'll take you*, a promise she had kept as badly as she kept all of them.

Hale's contract had built half the river trade on the back of that fire. She'd known it for years the way you know a load is shifting — not looking at it, feeling it. And now the Aldouses were behind her and Hale's deadline was ahead of her and the snow was coming down off the peaks like a creditor, and a dead prospector's compass was demanding interest on a debt she'd believed was buried.

Near midnight, Tamir stirred across the fire. She heard him sit up. Heard him look at her — she felt it, the way you feel eyes on the river at night.

"Captain," he whispered. "That valley. What happened there?"

Ulyana stared at the stars and found, to her horror, that the answer was already climbing her throat — fifteen years of held water finding its crack at last, rising, rising, patient as geology.

"Everything," she said. "Everything happened there. And tomorrow I'm going to tell you what."
