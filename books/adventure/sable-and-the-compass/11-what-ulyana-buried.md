## 11. What Ulyana Buried

The compass did not point north.

Ulyana had told herself, for three days now, that the needle was simply tired — that brass instruments went soft the way old men did, that a knock on the trail from Graywater Landing had knocked something loose in its little heart. She had held it up at dawn and squinted at it the way she'd squint at a lazy deckhand, willing it back to true. It had not gone back to true. It hung there in its housing, steady as a nail, pointing southeast into a fold of mountains she knew better than she wanted to.

She knew because she'd run freight through that valley for six years. She knew because the river bent around it like a scar bending around a wound. She knew because fifteen years ago, when she was nineteen and hungry and grateful to Corvin Hale for noticing her at all, she had steered a load of lantern oil up that valley road in a wagon with bad axles, and she had delivered it to a row of buildings Hale's foreman told her were empty warehouses awaiting demolition.

They had not been empty.

* * *

The fire came back to her the way rivers come back after flood — not all at once but in layers, silt over silt, each one heavier than the last.

First layer: the wagon. She remembered the wagon precisely, because she had complained about it. The left rear axle sang a high thin note whenever they topped a rise, and she had said so to the foreman, Doss Merrick, who walked beside her with his hands in his coat pockets and his eyes on the middle distance, the way men look when they are listening to instructions in their heads rather than to you.

"It'll hold," he said. "It always holds."

"Everything holds until it doesn't."

He laughed at that. She remembered being pleased that he laughed. That was the part that still made her sick twenty years later — not the axle, not the oil, not even the fire itself, but how pleased she'd been. Nineteen years old, third season working Hale's freight line out of Marrow Bend, and here was Doss Merrick walking beside her like she mattered, laughing at her joke like she was quick. Nobody else on the line talked to her. She was the girl who could read water, who could back a wagon down a landing ramp without cracking a plank, and the men treated her like a tool that happened to talk. Doss treated her like a person. Or she had thought he did. Later she understood that what he'd treated her like was a courier who wouldn't ask questions, and that he'd chosen her for exactly the qualities that made her lonely: young, eager, unconnected, invisible enough to move through a town without leaving an impression.

"You know what's in these barrels?" she'd asked him, somewhere past the second mile. Not suspicious. Making conversation. Wanting him to keep talking to her.

"Lantern oil. For the demolition crews." He said it easily, the way you say a thing you've said before. "Old Hale bought up that block last spring. Warehouses, mostly rotten. They're burning them down legal, permit and everything, clearing ground for the new depot."

"Burning them down? I thought you demolished things."

"Saves money." He shrugged. "Fire's cheaper than labor. Always has been."

She had nodded along. Fire's cheaper than labor. It sounded like wisdom. It sounded like the kind of hard practical truth a girl learned if she was going to make it in the freight business, and she had tucked it away like a coin in a pocket, pleased again, pleased to be let in on how the world actually worked.

The barrels were stenciled LAMP OIL — KETTLE FALLS REFINERY, forty of them, and they rode heavy and sloshed when the wagon took the ruts wrong. She remembered the smell of them leaking slightly at the bungs, sweet and mineral, soaking into the wagon bed. She remembered thinking the whole rig smelled like a lamp shop, like warmth, like winter evenings below decks with a book propped against her knee. Oil meant light. That was her whole association with it. She was nineteen; her associations were shallow as a saucer.

Second layer: the town. The valley settlement didn't have a name she ever learned officially — people called it the Bend, because the river bent there, and the bend made a flat shelf of land where somebody had built a mill, and where there was a mill there were houses, and where there were houses there was a church with a lean-topped steeple, and a schoolhouse, and a street of shops with painted signs. It sat close under the warehouses — closer than any sensible planner would have put it, but the shelf of land was narrow and nobody had imagined a fire.

Nobody had imagined a fire. That sentence lived in her chest now like a swallowed hook.

Doss had her unload at the warehouse row just after dark. There were men waiting — four or five of them, moving quick and quiet, no lanterns lit, which struck her then as thrift and strikes her now as exactly what it was. They rolled the barrels off her wagon and into the nearest building without a word of thanks, and Doss pressed coins into her hand and said, "Head back down the valley, Uly. Don't stop till you're across the ford."

"Why?"

"Because Mr. Hale wants you across the ford," he said, and smiled, and touched two fingers to his hat brim, and that was the end of the conversation, because she was nineteen and a man had smiled at her and given her orders, and obedience dressed up as trust felt almost like belonging.

She got maybe three miles. She was watering the team at the ford when the sky behind her turned orange.

Third layer: the sound. People think fire is quiet, and alone, at a distance, it is — a kind of vast soft roaring like wind in a canyon. But a town burning is not quiet. A town burning has voices in it. She heard them across three miles of dark valley, thin and terrible, and she heard the church bell, ringing and ringing, not an alarm at first but just ringing, the way bells ring when the man whose job is the bell cannot think of anything else to do with his hands.

She stood in the ford with the reins slack in her grip and the horses blowing steam, and she watched the glow climb, and some animal part of her brain did the arithmetic before the rest of her would allow it: the oil, the barrels, the men with no lanterns, the order to cross the ford, the smile. The arithmetic finished itself whether she consented or not. She had hauled the fire up that valley. She had been the match's delivery service.

She turned the wagon around. She drove back toward it. She got within a mile before the heat reached her across the open ground, a wall of it, pushing like a hand on her chest, and the road filled with people coming the other way — burned, blackened, carrying children, carrying nothing, faces lit orange from below like masks. And in that crowd, in the orange light, she saw a woman she recognized. Not by name. By face. A woman who had waved at her wagon earlier that evening as Ulyana rolled through the settlement street — a small courtesy, a wave between strangers, the kind of thing you forget in an hour.

Except the woman was carrying a child now, and the child wasn't moving right, and the woman's face was the face Ulyana would see for the next twenty years every time she closed her eyes in daylight.

Ulyana did not stop the wagon. That is the fact underneath all the other facts, the bedrock under the silt. She did not stop. She pulled the team aside onto the shoulder and let the procession of the burned pass her, and she sat there with her hands locked on the reins, and she told herself she was in the way, she'd only slow them down, there was nothing she could do, and every word of it was true and none of it mattered, because the real reason — the reason she has never once said aloud in twenty years — is that stopping meant being seen. Being seen meant questions. Questions meant the barrels, the stencils, the delivery, her name in a ledger in Corvin Hale's office. She was nineteen and she chose the ledger over the child. Not in words. In wheel tracks. But choices don't need words. The river doesn't announce which channel it takes. It just takes one, and then it is the river's shape forever.

By morning the settlement was ash and chimneys. Eleven dead, the papers said, though everyone knew the count ran higher because the mill families kept boarders who never made any list. The cause was reported as a defective flue in the mill office. There was an inquiry. Nobody from the valley had standing to press it further, and the ones who might have were dead or scattered, and Corvin Hale sent a wagon of relief goods down the valley with his own name stenciled on the crates, and the territorial paper called him a public benefactor.

Ulyana read that article sitting in the galley of a Hale freight boat, holding it very still, the way you hold still when you're standing on ice and you've heard it crack.

* * *

She never spoke of it. That was the discipline, the great labor of her adult life, more demanding than any sandbar, more constant than any tide. She built her silence the way other captains build seawalls — course by course, stone by stone. She changed employers within the year, quietly, citing better wages. She moved to the Sable Queen and the river trade, where nobody asked about her past because on the river your past is the least interesting thing about you; the river only cares what you can do tomorrow. She became excellent at tomorrow. She became famous, in a small way, for tomorrow — for reading water, for backing loads, for getting through. And every night, lying in her bunk while the current muttered against the hull, she performed the nightly maintenance of the seawall: *You didn't know. You were told they were empty. Doss lied to you, Hale lied through him, you were a tool, tools aren't guilty.* And most nights the wall held. Most nights she slept.

But walls have a cost, and the cost is this: whatever you wall off doesn't die. It waits. It grows roots into everything adjacent. Her refusal to carry passengers for Hale's line — Tamir thought it was business sense. It was the woman in the orange light. Her hatred of cargo she couldn't inspect personally — Marta thought it was fussiness. It was forty stenciled barrels. The way she checked and rechecked manifests, the way she needed to know what she was carrying, always, all of it, down to the last crate — the crew called it thoroughness and praised her for it, and she accepted the praise like a thief accepting change, because thoroughness was just the seawall wearing a work shirt.

And now she sat on a frozen mountainside with a dead man's compass in her palm, and the needle pointed at the valley, and the seawall was coming down.

Not all at once. Walls don't fall; they dissolve, grain by grain, once the water finds the crack. The crack had been Ede Voss. Because Ede, dying in her bunk, feverish and far from anything he loved, had gripped her wrist and said, *Carry it true, Captain. A lie'll steer you wronger than any current,* and she had taken his promise knowing — knowing in the place she kept the barrels and the bell and the orange light — that she was the wrongest-steering vessel on the river. She had accepted custody of another man's truth while sitting on top of her own buried lie, and the two weights together had been more than the hull could bear, and somewhere on the trail between Graywater and the low pass, the seams had started to open.

Tamir appeared at the edge of camp, stamping snow off his boots, saying something about the firewood being wet. She heard herself answer. Some captain-part of her was still running the boat, giving orders, checking the compass, watching the ridgeline for riders. That part never stopped. That was the horror of it — the competent part of her carried on perfectly well while the rest of her drowned. She had spent fifteen years being two women: the one who piloted, and the one who watched the pilot and knew.

Marta handed her tea. Marta looked at her a moment too long, the way cooks do, the way people who feed others learn to read hunger of every kind. "You sleep," Marta said. It wasn't a suggestion.

Ulyana lay in her bedroll and did not sleep. She stared at the canvas overhead and the fire played shadows on it, and the shadows were orange, and the bell rang somewhere behind her sternum, and she understood — with the flat, cold clarity that comes at altitude, where the air is too thin to support a lie — what the compass was doing.

It wasn't broken. It wasn't pointing at silver, either. Ede had told her once, in a lucid hour, that a good compass finds what you can't fake your way to. She'd taken it for an old man's poetry. It wasn't poetry. The needle pointed at the valley because the valley was the bearing she had refused to take for fifteen years. Every route she'd chosen since — the river, the debt, the boat, the whole careful circuit of her life — had been a way of sailing around that one stretch of water. And the compass, patient as water, was simply refusing to go around anymore.

Tomorrow they would cross the pass. Ahead of them were the Aldouses, and Hale, and snow, and a girl named Ingrid Voss who hated her father. Somewhere ahead, Ulyana knew with certainty she couldn't explain to Tamir or Marta, there would be a moment when keeping Ede's promise would require telling the truth about herself — because promises delivered by liars rot in transit, and she would not hand that girl a package wrapped in her own silence.

She would have to say it. Out loud. To a stranger. *I hauled the oil. I saw the crowd coming out of the fire, and I did not stop.*

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange detachment, the way you watch someone else's hands, and then she pressed them flat against the frozen ground, against the mountain, and felt how solid it was. The mountain didn't care what she'd done. The mountain would carry her weight regardless. It was the only honest creditor she'd ever had.

In the morning, breaking camp, Tamir glanced at the compass hanging at her neck and frowned. "It's still doing that?" he said. "Pointing off?"

Ulyana looked southeast, down the long white shoulder of the range, toward the smoke-colored memory of a valley she had sworn never to enter again.

"No," she said. "For the first time in fifteen years, it's pointing exactly where I'm going."
