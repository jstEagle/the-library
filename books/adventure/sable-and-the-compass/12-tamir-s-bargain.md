## 12. Tamir's Bargain

Tamir had never been good at sleeping in the mountains. The river rocked you. The mountains just sat there, enormous and indifferent, and every sound a mountain made sounded like footsteps.

He lay in his bedroll listening to one of those sounds now — or telling himself it was nothing, which amounted to the same thing — while Ulyana slept ten feet away with the compass on a cord around her neck and the map rolled inside her coat, and Marta slept the way Marta did everything, efficiently, without apology.

The fire had burned down to a red eye. Above the trees, the stars were hard and close, like nails driven into black wood.

*They've hired trackers,* Marta had said that morning, in her flat way, as if reporting the price of flour. *Two men asking after a riverboat crew gone overland. One of them had your description.*

*My description?*

*Young. Nervous. Walks like he's sorry for taking up space.* She'd shrugged. *I told them we were fur traders heading south. They didn't believe me, but they didn't have anything better.*

That was three days ago. Since then Tamir had done arithmetic he couldn't stop doing.

The Aldouses wanted the map destroyed. Hale wanted the map bought. Ulyana wanted the map delivered to a woman who, by all accounts, didn't want it. And Tamir wanted — this was the part he kept circling, the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone — Tamir wanted out. Out of the mountains, out of the cold, out of the long slow drowning of working a boat he'd never own for a woman who'd never be free of it either.

There was money in that stone. He could feel its weight.

He'd seen the Aldouses' kind before, back when he ran cargo on the lower river: men who paid cash for small services and never asked twice, because asking twice meant witnesses. A man who knew where a thing was hidden was worth more than the thing itself, sometimes. Worth enough to buy a berth on a steamer south. Worth enough to stop being a deckhand at twenty-three and start being something else.

He turned the thought over the way you turn a coin you're ashamed to have found.

Then he got up, quiet as a man stealing from his own house, and walked out past the firelight into the dark.

* * *

The market town was called Ferrow's Crossing, though there wasn't much crossing left in it since the mine played out — a straggle of buildings along a creek road, a saloon with a false front, a livery stable that smelled of honest horses and dishonest deals. Tamir came down off the trail two days later with a story ready: he was buying salt pork and boot nails for his party, which was even true, because Marta had sent him, which was also true, and the truest lies were always built on errands.

The trackers were easy to find. They wanted to be found. That was the whole design of them — two men sitting in plain view on the saloon porch, hats tipped back, watching the road the way spiders watch a web they've already spun.

Tamir made himself walk past them once. Just once, slow, letting his eyes catch and slide away. Then he went into the dry goods store and bought the salt pork and the boot nails and stood at the counter hating how loud his own breathing was.

When he came out, one of them was leaning against the rail not four feet from him. Younger than Tamir expected. Fair-haired, with a broken nose that had healed crooked, giving his face a permanent expression of mild disappointment.

"Salt pork," the man said, reading the package. "Long way from any boat."

"We're traveling."

"Fur traders." The man smiled without warmth. "That's what the fat one said too. Marta, is it? Cooks for a captain named Nystrom. Boat called the Sable Queen." He said the name gently, like handling something breakable. "You people should pick stories that don't contradict each other."

"I don't know what you want."

"I want my boots resoled and my brother to stop drinking before noon." The man pushed off the rail. "What I'm *paid* to want is information. So let me save us both some time. You're going to talk to somebody eventually — a man alone in the mountains talks to whoever's near. When you do, remember that my brother and I are patient, and Captain Nystrom is not rich, and dead men's maps spend poorly."

"I don't know what map you mean."

"No," the man agreed pleasantly. "You don't. But you know where she keeps it, and you know where she sleeps, and you know how many guns your party carries, because I watched you count them this morning when you thought nobody saw." He touched his hat brim. "Think about what that makes you worth. To us. Or to her, if she finds out you talked. Both numbers are interesting."

He walked away, unhurried, and Tamir stood in the road holding salt pork and feeling the exact temperature of the trap he'd walked into with his eyes open.

Because here was the thing — the thing that would keep him awake for the rest of his life if he lived long enough to have one: he hadn't come to town for salt pork. He'd come to find them. He'd lain awake two nights rehearsing how to do it. *I can tell you where the map is. I need passage south and two hundred dollars.* He'd practiced saying it in a whisper under his breath until the words stopped sounding like betrayal and started sounding like business.

And the fair-haired man had simply walked up and collected him, the way you collect a debt already owed.

* * *

Marta met him at the edge of camp with her arms crossed and her face arranged into nothing at all, which was worse than anger.

"You were gone six hours. Salt pork takes one."

"Had to wait on the nails."

"Mm." She took the package, weighed it in one hand like she was weighing him instead. "There were two men on the porch of the Ferrow saloon. Did you see them?"

"I saw them."

"And?"

"And nothing. I bought pork." He heard his own voice go up half a tone and hated it. "Marta, I bought *pork*."

She looked at him for a long moment. Marta's silences were load-bearing; you could stack a whole accusation on them and they wouldn't buckle. Then she said, "Ulyana trusts you. That's an expensive thing to be given, boy. Cheaper things get stolen every day." She turned toward the fire. "Supper's in an hour. Wash your hands."

He washed his hands. They looked exactly the same afterward, which felt like a personal insult.

* * *

That night he couldn't sleep again, and this time it wasn't the mountains.

It was the memory of Ede Voss dying by inches in the hold of the Sable Queen, gripping Tamir's wrist with a hand like a bundle of dry sticks, saying, *Boy. Boy, does she keep her word?* And Tamir had said yes — had said it easily, proudly, because at that moment it was true, because Ulyana Nystrom keeping promises badly but absolutely was the one fixed star in a sky full of debt and smoke. *Yes,* he'd said. *Better than anyone.*

Ede had closed his eyes then, satisfied, and handed over twenty years of regret wrapped in oilcloth, trusting a stranger's answer about a stranger's honor.

And now here was Tamir, that same stranger, lying in the dark composing offers to the men who'd hunted that dying old man across half a territory.

He tried the usual defenses. *They'll kill her anyway whether I talk or not. I'd only be saving myself. Isn't survival the first law?* But the defenses kept collapsing, because underneath them was the simple rotten truth: nobody had threatened him. Nobody had put a knife to his throat. The fair-haired man had named a price and Tamir had spent two nights deciding whether he could live with taking it, and the fact that the deciding was taking so long told him everything about what he'd become out here in the cold, hungry, scared, far from the river that used to define him.

He got up a third time. This time he didn't stop at the edge of the firelight. He walked.

* * *

He found the younger Aldous brother alone, which surprised him — he'd assumed the brothers moved as one animal, the way dangerous pairs do. But there he was at the horse trough behind the livery, splashing water on his face in the gray hour before dawn, and when he straightened up and saw Tamir standing there, he didn't reach for a weapon. He just smiled that disappointed smile.

"Well," he said. "The nervous one."

"I want to make a trade."

"Do you." He shook water off his hands. "Say it fast. My brother wakes ugly."

"The map. I can tell you where it is. Where she keeps it, where she sleeps, how many guns." The words came out exactly as rehearsed, which somehow made them worse — proof he'd polished them, turned them over night after night like a coin. "Two hundred dollars and passage south. On a boat. Not a wagon, not walking. A boat."

The younger brother studied him with something that wasn't quite respect and wasn't quite pity, and might have been recognition. "You've been practicing that."

"Answer me."

"Here's my answer." He stepped closer, and up close his eyes were pale and empty as winter sky. "No."

"No?"

"No. Because a man who sells his captain once will sell her twice, and sell *us* after that, and I don't buy merchandise that comes pre-rotted." He said it without heat, almost kindly. "But thank you. Genuinely. Because now I know three things I didn't know yesterday. I know she keeps the map on her body, or you'd have offered something easier. I know your party's short on guns, or you'd have offered to take it yourself. And I know your face." He tapped his own cheekbone with one finger. "That last one's the valuable one, boy. When this ends — and it ends soon, snows are coming — everyone who helped her answers for it. Everyone who *almost* helped us gets a choice. You've just made yourself eligible for both lists."

"I didn't tell you anything."

"You told me everything by standing there." The younger brother swung up onto his horse with an ease Tamir envied down to his bones. "Go home to your captain. Watch her sleep. Think about whose side you were actually on tonight, and whether it was hers." He gathered the reins. "Oh — and the offer stands. Two hundred dollars. Passage south. Any time before the passes close. Men like you always come back around. It's not a character flaw. It's just weather."

He rode off into the gray, and Tamir stood alone in the livery yard with his bargain still in his mouth, unspent, undelivered — aborted, but not undone. The offer existed now. It had been spoken aloud into the world, and the world had heard it, and the world kept records.

* * *

He climbed back to camp as the sun came up, and Ulyana was already awake, sitting by the rekindled fire with the compass in her palm, watching the needle do whatever strange thing it did these days. She looked up when his boots crunched the frost.

"You were gone early," she said.

"Couldn't sleep."

"Nobody sleeps up here." She nodded at the ground across the fire. "Sit. Eat. Today we make the high shoulder before noon or we make nothing."

He sat. He ate. The beans tasted like nothing, like punishment, like every meal he'd ever steal in the future.

And then Ulyana, without looking up from the compass, said quietly: "Ferrow's Crossing has two new riders in it. Marta counted their horses. Four, between them, and fresh shoes." She turned the compass slowly in her fingers. "One of them fits your description, Tamir. Young. Nervous. Walks like he's sorry for taking up space."

The fire popped. Somewhere above the tree line, a bird cried once and quit.

"So," Ulyana said, and finally raised her eyes to his, and they were calm the way deep water is calm, "you want to tell me what you traded in town yesterday? Because I'm going to find out either way, and I'd rather hear it from you while I can still choose what it costs."

The compass needle trembled between them, pointing at nothing north at all.
