## 13. First Snow

Marta smelled it before anyone saw it. Twenty years of feeding men on riverboats and trail camps had given her a nose for weather the way other women had a nose for burning bread, and what she smelled now was iron — that flat, mineral nothing-smell that meant snow coming down off the high country like a debt collector.

She said nothing at first. She watched Ulyana instead.

The captain had stopped in the middle of the trail an hour past noon and stood with the brass compass open in her palm, turning slowly, frowning at it the way she frowned at a sandbar that had shifted overnight. Tamir was up ahead breaking trail through the scrub pine, his breath ragged, his shoulders carrying more than his pack these days. Marta had been watching him too. She watched everyone. It was how she'd survived three husbands, two boat fires, and one famine winter — by noticing things before they became problems.

"Captain," she said quietly, coming alongside. "That sky's wrong."

Ulyana didn't look up from the compass. "It's pointing south-southeast again."

"I don't care if it's pointing to the moon. Look west."

Ulyana looked. Marta watched her read the sky the way she read water — the bruised light pressing down on the peaks, the wind gone still and heavy, the smell of it. Ulyana had grown up on this river country; she knew what a storm looked like before it knew itself.

"How far to shelter?" Marta asked.

"There's supposed to be old workings along this shoulder. Ede talked about them once — claims he walked out of, back when he still talked about anything." She closed the compass. "If there were workings, there were cabins."

"If," Marta said. "There's a word I've never liked."

They moved. That was the whole of their strategy for eleven days now: move, and then move some more. The Aldouses' trackers had lost them after the market town, or so Ulyana believed, but Marta put no faith in *so*. Men who hired trackers did not stop tracking because the trail got cold. They got better dogs.

Tamir came back down the line to walk beside her, which he'd been doing more often lately, and she understood why without asking. Guilt walks toward the people it trusts. He'd been strange since the town — jumpy, over-generous with his share of the watch, laughing at things that weren't funny. Marta had raised no children of her own but she'd fed enough young men to know the shape of a boy carrying something rotten in his pocket.

"You're favoring your left knee," she said.

"It's fine."

"It's not fine, and tonight I'll wrap it, and you'll let me." She handed him her water skin. "Drink. You breathe like a bellows and sweat like a liar."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. Then the first flakes came down between the trees, fat and slow and unhurried, the way snow falls when it has decided to fall all day and all night and doesn't need to prove anything.

* * *

They found the cabin as the light died.

It wasn't much — a squat stone-and-timber ruin shouldering into the slope below a shelf of granite, half its roof caved in, the door hanging from one leather hinge some long-dead prospector had cut and oiled. But it faced away from the wind, and it had a hearth, and above all it had walls, and Marta had learned on the river that walls were the difference between weather and catastrophe.

"Ede's mark," Ulyana said, crouching by the doorpost.

Marta looked. Scratched into the wood, low where a man would carve it kneeling, was a small V with a bar through it — the same mark they'd found stamped into his tin cup, his survey stakes, the wax seals on his letters. A man signs the places he means to come back to.

"He lived here," Tamir said. "Actually lived here."

"For a while, anyway." Ulyana pushed the door open. "Everyone lives somewhere for a while."

Inside, the cabin was one room, maybe twelve feet by fifteen, with a stone hearth at the far end and a bunk frame against the wall, its planks rotted to lace. Snow was sifting through the broken roof in slow gray threads, piling soft on the dirt floor. There was a table made of a split plank on trestles. There was a stool. There was nothing else, and yet the room had the particular emptiness of a place someone had loved badly and left anyway.

Marta set about making it livable, because that was her work in this world and she did it without being asked. She cleared the snow, patched the worst of the roof gap with the oilcloth from her pack, built a fire in the hearth using wood Tamir split from the fallen door — the hinge-leather burned sweet and sad. She put barley and salt pork and dried onions in the pot, because hot food was the one sermon she believed in.

Ulyana went out twice in the gathering dark to check the trail behind them. The second time she came back white-faced and quiet.

"Well?" Marta asked.

"Snow's filling our tracks. By morning there won't be a sign we passed." Ulyana held her hands to the fire. "Could be a mercy. Could be a trap closing."

"Could be both," Marta said. "Most things are."

Tamir sat close to the fire with his knee wrapped, saying little. Marta caught him looking at the walls — at the walls themselves, not at anything on them — and it took her a while to understand why, and longer to wish she hadn't.

Because the walls were covered in writing.

Scratched into the timber with a knife point, in columns and columns of a cramped, slanting hand: numbers, dates, assay figures, fragments of song lyrics, a prayer in a language none of them spoke, tally marks by the hundred. And near the hearth, where the firelight reached, whole paragraphs. A man alone in the mountains for years will talk to his walls, and Ede Voss had been a man alone for a very long time.

"Look at this," Tamir said softly. He'd risen despite his knee and was tracing the scratches with one finger. "It's him. It's all him."

Marta came to stand beside him. The firelight moved across the scratched words, making them seem almost alive, almost fresh, as though the man who wrote them might have stepped out minutes ago to check a snare.

*Third season. Silver shows true in the east vein. Enough to buy back everything.*

*Wrote to Ingrid again. No answer. Wrote anyway.*

*Hale came up himself. Hale. In MY cabin, drinking MY coffee, telling me what my claim is worth and what it isn't. A man who drinks your coffee is measuring your cup.*

Marta felt the cold move through her that had nothing to do with the roof gaps.

"Hale," she said aloud, and the name sat in the small room like a fourth traveler.

Ulyana had gone very still by the fire. Marta watched her — watched the way her eyes fixed on that last line and stayed there, the way her breathing changed, shallow and careful, the way a woman breathes when something inside her has begun to crack and she is holding the pieces together by force of habit.

"Captain?" Marta said.

"Keep reading," Ulyana said. Her voice was level. Too level. Marta had heard that voice once before, years ago, the night a deckhand fell overboard in flood current and Ulyana gave orders for six hours without her voice once shaking, and only afterward, alone in the pilothouse, had Marta heard her break.

Tamir read on, his finger moving down the wall.

*Aldous brothers offered partnership. Declined. They smile like men counting your teeth.*

*Sold me out. Both of them. Filed papers in Kestrel Gap I never signed, or signed drunk, or signed lies — same thing in a courtroom. My own claim, filed under their names.*

*I'll take the map to a lawyer in the Gap. Evidence. It's evidence, not treasure. Wish someone had told me twenty years ago the difference.*

*If I die up here, whoever finds this — the map matters. Not the silver. The paper. Paper is the only thing they can't dig out of the ground and steal.*

Below that, smaller, deeper-carved, as though the knife had pressed hard enough to hurt:

*Ingrid. If you ever see this. I kept the bearing. I just never followed it home.*

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Snow whispered against the oilcloth patch overhead, patient as sand in an hourglass.

Marta looked at Ulyana again and saw something she had never seen on the Sable Queen's decks, not in the worst storm, not facing Corvin Hale himself across a cabin table: fear. Not of the brothers, not of the mountain. Something older and closer than either.

"The compass," Marta said quietly. "It's been pulling you southeast for days. Where does southeast go, Captain?"

Ulyana didn't answer. She looked at the wall, at the words *I just never followed it home*, and Marta saw her jaw set in the particular way of a woman deciding to keep carrying something.

"Get some sleep," Ulyana said. "We move at first light, storm or no storm. We're losing days."

"We could lose our lives going down that shoulder in a whiteout," Marta said.

"We could lose them staying here, too, if those tracks fill and the brothers' dogs don't." Ulyana turned away, toward the door, toward the dark. "I'll take first watch."

"That's my watch," Marta said.

"Tonight it's mine."

And Marta, who argued with captains about portions and pace and the proper soaking of beans, did not argue about this. Some refusals are doors you don't knock on.

* * *

Marta woke in the black hours to the sound of scratching.

For one disoriented moment she thought it was the storm against the oilcloth. Then she realized Tamir was awake beside her, sitting up, staring at the wall by the dying firelight, and that the scratching was Ulyana — out there in the cold doorway with the wind knifing through the gap, carving something into the doorpost next to Ede's little barred V.

Marta lay still and listened to the small, stubborn sound of it. When Ulyana came back in, cheeks raw with cold, Marta pretended to sleep, and in the morning, going out to see to the firewood, she looked.

Beneath Ede's mark, in a woman's harder hand:

*U.N. Passed here. Kept the promise. Still keeping it.*

Marta stood a long moment in the falling snow, reading it. Then she went back inside and made breakfast, because that was her work, and because the mountain had stripped the rest of them down to essentials — fear, guilt, hope, hunger — and somebody had to keep the essentials fed.

They broke camp as the storm thickened around them. At the door, Tamir paused, and Marta saw him look back at the walls — at all those years of a man talking to timber because no one else would listen — and she saw him mouth something, silent, that might have been *sorry*.

Then the three of them stepped out into the white roar of the shoulder, and behind them the cabin swallowed its secrets again, and ahead — far below through a tearing in the clouds — Marta glimpsed smoke rising thin and straight from a valley camp.

Smoke meant people.

People, this deep in the range, this late in the year, meant either salvation or the Aldouses.

Marta pulled her collar up and followed her captain down toward it.
