## 14. The Aldouses Close In

The river came down out of the high country in a hurry, white and loud, and where it bent around a shoulder of granite it spread wide and shallow over gravel — the kind of crossing that looked easy and wasn't. Ulyana stood at the edge of it with her hands on her hips and read the water the way she'd been reading water since she was nine years old.

"Mid-channel's fastest," she said. "That's where it'll be knee-deep but honest. The edges look gentler. They're lying."

Marta snorted from behind her, shifting the pack on her shoulders. "Everything lies to you, Captain."

"Only the things worth trusting." She pointed with her chin. "We cross in line, arm to arm if we have to. Tamir, you're smallest — you go middle. If your feet go, you grab whoever's next to you and you *hold*, understand? The current here will take you a quarter mile before it spits you out, and there's a falls after that."

Tamir looked at the falls — a distant white stutter in the gorge below — and went pale in a way that had nothing to do with the cold. "Understood."

They'd been three days out of the ruined cabin in the pass, three days of snowmelt light and hard travel, and for three days Ulyana had felt it: the sensation of being watched that she'd learned on the river, where a sandbar could cost you everything and the men who wanted what you carried never announced themselves. She hadn't said anything. There was nowhere to run to that would be better than where they were, and saying it aloud would only make Marta sleep worse and Tamir jumpier than he already was.

But the feeling hadn't lifted. It had narrowed. Like something circling.

She tested the first step into the ford and the cold hit her shins like a slap. The compass hung at her throat under her coat, and she did not take it out. She didn't need to. For two days now she'd been aware of it the way you're aware of a bruise — a steady wrongness against her sternum, the needle pulling not north but toward the valley behind them, toward the fire, toward everything she'd spent fifteen years walking away from at exactly the pace that kept it from catching up.

*Later,* she told it. *One thing at a time.*

The water rose to their knees by mid-channel, then their thighs. Marta cursed steadily and creatively in two languages. Tamir gripped Ulyana's sleeve so hard she'd have bruises tomorrow. The current shoved at them like a crowd leaving a burning building, and Ulyana leaned into it and thought about nothing but footing, angle, weight — the pure arithmetic of staying upright.

Which is why she heard the shot only as a crack, late, flattened by the roar of the water, and understood it by the way Marta's hand closed on her arm and then slipped.

"Marta—"

"I'm fine." But Marta's voice was wrong, tight and small, and when they came up the far bank together, dragging themselves out onto the gravel bar, there was blood on Marta's sleeve, welling dark along the outside of her forearm. A graze. A long, ugly graze, torn open by a bullet that had been meant, Ulyana understood with a clarity like ice water, for someone else. For her.

"Down!" She hauled them both behind a shelf of rock at the treeline, and the second shot came then, cracking off stone above their heads and singing away into the trees.

"The ford," Marta said through her teeth, pressing her arm. "They let us get halfway. They wanted us in the water."

Of course they had. A man in mid-river is a man who can't run. Ulyana pressed her back to the rock and made herself think. Two shots, spaced — one rifleman working alone, or two men taking turns. She knew the answer before she finished the question. She'd known it since the landing at Hale's dock, since the two riders on the bank who had watched the Sable Queen like wolves watching a lame elk.

"Aldous," she said quietly. "Both of them."

"How do you know?"

"Because they're patient." She pulled Marta's jacket open and looked at the wound. Long, shallow, bleeding freely but not spurting. "You'll live. It needs cleaning and binding and you'll complain about both."

"I'll complain about more than that," Marta said, and grinned at her, gray-faced, and Ulyana loved her for it.

Tamir was crouched low, staring back across the river, and his face was doing something Ulyana had never seen it do. Not fear. Something worse. Something like guilt wearing fear's clothes.

"Tamir," she said.

He didn't answer.

"*Tamir.*"

"They know where we're going," he said. His voice cracked in the middle. "Kestrel Gap. They've known for days. I—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, because whatever else Tamir was, he wasn't a coward, and cowards lie at moments like this and he didn't. "I contacted them. At the market town. When we split up. I sent a boy with a note. I offered them the map's location. For money. Enough to start over somewhere far from boats."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped into still water, each ring wider than the last.

Ulyana found that she wasn't surprised. That was the worst part. Some part of her had watched Tamir watching the bundle, had seen the hunger in him, had filed it away and said nothing, because she knew hunger when she saw it. She'd worn it herself.

"You couldn't do it," she said.

"No." His eyes were wet and furious, mostly at himself. "I wrote the note. I paid the boy. And then I sat in that alley and I thought about you carrying Ede Voss up out of the mud when he couldn't pay, and I thought about Marta giving me her own gloves when mine froze stiff, and I ran after the boy and he was already gone." He dragged a breath. "I tried to buy it back. They wouldn't sell. The older one laughed at me. He said the note didn't matter, that they'd seen my face, and that when this was done they'd remember whose face it was."

"So they've been following us since the town," Marta said flatly. "Not hunting us. Herding us. Waiting for ground that suited them."

A ford. Yes. Ulyana looked back at the water, white and roaring, cutting off retreat as neatly as a drawn knife. The Aldouses hadn't missed. They'd chosen. Every hour she'd spent congratulating herself on losing them in the foothills, they'd simply been walking behind her, letting the mountains do their work, waiting for the one place where three travelers with packs would have to stand in open water with rifles on the bank.

"Captain," Tamir whispered. "What do we do?"

Ulyana looked at Marta's arm. Looked at the river. Looked at the treeline climbing steep and dark above them, and at the ridge beyond it, and at the sky going the color of old tin in the west.

"We stop being hunted," she said.

* * *

They climbed. It was the last thing the Aldouses would expect, because it was the hardest thing. No trail, just deer paths and scree, Marta's arm bound tight with a strip of shirt and her curses growing shorter as the altitude grew longer. Ulyana led, reading the slope the way she read water — where it would hold a boot, where it would slide, where the loose stone would announce them to anyone listening below.

By late afternoon they'd gained the shoulder of the ridge and could see back down the valley they'd come through, and there, tiny with distance, was the ford. Two figures moved along its near bank. One stopped, raised something to his eye, and swept the slopes across the river.

"He's looking where we'd have gone," Marta said. "If we'd kept to the trail."

"He's looking where *he'd* have gone," Ulyana corrected. "The Aldouses are rivermen gone bad. They think like boatmen. Downstream, always downstream, the easiest line." She almost smiled. "I don't."

They crossed the ridge at dusk and dropped into the next valley, and Ulyana allowed herself one hour of believing they'd won.

Then she saw the smoke.

It rose thin and straight from the bend of the stream below them — a cook fire, deliberately visible, the way a fisherman sets a lure. And beside it, unhurried, a man was tending a kettle. Even at this distance she knew the set of his shoulders. The elder Aldous. Silas, Ede had called him once, in a fever rant full of names and betrayals. Silas Aldous, who sold a dying man's claim out from under him and called it business.

And twenty yards downstream, half-hidden in the alders, the glint of a rifle barrel held by someone very still.

"They want a parley," Marta murmured beside her.

"They want me standing in the open while the younger one shoots me." Ulyana studied the camp. The fire was real; the kettle was real; the ease was theater. "But they also want something they can't shoot out of me. Which means they'll talk first. And talking is ground I choose."

"You can't mean to walk down there."

"I mean to walk down there with my hands empty and my terms ready." She turned to Tamir. "You stay here with Marta. If I raise my right hand, you take her up-slope and you don't stop until the stars change."

"Captain—"

"That's not a request, Tamir." She softened it, because he was already bleeding from the inside. "You gave them your face. Don't give them your life too. I'm not done needing either of you."

Marta caught her wrist as she turned. "Fifteen years I've cooked on your boat," she said. "You come back. That's an order."

"You can't order me. You're crew."

"Then consider it a mutiny."

Ulyana squeezed her good hand and went down the hill.

* * *

Silas Aldous poured her tea without asking whether she wanted it, which told her he'd grown comfortable assuming. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with his brother's coloring and none of his brother's restlessness. He sat on a log like a man on a porch, and the rifle lay across his knees, and twenty yards away in the alders his brother Renn lay with another one, and all three of them knew it, and the knowing was part of the conversation.

"Captain Nystrom," Silas said. "You've cost us a season."

"You've cost a man his life's work and a daughter her father," Ulyana said. "I'd say we're even."

"Ede Voss was a drunk and a fool who signed papers he couldn't read." Silas said it mildly, the way a man discusses weather. "The claim was ours inside a year. Legal. Witnessed."

"Witnessed," Ulyana repeated, and let the word sit there between them, and watched something flicker behind Silas's eyes — quick, quickly buried. Filed away. Everything was becoming evidence lately; she was learning to notice.

"Here's how it goes," Silas said. "You give us the map. You give us the dead man's effects, all of them, and you forget his name. In exchange, you walk. No more bullets, no more trackers, no word to Hale about your little mutiny on his freight contracts. You keep your boat. You keep your crew." He smiled with genuine warmth, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Captain, I've seen your ledger. I know what the Sable Queen is worth to you. This is the only trade on the table where you end the winter still owning her."

And there it was. The trap within the trap. Not the rifle in the alders — that was for if she refused crudely. The real weapon was the boat. They'd done their homework. They knew that Ulyana Nystrom would trade almost anything for the Sable Queen, because the Sable Queen was the last thing standing between her and the person she'd been before the river took her in: a girl with ash on her hands and a debt that wasn't money.

Fifteen years ago she'd have taken the trade. Ten years ago. Last spring, maybe.

But she'd stood at a dying man's bedside and given her word to a stranger, badly, clumsily, with no idea what it would cost — and the strange thing about a promise made honestly is that it doesn't care what it costs. It just stands there, being true, while everything around it negotiates.

"No," Ulyana said.

Silas sighed, genuinely regretful. "Renn," he called, conversationally, to the alders. "Come say hello to the captain."

The younger brother rose out of the brush with his rifle slung and walked into the firelight, and Ulyana saw her chance arrive wearing a young man's face.

He was twenty-five, maybe. Younger than Tamir by a hair. He had his brother's heavy jaw and none of his certainty, and as he stepped close — too close, closing to intimidate her, the way his brother had surely coached him — Ulyana moved.

She'd spent twenty years on a river where fights started fast and ended faster, where the man who hesitated fed the fish. She took Renn's rifle strap across his body, hooked his ankle, and put him down in the gravel with his own weight doing the work, and then she had his own skinning knife out of his belt and at his throat before Silas finished rising off the log.

"Don't," she said, without looking at Silas. "Your brother's breathing depends entirely on my mood, and my mood is currently very interested in arithmetic. One of you for one of us. Marta's arm. That's the price of the graze. Say yes and everyone walks away."

Silas had his rifle up. Ulyana met his eyes over the barrel and did not blink, and pressed the edge just enough to make Renn gasp.

"You'll hang," Silas said.

"For killing a man who ambushed me at a ford?" She shook her head slowly. "No, Silas. You'll hang. Or whatever passes for hanging in the camps — a council, a rope, a name read aloud. Because I've seen what Ede carried, and it isn't a treasure map. It's paper with signatures on it. Yours. Your witnesses'. And paper travels, Silas. Paper gets read. Ask yourself why a man like Corvin Hale wanted it burned instead of bought."

She watched the flicker again, stronger this time. Fear has a smell, and Silas Aldous was suddenly giving it off like a struck match.

"Walk away," she said quietly. "Go back over the divide and don't come down again. I'm not asking as a favor. I'm telling you the ground is shifting under this whole rotten claim, and the men standing on it when it moves are going to be the ones holding the signatures."

Silence. The fire popped. Renn breathed shallow under her blade, and she could feel his pulse hammering against the steel — young, terrified, alive.

*Kill him,* said some cold old voice in her, the voice of every captain who'd survived by striking first. *End it. He'd have shot you in the water. He shot Marta. End the bloodline of it here.*

And underneath that voice, another one — quieter, older, hers — that remembered a girl carrying a lantern-oil crate up a loading ramp, telling herself the same story Silas was telling himself: *it's legal, it's business, someone else signs the worst parts.* She knew exactly how a man got to be Silas Aldous. It happened one convenient cruelty at a time.

She lifted the knife from Renn's throat.

"Get up," she said.

He scrambled back against the log, hand pressed to the shallow line on his neck, staring at her like she'd spoken a language he'd heard of but never learned.

"Why," he rasped.

"Because I'm choosing it," Ulyana said. "That's the whole of it. Mercy isn't weakness, boy. Weakness is needing permission to spare a man. I don't need anyone's." She backed away, three steps, five, keeping them both in view. "Tell your brother what I said. Tell him the map reaches Kestrel Gap whether he likes it or not, and what happens after that belongs to people who read."

She walked backward into the treeline, and no shot followed her, and she counted that as the strangest victory of her life.

* * *

Marta and Tamir were waiting where she'd left them, and the relief on Tamir's face collapsed into something rawer the moment he saw her.

"You spared him," he said. "I watched. You had him and you — why?"

Ulyana sank down onto the cold stone beside Marta and let out a breath that shook more than she wanted it to. "Because I've been him," she said. "Standing there with a knife at my throat, waiting to find out if the world was the kind that finishes the job." She looked east, where the pass ran down toward the survey roads and the winter quarters and a woman named Voss who didn't want what her father had left her. "Somebody didn't finish it with me. Somebody gave me room to become something else. It seemed rude not to pass it along."

Marta laughed, then winced, then laughed anyway. "You're getting philosophical in your old age, Captain."

"I'm getting tired in my old age. Philosophy's cheaper."

They rested an hour, no more. Then Ulyana stood, rolled her shoulders, and looked down-valley, where the stream bent toward the lowlands and the first woodsmoke of the survey camps would be rising in a day or two.

"One more push," she said. "There's a survey party wintering down this drainage — I heard it at the last trading post. If we catch them before the snow seals the pass, we might not have to find Ingrid Voss at all."

"And if she's with them?" Tamir asked.

Ulyana touched the compass through her coat, and felt the needle pull, faint and stubborn, toward the burned valley behind them — and, beneath that pull, something new. Something almost like steadiness.

"Then God help her," Ulyana said, "because she's about to meet the woman carrying her father's last promise. And I'm not sure either of us is ready for that."

They shouldered their packs and started down.
