## 15. Ingrid Voss, Found Early

Ingrid Voss had learned to read weather the way other people read letters — quickly, and with an eye for what was about to go wrong. The sky over the eastern shoulder of the range had gone the color of pewter by midmorning, and the wind had swung around out of the north with a dry, mineral smell that meant snow before nightfall. She called the halt two hours early.

"Camp in the tree line," she told Okafor. "Not past it. If we're caught on open ground tomorrow we'll be digging ourselves out until spring."

Okafor nodded and turned back down the line of surveyors, passing the word along in his low carrying voice. Eleven people, four mules, one season's worth of instruments and notes packed into oilcloth. They'd spent six weeks running lines through the high country for the Kestrel Gap Consolidated, and now the mountain was telling them the work was done whether they liked it or not. Ingrid had been doing this long enough to know when the mountain was done negotiating.

She stood at the edge of the clearing while the others worked, watching the valley below through her field glasses. That was how she saw them first: three figures on the old mining trail, small as stitching against the gray rock, moving fast and badly. One of them limped. Another kept stopping to look behind them, which was either caution or paranoia, and after six weeks alone with her own crew Ingrid found she could no longer always tell the difference.

"Riders?" Okafor asked, coming up beside her.

"Walkers. Three." She passed him the glasses. "One's hurt, or close to it."

"Prospectors?"

"In this weather, off the main trail, moving like they're being chased?" She lowered the glasses. "Could be anything. Could be nobody. We'll know in an hour."

She didn't say what she was actually thinking, which was that the last time strangers came up this trail looking for something, they'd been looking for her father, and what they'd found instead was a shack full of empty bottles and a debt ledger with her name in it, sold onward like everything else he'd ever touched. Twenty years, and the trail still brought her things with his fingerprints on them.

* * *

They came into camp at dusk, just as the first flakes started coming down, fat and slow and unhurried, the way snow starts when it means to keep its word.

The tall one — a young man, dark-haired, hollow-eyed with exhaustion — stopped dead when he saw the tents. The woman beside him, broad and gray-streaked and built like a dock crane, put a steadying hand on his arm. And the third, walking between them with a stick cut from green birch and a bandage around her ribs that someone had tied with more determination than skill, looked up at Ingrid across the fire ring and said nothing at all.

It was the riverboat captain. Ingrid knew her on sight, though they'd never met. Word traveled up from the landings faster than mail did: Ulyana Nystrom, the Sable Queen, the best pilot on the lower water and the worst debtor. Hale's collar around her neck, everyone said, and wearing it like it fit.

"You're a long way from your boat," Ingrid said.

"My boat's moored at Ferro's dock, two hundred miles behind me." The captain's voice was rougher than the stories made it sound. "I'm looking for Ingrid Voss."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere behind Ingrid, Okafor went quiet in the middle of pegging a tent fly.

"You've found her."

"I have something for you." The captain reached inside her coat, and half the camp shifted — Ingrid noticed that, noticed her crew's hands drifting toward their belts, noticed the young man flinch at the movement — and drew out not a weapon but a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord. "From Ede Voss."

For a moment the clearing held very still. Snow ticked softly against canvas. The mules stamped.

"Ede Voss is dead," Ingrid said.

"Died six weeks ago. On my boat. Fever took him slow." The captain held the bundle out. "He asked me to bring you this. It's why we're here. All of us."

Ingrid looked at the bundle. Oilcloth, tied well, the kind of wrapping a careful man used for things he meant to survive weather. Her father had never been a careful man. He'd been a man who could find silver under a mountain and lose his boots in the same week, who could talk a claim out of the ground and talk himself out of every room he was ever welcome in. He'd been a man who left.

"He tell you what I am to him?" she asked.

"He talked about you. Near the end, mostly."

"Near the end." Ingrid almost laughed. "That's Ede. Everything important saved for the last minute, and then even the last minute too late." She looked at the captain steadily. "You crossed the range for this? In October?"

"We did."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and the captain seemed to know it was fair, because she didn't answer right away. The gray woman spoke instead, her voice dry as gravel. "Because she promised. And because some of us didn't get a vote."

"Marta," the captain said, without heat.

"I'm only saying what the man asked. He asked if we'd carry it. Nobody asked us to bleed for it." But Marta's eyes, when they met Ingrid's, weren't hostile. They were measuring, the way an older woman measures a younger one carrying weight she shouldn't be. "You'll want to hear how he died, I expect. Whether you want to or not."

"I don't," Ingrid said. "And I don't want the map."

The young man's head came up sharply. "You haven't even—"

"Tamir." The captain's voice was quiet but it closed the boy's mouth like a door.

Ingrid stepped forward, close enough that the snow fell between them instead of on either of them, and held the captain's gaze. "Twenty years ago that man walked out of Kestrel Gap owing money to half the town and owning nothing but a story about silver. I was nine years old. My mother waited three winters for him to send for us. Then she waited for him to stop being worth waiting for. Then she died, and I went into service at fourteen, and I dug survey lines for men like Corvin Hale until I was good enough at it that they paid me to lead instead of follow." She let that sit. "So you can understand that whatever Ede Voss carried in that oilcloth, I've already received my inheritance from him. It was twenty years of learning not to wait."

The captain didn't flinch. Ingrid gave her credit for that. Most people flinched.

"He said you'd say that," the captain said finally.

"Did he." 

"Said you were the hardest-minded person he ever knew. Said he hoped you got it from your mother."

The words landed somewhere Ingrid hadn't armored, and she hated him for it, hated him across twenty years and a mountain range, hated him most of all for dying before she could tell him so. "Get off my trail," she said. "There's a winter camp at Alder Fork, two days south. They'll feed you if you work. Come spring you can walk out however you like."

"And the map?"

"Keep it. Bury it. Throw it in a crevasse. It's nothing to me."

* * *

But they didn't leave.

Ingrid understood why by midnight, when she walked the camp perimeter out of habit and found the three of them huddled under a lean tarp near the mules, refusing the offer of tent space with a pride that was almost funny given that the tall boy was shivering hard enough to rattle his teeth. The storm had thickened. The snow came down in earnest now, the kind of fall that erased trails and filled passes and made decisions for reasonable people.

"You'll freeze," she said, standing over them.

"We've slept colder," the gray woman said.

"That's not a boast, that's a diagnosis." Ingrid jerked her chin toward the big mess tent. "There's a stove in there and coffee going stale. Take it or don't, but if your boy dies on my campsite I'll have to write a report, and I hate writing reports."

They took it. Of course they took it. And Ingrid sat apart with her tea and watched them across the stove light — the captain sitting straight-backed even exhausted, the woman Marta ladling coffee with the economy of someone who'd fed crews for decades, the boy Tamir staring into his cup like it owed him money — and she felt the old anger working in her chest like water finding cracks.

It wasn't fair, was the thing. That was the whole of it, the whole ugly arithmetic she'd been carrying since she was nine. Other people's fathers died and left saddles, tools, watches, stories. Hers had died — no, wasn't even dead yet, was dying slowly on somebody else's boat — and left a stranger with a parcel and a promise, and the promise had cost three people a season of their lives and a riverboat its captain, and none of it, none of it, changed a single day of what Ingrid had already lived. You couldn't deliver a childhood twenty years late. You couldn't wrap it in oilcloth.

"Voss."

She looked up. The captain stood at the edge of the firelight, coat on, snow in her hair.

"The boy needs sleep and your stove's winning. I'll take first watch outside." A pause. "I'm not asking you to take the map tonight. I'm asking you to hear one thing before morning, and then we'll get out of your way."

"There's a storm."

"There's a storm every year. I've heard your speech. I grew up on a river; I know what it sounds like when someone's decided." The captain's eyes were steady and dark and very tired. "Ede Voss spent his last weeks paying for something. Not in money. He'd have paid in money gladly, I think. He paid in telling the truth, which for a man like him was the harder currency. Whatever he did to you — and I believe you that it was bad, I believe every word — he knew it was bad. That's all I'll say. That's the one thing."

Ingrid set down her tea. "You knew him six weeks."

"Long enough to watch a man die honest. It's rarer than you'd think." The captain pulled her collar up. "Watch's mine. Sleep."

Ingrid didn't sleep. She sat in the mess tent until the stove burned down to coals, listening to the storm lean on the canvas, and at some point the boy Tamir stirred and looked at her across the embers with an expression she recognized — the look of someone carrying something heavy and wondering aloud, silently, whether to put it down.

"Don't," she said.

He blinked. "Don't what?"

"Whatever you're thinking of confessing. Don't do it to me. I'm not the priest on this trail." She stood, joints cracking in the cold. "Though for what it's worth, whoever you're afraid of — they already know. They always already know. That's the trick of secrets. Everyone's just waiting for you to catch up."

His face went white as the snow outside, and Ingrid climbed into the cold with the strange, unwelcome certainty that she'd just watched a man decide something, and that it would matter.

* * *

Morning came white and silent, a foot of new snow, the world scoured down to essentials. Ingrid stood at the camp's edge with the oilcloth bundle finally in her hands — she'd taken it in the night, without deciding to, the way you catch something falling — and turned it over twice.

She didn't open it. That was the discipline, the whole discipline of her life: don't open what will change you until you've chosen where you'll stand when it does.

The captain joined her, breath steaming. "Storm breaks by noon. After that, our tracks are yours to follow or forget."

"You could have sold it," Ingrid said. "Whatever's in here. Men like Hale pay for things like this. Everyone on this mountain would have understood."

"Yes," the captain said simply. "They would have."

Ingrid looked at her for a long moment. There was something underneath this woman, some ballast she was carrying low in the hull, and Ingrid had spent twenty years reading people for the cargo they hid. "What did he promise you?" she asked. "Exactly. His words."

The captain was quiet a moment. "He said: *carry it to my daughter, and tell her I never stopped knowing where she was.*"

The wind moved. High above, the storm was tearing itself apart against the peaks, blue sky showing through in ragged strips.

"He knew where I was," Ingrid repeated.

"Every year, he said. Asked travelers, read letters over men's shoulders in saloons. Never wrote. Said he had no right to write until he had something worth sending." The captain nodded at the bundle. "Then he had this, and no time."

Ingrid's hands tightened on the oilcloth until the cord bit her palms. *No right to write.* Twenty years she'd built her life on the belief that he'd simply forgotten, that she'd been easy to forget, that forgetting was the natural gravity of a man like Ede Voss. And here was the correction, arriving by hand, up a frozen trail, wrapped like something precious: he'd remembered every year. Remembering had just been cheaper than returning. It changed nothing. It changed everything. She hated it. She held it.

"Break camp," she said to Okafor, not turning around. "We're descending today, ahead of the melt-crust. Alder Fork by tomorrow night."

"And them?" Okafor glanced at the three by the dead fire.

"They walk with us. It's one trail." Ingrid tucked the bundle inside her coat, against her chest, where it lay cold and dense as ore. She still hadn't opened it. She still might not. But she'd caught it falling, and a thing caught isn't the same as a thing accepted, and she needed the distance between those two words to survive the next two days.

She was turning away when Tamir came running from the tree line, snow spraying, his face the color of ash.

"Captain," he gasped. "Captain — there's smoke in the valley. South fork. Riders — six, maybe eight, and they're not prospectors. They've got our scent, they're cutting the switchbacks—" He swallowed. "They found us."

Ulyana Nystrom went very still, and Ingrid watched her reach inside her coat for something small and brass, and watched her thumb it open, and watched her face change as she read the needle.

"Not south," the captain said quietly. "They think we'll run south. We run east." She looked up at Ingrid, and there was something wild and calm in her eyes, something that had already left the trail behind. "How good are your people on ice?"
