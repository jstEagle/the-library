## 16. The Needle and the Fire

The survey party's camp sat in a bowl of granite below the tree line, six tents and a cook fire and a mule line strung between two pines, and Ingrid Voss had dismissed Ulyana from it twice already.

The second time she'd done it with a surveyor's pencil still in her hand, not even looking up from her field book. "You've said your piece, Captain. The man is dead. The map is yours to burn or bury or sell. I don't want it."

Ulyana had walked back toward the edge of the camp where Marta stood with Tamir, both of them pretending to warm their hands at a fire the survey crew hadn't invited them to share. Snow was coming down in fat, indifferent flakes now — the real snow, the one they'd been outrunning for eleven days, the one that would close the passes behind them like a door.

"He won't take it," Marta said quietly.

"No," Ulyana said. "She will."

Marta looked at her. Twenty years of cooking on the Sable Queen had taught Marta to read Ulyana the way rivermen read water — the surface calm, the current underneath doing all the work. "What are you going to do?"

"Tell her something I've never told anyone."

Tamir's head came up sharply. Marta didn't move at all, which was worse.

"You don't have to," Marta said.

"I know." Ulyana pulled the compass from inside her coat. It hung on its cord against her sternum, brass worn bright at the hinge where Ede Voss's thumb had rubbed it for forty years. The needle lay steady in its glass, pointing south-southwest, pointing at a valley she had spent fifteen years making sure she never saw again. "That's the problem. I know exactly what I don't have to do. I've built my whole life out of things I didn't have to do."

She turned and walked back into the camp.

* * *

Ingrid Voss was tall the way her father had been tall, but that was where the resemblance ended if you took a man at his face. Ede Voss had gone soft and yellowed, a candle burning down in a draft. His daughter was cut from harder stock — wind-scoured, gray at thirty-eight, with hands that looked like they'd been frozen and thawed a hundred times. She was coiling rope when Ulyana approached, and she finished the coil before she acknowledged her, which told Ulyana everything about how this conversation would go.

"The pass closes in two days," Ingrid said. "Whatever you're going to say, say it walking."

They walked. Out past the mule line, past the last tent, onto a shelf of rock above the valley where the snow fell straight down and the wind couldn't get any opinion about it. Below them the land dropped away into timber and shadow, and somewhere down there, twelve miles as the needle flew, was Harrow Bend.

Ulyana stopped at the edge of the shelf. Her heart was doing something ugly and arrhythmic, and she let it.

"Ede talked about you," she said. "At the end. Not much. He was mostly fever by then. But when he could talk, it was you."

Ingrid's jaw tightened. "He talked about me. That must have been comfortable for him. Talking."

"It was," Ulyana said. "Men like him talk instead of doing. I know because I'm one of them."

"Then we're done—"

"Fifteen years ago I worked freight for Corvin Hale."

The name landed. Ulyana watched it land — watched Ingrid's face do the small, involuntary thing that faces did when they heard a name that meant money and menace in equal measure. Everyone in these mountains knew Hale. Hale owned the freight contracts, the warehouse leases, the debt paper on half the boats on the river including hers. Hale was weather. You didn't blame the weather; you just dressed for it.

"I was twenty-two," Ulyana said. "I'd just taken command of the Sable Queen off my father's death, and I was drowning. The boat cost more than I made. Hale held my paper. And he came to me one night with a job — simple freight, he said. Run a load of lantern oil upriver to Harrow Bend. Deliver it to a man named Cade at the old warehouses. No manifest, no questions, double pay."

Below them the snow thickened. Somewhere in the camp a mule complained and went quiet.

"I asked him once why no manifest," Ulyana said. "He smiled at me the way he does — you've seen it, everyone's seen it — and he said, *Miss Nystrom, curiosity is a tax the poor can't afford.* So I didn't ask again. I loaded forty tins of oil and I ran her upriver through the narrows at night, which was stupid, which was dangerous, which I did anyway because the pay covered three months of my debt payments, and I delivered it to Cade at the warehouses at Harrow Bend."

Ingrid had gone very still. Not soft. Still, the way game goes still.

"Harrow Bend burned two nights later," Ulyana said. "You'd know that. Everyone would. Eleven buildings. The company said faulty flues, winter fires, an accident of the season. It was in all the papers for a week and then it wasn't, because papers don't chase stories that make Hale's lawyers write letters."

"And you knew," Ingrid said. Flat. Not a question.

"I suspected. Then I knew. Because Cade came looking for me a month later, drunk, wanting to know if I'd kept the delivery note, because men were asking questions and he needed to prove the oil came from somewhere else. And I gave it to him. I burned my copy of the note myself, and I told myself that proved nothing, connected me to nothing, and I went back to running freight, and every month I made my payment to Corvin Hale with money that — some part of it, some fraction, some drop — had come from that night." Ulyana's voice stayed level. She was surprised by that. Fifteen years she'd imagined this confession, or rather she'd imagined everything except this confession, and in all those imaginings her voice always shook. It didn't shake. It came out clean and cold as river ice, and that was somehow worse. "I never spoke of it. Not to anyone. Not to Marta, who's slept ten feet from me for twenty years. Not to myself, really. I put it in a room in my head and I locked the room and I told myself the room didn't exist."

"Why are you telling me this?" Ingrid's voice cracked on the last word, and Ulyana finally saw under the anger — saw the girl underneath, the one who'd been left at a waystation outside Kestrel Gap at sixteen with a note and forty dollars, who'd grown up into this wind-hardened woman with a survey chain in her hand. "Why would you tell *me?* What does your sin have to do with my father?"

"Because your father found out."

The words came out before Ulyana decided to say them. But they were true, and lately true things seemed to come out of her whether she scheduled them or not.

"In the last days, when the fever had him, he talked. Raved, mostly. Silver lodes, the claim, your mother. But one night he was clear — clear as church bells — and he grabbed my wrist and he said, *You're carrying it too. I can see it. The weight.* And I laughed at him. I said, old man, you're burning up. And he said—" Ulyana stopped. She hadn't let herself remember this part fully until this moment, standing on a rock shelf in falling snow, and now it arrived whole. "*He said: I sold my silence for twenty years and it bought me nothing but the road. Whatever Hale paid you, it wasn't enough. Nothing is enough. Tell the truth while you can still choose who you tell it to.*"

The wind moved through the pines below them. Ingrid didn't move.

"He knew about the fire?" Ingrid said finally.

"He knew about Hale. He prospected for Hale's syndicate in the years after Harrow Bend — that's how they got his claim rights, that's how the Aldouses got their hooks in. Men like Hale collect secrets the way other men collect debts. Your father saw mine. Maybe he recognized it because he carried one of his own." Ulyana turned to face her fully. "I'm not telling you this to unburden myself. I don't get to use you for that. I'm telling you because you called him nothing, and you're right — he was nothing to you, he earned that. But I'm asking you to look at what being nothing costs. He died on my boat with a map in his gear and your name in his mouth, and the only thing he wanted in this world was for you to have it, and he waited too long to ask, and so did I, and so does everyone who locks the room."

Ingrid's eyes were wet. She hated that — Ulyana could see her hating it, see her blinking it back down with pure fury, the same fury that had refused the map twice.

"You want me to feel sorry for him," Ingrid said.

"No. I want you to take the map."

"He was nothing to—"

"He was," Ulyana said. "And here's the thing about the truth, Ingrid. It doesn't care if you love the man it comes from. It sits there. It waits. It rots if you carry it and it frees you if you spend it, and either way it's yours. That map is the truest thing your father ever did. You can refuse it. But then it's not his failure anymore. It's yours."

She pulled the wrapped bundle from her pack. Oilcloth, tied with cord, the corners worn round from eleven days of Tamir's hands almost touching it and stopping. She held it out.

Ingrid looked at it the way people look at deep water.

"I don't want to read it," she said. Her voice had changed — smaller, younger. "I don't want to open it and find out it's just silver coordinates. Or find out it isn't. I don't want—"

"Then don't read it," Ulyana said. "Take it unread. Carry it sealed until you're ready, or until you're not, or forever. A promise kept doesn't require the keeping to be pretty. Ede knew that better than anyone alive. Take it, and it's delivered, and whatever it becomes next is between you and the truth, not you and him."

For a long moment the only sound was snow landing on oilcloth, a soft hiss like breath.

Then Ingrid Voss reached out and took the bundle.

Her hands shook. Ulyana pretended not to see, the way you pretend not to see a wound someone is trying to hide. Ingrid tucked the bundle inside her coat, against her chest, the same place Ulyana carried the compass, and something in her posture shifted — not softened exactly, but settled, like a rope coming taut and holding.

"The Aldouses," Ingrid said. "The men hunting you. They'll come for it now."

"They'll come for it regardless. They've been coming since the day Ede boarded my boat." Ulyana touched the compass through her coat. "There's more in that map than silver. I believe that. Ede said enough, near the end, for me to believe it. When you're ready to open it, open it in front of witnesses. Good ones."

Ingrid looked at her for a long time. The anger was still there — it would be there for years, probably, banked down like a coal — but underneath it something else had surfaced, something tired and young.

"You said the compass points wrong," she said. "Your deckhand mentioned it. Points at some valley instead of north."

Ulyana's hand closed over the brass. "It pointed at Harrow Bend. For eleven days. Every mile we climbed, it swung truer toward it, like it was dragging me back to look at what I did."

"And now?"

Ulyana hadn't checked. She realized that — hadn't checked since the shelf, since the confession started forming in her like a splinter working toward skin. She drew the compass out now, into the open snow-light, and held it flat on her palm between them.

The needle trembled once, twice — a thing waking — and then swung.

South. True south, dead on, steady as a preached sermon, the way a needle ought to point when the instrument is honest and the hand holding it has nothing left to hide. It didn't waver. It didn't drift. It sat in the glass like a fact.

Ingrid looked at it, then at Ulyana, and for the first time there was no anger in her face at all. Just assessment, sharp and surveyor-clean, taking a bearing of her own.

"That's a strange instrument," she said.

"It belonged to a man who carried a lie for twenty years," Ulyana said. "I think it got used to pointing at them."

She put it away, and the cold came back into the space between them, ordinary cold, mountain cold, and down in the camp someone shouted that the mules needed seeing to, and the world resumed.

But as they walked back toward the fires, Ingrid spoke once more, without turning her head.

"Nystrom. The man who took your oil delivery. Cade." A pause, snow ticking on their shoulders. "Does Hale still own you?"

"Not after what's in that map," Ulyana said. "If I'm right about what's in that map."

"And if you're wrong?"

Ulyana thought about the Sable Queen moored three hundred miles behind her, about Hale's smile, about the ledger with her name in it. About how light her chest felt, lighter than it had been since she was twenty-two, despite everything still coming.

"Then I'll owe him a boat," she said. "I've owed him worse things for longer. Debts you can count are easy."

Over Ingrid's shoulder, at the edge of the tree line, Tamir was watching them — and he wasn't watching the map, or the compass, or the reunion. He was watching the trail below the camp, where the snow lay unbroken except for one set of tracks that hadn't been there an hour ago, leading up from the valley, and stopping.

Someone had climbed as far as the tree line. Looked. And turned back down.

Ulyana followed Tamir's stare, and felt the old cold return — the kind that had nothing to do with weather.

"They know where we are," she said quietly. "Get your kit. We leave within the hour."
