## 17. Kestrel Gap

The snow came down like a verdict.

Ulyana had crossed a lot of weather in her life — spring floods that ate whole landings, autumn gales that turned the river to iron shrapnel — but she had never walked through anything like the last two days on the high shoulder of the range. The wind didn't blow so much as it deliberated, finding every gap in a coat, every seam in a mitten, and pressing its cold thumb there until you understood it could take whatever it wanted from you whenever it wanted.

They came over the last rise an hour past noon, and Kestrel Gap opened below them like something out of a fever.

It was built wrong for a mining town. Most camps sprawled wherever the ore was, but Kestrel Gap had been wedged into a notch between two granite shoulders, its streets climbing in switchbacks, its buildings stacked on pilings and stilts and each other's roofs, timber and tin and canvas all crusted white. Smoke stood up from a hundred chimneys in columns so straight they looked drawn with a rule. Somewhere below, a stamp mill hammered ore with a rhythm like a giant's slow heart.

"God," Tamir said beside her. His voice was wrecked from six days of wind. "It's real."

"It was always real," Marta said. She had a sling of splinted boards under her coat where the Aldouses' ambush at the ford had done its work, and she'd refused every offer to turn back since, which told Ulyana more about Marta than twenty years of cooking together had. "Real is the problem."

Ingrid Voss walked ahead of them, as she had for three days now — not leading exactly, not following, just moving with the particular stubbornness of a woman who had decided not to decide. She carried the map wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, against her chest, and she hadn't let anyone else touch it since the morning she'd taken it unread. *He was nothing to me,* she'd said then. But she carried it like it was warm.

Ulyana touched the compass through her shirt. It hung at her throat on a cord Ede Voss had tied himself, and for eleven days it had pointed not north but toward this town, steady as scripture. Since Ingrid took the map, it had wavered — drifting, hunting, sometimes settling on true north for a few minutes before wandering again. She didn't know what to make of that. She wasn't sure she wanted to know.

"We go straight to the survey office," Ingrid said, not turning around. "I file the map, I log the claim reference, and then I'm done with all of you."

"And then we find out what Hale's already bought," Ulyana said.

Ingrid glanced back. Wind had scoured her face raw; only her eyes moved easily. "You think he's here?"

"I think he gave us until the snows." Ulyana looked up at the fat grey sky shedding itself over the Gap. "These are the snows."

* * *

They came down into Kestrel Gap through the lower street, where the buildings leaned close enough that the wind lost most of its teeth. The town was loud in the way towns are loud when winter has just arrived and everyone is still pretending it hasn't — men hauling the last barrels off sleds, a woman beating a rug off a second-floor rail, children throwing snowballs off a loading dock with the aimless savagery of the bored. Nobody looked twice at four half-frozen travelers. Half-frozen travelers were the town's chief import.

But Ulyana felt eyes anyway. She'd felt them since the ford, since the younger Aldous brother had lain in the shallows with her knife at his throat and watched her decide not to use it. Whatever mercy she'd shown him, she doubted it had slowed the brothers down. It had probably just confused them.

The survey office sat halfway up the second switchback, a stone building with a green door and a queue outside it — claim-jumpers and speculators and one weeping man arguing about a stake line. Ingrid went to the front of the line the way a blade goes into a sheath, and the clerk, a small precise man with ink-stained cuffs, took one look at her parka insignia and waved her through without argument. Survey parties outranked everybody in a town like this.

Ulyana, Tamir, and Marta waited by the stove. The heat hit Ulyana's hands and she nearly cried out, pins and needles flooding in where feeling had been. Tamir crouched with his palms out like a man warming himself at an altar.

"You did it," he said quietly. "We actually did it."

"Don't say it yet."

"The map's going to be filed in ten minutes. The promise is kept. Say it."

She looked at him — twenty-three years old, frostbitten at both cheeks, a healing cut across his brow from the ford, and grinning like the whole mountain belonged to him. She remembered him finding the bundle in Ede's gear, the hunger in his face when he'd held the wrapped map and seen a way out of the boat and the debt and her. He'd betrayed them, almost. He'd come within a signature of betraying them. And then he'd stood in a freezing river and confessed it to her face while the Aldouses' rifles cracked overhead.

"Almost," she said. "Say *almost.*"

His grin dimmed a degree. "Almost."

The clerk's voice rose behind the partition. "—can't file this without a claim registration number, miss, and I can't issue a number without knowing what's being claimed—"

"It's a lode survey. Silver. My father's original staking, restaked and documented."

"A silver lode in the Gap? There hasn't been—"

"There will be."

Ulyana closed her eyes and let herself have five seconds of it. Five seconds of *done*. Ede Voss dying at dawn on her deck, pressing the brass compass into her palm: *true bearing, Captain. A compass that lies to you is worse than no compass at all, and most men carry ones that lie.* She'd promised a dying stranger she'd never met before that muddy landing. She'd abandoned her boat — her boat, the Sable Queen, sixteen years of her life moored at Dessa Kovac's dock two hundred miles behind her — and dragged her crew over a mountain range ahead of the snows, and here she stood, and the map was forty feet away behind a partition, about to become somebody else's problem forever.

Then the clerk said, in a different voice:

"Oh."

Ulyana opened her eyes.

The clerk had unrolled enough of the map to see the header — the old surveyor's block, the names, the signatures. And his face had gone the color of the paper.

"Miss Voss," he said carefully, "you'll want to wait right here."

* * *

What came through the green door thirty seconds later was not the law. It was worse. It was hospitality.

Corvin Hale filled the doorway the way he filled every doorway, which was completely. He wore a good wool coat and a better expression, the one Ulyana knew from a hundred negotiations on the Sable Queen's aft deck — patient, regretful, faintly disappointed in you, a man perpetually forgiving debts you hadn't incurred yet. Snow dusted his shoulders. He'd been waiting somewhere close. Maybe in this building. Maybe for days.

"Captain Nystrom," he said warmly. "You look terrible."

"Hale."

"Marta. Tamir." He nodded to each of them like a host greeting guests. "You've made my people work very hard this season. Trackers don't come cheap above the snowline."

"Your trackers quit at the ford," Marta said. "Something about the company they were keeping."

Hale smiled as if she'd paid him a compliment. Then his eyes went to Ingrid, standing rigid at the counter with the map half-unrolled in front of her, and Ulyana watched him do the arithmetic — the daughter, the map, the timing — and arrive at a sum he liked.

"So," Hale said. "You're the Voss girl."

"Ingrid Voss. Surveyor, second party, Northern Range District." Her voice was flat as slate. "And you're the man whose name is signed as witness on my father's claim transfer."

Something flickered across Hale's face — quick, quickly buried. Ulyana caught it because she'd spent years learning to read him. It wasn't guilt. It was recalculation.

"Am I?" Hale said mildly. "I witness dozens of instruments a year. Half the paper in this territory has my name on it somewhere. It usually means nothing."

"It means nothing when the instrument is honest."

Behind the partition, the clerk had gone very still, the way clerks do when they realize the document on their desk is a live thing.

Hale turned back to Ulyana, and now he dropped the warmth entirely, and what was underneath was almost a relief, because at least it was honest. "Captain. Walk with me."

"I'll walk nowhere with you."

"Then I'll say it here." He spread his hands, and his voice lifted just enough to carry — to the queue outside the door, to the men drifting in off the street behind them, to the clerk, to everyone. That was when Ulyana understood the shape of the trap, fully sprung, every thread pulled taut and converging on this one stone room. Not an ambush. An audience.

"Captain Nystrom owes me four thousand, one hundred and sixty dollars," Hale announced, conversational, reasonable, a businessman stating facts. "Principal, interest, and penalties, secured against the steamboat Sable Queen and personally guaranteed. She defaulted on the crossing season. She abandoned a contracted freight run mid-route." He let that hang. Abandonment sounded so much worse than *kept a promise to a dead man*. "Her boat sits at a friend's dock downriver, and her debt follows her anywhere she goes. Including here. Because three weeks ago, gentlemen—" he addressed the room now, generous, magnanimous, "—I purchased the outstanding paper on this district. Every delinquent claim lease in Kestrel Gap. Every debt of consequence. Including several held by parties who may find the coming audit inconvenient."

The room had gone quiet. Even the stamp mill seemed to pause between strokes.

"So here is my offer," Hale said, "and I make it publicly, so there can be no question later of coercion or bad faith. Captain Nystrom. The document Miss Voss holds — the Voss lode survey, the original staking papers, whatever else the old man put in that bundle — belongs to me. I paid for it once, through men who failed to collect. I'm prepared to pay for it again. Your debt dies today. All of it. You walk out of these mountains free, boat and all, and nobody remembers any of this by spring."

"And if I refuse?"

Hale sighed, genuinely sorry. "Then the debt stands, the boat goes to auction at ice-out, and the district audit proceeds with my full cooperation — including everything in the Voss file, which I will obtain through proper channels regardless. The only difference, Captain, is whether you're ruined standing next to it or clear of it." He paused, and added, softly, the knife slid in gentle: "And whether your friends are ruined with you. Tamir Osei has wages owed on the Sable Queen's books. Marta Brandt has a sister in Dessa Kovac's boarding house whose rent is paid out of the same account. Debts are like rivers, Captain. Everything downstream gets wet."

Ulyana stood very still.

This was the moment. She knew it the way she knew a sandbar under brown water — not by seeing it but by the way the current bent around it, the way the surface lied. Every road she'd walked since that muddy landing had bent toward this room. The Aldouses chasing the map. Hale buying the town's paper. Ede dying with a compass in his fist and a word on his lips — *bearing* — that had stopped meaning navigation somewhere on the high shoulder of the range.

And underneath it all, older than any of it, the thing the compass had been pointing at since the camp above the valley: the fire, fifteen years ago, the lantern oil she'd delivered for Hale to warehouses that weren't empty. Her silence, compounding all these years like interest.

He thought she was cornered. He thought the choice was the map or the ruin, the same trade he'd offered on the river, offered again now with witnesses and winter and her friends' names in his ledger.

He still didn't understand what the map was.

Neither, quite, did she. But she'd watched the clerk's face when he read the header. She'd watched Hale's face flicker when Ingrid said *witness*. Ede Voss had spent twenty years alone in a cabin scratching notes into the wall, and men do not flee their partners and hide a survey for two decades over silver. Silver can be restaked. Silver can be stolen honestly, the way the territory steals everything.

Documents like this one are never treasure. They are testimony.

"Captain," Hale prompted, patient, public, certain. "The offer stands. The map for your freedom. Yes or no."

The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the window. Ingrid Voss stood at the counter with her hand flat on the oilcloth wrapping, looking at Ulyana — not at Hale, at Ulyana — with an expression that had thawed perhaps one degree since the day she'd said *he was nothing to me*, and was asking, without words, the only question that mattered: *what did my father actually leave me?*

Ulyana reached up and touched the compass at her throat. It hung quiet against her skin, needle settled, pointing — for the first time in days, she would have sworn it — dead true.

"No," she said.

Hale's eyebrows rose, theatrical disappointment for the audience. "Captain—"

"Not the map. Not for the boat, not for the debt, not for Tamir's wages or Marta's sister's rent. You want to know why?" She turned to face the room — the queue in the doorway, the drifters, the clerk frozen behind his partition, all of them watching, all of them exactly where Hale wanted them, which meant, which meant they were exactly where she wanted them too. "Because you made your offer in public, Corvin. So here's mine. Open it."

She nodded at Ingrid. "Open the map. Right now. In front of everyone."

Hale moved — fast, for a big man — one step toward the counter, hand out, voice dropping its warmth for the first time in front of witnesses: "That document is—"

"Witnessed," Ingrid said, and stepped back, and put the counter between them, and began, deliberately, slowly, to unwrap the oilcloth.
