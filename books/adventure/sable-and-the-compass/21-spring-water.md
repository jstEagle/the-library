## 21. Spring Water

The river came back before anything else did.

Ulyana heard it first — a low, wet muttering under the ice that had held the valley silent for four months — and then one morning in late March she stood on the bank above Grayling Bend and watched the water open like an eye. A seam of black appeared midstream, widened, and by afternoon the whole channel was running loud and brown and alive, carrying broken plates of ice downstream to be ground against the shallows.

She had not owned a boat when the ice closed. She did not own one now.

It didn't sit the way she'd expected it to sit. She'd spent fifteen years believing that the Sable Queen was the only thing standing between her and nothing at all — that without the boat she was just a woman with a debt and a bad history, a person the world had already taken its cut from. But she'd walked out of Kestrel Gap with nothing but a compass on a cord around her neck, and the world had not swallowed her. Winter had passed over her like weather. She was still here, and the river was back, and this morning there was work.

"You're smiling," Marta said behind her. "I want it noted for the record."

"I'm not smiling."

"Your face is doing something. I've known you nine years. Your face has exactly four settings and none of them is that."

Ulyana turned. Marta stood on the frozen mud with her arms crossed against the cold, breath steaming, looking as unimpressed as ever — but thinner than last autumn, and grayer at the temples, and she'd earned every strand. The mountain had taken something out of all of them. It had also, strangely, put something back.

"The *Kestrel Lark* launches Thursday," Ulyana said. "There's caulking to finish and a hold to scrub and Tamir has been negotiating our freight rates like he invented money. If you're going to stand there critiquing my face, you can do it while hauling pitch."

Marta snorted and fell into step beside her, boots crunching on the last crust of snow along the bank path. They walked down through the willow scrub toward the boatyard — if you could call three sheds and a slipway a boatyard — where the *Lark* sat propped on ways above the high-water line, small and sharp-bowed and honest about what she was. Forty feet. Shallow draft. Room for six tons of freight and a crew of three who would have to like each other, because there was nowhere to hide on her.

The Sable Queen had been ninety feet of polished oak and brass and debt. The *Lark* was raw pine and iron nails and no debt at all.

Ulyana stopped at the foot of the ways and looked up at her, and let herself feel it plainly for once: not grief, not yet, but the strange lightness of wanting a thing you could actually carry.

* * *

Tamir was up on the deck, arguing with a man from the freight office, and losing gracefully, which was his particular gift.

"All I'm saying," Tamir called down when he saw them coming, "is that grain rates went up twelve percent since fall, and we're being quoted at fall prices by a man whose office has a stove in it."

"And all I'm saying," said the freight man, a round, patient fellow named Osk, "is that your vessel is forty feet and your captain is a woman half the valley is convinced is either a fraud or a hero, depending on which version of the Kestrel Gap story they heard, and neither frauds nor heroes get good rates until they've run a season without incident."

"That's discrimination."

"That's bookkeeping." Osk touched his hat brim to Ulyana and Marta both. "Captain. Ma'am. Twelve percent off fall rates, paid monthly, and I'll throw in first pick of the spring cargo manifests because I like you and because Ingrid Voss asked me to. Sign there."

Ulyana signed. Her name went down on the paper in her own hand, under her own title, and nobody's signature sat above hers except her partners'. She had read enough ledgers in Corvin Hale's offices, those years ago, to know what a clean page looked like. This was one.

Osk gathered his papers and paused, halfway down the bank, and looked back at her with the mild curiosity of a man deciding whether to ask something he'd been chewing on all winter.

"They say Hale's finished," he said. "Out east. That the mining council's findings went downstream with the ice-breakers and his backers pulled out inside a month. You hear that?"

"I heard it in February."

"And it's true?"

Ulyana thought about the letter she'd gotten in February — a single sheet, unsigned, forwarded through three postmasters, containing one sentence: *You were right to keep it.* She thought about the trial testimony she'd never had to give, because the map itself had done the talking; about the Aldous brothers' names struck from every claim register in two territories; about Hale's signature, reproduced in the council's published findings, sitting beneath a fraudulent transfer dated twenty-two years back, witnessed in his own careful hand.

"He's finished," she said. "Whether he knows it yet is another matter."

Osk nodded, satisfied, and crunched away up the bank.

Tamir dropped down from the deck rail and landed beside them, brushing pine dust off his sleeves. He'd filled out over the winter, working the Kestrel Gap timber camps with Marta while Ulyana took a winter pilot's wage guiding supply sledges across the frozen river. His hands were scarred now in new places. He carried himself differently — less like a boy dreaming of leaving, more like a man who had left, and come back, and knew the difference.

"Ingrid's at the claim," he said. "Sent word. She wants us up there Sunday — says the first assay came back."

Marta raised an eyebrow. "And?"

"She wouldn't say in the note. She wrote *come see* and then, underneath, in smaller letters, *bring the compass.*"

* * *

Sunday came in bright and cold, the kind of thaw day where everything drips. The trail up Voss Creek was still packed with old snow in the shadows, and they climbed it in the blue morning with their breath hanging around them, past the ruins of the cabin they'd sheltered in during the first storm — Ede's cabin, though none of them had known it then. Someone had nailed a board over the collapsed doorway since. On the board, in charcoal, someone had written: *E. VOSS. HE KEPT HIS BEARINGS.*

Ingrid's hand, Ulyana guessed. Or maybe the whole camp's. Word traveled in mining country faster than mail.

The claim itself sat in a fold of the mountain two miles up: a scatter of tents and timber shacks, a stamping shed not yet assembled, and the adit — the mine mouth — cut into the hillside like a held breath. Twenty men worked it now, men who'd come down out of Kestrel Gap after the council voided the Aldouses' paper and re-registered the lode to Ede Voss's estate, sole heir: Ingrid.

She met them at the adit, in a canvas coat and a surveyor's hat, a leather ledger under one arm. She looked, Ulyana thought, exactly like what the mountain had made her: hard-used, clear-eyed, twenty years of grievance burned down to something more useful than anger.

"You came," Ingrid said.

"You asked."

"I ask people things all the time. Most of them don't come." She looked at each of them in turn — Marta, Tamir, and last Ulyana, longest. "Assay results are in. First crushings off the vein. Do you want the number?"

"Give it plain," Marta said.

"Eleven hundred ounces to the ton at the face. Better deeper. It's not a good claim." Ingrid's mouth twitched, the closest thing to a smile Ulyana had seen on her. "It's a *great* one. My father found a great one and threw it away rather than come home and tell me about it. I've spent all winter deciding how I feel about that."

"And how do you feel?" Tamir asked carefully.

"Rich," Ingrid said. "And furious. Both at once. It turns out they fit together fine." She flipped the ledger shut. "I'm having the stamp mill built by June. I'll need freight hauled — ore down to the landing, supplies up. Seasonal contract, three years, decent rates. I could give it to anyone."

"But you're giving it to us," Ulyana said.

"I'm giving it to the crew that carried his map through the passes in the first snow rather than sell it." Ingrid shrugged, as if the logic were obvious, and maybe to her it was. "I told you in the fall I wanted nothing of his. I was wrong about some of that. Not about him — about me. Turns out I wanted nothing of his *except everything,* and the only way to take it was to take all of it, the claim and the abandonment and the dying promise, the whole inheritance, unsanded." She looked up at the gray rock of the adit mouth. "He was nothing to me. The vein is everything. I've made my peace with loving a dead man's geology."

Marta laughed — a short, real bark of a laugh — and even Ingrid smiled at that, briefly, before her face went serious again.

"There's one more thing," she said. "I asked you to bring the compass."

Ulyana reached under her collar and drew it out. The brass case was bright again — she'd polished it through the winter, an unconscious ritual, evenings in whatever bunkhouse or rented room she had — and the needle swung free and steady inside its glass. It pointed north. It had pointed north, faithfully, stupidly, honestly, since the day on the mountainside when Ingrid had taken the map unread and something in Ulyana's chest had finally set down its load.

Ingrid took it in both hands and turned it over, reading the engraving on the case back the way she must have read it a dozen times already: *E.V. — TRUE BEARING IS THE ONE YOU KEEP.*

"My mother gave him this," she said. "Before he left. Did you know that? I found her letters in the Kestrel Gap archive. She bought it with her first wages. Engraved it herself, badly — look, the T is crooked." Her thumb moved over the letters. "He carried it twenty years and it never brought him home. Then he handed it to a stranger on a riverbank and it worked fine."

"Maybe it needed a different hand," Ulyana said.

"Maybe." Ingrid held it out. "Keep it."

Ulyana didn't move. "It's yours. By rights—"

"It's a compass, Captain. It points north. That's all it ever did — all the rest was us, reading into it." Ingrid pressed it back into her palm and closed Ulyana's fingers over the case, and her grip was strong and work-roughened and brief. "He trusted you with the truest thing he had. I'm not taking it back from his heir-by-promise. Besides—" and here the dry humor surfaced again, "—you'll need it. Ore boats run a channel nobody's dredged in years. You'll want every honest instrument aboard."

* * *

They came down off the mountain in the gold part of the afternoon, the three of them, single file along the melting trail, and below them the river lay open and shining all the way to the bend where the *Kestrel Lark* waited on her ways, nearly ready, tarred and caulked and smelling of pitch and spring.

Tamir walked ahead, talking — about freight schedules, about the ore contract, about whether they should paint a name-board on the pilothouse or leave her plain — and Marta walked behind him answering less and less, in the way that meant she was listening to all of it and filing it away. Ulyana walked last, as she always did on trails, where she could see her whole small world strung out in front of her.

At the bend, Tamir stopped dead.

"Captain," he said. "Come look at this."

The launch ways ran down into the water, and the water had come up to meet them overnight — the spring rise, early and strong. The *Lark* floated. Nobody had meant her to float until Thursday; the wedges were still under her bilge, and the river had simply decided otherwise, lifting her gently off the ways so she strained at her mooring lines like a dog hearing its name.

"She's impatient," Marta said.

"She's *floating,*" Tamir said. "Look at her lines. Look at how she sits. Captain, she sits *right.*"

Ulyana stood on the bank and looked at the small boat riding the brown spring water, and felt the thing she had spent a whole winter learning to feel without flinching: the pull. Not the old pull — not the desperate, drowning-man's grip she'd had on the Sable Queen, the love that was mostly fear. This was lighter. This was the pull of a thing not yet begun.

"We could take her out tonight," Tamir said, and then caught himself, and laughed at himself. "We can't. There's no cargo, no clearance, the caulking needs two more days—"

"The caulking's done," Marta said. "I did it yesterday while you were negotiating."

"The clearance—"

"Osk signed the manifest this morning. He mentioned it. You were busy being outraged about grain rates." Marta looked at Ulyana. "Well, Captain?"

Ulyana put her hand in her pocket and closed it around the compass, feeling the needle swing inside the case, finding north the way it always did now — no drama in it, no mystery, just the plain magnetic truth of the world, which turned out to be enough.

"High water runs out of Grayling Bend by dark," she said. "If we're going to break her in, better on a full channel." She stepped down onto the ways, onto the wet planking, and put her boot against the hull the way she'd greeted every boat she'd ever loved. "Get the lines off. Marta, take the tiller. Tamir — forward, and mind the painter."

They moved. They were already moving, the three of them, quick and easy, a crew before they'd quite agreed to be one, and the *Lark* came off the last wedge with a soft groan of wood and slid stern-first into the current that had been waiting all winter to carry something somewhere.

Ulyana took the wheel. The river took the rest.

Below Grayling Bend the channel opened wide and fast, and the little boat leaned into it gladly, and the wind coming off the meltwater smelled like willows and mud and the beginning of the season. Ahead, far downstream where the valley narrowed toward the lowlands, she could just make out a shape on the water — a raft, or a skiff, or something, too far off to name, coming upriver against the current with the slow deliberate push of someone who had a long way to go and meant to arrive.

Tamir saw it too. He shaded his eyes.

"Freight, do you think?" he said. "This early?"

Ulyana watched it a moment longer. Something about the way it held the current — steady, unhurried, aimed — put a faint cold thread through the warmth of the evening, though she couldn't have said why.

"Whatever it is," she said, and eased the wheel over, "it's coming to us. Let's go meet it."
