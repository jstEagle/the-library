## 22. True Bearing

The river had come back to itself in the night.

Ulyana felt it before she saw it — a change in the way the water spoke against the hull, a loosening in the current's grip, the ice-voice gone and the old wet language of spring running underneath instead. She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel Star* with her coffee going cold on the rail beside her and watched dawn come up gray-gold over the willow flats, and she thought: *there you are.*

The break-up had been late this year. Three weeks of waiting at the dock at Kestrel Gap while the ice groaned and shifted and finally surrendered in great grinding plates that rode the current downriver like a fleet of white ships going to war. The whole town had turned out to watch it go. Ingrid had stood next to her on the bank, arms crossed against the cold, and said nothing for a long time, and then said, "My father used to talk about that sound. I thought he was lying about it being beautiful."

"It is," Ulyana had said.

"I know," Ingrid had said. "I heard it."

That had been three weeks ago. Since then there had been cargo to load — freight for the landings downriver, mail sacks, two crates of survey instruments consigned to Ingrid's crew working the lower claim — and a boat to learn. Because the *Kestrel Star* was not the *Sable Queen*, and never would be, and Ulyana had made her peace with that the way you make peace with a new pair of boots: by wearing them until they stopped arguing.

She was smaller. Shallower of draft, which was a mercy on a river still shedding its winter sandbars like a dog shedding a coat. Slower. Her wheel answered a half-beat later than Ulyana's hands expected, and every time it happened she felt the ghost of the *Sable Queen* rise up in her palms — that familiar heavy pull, the big boat leaning into a turn like a horse that knew your mind — and every time she let the ghost pass through her and kept steering.

Marta had told her once, somewhere on the trail last autumn, huddled under a tarp with snow coming down sideways: *"A boat is not a soul, girl. A soul is what you bring aboard."* Marta said things like that when she was peeling potatoes, which made them easy to dismiss and impossible to forget.

Ulyana brought her coffee up and drank it cold anyway. Below her feet she could hear Tamir moving around the engine room, talking to the machinery in his low running commentary — he talked to engines the way other men talked to horses, coaxing, flattering, occasionally threatening — and the engine answered him with its steady good heartbeat. He'd found his berth. Not the one he'd dreamed of on another man's boat, bound for cities he couldn't name. This one. Theirs. One-third each, signed on a page in front of the mining council's clerk, because after everything none of them trusted a handshake alone.

"Sandbar," Tamir called up through the deck grating, unnecessary and welcome. "Long one, left side, maybe a cable ahead."

"I see it," Ulyana called back.

And she did. She'd have seen it blind. It was the same bar that had lived off Miller's Landing for as long as anyone could remember — the one the old pilots called the Widow's Comb, long and pale and patient, shifting a few yards every season but never enough to forgive a careless helm. She'd threaded it a hundred times on the *Sable Queen*. She knew its moods the way you know a difficult relative: the way it built a false channel along its upstream tip that looked deep and wasn't; the way the true channel hugged the far bank closer than any sane pilot would like; the way, in low water, it showed a single snagged log at its crest like a raised finger, warning.

The log was there now, black and slick, just breaking the surface.

"Morning, old friend," Ulyana murmured.

She brought the *Kestrel Star* over easy, no drama, giving the bar the wide respectful berth it had earned across decades of eating hulls, and took the true channel close along the far bank where the current ran green and fast and honest. The boat leaned. Held. Came through clean into the long straight below, where the valley opened out and the morning sun found the water and set it burning.

Behind her, from the passenger bench by the pilothouse door, someone exhaled.

She'd almost forgotten the man was there. A freight agent for one of the downriver houses, riding to the next landing — a round, comfortable, talkative man named Ostergaard who had boarded an hour ago and had been trying ever since to make conversation with a captain who mostly answered in weather. Now he leaned forward, elbows on knees, watching the Widow's Comb slide past astern.

"That was fine work," he said. "I've come down this stretch twenty times and I've never seen a boat take that channel so close without so much as a shudder. You've run it before."

"A few times."

"On the *Sable Queen*, they say." He said the name carefully, the way people did now, the way they said *Kestrel Gap* or *the Aldous brothers* — names that had become stories over the winter, told in mining camps and river towns with embellishments Ulyana barely recognized. "They say she was seized. That's a hard thing, losing a boat."

Ulyana watched the water. A kingfisher went past low and electric blue, hunting the shallows.

"It was a hard winter," she said. "But I didn't lose her. She was taken. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

Ostergaard nodded slowly, sensing the shape of something bigger than he'd bargained for, and then — because he was the kind of man who couldn't leave a silence unfilled — his eyes went to the compass.

It hung at her throat on a leather cord, brass-cased, old, the lid scarred and the hinge worn bright. Ede Voss's compass. It swung gently with the motion of the boat, catching the light.

"Now that's an interesting piece," Ostergaard said. "Old pattern. Surveyor's make, isn't it? My father carried one like it. They don't make the cases anymore." He squinted. "Doesn't look like a standard binnacle compass. You navigate by that?"

Ulyana put her hand around it. The brass was warm from her skin.

"No," she said. "I navigate by the river. The river tells me everything I need — where the deep water runs, where the lies are. This doesn't point north."

"It doesn't?"

"Not most of the time." She smiled, and found to her mild surprise that the smile didn't cost anything. "Last year it pointed at a burned valley in the hills east of here. Before that, at a promise I hadn't kept yet. It pointed at my own worst winter for the better part of four hundred miles." She turned the compass over in her hand, feeling the worn hinge work. "It's a strange instrument. It only points at whatever you're refusing to look at."

Ostergaard laughed uncertainly, checking her face to see if she was joking. She wasn't, quite.

"And now?" he asked. "What does it point at now?"

Ulyana opened the lid.

The needle swung, wobbled, settled. Steady as a plumb line. Pointing downriver, down-valley, dead true north, like any honest instrument in the world.

"Now it points north," she said. "Whatever it was pointing me toward — I got there. Or it got out of me. One of those."

* * *

They made the first landing before noon — a mud bank and a plank dock and a cluster of cabins that smelled of woodsmoke and thawing earth. Marta came up from the galley to supervise the unloading, which meant standing with her arms folded criticizing how everyone else did it. Tamir worked the crane winch with two of the landing's men, calling the swings, and Ulyana watched him and thought about the boy who had crept out of a camp in the dark last autumn to sell them all to the Aldous brothers, and who had stood in the snow outside their shelter unable to do it, and who had confessed it later with his voice shaking apart — and how she had told him, in words she'd rehearsed all her life without knowing it: *You came back. That's the whole of the law.*

He caught her looking and grinned, gap-toothed, sunburned, alive.

"Captain! Freight agent says there's a rumor of a snag below the Narrows. Wants to know if we're insured."

"Tell him we're careful. It's cheaper."

Marta climbed up to the pilothouse as they cast off again, bringing two cups of coffee and the particular expression she wore when she intended to say something Ulyana wouldn't want to hear.

"You're smiling at the river," Marta said.

"I'm allowed."

"You are. I'm noting it. Last spring you smiled at the river like a woman greeting a creditor she owed money to. Today you smile like—" She waved a hand, searching. "Like a woman walking home."

Ulyana took the coffee. It was hot and too strong and perfect. "The debt's gone, Marta."

"The debt's gone," Marta agreed. "Debts are the easy ones. It's the other ledgers that take longer." She sipped. "How's the compass?"

"Pointing north."

"Mm. And how are *you* pointing?"

Ulyana considered the question more seriously than it was asked. Downriver the valley widened toward the lowlands, toward the landings and towns where the story of Kestrel Gap had arrived ahead of them all winter — the story of the map, and the fraud, and the captain who gave away a boat rather than sell a dead man's promise. Some tellings had her a saint. Some had her a fool. Both were wrong; the truth was smaller and harder and hers.

"There's a place," she said, "about six miles down, on the east bank. Burned homestead. Fifteen years ago this spring."

Marta went very still. She knew. Of course she knew — Marta knew everything that happened within earshot of her stove.

"The fire," Marta said quietly.

"My family's place. What was left of it." Ulyana turned the compass lid closed with her thumb. "I've gone past it forty times since. Never stopped. Never looked. Kept the wheel steady and my eyes forward, because looking would mean—" She stopped. Started again. "This morning I sent word to Tamir. We stop there today. An hour. I'm going to walk up to it."

Marta looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached over and squeezed Ulyana's forearm, brief and hard, cook's hands, strong as rope.

"Good," she said. "I'll make lunch for after. People don't know it, but grief goes easier fed."

* * *

The burned homestead announced itself before she saw it — a lone cottonwood on the east bank, half-dead, fire-scarred up one whole side but stubbornly, absurdly alive, throwing new leaf-buds along its wounded trunk. Her father had planted it the year she was born. It should have died with everything else. Instead it had spent fifteen years doing the one thing she hadn't managed: staying rooted in the ruined ground and growing anyway.

Ulyana tied off at the bank and walked up alone.

The chimney still stood. Chimneys always did — the fire took the wood and left the stone standing like a monument to the part of a house that never held anything warm. Around it the foundation lines showed through the grass, and the lilac hedge her mother had planted had gone feral and huge, blooming early, filling the whole clearing with a sweetness that hurt to breathe.

She stood among the foundations with the spring wind moving the grass and let herself look.

Not the way she'd looked in nightmares — the flames, the shouting, the oil drums in the wagon bed, Hale's foreman saying *empty warehouses, just set it by the east wall.* Not that. She looked at the small things. The corner where her bed had been. The stone step where her father sat evenings. The well cover, rotted through. The grave markers she and the neighbors had raised afterward, two of them, weathered soft, her mother's name nearly illegible now.

"I delivered it," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the open air, unused to this ground. "The oil. I was seventeen and he paid our boat debt and I believed him, and then I stood in the crowd and watched it burn and I said nothing for fifteen years. And then I said it. To a stranger's daughter, on a mountain, because she needed to hear that secrets rot." She crouched and put her palm flat on the warm stone step. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I just wanted you to know I stopped carrying it. Most days."

The wind moved through the lilacs. The cottonwood creaked its one-sided creak. Somewhere a meadowlark was conducting its business with tremendous confidence, and Ulyana found — unexpectedly, stupidly, wonderfully — that she was laughing a little even as the tears came, because the meadowlark did not care about any of it, and there was a freedom in that.

She stayed an hour, as promised. She pulled weeds from the graves. She told her mother about the boat — *smaller, ours, the engine has opinions* — and told her father about the sandbar at Miller's Landing — *still there, still cheating, I greeted it like family* — and at the end she took the compass from around her neck and held it up, open, letting it swing.

North. True and level. But she understood now what Ede Voss had understood at the end, pressing it into her hands with his fever-bright eyes: the needle had never been broken. It had simply refused, all those miles, to pretend. An instrument that points at truth is not malfunctioning. It's the only kind worth owning.

She hung it back around her neck and walked down to the boat, and her crew were waiting on the deck — Tamir at the rail, Marta at the galley door with food already going, and moored alongside, flying the survey pennant, a lean little workboat with Ingrid Voss standing in her bow.

Ingrid came aboard without asking, which by now was her privilege. She looked at Ulyana's face and read the whole hour off it, the way surveyors read country.

"Well?" she said.

"It's still there," Ulyana said. "The lilacs took over everything. She'd have liked that."

Ingrid nodded. Then, awkwardly, formally, the way she did the few things that mattered most: "Claim's producing. First shipment goes down on your boat next month, if you'll carry it."

"Captain's honor," Ulyana said. "Which, as you know, is a real thing, badly kept, absolutely kept."

Ingrid almost smiled. From her it was practically an embrace. She clasped Ulyana's shoulder once, hard, and went back to her own vessel, and Ulyana rang the bell and cast off into the long gold afternoon.

* * *

Below the Narrows the light went long and amber, and the *Kestrel Star* ran easy with the current, her engine humming, her holds full, her small crew scattered about her doing the thousand small tasks that made a boat a living thing. Ostergaard the freight agent had disembarked at the last landing, pressing Ulyana's hand and saying he'd tell people what he'd seen, meaning the sandbar, meaning the channel, meaning more than he knew.

Now it was just the three of them and the river.

Tamir came up to take the wheel for the evening stretch — he'd earned his pilot's hours honestly, and Ulyana taught him the way her father had taught her: by standing back and letting the river do the lecturing. Marta brought supper up on deck because the evening was too fine for the galley. They ate with their backs against the pilothouse house, plates on knees, watching the banks slide past green and dripping with new growth.

"Miller's bar tomorrow," Tamir said, mouth full. "Going to thread it myself, if you'll let me."

"I'll let you," Ulyana said. "And I'll stand behind you saying nothing, which will be the hardest work I do all week."

Marta snorted. "He'll do fine. He talks to engines; bars are just slow engines."

The sun went down behind the western ridge and the water turned to hammered copper, and Ulyana sat between her people with the compass warm against her chest and felt the whole of the last year settle into its final shape — not the shape she'd have chosen, never that; the *Sable Queen* still ached in her like a lost limb, some mornings worse than others — but a true shape, borne out, like a line on a chart that matches the country it claims to describe.

Ede Voss had made her promise one thing, a dying man on a dirty deck: *carry it to her before the passes close.* She had carried it farther than any map ever went — through snow and ambush and betrayal and confession — and delivered it intact. And in the delivering, everything else had come due and been paid: the fire, the oil, the silence, the debt, the boat. All of it traded away, all of it told, all of it set down at last on the true bearing.

A passenger had asked her that morning what the compass was.

*It belonged to a man who kept his last promise,* she'd said. *So did I.*

Ahead, the river bent south around a wooded point, and beyond the bend the first stars were coming out over water that ran deep, and free, and clear all the way down.
