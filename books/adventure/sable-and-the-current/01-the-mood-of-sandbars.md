## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The Verre River had a mood, and Yara Okafor had learned to read it the way other people read faces.

This morning it was sulking. A low gray ceiling of cloud pressed down on the water, and the current ran slow and thick with silt, the color of old pewter. No wind to speak of. The kind of day when the river gave you nothing and expected everything.

Yara stood at the tiller of the *Marrow*, their cargo boat, one bare heel hooked over the tiller bar, and let the boat tell them what it needed. Twenty years on this water—no, nineteen, since they'd taken the wheel at fourteen, the year their mother drowned, the year everything became theirs to carry—had taught them that a boat was never really steered. It was negotiated with. The *Marrow* was a stubborn old thing, forty feet of patched hull and a diesel engine that ran on prayer and cheap red fuel, and she liked to drift left when the current slackened. So Yara held her a hair right and let the river think it was winning.

"Sandbar coming," they said, not loudly, to the deck itself, because there was no one else to say it to.

The sandbars of the Verre were famous in the way that bad weather is famous. They moved. A bar that had grounded a barge in April might be gone by June, migrated forty yards downstream by the slow patient labor of the current, and a channel that had been safe for a generation could silt shut in a single flood season. The ferry guild published charts, of course—expensive charts, updated quarterly, stamped with the guild's seal of a ferry wheel crossed by a ledger line—but every working captain knew the charts were already lies the moment the ink dried. The river didn't read. The river just moved.

Yara read the water instead. Read the way the current dimpled over a shallow, the way a smooth stretch of river could mean depth or could mean a bar so fine the water passed over it without argument. Read the color: green-brown for deep, tea-gold for shallow, and the pale, milky gray-green that meant sand just beneath, waiting. Read the birds. Herons stood where the water was kind. Gulls argued where fish were confused by current seams. This morning a heron stood on a spit that hadn't been on any chart Yara had ever seen, one leg lifted, regarding the *Marrow* with the flat professional disinterest of a fellow worker.

Yara swung wide around the heron's island and felt the keel whisper over sand, a soft dragging kiss, there and gone. Good. That was the channel, then, a boat-length left of where they'd guessed. They logged it mentally—third bar down from Widow's Bend, favor the far bank, two feet of water over the crown at this stage—and kept the wheel.

The cargo was salt pork in waxed barrels, a crate of glass lamp chimneys, forty bolts of undyed wool, and a coffin, which was not unusual. Downriver towns always needed coffins. The coffin rode amidships, lashed down, lid on, and Yara tried not to think about it, and thought about it anyway, the way you always think about the thing you're trying not to think about. Somebody downriver was going to open that crate and weep over it, and Yara would have already been paid and gone, three towns away, carrying someone else's barrels. That was the job. The river moved everything—grain, glass, wool, grief—and kept none of it.

*Like me,* Yara thought, and then was annoyed at themselves for thinking it, and put the thought over the side like bilge water.

* * *

The guild dock at Harrow's Landing came into view just past noon: a long gray timber structure on pilings, the guild flag—blue with the wheel and ledger—hanging dead in the still air. Behind it the town climbed its little hill in terraces of slate roofs, and above the town, far above, faint as a pencil line, the mountains. The Serrance Range. White-capped even in late summer, a wall of stone and ice that ran the whole eastern horizon like a sentence no one downriver had ever finished reading.

Yara looked at the mountains the way they always did—quickly, sidelong, the way you look at a scar—and then looked back at the water, which was honest about what it was.

Mooring at the guild dock was a ritual of small humiliations. First the harbor fee, collected by a boy with a slate who couldn't have been more than twelve and already had the guild's manner down: palm out, eyes elsewhere, the fee non-negotiable. Then the mooring fee, collected at the office by a woman named Pell who had a voice like a filing cabinet. Then the cargo tax, assessed on manifest, and the wharfage, and the light fee, though the guild's one harbor lantern had been dark since spring. Yara paid it all in counted coins from a tin box under the tiller, and watched the tin get visibly lighter, and did the arithmetic they always did.

The debt stood at two hundred and forty crowns. It had stood at three hundred and eleven in the spring. At this rate—good runs, no breakdowns, no fines—Yara would clear it in a little over two years, and the *Marrow* would be theirs outright, and the guild would have no more claim on them than on any other licensed master.

Two years. Yara was twenty-four. They would be twenty-six when the boat was truly theirs, and the thought arrived with its usual strange double weight: relief, and underneath the relief, a small cold question, the one they never let surface all the way. *And then what?*

The debt had come to them the way most debts come to the poor: inherited. Their mother, Adaeze Okafor, had licensed the *Marrow* through the guild twenty-two years ago, and when she went up into the mountains and never came back—when the search parties came down empty-handed and the guild's lawyers came around with their faces arranged into sympathy—the debt hadn't drowned with her. Debts don't drown. Debts are the one cargo the river never loses. Fourteen-year-old Yara had signed the same papers their mother had signed, in a guild office that smelled of lamp oil and cold tea, and the clerk had looked at their shaking hand and said, *Your mother was a capable woman. We expect the same,* and Yara had said nothing, because at fourteen they had already learned that the guild did not want conversation. It wanted signature.

They were capable. They knew that now, knew it in their hands and their spine. There wasn't a stretch of the Verre from the salt marshes to the last navigable rapids that Yara couldn't run blindfolded in fog, and had, twice, once for a doctor and once for a bribe. They could read a sandbar's mood from a hundred yards. They could back a forty-foot boat into a berth the width of a church aisle. Other captains asked their advice; the guild's own pilots, the ones who'd never touched a tiller they didn't own, consulted Yara's notes on the shifting channels above Widow's Bend. Competence was the one thing the river had given them for free, and they had ground it like a lens, and through it they could see their whole life laid out with terrible clarity: run the cargo, pay the debt, patch the hull, run the cargo, pay the debt. A life as smooth and repetitive as the wake behind the boat, closing over itself, going nowhere the river wasn't already going.

"Okafor." Pell stood at the top of the gangplank with her manifest board. "You're late."

"The river was slow," Yara said.

"The river is always slow. You're always late." Pell made her marks. "Pork, chimneys, wool, one coffin. Receiver's the undertaker on Chapel Street, he'll want it tonight." She looked up, and for a moment something almost human moved behind her eyes. "Voss is in town."

Yara's hands went still on the manifest they were countersigning.

"Odile Voss," Pell said, unnecessarily, because there was only one Voss. "Enforcement captain. She's asking after salvage. Anything come up out of the river lately, she wants to hear about it. Anything at all." Pell's filing-cabinet voice didn't change, but she said the next part slightly faster, slightly lower, the way people speak when they're saying more than they're supposed to. "There's a reward posted. For salvage declarations. And a fine for undeclared salvage that would buy a boat like yours twice over."

"I haul pork and coffins," Yara said. "The river doesn't give me presents."

"No," Pell agreed. "It never does." She stamped the manifest, and Yara took their copy, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it was somehow one of the longer conversations Yara had had that week.

* * *

They delivered the coffin at dusk.

Chapel Street smelled of tallow and cut pine. The undertaker was a wide, sad man named Brech who paid in exact coins and insisted on helping carry, though Yara hadn't asked and didn't need it. Together they bore the coffin up three steps and into a parlor lit by lamps whose chimneys—Yara noticed, with the small useless precision of the mind that notices everything—were cracked. From the crate. Their crate.

"I'll take the chimneys back and swap them," Yara said. "No charge."

"Leave them." Brech set the coffin down and rested a hand on its lid, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder. "The widow can't afford new. She'll say the cracks let the light in different." He looked at Yara then, really looked, the way people sometimes did when they were too tired to maintain the usual distance. "You knew her husband? Ferro the lock-keeper?"

"No," Yara said. "I never met him."

"He used to wave your boat past the lock without the fee. Every time. Said a river that charges for passage isn't a river, it's a shop." Brech almost smiled. "The guild fined him for it eleven times. He paid every one. Said it was worth it."

Yara carried that out of the parlor and down the steps and into the blue evening, and it sat in their chest all the way back to the boat, heavy as ballast: *a river that charges for passage isn't a river.* Ferro the lock-keeper, who Yara had never met, who had waved them through his lock for years without knowing he was doing it, had handed them a sentence they would carry longer than the coffin.

The *Marrow* rode low and quiet at her berth. Yara lit the cabin lamp, ate cold pork and bread standing up, and did the day's arithmetic in the tin box's company. After fees and Brech's payment and the fuel they'd have to buy tomorrow: the debt stood at two hundred and thirty-eight crowns. They wrote it in the ledger in their small, hard hand, the same ledger their mother had started, the first pages in a looping confident script, the later pages in Yara's. Two handwritings, one column of numbers, twenty years of the same slow subtraction.

Then they did what they did every night, the thing they'd never told anyone, the thing they'd have denied with a straight face if asked. They climbed to the wheelhouse roof with the tin lamp, sat with their back against the wheel housing, and looked at the mountains.

The Serrance Range at night was a black serration against a sky full of hard stars. Somewhere up there, past the foothills, past the timberline, past everything the river touched, there were passes. The maps in the guild office showed three of them, all marked with the guild's seal and a toll schedule, because the guild had bought or bullied its way into every way through the range and posted its prices at every trailhead. But the boatmen told a different story, the old ones, the ones who drank slow and talked slow. They said there was a fourth way. A high pass, free as weather, that the first travelers had used before the guild was anything but a wharf and a dream. They called it the Sable Current, because in winter the wind streamed the snow through it in a long dark river of its own, flowing east to west, a current of the sky.

Yara's mother had believed in it. That was the word the gossips used, *believed*, with a small curl of pity. Adaeze Okafor had spent years asking after it in river towns, trading stories for stories, filling a journal with fragments, and then one autumn morning she had kissed her child on both cheeks, said *keep the boat, keep the ledger, I'll be back before the ice*, and gone upriver with six other dreamers and a hired guide, and the river had never given any of them back.

Yara had been four when she left. Fourteen when the lawyers finished. Twenty-four now, and the mountains still stood there, and the question still stood there with them, the one that rose every night at this hour no matter how Yara tried to ballast it down: *What if she was right? What if it's there? And what kind of person does it make you, that you've never once gone to look?*

The answer was always the same, and always insufficient: *The kind of person with a debt. The kind of person with a boat. The kind of person the river keeps.*

A heron crossed the harbor, low, a gray ghost against the darker gray of the water, and Yara watched it go and felt the restlessness move in their chest the way the tide moves under a hull—you couldn't see it, but you could feel the whole boat lifting, wanting, straining at its lines. That was the truth of their life that the ledger didn't record. They were not unhappy. That was the trap of it. They were good at this, and being good at something is a kind of harbor, and harbors are where boats go to rust. Some nights Yara could feel it happening, feel themselves becoming a fixture of the Verre, a competent gray presence like Pell's office or the dark harbor lantern, and the feeling was not quite fear and not quite grief but had the taste of both.

The lamp guttered. Yara trimmed the wick, and in the fresh light their eye fell on the tide table nailed by the wheel, the guild's printed sheet, and they frowned at it, because something was off in the figures. Tomorrow's low tide at Widow's Bend was listed at two feet below chart datum. Then, penciled in a clerk's hasty hand across the corner, a correction, an asterisk, a note: *anomalous draw-down forecast — all masters take note — cause unknown.*

Two feet below datum. Yara had run this river their whole life and had never heard of such a thing. The tide at Widow's Bend didn't miss its mark by two feet; the tide at Widow's Bend was punctual as a debt.

They looked down the dark harbor, toward the black line of the channel, toward Widow's Bend, six miles downriver, where the banks narrowed and the current ran deep and the herons stood on bars that weren't on any chart. An anomalous draw-down. The river pulling back, further than it had ever pulled, exposing—what? The river never showed what was under it. That was its whole nature. That was the bargain everyone accepted: the river carried your cargo and kept its secrets.

Yara sat with the lamp and the stars and the black serration of the mountains, and felt the restlessness in their chest go suddenly, terribly still, like water before a bar shifts.

They would run down at first light, they told themselves. Just to look. Just to see what the river was hiding.
